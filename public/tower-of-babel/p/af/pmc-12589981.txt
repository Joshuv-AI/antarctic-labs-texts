A cluster randomized controlled trial of a train-the-trainer behavioral intervention delivered via respite care centers to improve nutritional outcomes and quality of life persons with dementia and their caregivers
Authors: Suparna Qanungo, Mohan Madisetti, Martina Mueller, Teresa J. Kelechi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate a behavioral intervention, Partners at Meals (PAM), designed to empower caregivers (CGs) to improve caloric intake, weight, and quality of life (QOL) of persons with dementia (PWD), and to address dysfunctional behaviors during mealtime. The study also aimed to assess differences in caregiver psychosocial outcomes, including depression, burden, and QOL.DesignA cluster randomized controlled experimental design was used, in which participating Respite Care Centers (RCCs) were randomized to either the PAM intervention group (n = 3) or the control enhanced usual care (EUC) group (n = 3). Enrolled PWD/CG dyads were assigned to PAM or EUC based on the RCCs they attended.Setting and participantsThis longitudinal 6-month clinical trial was conducted across six RCCs in the Southeast coastal region of the United States. A total of 53 PWD/CG dyads provided post-enrollment data: PAM (n = 27) and EUC (n = 26).MethodsThe PAM intervention was delivered using a train-the-trainer approach, based on the C3P (Change the Person, Change the People, Change the Place) model, and was implemented through RCC volunteers who provided adaptive mealtime strategies via telehealth to CGs in the home. Primary outcome measures for PWD were changes in body weight and feeding/dysfunctional behaviors from baseline to 6-month follow-up or end of study. Secondary outcomes included mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), caloric intake, and QOL. Caregiver outcomes included changes in burden, depression, and QOL. Between- and within-group comparisons were performed using pooled and paired t-tests or chi-square tests as appropriate. Generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) were used to assess outcomes over time.ResultsAt enrollment, PWD participants had a diagnosis of mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease or related dementia, with a mean age of 77.6 ± 9.8 years. The mean age of caregivers was 66.3 ± 11.8 years. The PWDs in the PAM group started with greater weight loss prior to study enrollment but showed a slightly lower > 5% weight loss from baseline to end of study (20.8%), compared to the EUC group (22.7%), although not statistically significant (p = 0.275). While the EUC group experienced a slight decrease in MUAC from baseline to follow-up of 0.2 ± 7 cm, those in the PAM group showed an increase of 1.0 ± 2.0 cm, suggesting better maintenance of nutritional status. The estimated mean daily caloric intake between the PWD groups upon enrollment (368 ± 131, p = 0.006) showed the EUC group consumed more calories than the PAM group throughout the study. Although no significant differences were found in the unadjusted changes in mealtime scores for feeding difficulty (−0.8 ± 3.1, p = 0.411), dysfunctional behavior (−1.4, 6.0, p = 0.605) or QOL (0.3 ± 6.6, p = 0.482), the PAM group exhibited a decrease in dysfunctional mealtime behaviors over the study period. No significant differences in the unadjusted means scores at baseline or changes from baseline to follow-up were observed on measures of CG depression (−1.3 ± 4.6, p = 0.371) or burden (−1.4 ± 5.4, p = 0.354). Results show that there was a significant difference in the unadjusted QOL score between groups at end of study follow-up (10.8 ± 15.8, p = 0.031); mean health state scores among caregivers in the PAM group (81.2 ± 3.4) were higher at the end of study compared to the EUC group (68.9 ± 3.7), indicating better general health state and QOL.ConclusionFindings suggest that the PAM intervention, using a train-the-trainer approach shows promise in improving nutritional outcomes and reducing dysfunctional mealtime behaviors in PWD, as well as enhancing the QOL of CG. This approach extends the reach of the intervention and fosters the idea that mealtime interventions to enhance in-home caregiving may offer a viable approach to maintaining and improving the nutritional status of the PWD and overall well-being of both PWD and their CG, which is essential for addressing the multifaceted challenges of dementia care.

Introduction

Caring for persons with dementia (PWD) is emotionally, physically, and financially demanding, often requires significant time and energy, leading to heightened stress and an increased risk of poor health among caregivers. Persons with dementia often face significant challenges related to nutrition as their ability to prepare and consume appropriate foods declines over time [1]. These challenges include difficulty preparing food safely, selecting a suitable diet, consuming balanced meals and appropriate portions, using utensils, and, as the disease progresses, recognizing hunger and accepting assistance with eating [2]. Behavioral and cognitive dysfunction also pose significant barriers to maintaining adequate nutrition, as they can lead to agitation, refusal to eat, and difficulty following mealtime routines [3]. These impairments frequently result in eating difficulties, leading to weight loss and nutritional deficiencies that can further exacerbate PWD health decline. Consequently, informal family caregivers (CG) must proactively address these changes to support proper nutrition and overall health, while attending to their own health to ensure they have the physical and emotional resilience needed to provide consistent and effective care.

However, studies show that nearly 30-50% of CG of PWD experience negative health outcomes including clinical levels of depression, which can significantly impact their mental health and overall well-being, and ability to provide care [4]. Depression in caregivers is often compounded by a sense of burden stemming from the progressive nature of dementia, which necessitates continuous adjustments to care strategies and lifestyle. In addition, the quality of the CG and PWD dyadic relationship, accommodation of feeding needs, aversive behaviors of the PWD at mealtime (agitation or refusal to eat) and the environment in which mealtimes occur can affect the PWD nutritional status [5]. These challenges highlight the importance of equipping CG with resources and support to improve their capacity to provide nutritional support for PWD.

Proper nutrition plays a critical role in maintaining the health and functionality of PWD, yet mealtime can become a source of conflict or difficulty due to behaviors such as refusal to eat or difficulty swallowing. Additionally, these mealtime dynamics can influence a PWD's ability to care for themselves, as poor nutrition and hydration may worsen cognitive and physical decline. These challenges may contribute to unintentional weight loss and malnutrition in PWD, further complicating the caregiving process [6]. Understanding caregiver mental health needs and providing practical strategies for managing mealtime behaviors are critical components of interventions designed to improve outcomes for both CG and PWD.

To address these challenges, we evaluated Partners at Meals (PAM), a behavioral intervention designed to empower caregivers to address dysfunctional behaviors, and improve caloric intake, weight and quality of life (QOL) of PWD during mealtime. The study also sought to determine differences in CG psychosocial variables including depression, burden, and QOL.

Methods

Trial design

Details of the study protocol, theoretical foundation, and methodology have been previously described [7]. This longitudinal 6-month clinical trial was conducted among three large RCC organizations operating six Respite Care Centers (RCCs) in the Southeast coastal region of the US. Using a cluster randomized controlled experimental design based on each RCCs respective average monthly client census, the organizations were randomized to either the PAM intervention (n = 3) or the control enhanced usual control care (EUC) group (n = 3). Enrolled PWD/CG dyads were then allocated to the PAM or EUC control groups based upon their RCC of attendance. The trial is reported according to the CONSORT statement and its reporting of cluster randomized trials [8,9]

Volunteers (VOL), providing unpaid services at PAM RCCs were approached, trained by the researchers to deliver the PAM intervention, and then assigned to enrolled PWD/CG dyads at their centers. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Medical University of South Carolina Institutional Review Board. The study was conducted in compliance with Good Clinical Practices (GCP) of the International Conference of Harmonization (ICH) and accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to data collection activities. The CGs and VOLs received compensation for their time in the completion of research activities while in the study. The study is registered at ClinialTrials.gov (NCT#03622814).

Participants

Persons with dementia (PWD)

The PWD participation was limited to individuals aged 45 years or older who attended a participating RCC at least once per week, had a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease or a related dementia in the mild to moderate stage, as demonstrated by a Functional Assessment Staging Scale (FAST) [10], score of 4 or greater. Eligible PWD also needed to require some level of supervision and exhibit mild dysfunctional behaviors during meals (e.g., needing redirection). Exclusion criteria included the presence of wasting disorders, heart or renal failure, COPD, or end-stage cancer, receiving enteral feeding, undergoing active treatment by a speech pathologist or therapist, diagnosed with dysphagia (as identified by their caregiver), or enrolled in or qualifying for hospice care.

Caregivers (CG)

The CG were eligible to join the study if they resided with, or on the same property as the PWD, provided at least four hours of care per day, and assisted with activities of daily living, including meal preparation. After receiving study instruction, CG were required to demonstrate competency in their ability to use a tablet device to conduct telehealth visits (PAM group only), open emails, and enter data into a secure online data management survey platform (Research Electronic Data Capture - REDCap). CG who received payment for their services were excluded from the study.

Interventions

PAM intervention

Grounded in the C3P (Change the Person, Change the People, Change the Place) model [1], PAM was developed utilizing a train-the-trainer approach designed to provide CG support by engaging RCC volunteers (VOL) to implement evidence-based adaptive mealtime strategies via telehealth in the home. The VOLs were trained in C3P and adult education by a member of the research team. Training topics included, among others: dementia and mealtime behaviors, adaptive strategies to promote feeding and nutrition, adult education principles, and the use of the HIPAA-compliant telehealth (virtual) platform, Doxy.me which was used for interaction between both VOLs and CGs. More details are provided in our previously published protocol paper [7].

After training, VOLs were paired with a consented CG/PWD dyad from the same RCC and an initial in-person meeting with the CG was scheduled. Prior to this initial meeting, the CG completed the Edinburgh Feeding in Dementia Scale (EdFED) with additional researcher developed dysfunctional mealtime behavioral items (XL) and the C3P checklist to assess the Place, People, or Person. At the initial VOL/CG virtual meeting, which was attended by the researchers, the assigned VOL provided instructional training to the CG using the C3P model, reviewed EdFED/XL results, and jointly developed a meal plan with adaptive strategies to facilitate the mealtime process and reinforce caregiving skills. The CG and VOL were provided tablets with data plans for use throughout the study and were required to hold monthly virtual meetings at an agreed upon time. At least one virtual meeting per dyad was observed by the research team to monitor fidelity and assess VOL competency. For the reminder of the study (months 1–6), CG were given a standardized scale and cloth tape measure and instructed to weigh the PWD and record mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC). The CG also completed a series of surveys electronically and uploaded a detailed food log and photos of three typical meals (a breakfast, lunch, and dinner), and a 2−5 minute video of an everyday PWD mealtime interaction to a secure server.

EUC control intervention

Dyads (CG/PWD) in the control group received educational materials but did not participate in the VOL-facilitated PAM intervention. At baseline, CG were provided with a tablet device with a data plan, and a study manual containing basic health and nutrition information, instructions, and selected materials adapted from The Savvy Caregiver Program [11]. Selected topics include dementia caregiving strategies, self-care, communication, and managing challenging behaviors. The CG in the control group completed the same PWD measures, completed identical monthly assessments, and uploads as the PAM intervention group.

Outcomes

Person with dementia (PWD) measures

Functional ability was assessed at baseline to describe the PWD study population using the Functional Assessment Staging Scale (FAST). The FAST is a seven-stage, 16-item tracking system for the progression of dementia. Different ratings of individual functional abilities are categorized in accordance with stages of dementia. Stages range from 1 (Normal aging process) to 7 (Severe dementia), with the stage 4 mid-point being indication of Mild dementia. Higher stages correspond with ratings of lower functional ability.

Cognitive impairment was assessed with the Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) at baseline to describe the population [12]. The MMSE is an 11-item instrument that assesses five different domains of cognition, namely: (1) Orientation, (2) Memory, (3) Attention and calculation, (4) Language, and (5) Design copying. The MMSE has a maximum score of 30. Higher scores on this instrument are indicative of better cognitive functioning.

Body weight was measured in pounds using a standard analog scale that was first calibrated by the researchers and given to participants to use at-home while in the study. The CGs were instructed to weigh the PWD before a lunch and after removing heavy outer garments. As part of the study’s Data and Safety Monitoring Plan (DSMP), the researchers conducted a continuous review of all PWD weight data throughout the course of the study to monitor for indications of possible malnutrition that may require medical intervention. Because of the progressive weight loss seen in Alzheimer’s disease, maintenance of baseline weight was considered a positive outcome. The CG of PWD identified with a ≥5% weight loss from their previous reported monthly weight were contacted by the researchers and referrals provided.

Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) was used as a proxy for nutritional health status [13] and was recorded monthly by CG using a cloth tape measure and estimated to the nearest centimeter. The MUAC was measured at the thickest part of the right upper-arm between the top of the shoulder to the tip of elbow.

Caloric intake was calculated by the researchers using ESHA Food Processor software version 11.6 (ESHA Research, Salem, Oregon) and its underlying food composition datasets. Nutrient data in the ESHA database includes, among others, the USDA Nutrient Database for Standard Reference and over 1000 additional data sources. A mean daily estimate for PWD caloric intake was derived from the sampling of returned dietary food logs and before/after photographs of 3 every-day meals (a breakfast, lunch, and dinner). Missing values were imputed for composite recipes [14].

Dysfunctional eating behavior was measured with the 10-item EdFED [[15], [16], [17]] with 10 additional study specific items (XL) developed by the researchers. Scored on a 3-point Likert scale, each item on the EDFED/XL is rated as: 0 = ‘never’, 2 = ‘sometimes’, and 3 = ‘often’. There are no cut-off points and total scores range from 0−30. A higher score is indicative of greater feeding difficulty and a higher XL score suggests increased dysfunctional behavior at mealtimes.

Quality of life was assessed using the 13-item Alzheimer’s Disease Quality of Life (QOL-AD) scale [18] which evaluates physical condition, mood, memory, functional abilities, interpersonal relationships, participation in meaningful activities, financial situation, and overall QOL. Scored on a 4-point Likert Scale (1 = ‘poor’, 2 = ‘fair’, 3 = ‘good’, 4 = ‘excellent’), the QOL-AD produces a range of scores from 1 to 52. There are no cut-off points and higher scores on this instrument are indicative of better perceived QOL.

Caregiver (CG) measures

Depression was assessed with the 10-item version of the Center for Epidemiological Studies—Depression scale (CES-D). Scores range from 0 to 30; a score of 15 or greater is considered extremely high depression [19,20]. Burden was assessed using the 12-item Zarit Burden Scale. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 0 = ‘never’ to 4 = ‘nearly always’ with higher scores indicating greater burden [21,22]. Quality of life was measured with the European Quality of Life (Euro-QOL-5D) (used with permission). The Euro-QOL 5D-3 L provides a self-rating on 5 domains including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and depression. Domains are rated on three levels and a 3-point scale (1 = ‘no problems’, 2 = ‘some problems/moderately difficulty’, and 3 = ‘unable/extreme difficulty’ to generate a five number descriptive profile. Higher level scores in each of the five domains are indicative of diminished QOL in those areas [23,24].

Sample size

The primary measures against which the study was powered is the change in weight and dysfunctional behavior for the PWD. Sample size and power calculations were based on two-sided pooled t-test of mean differences in weight changes at the follow-up visit between the two groups, PAM and EUC, with significance level set at 0.05. Sample size requirements were adjusted to account for a three-level (RCC, VOL, PWD/CG) cluster randomization scheme performed by the biostatistician, using the variance inflation factor method described by Teerenstra et al. [25]. For a 4.8 pound difference in weight change in PWD from baseline between the two groups assuming a standard deviation of 5.0 pounds (approximately equivalent to a 5% change in weight for a person weighing 100 pounds and considered a clinically relevant difference), a sample size of 24 per RCC (or per group) would provide at least 85% power for intracluster correlations of up to 0.05 and 0.1, respectively. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study sample in terms of demographic and clinical features as appropriate. The intent-to-treat sample comprised all participants with at least one post-baseline measurement. First, PWD/CG outcomes and change from baseline in outcomes were compared between and within the groups using pooled and paired t-test or chi-square test, as appropriate. Next, outcomes were assessed over time and compared between the groups using a generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) approach with PAM/EUC as the primary independent variable and the primary outcome measures as the dependent variable in individual models. Group (PAM or EUC), visit, and a group-by-visit interaction terms were included as a fixed effects; a term representing the cluster (RCC) effects was included in the model as random effect to account for correlation among PWD/CG dyads within the same RCC. Although standard dyadic analysis of these data was not possible since different instruments were used for assessments of the individual members of the dyad due to the nature of the underlying disease of the PWD, in a third step, PWD models were adjusted for covariables obtained from the caregiver to account for influences of caregiver burden, depression and QOL on PWD outcome measures and vice versa; CG models were adjusted for PWD QOL and mealtime behaviors. Adjusted least squares means and differences in means are reported with their respective 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were conducted using SAS Statistical Software Version 9.4 (Copyright© 2016 by SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were collected between October 2018 and May 2023.

PWD demographics and characteristics

At time of study enrollment, PWD were on average 81.0 ± 7.7 years of age in the PAM group and 73.9 ± 10.5 years in the EUC group. Both groups were similarly distributed in terms of race, ethnicity, marital status, and education levels. Diagnoses of Alzheimer’s, vascular, and unclassified dementias were most prevalent among the two groups. Regular diets were present for 74% of PWD in the PAM group and 81% of the EUC group, while nutritional supplements were given to a higher percentage of PWD in the EUC group than the PAM group (39% versus 22%, respectively). Conversely, oral health/dental conditions among PWD were more prevalent among the EUC group (50%) compared to the PAM group (26%). Although MMSE scores were comparable (12.6 ± 6.5 PAM versus 12.2 ± 6.7 EUC), 66% of PWD in the PAM group had FAST scores ranging from Stage 4 (mild dementia) to Stage 6 (moderately severe dementia) whereas 84.6% of PWD in the EUC group had scores between Stage 6 (moderately severe dementia) to Stage 7 (severe dementia) indicating greater functional impairment among the EUC group. The PWD in the PAM group had better “adequate” hearing (74.1% versus 46.2%) and “adequate” vision (81.5% vs. 42.3%) compared to PWD in the EUC group. Mood disorders were present among 37% of PWD in the PAM group compared to 62% of the EUC group.

CG demographics and characteristics

CGs in the PAM group had a mean age of 69.4 ± 10.8 years, while those in the EUC group were slightly younger with a mean of 62.9 ± 12.2 years. Overall, most CGs were female, White, non-Hispanic, married and retired. Caregivers among both groups were comparable in terms of the highest level of education attained and ADLs performed, spending more than 4 h per day caregiving. Approximately 75% of CGs in both groups had received some form of training in dementia care and over 90% had been providing care for 1 to ≥5 years. When using the EuroQol 5D-3 L to describe QOL for the two CG groups, at baseline both groups expressed having problems or some degree of difficulty in four of the five dimensions. Overall, the PAM group indicated a better health status upon enrollment. Interference with usual activities, pain, and anxiety was reported more frequently among CGs in the EUC group (30%, 65% and 35%, respectively) compared to the PAM group (12%, 43%, and 29%, respectively). Among this sample 22% of CG in both groups reportedly did not eat consistently (sit together at the table) with the PWD at mealtimes.

PWD weight, mid upper arm circumference, and caloric intake - nutrition

At baseline, mean PWD body weights were 173.0 ± 30.5 lbs among the PAM group and 181.9 ± 39.7 lbs among the EUC group, estimated mean daily caloric intakes were 1207 ± 91 kcals and 1575 ± 90 kcals, and MUACs were 28.8 ± 7.4 cm and 29.5 ± 7.1 cm, respectively. No discernable differences were observed between the groups for weight status in the six-months prior to study enrollment, with 40% of PWD in the PAM group having maintained their weight and 42% in the EUC group. However, prior mean weight loss was higher among the PAM group (11.9 ± 12.5 lbs) compared to the EUC group (8.8 ± 7.9 lbs). At the end of the study, no significant differences were observed in the unadjusted mean weight (p = 0.995) or percent change from baseline (p = 0.931) between the groups (Table 3). PWD mealtime feeding and dysfunctional behavior, and quality of life

No significant differences were observed in the unadjusted changes in mean scores at baseline and follow-up between groups for PWD mealtime feeding difficulty (EDFED), dysfunctional behavior (XL), or quality of life (QOL-AD) scores (Table 3). Differences in unadjusted mean change from baseline between the groups were also not statistically significant (EDFED: −0.8 ± 3.1, p = 0.411; XL: −1.4, 6.0, p = 0.605; and Qol-AD: 1.3 ± 6.6, p = 0.482). Similarly, no statistical differences over time were observed between the groups in the assessment of PWD feeding difficulties or dysfunctional mealtime behaviors when adjusted for caregiver outcomes. While not statistically significant, mean EDFED scores among PWDs in the PAM group (3.4 ± 0.8) were higher upon enrollment compared to the EUC group (2.7 ± 0.7), which might suggest more feeding problems (−1.2; 2.7 CI, p = 0.457), as was the presence of dysfunctional mealtime behaviors measured on the researcher-devised XL instrument (−1.2; 5.7 CI, p = 0.457) (Table 3). Over the 6-month study, baseline EDFED scores within both groups trended higher indicating an increase in problems and issues related to feeding and eating (Table 4a). However, mean XL dysfunctional mealtime behaviors scores among the PAM group decreased from baseline to end of study (5.9 ± 0.8–4.2 ± 0.6), whereas they increased among the EUC group (2.5 ± 0.7–3.1 ± 0.7).

Discussion

In our train-the-trainer study of a behavioral mealtime intervention PAM, delivered virtually via Respite Care Center volunteers, we sought to improve the nutritional outcomes and QOL for PWD and their CG. Over a 6-month period, PAM appeared to be associated with modest improvements in MUAC and caloric intake, fewer dysfunctional mealtime behaviors, and generally higher QOL health state scores for caregivers compared to usual care. These findings suggest that the intervention developed to enhance in-home caregiving may offer a viable approach to maintaining and improving the nutritional status of the PWD and overall well-being of both PWD and their CG. These findings are similar to those reported by others conducting studies on training programs for home-based care. For example, the “More at Home with Dementia” program which focused on caregiving training to improve the well-being of both PWD and CGs, delayed nursing home admissions and reduced psychological morbidity in CG [26]. Gresham et.al. reported that a residential training for caregivers in the “Going to Stay at Home” program significantly decreased CG unmet needs and behavioral symptoms in PWD [27], while another group reported reduced feelings of isolation with caregiver training programs [28].

The demographic differences between the groups revealed that PWD in the PAM group were on average older compared to the EUC control group, had a higher prevalence of mood disorders and greater functional impairment, as indicated by higher FAST scores. The CG in the PAM group were older, reported better health status, and experienced less interference with usual activities, pain, and anxiety compared to the EUC group. These differences could influence intervention outcomes, as older PWD with greater functional impairment and mood disorders may have more complex care needs, potentially affecting the efficacy of the PAM intervention. Additionally, the better health status and lower burden among PAM caregivers might enhance their ability to implement the intervention effectively, leading to improved outcomes for both PWD and CG.

The primary nutritional outcomes assessed were body weight, MUAC, and caloric intake. Although no significant differences were observed in the unadjusted mean weight or percent change from baseline between the PAM and EUC groups, the PAM group showed a trend towards improved MUAC and caloric intake. The PWD in the EUC group were enrolled with slightly higher MUAC, and given more nutritional supplements, yet they experienced a slight decrease in MUAC from baseline, compared to the PAM group who showed an increase, suggesting better maintenance of nutritional status. Regarding body weight, the PWDs in the PAM group started with greater weight loss prior to study enrollment, but when evaluated for more than 5% weight loss from baseline to end of study, it was slightly lower for PWDs in the PAM group versus the EUC group. This finding indicates a possible pattern of weight maintenance in the PAM group. This aligns with previous research indicating that targeted interventions can help mitigate weight loss and malnutrition in PWD [29]. A recent study of energy intake and expenditure in PWD including Alzheimer’s disease, and those with mild cognitive impairment, showed that while intake (calories) was similar between the two groups, resting energy expenditure (higher energy levels) of these memory-impaired populations was higher compared with individuals without dementia or MCI, which may account for weight loss and malnutrition rather than reduced energy intake (calories) [30].

Specifically, the PAM intervention aimed to address dysfunctional mealtime behaviors and improve QOL for PWD. Although no significant differences were found in the unadjusted changes in mealtime feeding difficulty (EdFED) or dysfunctional behavior (XL) scores, the PAM group exhibited a decrease in dysfunctional mealtime behaviors over the study period. This suggests that the intervention may have helped caregivers implement more effective mealtime strategies, reducing those for problematic behaviors. Additionally, while QOL-AD scores did not show significant differences, the trend towards improvement in the PAM group highlights the potential for behavioral interventions to enhance the overall well-being of PWD.

Caregiver outcomes, including depression, burden, and general health state, were also assessed. Although no significant differences were observed in the adjusted mean scores for depression and burden, the PAM group reported consistently higher QOL health state scores throughout the study. This suggests that the support provided through the PAM intervention may have positively impacted caregivers' perceived health and well-being. Previous studies have shown that caregiver support interventions can reduce burden and improve mental health, which is crucial for sustaining long-term caregiving efforts [31].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. The sample size was relatively small, and the study was conducted in a specific geographic region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The study was conducted and funded during the COVID-19 pandemic, thus we did not reach our targeted enrollment of 30 dyads per group chiefly due to persistent RCC operational challenges. The analysis did not account for the quality or quantity of nutritional supplements, even though it is clear that PWD in EUC received more nutritional supplements despite slightly higher decrease in MUAC from baseline. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported measures and the potential for variability in volunteer training and implementation fidelity may have influenced the outcomes. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in larger, more diverse populations and explore the long-term effects of the PAM intervention on both PWD and caregivers.

Conclusion

The findings of this study have important implications for the design and implementation of interventions aimed at improving nutritional outcomes and QOL for PWD and their caregivers. The train-the-trainer model used in the PAM intervention demonstrates the feasibility of leveraging RCC volunteers to deliver evidence-based strategies in a community setting. This approach extends the reach of the intervention and fosters a supportive network for CG, which is essential for addressing the multifaceted challenges of dementia care. We believe the PAM intervention shows promise in improving nutritional outcomes and reducing dysfunctional mealtime behaviors in PWD, as well as enhancing the QOL of CG. These findings underscore the importance of providing targeted support and resources to CG to improve the overall care and well-being of PWD. Continued research and refinement of such interventions are essential to address the growing needs of the dementia population and their CG.
