Assessing the integration of digitalization and AI in general medicine curricula in German-speaking Europe: a comprehensive survey
Authors: Anika Zahn, Martin Mücke, Julia Sellin, Gülay Ateş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of digitalisation, including artificial intelligence (AI), is becoming increasingly important in healthcare. It is transforming areas such as diagnostics, therapeutic planning and patient monitoring. However, research indicates that many medical students and physicians feel unprepared to use these technologies in their daily practice. This study provides insights into the opportunities and challenges of integrating digitalisation and AI into medical curricula with a focus on general medicine curricula in German-speaking European countries.ObjectiveThe objectives are to assess the perceived importance of digital and AI topics in medical education, evaluate the status of their integration into general medicine curricula and identify the challenges of their implementation.DesignA sequential mixed-methods approach was used. First, a qualitative analysis of documents from university websites was conducted. Subsequently, 59 general medicine departments in Austria, Germany and Switzerland were invited to participate in a quantitative anonymous online survey. Descriptive statistics were analysed using SPSS 30.0.ResultsThe survey was completed by half of Austrian universities and more than one-third of the German universities. None of the Swiss universities responded, resulting in a total response rate of 28.3%. The results reveal discrepancies between perceived importance and actual implementation, indicating a clear need for more courses on digitalisation and AI in medicine for medical students. Identified challenges with impact on limited implementation (especially of AI-related topics) were curriculum overload, a lack of qualified lecturers, lack of materials and a lack of resources.ConclusionsGeneral medicine is the largest segment of healthcare services in Austria and Germany. Respondents highlighted a strong demand for digitalisation and AI to be integrated into general medicine curricula, which is consistent with other studies and the current literature. Our results underscore the urgent need for the systematic inclusion of competencies regarding digital tools and AI to adequately prepare future physicians for real-world challenges.

Introduction

The European Health Data Space (EHDS) regulation was implemented in March 2025, marking a significant milestone in ongoing digitalisation efforts within European healthcare systems [1]. The regulation aims to standardise electronic health records (EHRs)/personal health records (PHRs) and improve their accessibility across member states, fostering enhanced patient empowerment and cross-border health data exchange. Currently, most European countries have either well-established national regulations or are actively developing digitalisation initiatives designed to improve healthcare quality and accessibility through digital technologies and artificial intelligence (AI) [2].

The integration of digital technologies, including AI, has grown significantly across various clinical and administrative healthcare domains, such as diagnostics, therapeutic planning and patient monitoring [3]. The rapid increase in FDA-cleared AI and machine learning (ML)-enabled medical devices highlights the United States' leading role in advancing clinical AI innovation, which is having an increasingly significant impact on medical education expectations worldwide. Within this landscape, radiology has been a major driver of this growth, accounting for 76% of FDA-approved medical AI applications between 1995 and 2023. The primary applications of AI in radiology include imaging analysis and clinical decision support. Cardiology follows with a substantially smaller percentage (10%) [4,5]. In the field of general medicine, AI-assisted tools with impact on resource management and streamlining of processes are of particular interest. These are expected to be adopted more widely in general medical practices, for example in the context of AI-assisted diagnosis tools or the automated or assisted writing of electronic physician letters during patient consultations [6–8].

A significant gamechanger in the widespread adoption of AI tools was the development of large language models and generative AI, which has led to a significant positive change in overall attitudes towards AI, including in the field of healthcare [9,10]. AI applications are now commonly used to facilitate learning in medical education [11] and to provide writing assistance to students and medical practitioners [12].

In response to evolving digital trends, the German Federal Ministry of Health published a revised draft of the regulation on medical education in 2023, mandating the inclusion of education on data usage and digital applications in medical curricula [13]. Similarly, in 2023, Austria's Federal Ministry of Labour, Social Affairs, Health, Care and Consumer Protection set up a commission to develop a national eHealth strategy [14]. In 2023, Switzerland's Federal Council endorsed the DigiSanté programme, which was initiated by the Federal Department of Home Affairs and is aimed at promoting the digital transformation of the healthcare sector [15].

Reflecting these recent transformations, an important question arises: How well are medical students and practicing physicians prepared to meet the challenges posed by a rapidly evolving digitalised healthcare environment? Indeed, both medical students and physicians report feeling inadequately prepared for the digitalised professional environment [11,16–18]. There is a strong interest and demand for educational formats that address practical topics regarding digitalisation and AI from theoretical, ethical-legal, and methodological perspectives [6,11,16,18,19]. Student surveys from several countries indicate that current medical education is not keeping pace with world-wide advances in technology [16,20–22]. These international studies highlight the global challenge of integrating rapidly evolving digital and AI technologies into curricula in a timely and systematic manner.

Notable uncertainties regarding the use of digital tools persist also among current medical practitioners [6,9,12], further highlighting the need to evaluate the current status of digital and AI training within medical education curricula. While existing studies predominantly focus on students' and general medicine practitioners' attitudes, experiences and acceptance of digital technologies [7,8,10–12,23–25], there is a lack of research examining the perspectives of leaders responsible for general medicine curricula on the importance and implementation of these technologies [26].

This study is the first to explicitly focus on the viewpoints of leading educational professionals in general medicine in German-speaking countries. It aims to explore the opportunities and challenges of integrating competencies regarding digitalisation and AI into general medicine curricula at universities in Austria (AT), Germany (DE), and Switzerland (CH). To contextualise these efforts, it is important to consider the educational structures within the region. In Germany and Austria, medical education follows similar, largely harmonised systems under central government supervision. In contrast, Switzerland employs a Bachelor-Master framework. Despite these differences, the systems are sufficiently similar to facilitate smooth exchange and mutual recognition of qualifications.

The objectives of the study are threefold: (1) to assess the perceived importance of digital and AI topics in general medicine curricula; (2) to evaluate the extent to which they are integrated into the curricula of different universities; and (3) to identify the challenges and required resources for their effective implementation.

Methods

The project used a sequential mixed methods design [27], beginning with a qualitative document analysis of national websites to assess the progress of digitalisation and AI implementation in general medicine curricula. This initial phase was followed by an anonymous quantitative online survey conducted at universities in AT, DE, and CH. The combined approach aimed to comprehensively understand the current state of integration of digital and AI-contents in general medicine curricula and to identify associated challenges.

The Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Rheinisch-Westfälische Technische Hochschule Aachen (RWTH Aachen) has reviewed and approved this study (EK 24-401). The data was collected anonymously, so a written confirmation is not required.

Population and field approach

A full census could be conducted as part of a qualitative document analysis and online survey due to the limited number of medical faculties in each country. A web-based search was performed to identify all universities with medical faculties in AT, DE and CH. The objective was to compile a comprehensive list of contact persons responsible for the local curriculum in general medicine. When such contacts could not be found via websites, one author contacted the department office by telephone to obtain the necessary information – a request was made for the names of individuals knowledgeable about the integration of digitalisation and AI within the general medicine curricula at the corresponding site.

All collected relevant individuals were then contacted via personalised e-mails between December 2024 and April 2025 to participate in an anonymous online survey. Each contact person was approached three times: initially with an invitation email, followed by two reminder emails. In some cases, more than one person or department office was contacted to ensure comprehensive coverage and facilitate communication (eight universities). Of these duplicates, five did not participate, but wanted to be included to receive information and reminders.

Survey invitations were sent to all public and private medical faculties in AT (n = 8), DE (n = 43), and CH (n = 8). Single exception is the German Hamburg Campus of the University of Neumarkt a.M., which is affiliated with the Romanian UMFST Targu Mures in Transylvania.

Questionnaire

The online survey consisted of 19 questions focusing on current teaching related to digitalisation and AI, covering their relevance, associated requirements, and challenges. To detect differences in how digitalisation and AI are implemented, a five-point response scale was used with the options: yes, no, do not know, in planning, and no need. Challenges or barriers were assessed using a separate five-point Likert scale ranging from none to major. Additionally, questions about respondents' socio-demographic characteristics and the country of the surveyed institution were included to provide national context. The respondents were also invited to provide further comments in a final open-text field. Some questions were mandatory and other optional to reduce respondent burden. The questionnaire was available in English and German. Three individuals piloted the online questionnaire before the invitations were sent out. They provided feedback on the language and technical aspects, such as grammar, readability, the order of questions and the functioning of filter questions.

Analyses

All the data were entered into SPSS 30.0 and analysed descriptively. Due to the small sample size, the data were analysed collectively, and no group comparisons were conducted. Where relevant, the variable values were summarised and presented as aggregated data, which were then discussed. Descriptive statistics were used to report the frequency and distribution of the variables. Graphs were created using Excel 16.0. Since no valid responses were received from Swiss participants, only the responses from Austria and Germany were analysed. The results and discussion sections focus on these valid responses, resulting in varying number of responses per question (e.g., n = 13 or n = 19).

Results

Document analysis: website research

Information and content related to digitalisation and AI were screened from the websites of university general medicine departments. A website investigation revealed that 2 out of 8 Austrian general medicine departments, 12 out of 43 German departments, and 1 out of 8 Swiss departments made references to digitalisation or AI on their websites. These references predominantly pertained to projects or scientific foci, with minimal to no insight provided into the curricula. In light of the paucity of information regarding the implementation status and curriculum integration of these topics online, it was deemed necessary to conduct an anonymous online survey. The aim of this survey was to assess the integration and relevance of these topics with greater rigour (Table 1). However, Table 1 and subsequent analyses focus on Austria and Germany due to a lack of available information on Swiss websites and unanswered survey responses.

In Austria, a comprehensive personal health record (PHR; in Austria: Elektronische Gesundheitsakte – ELGA) system is well established and broadly used. As of 2022, 94% of physician offices with social health insurance contracts, 86% of ambulatory practices, and 68% of hospitals were connected to ELGA [28]. Since 2025, radiology images and diagnoses have been included in a structured manner. However, uptake among private practice physicians without social health insurance contracts is lower due to self-funding requirements. Telemedicine, video consultations, and remote monitoring services are reimbursed by some health insurers. The piloting phase of digital health applications and reimbursement models ended in Austria with 2025. The intention is to make digital health applications (DiGAs) available on prescription (covered by health insurance) by 2027 [29].

In Germany, a PHR (elektronische Patientenakte – ePA) has been implemented since 2021, with automatic creation for all statutory insurance holders from 2025 unless they opt out. Despite this, usage remains voluntary for patients and healthcare professionals alike, with only 1%–2% of beneficiaries regularly using PHRs as of 2021. Telemedicine, video consultations, electronic prescriptions and electronic sick leave notices have been fully implemented for all statutory insured persons. Mobile health (mHealth) and digital health applications (DiGA) require a prescription [30], and reimbursement by health insurers depends on their status of assessment by the Ministry for Drug and Medical Product Safety (BfArM) [2].

Anonymous online survey

Response rate and respondents' background

Overall, 59 eligible universities were identified and contacted. Of the eight Austrian universities contacted, four responded (50%). In Germany, 43 institutions were invited to complete the survey, and 15 replied (34.9%). No responses were received from Switzerland (Figure 1). Overall, the response rate across all countries was 28.3%. Given the lack of responses from Switzerland, the descriptive analyses focus exclusively on the 19 responses from Austria and Germany.

Survey response flow by general medicine institution per country (n = 59 institutions).

Of the 19 participants, 8 reported being female, 5 male, and 6 did not specify. Two of the participants were heads of departments, 4 were teaching coordinators, and 7 were involved in teaching. The year in which the 11 respondents (n = 19) began teaching general medicine ranged from 1999 to 2021.

Courses offered on digitalisation and perceived importance

A question was asked to distinguish the perceived importance of teaching related to digitalisation between medical studies overall and general medicine specifically. Of the 19 respondents, 4 rated its importance as (rather) unimportant, 5 as moderately important and 9 as (rather) important. The responses regarding the importance of implementing digitalisation-related educational content in medical studies and in the field of general medicine were very similar (Figure 2).

Electronic personal health record, e-sick leave, e-prescriptions

The next step involved capturing the thematic focuses of digitalisation topics offered in general medicine teaching. Content regarding nationally implemented electronic versions of sick leaves (eSickleave), rescriptions (ePrescription), and medical reports (eDoctor's letter) are taught at 12 institutions (63.2%) and are planned at 2 (10.5%). Content regarding electronic diagnostic tools are taught at 11 universities (57.9%) and are planned at 2 (10.5%). The topic of PHR is currently taught at 6 of the 19 general medicine departments and is in the planning stage at 6 (31.6%). Even so, 7 respondents stated not teaching about PHR, 6 have no courses on diagnostic decision support systems and 4 do not teach about eSickleave, ePrescriptions, or eDoctors' letter (Table 2).

Digital systems, telemedicine, mHealth, DiGAs

Content regarding digital systems for administration and management, such as online appointment scheduling systems and automatic documentation assistants, have been integrated into the general medicine curriculum in 9 cases (47.4%), and 2 cases (10.5) are planned. Six respondents stated that no courses on this topic are offered. Telemedicine and video consultations are taught in 12 departments of general medicine (63.2%) and are in the planning phase in 2 departments (10.5%). Mobile health (mHealth) and wearables are covered in student teaching at six institutions (31.6%), are planned at three (15.8%), and are not included in any general medicine courses at 8 departments. Digital health applications (in German: DiGAs) are currently taught at 13 institutions (68.4%) and are in the planning phase at 1 general medicine department (5.3%). One respondent expressed no need for these topics except for DiGAs (Table 2).

Digital collaboration, big data, data protection and ethics

Topics regarding digital interprofessional collaboration is taught at 8 departments (42.1%), with 1 (5.3%) in the planning phase and 6 offering no courses. Content regarding big data is currently taught in 2 general medicine departments (10.5%) and is also planned in 2 (10.5%) more. This leaves 11 general medicine departments (57.9%) with no teaching on this topic. Data protection is included in the curriculum at 7 institutions (36.8%) and is planned at 3 institutions (15.8%). Ethical aspects of digitalisation are taught at 3 institutions (15.8%) and are planned at 2 (10.5%). Of these topics, data protection was the only one for which no perceived need was indicated by one respondent (5.3%) (Table 2).

Required courses on digitalisation in general medicine

Despite some digitalisation courses not yet being offered, over 80% of the 17 participants considered four out of seven digitalisation topics to be essential for teaching in general medicine. These topics were PHR, ePrescriptions, diagnostic decision support systems, digital health applications and the legal and ethical aspects of digital application use. A further 13 participants (76.5%) highlighted remote patient monitoring and telemedicine as necessary topics. The importance of teaching digital systems for administration and management was also identified by 11 participants (64.7%) (Figure 3).

Importance of and courses offered on AI

All 19 respondents answered the question regarding the importance of teaching AI in general medicine in their department. The respondents rated the importance of AI in general medical teaching as slightly less relevant compared to its importance in medical studies (Figure 4).

Importance of AI in medical studies and general medicine (n = 19/19). Source: AM-DI-KI project, online survey 2024–2025, n = 19, own data.

Seven respondents (36.8%) stated that AI was (rather) unimportant in general medicine teaching (6; 31.6% for medical studies), 5 (26.3%) rated it as moderately important (3; 15.8% for medical studies), and 6 (31.6%) as (rather) important (8; 42.1% for medical studies) (Figure 4).

Participants were also asked whether AI-related topics were already included in their general medicine curriculum. Notably, none of the institutions indicated that AI topics were unnecessary. Nevertheless, at the time of the survey, 9 (74.4%) out of 19 general medicine departments were not teaching any AI topics; of these, 2 (10.5%) were planning to integrate AI content in the future. Of the remaining 10 (52.6%) institutions, 3 (15.8%) were teaching at least one AI topic, and 4 departments of general medicine covered up to five topics (mean = 3, SD = 2). The most frequently taught topics were ‘using AI applications’ (7 mentions), followed by ‘AI systems for diagnostic decision support (DDSS)’ and ‘potential bias’ (6 mentions each); details in Figure 5.

Required courses on AI in general medicine

Nevertheless, the following question remains: do they see a requirement for teaching AI topics in general medicine? The vast majority (12 out of 16, or 75%) stated a need for such courses, covering application areas of AI in medicine and critical assessment of AI systems. Knowledge of the ethical aspects of using AI in medicine and practical skills in using AI tools were also stated as requirements for general medicine.

More than half of the respondents (9 out of 16; 56.3%) agreed that the basics of AI and ML should be included in the general medicine curriculum, while 5 (31.3%) disagreed. Only 4 respondents (25.0%) considered basic AI or ML programming skills to be relevant, while 6 (37.5%) regarded them as irrelevant, and 6 (37.6%) were uncertain. These results highlight the differing opinions on the necessity of the basics of AI and ML and programming basics within general medicine curricula (Figure 6).

Framework within curriculum

Regarding the educational framework for digitalisation courses, 4 of the 19 respondents (21.1%) supported exclusive integration within the compulsory curriculum, 1 respondent (5.3%) favoured offering courses outside the compulsory curriculum as elective courses, and 12 respondents (63.2%) preferred integration into both formats. Two participants (10.5%) opposed the inclusion of any digitalisation content within general medicine curricula.

Regarding AI-specific courses, 2 out of 18 participants (11.1%) supported inclusion solely within the compulsory curriculum, another 2 (11.1%) favoured inclusion exclusively outside of the compulsory curriculum (elective courses), 6 (33.3%) preferred integration in both formats, and an equal number opposed the inclusion of AI course content entirely. Two participants (11.1%) were uncertain (Table 3).

Nine of the 13 respondents (69.2%) viewed the overloading of the compulsory curriculum as a major challenge, and 8 (66.6%) reported time constraints within the curriculum to be a factor. Six respondents each considered limited resources and a lack of compatibility witch existing IT systems to be major challenges. Four respondents (30.7%) considered the latter two to be moderate challenges, and 3 respondents (23.0%) considered them to be minor or not a challenge at all (Figure 7).

Figure 8 illustrates the perceived challenges of integrating AI and digitalisation into curricula, summarising the number of respondents who rated 4 (‘somewhat of a challenge’) or 5 (‘a major challenge’). Hence, 10 respondents (79.9%) identified significant challenges in incorporating AI into general medicine curricula, primarily due to a shortage of qualified lecturers and a lack of adequate teaching materials. Six respondents (46.1%) reported similar issues in digitalisation. Therefore, lecturers' and students' levels of interest do not appear to constitute a barrier to implementing digitalisation or AI courses (Figure 8).

Discussion

National implementations and progress in digital health systems make it necessary to integrate and train future physicians in areas of digitalisation and AI. While the use of electronic personal health records (PHR) has only recently been introduced in Germany (elektronische Patientenakte; ePA) [31], ePrescription and eSickleave are well established and widely used. Austria relies widely on the national PHR called the ELGA, and both countries have introduced several telemedicine and digital health modalities covered by health insurance. European-wide assessments, such as the Digital Health Index and the eGovernment Benchmark, rank Austria and Germany above the EU-27 average in terms of digital health readiness and data utilisation [32]. Developments in the digitalisation of the health sector across Europe create the framework for incorporating digital and AI competencies into general medicine education [33]. This area of medicine is of special importance, as general medicine is a key entry point into healthcare systems for patients, and confronts both practitioners as well as patients with new regulations and opportunities regarding digital healthcare on a daily basis. The education of future practitioners needs to be aligned with national developments and regulations in the area of digital health, which necessitates the integration of a wide range of digitalisation and AI topics.

To capture the current situation in German-speaking countries (Austria, Germany and Switzerland), general medicine departments were invited to participate in an online survey about integrating digitalisation and AI into general medicine curricula. However, no responses were received from Swiss departments. Consequently, the focus of this study is on the status, perceived importance and challenges in general medicine curricula at universities in Austria and Germany. While the response rates from general medicine departments in Austria (50.0%) and Germany (34.9%) were satisfactory, it is important not to overinterpret or generalise these results.

The findings align with previous research showing strong interest among students and faculty in including digitalisation and AI topics [6,10,11,16,18,19]. However, implementation remains insufficient. There is a notable discrepancy between the perceived relevance of digital and AI topics, and their actual presence in curricula, particularly regarding ethics and legal issues, diagnostic tools, mobile health technologies, and digital health applications [16,18,26]. Despite the fact that several universities have initiated efforts to renew their curricula, particularly in the context of digitalisation and AI, there is a clear and present need to address issues in general medicine, which has been identified as a key area for improvement in the routine practice [34]. Comprehensive regulations regarding curriculum content are typically needed to facilitate efficient implementation [18,35].

Concerning AI-related topics, teaching frequency often does not match perceived relevance. While 47.4% of respondents identified the basics of AI as important, only 15.8% reported that its concepts and terminology are included in teaching content, which is in line with other studies [20–22]. Notably, 63.2% of respondents considered AI ethics highly relevant, yet only 15.8% reported its inclusion in curricula, with limited plans for future integration. Further research is required to explore whether respondents consider these subjects adequately addressed by other departments (e.g., medical ethics and medical informatics), leading them to perceive no need for incorporating these topics into general medicine curricula. However, digitalisation and AI should be taught with a clear focus on their use, opportunities, and risks within the context of general medicine work practices. This approach fosters robust understanding and thoughtful usage of digital application, supporting the development of competent professionals and advancing future innovations [8].

Key challenges reported include curriculum overload, time constraints, and lack of teaching materials and qualified lecturers. As highlighted by Yousefi et al., personnel training is critical for successful AI-based tool implementation [23]. The absence of adequate AI training can pose risks, such as physicians potentially trusting AI outputs without recognising issues like ‘AI hallucinations’. Ryan et al. also highlight this issue, finding that physicians tend to follow AI recommendations, including those that may be incorrect due to ‘AI hallucinations’. The inability of medical professionals to recognise these hallucinations, particularly in complex scenarios, further worsens this issue [36]. Blease et al. emphasise that many physicians lack sufficient understanding of the legal and ethical implications of AI use. Effective education is essential to mitigate these risks, supported by the respondents' expressed need for AI training [12]. Gaps in training might lead to errors in documentation, data security issues, and adverse impacts on patient outcomes. Effective education can mitigate these risks, and the survey revealed a clear need for AI-related teaching content.

Despite these obstacles, there are opportunities. AI-generated responses, such as those from LLM models, have been found to be more empathetic and readable by patients, all while maintaining accuracy [25]. The use of EHR/PHR, mHealth and AI tools can help caregivers improve patient care, highlighting the importance of medical students becoming proficient in digitalisation and AI, particularly those training to be general practitioners due to their future responsibility as patients' central point of contact to the health care system. Future physicians must understand the benefits and limitations of AI tools to integrate them sustainably, which is achievable through comprehensive education. [3,23,37]. The implementation of education topics regarding digitalisation in general and AI tools in particular faces challenges, including societal acceptance and constraints on the curriculum, as identified by Yousefi et al. [23] or Longoni et al. [38] Furthermore, Zheng et al. found that AI education that is to focused on theory and ethics can lead to a lack of student engagement in spite of improved competencies. This calls for practical teaching on AI applications and limitations [39]. The literature identifies the observed negative trend as being caused by one-sided teaching that focuses primarily on theoretical and ethical aspects. The systematic integration of practical, case- or problem-oriented teaching approaches dealing with digitalisation and AI-specific topics into general medicine curricula should be considered and requires proactive assessment of developments and future labour market requirements. Future research on these trends should be translated into collaboratively developed learning objectives and the establishment of sustainable curricular structures within institutions.

This study is among the first to systematically examine the factors required to develop comprehensive and effective digitalisation and artificial intelligence (AI) educational strategies within general medicine curricula. While many studies address digital competencies more broadly [9,10,16,17], few focus specifically on general medicine curricula or primary care physician training. [8,12,23,25,33]. By positioning general medicine departments as knowledge brokers, this research identifies priority topics for educators and contributes to preparing healthcare professionals for a future healthcare landscape increasingly shaped by the routine use and growing importance of digital and AI-based technologies.

Conclusions

Although the initial aim of this study was to capture perspectives from Austria, Germany and Switzerland, this was constrained by the limited response rate, with no responses from Switzerland. Nevertheless, the findings offer valuable insights into the development of general medicine curricula, emphasising the importance of key competencies and the contextual constraints that influence their implementation. Building on these insights, it becomes evident that the pace at which digitalisation and AI are being integrated into general medical curricula in Germany and Austria remains slow, likely as a result of substantial resource constraints faced by both countries. Universities and their departments of general medicine have a responsibility to actively shape the future workforce by systematically integrating the implications of digitalisation and AI in health care into curricula. This should include the specific impact on their specialist field, including related professional skills, knowledge and the reflective use of technologies. This is particularly important given that these technologies are already embedded in routine clinical practice, and major regulatory frameworks such as the AI Act and the European Health Data Space (EHDS) further structure their development and use in healthcare. Without educational efforts, the medical profession risks falling behind current and future technological and societal developments.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the online survey data consists of responses from Austria and Germany, with none from Switzerland. Although telephone interviews were considered as an alternative to improve response rates, this approach was dismissed as redesigning the online questionnaire for telephone interviews would have required more time and resources. Redesigning a suitable telephone questionnaire would also have caused issues with comparability. However, as the remaining two countries share structural similarities, the interpretation of our findings is more straightforward. Secondly, despite the relatively satisfactory response rate to the survey, the findings may be limited in their generalisability due to self-selecting bias, as participation was voluntary and likely driven by interest in the topic. Therefore, these results may overrepresent those interested in digitalisation and AI use, and should be interpreted with that in mind. Future research should be expanded to include other European countries and beyond, to explore the factors that influence the development of general medicine curricula through a more global lens. Thirdly, the absence of any responses from Swiss universities might hint at barriers regarding language (with Switzerland being a multilingual country with Swiss German spoken by only about 2/3 of the population) or engagement, despite the survey being available in both English and German. While these factors may affect the representativeness of the results, the study still offers valuable insights into the future development and integration of digitalisation and AI into general medicine curricula. These limitations highlight the need for broader, more diverse studies, but they do not diminish the importance of the present findings. Overall, our approach provides a robust basis for guiding the development of future curricula covering the rapidly evolving fields of health care digitalisation and AI in medicine.
