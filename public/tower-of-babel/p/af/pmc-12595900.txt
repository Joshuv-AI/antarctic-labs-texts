Conductive heat loss in simulated outdoor settings: a preliminary experimental human study
Authors: Tomasz Sanak, Mateusz Putowski, Stanisław Krzeptowski-Sabała, Janusz Pobędza, Paweł Walczak, Konrad Mendrala, Tomasz Darocha, Sylweriusz Kosiński
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about the nature and magnitude of conductive heat loss in outdoor environments. A two-phase rescue scenario was employed in which a person immobilized on a spineboard was placed directly on the ground (Phase 1) and then lifted upwards under windy conditions (Phase 2). The driving force for conductive heat loss was determined on the basis of the temperature difference between the skin and the spineboard. Background

Accidental hypothermia is the result of heat loss exceeding its endogenous production. Heat can be lost by conduction, convection, and radiation and through evaporation. The importance of individual heat loss routes depends on environmental conditions and the circumstances of the event. The priorities for out-of-hospital treatment of a hypothermic patient who is not in cardiac arrest are to avoid causing cardiovascular collapse during rescue, prevent further decreases in core temperature and rewarm the patient in a safe manner [1–4].

Early and aggressive steps should be taken to prevent additional heat loss in wilderness and combat settings. To minimize conductive heat loss, the casualty should not be placed directly on the ground or floor of the evacuation helicopter, and insulating material should be placed between the casualty and any cold surface as soon as possible [1, 5, 6]. However, it is not known, what materials will function as intended in extremely cold conditions [6]. The material of the stretcher, plastic and synthetic polyester blankets, which adapt almost instantly to the ambient temperature, appear to be an important factor in cooling the patient [7]. Medical personnel may not be aware that a cool spineboard or mattress does not provide adequate insulation from the cold ground.

Little is known about the nature and magnitude of conductive heat loss towards the ground in outdoor settings [7–9]. Recognized scientific societies, such as the Wilderness Medical Society, base their recommendations for preventing conductive heat loss on classic thermographic studies of humans immersed in water dating back to the last century [1, 10, 11]. Thermal manikins offer a credible alternative to human studies for assessing heat transfer from the human body to the environment [12, 13]. However, there may be limitations to the use of manikin thermal modelling. Although manikins provide repeatable and consistent data, eliminating human variability and ethical issues, they may not perfectly replicate all aspects of human physiological responses, such as thermoregulatory vasoconstriction. It remains unclear whether manikin thermal modelling provides data on thermoregulatory characteristics similar to those of physiological studies in humans in the same environment, particularly in outdoor settings [12, 14].

The aim of this preliminary study was to assess total heat loss in human volunteers under simulated outdoor conditions, with a focus on heat flux toward the ground, which primarily represents thermal conduction. The secondary objective is to assess the feasibility and safety of human trials using the established protocol and to provide a basis for further larger-scale studies.

Methods

Study design and participants

An experimental study was conducted on healthy adult volunteers in a climatic chamber using an outdoor rescue scenario. The study comprised one preliminary session to evaluate the attachment and operation of the sensors and seven experimental sessions to collect data: one session for one experimental participant. The volunteers were under constant supervision by medical staff experienced in diagnosing and treating hypothermia. Each volunteer had the right to stop the test without giving a reason. Throughout the entire study period, thermal comfort on the CDS (Cold Discomfort Scale) was examined.

Seven healthy volunteers were enrolled in the study: 3 women and 4 men. After the initial examination, each of the volunteers was weighed. The volunteers were dressed in the same way: a short-sleeved cotton T-shirt, a 100 g/m2 fleece jacket, trekking trousers, a multifunctional scarf rolled up in the form of a headband, socks, and shoes. The structure and number of layers of clothing and insulating cover on the front and back of the torso were identical.

Data collection was performed at the Thermoclimatic Research Laboratory of Krakow University of Technology. The study was carried out in accordance with the requirements established in the Act on the Medical Profession, with written informed consent from all the subjects. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Jagiellonian University Medical College 1072.6120.55.2018 and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental protocol

On the day of the study, the volunteers were randomly placed on spineboard (FERNO, 184 × 39 × 2.5 cm; Laerdal Medical AS, Stavanger, Norway). The initial spineboard temperature was stabilized at 12 +/–1 °C. Two layers of thermal insulation (a 3 mm thick fleece blanket and ultrathin metallized foil) were applied on the volunteers from the neck to the feet and thereafter tightly folded under and between the body and the spineboard. The calculated insulation value of the insulating set in the windless condition was 5.5 clo [15]. The subjects were attached to the spineboard with three straps. After checking the proper functioning of the sensors, the volunteers were placed on the spineboard inside the climatic chamber (Fig. 1). The air temperature was previously set at -10 °C. In Phase 1, the subjects were placed directly on the concrete floor for 20 min. In Phase 2 of the experiment, the subjects were lifted on a transport platform (60 cm above the ground, 2 points of support) for the next 20 min. For the first 20 min, the air was still, and for the next 20 min, a fan was turned on from the subject’s side at approximately 8 m from the subject, creating air movement at a speed of approximately 8 m/s. After completing the second phase, the objects were moved to room temperature and released.

The test person was positioned on the spineboard of the thermoclimatic chamber

Data collection

The contact area between the back of the body and the spineboard was determined by an individual print. The study participants were placed on a cool surface for approximately 3 min, after which the surface was scanned in infrared mode. The thermal trace that remained on the cold surface was recorded and processed electronically. Areas of heat transfer were identified on the basis of maximum infrared radiation and surrounded by a marker that allowed for accurate calculation of the conductive loss area (Fig. 2).

Areas of heat transfer identified by infrared radiation and surrounded by a marker

For each heat transfer pathway, the amount of heat is dependent on the driving force, the body surface area involved and the resistance to heat flow (e.g., clothing insulation). Heat exchange by conduction can be described as follows: Q ˙ = hK × ΔT × A where Q ˙ indicates heat transfer (W); hK indicates the heat exchange coefficient by conduction (W × m−2 × °C−1); ΔT indicates the temperature gradient between the surface and the skin (°C); and A indicates the contact area (m2) [14]. The heat exchange coefficient corresponds to the slope of the least squares regression of the heat flux as a function of the temperature gradient between the spineboard and the skin. Heat flux sensors are calibrated for measuring heat flux (heat flow per unit area) and respond to all three types of heat transfer: conduction, convection and radiation. Evaporative heat loss in nonsweating adults represents only a small fraction of heat production [16]. Consequently, cutaneous thermal flux well represents total heat loss under the circumstances of this experiment. The heat flux through the body surface adjacent to spineboard was considered conductive. The heat flux from the anterior chest wall was the sum of heat exchange by radiation convection and conduction.

The heat flux sensors gSKIN XM 26 9 C and gSKIN XP 26 9 C (GreenTEG AG Switzerland) were used (avg. sensitivity 4.0–20.0µV; temperature dependence 0.25%/°C; sensitivity calibration accuracy ± 3%). A functionality test of sensors was performed before installation, according to manufacturer’s recommendations. The skin of the participants was cleaned with alcohol wipes and shaved where necessary. Heat flux sensors were attached via a single layer of tape (Transpore surgical tape, 3 M, United States) directly to the skin: the middle of the sternum (C1), right scapula (C2), and left buttock (C3). Thermistor temperature sensors (NTC SR PASSIVES NTCM-HP-10 K) were attached next to the heat flux sensors (T1, T2, and T3, respectively). One temperature sensor was attached on the front part of the spineboard, approximately 20 cm away from the patient’s head.

Data analysis

The time points were extracted from continuously recorded data at one-minute intervals to eliminate artifacts. Recordings that had unrealistic fluctuations and artifacts, defined as values that were 3 standard deviations from the surrounding values, were removed and replaced by the closest artifact-free value. All the variables are presented as the means ± SDs. To analyse the relationship between heat flux (W/m²) and temperature (°C), a linear regression model with the corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) was used. Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 29.0, for MacOS.

The mean temperature difference between the buttocks and scapula was 5.2 ± 0.9 °C. The mean temperature difference (back skin-spineboard) was 23,6 ± 2,9 °C and increased over time. A significant drop in both the skin temperature and the spineboard temperature was recorded in Phase 2 (Fig. 3).

Temperature recordings

The average heat loss through the skin‒spineboard contact area was 467 ± 97 W/m2 (Figs. 4 and 5a, b and c). In Phase 1 (spineboard placed directly on the ground, windless), the heat flux averaged 385 ± 34 W/m2. In phase 2 (spine board raised, wind blowing), a significant increase in heat flux was noted, to an average of 560 ± 45 W/m². Multiplying the heat flux per area times the contact area resulted in an average heat loss of 159 watts (131 watts in Phase 1 and 190 watts in Phase 2).

The skin temperature of the sternum remained relatively constant, averaging 32.8 ± 0.4 °C. The mean heat flux through the anterior body area (not in contact with the spineboard) was 351 ± 71 W/m2. In Phase 1, the heat flux averaged 294 ± 34 W/m2, and 415 ± 41 W/m2 in Phase 2. Multiplying of the heat flux per area times the anterior body area resulted in an average heat loss of 569 watts.

There was a linear relationship between the spineboard temperature and the average temperature on the scapula and buttock (R² = 0.897, p < 0.001). A 1 °C decrease in the spineboard temperature resulted in a 0.59 °C (95% CI 0.52–0.65 °C) decrease in the back skin temperature (Fig. 6). In both phases, a strong linear relationship was found between back skin temperature and heat flux. In Phase 1, the relationship was positive (β=+31 W/m² per 1 °C decrease in skin temperature; R²=0.986; p < 0.001), whereas in Phase 2, it was negative (β=−20 W/m² per 1 °C decrease; R²=0.946; p < 0.001). Discussion

The average heat flux through the skin-to-spineboard interface, which is primarily a conductive heat loss, is one-fourth greater than the heat flux through the skin of anterior body. Heat loss through this route accounts for approximately one-fifth of the total heat loss and depends on the temperature gradient between the skin and the substrate and the body surface in contact with the substrate.

In an experimental study conducted under stable operating room conditions, at a water mattress temperature of 25 °C, the heat loss per area from the back to the mattress was 120 W/m2 [9]. This means that a relatively small driving force (a temperature difference of approximately 10 degrees Celsius) generated significant, conductive heat loss. There was a linear relationship between the heat flux and the temperature gradient between the water mattress and the skin. The multiplication of the heat flux per area times the contact area resulted in a heat transfer of 47.5 W towards the substrate [9]. In our experiment, the driving force was more than twice as large, and the mean heat loss was approximately 3 times greater, reaching 159 W. A thermal power deficit of 159 W means that 159 Joules of thermal energy are lost each second. If the specific heat of human tissue is approximately 3 kJ · kg− 1 · °C− 1 [17], then the heat loss required for a temperature decrease of 1 °C in a 70 kg human is 210 kJ. Dividing the heat loss by the thermal power deficit lasts approximately 22 min, during which the core temperature decreases by 1 °C, which is solely due to conductive heat loss to the substrate. In our experiment, a rapid decrease in skin temperature adjacent to the spineboard was observed, indicating a local vasoconstrictive reaction. This defensive reaction of lowering the skin temperature could not be effective, however, because the spineboard temperature decreased faster, increasing the temperature gradient, which was the main driver of the heat loss.

Surprisingly, the study showed greater heat loss after lifting the spineboard to a height approximately equivalent to carrying and creating conditions simulating wind. Air movement significantly increased heat loss from the back of the body, with a slight increase in heat loss from the anterior body. This may be attributed to the compression of insulation materials under the body weight, resulting in a deterioration of their insulating properties [18–20]. Therefore, lifting and carrying a patient immobilized on a spineboard under windy conditions may result in greater heat loss than leaving the patient on the ground. In a study by Lundgren et al., additional insulation on a spineboard by means of an insulation mat significantly reduced the need for shivering in a cold environment. It was concluded that spineboards, as well as other materials used for prehospital transportation of patients in cold environments, should be well insulated to reduce conductive heat loss [8]. The results of our study are consistent with these observations. Further research is needed to identify which equipment and supplies is optimized for use in cold environments and to ensure that interventions implemented in extreme conditions will not fail [6].

Limitation

The research method used allowed for data collection under simulated conditions but was close to real-world conditions. Cold adaptation mechanisms, such as drop in skin temperature and intense shivering observed in all volunteers, compromised the quality of the collected data but also confirmed the feasibility of the research protocol. We made every effort to ensure the accuracy of the collected data, but their analysis may be subject to error due to the small sample size and artifacts that required filtering and processing of the raw data. Reliable data can only be obtained from a properly designed study with the number of participants that can be estimated from this preliminary study. Our measurements are based on healthy subjects. In patients with impaired thermoregulatory responses and impaired heat production, heat flux values ​​may differ. The results of this preliminary study cannot be used as a basis for making clinical decisions, but they may be a basis for further studies on a larger scale or for designing mathematical models in manikin studies. The experiment was completed in 2020, but the pandemic prevented further research.
