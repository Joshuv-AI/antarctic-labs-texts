A 1-km Dataset of Water Consumption and Irrigation for Major Grain Crops in the Yellow River Basin Based on the Crop Water Production Function
Authors: Zheng Wang, Changxiu Cheng, Kaixuan Dai, Zanmei Wei, Bin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate estimation of crop water consumption and irrigation in the Yellow River Basin (YRB) is vital for water management and food security. Existing forward-logic methods (e.g., crop hydrological modeling method) fail to fully simulate key processes, leading to simulation biases. In contrast, the reverse-logic remote sensing inversion method covers full processes but is plagued by mixed-pixel effects. These limitations lead to a lack of spatially detailed and accurate datasets. Leveraging expanded field experiment data and multi-source high-spatial-resolution yield data, this study achieved the extension of reverse-logic Crop Water Production Functions (CWPFs) from field to basin scale. Integrating CWPFs with the soil water balance principle, we developed the 1-km resolution dataset of water consumption and irrigation for three major grain crops (wheat, maize, soybean) in the YRB (2000–2020). Validations at both the field and prefecture levels confirmed the CWPFs’ reliability and the dataset’s accuracy. Superior in spatial detail and accuracy, this dataset provides a robust basis for optimizing water resource allocation and safeguarding the water-food synergy in the YRB.

Background & Summary

The Yellow River Basin (YRB) is a vital grain-production region in China. Its grain output constitutes approximately 35.33% of the national total, according to the “Report on Ecological Protection and High-Quality Development of the YRB (2023)”1. However, water scarcity presents a significant challenge to agricultural production in the YRB2. Most of the basin is located in arid and semi-arid areas, leading to extreme pressure on agricultural water use. Therefore, accurately estimation of the water consumption and irrigation for major grain crops in the YRB carries significant implications. It not only provides a critical foundation for developing water resource allocation optimization policies, but also contributes to ensuring sustainable water resource utilization and safeguarding food security.

Considerable progress has been made in estimating crop water consumption, with five primary methods commonly employed. Among these, four methods (excluding the field experiments method3–5, which cannot generate spatial datasets, as shown in Table 1) adopt forward- or reverse-logic during estimation.

Forward-logic refers to predicting crop water consumption based on causative factors. Methods following this logic tend to overlook key variables, such as management practices, or rely on assumptions that are not universally applicable, consequently failing to fully simulate key processes. Examples include the crop hydrological modeling method, machine learning method, and crop coefficient method. The crop hydrological modeling method estimates water requirements by simulating the water cycle and its interaction with soil moisture during crop growth. It offers a well-defined mechanistic basis that allows adjustment to local conditions, enabling relatively accurate estimation of crop water consumption. However, these models not only rely on numerous empirical assumptions due to their forward-logic, but are also computationally intensive and challenging to parameterize, making them more suitable for coarse-resolution simulations at large scales (Table 1). For instance, Chiarelli et al.6 and Mialyk et al.7 applied different crop hydrological models to develop global crop water requirement datasets at a 5-arcmin resolution. The machine learning method establishes statistical relationships between site‐based crop water consumption and influencing variables (such as climate and soil variables) to extrapolate spatial patterns of water consumption. This method enables the efficient generation of high-spatial-resolution data, as evidenced by a random forest model developed by Wang and Shi8 that estimated crop water consumption and irrigation across China at a 1-km resolution. However, this method not only adopts forward-logic, making it difficult to account for critical factors like management practices, but also places high demands on data quality and computational resources9–11 (Table 1). The crop coefficient method12–14, which adjusts reference evapotranspiration (ET0) using crop coefficients (Kc)15,16, is easy to calculate and apply at large scales. Nevertheless, this method also adopts forward-logic, heavily relies on empirical parameters and limited variables, thereby compromises accuracy (Table 1). Conversely, the remote sensing inversion method17–19 employs a reverse-logic estimation, estimating crop water consumption from observed signals. This method reflects actual crop water conditions, which addresses the limitations of forward logic in capturing key variables. However, challenges such as mixed-pixel effects still persist, impeding accurate retrieval of crop-specific evapotranspiration over large scales19,20. Consequently, a spatially detailed and accurate dataset of crop water consumption and irrigation in the YRB is still lacking.

Crop water production functions (CWPFs) serve as crucial tools at the field-scale for describing the relationship between crop yield and water consumption. In recent years, they have found widespread application in crop yield simulation and field management optimization21–23. Numerous studies indicate that a significant quantitative relationship typically exists between crop water consumption and crop yield21–23.

However, CWPFs have not been applied in the estimation of crop water consumption in previous studies. The primary reason lies in the need to address two critical scientific challenges, which prevent the scaling of CWPFs from field-scale to basin-scale. First, field experiment data has been limited over the past decades. However, recently years, both the volume of field experiment data and the number of experiment sites have been increasing annually. The expansion in experiment data volume enhances the stability and reliability of CWPFs, while the increase in experiment sites enables the development of CWPFs that reflect regional variations. Second, this method requires high-spatial-resolution crop yield datasets encompassing multiple crops, spanning long historical periods, and possessing authenticity and reliability. Currently, several studies have developed crop yield datasets using remoting sensing, machine learning, and mechanistic modeling techniques. Instances include GDHY (global, 0.5° resolution, 1981–2016, 4 crops)24, GGCP10 (global, 10-km resolution, 2010–2020, 4 crops)25, and ChinaCYWP (China, 1-km resolution, 2001–2015, wheat and maize only)26. The Spatial Production Allocation Model (SPAM) is a mechanistic model that employs a top-down strategy using cross-entropy methods to reasonably estimate the distribution of 42 crops within resolution cells27,28. The SPAM model effectively simulates the growth and spatial distribution of multiple crops over long time scales and is now open-source, meeting the needs of this study for multiple crops, long time scales, and high spatial resolution in the YRB. A previous study generated 1-km resolution crop harvest area maps from 1990 to 2020 based on the SPAM model29. Therefore, this study resampled the 10-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset (2000–2020, five-year intervals) from MapSPAM to 1-km resolution, then spatially matched it with 1-km resolution crop harvest area maps29 to generate a 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset.

Consequently, establishing CWPFs applicable to different sub-regions in the YRB based on extensive field experiment data, then deriving crop water consumption by integrating high-spatial-resolution historical yield datasets, constitutes a proven method for estimating crop water consumption. This method: (1) ensures ease of calculation, (2) employs a reverse-logic estimation method, deriving parameters from observed results to covers full processes, thereby better reflecting real-world conditions, (3) enables rapid, large scale, high-spatial-resolution simulations, and (4) generates crop water consumption datasets that capture spatial details in the YRB.

Additionally, existing methods for crop irrigation estimation can be broadly categorized into two types. The first type relies on the principle of soil water balance and is currently the most widely applied method for estimating crop irrigation. This method is suitable for both calculating total regional irrigation and generating high-spatial-resolution irrigation datasets7,8,30. Typically, it estimates crop water consumption first, then derives irrigation based on water balance principles. The second method involves surveys and quota extrapolation to estimate total irrigation use for a given region31. Primarily used for regional total irrigation estimates, it struggles to generate high-spatial-resolution irrigation datasets. Therefore, this study adopts the water balance-based method to estimate crop irrigation.

This study focuses on generating a dataset of water consumption and irrigation for major grain crops (wheat, maize, and soybean) at a 1-km resolution in the YRB from 2000 to 2020 at five-year intervals based on CWPFs. The study involves the following steps: (1) Establishing optimal CWPFs for different sub-regions and crops in the YRB, based on extensive field experiment data. (2) Integrating multi-source datasets to generate a 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset. Then, calibrate it with prefecture-level city yield statistics. (3) Estimating water consumption for wheat, maize, and soybean at 1-km resolution using the optimal CWPFs and the 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset. Subsequently, estimating irrigation at 1-km resolution for these crops based on soil water balance principles. (4) Validation and comparison. This study aims to provide theoretical support for formulating water resource allocation optimization policies in the YRB and to offer a scientific basis for ensuring sustainable water resource utilization and safeguarding food security.

Methods

Unless otherwise specified, all references to crop water consumption in this manuscript refer to crop water consumption per unit area (mm), all references to irrigation in this manuscript refer to irrigation per unit area (mm), and all references to yield in this manuscript refer to yield per unit area (kg/hm2).

Study area

The study area covers 66 prefecture-level cities (Table S1) in the YRB (Fig. 1). The YRB is one of the major grain-producing regions in China, characterized by its complex and variable topography, climate, and soil conditions. Water scarcity is severe in this basin, with per capita water availability at only 27% of the national average. Moreover, the efficiency of water resource utilization in this basin remains low, with agricultural water efficiency in urgent need of improvement. Furthermore, the rates of water resource development and utilization have reached as high as 80%, far exceeding the 40% ecological warning threshold typically observed in most river basin2.

66 prefecture-level cities within the Yellow River Basin (YRB).

Data resources

Field experiment data

The literature review method was applied to collect over 1200 pieces of field experiment data from the YRB and surrounding areas since 2000 (including 534 wheat experiments, 623 maize experiments, and 69 soybean experiments) (Table S2). Each piece of data contained crop planting and harvesting dates and locations, detailed field management practices (such as irrigation and fertilization), along with yield and water consumption.

Yield-related data

The 10-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset was obtained from yield and spatial distribution data for the years 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2020 provided by MapSPAM32–35 (https://www.mapspam.info/data/). Data for 2015 was missing and interpolated using the 2010 and 2020 data. The 1-km resolution crop harvest area maps were obtained from Science Data Bank29,36 (10.57760/sciencedb.18098), covering the time span from 1990 to 2020 at five years intervals. This study used data from the period 2000 to 2020. Additionally, statistical yield data for prefecture-level cities were obtained from national, provincial, and municipal statistical yearbooks (https://www.stats.gov.cn), covering the period from 1998 to 2022. These statistics were averaged to represent yields around the years 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 (e.g., “around 2005” refers to the average of 2003 to 2007).

Precipitation data

Precipitation data was used to estimate irrigation. This study obtained the precipitation data from the ChinaMet dataset37–40, which provides daily precipitation at a 1-km resolution for China from 1980 to 2024. The dataset is provided by National Cryosphere Desert Data Center (http://www.ncdc.ac.cn). Precipitation data for the study area were extracted for the period from 1998 to 2022.

Framework

The dataset generation process is illustrated in Fig. 2 and consists of four parts. (1) Field experiment data for wheat, maize, and soybean in the YRB was collected and filtered using the literature review method. Subsequently, CWPFs were established based on field experiment data. Five commonly used CWPFs were tested for their applicability, ultimately establishing optimal CWPFs for different sub-regions and crops in the YRB. (2) The 10-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset and 1-km resolution crop harvest area maps were integrated. After calibration using prefecture-level city yield statistics, the final 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset was generated. (3) Based on the optimal CWPFs and 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset, water consumption for wheat, maize, and soybean in the YRB was estimated at a 1 km resolution for the period 2000 to 2020 at five-year intervals. Subsequently, irrigation for these crops was estimated based on soil water balance principles. (4) Crop water consumption and irrigation for wheat, maize, and soybean were validated using observed and statistic data, with comparisons made against existing datasets.

Determination of the optimal crop water production function (CWPFs)

Determination of the optimal CWPFs requires extensive field experiment data. This study used a literature review method to collect and filter the field experiment data.

The data collection strategy involved searching for articles published from 2000 to 2023 on the Web of Science and China National Knowledge Infrastructure platforms using the following keywords: (1) “The YRB/The Loess Plateau/Hetao Plain/Qinghai/Gansu/Sichuan/Ningxia/Inner Mongolia/Shaanxi/Shanxi/Henan/Shandong” (2) “yield” (3) “water use efficiency/water consumption” (4) “wheat/maize/soybean”. The logical relationship between (1), (2), (3), and (4) is “AND”, while the logical relationship within (1), (2), (3), and (4) is “OR”. For instance, search for keywords containing “The YRB” and “yield” and “water use efficiency” and “wheat”.

The filtering strategy is as follows: (i) Exclude duplicate articles and those without field experiment. (ii) Exclude articles with unclear descriptions of experimental method. (iii) Exclude articles with significantly anomalous yield or water consumption. (iv) Exclude articles related to intercropping or relay cropping. (v) Exclude studies whose objectives hinder the establishment of CWPFs (e.g., those claiming increased yields with reduced water consumption due to specific study aims). (vi) For studies examining the impact of tillage practices on yield, retain only one locally common practice unless additional data for that sub-region is unavailable. This is because tillage practice variations (e.g., mulching, ridge-furrow systems, especially plastic mulching) can introduce confounding effects that obscure the relationship between yield and water consumption41–43.

After collection and filtration, this study ultimately obtained over 1200 pieces of field experiment data from the YRB and its surrounding areas. These included 534 pieces of data from 52 wheat experiments, 623 pieces of data from 50 maize experiments, and 69 pieces of data from 5 soybean experiments (Table S2). Each piece of data contained crop planting and harvesting dates and locations, detailed field management practices (such as irrigation and fertilization), along with yield and water consumption.

About 85% of the field experiment data was used to determine the optimal CWPFs, while the remaining 15% was used to validate both the optimal CWPFs and crop water consumption dataset. It is essential to note that in order to simultaneously validate both the optimal CWPFs and crop water consumption dataset, we must ensure that 15% of the field experiment data aligns with actual local field management practices and can be considered actual results. This is because the crop water consumption dataset is designed to estimate actual water consumption.

In addition, to ensure the establishment of optimal CWPFs, wheat in the YRB was divided into 12 production sub-regions, maize into 12 sub-regions, and soybean into 3 sub-regions. The division method involved first classifying all experimental sites into groups (Fig. 1) based on location and then delineating Thiessen polygons around group centers. Prefecture-level cities that span multiple Thiessen polygons were assigned to the Thiessen polygon that has the closest yield value to the yield of these cities, based on the yield corresponding to the center of the Thiessen polygon. This resulted in sub-regions fragmented by prefecture boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. S1.

Subsequently, five widely used CWPFs44–46 (Eqs. 1–5, respectively representing CWPF1 to CWPF5) were applied to fit the field experiment data to determine the optimal CWPFs. Additionally, this study provided 90% confidence intervals for the CWPFs.

Integration and calibration of the crop yield distribution dataset

Developing the 1-km resolution yield distribution dataset in our study involved two core stages: integration and calibration. Integration aimed to spatially align 10-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset with 1-km resolution crop harvest area maps. Calibration ensured the accuracy of integrated 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset. These two stages allow us to achieve high-resolution spatial detail while maintaining numerical accuracy.

During integration, this study first resampled a 10-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset to 1-km using the bilinear interpolation method, then spatially matched it with 1-km resolution crop harvest area maps. These maps, covering the years 1990 to 2020 at five-year intervals, were generated using the SPAM model based on the cross-entropy method29. The publicly available 10-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset, covering the period from 2000 to 2020 at five-year intervals, was sourced from MapSPAM.

During calibration, the 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset was statistically calibrated to ensure that its average yield aligns with that at the prefecture-level city scale (Eqs. (6, 7)).

Overall, the spatial distribution of yields shows little difference, indicating the reliability of both the 10-km resolution yield dataset and the integration and calibration process. However, subtle variations are observed in localized regions. For instance, the maize yield in Wuwei city, Gansu Province, recorded in the statistical yearbook for 2000 was 9763 kg/hm2. The 10-km resolution yield distribution dataset from MapSPAM may not fully account for the impact of such policy factors. Therefore, we conclude that the yields after integration and calibration are more reasonable overall.

The 1-km resolution crop water consumption dataset was generated based on the optimal CWPFs and the 1-km resolution crop yield distribution dataset. Taking the year 2020 as an example, the spatial distribution of crop water consumption is shown in Fig. 4(a,c,e). Additionally, Mann-Kendall test and Sen’s slope method was applied to analyze temporal trends in crop water consumption (Fig. 4(b,d,f)).

Spatial distribution of water consumption for different crops in 2020 and its trend from 2000 to 2020.

Spatially, both wheat and maize exhibit higher water consumption in the northwest and east of the YRB, such as central Gansu, Ningxia, Inner Mongolia, Henan, and Shandong Province. Conversely, water consumption is lower in the central part of the basin, particularly in eastern Gansu, north Shaanxi, and Shanxi Province. For soybean, water consumption is higher in the southern YRB (summer soybean production regions) and lower in the northern regions (spring soybean production regions). Temporally, water consumption for wheat, maize, and soybean in the YRB shows either an increasing trend or no significant trend from 2000 to 2020, with almost no regions exhibiting a decrease. A single exception is observed for maize in Linxia City, Gansu Province, where water consumption declined at a rate of 4.6 mm/year. These trends are similar to that described by Lei et al.56 and Liu et al.57. Additionally, the increase in water consumption for wheat is more pronounced than that for maize and soybean.

Data Records

The crop water consumption and irrigation dataset is available at Science Databank70. The spatial scope of the dataset covers 66 prefecture-level cities in the YRB (Table S1). The dataset has a spatial resolution of 1-km and covers the period from 2000 to 2020 at five-year intervals (for instance, 2005 denotes the average from 2003 to 2007). Specifically, the crop water consumption dataset includes crop water consumption per unit area (mm) (referred to directly as “crop water consumption” in this paper) and total crop water consumption (m³); irrigation dataset includes irrigation per unit area (mm) (referred to directly as “irrigation” in this paper) and total irrigation (m3). Unlike single values, all data in this dataset additionally provide upper and lower bounds of the 90% confidence interval to account for uncertainty.

Data storage format is “AAAA_TTTT_XXXX_YYYY_MMM.tif”. In this format, “AAAA” represents the data category, “wate” for water consumption and “irri” for irrigation. “TTTT” denotes whether the value reflects per-unit-area or total quantity, with “unit” for per-unit-area quantity and “tota” for total quantity. “XXXX” represents crop type, with “whea” for wheat, “maiz” for maize, and “soyb” for soybean. “YYYY” indicates the year. “MMM” denotes the upper and lower bounds of the 90% confidence interval for the dataset, with “max” for the upper bound and “min” for the lower bound.

Technical Validation

Simulation results of different CWPFs

The simulation results for five CWPFs across different sub-regions are shown in Fig. S2. Approximately 85% of the field experiment data were used in the simulations (see Methods-Determination of optimal CWPFs). In each scatterplot of Fig. S2, each point represents yield-water consumption data from a field experiment, with dashed lines of different colors indicating different CWPFs. The maps on the right side of Fig. S2 display the geographic locations of the sub-regions corresponding to each scatterplot on the left. The simulation performance of the five CWPFs for all crops and sub-regions, as demonstrated by R2 and RMSE (kg/hm2), is shown in Fig. S3. The larger R2 is, the smaller RMSE becomes (i.e., the closer it is to the lower-right corner of Fig. S3), indicating better simulation performance.

A comprehensive analysis of the Fig. S2 and Fig. S3 reveals that, overall, CWPF1 and CWPF3 exhibit higher R2 values, lower RMSE, and better simulation performance. However, while CWPF3 outperforms CWPF1 in some sub-regions, its unique parabolic structure often leads to an underestimation of yields when crop water consumption is high. Consequently, CWPF1 is primarily used as the optimal CWPF for simulations in this study, with CWPF3 employed as a compensatory measure in sub-regions where results are significantly poorer.

Performance of the optimal CWPFs

The optimal CWPFs and their performance for different crops across different sub-regions are shown in Fig. S4, which includes the upper and lower bounds of the 90% confidence interval. Overall, maize exhibits the best simulation results, followed by wheat, and finally soybean. The performance of the optimal CWPFs in the western study area are generally comparable to that in the central and eastern sub-regions, with the exception of wheat sub-region 1, where the simulation performance was slightly lower.

The remaining 15% of field experiment data was used to validate the optimal CWPFs, with the result shown in Fig. 8 (see Methods-Determination of optimal CWPFs). The red components (red scatter points, red solid line, and red text) represent the validation results for the optimal CWPFs. The R², RMSE, and nRMSE results indicate that the validation performance of the optimal CWPFs is “good”71.

Validation of the optimal CWPFs and crop water consumption dataset.

Furthermore, to investigate the temporal and spatial generalization performance of optimal CWPFs, we conducted two additional validation tests. For the temporal generalization validation, we used field experiment data from 2011 and later for training, and data from 2010 and earlier for validation. The results are shown in Fig. S5. The red points and solid line in Fig. S5 represent the validation results for temporal generalization. The R², RMSE, and nRMSE results indicate that the validation results for temporal generalization are “good”71. For spatial generalization validation, we randomly selected one experiment site from each sub-region, used the remaining field experiment data for training, and used the data from the selected experiment site for validation. The blue points and solid line in Fig. S5 represent the validation results for spatial generalization. The R2, RMSE, and nRMSE results indicate that the validation results for spatial generalization are “excellent”71. In summary, we believe that the optimal CWPFs possess good temporal and spatial generalization capabilities.

Comparison of crop water consumption with existing datasets

The crop water consumption dataset from this study was compared with datasets generated through two commonly used methods: the first dataset generated through the crop coefficient method and the second dataset generated through crop hydrological modeling method. Currently, there is a lack of publicly available datasets for crop water consumption generated through the crop coefficient method. Therefore, the first dataset of crop water consumption was calculated based on Kc and ET015. Specifically, for the YRB, the Kc values and crop growing periods used in this study are shown in Table S3. Daily ET0 dataset were obtained from Hobbins et al.72, covering the period from 1980 to 2020, with global spatial coverage at a resolution of 0.5° × 0.625°. This study extracted data for the YRB from the period 2013 to 2018, average them to represent results around 2015. The second dataset, obtained from Mialyk et al.7, was estimated using the ACEA model and provides averaged crop water consumption for the period from 2010 to 2019, with global spatial coverage at a resolution of 5-arcmin. Data for the YRB were extracted for this study. The crop water consumption dataset generated in 2015 from this study was compared with these two datasets, and the comparison results are presented in Fig. 9.

Comparison of water consumption dataset from this study with datasets generated through two commonly used methods.

Overall, the spatial distribution patterns of the three crop water consumption datasets are largely consistent. Both this study and the ACEA model estimated similar levels of crop water consumption; however, both estimates were lower than those obtained using the crop coefficient method.

The crop water consumption dataset from this study is superior to the other two datasets in three primary aspects. First, dataset from this study has a higher spatial resolution of 1-km. In contrast, the crop coefficient method dataset has a spatial resolution of 0.5° × 0.625°, and the dataset generated by the ACEA model has a resolution of 5-arcmin. Second, the dataset from this study has been more thoroughly localized and better reflects spatial details. For instance, the water consumption dataset generated through the crop coefficient method fails to account for complex local conditions due to insufficient influencing variables and inadequate localization, making it difficult to capture spatial details (local variability). Third, the dataset from this study used a reverse-logic estimation during the estimation process, ensuring a comprehensive and realistic consideration of influencing variables. In contrast, the other two datasets employed a forward-logic estimation. The advantages of reverse-logic estimation can be observed from three perspectives. (1) The dataset from this study better matches reality by using actual variable values rather than assumptions. For instance, the crop coefficient method significantly overestimates crop water consumption (Fig. 9b,h,n). This likely results from assumptions of adequate water supply when estimating ET0, despite much of the YRB being arid or semi-arid with frequent water shortages. (2) The dataset from this study provides more accurate simulations of crop water consumption in certain irrigation districts in the western YRB. For instance, the ACEA model likely underestimates wheat water consumption in the Qingyuan Irrigation District of Wuwei, Gansu province (Fig. 9d,e,f). This may result from incomplete consideration of variables (or unreasonable assumptions) within the model. (3) The dataset from this study is not directly influenced by the length of growing period. In contrast, results generated through crop coefficient method are significantly affected by growing period length, leading to markedly underestimated water consumption for summer maize (Fig. 9k).

In addition, this study estimated crop water consumption based on real, dynamic single-crop distribution maps29 rather than traditional static, non-crop-specific farmland distribution maps. Consequently, the water consumption dataset for wheat, maize, and soybean from this study dynamically adjust in real time according to their actual planting distributions. This enables the crop water consumption datasets to more accurately reflect real-world variations in water consumption, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of water resource management and agricultural decision-making.

Accuracy assessment of irrigation with prefecture-level city statistics

The irrigation dataset from this study was compared with statistic irrigation data to validate the estimation accuracy. The validation results are shown in Fig. 10. The statistic irrigation data was derived from irrigation for irrigated land in prefecture-level cities, obtained from provincial water resource bulletins, with averages calculated for the period from 1998 to 2022. Generally, water resource bulletins report agricultural water consumption, with agricultural irrigation accounting for approximately 90% of this consumption. Irrigation for irrigated land in prefecture-level cities constituting about 90% of agricultural irrigation. This ratio was extrapolated based on more detailed irrigation data published by the water resource bulletins of Gansu Province. However, the irrigation for irrigated land still includes other crops such as potatoes and highland barley. Accordingly, we assumed that wheat, maize, and soybean together account for 80% of the total irrigation for irrigated land. This is also consistent with the previous study, in which wheat and maize together account for approximately 65% of total agricultural irrigation in the YRB73. Since multiplying the 90% and 80% ratios yields approximately 70%, and this study includes soybean in the irrigation estimates. Additionally, the estimation accuracy of four existing irrigation datasets was compared simultaneously6–8,20, with results presented in Fig. 10.

Estimation accuracy of irrigation from this study and four existing irrigation datasets.

The R2 for the irrigation dataset from this study is 0.7727, with an RMSE of 0.1197 km3 yr−1, indicating a high level of accuracy in irrigation estimation. Compared to the four existing irrigation datasets, the R2, RMSE, and slope of irrigation dataset from this study exhibit slight superiority.

Additionally, to compare the absolute irrigation estimated by different studies, we calculated the total irrigation at both the provincial and study-area scales (Fig. S6). The results show that the irrigation estimated by our method are closest to the statistical irrigation, followed by the estimation of Wang and Shi8. Our estimates show relatively small deviations from the statistical irrigation across provinces, with slight overestimates in some eastern provinces (Shanxi, Henan, and Shandong) and slight underestimates in some western provinces (Ningxia and Inner Mongolia). This pattern may be related to the cropping structure in the western region, where a larger share of other crops may result in a lower proportion of irrigation water allocated to wheat, maize, and soybean. However, we simply applied a uniform coefficient (approximately 70%, 90% × 80%) to the statistical agricultural irrigation for all regions in our calculation. In contrast, the irrigation of Zhang et al.20 substantially overestimates irrigation in the eastern provinces.

Comparison of irrigation with existing datasets

The irrigation dataset from this study was compared with irrigation datasets generated by Wang and Shi8 and Chiarelli et al.6, respectively. The comparison results are shown in Fig. 11. This comparison was made because the datasets generated by Wang and Shi and Chiarelli et al. focus on irrigation for individual crops rather than providing total irrigation for all crops. Specifically, Wang and Shi8 generated the irrigation dataset through the machine learning method (the random forest algorithm), while Chiarelli et al.6 used the crop hydrological modeling method (the WATNEEDS model).

Comparison of irrigation dataset from this study with two existing irrigation datasets.

Overall, among the three irrigation datasets, maize irrigation exhibits the most similar spatial distribution, with higher values in the northwest and lower values in the southeast across the YRB. In contrast, the irrigation patterns for wheat and soybean show more noticeable differences.

The irrigation dataset from Wang and Shi8 and Chiarelli et al.6 struggle to capture spatial details. Although dataset from Wang and Shi achieved a higher spatial resolution of 1-km, it lacked spatial local variability. The dataset from Chiarelli et al. has an even lower spatial resolution of only 10-km. Furthermore, the irrigation from Chiarelli et al. is relatively lower compared to the other two datasets, suggesting that the crop hydrological model estimates might be conservative. This could be attributed to the complex climate variations in arid and semi-arid regions, inadequate calibration, or overestimation of water use efficiency.

In addition, similar to the crop water consumption dataset, the irrigation dataset for wheat, maize, and soybean from this study also dynamically adjusts in real time according to their actual planting distributions. Consequently, the irrigation dataset from this study can more accurately reflect actual irrigation practices, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of water resource management and agricultural decision-making.

Uncertainty, limitations and prospects

Unlike single values, all data in this dataset provide upper and lower bounds of the 90% confidence interval to reflect uncertainty. The consideration of uncertainty is crucial in the field of agricultural water resource simulation and assessment74–76. This enables decision-makers to evaluate risks in water resource management and account for potential variability when formulating policies, thereby enhancing the effectiveness and reliability of decision-making. However, most other datasets fail to address this aspect.

Additionally, this dataset inevitably introduced some uncertainty. Firstly, during the integration of developing the 1-km resolution yield distribution dataset, the 10-km resolution yield distribution dataset (from MapSPAM) in 2015 was interpolated from the dataset in 2010 and 2020. This is because, MapSPAM only provides yield distribution dataset for 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2020, resulting in missing data for 2015. Although interpolation was unavoidable, we acknowledge that the linear interpolation method implicitly assumes yield changes during this period are linear and relatively smooth. In reality, however, yield changes over time may not be strictly linear. Furthermore, this interpolation was performed on every 10-km grid, assuming uniform rate of change across each large grid, whereas yield change rates may actually vary across different regions. Therefore, we supplemented our analysis with an experiment to illustrate how the 2015 interpolation affects accuracy. We interpolated yield from 2000 and 2010 to generate projections of 2005. After performing the same data integration and calibration procedures, we compared these projections with actual dataset in 2005. The nRMSE of wheat, maize, and soybean are 0.0985, 0.1111, and 0.0924, respectively. This demonstrates the robustness of the method at the spatial and temporal scales examined in our study. Secondly, during the calibration of developing the 1-km resolution yield distribution dataset, this study introduced calibration coefficients using prefecture-level statistical yields to calibrate the integrated 1-km resolution yield distribution dataset. However, the calibration coefficients do introduce uncertainty. One source is the inherent uncertainty in official statistical data, as these coefficients partially rely on agricultural census data. Agricultural censuses typically involve extensive data and detail, and time constraints in data collection, organization, and analysis may introduce errors into census results. On the other hand, the calibration coefficient implicitly assumes spatial homogeneity-that deviations within a prefecture-level city are spatially uniform. If deviations vary across different regions within a prefecture-level city, this uniformity assumption cannot correct for spatial heterogeneity within the city. Nevertheless, despite absolute yield values carry uncertainty, the core strength of the yield dataset lies in its reliable spatial patterns and long-term trends. This strength is ultimately conveyed to the crop water consumption/irrigation dataset in this study through the transmission of the CWPFs and soil water balance principle. Thirdly, we applied a soil water balance equation to estimate irrigation. Given the multi-year average framework of this study, we assumed that the change in soil moisture and surface runoff in the cropland of YRB is zero, and that drainage accounts for 15% of irrigation. While these simplifying assumptions are reasonable for a large-scale, long-term average study, they represent a necessary compromise. They were adopted because data and time constraints preclude more detailed, process-based modeling at each grid cell. It is important to acknowledge that these assumptions are a source of uncertainty in our irrigation estimation. Fourthly, the coefficients used to approximate the irrigation share of wheat, maize, and soybean (90% for irrigated land in agricultural irrigation, and 80% for the three crops in irrigated land irrigation) are estimates based on regional data and cropping structure. These coefficients may vary spatially, introducing uncertainty into the validation. However, the correlation (R² = 0.77) between our estimates and the scaled statistical data suggests that the influence of this uncertainty on our irrigation estimation is likely limited.

This study also has several limitations. Firstly, this study employed CWPFs for the entire growing period based on the fact that current field experiment data cannot yet reliably support the regional application of CWPFs that distinguish different growing periods. Nevertheless, the objective in this study is to capture total water consumption and irrigation requirements across the entire growing period, along with their spatiotemporal patterns. Therefore, we believe that this simplification reasonable and feasible under current conditions. However, in future study, we will attempt to use CWPFs that distinguish different growing periods21,77,78 to obtain the water consumption and irrigation for each growing period. Secondly, the five-year temporal resolution limits the analysis of inter annual variability and climate extremes. This constraint primarily stems from the input data used in this study (dataset from MapSPAM and crop harvest area maps), which inherently have a five-year resolution. While interannual fluctuations cannot be analyzed, the five-yearly data from 2000 to 2020 sufficiently reveal long-term trends and periodic changes in crop water consumption over the past two decades-a core focus of this study. If annual yield dataset that meeting our study requirements become available in the future, this study framework could be directly adapted to generate products with higher temporal resolution. Thirdly, although articles that published between 2000 and 2023 were focused on when collecting field experiment data, data prior to 2010 constitutes a very small proportion. Therefore, dataset in this study may not represent earlier technologies, leading to potential inaccuracies for earlier years. To partly address this uncertainty, we provided 90% confidence intervals for each grid in the crop water consumption and irrigation dataset. Users should therefore interpret the results for earlier periods cautiously, particularly in conjunction with the provided confidence intervals. Fourthly, the dataset currently includes only three crops: wheat, maize, and soybean. These three crops represent the most widely cultivated and water-intensive major crops in the YRB, with the most abundant field experiment data available. Therefore, they adequately represent the bulk of regional agricultural water consumption. For other crops such as cotton and rice, although cultivated within the basin, their distribution is limited and experiment data are relatively scarce. In future study, we will attempt to integrate remote sensing observations and additional literature data to gradually incorporate more crops into the dataset. Fifthly, the sub-region division method in this study, being a method based on experimental site locations and yield, may indeed introduce artificial boundaries that do not perfectly align with actual agro-ecological conditions or irrigation management regions. This is a compromise method adopted because the available field experiment data and regional data for the YRB are insufficient to support a more objective regionalization. Data users should be reminded to interpret results falling on the margins of the sub-regions with greater caution.

Usage Notes

This study generated a dataset of water consumption and irrigation for major grain crops (wheat, maize, and soybean) at a 1-km resolution in the YRB from 2000 to 2020 at five-year intervals based on CWPFs. Through validation and comparison, this dataset demonstrates good performance, and outperforms existing datasets in terms of spatial resolution, spatial detail, and accuracy. This dataset serves as a crucial resource for study and policymaking focused on optimizing water resource allocation. For instance, acting as an input for water resource allocation models, it can facilitate equitable distribution across different regions and users. Governments and managers can leverage this dataset to evaluate the effectiveness and adaptability of current water management policies and irrigation practices, thus providing a scientific basis for policy adjustments and optimizations. Additionally, this dataset can provide essential resources for ensuring sustainable water resources utilization. For example, by analyzing agricultural water resource utilization, this dataset enables the assessment of agricultural activities’ impacts on ecosystems, ensuring that sustainable water use preserves biodiversity and ecological balance. It also supplies data for governments to develop rational water resource management policies, promoting equitable distribution among agricultural, industrial, and residential users. Furthermore, this dataset is vital for safeguarding food security. For instance, it can provide data for the management and optimization of agricultural irrigation, ensuring adequate water supply during critical growth periods to optimize crop yield. As an important input, it also fosters the development of sustainable agricultural practices, such as the implementation of precision irrigation techniques, thereby enhancing resource use efficiency in food production.

In future study, a key challenge will be to incorporate additional influencing factors, such as soil moisture and soil salinity21,79, to establish more scientific and stable relationships of crop yield and water consumption. Moreover, despite the increasing volume of field experiment data, challenges remain in accessing high-quality and sufficient data. Future efforts could involve employing supplementary tools, such as remote sensing imagery and crop hydrological models, to address this gap. Through these potential improvements, future study and practices are expected to be more scientific, precise, and sustainable. This will facilitate the advancement of sustainable agriculture and efficient water resource management while also protecting the ecological environment and promoting socio-economic development.
