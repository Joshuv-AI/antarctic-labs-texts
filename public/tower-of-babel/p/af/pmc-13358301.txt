AI-enhanced electrocardiography as a digital biomarker platform in cardiovascular medicine: clinical applications, validation gaps and future implementation pathways
Authors: Damiano Venturiello, Walter Vignaroli, Giuseppe Nasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence applied to the ECG is expanding the clinical role of this widely available diagnostic tool beyond conventional waveform interpretation by enabling identification of electrophysiological patterns associated with cardiovascular structure, function and risk. Within this evolving framework, the artificial intelligence enhanced ECG is emerging as a scalable digital biomarker platform supporting screening, diagnosis and prognostic stratification across the cardiovascular disease continuum.This narrative review synthesises current evidence on the clinical applications of artificial intelligence enhanced ECG, including detection of cardiac rhythm disorders, early identification of structural heart disease, decision support in acute coronary syndromes, prediction of clinical outcomes and population level cardiovascular screening using wearable technologies. Deep learning models applied to standard and simplified electrocardiographic recordings demonstrate strong diagnostic performance in identifying atrial fibrillation, left ventricular dysfunction, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, cardiac amyloidosis and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. These approaches also enable estimation of long-term cardiovascular risk from apparently normal tracings and support identification of disease patterns extending beyond overt cardiac conditions.Despite these advances, clinical implementation remains limited by the need for prospective multicentre validation, improved model interpretability, standardised evaluation strategies and integration into routine clinical workflows. This review highlights current validation gaps and outlines key opportunities for translation of artificial intelligence-enhanced ECG into digital cardiology practice.

Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has assumed an increasingly important role in cardiovascular medicine owing to its ability to analyse large volumes of clinical data and biological signals and to identify complex patterns that cannot be recognised using traditional interpretation methods.1 In cardiology, this evolution has been facilitated by the progressive digitalisation of diagnostic testing and the availability of large-scale datasets enabling the development of predictive models for diagnosis and cardiovascular risk stratification.1

Among the various sources of cardiovascular data, the ECG represents one of the most suitable tools for the application of machine learning algorithms because it is non-invasive, low-cost and universally available. Traditionally interpreted through visual assessment of morphological features such as intervals, amplitudes and ST-segment deviations, the ECG can now be analysed directly at the signal level using deep learning models, enabling identification of clinically relevant information not accessible through conventional interpretation.2 3 Convolutional neural networks (CNN) have demonstrated high performance in ECG analysis, supporting automated arrhythmia classification and detection of subclinical structural cardiac conditions including left ventricular dysfunction, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and cardiac amyloidosis.4 5

Beyond diagnostic applications, AI applied to the ECG has shown increasing potential for prognostic stratification by identifying individuals at increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF), heart failure (HF) and cardiovascular mortality even in the presence of apparently normal tracings.6 These findings support the interpretation of the ECG as a digital biomarker of cardiovascular health status rather than solely as a tool for detection of overt electrical abnormalities.

The concept of AI-ECG as a digital biomarker platform extends beyond traditional diagnostic classification tasks. Unlike conventional electrocardiographic interpretation, which focuses primarily on identification of rhythm disturbances or overt electrical abnormalities, Al-based analysis appears capable of extracting latent signal representations associated with cardiac structure, myocardial function, biological ageing and future cardiovascular risk. In this framework, the ECG functions not merely as a diagnostic tracing but as a scalable source of physiological information reflecting multiple dimensions of cardiovascular health. Establishing AI-ECG as a true biomarker platform will require demonstration that these algorithm-derived signatures provide clinically meaningful information beyond conventional ECG interpretation, remain reproducible across diverse populations and acquisition settings and improve patient-centred outcomes when incorporated into clinical decision-making.

More recently, multimodal AI models integrating electrocardiographic signals with cardiovascular imaging, circulating biomarkers, wearable-device recordings and longitudinal electronic health record data have opened new perspectives for predictive and personalised cardiovascular medicine.7

Materials and methods

This structured narrative review aimed to provide a thematic overview of current evidence regarding clinical applications of AI-enhanced electrocardiography and the principal challenges affecting its translation into routine cardiovascular practice.

A targeted literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science. Search terms included combinations of ‘artificial intelligence’, ‘machine learning’, ‘deep learning’, ‘electrocardiography’, ‘arrhythmia’, ‘heart failure’, ‘cardiomyopathy’, ‘acute coronary syndrome’, ‘screening’ and ‘risk prediction'.

Publications published between 2017 and 2026 were considered. Additional relevant studies were identified through manual review of reference lists from key articles and recent review papers.

Studies were selected based on their relevance to major clinical applications of AI-ECG, including arrhythmia detection, structural heart disease identification, acute coronary syndrome assessment, prognostic stratification, wearable-device applications and multimodal cardiovascular integration. Priority was given to landmark studies, large validation cohorts, implementation studies, randomised trials when available and influential contemporary reviews.

The selected literature was synthesised using a thematic narrative approach focused on clinical applicability, validation challenges and future implementation pathways.

Fundamentals of AI applied to the ECG

AI applied to the ECG is primarily based on machine learning techniques that enable identification of relationships between digital signal patterns and clinical conditions using large repositories of annotated electrocardiographic recordings linked to clinical outcomes.4 8

Among available methodological approaches, deep learning currently represents the most widely used framework for AI-ECG analysis because it enables direct processing of raw electrocardiographic signals without requiring manual feature selection, allowing extraction of complex representations beyond conventional interval-based and morphology-based interpretation.3

CNN are among the most adopted architectures for automated ECG analysis and support both diagnostic classification and risk prediction through hierarchical recognition of signal patterns distributed across the tracing.9 More recently, self-supervised learning approaches trained on large unlabelled ECG datasets have enabled development of generalisable signal representations adaptable to multiple downstream diagnostic tasks, improving scalability across heterogeneous clinical environments.10

Across multiple clinical scenarios, deep learning-based ECG models have demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to or exceeding conventional interpretation strategies, supporting their emerging role as scalable decision-support tools in cardiovascular medicine.4 In parallel, multimodal approaches integrating ECG data with other clinical information are also being investigated to improve cardiovascular risk assessment.7

Applications of AI-ECG in arrhythmia diagnosis

Automated identification of arrhythmias represents one of the earliest and most mature clinical applications of AI applied to the ECG and the first domain in which deep learning algorithms demonstrated clinically relevant diagnostic performance.11 By enabling recognition of complex morphological and temporal signal variations across the entire tracing, AI-ECG improves diagnostic standardisation and reduces reliance on purely visual interpretation, particularly in high-volume settings or where expert interpretation is limited.3

Among arrhythmias, AF represents the principal application domain of AI-ECG (table 1). Deep learning algorithms applied to sinus-rhythm ECGs can identify electrophysiological signatures associated with atrial remodelling and detect individuals at increased risk of incident AF even in the absence of overt arrhythmic findings at the time of recording.1214 In a retrospective study by Attia et al12 involving 180 922 patients and 649 931 sinus-rhythm ECGs, a CNN-based algorithm identified the presence of AF with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.87, sensitivity of 79.0%, specificity of 79.5% and overall accuracy of 79.4% using a single standard 10 s 12-lead ECG. The use of multiple ECGs recorded from the same patient further improved diagnostic performance, increasing the AUC to 0.90, with sensitivity of 82.3%, specificity of 83.4% and overall accuracy of 83.3%, suggesting that longitudinal analysis of electrocardiographic signals may enhance the predictive capability of AI-ECG. Longitudinal modelling studies further demonstrated progressive increases in algorithm-estimated risk several years before clinical diagnosis and identified individuals at increased risk of AF-related stroke up to 3 years before the event.13 14 These findings support the role of AI-ECG as a scalable tool for opportunistic screening and targeted rhythm monitoring in populations at risk of thromboembolic complications.

Integration of AI algorithms with wearable and single-lead electrocardiographic technologies has further expanded opportunities for detection of paroxysmal arrhythmias in ambulatory settings.1517 Deep neural networks applied to smartwatch-derived physiological signals demonstrated high diagnostic performance in controlled cohorts and clinically meaningful accuracy in real-world populations, while large-scale digital screening studies confirmed that wearable-device notifications can identify individuals with previously undiagnosed AF and activate downstream diagnostic pathways.16 17

Beyond AF detection, AI-ECG has also demonstrated promising performance in automated classification of supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, including conduction disturbances and complex tachyarrhythmias, supporting potential applications in telecardiology programmes, automated reporting systems and high-throughput electrocardiographic triage workflows.4 18 19

Overall, these findings support the role of AI-ECG not only as a diagnostic classifier of arrhythmias but also as a tool for early risk stratification and scalable screening across both hospital-based and community-level care settings.

Identification of structural heart disease using AI-ECG

Recent studies have demonstrated that AI applied to the ECG enables identification of electrophysiological signatures associated not only with cardiac electrical activity but also with structural and functional myocardial abnormalities that are not detectable using conventional interpretation. These findings support the role of AI-ECG as a scalable tool for early identification of subclinical structural heart disease.4

Importantly, the randomised pragmatic EAGLE trial marked an important milestone in AI-ECG.20 EAGLE trial evaluated the impact of integrating a 12-lead Al-enabled ECG algorithm into routine clinical practice for the early identification of reduced LVEF (<50%) in patients without a prior diagnosis of HF. A total of 22 641 adult patients managed by 120 primary care teams were enrolled and randomised to receive either AI-ECG-based clinical decision support or usual care. The primary endpoint was a new diagnosis of low ejection fraction within 90 days of ECG acquisition. Implementation of the algorithm resulted in a significantly higher rate of new diagnoses compared with standard practice (2.1% vs 1.6%; OR 1.32), with an even greater effect among patients with a positive AI-ECG result (19.5% vs 14.5%; OR 1.43).

The study by Attia et al22 furthermore demonstrated that a deep-learning algorithm applied to a standard 12-lead ECG can identify left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF≤35%) using electrocardiographic signals alone. The model was developed and validated using a dataset of 97 829 patients, derived from more than 625 000 paired ECG-echocardiogram recordings. In the independent test cohort (52 870 patients), the algorithm achieved an AUC of 0.93, with 86.3% sensitivity, 85.7% specificity, 85.7% overall accuracy and a negative predictive value of 98.7%. Performance was even higher among individuals without major comorbidities, reaching an AUC of 0.98, with 95.6% sensitivity and 92.4% specificity. Similar performance has been observed across heterogeneous acquisition settings, including smartphone-digitised ECGs, supporting the feasibility of scalable point-of-care screening strategies.23 24

A landmark study by Ko et al27 developed and validated a CNN applied to standard 12-lead ECGs for the detection of HCM, using a cohort of 3060 patients with HCM and 63 941 controls. In the independent test dataset, the model achieved an AUC of 0.96 (95% Cl 0.95 to 0.96), with 87% sensitivity, 90% specificity, a positive predictive value of 31% and a negative predictive value of 99%, indicating a particularly strong ability to exclude disease in screening settings. Performance was especially high among individuals younger than 40 years, with 95% sensitivity and 92% specificity and remained robust even in patients with apparently normal ECGs (AUC 0.95, sensitivity 93%, specificity 87%) and in those meeting conventional ECG criteria for left ventricular hypertrophy (AUC 0.95), demonstrating the algorithm’s ability to distinguish HCM from other causes of myocardial hypertrophy.

Large real-world and multicentre studies demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy across diverse populations, including paediatric cohorts and showed correlations between algorithm outputs and echocardiographic markers such as ventricular wall thickness and myocardial mass, reinforcing the role of AI-ECG as a non-invasive digital biomarker of myocardial structural phenotype.2528

Cardiac amyloidosis

AI-enhanced ECG has also shown promising performance in identification of cardiac amyloidosis, an infiltrative cardiomyopathy frequently underdiagnosed in early stages29 (table 3). AI-ECG models can detect electrophysiological signatures of myocardial infiltration months before clinical diagnosis and maintain diagnostic performance even in patients with apparently normal ECGs or when reduced-lead configurations are used.30 31

An AI-enabled ECG algorithm30 trained on 12-lead ECGs from 2541 patients with cardiac amyloidosis and 2454 matched controls demonstrated high diagnostic performance for detecting both AL and ATTR cardiac amyloidosis, achieving an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI 0.90 to 0.93), with sensitivity 0.84, specificity 0.85 and positive predictive value 0.86 in the holdout cohort; notably, the model identified cardiac amyloidosis more than 6 months before clinical diagnosis in 59% of patients with available prediagnostic ECGs, including 46% of AL and 70% of ATTRwt cases, supporting its role in early disease recognition. The algorithm maintained consistent performance across age, sex and amyloid subtypes and remained effective even in patients with ECGs interpreted as normal by conventional criteria, indicating the ability of deep learning models to detect subclinical electrophysiological signatures not appreciable by standard interpretation. Single-lead and six-lead configurations also showed good diagnostic accuracy, with the best single-lead model (V5) reaching an AUC of 0.86 and the best six-lead configuration achieving an AUC of 0.90, suggesting feasibility for point-of-care and wearable screening applications. Sequential multimodal strategies integrating electrocardiographic preselection with confirmatory imaging further improve diagnostic yield and facilitate differentiation from phenotypically overlapping conditions such as HCM and hypertensive hypertrophy.30 31

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction

More recently, AI-ECG models have demonstrated the ability to support identification of HF with preserved ejection fraction, a condition characterised by diagnostic complexity and frequent under-recognition in early stages.32 Algorithms applied to standard and reduced lead ECG recordings showed good diagnostic accuracy for detection of diastolic dysfunction and enabled identification of high-risk individuals who subsequently developed disease during follow-up, supporting a role for targeted referral to confirmatory imaging evaluation.32

Overall, these findings indicate that AI-ECG enables detection of electrophysiological signatures associated with myocardial structural remodelling across multiple cardiomyopathies and supports its role as a scalable tool for early identification of subclinical structural heart disease and prioritisation of patients for advanced diagnostic investigations.

AI-ECG in acute coronary syndromes

The ECG represents a fundamental tool in the initial evaluation of patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome, enabling rapid identification of time-dependent conditions such as ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction requiring urgent reperfusion therapy33 (table 4). However, in less typical clinical presentations, particularly in non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, conventional electrocardiographic interpretation may have limited sensitivity. In this context, AI applied to the ECG has shown increasing potential for early risk stratification and decision support in patients presenting with acute chest pain.33

A prospective multicentre study including 8493 patients with suspected acute myocardial infarction demonstrated that an AI-enhanced electrocardiographic algorithm identified high-risk and low-risk individuals with diagnostic accuracy comparable to the HEART score and superior to the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score, supporting its role as a rapid non-invasive triage tool in emergency settings.33 Integration of algorithm outputs with conventional clinical scores further improved prognostic stratification and reduced the probability of missed infarction before biomarker availability.

AI-enhanced electrocardiography is also particularly promising for recognition of coronary occlusion beyond conventional ST-segment elevation criteria. An international multicentre study demonstrated high diagnostic performance for identification of occlusion myocardial infarction, with AUC values between 0.93 and 0.95 and sensitivity exceeding that of standard criteria, including in the absence of ST-segment elevation.34

In addition, algorithm-assisted electrocardiographic interpretation improved diagnostic accuracy and reduced false-positive catheterisation laboratory activations while maintaining high sensitivity for true occlusive events in patients with suspected ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, supporting a role for AI-enhanced electrocardiography in optimising emergency triage pathways.35

The ability of AI-ECG to identify occlusion myocardial infarction in the absence of conventional ST-segment elevation is particularly illustrative of the broader digital biomarker paradigm. Rather than relying solely on predefined electrocardiographic features recognisable by human interpretation, AI models appear capable of detecting latent signal signatures associated with acute coronary occlusion. This example highlights how AI-ECG can extract clinically meaningful physiological information beyond traditional waveform analysis, supporting its emerging role as a scalable cardiovascular biomarker platform.

Prognostic stratification and prediction of cardiovascular outcomes using AI-ECG

Beyond diagnostic applications, AI applied to the ECG has demonstrated increasing potential for prognostic stratification across heterogeneous cardiovascular populations. Algorithmic analysis of ECG signals enables extraction of representations associated with future clinical events, supporting the role of the ECG as a predictor of disease trajectories over medium-term and long-term follow-up.1 6

One of the most extensively investigated applications concerns prediction of incident HF. Deep learning models originally developed to identify left ventricular dysfunction can recognise individuals at increased risk of developing HF even in the absence of clinically evident abnormalities at the time of ECG acquisition, supporting early risk stratification and targeted referral for confirmatory imaging evaluation.36

AI-enhanced ECG has also demonstrated prognostic value in patients with established acute HF. Multimodal models integrating electrocardiographic, clinical, laboratory and echocardiographic data showed good predictive performance for both in-hospital and long-term mortality, supporting identification of high-risk phenotypes in complex clinical settings.37

Another important application concerns prediction of all-cause mortality in both selected cardiovascular cohorts and general populations. Deep learning models applied to standard ECGs can estimate long-term mortality risk independently of traditional clinical variables, indicating that electrocardiographic signals contain integrated prognostic information reflecting global cardiovascular health status.4 36 38 39

In particular, the discrepancy between chronological age and algorithm-derived electrocardiographic age has emerged as a marker of cardiovascular biological ageing. In a large cohort of more than 1.5 million individuals, electrocardiographic age exceeding chronological age by more than 8 years was associated with increased mortality risk, with consistent results across independent validation datasets.38

Although AI-enhanced electrocardiography consistently identifies signatures associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, whether these signals reflect causal disease mechanisms or surrogate correlates of systemic risk remains uncertain and requires prospective validation.

Population screening, wearable devices and digital biomarkers

The integration of AI with electrocardiographic analysis has expanded the role of the ECG from a diagnostic test performed in selected clinical settings to a scalable platform for population-level cardiovascular phenotyping. AI-enhanced ECG analysis enables identification of electrophysiological signatures associated with arrhythmic risk, structural heart disease and systemic cardiovascular vulnerability, supporting the transition from episodic testing towards longitudinal cardiovascular monitoring across community-based populations.40 41

Algorithms applied to single-lead electrocardiographic recordings obtained using smartwatches, electrocardiographic patches and photoplethysmography-based sensors have demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for detection of AF and enable identification of intermittent arrhythmic episodes frequently missed by conventional short-duration recordings. These approaches support the feasibility of large-scale remote rhythm surveillance strategies aimed at early stroke prevention through opportunistic detection of previously unrecognised arrhythmias.15 40 41

Beyond arrhythmia detection, AI-enhanced ECG can identify physiological alterations not directly related to conventional electrocardiographic abnormalities. Deep learning models applied to electrocardiographic signals have demonstrated the ability to estimate cardiovascular biological age and detect metabolic and electrolyte disturbances such as hyperkalaemia, suggesting that electrocardiographic analysis captures organism-level physiological information extending beyond cardiac electrophysiology alone.2 5 15

Multimodal integration and future perspectives of AI-ECG

Recent developments in AI applied to the ECG are moving beyond isolated signal analysis towards multimodal frameworks integrating electrophysiological information with cardiovascular imaging, circulating biomarkers and longitudinal electronic health record data. These approaches enable predictive models that better reflect real-world clinical decision-making and support the transition from single-test interpretation towards integrated digital cardiovascular phenotyping platforms.42 43

Integration of ECG signals with structural and functional imaging data represents a particularly promising strategy for improving risk stratification in patients with cardiomyopathies and arrhythmic syndromes. Multimodal models combining ECG features with imaging biomarkers have demonstrated improved classification of arrhythmic risk among patients eligible for implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation and may support more precise identification of individuals at highest risk of sudden cardiac death.36

Another important development concerns the emergence of foundation models for electrocardiographic interpretation trained on large heterogeneous datasets to generate generalisable signal representations reusable across multiple diagnostic tasks. Multicentre validation studies demonstrated strong performance in classification of electrocardiographic diagnoses and prediction of digital biomarkers such as reduced LVEF, incident AF risk and genotype-associated long QT syndrome, particularly in settings with limited annotated training data.10

Advances in generative AI and large language models may further support structured interpretation of multimodal diagnostic data and integration of decision-support outputs within electronic health record infrastructures, improving workflow efficiency and clinical usability. However, their implementation still requires prospective validation and appropriate regulatory oversight to ensure reliability and patient safety.7

Barriers to clinical translation of AI-ECG

Despite expanding evidence supporting the diagnostic and prognostic performance of AI-enhanced electrocardiography, several methodological, technical and regulatory barriers continue to limit its large-scale implementation in routine clinical practice.

One of the principal challenges is the limited availability of prospective multicentre validation studies assessing the integration of AI-ECG models into routine clinical practice. Most currently available models have been developed using retrospective datasets, and only a small number of implementation trials have evaluated their impact on real-world care pathways and therapeutic decision-making.44

Clinical utility beyond diagnostic accuracy

Although AI-ECG models have demonstrated consistently high diagnostic performance across a wide range of cardiovascular applications, improved detection does not necessarily translate into improved patient outcomes. This distinction is particularly relevant in screening settings, where identification of individuals at increased risk of disease may increase diagnostic testing and healthcare utilisation without a corresponding reduction in major cardiovascular events.

The debate surrounding AF screening illustrates this challenge. AI-enabled ECG algorithms can identify individuals at increased risk of incident AF from sinus-rhythm recordings and may facilitate targeted rhythm monitoring. However, uncertainty remains regarding whether earlier identification of subclinical or low-burden AF consistently translates into reductions in stroke, HF or mortality. Recent screening studies, including SCREEN-AF,45 have highlighted that increased detection of previously unrecognised AF does not necessarily translate into clear improvements in patient-centred outcomes, highlighting the complexity of linking enhanced detection strategies to meaningful clinical benefit.

Furthermore, widespread implementation of AI-guided screening strategies may introduce potential unintended consequences, including overdiagnosis, increased downstream testing, additional specialist referrals and higher healthcare costs. As a result, evaluation of AI-ECG should extend beyond traditional measures of diagnostic accuracy and include assessment of clinical utility, cost-effectiveness, impact on healthcare resource utilisation and patient-centred outcomes. Large pragmatic implementation trials will, therefore, be essential to determine whether AI-guided screening strategies ultimately improve cardiovascular outcomes in real-world populations and to establish the clinical value of AI-ECG-derived digital biomarkers beyond predictive performance alone.

Generalisability and dataset shift

Dataset shift and limited generalisability across heterogeneous clinical environments represent additional important barriers. Algorithms trained on single-centre datasets or selected populations may show reduced performance when applied to different demographic groups, acquisition systems or disease prevalences, highlighting the need for robust external validation across geographically diverse cohorts.46 Performance degradation may be particularly relevant across demographic subgroups that are under-represented during model development. Differences in sex, race and ethnicity, body mass index, comorbidity burden and baseline disease prevalence may influence both algorithm calibration and diagnostic accuracy. As a result, models demonstrating excellent performance in development cohorts may show reduced generalisability when deployed in more diverse real-world populations.

Technical factors may further contribute to variability in model performance. Differences in ECG acquisition hardware, signal preprocessing methods, sampling frequency and lead placement can alter electrocardiographic morphology and affect algorithm outputs. Even minor deviations in electrode positioning may introduce signal variations that challenge models trained on highly standardised datasets.

Further methodological limitations include label noise related to imperfect diagnostic reference standards, temporal leakage between training and validation datasets and performance drift after deployment in new clinical environments, all of which may reduce real-world robustness and require continuous lifecycle monitoring.

Interpretability and algorithmic bias

Algorithm interpretability also remains a key challenge. Many deep learning models operate as ‘black-box’ systems, which may limit clinician trust and complicate integration into high-stakes diagnostic workflows. Emerging explainability approaches, including saliency mapping, feature-attribution methods and hybrid modelling strategies combining deep learning outputs with conventional electrocardiographic features, may partially improve transparency and facilitate clinical integration.47

Algorithmic bias represents another important concern, as models trained on non-representative datasets may show reduced performance in under-represented demographic subgroups, potentially reinforcing existing disparities in access to cardiovascular care. Development and validation of AI-enhanced electrocardiography using diverse datasets with explicit subgroup performance reporting is, therefore, essential for equitable implementation.48

Wearable devices and signal quality

Additional challenges arise from variability in signal quality when AI-enhanced electrocardiography is applied to wearable and consumer-grade monitoring devices, where motion artefacts and hardware differences may affect diagnostic reliability. Evidence supporting the impact of wearable-device-based screening strategies on long-term cardiovascular outcomes also remains limited.40

Regulatory and governance challenges

Finally, regulatory approval pathways and organisational integration frameworks are still evolving. Adaptive machine learning systems require continuous postdeployment performance monitoring, creating additional challenges for lifecycle regulation and clinical governance of algorithm-assisted decision-making.7

Future directions

Future progress in AI-enhanced electrocardiography is likely to depend less on incremental improvements in diagnostic accuracy and more on successful integration into clinical workflows. Integration with electronic health records, cardiovascular imaging, wearable technologies and other clinical data sources may enable more comprehensive risk assessment and personalised cardiovascular care. In parallel, foundation models and multimodal AI systems could improve scalability across multiple diagnostic and prognostic applications.

Demonstration of clinical utility and cost-effectiveness beyond predictive performance will represent a critical step towards large-scale implementation. Prospective implementation studies and pragmatic randomised trials will be required to determine whether AI-guided ECG strategies improve patient outcomes, healthcare efficiency and resource allocation in real-world settings.

Ultimately, AI-ECG is likely to evolve from a stand-alone diagnostic support tool into an integrated component of digital cardiovascular care infrastructures.

Conclusions

AI-enhanced electrocardiography is emerging as a scalable digital biomarker platform capable of supporting cardiovascular screening, diagnosis and prognostic stratification across a broad spectrum of clinical conditions. Evidence accumulated over the past decade demonstrates that AI-based analysis can extract clinically relevant information from ECG signals beyond conventional interpretation, enabling identification of arrhythmias, structural heart disease and future cardiovascular risk.

Despite these advances, routine clinical implementation remains limited by challenges related to prospective validation, generalisability, interpretability, regulatory oversight and demonstration of clinical utility. Future research should prioritise implementation studies and outcome-based evaluations to determine whether AI-guided strategies improve patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency.

If successfully validated and integrated into clinical workflows, AI-ECG may become an important component of next-generation digital cardiovascular medicine.
