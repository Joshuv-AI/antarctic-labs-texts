Barriers and facilitating factors for COVID-19 vaccination in rural appalachia
Authors: Kathryn M Cardarelli, Rachel Gillespie, Anna Goodman Hoover, Diane B Francis, Marc T Kiviniemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince the onset of the pandemic, COVID-19 mortality has disproportionately impacted populations impacted by health inequities. This study aimed to identify the barriers and facilitators to COVID-19 vaccination uptake in Appalachian Kentucky as a first step to developing vaccination interventions.MethodsKey informant interviews were conducted with 36 community leaders in 14 Appalachian Kentucky counties from January-June 2023. Interviews focused on leaders’ perspectives about their community’s COVID-19 experiences, community impacts of the pandemic, and vaccine facilitators and barriers in their communities. Iterative inductive-deductive coding was used to characterize responses.ResultsThere were barriers and facilitators at multiple levels of the socioecological model. For example, individual barriers included distrust of federal government and diminished perceived risk, and interpersonal barriers included anti-vaccination norms. Community barriers included logistical challenges, as well anti-vaccination messages from healthcare providers. Perceived politicization of the vaccine and frequent changes to recommendations during the pandemic presented policy-level barriers. Facilitating factors included pro-vaccine messages from trusted community leaders, beliefs about reducing risk for others, and cues to action such as community events.ConclusionsThe challenges to and opportunities for improving COVID-19 vaccination uptake identified here can inform vaccination education, distribution, and policy efforts in both Appalachian Kentucky and other communities with similar characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24484-4.Keywords: COVID-19, Community-based participatory research, Appalachia, Vaccine access, Vaccine uptake

Background

Since the beginning of the pandemic, COVID-19 mortality in the United States (US) has disproportionately affected populations with existing health inequities, including socioeconomically disadvantaged populations and those residing in highly rural areas, both of whom experience higher mortality rates [1, 2]. The Appalachian region of the US is characterized by these drivers of disparities for COVID-19 and many other chronic and infectious diseases [3–5]. Appalachian communities generally face higher levels of poverty and unemployment amidst lower levels of education than the US as a whole, while also being predominantly rural [6].

Fifty-four of Kentucky’s 120 counties meet the Appalachian Regional Commission’s definition of Appalachia [3]. These Appalachian counties have higher than average levels of many risk factors associated with more severe COVID-19 cases [7], including the nation’s second-highest rates of cigarette smoking [8, 9]; some of the nation’s highest rates of adult obesity [10, 11]; and the highest rates of both obstructive lung disease and lung cancer [5, 12].

COVID-19 vaccination has been shown to reduce the likelihood of length and severity of illness and mortality [7]. However, these benefits only accrue if individuals are vaccinated. In many US population groups, uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine is suboptimal [13–16]. This includes Appalachian Kentucky, where adult vaccination rates for the study region have been historically lower than state and national rates among an already highly vulnerable and susceptible population [17, 18]. Uptake rates at the end of the declared public health emergency (May 2023) were 8% points lower than the Kentucky-wide average (65.6%) whereas the uptake rate for Appalachian counties was 57.7%. The 14 study counties rate was nearly identical to that for all Appalachian counties, at 56.7% [19].

While factors related to COVID-19 vaccination uptake have been well studied in racial and ethnic minority populations [20–22], little broad-based examination has occurred regarding factors that lead to low vaccination rates in Appalachian Kentucky populations. A 2021 statewide survey of Kentucky adults found that lower intent to receive the COVID-19 vaccine was associated with lower educational attainment, lower income, Republican political affiliation, and rural residence [23]. In addition, individuals with lower vaccination intentions had perceptions of low vaccine safety and efficacy, as well as mistrust of vaccine production [23]. In rural populations that historically have distrusted government institutions and their messaging [24, 25], vaccination implementation approaches that leverage culturally appropriate community partnerships and strategies will be critical for improving vaccination uptake, which could provide an effective foundation to facilitate combatting the pre-existing health inequities of the region.

As a first step toward developing vaccination interventions for Appalachian Kentucky populations, the objectives of the present study were to identify barriers to and facilitating factors for adults obtaining COVID-19 vaccination in this population. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically focus on barriers and facilitators of COVID-19 vaccination in Appalachian Kentucky.

Methods

Between January and June 2023, the study team conducted a series of semi-structured qualitative interviews with key informants in 14 Appalachian Kentucky counties. These counties were selected based on their designation as Appalachian, the perceived vulnerability of their populations for COVID-19 complications and mortality (based on epidemiologic risk factors), similar sociodemographic composition and our robust partnerships in each county. Trained interviewers from the University of Kentucky interviewed 36 individuals across the 14 counties. Participants received a $25 gift card as an incentive for participation. The study protocol was approved by the University of Kentucky Institutional Review Board (protocol #79294).

Sampling & recruitment process

Study team members employed purposive snowball sampling to identify initial key community stakeholders for participation in the interview. The rationale for this intentional identification of individuals was two-fold: (1) a need to capture the experience and perspectives of local health professionals; and (2) a need to understand the perspectives of highly connected and trusted local leaders, including elected officials, teachers, and community-based organization representatives. A Community Advisory Board (CAB) was formed to ensure that our approach was responsive to the cultural, social, historical and economic contexts of our target communities. The CAB met quarterly with study team members and consisted of seven members who served in positions that focused on the Appalachian region and displayed a keen level of knowledge about local operations, attitudes, and activities. CAB members were paid for their contributions. Potential interviewees were identified by the study’s CAB members, which had been formed at the inception of the project to guide the study team throughout the duration of project funding. Potential interviewees were also identified by project investigators with professional and/or personal ties to the target communities, community leaders (i.e. county Judge Executives), and local public health officials (i.e. local Public Health Department Directors). All interview participants must be current residents of an Appalachian Kentucky county at the time of the interviews, lived and/or worked in one of the fifteen study counties, English-speaking, and at least 18 years old. If at any time during it was discovered the identified potential interviewee did not confirm the above criteria, they were not eligible and excluded from participating in the study.

Recruitment took place from January-May 2023. Potential interviewees initially were invited to participate via email messages sent in January 2023. At least three follow-up attempts were made to reach those who did not respond to the initial message. A total of 97 individuals were identified initially. Of those, 8 individuals had emails that were no longer in service or were inaccurate, causing email invitations to bounce back; 49 never responded to the invitation; 4 declined to participate, which includes those who responded to the interview invitation but did not participate in an interview; and 36 agreed and completed the interview (for a 37% response rate) in which data saturation was met.

Interview process

Prior to conducting interviews, our team developed an interview guide (see Additional file 1), which included questions about knowledge and perceptions of COVID-19 risk among members of the community; community experiences with COVID-19 infection and illness; community residents’ willingness to receive the vaccination; preferences for vaccination delivery locations; potential community messaging to encourage vaccination and other COVID-19 preventive measures; and structural resources, facilitators, and barriers for vaccination.

Interviews were conducted and recorded via Zoom. A REDCap link with an eConsent feature that included a cover letter, option to participate, and demographic questions was shared with the participant prior to the interview [26]. At the time of the interview, additional verbal consent to participate in the interview and to be recorded was provided before proceeding. Each interview was conducted by a trained member of the research team. Interviews lasted 30–45 min. Interview recordings were deidentified by a member of the research team and then transcribed verbatim by a transcription service (Rev; Austin, TX, USA).

Data analysis

Investigators employed an iterative inductive-deductive approach to data analysis, with team members discussing thematic findings and resolving any coding discrepancies. The team met multiple times to iteratively revise the codebook, discuss preliminary analyses, and make final coding determinations for variation that occurred between coders. This deliberative approach aligns with Consensual Qualitative Research, which posits that a team of coders analyzing data independently, then coming to a unified analytic vision through team dialogue may reduce individual bias while better capturing data complexity [27]. To strengthen rigor further, emerging findings were shared with the Community Advisory Board, who reviewed and provided feedback and additional information about local contexts. At that point, a final codebook was developed, and consensus was achieved.

We continued this iterative process by deductively applying the socioecological model (SEM) to organize the themes. The SEM offers a comprehensive framework for understanding health behaviors by positing that multiple interacting levels influence a person’s health within their environment, including individual, community, and policy levels [28]. While our initial theme development was inductive, we subsequently mapped the themes onto the SEM deductively as an organizing framework. Although some themes spanned multiple socioecological levels, each barrier (or facilitator) is described at the single level that best captured the main theme of the participants’ comments.

Reported barriers of COVID-19 vaccination

Individual-level

Four individual-level barriers clearly emerged from the data: distrust of federal government entities, concerns related to the safety of the vaccine, general COVID-19 fatigue, and diminished risk perceptions about the severity of COVID-19.

Distrust of federal government entities

Participants explained that a pervasive mistrust of federal government entities already existed among residents of Appalachian Kentucky prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. This distrust was illustrated by one participant’s statement, “It’s just the whole nature of the demographic that lives in our rural community is that there were so many that didn’t trust the government from the get-go.” Another participant noted, “We are not going to let the government dictate what we do.” Multiple respondents reported that Appalachian residents are particularly averse to federal government directives, and the COVID-19 pandemic response underscored this distrust (see Policy-Level Barrier section below).

COVID-19 vaccine safety concerns

Many participants cited the rapid production of the vaccine as a potential concern for community members, suggesting that without long-term study, the safety of the vaccine was questionable. For example, one participant said, “It’s so new, it was so rushed that we don’t know what the side effects are going to be down the road. So you might take it now and everything be fine, and then 10 years from now it causes heart problems or it causes you to be sterile.” Another participant noted a community reaction, “Well, I think I might get [the vaccine] after I see that other person don’t sprout a third head.”

COVID-19 fatigue

Many participants reported “COVID fatigue” among those in their communities, a condition characterized by not wanting to think about or respond to risk of contracting COVID-19. For example, one participant said, “I think we’re suffering from COVID burnout, and people just don’t want to hear or talk about the dangers.” Another noted, “They just got sick of it. Everywhere you turn something about COVID and threw their hands up and felt like that there wasn’t anything they could do about it basically.” One participant put it more bluntly, “I think everyone, they’re done with COVID.”

Diminished risk-perception

In addition to COVID fatigue, participants reported that many in their community saw little risk from COVID-19 and that perceptions of risk may have diminished over time. One participant said, “The greater majority are very dismissive. It’s just a cold, it’s just a flu. It’s nothing to worry about. We’re moving on,” while another said, “So right now, in this moment, I don’t think people are as concerned about it”.

Interpersonal-level

Two major interpersonal-level barriers were identified- misinformation about the COVID-19 vaccine and social pressures around receiving the vaccine.

COVID-19 vaccine misinformation

An important barrier cited routinely by participants was pervasive misinformation about the COVID-19 vaccine among community members and healthcare providers. For example, a participant shared a common misperception, “One [29] made the reference to the misinformation about making girls sterile if they got vaccinated with the COVID vaccine.” This was corroborated by another key informant, who said, “whole lot of them was listening to rumor that they were injecting you with little things so they could track you wherever you went…there was a rumor going around if you took the vaccine and you were a young lady, you weren’t going to be able to have children.” Another participant noted, “I even had a family member tell me that, ever since she took the vaccine, she could get a magnet stuck to her arm.” Social media was portrayed by several participants as an important catalyst for spreading misinformation in their communities.

Social pressures

Social pressures may have encouraged “covert vaccination” among some residents. In such situations, prevailing social norms meant that residents did not want to disclose receiving the COVID-19 vaccination. One participant said this about a friend, “[The] only way she could get her father to get vaccinated was he said, ‘I’ll do it so long as you don’t ever tell my friends I did it…I don’t want my friends to know.’” Another participant said, “I know people who are vaccinated but don’t want nobody to know. It’s a very secretive thing to them, like, please don’t tell.”

Community-level

Three community-level barriers were identified as major themes throughout the interviews including: logistical challenges, local churches, and a lack of support among healthcare personnel to getting the vaccine.

Logistical challenges (e.g., transportation)

A major community-level barrier participants described was related to the logistical burden of getting people around the community. Lack of access to reliable transportation historically has contributed to health inequities in the region, and participants reported it as an important barrier to accessing COVID-19 vaccination. One participant stated, “The biggest barrier for our community is transportation because our roads are bad…we don’t have the transportation services that other bigger, more metropolitan areas have.” Although transportation challenges are not a barrier specific to COVID-19 vaccination, these challenges were perceived as a major barrier to accessing any healthcare services.

Local churches

Several participants identified churches as a potential barrier to receiving the vaccine in their community. “The issue is what is the church leadership’s stance on immunization? I think that you’re going to get into, there’s going to be some church leadership who doesn’t believe in it, and guess what? Their congregation probably is less likely to be immunized. Those are the ones you’d really ideally like to be [giving vaccinations] at… If the church leadership is pro, then I think you’re much more likely to also have a pro congregation.” Participants reported that while some pastors supported COVID-19 vaccination practices (see Facilitators below), others were vehemently opposed to it and discouraged COVID-19 vaccination.

Lack of supportive healthcare personnel

Healthcare providers actively dissuading COVID-19 vaccination, while simultaneously spreading misinformation, was reported as a barrier to vaccination uptake in some communities. Further complicating this were reports of healthcare personnel either not advocating for patients to receive the COVID-19 vaccination or warning patients against receiving the vaccine, “The issue is there are some physicians who did not advocate vaccination. So if your physician tells you not to get vaccinated, guess what? You’re probably not going to get vaccinated.” Another participant reported, “When you go to the doctors and you’re back there with the nurses and you bring up the vaccine and ‘No, I’m not taking that vaccine. I will quit my job before I have to take that vaccine.’ I mean these are medical professionals that’s telling patients this type of information. I think that kind of puts a barrier to some people getting the vaccine because they think with their self, well, if this is my nurse, these are medical professionals, if they’re not getting it, there’s something wrong with this vaccine.”

Policy-level

Two primary barriers at the policy-level were reported as hindering COVID-19 vaccination uptake in communities: the exacerbated politicization of the vaccine overall and the frequent changes in information and COVID-19-related guidance that was disseminated throughout the communities.

Perceived politicization of COVID-19 vaccine

Most participants noted that COVID-19 vaccine and other pandemic responses were actively used to advance political agendas, which resulted in deterring residents from uptake. For example, one participant noted, “Once it became political, it knocked a lot of people out of getting the vaccine.” Participants reported that this level of politicization was unique in their experiences from other vaccination practices, with one participant stating, “I’ve never seen anything like it in my life. I didn’t feel judged when I didn’t take a flu shot. There’s a lot of emotion and politicalism that I think has been tied to it for people.”

Frequent information and recommendation changes for COVID-19

Several participants noted that mistrust of federal government entities was amplified by a perception of repeated changes and in some cases inconsistencies in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s COVID-19-related recommendations. “I think a lot of people who were really got burned out on the CDC telling them everything to do and then changing it,” noted one participant who continued, “There was some flip-flopping and there was already a distrust of the federal government for people down in this region”.

Reported facilitators of COVID-19 vaccination

Individual-level

There was a distinct difference in the experiences shared and corresponding perception of risk between older and younger adults when asked about what facilitated community members to receive the vaccine. Ultimately, a single individual-level theme was identified as a facilitating factor for getting the COVID vaccination within our study counties: age-related experiences and risk perceptions.

Age-Related experiences and risk perceptions

A frequent observation was that beliefs about the COVID-19 vaccine were associated with age. Participants noted that adults of older age (e.g., age 60 and older) generally seemed more accepting of broad vaccination practices; in contrast, they shared that vaccination did not seem as acceptable among younger residents. For example, one participant noted, “I feel like the majority of people 60 and above didn’t have the reservations about getting vaccinated…because they were around when other immunizations came along and so they lived through different pandemics and different immunizations.” Relative to the older adults, younger adults often were cited as more suspicious of vaccines in general and more susceptible to misinformation spread through social media. Participants also noted that older populations may have higher perceptions of risk because of recognizing how age increases the potential for complications due to COVID-19 infection.

Interpersonal-level

One interpersonal theme was revealed to have contributed to the uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine: trusted knowledge brokers. Participants consistently denoted the importance of information stemming from locally known individuals in the community that were viewed as trustworthy leaders. This included local physicians who practiced and lived in the community, county administrators such as Judge Executives, or church Pastors. Typically, these “trusted knowledge brokers” held some form of a leadership position in the community, yet, because they were also residents, their familiarity was instrumental when the COVID-19 vaccine was rolled out in each respective community.

Trusted knowledge brokers

A prominent theme among key informant interviews was that uptake was facilitated when it was encouraged by the individuals whose thoughts about COVID-19 vaccination were most influential – trusted local healthcare providers and local elected officials known to residents. One participant stated, “Our providers are predominant…most all of them are local or they have been here so long that they are part of the community, and the patients really take stock into whatever their healthcare providers tell them…they trust them.” Asked about other individuals whose views are valued, a participant responded, “Trusted sources…the maintenance supervisor, the school superintendent, the school’s nurse as well.” Other examples of trusted community leaders described by participants were county judges, clergy and local health department directors. Importantly, these trusted knowledge brokers were typically very localized and known by the individuals. One participant, describing the role of clergy as effective communicators to encourage vaccination said that the communicator would be trusted “…if that was their pastor or the deacon of their church, not necessarily the deacon of your church or mine, it had to be the one that they belong to and believed in.” The influence of these individuals also spanned to online mediums with one participant acknowledging how misinformation can spread through social media platforms, however, when delivered “…by the local medical community and the local health department, our feedback has been overwhelmingly positive”.

Community-level

Two major community-level themes were identified to have facilitated COVID-19 vaccination uptake in these Appalachian communities by interviewees: community belonging and responsibility and vaccination access activities.

Community belonging and responsibility

An important value among Appalachian Kentuckians that participants frequently cited is caring for others in their communities, so getting vaccinated to protect others was described as a facilitator. One participant stated, “Some people got vaccinated because they felt like it was the right thing to do…people did it out of a sense of helping your neighbor.” Many participants expressed the desire to protect loved ones with pre-existing conditions or advanced age as an important factor motivating individuals to receive the COVID vaccination. For example, “I think that most people that were concerned about their own health or their spouse’s health or their family’s health overall did it, people specifically in age groups that were over 60.” Another participant remarked that, “We saw a lot of people who, that concern for others was more so than concern for themselves.”

Vaccination access activities

Provision of COVID-19 vaccines at community events and/or in partnership with existing events was a frequently mentioned successful strategy to facilitate uptake. Provision of COVID-19 vaccines at events, such as football games, fall festivals, and church-sponsored events, was mentioned by key informants as a successful tactic, “If people know they can come get a funnel cake and a candy apple and some donuts or whatever, and you just happen to be sitting there, they may get a vaccine. The focus definitely has to be something else.” In additional to community events, incentives also emerged as a cue to move people to get vaccinated. One participant said, “I know at one point they offered incentives for getting your vaccination like a $50 Walmart card or a $25 gas card. And for some people they might just need that money and they would get vaccinated.” Another noted, “If there was an incentive, like a gift card or something like that, that helped to a point where we gave out t-shirts to anybody that got one there for a while. That incentive seemed to help a lot.”

Policy-level

One participant noted that workplace mandates for vaccination was a motivator for uptake, but this was not mentioned by others. No facilitating factors at this level of the SEM were identified as a major theme in the interviews.

Discussion

Several key themes emerged from our synthesis of the key informant interviews. First, although interviewees discussed both facilitators of and barriers to COVID-19 vaccine uptake, participants described meaningfully more barriers than facilitators. Although it is possible that this reflects the known bias towards focusing on affectively negative items or another cognitive bias, this may well reflect the reality of COVID-19 vaccination in these communities given their relatively low rates of vaccine uptake [30, 31].

Second, the interview responses reflect the complexity and systemic nature of COVID-19 vaccination uptake. Consistent with other recent findings in Kentucky [23, 24], for both facilitators of uptake and barriers to uptake, the interviewees highlighted factors ranging from individual-person factors, such as incorrect beliefs about vaccination and varying levels of perceived risk, to broad interpersonal factors, including messages propagated by trusted community leaders. In addition to the multiple socioecological levels represented in responses, the responses collectively highlight the complex and systemic nature of vaccination uptake. For example, the shifting and (at least in the perception of many community members) contradictory advice from federal public health authorities, a policy-level factor, shaped and reinforced individual-level feelings of mistrust in governmental entities and, likely, perceptions of vaccine efficacy and trustworthiness of communicators.

Finally, the responses frequently highlight a syndemic effect of COVID-19 morbidity risk factors, limited health care access, and lower socioeconomic status that drives both vaccine uptake and the impact of COVID-19 impact on communities. While some of these syndemic features may be specific to Appalachian populations, similar syndemic effects are likely to characterize other rural communities given that any other rural areas of the United States share one or more of these features. Our findings highlight the importance of considering facilitators and barriers to vaccine uptake using a systems thinking, multi-level approach. Even for those factors that may be unique to Appalachia, the findings are important; as to our knowledge, this study is the first to examine multi-level factors at play in Appalachian communities. The centrality of community for both COVID-19 perceptions and vaccine uptake weaves throughout our findings. Participants often cited community-level structural challenges, including lack of transportation options, as important barriers to vaccination. Additionally, community settings provide the backdrop through which normative beliefs are formed and strengthened. Given the importance of normative beliefs to vaccination intention [32], community-level discourse may play a critical part in whether residents choose to vaccinate. Participants further perceived the sense of belonging to a community as supportive of vaccination uptake, which aligns with recent findings about the connections between vaccination intentions, social identity [33], and altruistic reasoning [34]. Participants cited protecting others in their community as a facilitating factor for their vaccination. Finally, participants saw “the community” as critical for providing venues and opportunities through which COVID-19 vaccination can be facilitated. This finding reinforces growing evidence from attempts to deploy mobile vaccination clinics at community events to support vaccine uptake, particularly among underserved populations [35, 36].

The strong social networks that participants described within their region also provide important opportunities for spreading misinformation and mistrust throughout communities. Word-of-mouth and individual anecdotes appear to have spread far and wide within partner communities, with multiple participants describing specific vaccine “stories” and conjecture that had become commonly shared. Some participants explicitly recognized social media, particularly Facebook, as an initial source of misinformation that shared discourse within their communities, fomenting mistrust of medical information. This migration of mistrust through community networks reflects previously identified processes related to other potentially stigmatizing medical issues, such as HIV medication adherence among underserved populations [37, 38]. It is important to note the pivotal anchor those viewed as trustworthy knowledge brokers are in the community to mediate the effects of false information spread within social networks. Previous studies have harnessed these figures, such as local healthcare providers, to address vaccine hesitancy with effective messaging to combat the uncertainty individuals were experiencing online [39, 40]. However, future research is needed to better understand whether strong social networks can be leveraged as an effective and sustainable avenue for spreading factual messaging, such as through social media.

Several interview participants reported that local clergy played an important role in fostering vaccine-related attitudes and beliefs among their parishioners. As Bouchard, Price, and Swan have described, rural residents rely “on social capital and social networks that accrue from their participation in the Church,” with faith providing “steadfast consistency…in their lives” [41]. (p48) By leading organizations that are both institutional and social anchors of Appalachian communities, local clergy can wield great influence over church members. Participants in our study shared stories of local clergy opposing vaccination, actively chilling vaccine uptake among their church members. Conversely, participants also reported that some churches engaged in prosocial, pro-vaccine activities and events, including hosting vaccine clinics. This role of clergy in impeding or promoting COVID-19 vaccination appeared to be both inconsistent and highly specific geographically and socially. As discussed above, the “trusted messenger” phenomenon is such that individuals may trust the pastor of the church they attend but not a pastor at a church very close by. Thus, given that many churches in our study counties and other rural areas are quite small, the necessary level of specificity and heterogeneity may present complications for enacting the scale of faith-based public health partnerships and intervention approaches that have proven effective for less divisive health-related issues, such as cancer prevention [42].

Despite concerns about stereotyping diverse individuals within a broad geographic region, Appalachian residents have often been labeled as distrusting of “outsiders” [43]. This observation reflects a prevalent Central Appalachian culture of localism, in which local control is valued above “distant bureaucracies and powers” [44]. Furthermore, rural populations more broadly are recognized as distrusting of and disaffected with “federal government” in general and federal regulatory agencies in particular [45]. Our study found evidence of such distrust in relation to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. In short, this study identifies important challenges for assuming that federal agencies and officials are credible arbiters of information in this region and instead suggests that local leaders may be more trusted for vaccine information. Notably, our participants appeared to describe “the government” as a monolithic entity, whereas in reality, many independent agencies are involved in Appalachia, including those responding to COVID-19. This monolithic conception of government is consistent with findings from other examinations of public perceptions of government interventions [46]. Interventions to address this mistrust may benefit from highlighting the multiple agencies involved and addressing trust and credibility at the unit versus the collective government level.

The study’s findings, highlighting key themes of trusted knowledge brokers, community belonging, and positive community cues, have important implications for designing effective interventions. The multi-level nature of the findings emphasizes the need to address various aspects of the socioecological framework simultaneously. Interventions could target both individual and structural factors such as beliefs and attitudes, community norms, and broader societal challenges. This comprehensive approach recognizes that COVID-19 vaccination behavior is influenced by a complex interplay of personal, social, and environmental factors. Furthermore, health communication interventions are more effective when they address multiple levels [47, 48].

The emergence of trusted knowledge brokers suggests that interventions should identify and engage local messengers, such as local community leaders, respected clergy, and other influential figures. These individuals can help bridge intervention programs and community members, lending credibility to key messages and fostering trust in evidence-based initiatives. The engagement of trusted messengers recognizes that information dissemination is not merely about the content, but also about the source of the content.

The findings underscore the importance of tailoring COVID-19 vaccination interventions to rural communities. The positive community cues suggest that interventions should leverage and amplify existing positive norms and values within the community. Thus, intervention programmers should capitalize on community values and strengths. This might involve showcasing COVID-19 vaccine success stories across the communities and celebrating local improvement in COVID-19 vaccine rates. Such an approach not only increases the likelihood of success but also promotes community buy-in and sustainability.

Limitations

Limitations to the current study should be considered. First, given that these perspectives were solicited specifically to inform subsequent development of intervention tools for implementation in the target counties, many key informants had direct experience related to COVID-19 and vaccination in one or more of the 14 counties. Although these counties, and Appalachian Kentucky more broadly, share many common features with other areas of Appalachia, it should be noted that the study population, focus of the questions and, therefore, the responses are based on response from these 14 counties.

Second, we selected a key informant approach given both the breadth of topics being addressed and the importance of ascertaining what worked and didn’t work to promote COVID-19 vaccine uptake in the target counties. Although this approach has many strengths, a potential limitation is that key informants reported on their own perceptions of what motivated or inhibited other individuals in their communities regarding vaccination uptake, as well as describing their own perceptions of other people’s attitudes and beliefs. As such, the reports are limited by the biases that exist in each respondent’s perceptions of other residents’ thoughts and feelings.

Finally, given both the nature of the study population and the challenges of recruiting busy professionals to participate in research, the study did not use a random sampling process to recruit participants. Potential interviewees were identified by the study team, the Community Advisory Board, and other known contacts with knowledge of the study population. Thus, there may be community perspectives that are not captured here due to this sampling process.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate both challenges to and opportunities for improving COVID-19 vaccination uptake in this rural, geographically isolated region of Kentucky. Development of acceptable, effective interventions to increase COVID-19 vaccination in populations experiencing health inequities must be informed by local perspectives. Community-engaged approaches to develop interventions that are responsive to factors at multiple levels of the socioecological model are critical to increase COVID-19 vaccination uptake in these populations.
