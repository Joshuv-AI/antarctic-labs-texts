Purslane supplementation improved antioxidant capacity and milk quality potentially through rumen microbiota in dairy goats
Authors: Mengyu Wang, Yalun Ren, Huijun Shen, Kaixin Li, Xiwang Xu, Huaiping Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurslane is a common plant with medicinal and edible homology that displays potential as a dietary supplement. In this study, this experiment aimed to investigate the effects of purslane on the health and milk quality of dairy goats in peak-lactation period. Twenty primiparous goats with similar days in milk (90 ± 10 d) and milk yield (1.54 ± 0.10 kg/d) were randomly divided into two treatments, each consisting of 10 replicates with one goat per replicate. The goats were fed with a basal diet supplemented with or without 2.5% purslane. After eight weeks, milk samples were collected for milk composition and biochemical analysis. Serum samples were obtained to identify serum parameters. Rumen fluid was collected for 16S rRNA sequencing. The metabolome of rumen fluid and serum was analyzed via untargeted metabolomics. The results showed that compared to the control, purslane decreased the relative abundance of harmful bacteria in the rumen, including [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group and Butyrivibrio (P < 0.05); ruminal concentrations of propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, isovalerate, and valerate were greater in goats fed with purslane (P < 0.05); purslane markedly enhanced antioxidant capacity, which was evidenced by the increased activities of serum antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) (P < 0.05). Also, nontargeted metabolomic analysis revealed that purslane increased serum metabolites involved in glycolipid metabolism, which were associated with the enhanced anti-inflammatory system (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, purslane increased immunoglobulin M (IgM) content and up-regulated beneficial metabolites in milk, such as flindersine, pyruvate, and didymin (P < 0.05). Correlation analysis further revealed that the relative abundance of ruminal Firmicutes, [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group, and Butyrivibrio had negative relationships with serum SOD, GSH-Px activities and milk IgM content (P < 0.05). More importantly, linalool oxide, a component of purslane, was released into milk, which is considered as a potential anti-inflammatory compound. In conclusion, this study demonstrated that purslane improved antioxidant capacity and milk immunoglobulins in dairy goats, which may be associated with the modulation of ruminal microbiota.Keywords: Antioxidant capacity, Milk quality, Ruminal microbiota, Purslane, Dairy goat

Introduction

In the world's expanding demand for high-quality protein, milk has become an essential high-nutritional animal protein source. Most of people consume milk and dairy products from cows. Notably, goat's milk includes more nutrients than cow's milk and is easily digested and absorbed by consumers (García et al., 2014; Fan et al., 2023). With the increasing emphasis on a healthy diet, the goats' milk market is expected to grow dramatically. However, goats' milk quality varied greatly, which is commonly impacted by a range of factors such as nutrition, management, and surroundings (Vacca et al., 2018; Fernandes et al., 2023). It is important to acknowledge that dairy goats experience heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses during lactation, leading to detrimental effects on milk lactation and milk quality (Silanikove et al., 2014). Consequently, there is a pressing necessity for the investigation and advancement of innovative approaches to enhance health status and milk quality. The utilization of plants or their extracts as feed additives in the dietary regimen of lactating goats to confer health benefits and improve milk quality has gained widespread acceptance in recent years.

Purslane (Portulaca oleracea L.) is an annual herbaceous plant in the Portulaceae family and has a long history worldwide. As a potential botanical food source, purslane's various benefits such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-diabetic properties have been extensively studied in rat and mouse models (Baradaran Rahimi et al., 2019; Bao et al., 2022). A previous study showed that dietary supplementation with dried purslane to the diet elicited an increase of egg production and egg weight in laying hens (Aydin and Dogan, 2010). Recently, purslane was shown to improve growth performance in broilers, which was associated with the increased abundance of intestinal Lactobacillus (Wang et al., 2021b). A study in 2014 indicated that purslane could suppress the growth of pathogenic bacterial taxa from cow mastitis (Peng et al., 2014). However, in Nubian goats, purslane supplementation at a dose of 5 g/kg body weight caused weakness of the fore and hind limbs with inability to stand, greenish watery diarrhea and polyuria (Obied et al., 2003). Moreover, nitrate and nitrite poisoning induced by purslane has been reported in goats and sheep from Northeast Brazil, with animals exhibiting clinical signs of cyanotic mucous membranes and bloat (Simoes et al., 2018). The detrimental effect was attributable to the accumulation of nitrate in a short period of time after consuming high doses of purslane (Simoes et al., 2018). However, the use of purslane in goats is still at the preliminary stage and its effect on lactating dairy goats has not been deciphered.

As a naturally occurring bioreactor for ruminants, rumen can ferment indigestible plant biomass and obtain nutrients for use in their dairy products. In fact, numerous studies have revealed the linkage between rumen microbiome and mammary gland, which possibly has an important effect on milk-related traits (Hu et al., 2024). It was reported that lactating cows with different milk protein yields have distinct bacterial richness and abundance (Xue et al., 2019). Several ruminal bacteria such as Lachnospira, Butyrivibrio, and Prevotellaceae contributed to milk yield and composition (Xue et al., 2018). Consistently, as demonstrated in a previous study, inulin isolated from Helianthus tuberosus L. could potentiate ruminal microbiota-mediated lactation performance in dairy cows (Wang et al., 2021c). However, it remains poorly understood whether the effect of purslane on milk quality is related to ruminal microbiota.

In this study, the hypothesis was that purslane exerted a promotive effect on goat health and milk quality, which might be correlated with the modulation of ruminal microbiota. Therefore, this research was conducted to explore the role of purslane on antioxidant and milk quality in dairy goats, and 16S rRNA sequencing, metabolomic technology, and correlation analysis were integrated to elucidate a pathway explanation mediated by ruminal microbiota in this process. Additionally, high resolution analysis was conducted to identify purslane ingredients present in goat milk, potentially revealing functional components of purslane. This study will provide a valuable scientific basis for the utilization of purslane in the feed industry and ruminant production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animals Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Northwest A&F University, China (approval No. DK2021054).

Chemical analysis of purslane

Purslane was provided by Qinruihuabo instrument Co., Ltd. (Xianyang, Shaanxi, China). Referring to a previous study, the chemical components in plant powder were identified using ultra performance liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC-HRMS) method (Chen et al., 2023). Briefly, after grinding in liquid nitrogen, 500 mg of purslane powder from the whole plant was mixed with 1 mL of methanol solution for ultrasonic extraction in a water bath for 90 min. Followed with centrifugation, the supernatant was obtained and then filtered through a pore plate before vacuum drying. Next, the sample was redissolved by adding 200 μL of 40% methanol aqueous solution. Then, combined technology of ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC)-high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was conducted. Samples were separated using T3 column in an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (column temperature = 35 °C; flow rate = 0.3 mL/min; injection volume = 2 μL). The mobile phase consisted of A = water and 0.1 % formic acid, and B = acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Primary and secondary spectrums were collected by Q Exactive mass spectrometry (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and detection was performed in both electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative modes. Animals and experimental design

After a 10-day adaptation, 20 Guanzhong dairy goats (primiparous) with similar days in milk (90 ± 10 d) and milk yield (1.54 ± 0.10 kg/d) were selected and randomly enrolled into two groups. Each group included ten replicates with one goat per replicate. Goats in each group were received with a basal diet supplemented with (purslane group, PUR) or without (control group, CTR) purslane powder in dose of 25 g/kg of a basal diet. The forage-to-concentrate ratio in the basal diet sets as 5:5 and the detailed composition and nutritional levels of the basal diet is exhibited in Table 2. Throughout the trial, goats were individually housed in pens and fed twice daily (07:00 and 15:00) with free access to water. The feeds offered were recorded daily. Before feeding the next day, the amount of residual feed was also recorded to calculate daily feed intake (FI). Experimental diet was collected and then dry matter (DM) content was analyzed to calculate dry matter intake (DMI) (DMI = FI × DM). Moreover, the goats were milked twice daily (07:00 and 15:00) and recorded milk yield. During the whole experiment, the behavioral performance of all dairy goats, including rumination, activity pattern, stool shape, and spiritual state, was monitored and no abnormalities were found. After eight weeks, eight goats from each group were randomly chosen for milk, ruminal fluid and blood sampling.

Samples collection and chemical analyses

From 53 to 55 d, 10 g of fecal sample from each goat was collected by rectal massage twice a day (06:00 and 18:00) over three consecutive days. One milliliter 10% hydrochloric acid solution was mixed with 10 g of feces, and stored in the refrigerator at −20 °C for further analysis. At the end of collection period, the feces of each goat were mixed and dried in the oven at 65 °C. Moreover, the experimental diets were collected from 53 to 55 d at 06:00 and 18:00, and then dried in an oven at 65 °C.

Before analysis, the dried experimental diets and fecal samples were moistened in the air for 24 h and then crushed to 1 mm with a grinder. Ca and P contents in experimental diets was measured based on the method of NY/T 3318-2018. DM (GB/T 6435–2014), crude protein (CP, GB/T 6432–2018), ether extract (EE, GB/T 6433–1994), neutral detergent fiber (NDF, GB/T 20806–2006), and acid detergent fiber (ADF, NY/T 1459–2007) contents in experimental diets and feces were identified. Ash (GB/T 6438–2007) content of the diet was determined. The organic matter (OM) content in the diet was calculated according to the following equation, OM = DM – Ash. Metabolizable energy (ME) value in the diet was calculated according to the NRC (2007). Apparent digestibility of nutrients was calculated using acid insoluble ash (AIA) as an endogenous marker. Rumen fluid analysis

At the end of the experiment, rumen fluid samples were collected using a stomach tube 3 h after morning feeding and then filtered by four layers of gauze and divided into four portions: (1) the pH value was identified with a pH meter (PHS-3B, Shanghai Hongyi Instrumentation Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). (2) Rumen fluid samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Next, 100 μL of supernatant was mixed with 100 μL of deionized water and 800 μL of 0.2 mol/L hydrochloric acid. The ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) content was analyzed using phenol-sodium hypochlorite colourimetric method (Broderick and Kang, 1980). (3) Add 400 μL of 25% metaphosphoric acid to 2 mL of supernatant, then centrifuge at 10,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. The contents of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) were determined using gas chromatography (Agilent 7890A, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). (4) Rumen fluid was quickly stored in liquid nitrogen for 16S rRNA sequencing.

The 16S rRNA sequencing of rumen fluid

The 16S rRNA sequencing was conducted by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Genomic DNA was extracted with OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (Cat. #D5625–01, Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). PCR amplification of the selected V3-V4 variable region was conducted using specific primers (338F, 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3'; 806R, 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Then, the PCR product was extracted, purified, and quantized using 2% agarose gel, AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Cat. # AP-GX-4; Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA), and Microplate reader (FLx800, BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) fluorescence quantitative system. After that, the library was constructed using TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Cat. # FC-121–4001, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), followed with quality inspection and sequencing by Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (2100 bioanalyzer, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), Quantus Fluorometer (E6150, Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) and Illumina novaseq6000 sequencing platform (Illumina novaseq6000, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). After the sequences were quality-controlled, denoised, and merged, amplicon sequence variants (ASV) with 100% identity were obtained using DADA2 with the default parameters of Qiime2 (q2cli version 2022.11.1). Based on the amplicon sequence variants (ASV) level, α-diversity, including Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Ace indexes, was evaluated with Qiime2 software (q2cli version 2022.11.1). Furthermore, a Venn diagram was plotted with Qiime2 software (q2cli version 2022.11.1) at the ASV level. Using the Qiime2 classification sklern algorithm, representative sequences were selected from various ASVs and then aligned and annotated using the Silva database (version 138). Graphpad Prism 9.5 software was used to plot bacterial composition at the phylum and genus levels. STAMP difference analysis was adopted to compare the bacterial abundance (Wilcox test analysis).

Serum parameters analysis

On 56 d, whole blood was collected from jugular vein using vacuum tubes 3 h after the morning feeding and serum was obtained after centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min. The contents or activities of glucose, non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA), total triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), β-hydroxybutyric acid (D3-H), urea nitrogen (UN), total protein, albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in serum were determined using automatic biochemical analyzer (Cobasc 800, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Serum superoxide dismutase (SOD, EC 1.15.1.1), catalase (CAT, EC 1.11.1.21), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, EC 1.11.1.9), malondialdehyde (MDA), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin M (IgM) activities or contents were measured using corresponding assay kits (SOD, Cat. # A001-3-2; CAT, Cat. # A007-1-1; GSH-Px, Cat. # A005-1-2; MDA, Cat. # A003-1-2; T-AOC, Cat. # A015-1-2; TNF-α, Cat. #H052-1-2; IgA, Cat. #H108-1-2; IgG, Cat. #H106-1-1; IgM, Cat. #H109-1-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Milk yield and composition analysis

Pipeline milking was performed twice daily (06:00 and 18:00), and milk yield was recorded daily. At 06:00 and 18:00 on 56 d, milk samples were acquired, mixed in a 6:4 ratio based on volume, and stored in potassium dichromate for further analysis. The determination of milk fat, protein, lactose, and total solid was identified using milk analyzer (DairySpec FT, Bentley Instruments, Chaska, MN, USA) in Xi'an Animal Husbandry Station (Xi'an, Shaanxi, China). The contents of IgA, IgG, and IgM in milk were measured by assay kits (IgA, Cat. #H108-1-2; IgG, Cat. #H106-1-1; IgM, Cat. #H109-1-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) based on the manufacturer's manual.

Nontargeted metabolomics of serum and milk

Nontargeted metabolomics were performed by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To remove the protein, serum or milk samples were mixed with cold methanol/acetonitrile/H2O (2:2:1, v/v/v) and centrifuged. For liquid chromatography-mass spectrometer (LC-MS), the samples were re-dissolved in acetonitrile/water solvent (1:1, v/v), vortexed, and centrifuged. Next, the supernatant was injected and separated using an UHPLC (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (column temperature = 25 °C; flow rate = 0.3 mL/min; injection volume = 2 μL). The mobile phase consisted of A = 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonium hydroxide in water, B = acetonitrile. After that, MS analysis was conducted using a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the detection was conducted in electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative modes, respectively. Before peak alignment and picking using XCMS software (version 3.7.1), the raw data need to be converted to MzXML files through ProteoWizard tool (version 3.0.6428). Isotopes and adducts were annotated using CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms of MEtabolite pRofile Annotation). Only variables with more than 50% non-zero values in at least one group were retained in the extracted ion features. Compound identification of metabolites was performed by comparing of accuracy m/z value (<10 ppm), and MS/MS spectra with an in-house database. The differential metabolites were determined by a fold-change threshold of 1.5 and P-value <0.05 from student's t test.

High resolution analysis of purslane ingredients in milk

Sample processing: 200 μL milk sample was mixed with 600 μL of methanol solution containing an internal standard. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through an orifice plate before being blown dry with nitrogen gas. Then, the milk sample was redissolved by adding 100 μL of 40% methanol aqueous solution.

UHPLC-HRMS: samples were separated using T3 column in an UHPLC (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (column temperature = 35 °C; flow rate = 0.3 mL/min; injection volume = 2 μL). The mobile phase consisted of A = water and 0.1% formic acid, B = acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Primary and secondary spectrums were collected by Q Exactive mass spectrometry (Q Exactive HFX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and detection was performed in both ESI positive and negative modes.

Statistic processing: compounds were identified by matching with the first-order accurate mass (molecular mass error <25 ppm), second-order fragment spectrogram, and retention time (RT) et al., in in-house TCMDB database. It was considered to be reliable when MS2 fragment score was more than 0.7. Screening standard of purslane ingredients in milk was as follows (1) it can be identified in purslane medicinal herb; (2) average peak area of the compound in purslane group was more than 2 folds than the control group, that is, fold-change threshold ≥ 2 and P-value < 0.05 from student's t-test.

Correlation analysis

Spearman's correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationships among phenotypes, the relative abundance of ruminal microbiota, as well as metabolites in serum and milk using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). The correlation coefficient (r) ranged from −1 to 1. The |r| value represented the degree of correlation between variables. Significant correlations were identified at P < 0.05, and extremely significant correlations were observed at P < 0.01.

Statistical analysis

16S rRNA sequencing results were analyzed by Wilcox test. Apparent digestibility, ruminal fermentation parameters, serum parameters, milk composition and immunoglobulins levels were analyzed by student's t test (SPSS 22.0 software). Feed intake and milk yield between the two groups at different time points were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA and the data were tested using Mauchly's method. If the observed values did not meet the covariance matrix sphericity test (P < 0.05), Greenhouse-Geisser was used for correction. Results

Chemical components analysis of purslane

The bioactive components of purslane were identified using UPLC-HRMS analysis. The ion peak area of malioxamycin, citric acid, dopamine, (−)-salsolinol, N-acetyl-D-norleucine, and phenylalanine are among the top six, which are the main active constituents of purslane (Table 1).

Purslane affected ruminal microbiota and fermentation parameters in dairy goats

As shown in Table 3, purslane supplementation did not result in significant difference in FI during the whole experimental period (P = 0.241). Moreover, there was a time effect (P < 0.001) on FI, but no interaction effect between the two groups (P = 0.884). Furthermore, purslane did not significantly affect the apparent digestibility of DM, CP, EE, NDF, and ADF (P > 0.05; Table 4). 16S rRNA sequencing results revealed that there were 6387 and 5915 ASVs in the CTR and PUR groups, respectively, with 2629 shared ASVs (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, α-diversity results showed that purslane treatment significantly decreased Shannon index (P = 0.021), and had tendency to decrease Simpson index (Fig. 2B, P = 0.064). However, the PCoA and NMDS plots did not suggest a considerable separation between the two groups (Fig. S1). Further analysis indicated that over 70% of the total ruminal bacterial community was populated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidota. A reduced amount of Firmicutes was observed from the purslane group than that of the control group (P = 0.002) (Fig. 2C). At the genus level, the predominant strains were Prevotella and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group. Moreover, with the supplementation of purslane, the relative abundance of WCHB1-41, U29-B03, [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group, Butyrivibrio, Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group, Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group, and Acinetobacter was significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

Purslane altered serum parameters and metabolomic profiling in dairy goats

After supplementation with purslane, serum NEFA, TG, D3-H, and ALB contents were increased (P < 0.05), whereas GLB content was reduced significantly (P = 0.013). Moreover, purslane had tendency to increase glucose content (P = 0.054). TC, UN, total protein contents and ALT, AST, and ALP activities in serum had no significant alterations between these two groups (P > 0.05; Table 6). Correlation analysis among ruminal microbiota, serum indicators and metabolome, milk composition and metabolome

To identify the potential rumen microbiota-host metabolic interactions, Spearman's correlation analysis between ruminal microbiota and serum metabolome was assessed (Fig. 5A). The results showed that the relative abundance of [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group in the rumen was negatively correlated with butanoic acid and isovaleric acid contents in serum (P < 0.05). Moreover, the relative abundance of WCHB1-41 was also negatively correlated with butanoic acid and isovaleric acid contents in serum (P < 0.01). The relative abundance of Firmicutes was negatively correlated with papaverine, galactinol, taurodeoxycholic acid and taurochenodeoxycholate contents in serum (P < 0.05). Moreover, there were positive correlations between the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group, Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group, and myristoyl-l-carnitine, pantothenol, dicyclomine contents in serum (P < 0.01). Next, Spearman's correlation analysis revealed 13 significant negative relationships between ruminal microbiota and serum antioxidant, immune indicators (Fig. 5B). In particular, the relative abundance of Firmicutes and [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group had negative correlations with SOD activities in serum (P < 0.01). The relative abundance of Firmicutes, [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group, and Butyrivibrio had negative correlations with GSH-Px activities in serum (P < 0.05). Further, an association study between ruminal microbiota and milk metabolites unveiled 41 significant correlations (Fig. 5C). It is worth noting that the relative abundance of U29-B03 was negatively correlated with tetrahydro-l-biopterin, flindersine, didymin, enalaprilat (P < 0.01), but positively correlated with catalposide, maltitol, homatropine (P < 0.05) and 6-benzyloxycarbonylaminohexanoic acid, guanidinopropionic acid abundance (P < 0.01) in milk. Additionally, the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Acinetobacter had negative correlation with IgM content in milk (P < 0.01). The relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group had positive correlation with milk fat percentage (P < 0.05). Moreover, the relative abundance of [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group showed a positive correlation with total solid in milk (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

During the lactation period, dairy goats are susceptible to infections such as mastitis, resulting in poor lactation performance, and in turn restricting the development of the dairy goat industry. Purslane is an annual herb widely distributed around the globe, containing bioactive compounds like alkaloids etc. that have a variety of pharmacological effects, such as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Rahimi et al., 2019). In animal husbandry, supplementation of purslane promoted growth performance, improved feed efficiency and antioxidant status in broilers (Sadeghi et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021b). Moreover, feeding purslane to Nile tilapia could significantly increase plasmatic antioxidant enzyme activities including SOD and CAT in a dose-dependent manner (Abdel-Razek et al., 2019). Although purslane has been studied in other animals due to its high safety and multi-functionality, its application in goats remains scarce. The study confirmed that purslane treatment significantly enhanced antioxidant capacity in serum of dairy goats. Milk immunoglobulins levels and beneficial metabolites – such as flindersine – were increased after purslane supplementation, which provides the benefit of enhancing consumers’ immune function. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that these beneficial consequences caused by purslane were attributable to the improvement of ruminal microbiota. More importantly, linalool oxide, a derivative of purslane, was enriched in purslane-fed goat milk, which may be a potential functional substance for human health.

Ruminal microbiota is fundamental to the digestion of plant material in ruminants. It has been widely assumed that natural plants can influence and regulate ruminal microbiota composition in goats. For example, cassava foliage increased the relative abundance of Butyrivibrio_2 and Prevotella_1 in the rumen of Hainan black goats (Li et al., 2023). After treatment with hemp, the ruminal proportion of Prevotella_1 was decreased, while Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group abundance was increased in Xiangdong black goats (Ran et al., 2024). Likewise, in the present study, supplementation of purslane to a basal diet significantly reduced the relative abundance of phylum Firmicutes and several genera, including Acinetobacter, Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group, Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group, WCHB1-41, U29-B03, [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group, and Butyrivibrio. Acinetobacter, generally identified as an opportunistic pathogen, was predominant in cows with mastitis, as previously stated (Ayemele et al., 2021). Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group and Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group are members of Lachnospiraceae family. Although Lachnospiraceae has been assumed to be helpful bacteria capable of producing beneficial metabolites, certain taxa exhibited pathological supply in subjects with inflammatory conditions (Vacca et al., 2020). Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group could exacerbate inflammatory responses and immune dysfunction in aged atherosclerotic mice (Sun et al., 2021). Evidence from a lamb experiment suggested that mulberry leaf silage enhanced antioxidant activity and immunomodulatory function, possibly involving in the decreased amount of Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group in the rumen (Wang and Luo, 2021). Thus, purslane treatment decreased the relative abundance of Acinetobacter, Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group and Lachnospiraceae_XPB1014_group here, which may reduce the risk of metabolic and oxidation or inflammation-related injuries in lactating goats. Furthermore, WCHB1-41 was positively linked with the production of D-lactate (Kim et al., 2021), which is easily deposited in the rumen and causes rumen acidosis (Ewaschuk et al., 2004). In this work, the decreased proportion of WCHB1-41 seemed to help prevent rumen acidosis to some extent. In dairy cows with high milk fat, U29-B03 was abundant in the rumen, and its relative abundance was positively correlated with the percentage of short and medium chain fatty acids in raw milk (Si et al., 2023). Thus, purslane supplementation significantly lowered the relative abundance of U29-B03, which possibly tended to inhibit fat deposition in milk in the present study. [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group and Butyrivibrio were considered as potential beneficial bacteria in inflammatory or metabolic disorders (Kulkarni et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023b). However, a previous study suggested that Isatis leaf could decrease the relative abundance of Butyrivibrio in the rumen of fattening sheep, concomitant with the enhanced immunity (Cao et al., 2024). Thus, it is speculated that these two taxa play beneficial or detrimental effects, depending on the animal's own health status. As major microbiota-derived metabolites, VFAs can provide more than 75% of the host's energy (Zhao et al., 2021). In this experiment, supplementation with purslane in diets significantly elevated ruminal propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, isovalerate, and valerate levels, suggesting that purslane might promote rumen fermentation and enhance energy supply for dairy goats. Correspondingly, due to higher VFAs release in the rumen, the pH value was lower in the purslane-treated group. Nevertheless, it is still within the normal range (6–7), indicating that there is no potential risk of rumen acidosis. Collectively, the results demonstrated that purslane positively affected ruminal microbial composition and provided health benefits for the ruminal environment, particularly for VFAs production in lactating dairy goats.

Serum biochemical indexes can reflect metabolic status and physiological function of animals to a certain degree. NEFA, TG and D3-H, as indicators of aspects of energy status, represent the magnitude of fat mobilization from body reserves (LeBlanc, 2010). In the current study, purslane elicited an increase in serum NEFA, TG, and D3-H contents, possibly contributing to alleviation of negative energy balance induced by the postpartum period, and thereby meeting the energy requirements of dairy goats. During the course of lactation, mastitis is a common disease, causing considerable economic losses to the dairy goat industry. It has been widely assumed that mastitis pathophysiology is strongly implicated in an oxidative stress and inflammatory response (Khan et al., 2023). One series of studies have suggested that bioactive compounds from plants, such as polyphenols and tannins, could improve the health of ruminants through enhancing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacities (Ma et al., 2021; Santillo et al., 2022). Similarly, purslane significantly enhanced serum SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities, which was indicative of an enhanced antioxidant effect. Importantly, SOD and GSH-Px activities had negative correlations with Firmicutes, Butyrivibrio, and [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group in the rumen. Therefore, it is plausible that the decreased abundance of these two genera is associated with purslane-enhanced antioxidant capacity, which contributed to a decreased susceptibility to diseases in lactating goats.

A wide spectrum of scientific studies had proposed that gut/rumen microbiome could affect host health by regulating host metabolome (Xue et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023a). In this study, purslane exhibited a notably positive impact on anti-inflammatory metabolites in serum of dairy goats, including butanoic acid, 15-oxo-5z,8z,11z,13e-eicosatetraenoic acid, trans-vaccenic acid, and papaverine. Butanoic acid could inhibit inflammatory responses through decreasing the expression of inflammation-related factors (Couto et al., 2020). A derivative of eicosatetraenoic acid; 15-oxo-5z,8z,11z,13e-eicosatetraenoic acid, is generated by lipoxygenase oxidation. Extensive research has highlighted the potential of eicosatetraenoic acid and its derivatives to attenuate cardiovascular complications, cancers, and inflammatory diseases (Zhou et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023a). Papaverine is an alkaloid that played anti-inflammatory effects in various pathological conditions, such as neuroinflammation and Parkinson's disease (Lee et al., 2019). Trans-vaccenic acid is a naturally occurring trans-fatty acids, produced by bacterial isomerases through biohydrogenation of unsaturated fatty acids in the rumen (Ganguly and Pierce, 2015). It has been commonly reported that trans-vaccenic acid possessed anti-inflammatory properties and could mitigate metabolic complications (Cristina et al., 2025). Furthermore, isocitric acid in serum was down-regulated following purslane treatment, which is an intermediate metabolite of tricarboxylic acid cycle, and the decrease of which was assumed to be associated with the improvement of systemic inflammation (Hong et al., 2017). Briefly, the alterations in serum metabolites induced by purslane contributed to the enhancement in overall health of dairy goats. In addition, it should be noted that there were significant correlations between serum metabolites and rumen microbiota. For example, papaverine and trans-vaccenic acid were inversely correlated with the ruminal richness of U29-B03. Isocitric acid was positively related to the richness of WCH-B1 in the present study. These data further demonstrated that supplementation of purslane changed serum metabolites mediated by rumen microbiota in dairy goats, which promoted the health of dairy goats.

The effectiveness of plants and active botanical ingredients in regulating production performance, particularly milk quality, has been extensively demonstrated in cows and goats (Zhao et al., 2022; Banakar et al., 2023; Di Meo et al., 2023). It has been revealed that linseed supplementation increased milk fat percentage in Cilentana grazing goats (Musco et al., 2022). Contrary to what was found in this experiment, purslane decreased milk fat rate. This variability was probably due to differences in the chemical composition of plants, with linseed containing high levels of alfa-linoleic acid, while purslane does not. Furthermore, purslane elevated IgM content in milk, which negatively correlated with the proportions of Firmicutes, [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group, Butyrivibrio, and Acinetobacter in the rumen. Meanwhile, these findings suggested that purslane positively influenced various milk metabolites that exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The metabolite 5-aminolevulinic, an essential precursor of heme, has been utilized as a dietary supplement in the management of inflammatory bowel disease (Yadav et al., 2021). Flindersine is a functional natural extract isolated from medicinal plants such as Toddalia asiatica, which had antioxidative, antilipidemic, and antidiabetic bioactivities in type 2 diabetic rats (Irudayaraj et al., 2022). Didymin, a flavonoid glycoside, has been identified as a potential agent for preventing cardiovascular complications in diabetes through enhancing antioxidative and anti-inflammatory actions (Shukla et al., 2018). Preclinical research has shown that didymin attenuated ulcerative colitis by promoting the conversion of pro-inflammatory M1-like to anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophage phenotype through fatty acid oxidation (Lv et al., 2021). Moreover, pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that purslane impacted pyruvate metabolism. According to an earlier study, pyruvate, as a crucial intermediate in glucose metabolism, had free-radical scavenging activity and inhibited inflammatory cytokines, thereby presenting powerful antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Das, 2006). The observed increase in beneficial metabolites in milk suggested a potential role in mitigating inflammatory disorders among consumers. Additionally, significant positive or negative correlations between ruminal microbiota and milk metabolome were identified. Remarkably, the presence of flindersine and didymin was negatively correlated with the relative abundance of U29-B03, indicating a potential contribution of decreased U29-B03 to the secretion of antioxidant substances in milk. Overall, present results showed that the improvement of milk quality caused by purslane is closely related to ruminal microbiota.

Finally, through high resolution analysis for milk, this study found ten up-regulated compounds such as dimethachlor ethanesulfonic acid, phenazine-1-carboxylate, trans-traumatic acid, dimethachlor, linalool oxide, and 3-Aminoadipic acid, were identified in the purslane group compared with the control group. Furthermore, purslane components, including trans-traumatic acid, 3-aminoadipic acid, biotin sulfone, and linalool oxide, were excreted into milk in this study. Linalool oxide, a monoterpene commonly found in citrus cultivars, has been shown to possess inhibitory effects on inflammation cytokines, suggesting its potential as an anti-inflammatory agent (Yang et al., 2023b). Here, it is reasonable to suspect that linalool oxide is one of the active constitutes in purslane. However, the biological functions of trans-traumatic acid, 3-aminoadipic acid, and biotin sulfone derived from plant extracts have not yet been elucidated currently. Additional research is needed to ascertain the efficacy of these three substances as active components of purslane.

Notably, the potential toxic effects caused by high dose of purslane cannot be ignored. A previous study indicated that feeding purslane to goats at a dosage of 5 g/kg of body weight or allowing unrestricted access for 15 to 40 days, resulted in severe clinical symptoms and even led to animal death (Obied et al., 2003). This phenomenon was linked to nitrate accumulation at toxic levels within the animals' systems. High doses of purslane was essential for inducing experimental poisoning, indicating that, similar to other nitrate-containing plants, excessive consumption of purslane is a crucial factor in the occurrence of the poisoning (O'Hara and Fraser, 1975). Therefore, in this study, a lower content of purslane (25 g/kg of basal diet) was introduced to dairy goats. Throughout the experiment, no behavior abnormalities were found, which underscores the safety of using purslane within a controlled and reasonable dosage range. Based on these findings, it is strongly recommended that farmers carefully regulate the quantity of purslane incorporated into their animals' diets to prevent potential health issues arising from overconsumption. By employing purslane as a feed additive in a scientific and judicious manner, its beneficial properties can be effectively harnessed without compromising the health of the livestock.

Conclusion

In conclusion, supplementation with purslane in diets decreased harmful bacterial profiles and increased VFAs contents in the rumen, which benefited ruminal ecology in dairy goats. Furthermore, purslane altered serum metabolomics, which was associated with the enhanced antioxidant capacity. Milk IgM content and bioactive metabolites with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory abilities, including 5-aminolevulinic acid, pyruvate, flindersine, and didymin, were increased in purslane-treated goats. Spearman's correlation analysis showed a clear correlation between the differential bacterial taxa and antioxidant enzymes, and milk performance, suggesting that ruminal microbiota may be responsible for these positive results induced by purslane. In addition, it was noteworthy that several purslane components such as linalool oxide were released into the milk of dairy goats fed with purslane, which will be beneficial for human health. This study presented a potential opportunity to meet the increasing market demand for health-conscious products.
