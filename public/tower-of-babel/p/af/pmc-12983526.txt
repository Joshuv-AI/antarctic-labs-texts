Innate immune functions of bitter taste receptors across physiological systems
Authors: Xinyao Huang, Xiaoxuan Yu, Yingjin Li, Shuhan Chang, Rongle Wei, Dan Zhao, Zhe Zhao, Zhiguang Sun, Lei Huang, Minye Qu, Ziyun Li, Renjun Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) were initially regarded as sensors for potentially toxic substances, but accumulating evidence indicates that they also play an important role in the regulation of innate immunity. The aim of this study is to summarize the major immunological functions of bitter taste receptors and to clarify their relevance across different physiological systems.Main bodyCurrent studies suggest that bitter taste receptors contribute to microbial sensing and immune defense in the respiratory tract, maintain microbial homeostasis and antimicrobial activity in the digestive system, and participate in inflammatory regulation and local immune balance in the musculoskeletal and reproductive systems. In addition, they are involved in immune-related processes in the nervous, circulatory, endocrine, and urinary systems.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings indicate that bitter taste receptors act as widely distributed immune-sensing molecules that link environmental stimuli to innate immune responses, providing a broader framework for understanding multisystem immune defense mechanisms.Keywords: Bitter taste receptors, Innate immunity, Immune regulation, Inflammation, TAS2Rs

Introduction

The Theory of Evolution posits that species are mutable, organisms evolve, and natural selection drives the evolution of life. In the fierce struggle for survival, plants, unable to actively evade danger, evolved various toxins to prevent predation. To survive, animals gradually developed a diversity of Bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) to identify these toxic substances. Humans with the ability to recognize bitterness were able to avoid toxic foods, allowing them to survive and eventually dominate their populations. Thus, the ability to detect bitterness is not only a key aspect of biological evolution but also reflects its significance in survival strategies. The old proverb “bitter medicine is good for you” reveals the importance of bitterness in medicine, as many traditional medicines are bitter due to their active components that combat diseases and promote health. Recent studies have shown that TAS2Rs are not limited to the gustatory system but are also expressed in various tissues, including immune cells, airway epithelial cells, and the digestive tract, where they play roles in multiple physiological systems [1–4]. These receptors can detect and respond to harmful external substances, activating the body’s defense mechanisms. For instance, in the respiratory and digestive systems, TAS2Rs can detect the presence of pathogens or harmful compounds, activating immune cells for a rapid response to infection. These findings indicate that the function of TAS2Rs extends far beyond traditional taste regulation, involving broader physiological regulation and likely contributing to innate immune responses. However, it is important to note that current evidence is largely derived from expression profiling and pharmacological activation studies, while direct molecular and genetic evidence confirming causality remains limited.

Innate immune responses are the first line of defense against microbial invasion, with core mechanisms relying on pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which recognize highly conserved microbial structures (known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns, or PAMPs) or host tissue damage-associated molecules (DAMPs), thereby initiating the host’s defense response [5, 6]. PRRs are widely distributed in innate immune cells, including macrophages and dendritic cells, effectively promoting immune responses and tissue repair by recognizing pathogens and damage signals [7, 8].

Although significant progress has been made in research on TAS2Rs, many mysteries remain regarding their specific mechanisms in innate immune responses. Most existing studies focus on specific TAS2R subtypes or certain cell types, lacking a comprehensive analysis and review. Therefore, this study aims to provide an in-depth review of the role of TAS2Rs in innate immunity, with a particular focus on their mechanisms of action in eight major systems—the respiratory system, digestive system, musculoskeletal system, reproductive system, nervous system, circulatory system, endocrine system, and urinary system. By systematically summarizing the findings of various studies, this paper seeks to reveal the critical role of TAS2Rs in regulating innate immune responses across different systems, exploring their potential signaling pathways and targeting mechanisms, and providing new perspectives for future research and clinical applications (Fig. 1).

TAS2Rs in respiratory system

TAS2Rs play a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis in the airways and defending against external pathogens. As part of the upper airway’s intrinsic immune system, they activate immune responses by recognizing pathogen-associated molecules, promoting the production of NO, enhancing ciliary movement, and stimulating the release of antimicrobial substances [9–11]. In the innate immune response, they enable a rapid reaction to pathogenic stimuli, triggering a downstream immune cascade that strengthens the physical and chemical barriers of the airway. Moreover, TAS2Rs regulate inflammation through a dual mechanism: inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory factors while modulating the production of anti-inflammatory factors to maintain immune balance [12, 13]. Isolated solitary chemosensory cells (SCCs) also enhance immune defense through neurogenic inflammation and antimicrobial actions [14]. A detailed explanation of these processes is provided below, highlighting the potential of TAS2Rs.

Innate immune defense of the upper respiratory tract: synergistic roles of the mucociliary system and immune molecules

The respiratory system serves as the body’s first line of defense against external pathogens, primarily comprising the conducting and respiratory zones. The conducting zone includes the nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi, while the respiratory zone is mainly composed of alveoli. As the first site exposed to external air, the nasal cavity and sinuses must establish a robust innate immune defense mechanism to maintain a sterile environment in the lower respiratory tract and lung parenchyma. In addition, to facilitate gas exchange, a complex defense mechanism exists in the upper and lower respiratory tracts to maintain the sterility of the lungs [15, 16]. Innate immunity provides the first line of defense for the respiratory tract against microbial pathogens [17]. Innate immunity in the upper respiratory tract relies heavily on the mucociliary clearance system [18]. Goblet cells in the airway columnar epithelium secrete a viscous gel that traps pathogens and debris, preventing their penetration into the lower respiratory tract. Simultaneously, cilia perform rhythmic movements to expel the trapped particles and pathogens. In addition, airway epithelial cells secrete cytokines and chemokines, such as IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP, to activate downstream immune cascades [7, 19, 20]. In summary, the innate immunity of the airways is primarily concentrated in the upper respiratory tract, with mechanisms dependent on the mucociliary clearance system, where mucus traps pathogens and cilia clear them [21]. At the same time, immune molecules secreted by airway epithelial cells effectively activate immune responses, thereby enhancing overall defense [22].

The role mechanism of TAS2Rs in pathogen defense of the upper respiratory tract: from pathogen recognition to enhanced barrier function

TAS2Rs play a crucial role in triggering host immune defenses by recognizing pathogen-associated molecules. Lactones such as acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs), secreted by bacteria, play an important role in bacterial quorum sensing. When the concentration of AHLs reaches a certain threshold, bacteria begin to form biofilms to resist the host immune system. However, before biofilm formation, TAS2Rs in the airway epithelium can detect these compounds and rapidly activate innate immune responses to prevent the spread of pathogens [9]. Quinolone quorum sensing molecules also trigger immune defense mechanisms by activating TAS2Rs, further enhancing host defenses [10]. One of the unique physiological features of TAS2Rs is their rapid response. Within seconds to minutes, TAS2Rs bind to pathogen molecules like AHLs, initiating downstream immune signaling pathways, quickly triggering immune responses, and releasing antimicrobial substances. This makes TAS2Rs a key front-line defense against foreign pathogens in the upper respiratory tract [23, 24].

Beyond recognizing pathogen molecules, TAS2Rs promote host defense responses through various mechanisms. For example, the activation of TAS2R38 triggers calcium signaling pathways that promote the production of NO. NO not only accelerates the frequency of ciliary beating, enhancing the physical barrier function of the airway epithelium, but also promotes the phagocytic activity of mononuclear macrophages (MΦs), thereby enhancing immune responses at the site of inflammation [11, 25]. Additionally, activation of TAS2R14 triggers intracellular calcium elevation and nitric oxide (NO) production, leading to increased ciliary beat frequency and enhanced mucociliary clearance. These effects resemble the responses observed with TAS2R38 stimulation and support a role for TAS2R14 in regulating airway innate immunity [11].

The activation of TAS2Rs strengthens both the physical and chemical barriers of the airways. A core mechanism is the production of large amounts of NO, a potent antimicrobial molecule that directly damages the cell membranes and organelles of pathogens, inhibiting their growth and reproduction [26]. Furthermore, NO enhances the host’s immune defense through multiple pathways. First, the activation of TAS2R38 and TAS2R14 leads to NO production [11, 27]. NO promotes the diffusion of antimicrobial substances such as lactoferrin, lysozyme, and defensins onto the airway mucosal surface, exerting synergistic antimicrobial effects. These antimicrobial substances destroy bacterial cell walls, lyse bacteria, and inhibit their metabolic activities, significantly enhancing the host’s resistance to pathogens [28]. In addition, NO works synergistically with other reactive oxygen species, such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, to amplify the pathogen-killing effect [23, 29]. Interestingly, studies have shown that MΦs also express calcium-signaling-related TAS2Rs. Upon activation, these receptors stimulate endothelial and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (e/nNOS), driving NO production and regulating the balance of intracellular signaling molecules cGMP and cAMP [30]. Specifically, TAS2R inhibit the production of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) via Gα and phosphodiesterase (PDE). Notably, cAMP suppresses phagocytosis. Given that cAMP inhibits phagocytosis, when macrophages (MΦ) maintain a reduced cAMP level and are co-stimulated with agonists that elevate cAMP levels, the Gβγ subunit activates phospholipase C (PLC). This activation leads to the production of inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and calcium (Ca²⁺), which in turn triggers the generation of nitric oxide (NO). NO then activates soluble guanylate cyclase (SGC) to produce cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), thereby directly enhancing phagocytosis [31]. Further clinical studies have revealed a close association between TAS2R38 genotypes and susceptibility to Gram-negative bacterial infections. TAS2R38 is considered an independent risk factor for chronic sinusitis [32, 33]. These findings suggest that, in addition to TAS2R38 significantly promoting NO production, TAS2R4 and TAS2R16 also trigger NO generation through specific taste signaling pathways in response to their ligands in a dose-dependent manner. These three TAS2Rs are all expressed in human nasal epithelial ciliated cells [2].

Although PKC regulates airway signaling, TAS2R14 does not directly activate PKC. In nasal epithelium, neuropeptide Y (NPY) activates PKC via NPY2R, suppressing TAS2R14-induced nitric oxide production and ciliary beating without altering calcium responses [34]. Some flavones activate TAS2R14 and enhance calcium signaling, NO production, and ciliary motility, but their PKC-inhibitory, anti-inflammatory effects occur through parallel pathways, not TAS2R14 [11]. Flufenamic acid, a potent TAS2R14 agonist, shows strong effects in airway models, though its efficacy versus flavones has not been systematically compared [35]. These findings indicate that PKC modulation is not a universal consequence of TAS2R14 activation and highlight tissue-specific signaling differences, suggesting TAS2R14 functions diverge across physiological systems.

In summary, TAS2Rs significantly enhance the physical and chemical barrier functions of the airways by rapidly recognizing pathogen molecules, activating innate immune mechanisms, and enhancing ciliary movement, NO production, and the release of antimicrobial substances [36]. This dual mechanism allows the airway epithelium to defend against pathogen invasion more effectively, thereby maintaining host health.

Dual role of TAS2Rs in respiratory immune regulation: balancing pro‑ and anti‑inflammatory responses

Research indicates that TAS2Rs not only play a role in physical and chemical barriers against bacteria in the upper respiratory tract but also in regulating the host immune response. Their activation can regulate inflammatory responses through various pathways, influencing the intensity and direction of immune system reactions [37]. TAS2R downstream signaling pathways have been confirmed to be related to inflammatory pathways, particularly with a complex relationship involving TNF and IL-10 [13]. TAS2R agonists (e.g., quinine, denatonium, sulfasalazine, colchicine, strychnine, chloroquine, erythromycin, o-phenanthroline, ofloxacin, and calyptodin) have been shown to inhibit LPS-induced pro-inflammatory responses. In lung macrophage models, these agonists effectively reduced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, CCL3, and CXCL8, indicating that TAS2R activation may have anti-inflammatory effects. This process may involve inhibiting specific inflammatory signaling pathways, reducing the transmission of pro-inflammatory signals, and thereby regulating the strength of the host’s immune response. However, it is worth noting that while TAS2R activation inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, it also suppresses the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 [12]. This dual regulatory effect suggests that TAS2Rs are not simply anti-inflammatory molecules, and their mechanisms may be more complex. TAS2Rs may finely regulate the balance of inflammatory responses to prevent excessive immune activation or suppression, thereby protecting tissues from damage. Therefore, TAS2Rs may act through a “regulatory balance mechanism,” where on one hand, they inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines to reduce inflammation, and on the other, they limit excessive production of anti-inflammatory factors to prevent insufficient immune response leading to persistent infections or chronic inflammation. This regulatory mechanism highlights the complex role of TAS2Rs in maintaining immune homeostasis. Moreover, recent clinical studies propose intratonsillar immunotherapy (ITIT) as a novel approach for IgE-mediated airway allergies, offering faster symptom relief and fewer injections than conventional subcutaneous immunotherapy. ITIT enhances immune tolerance by increasing regulatory T cells and modulating cytokine profiles, while reducing eosinophil infiltration. These findings highlight a promising clinical application of immune regulation in the respiratory system, aligning with TAS2Rs’ role in balancing airway inflammation [38].

In conclusion, by activating multiple signaling pathways, TAS2Rs can inhibit the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and, to some extent, regulate the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines. This dual regulatory characteristic gives them a unique function in modulating immune responses, playing an important role in defending against infections and controlling inflammatory responses.

Immune functions of TAS2Rs in solitary chemosensory cells: neurogenic inflammation and antimicrobial mechanisms

SCCs are specialized cells in the upper respiratory tract, primarily including tuft cells and brush cells, which make up about 1% of the nasal epithelium [39]. These cells express TAS2Rs, and by recognizing bitter compounds (such as AHLs), they activate downstream signaling pathways, playing an important role in immune defense [40].

The activation of TAS2Rs in SCCs initially limits pathogen invasion through neurogenic inflammation. Stimulation of TAS2Rs in SCCs activates the trigeminal afferent nerves, leading to the release of acetylcholine (ACh) [41, 42]. ACh activates sensitive trigeminal nerve fibers, prompting the secretion of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P. Substance P activates the neurokinin 1 receptor (NK1R) in endothelial cells and mast cells, triggering degranulation and plasma extravasation, resulting in neurogenic inflammation, an effective defense against bacterial infections [14, 43]. Furthermore, in rodent models, the sensitization of the trigeminal nerve by bitter compounds can lead to reflexive reductions in respiratory rate, limiting continuous inhalation of pathogenic microorganisms and thus enhancing defense [44, 45]. Furthermore, TAS2R108 agonists reduced respiratory rate, suggesting that TAS2Rs expressed in brush cells may directly regulate respiration and enhance immune defense through the classical conduction pathway [46].

In addition to neurogenic inflammation, TAS2Rs in SCCs directly combat pathogens. In human nasal SCCs, TAS2R47 agonists (such as benzethonium chloride) can trigger intracellular calcium waves, which spread to neighboring cells through gap junctions, promoting the release of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) [47]. These AMPs, such as β-defensin 1 and β-defensin 2, exhibit significant antimicrobial effects against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [44, 48–51].

The innate immune functions of TAS2Rs in the respiratory system are primarily mediated by the canonical Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃/Ca²⁺ pathway. Activation by pathogen molecules triggers calcium influx through this pathway, stimulating nitric oxide production and directly enhancing ciliary clearance and antimicrobial activity [9–11, 25, 27]. In contrast, the Gα/PDE/cAMP pathway serves a more modulatory role: within immune cells, TAS2Rs engage this pathway to reduce cyclic adenosine monophosphate levels [30]. Since cyclic adenosine monophosphate suppresses phagocytosis, this reduction synergizes with calcium signaling to ultimately enhance macrophage phagocytic function via the nitric oxide/soluble guanylate cyclase/cyclic guanosine monophosphate axis [31]. This indicates that extra-oral TAS2Rs do not employ novel tissue-specific signaling pathways; rather, their functions rely on tissue-specific cooperation between these two classic pathways—the Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃/Ca²⁺ pathway dominates rapid defense responses in epithelial cells, while the Gα/PDE/cAMP pathway fine-tunes functional balance in immune cells [9–11, 30, 31]. Such division of labor enables TAS2Rs to flexibly regulate a broad spectrum of immune processes, from physical barrier enhancement to inflammatory balance [23, 24, 36] (Fig. 2).

In the respiratory system, AHLs produced by Gram-negative bacteria activate TAS2Rs in airway epithelial cells, which are coupled with heterotrimeric G protein subunits. The gustducin G protein dissociates into its α, GANT3, and βγ subunits. The βγ subunits activate PLCβ2, leading to the release of Ca²⁺, which results in an increased Ca²⁺ concentration from inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3)-sensitive calcium stores. This activates the transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 5 (TRPM5), altering the membrane potential (depolarization), and increases ciliary beat frequency (CBF) in the airway. Simultaneously, calcium signaling can further activate nitric oxide synthase (NOS), leading to the production of large amounts of NO. NO accelerates ciliary movement through the SGC and protein kinase G (PKG) pathways, enhancing the clearance of foreign particles and strengthening the physical barrier of the airways. TAS2R inhibit the production of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) via Gα and phosphodiesterase (PDE). Notably, cAMP suppresses phagocytosis. Given that cAMP inhibits phagocytosis, when macrophages (MΦ) maintain a reduced cAMP level and are co-stimulated with agonists that elevate cAMP levels, the Gβγ subunit activates phospholipase C (PLC). This activation leads to the production of inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and calcium (Ca²⁺), which in turn triggers the generation of nitric oxide (NO). NO then activates SGC to produce cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), thereby directly enhancing phagocytosis. Moreover, NO signaling promotes the migration of MΦs toward airway epithelial cells, activating their phagocytic function to engulf pathogens. NO itself directly disrupts the cell membrane and organelles of invading pathogens, inhibiting their growth and reproduction

Bitter compounds or bitter receptor agonists stimulate TAS2Rs on SCCs in nasal epithelium. Through downstream signaling pathways, they trigger intracellular calcium waves, which propagate through gap junctions to adjacent cells, inducing the release of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), including β-defensin 1 and β-defensin 2, to combat bacteria. Furthermore, the calcium response leads to the release of ACh. ACh triggers the stimulation of acetylcholine-sensitive peptidergic trigeminal nerve fibers, which secrete calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P. Substance P activates neurokinin 1 receptors (NK1R) on mast cells, leading to degranulation and plasma extravasation, causing neurogenic inflammation that combats bacterial infection.

Additionally, in rodents, the release of ACh can reduce breathing rates via neuroregulation, limiting the body’s continued inhalation of pathogens.

TAS2Rs in digestive system

TAS2Rs play a crucial role in maintaining gut health and immune balance through mechanisms involving various cell types in the digestive system. These receptors are expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, enteroendocrine cells (EECs), tuft cells, Paneth cells, goblet cells, microfold cells, and gingival solitary chemosensory cells (gSCCs) [52]. By activating neurogenic inflammatory responses and promoting the release of antimicrobial peptides, TAS2Rs effectively protect the digestive tract from harmful substances. Additionally, in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), these receptors regulate immune balance, helping to reduce the risk of excessive inflammatory responses, thus supporting intestinal health and structural stability.

Innate immune regulation of TAS2Rs in the digestive system: multicellular collaboration and pathogen defense

TAS2Rs are expressed in multiple cell types within the human digestive system, including intestinal epithelial cells, enteroendocrine cells (EECs), tuft cells, Paneth cells, goblet cells, and microfold cells. They activate innate immune responses through interactions between the epithelium, subepithelial layers, afferent nerve fibers, and the brain [48]. In tuft cells, the activation of TAS2Rs triggers local immune responses to clear invading pathogens [1, 53]. TAS2Rs may induce proliferation of tuft cells, protect colonic epithelial integrity, and enhance immune defense through the classical taste transduction pathway [54]. In EECs, TAS2R activation leads to the secretion of anorexigenic hormones such as cholecystokinin (CCK), regulating taste aversion and delaying gastric emptying [48]. During parasitic infection, tuft cells secrete interleukin-25 (IL-25), which activates type II innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), enhancing immune defenses [48, 55]. These mechanisms play an essential role in repairing damaged intestinal epithelium in ulcerative colitis [56]. Furthermore, goblet cells produce mucus layers that protect the intestines, while Paneth cells secrete defensins to regulate microbial density and protect intestinal stem cells [48, 57, 58]. Through the actions of TAS2Rs, these mechanisms collectively maintain gut health and enhance innate immune responses.

Notably, gSCCs recognize a variety of TAS2Rs, including TAS2R118, TAS2R119, TAS2R126, TAS2R134, TAS2R135, TAS2R137, TAS2R138, and TAS2R143. This recognition activates downstream signaling cascades, triggering innate immune responses [59]. Animal experiments and in vitro studies suggest that oral cells expressing TAS2Rs can generate innate immune responses upon detecting bacterial signals, regulating oral microbial homeostasis [60, 61].

Roles of TAS2Rs in the digestive tract: immune balance, inflammatory bowel disease, and antimicrobial peptide release

TAS2Rs play vital roles in multiple cells within the digestive system, regulating neurogenic inflammatory responses through the release of acetylcholine and cholecystokinin, thereby protecting the digestive tract from harmful substances. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), TAS2Rs regulate immune balance, reducing the risk of excessive inflammatory responses. Furthermore, they enhance the gut’s antimicrobial capabilities by inducing the release of antimicrobial peptides, maintaining digestive system health and stability. TAS2Rs have paracrine functions in various intestinal cells. Upon stimulation by bitter compounds, these receptors trigger intracellular Ca2+ release, leading to the release of ACh or CCK. ACh activates afferent nerve fibers containing nociceptors, inducing neurogenic inflammatory responses as a protective mechanism against potential harmful substances in the gut [62, 63].

In IBD, the role of TAS2Rs and their interaction with the immune system becomes especially important. Studies show that α-defensins produced by Paneth cells are significantly reduced in patients with ileal Crohn’s disease, while ulcerative colitis patients exhibit a lack of mucus layers. These changes weaken the intestinal barrier, making it more susceptible to bacteria and other harmful substances. Bitter chemoreceptors in the colon, by interacting with bacterial metabolites or regulating metabolism, directly participate in the modulation of inflammation during IBD. Research indicates that α-gustducin (responsible for transducing extracellular taste signals to the intracellular environment) knockout mice with deficient taste signaling exhibit more severe tissue damage and excessive inflammatory responses when facing DSS-induced colitis [62]. This is due to elevated expression of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and reduced expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-5, IL-10, and IL-13 [62]. This suggests that under normal conditions, TAS2Rs help regulate immune balance in the intestines by sensing and responding to bitter substances, preventing excessive inflammatory responses. A lack of functional taste signaling may lead to a skewed immune response toward a more intense type I inflammation rather than a milder type II inflammation [64, 65]. Specifically, parasitic infections activate tuft cells in the small intestine, which express TAS2Rs. This triggers a positive feedback loop of IL-25 through the TAS2R downstream signaling pathway, leading to the expansion of ILC2s and IL-13-driven proliferation of tuft and goblet cells [48, 66–68]. Type I immune responses are typically associated with more severe and destructive inflammation, while type II responses tend to be milder and help maintain normal intestinal function and structure. Furthermore, host-microbe cross-talk contributes to intestinal immune tolerance through transferrin regulation. Bacterial products up-regulate transferrin, which interacts with CD14 to inhibit pattern recognition receptor signaling and promote tolerance. Reduced transferrin in germ-free mice and ulcerative colitis impairs tolerance, while restoration of microbiota or transferrin supplementation rescues it, revealing a novel complement to TAS2R-mediated immune regulation [69].

Moreover, bitter agonists like salicin have demonstrated potential in inhibiting inflammatory responses by activating the TAS2R143/gustducin signaling pathway. Salicin suppresses the expression of inflammatory factors induced by LPS (lipopolysaccharides) in gingival fibroblasts, reducing neutrophil infiltration and mitigating periodontal tissue inflammation and damage, thereby protecting the digestive system from harmful substances [70]. It can also activate TAS2R16 in human gingival fibroblasts in vitro, triggering downstream Gα-gustducin and PLCβ2 signaling, which evokes a robust cytosolic Ca²⁺ response and subsequently suppresses LPS-induced intracellular cAMP accumulation and NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, promoting the resolution of inflammation [71]. Meanwhile, TAS2R14 is involved in innate immunity by regulating the internalization and survival of gram-positive bacteria in gingival epithelial cells [72]. TAS2R50, by recognizing and responding to specific bitter or potentially harmful substances, may participate in the regulation of innate immune cell activity. Resveratrol (RSV), through TAS2R50 activation, reduces the release of IL-6 induced by LPS in human gingival fibroblasts (HGF-1), indicating the important role of TAS2Rs in regulating inflammatory responses and their involvement in immune cell activation, which may further regulate innate immune responses [73, 74].

Additionally, TAS2Rs promote the gut’s defensive response by inducing the release of antimicrobial peptides. Studies have found that in the crypts of the human jejunum, particularly in obese individuals, the bitter agonist benzethonium chloride (DB) induces the release of antimicrobial peptides such as α-defensin 5 and REG3A [75]. These antimicrobial peptides play a crucial role in the gut by effectively inhibiting the growth and proliferation of harmful bacteria. Furthermore, DB can regulate the expression of other innate immune factors, further enhancing the gut’s immune defenses [75]. This multi-layered immune response mechanism ensures the gut can respond rapidly to various challenges, effectively protecting overall health. Additionally, activated gSCCs secrete antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and other immune molecules in response to bitter signals, inhibiting the overgrowth of oral bacteria and maintaining the immune balance of the oral microbiota [59] (Fig. 3).

In the digestive system, bitter stimulation of TAS2Rs expressed in intestinal enteroendocrine cells triggers intracellular Ca²⁺ release, leading to the secretion of ACh or CCK. Acetylcholine activates the sensory neurons of the trigeminal nerve that contain nociceptors, inducing neurogenic inflammation, which acts as a protective mechanism against pathogens. Bitter receptor agonists (DB) stimulate TAS2Rs expressed in Paneth cells of the jejunal crypts in obese individuals, leading to the release of antimicrobial peptides (such as α-defensin 5 and REG3A) to combat pathogens. Parasites activate TAS2Rs expressed in tuft cells of the small intestine, triggering an IL-25-driven positive feedback loop through downstream TAS2R signaling pathways. This results in the amplification of ILC2 and tuft and goblet cell proliferation driven by IL-13

TAS2Rs in musculoskeletal system

In the Musculoskeletal system, innate immune signaling is important for removing damaged and apoptotic cells and for stimulating normal tissue repair and regeneration [76, 77]. The activation of TAS2Rs plays a crucial role in skeletal inflammation by inhibiting the production of various pro-inflammatory cytokines [78, 79]. Experiments have shown that TAS2R46 is expressed on the surface of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), while TAS2R38 is significantly expressed in infiltrating phagocytes during bacteria-induced osteomyelitis, participating in local host defense by binding to AHL-12 [80, 81]. Notably, while TAS2R46 activation in airway smooth muscle selectively inhibits Gq-mediated calcium signaling without inducing calcium release, the opposite is observed in human bone marrow MSCs. In MSCs, TAS2R46 agonists such as denatonium increase intracellular calcium release, accompanied by reduced cAMP, indicating active Gq pathway engagement consistent with calcium mobilization [80]. Additionally, activation of TAS2Rs not only increases intracellular calcium concentration but also inhibits the secretion of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and may regulate the activity of calcium-sensing receptors (CaSR), potentially affecting the secretion and proliferation of the parathyroid gland [82, 83].

Calcium signaling mechanism of TAS2Rs activation in osteoblast proliferation and differentiation

Activation of TAS2Rs affects intracellular calcium concentration through the PLC/IP3 pathway, significantly influencing osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Increased Ca2+ in osteoblasts can induce cell proliferation via calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase (CaMK) pathways [84]. In this process, calcium activates calmodulin (CaM), which further activates CaM kinase II (CaMKII) and calcineurin (Cn). CaM, when bound to Ca2+, plays various roles in regulating calcium pumps and channels, which are crucial for osteoblast differentiation. The activation of Ca2+/CaM allows Cn to dephosphorylate and bind to nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT) transcription factors, which then translocate to the nucleus to regulate gene expression. Moreover, extracellular calcium stimulation of CaSRs activates multiple targets, including phospholipase C, protein kinase C, extracellular signal-regulated kinase, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, AKT, and the cAMP/protein kinase A pathway, all of which play essential roles in regulating osteocyte proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [85–87].

The role of TAS2Rs activation in pathogen recognition and defensin production

TAS2R38 plays a key role in pathogen recognition, primarily through G-protein-dependent signaling pathways. This pathway is associated with the scaffold protein IQGAP1, demonstrating its significant role in cytoskeletal reorganization and cell morphology. IQGAP1 integrates signaling cascades and is a crucial regulator of chemotaxis, consistent with observations of AHL-12-induced chemotaxis. AHL-12, a highly lipophilic quorum-sensing molecule produced by bacteria, can quickly diffuse across the cell membrane and freely diffuse. Once TAS2R38 is activated, AHL-12 promotes chemotaxis and defense-related functions, attracting phagocytes to biofilm formation sites [88]. These phagocytes can clear planktonic bacteria before biofilm formation, effectively preventing infection spread. Thus, TAS2R38 acts as a novel PAMP receptor, participating in early immune responses and enhancing local host defense [81].

In addition to pathogen recognition, TAS2Rs are essential in innate immune defense against microbial attacks. Bitter receptor agonists promote the production of antimicrobial peptides through calcium waves. Antimicrobial peptides play a crucial role in innate immunity against infectious microbes such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi [89]. Defensins, a vital subfamily of antimicrobial peptides, can kill microorganisms by disrupting their cell membranes without relying on the cellular immune system. Studies have shown that osteoblasts can produce antimicrobial peptides like HBD-2 in osteomyelitis animal models, modulating immune responses [90, 91]. Varoga D. and colleagues confirmed the expression of HBD-3 in the mandible and cultured osteoblasts using RT-PCR and immunohistochemistry [92]. In cases of suppurative arthritis, antimicrobial peptides like HBD-2 were also found in joint cartilage [93]. Therefore, TAS2R38 not only plays an essential role in pathogen recognition but also may enhance the host’s immune defense by promoting the production of defensins.

Functions of TAS2Rs in neutrophil migration and bone marrow‑derived cells

The impact of TAS2Rs activation on neutrophil migration has been widely studied. Kobayashi and colleagues found that when TAS2R agonists were introduced to neutrophils derived from mouse bone marrow, functional TAS2R126/143 were expressed [94]. The results showed that TAS2R agonists enhanced CXCL2-induced neutrophil migration, accompanied by increased myosin light chain 2 (MLC2) phosphorylation. Pretreatment with a Rho-associated coiled-coil kinase (ROCK) inhibitor blocked neutrophil migration, whereas inhibiting the small GTPase RhoA had no effect [95]. Additionally, neutrophils isolated from TAS2R126/135/143-deficient mice showed significantly reduced migration capacity. These findings confirmed the role of TAS2R agonists in neutrophil migration and pMLC2 production, revealing the enhancement of TAS2R126/143-ROCK-MLC2-dependent signaling in regulating neutrophil migration [94, 96, 97].

Besides its role in neutrophils, TAS2Rs also play significant functions in bone marrow-derived cells. TAS2Rs are expressed in bone marrow (BM)-derived cells, where they sense changes in the BM microenvironment. The widely used bitter agonist denatonium has been shown to activate TAS2R in various cell types [98, 99]. Studies have extended these findings to show TAS2R expression in CD34 cells derived from umbilical cord blood (UCB), elucidating its role in the extrinsic regulation of hematopoietic stem cell functions [100, 101]. Salvestrini V. and colleagues demonstrated that acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells express fully functional TAS2R and that TAS2R plays a role in regulating leukemia cell function [102]. In summary, TAS2Rs not only play an essential role in neutrophil migration but also participate in regulating the functions of bone marrow-derived cells, significantly impacting immune responses and hematopoietic processes [103].

Within the musculoskeletal system, the present study clarifies the association of TAS2R functions with specific signaling routes. Activation of TAS2R46 in human mesenchymal stem cells increases intracellular calcium release accompanied by reduced cAMP, suggesting the involvement of the Gq pathway—part of the canonical Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃/Ca²⁺ cascade83. In contrast, the role of the Gα/PDE/cAMP pathway is not explicitly detailed. The downstream events initiated by this calcium signal are pivotal for functional execution: it regulates osteoblast proliferation and differentiation via pathways including calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinases [87–90], while a G-protein-dependent signaling mediated by TAS2R38 promotes phagocyte chemotaxis toward bacterial biofilm sites [84]. Furthermore, bitter receptor agonists promote antimicrobial peptide production via calcium waves, and neutrophil migration relies on the enhancement of the specific TAS2R126/143-ROCK-MLC2 signaling axis [92, 97, 99, 100]. Consequently, TAS2Rs in the musculoskeletal system do not employ entirely unique pathways; their innate immune functions primarily converge on the calcium signal triggered by the Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃/Ca²⁺ pathway. This initial signal acts as a common hub, connecting to divergent downstream effectors in different cell types to specifically coordinate diverse processes from bone metabolism to immune cell recruitment [83, 84, 87, 97]. (Fig. 4)

In the skeletal system, bitter receptor agonists activate TAS2Rs expressed in osteoblasts, triggering calcium signaling that causes CaM to bind with Ca²⁺, which then activates calcineurin (Cn). Cn binds to and dephosphorylates the nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) transcription factor. NFAT then translocates to the nucleus to regulate gene expression. Additionally, bitter receptors, through extracellular calcium stimulation, inhibit the activity of pro-inflammatory factors and regulate the activity of immune cells (such as leukocytes, mast cells, and macrophages). In bone marrow (BM)-derived macrophages, AHL-12 may utilize TAS2R38 to attract phagocytes to biofilm formation sites, clearing planktonic bacteria, preventing the spread of infection, and participating in early immune responses. Aebutin activates bitter receptors expressed in bone marrow-derived neutrophils, regulating neutrophil migration through TAS2R126/143-ROCK-MLC2 dependent signaling pathways.

TAS2Rs in reproductive system

In the reproductive system, TAS2Rs play a significant role, particularly in innate immune responses [9]. First, immunomodulation of the reproductive system maintains reproductive health by protecting the reproductive organs and spermatozoa, as well as through the activity of various immune cells and cytokines [104]. In male reproduction, research suggests that TAS2Rs may influence sperm chemotaxis and fertility [105, 106]. In contrast, in female reproduction, they are thought to impact the ovarian environment [107, 108] and have potential as therapeutic targets for preventing preterm labor, especially in immune defense mechanisms in the placenta, cervix, and vaginal opening [109, 110].

Immunomodulatory roles of TAS2Rs in male fertility and sperm motility

TAS2Rs play an essential role in regulating sperm function. Research shows that each sperm has a unique activation profile for TAS2R ligands [111]. They are expressed in spermatocytes, spermatogonia, sperm, and stromal cells, especially in the tail of sperm cells, which is likely linked to sperm motility during fertilization [112, 113]. Taste receptors may be involved in regulating male fertility, potentially impacting spermatogenesis, epididymal sperm maturation, sperm migration through the female reproductive tract, and/or the eventual fusion of sperm and egg [114, 115]. A study by Gentiluomo M. further confirmed the relationship between TAS2R14 and sperm motility, emphasizing the critical role of TAS2Rs in sperm production and movement [116, 117]. In addition, TAS2R143 may regulate the expression of blood-testis barrier tight junction proteins in testicular sertoli cells (SCs) through the NF-κB signaling pathway for maintaining the immune microenvironment. This may enhance new targets for the study of male reproductive immunity [118, 119]. More importantly, TAS1R has been shown to regulate male reproductive processes and participate in immunity through the PKA/CREB/StAR signaling pathway, the AMPK/NR4A1 signaling pathway, and the cAMP-PKA-SP1 pathway [120–122]. Whether male reproductive immunity can be regulated by activating TAS2R in the future remains to be investigated.

TAS2Rs in the female reproductive system: immune defense and fertility regulation

TAS2Rs are not only found in the male reproductive system but are also prominently expressed in the female reproductive system, especially in the ovaries, myometrium, placenta, cervix, and vaginal opening [107, 109]. In the ovaries, activation of ovarian taste receptors can attenuate cAMP levels while elevating cGMP concentrations. This activation may suppress ovarian progesterone production via the NO/cGMP and apoptotic signaling pathways [123]. In the myometrium, placenta, cervix, and vaginal opening, TAS2Rs primarily contribute to immune function.

Notably, despite advances in understanding peripheral roles of TAS2Rs, much of the research in the reproductive system has focused on males, leaving many aspects of female reproductive function underexplored. In response, Semplici B. and his team have focused on the role of taste receptors in female reproduction, promoting further research into the female reproductive system and fertilization. Studies indicate that TAS2Rs are expressed in somatic ovarian follicular cells, a finding that originated from research on granulosa and cumulus cells, revealing the potential functions of TAS2Rs in the ovaries [107]. Additionally, research has shown that oral administration of KDT501, a TAS2R108 ligand (humulone), effectively improves endocrine and metabolic disorders associated with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), increases ovarian mass, and improves reproductive function. Notably, KDT501 has shown greater efficacy than pioglitazone, a PPARγ activator [108, 124, 125]. Thus, TAS2Rs improve ovarian health by regulating the endocrine system and enhancing overall female health.

The role of TAS2Rs in the female reproductive system is also linked to uterine myometrium and placenta, particularly concerning preterm birth prevention and defense against external factors [126]. Current research on the human and mouse uterus primarily focuses on the myometrium, showing that bitter agents can activate downstream signaling components of TAS2Rs [109]. Specifically, TAS2R activation mediates the Gβγ/PLCβ/IP3 and Gα-gustducin/AC/cAMP pathways, reducing LTCC expression and inhibiting calcium influx induced by uterotonins via voltage-dependent calcium channels (VDCCs), which maintains uterine smooth muscle relaxation and helps prevent preterm labor [127–129]. In the syncytiotrophoblasts of the placenta, the high expression of TAS2R38 suggests that its activation may enhance innate immune defenses, similar to the activation of TAS2Rs in human upper respiratory tract cells, where they promote NO production through quorum sensing molecules (QSMs) to defend against pathogens [130, 131]. Additionally, TAS2R activation may trigger the release of CCK, which upregulates ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transport proteins, facilitating the expulsion of harmful compounds [109].

TAS2Rs are expressed not only in the uterus but also in the cervix and vaginal opening, where they contribute significantly to innate immunity. Research has found that TAS2R143/TAS2R135/TAS2R126 clusters may be expressed in the epithelium of the mouse vagina and cervix [132]. Based on this, it is hypothesized that invading pathogens in the vagina activate TAS2Rs in basal epithelial cells, promoting cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation into protective superficial epithelial cells, enhancing the defense barrier. In the cervix, hormone interaction with TAS2Rs increases cytoplasmic Ca2+, which stimulates mucin secretion, helping protect the mucosal barrier [109].

Although most research on the immune function of TAS2Rs has focused on the respiratory tract, the reproductive system is another crucial area. Like the respiratory system, the reproductive system has many epithelial mucosal tissues that play a role in innate immune responses. TAS2Rs, as part of the innate immune sensing mechanism, are expressed in the reproductive system and are closely related to sperm formation, follicle maturation, and possibly the detection of and defense against harmful factors during fertilization. However, the specific mechanisms of these roles remain largely undetermined, requiring further experimental research for verification.

TAS2Rs utilize both the canonical Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃ and Gα-gustducin/AC/cAMP pathways to exert their functions. In the myometrium, TAS2R activation is explicitly described as mediating both the Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃ and Gα-gustducin/AC/cAMP pathways to inhibit calcium influx and maintain uterine relaxation, thereby preventing preterm labor [127–129]. The downstream signals triggered by these pathways yield tissue-specific effects: in the ovaries, TAS2R activation may suppress progesterone production via the nitric oxide/cyclic guanosine monophosphate pathway [123]; in the cervix, calcium signaling stimulates mucin secretion to enhance the barrier [109]; and in the placenta, TAS2R38 likely promotes nitric oxide production by sensing quorum-sensing molecules to bolster immune defense [130, 131]. However, the present study has not yet elucidated the specific role of the Gα/PDE/cAMP pathway. Therefore, TAS2Rs in the reproductive system do not employ novel specific pathways; their innate immune functions primarily rely on the classic G-protein-coupled dual-signaling system (Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃/Ca²⁺ and Gα/AC/cAMP). By modulating local calcium signaling and cyclic nucleotide levels, this system coordinates immune-related processes ranging from uterine function maintenance to mucosal defense [109, 127–131] (Fig. 5).

In the syncytiotrophoblast of the female reproductive system, quorum sensing molecules (QSM) released by microorganisms may activate signaling pathways similar to those in the upper respiratory tract via TAS2R. This mechanism involves calcium signaling, which activates nitric oxide synthase (NOS) and promotes the production of large amounts of NO, which has antimicrobial effects. Additionally, TAS2R may trigger the release of CCK from cells expressing TAS2R, which in turn upregulates ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, promoting the expulsion of harmful compounds from the cells. In the vagina, invading pathogens may activate TAS2R in basal epithelial cells. Through the downstream signaling pathway of the gustducin G protein, which dissociates into its α, GANT3, and βγ subunits, the cells respond by migrating, proliferating, and differentiating into superficial protective epithelial cells, thus enhancing the defensive barrier. Meanwhile, in the cervix, hormones activate TAS2R in epithelial cells, leading to an increase in cytoplasmic Ca²⁺, which stimulates the secretion of mucins, helping to protect the mucosal barrier. In the ovaries, activation of taste receptors can respectively reduce cAMP and increase cGMP levels.

TAS2Rs in nervous system

TAS2Rs act as immune sentinels in the central nervous system (CNS), translating microbial and neurotoxic signals into innate immune responses. In humans, TAS2R14 is highly expressed in neural tissues, with cerebellar levels approximately 100 times higher than in tongue cells [133]. In mice, TAS2R1, TAS2R4, and TAS2R107 are also expressed in neural tissues [134]. Cerebrospinal fluid-contacting neurons (CSF-cN) express orphan TAS2Rs that regulate cytokine expression during bacterial infections, thereby enhancing CNS defense and improving host survival. Upon Streptococcus pneumoniae infection, CSF-cN respond strongly to bacterial supernatants and metabolites, releasing neurosecretions that limit bacterial spread, upregulate innate immune components, and prevent excessive neuroinflammation [135]. TAS2Rs’ ability to detect neurotoxins and microbial molecules contributes to protection against neuroinflammatory damage. However, dysregulation or overactivation of these receptors can increase oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, triggering neuroinflammation. Consequently, precise modulation of TAS2Rs in neurons is critical for mitigating neural damage and represents a potential target for CNS infection therapies [136–138].

TAS2Rs in the central nervous system: immune defense and neuroinflammation modulation

TAS2Rs function in both the peripheral and central nervous systems. Cerebrospinal fluid-contacting neurons express orphan TAS2Rs. These receptors enhance CNS defense by regulating cytokines during bacterial infection. In zebrafish infected with Streptococcus pneumoniae, these neurons respond strongly to bacterial products such as dimethyl disulfide. They release neurosecretory factors to limit bacterial spread. They also upregulate cytokines and complement proteins, boosting innate immunity. Orphan TAS2Rs detect bacterial metabolites like dimethyl disulfide. This detection further promotes immune responses mediated by these neurons [135]. Overall, during CNS infections, CSF-cN limit bacterial spread through direct immune responses and prevent excessive inflammation, promoting host survival. TAS2Rs’ responses to microbial molecules and neurotoxins prevent neuroinflammatory reactions, whereas downregulation or excessive activation increases oxidative stress and inflammasome signaling, activating astrocytes and other innate immune cells to release pro-inflammatory factors and exacerbate neuroinflammation [139–143]. Therefore, modulation of TAS2Rs can mitigate neuronal damage, control innate immune cell activation, and maintain CNS homeostasis, establishing these receptors as both frontline defenders against microbial and toxic challenges and key regulators of neuroinflammation [141, 144]. By regulating TAS2R activity, it is possible to control the activation of innate immune cells, avoiding excessive inflammatory responses. This offers a new direction for developing therapeutic strategies targeting neuroinflammation, especially in the prevention and treatment of CNS infections and related diseases (Fig. 6).

In the nervous system, under the condition of zebrafish larvae infected with Streptococcus pneumoniae, the contact of CSF-cN with bitter compounds activates TAS2R, leading to an increase in intracellular calcium ion (Ca²⁺) levels and promoting the release of peptides, cytokines, and complements, thereby potentially contributing to immune responses. In astrocytes, TAS2R, by recognizing quorum-sensing molecules or toxic metabolites released by pathogens, may activate the NLRP3 inflammasome through calcium response, directly affecting NF-κB; alternatively, it can activate NF-κB via signaling molecules like PDK1, Akt, mTORC1, and SIRT1, inducing the release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, resulting in neuroinflammation. Similarly, in neurons, TAS2R, upon recognizing quorum-sensing molecules or toxic metabolites, may activate NLRP3 and NF-κB via calcium response, producing inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which further trigger neuroinflammation

TAS2Rs in circulatory system

In the circulatory system, TAS2Rs are non-traditional receptors that integrate pathogen recognition with vascular and immune regulation. They are expressed in systemic arteries, vascular smooth muscle, cardiomyocytes, and immune cells including monocytes and M1/M2 macrophages, mediating antimicrobial responses and modulating vascular tone. TAS2R activation in vascular smooth muscle induces relaxation or contraction through IP3-mediated calcium release or other non-IP3-dependent mechanisms such as calcium channel opening [145]. For instance, dextromethorphan acts via TAS2R1 to induce pulmonary vasoconstriction and airway relaxation [146]. In the heart, TAS2R signaling influences coronary and systemic blood flow, affecting myocardial perfusion and cardiac function [128, 147].

Expression of TAS2Rs in the circulatory system

TAS2Rs are expressed in rat mesenteric and cerebral arteries, human omental arteries, and vascular smooth muscle, where they regulate blood flow through contraction or relaxation [127, 128, 145]. In immune cells, TAS2Rs including TAS2R10, TAS2R14, TAS2R30, and TAS2R46 are expressed in monocytes and M1/M2 macrophages [3]. These receptors enable immune cells to detect pathogens, initiate antimicrobial signaling, and coordinate inflammatory responses, demonstrating their dual role in vascular and immune regulation.

Innate immune functions of TAS2Rs in the circulatory system: immunomodulation and vascular responses

TAS2Rs play critical roles in innate immunity. They recognize pathogens, activate downstream signaling pathways, and trigger antimicrobial responses [128]. In cardiomyocytes, pathological TAS2R activation or signaling disruption inhibits Nrf-2 nuclear translocation, downregulating protective and detoxifying enzymes [1, 148, 149]. TAS2R signaling also promotes expression of inflammation-related genes through CaMKII and NF-κB/IκB pathways, and via NLRP3 induces IL-1β and IL-18 production, amplifying inflammatory responses [150, 151]. These mechanisms also impact immune cells in cardiac capillaries. Macrophages, as primary immune effectors, polarize to M1 under inflammatory conditions, secreting pro-inflammatory factors, whereas TAS2R activation or anti-inflammatory signals promote M2 polarization, suppressing inflammation and supporting tissue repair [3, 128]. TAS2R signaling also modulates neutrophils, natural killer cells, and cytotoxic T cells, collectively coordinating cardiac immune responses [128, 152]. For example, TAS2R46 protects monocytes and macrophages from oxidative stress while maintaining antimicrobial activity, emphasizing the essential role of TAS2Rs in cardiovascular immune homeostasis [3].

The specific signaling pathways of TAS2R activation vary by cell type. In vascular smooth muscle, TAS2R activation regulates vascular tone via IP3-mediated calcium release, suggesting its reliance on the canonical Gβγ/PLCβ/IP3/Ca²⁺ pathway [145]. However, in immune cells such as monocytes and macrophages, TAS2Rs participate in innate immune regulation, yet the text does not specify which G-protein-coupled pathway is involved [3]. In tissues like cardiomyocytes, TAS2R signaling promotes inflammation-related gene expression through the CaMKII and NF-κB/IκB pathways and induces cytokine production via the NLRP3 inflammasome [150, 151]. Therefore, TAS2Rs in the circulatory system do not employ novel tissue-specific signaling pathways. Instead, they selectively utilize specific branches of classic pathways depending on cell type, ultimately executing their innate immune and regulatory functions through conserved downstream effectors [145, 150, 151] (Fig. 7).

In the circulatory system, in cardiomyocytes, pathological activation of TAS2R or disruptions in its signaling pathway inhibit the nuclear translocation of Nrf-2, thereby downregulating the synthesis and secretion of protective and detoxifying enzymes. Concurrently, TAS2R activation promotes the expression of inflammation-related genes via the CaMK II and NF-κB/IκB signaling pathways and produces inflammatory factors like IL-1β and IL-18 through the NLRP3 pathway, further amplifying the inflammatory response and ultimately inducing neuroinflammation. Both of these effects influence immune cells in cardiac capillaries. In vascular smooth muscle cells, dextromethorphan activates TAS2R, leading to a significant increase in IP3 and Ca2+ levels, which in turn activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), resulting in an increase in the phosphorylated form of MLC (pMLC) and promoting the contraction of pulmonary artery smooth muscle, thereby affecting vascular contraction status and blood flow regulation. In monocytes/macrophages, TAS2R activation by absinthin reduces ROS and RNS levels via signaling pathways, providing antioxidant and antimicrobial effects

TAS2Rs in endocrine system

TAS2Rs play an important role in immune regulation within the endocrine system. TAS2R144 participates in detoxification at the blood-brain barrier in choroid plexus epithelial cells by regulating calcium ion channels, influencing the detection and clearance of harmful substances [153, 154]. TAS2R4 and TAS2R36 affect thyroid hormone synthesis in thyroid cells, potentially helping these cells adapt to external stimuli by influencing immune responses [155]. Additionally, the gene polymorphism of TAS2R42 is associated with thyroid hormone homeostasis, suggesting its potential role in endocrine immune regulation [156, 157].

Roles of TAS2Rs in the endocrine system: hormone‑immune interplay

In the endocrine system, various TAS2Rs subtypes have specific expressions and functions. TAS2R144 is expressed in the choroid plexus epithelial cells, where it is involved in detoxification functions at the blood-brain barrier by regulating calcium ion channels [153, 158]. TAS2R109 expression is regulated by estrogen, indicating its role in sex hormone regulation [153]. In thyroid cells, TAS2R4 and TAS2R36 are related to the regulation of thyroid function and can affect the synthesis of thyroid hormones [155]. Additionally, TAS2R42 gene polymorphism is closely associated with the homeostasis of thyroid hormones [156]. TAS2R38 is expressed in lymphocytes infiltrating the skin and regulates immune cell migration and function [159].

TAS2Rs expressed in intestinal and thyroid cells can influence hormone release [160]. For example, when intestinal TAS2Rs bind to carbohydrates, they stimulate the release of GLP-1, which helps maintain blood glucose homeostasis and plays a protective role [161, 162]. Bitter substances can directly trigger the secretion of GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) by stimulating intestinal endocrine cells, thereby modulating the function of gut immune cells [163–166]. Specifically, Isosinensetin stimulates the secretion of GLP-1 through activation of the hTAS2R50 and Gβγ-mediated signaling pathways [167, 168]. Additionally, TAS2Rs can activate the release of CCK, which helps regulate systemic immune balance [48, 109, 169]. The mechanism of CCK release by TAS2Rs activation is dependent on the Ca2+ inward flow signaling pathway [170]. Sex hormones regulate the expression of TAS2Rs at the blood-brain barrier, affecting their ability to detect and eliminate harmful substances. TAS2R144, expressed in choroid plexus epithelial cells, triggers intracellular calcium changes via the PLCβ2-IP3-TRPM5 signaling pathway, contributing to the detoxification function of the blood-brain barrier. Studies have shown that estrogen significantly downregulates the expression of TAS2R109, TAS2R144, and their related signaling genes through estrogen receptors, while progesterone may regulate these genes via non-classical receptor mechanisms, such as membrane receptors (mPR) [153, 171].

TAS2Rs are also expressed in thyroid cells, where they influence thyroid hormone synthesis by regulating thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)-dependent increases in intracellular calcium and iodine ion efflux [172]. TAS2R agonists (e.g., chloramphenicol and colchicine) can inhibit TSH-dependent calcium increases and iodine efflux, thereby affecting the production of thyroid hormones T3 and T4. Propylthiouracil (PTU), used to treat hyperthyroidism, is a known agonist of TAS2R4 and TAS2R36, suggesting a potential immune function for TAS2Rs in the thyroid [155]. Additionally, TAS2R polymorphisms may affect thyroid hormone homeostasis, as TAS2R42 gene polymorphism is associated with increased serum T4 levels [173]. This indicates that TAS2Rs may help thyroid cells adapt to external stimuli by detecting harmful compounds, indirectly influencing immune responses [156, 174]. It has also been shown that functionally impaired TAS2Rs negatively affect glucose homeostasis, which may influence the innate immune response [175].

In immune cells, TAS2R38 is expressed on lymphocytes infiltrating the skin. TAS2R38 agonists (e.g., phenylthiourea, PTC, and propylthiouracil, PROP) can dose-dependently inhibit lymphocyte migration. TAS2R38 may regulate lymphocyte migration in the skin by recognizing endogenous ligands [159]. Lymphocyte migration is a key step in reaching infection or inflammation sites to perform immune functions. Thus, by inhibiting lymphocyte migration, TAS2R38 may affect the distribution and function of lymphocytes in tissues like the skin, influencing the efficiency and intensity of innate immune responses.

TAS2R functions involve specific signaling pathways. In choroid plexus epithelial cells, TAS2R144 triggers intracellular calcium changes via the PLCβ2-IP3-TRPM5 signaling pathway [153, 171]. In intestinal endocrine cells, hTAS2R50 stimulates GLP-1 secretion through a Gβγ-mediated signaling pathway, while TAS2R activation of CCK release depends on the calcium inward flow signaling pathway [167, 168, 170]. In thyroid cells, TAS2R4 and TAS2R36 regulate thyroid-stimulating hormone-dependent increases in intracellular calcium and iodide efflux [155]. However, the role of the Gα/PDE/cAMP pathway in these processes is not elucidated. Therefore, TAS2R functions in the endocrine system primarily rely on the Gβγ/PLCβ/IP3/Ca²⁺ pathway and the calcium signals it initiates, rather than novel tissue-specific pathways [153, 155, 167, 171] (Fig. 8).

In the endocrine system, TAS2R activation in thyroid cells leads to an increase in intracellular calcium ion concentration, potentially affecting the absorption and utilization of iodine ions and influencing the synthesis of thyroid hormones (T3 and T4). In the choroid plexus epithelial cells, TAS2R activation by DB triggers a calcium response via the PLCβ₂ and IP₃ signaling pathways and activates cation flow mediated by the TRPM5 channel, which may play a role in the chemical surveillance and detoxification functions of the blood-brain barrier. Upon bitter activation of TAS2R, intracellular calcium levels rise, further promoting the secretion of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and CCK. In skin-infiltrating lymphocytes, TAS2R is activated by bitter agonists, potentially influencing lymphocyte migration and distribution through intracellular signaling pathways, though the specific mechanisms remain unclear

TAS2Rs in urinary system

In the urinary system, the urethra relies on the activity of innate immunity to maintain an infection-free urethra by effectively isolating and destroying potential pathogens through immune action and regulation [176–178]. TAS2Rs are expressed in cholinergic urethral brush cells (UBC), which monitor harmful substances such as bacterial products. By recognizing bitter compounds, they activate the release of acetylcholine, which interacts with nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in nearby nerve fibers, triggering reflex contraction of the bladder detrusor muscle to help expel harmful substances. This mechanism enhances the immune defense function of the urinary system, protecting the urethra from infection and inflammation [179–181].

TAS2Rs in the urethra: sentinel function and immune defense

TAS2Rs are expressed on cholinergic urethral brush cells (UBCs) in the urethra, where they play a critical role in monitoring the environment for potential harmful substances, such as bacterial products [179, 181]. UBCs are multimodal chemosensors of TAS2Rs [182]. TAS2Rs can detect specific bitter compounds, including denatonium and bitter compounds produced by certain Gram-negative bacteria, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which may reach high concentrations during urethral infections [179]. These bitter compounds become targets of the innate immune defense system. When TAS2Rs are activated by these compounds, they induce the release of ACh, which communicates with adjacent sensory nerve fibers via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs, especially those containing the α3 subunit) through paracrine signaling, triggering a classical taste transduction pathway [181, 183, 184]. This signaling process causes a reflexive contraction of the bladder’s detrusor muscle, helping to expel harmful substances from the urethra and strengthening local immune defense [179, 185].

Studies have shown that in anesthetized rats, the application of bitter substances in the urethra can reflexively increase detrusor muscle activity through ACh-mediated signaling, promoting the expulsion of harmful substances, thus highlighting the importance of TAS2Rs in maintaining urethral health [179, 186]. Currently, urethral brush cells expressing TAS2Rs are only found at the entrance of the urethra in mice, and such receptors have not been discovered in more central organs of the urinary system, such as the bladder or kidneys [179]. However, some studies suggest that TAS2R126, TAS2R108, and TAS2R143 are highly expressed in the mouse bladder, which could become new research targets in the future [180].

TAS2R functions explicitly involve the canonical Gβγ/PLCβ/IP₃/Ca²⁺ signaling pathway. Activation of TAS2Rs by bitter compounds in urethral brush cells triggers acetylcholine release [181]. The released acetylcholine acts in a paracrine manner on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors of adjacent sensory nerve fibers, ultimately causing reflexive contraction of the bladder detrusor muscle to aid in expelling harmful substances [179, 185]. This cascade of events constitutes an innate immune defense mechanism of the urethra against pathogens [179]. However, the present study does not clarify the role of the Gα/PDE/cAMP pathway in this process. Therefore, TAS2Rs in the urinary system do not employ novel tissue-specific signaling pathways; their innate immune function clearly relies on the classical taste transduction pathway and the neuroimmune coupling it initiates [179, 181] (Fig. 9) (Table 1).

Discussion

Research on TAS2Rs holds significant potential in basic medical research and clinical applications. As our understanding of their role in innate immune responses deepens, these receptors are seen as potential targets for developing novel therapeutic methods. TAS2Rs are involved not only in taste perception but are also widely distributed across various systems, helping the host defend against harmful substances by recognizing pathogen-associated molecules and regulating immune responses. These characteristics make TAS2Rs promising candidates for treating respiratory diseases, inflammatory bowel disease, neuroinflammation, cardiovascular diseases, and urinary tract infections. Despite these promising insights, most mechanistic conclusions are inferred from expression analyses or pharmacological models, and future studies should prioritize genetic knockout, CRISPR editing, or lineage tracing approaches to provide definitive functional validation of TAS2R-mediated immune regulation.

This review summarizes the roles of TAS2Rs in the respiratory, digestive, musculoskeletal, reproductive, nervous, circulatory, endocrine, and urinary systems, highlighting their potential in regulating host defenses through different mechanisms in innate immunity. In the respiratory system, they detect pathogens and activate immune responses, such as NO production and enhanced ciliary beating, strengthening airway barriers [9, 11]. In the digestive system, TAS2Rs regulate inflammation and antimicrobial peptide secretion, with expression correlating to disease activity in IBD [1, 48, 55]. In the musculoskeletal system, they influence osteoblast differentiation, bone marrow immunity, and inflammation, though calcium-mediated mechanisms and long-term effects remain unclear [81, 85]. In reproductive tissues, TAS2Rs participate in sperm chemotaxis and ovarian immune regulation, potentially affecting myometrial relaxation and placental immunity [114, 123], but detailed mechanisms remain undefined. In the nervous system, TAS2Rs modulate neuroinflammation and detect harmful stimuli [135], though most evidence comes from animal models. In the circulatory system, TAS2Rs regulate vascular tone and inflammation [1, 128, 150], yet their interactions with immune cells and oxidative stress require further study. In the endocrine system, TAS2Rs influence hormone secretion and immune balance [155, 156], but molecular pathways remain underexplored. In the urinary system, TAS2Rs detect pathogens and regulate bladder reflexes [161, 179], yet their roles in deeper organs and chronic diseases are poorly defined.

Across all systems reviewed, the immunoregulatory roles of TAS2Rs are primarily supported by correlative evidence derived from expression profiling and pharmacological activation. While these studies suggest the involvement of TAS2Rs in diverse immune processes, they do not provide definitive mechanistic validation. However, recent research has begun to fill this gap through genetic and molecular approaches. In a murine model, knockout of Tas2r126, Tas2r135, and Tas2r143 abolished agonist-enhanced neutrophil migration and myosin light chain 2 phosphorylation, indicating that these receptors regulate immune cell migration through a ROCK-dependent pathway [94]. In the gastrointestinal tract, Tas2r105-deficient mice showed exacerbated DSS-induced colitis with increased inflammatory cytokine expression and altered immune cell infiltration, revealing the receptor’s protective role in gut immune homeostasis [195]. In human gingival epithelial cells, CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of TAS2R14 reduced β-defensin-2 production and impaired resistance to Staphylococcus aureus, demonstrating direct control of mucosal immunity [72]. Additionally, TAS2Rs expressed in mouse intestinal tuft cells have been shown to signal through gustducin, PLCβ2, IP3 receptor 2, and TRPM5 to promote IL-25 release in response to helminth infection, linking TAS2R signaling to innate lymphoid cell activation and epithelial remodeling [67]. These findings provide concrete molecular and genetic support for TAS2R-mediated immune regulation and underscore the importance of extending such approaches to other systems and receptor subtypes.

Building on these mechanistic insights, increasing evidence suggests that TAS2R signaling pathways are more diverse than previously thought, particularly with respect to G protein coupling. Recent studies indicate that TAS2R signaling is not exclusively mediated by the classical gustatory G protein gustducin. In particular, airway smooth muscle (ASM) expresses minimal gustducin, and TAS2R-induced bronchodilation is instead mediated primarily via Gαi1, Gαi2, and Gαi3 subunits. In experiments suppressing gustducin and transducin proteins, no disruption of TAS2R signaling was observed in ASM, whereas knockdown of Gαi isoforms significantly impaired TAS2R-mediated responses. Moreover, immunoprecipitation studies demonstrate an agonist-dependent physical interaction between TAS2R14 and Gαi2 in HEK293T cells [196]. This suggests that TAS2Rs can couple promiscuously to multiple G proteins depending on the cellular context. Additional evidence also implicates Gαt subfamily members in modulating TAS2R effects, particularly in airway relaxation and cross-talk with acetylcholine receptor signaling [197]. This functional flexibility highlights the importance of elucidating tissue-specific G protein coupling mechanisms, which may critically influence downstream immune and physiological outcomes.

Complementing these signaling discoveries, recent structural and pharmacological insights have further illuminated the functional landscape of TAS2Rs, especially TAS2R14, and provided a foundation for rational ligand design. Recent cryo-EM studies have revealed new structural insights into TAS2R14 that enhance our understanding of its function across tissue types. In gustatory and non-gustatory systems, TAS2R14 couples with both Gαgust and Gαi1. Structures show that a cholesterol molecule can occupy the canonical orthosteric site, while a synthetic bitter tastant, cmpd28.1, binds a previously unrecognized intracellular positive allosteric modulator site, suggesting novel dual-site activation mechanisms [133]. These observations support the emerging view of TAS2R14 as a metabolite sensor, responsive to cholesterol and bile acid derivatives. Building on this framework, complementary cryo-EM and NanoBRET analyses demonstrate that the bitter anti-inflammatory drug flufenamic acid binds TAS2R14 simultaneously at the canonical extracellular pocket and a distinct intracellular site with comparable micromolar affinity. This dual engagement establishes a clear role mechanism in which intracellular ligand binding directly contributes to receptor activation, reinforcing the concept that TAS2R14 integrates signals across the membrane and supporting its broader role in innate immune functions across physiological systems [198]. Linking structure to function, functional studies show that TAS2R14 regulation depends on the ligand. Most agonists induce β-arrestin recruitment and receptor internalization, leading to desensitization. In contrast, diphenhydramine maintains G protein-mediated calcium signaling with minimal β-arrestin involvement. This biased signaling arises from a distinct deep-pocket binding mode and allows sustained receptor activity, supporting a stable role for TAS2R14 in innate immune regulation [199]. These findings underscore the structural versatility of TAS2R14. They also open the door for the design of tissue-specific or function-biased TAS2R modulators with therapeutic potential.

Growing translational evidence reveals the diverse applications of TAS2Rs, positioning them as promising therapeutic targets across multiple disciplines. Clinical ex vivo studies show TAS2R4 expression in human scalp hair follicles, where activation by rebaudioside A suppresses keratinocyte proliferation and induces premature catagen through TGF‑β2 upregulation, effects reversed by TAS2R4 knockdown, thus confirming receptor‑specific signaling and supporting drug‑free strategies for unwanted hair growth [200]. Beyond dermatology, TAS2R38 has been identified as a genetic modifier influencing chronic rhinosinusitis severity and Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonization in cystic fibrosis, with genotype correlating to bitter taste perception and disease phenotype, enabling PROP taste testing for noninvasive risk stratification and informing TAS2R38‑targeted topical therapy development [201]. In oncology, seven TAS2R genes serve as prognostic markers in colon carcinoma, facilitating molecular subtyping with distinct immune microenvironments and improving prognostic precision, thereby guiding clinical decision‑making [202]. Together, these advances highlight TAS2Rs as invisible yet powerful modulators in innate immunity and translational medicine, warranting further studies to elucidate subtype‑specific signaling pathways, develop selective and tissue‑targeted modulators, and expand rigorous clinical trials to fully harness their potential in precision therapy.

Despite progress, TAS2R research faces gaps in mechanistic validation, tissue specificity, and translational application. Future work should focus on genetic and molecular validation across tissues, elucidating G protein coupling diversity, characterizing intracellular signaling cascades, and developing selective ligands. Integrating clinical trials will be critical to translating TAS2R biology into precision therapies.
