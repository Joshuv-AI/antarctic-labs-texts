Abattoir‐Based Prevalence and Histopathological Analysis of Paramphistomes (Platyhelminthes: Digenea) in the Livers and Rumens of Ruminants
Authors: Hafiz Muhammad Rizwan, Muhammad Adeel Hassan, Urfa Bin Tahir, Neil Sargison, Muhammad Sohail Sajid, Razia Kausar, Mohsin Raza, Haider Abbas, Muhammad Usman, Dalia Fouad, Farid S Ataya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParamphistomosis, a parasitic disease of ruminants caused by flukes of the family Paramphistomidae (Platyhelminthes: Digenea) and leads to significant economic losses. Primarily, their predilection site is rumen as evident from their common name ‘ruminal flukes’ and occasionally find in other visceral organs. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of paramphistome infection in the rumens and livers of livestock followed by histopathological examination of the affected tissues. A total of 384 animals were screened for paramphistome infection at a local abattoir in the Narowal district. Liver and rumen tissue samples from infected animals were excised, fixed in buffered formalin, and processed using the paraffin‐embedding technique. Tissue‐paraffin blocks were sectioned with the thickness of 5 µm through microtome and stained using the Harri's haematoxylin and eosin methods. The prevalence of paramphistomosis in ruminants was 49.74% with sex and age of host having a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) with the magnitude of infection; however, species of host and months were not significantly associated (p > 0.05). Of the total cases having rumens positive for paramphistomes, 7.29% were also tested positive for liver infections; however, no adult or immature Fasciola spp. were detected. Infected rumens had significantly (p < 0.05) reduced epithelial thickness (30.50 ± 1.83 µm), accompanied by severe haemorrhages in hepatic tissues and blood congestion in hepatic vessels. Further, the central hepatic vein was found having a significantly larger diameter (39.56 ± 2.72 µm) as compared to that of healthy livers (29.76 ± 3.15 µm). The paper provides a maiden data on the paramphistome infection in livers along with rumens of ruminants in Pakistan. Hence, the findings suggest that paramphistome infection may pose a potential threat to nutrient absorption and overall metabolic health in ruminants.Keywords: digenea, flukes, histopathology, liver, paramphistomes, rumen, ruminants

Introduction

Paramphistomes (Platyhelminthes: Digenea) are trematodes commonly called flukes belonging to the family Paramphistomatidae, which includes several genera of common livestock helminths. Paramphistomosis is cosmopolitan with a higher magnitude of infection in regions providing a favourable microenvironment for the development of intermediate hosts, that is, mud‐snails or water‐snails (García‐Dios et al. 2024; Huson et al. 2017). The most commonly reported paramphistome genera in Asia are Paramphistomum, Gastrothylax, Fischoederius, Carmyerius (Hanna et al. 1988) and Explanatum (Chaudhry et al. 2017). These parasites have heteroxenous life cycles involving molluscs as intermediate hosts and migratory larval stages in their final hosts (De Waal 2010). Adult Paramphistomum spp., Gastrothylax spp., Fischoederius spp. and Carmyerius spp. have a predilection site of fore‐stomach and hence, referred as ruminal flukes. Adult Explanatum (E.) explanatum have a predilection site of the bile ducts. Heavy loads of immature stages of paramphistomes can lead to significant health issues in ruminants, including diarrhoea, anemia, weight loss, and even death (Rolfe et al. 1991). Adult ruminal flukes are cone‐shaped, bright‐coloured (pink to red), and 5 to 12 mm long and 2 to 4 mm wide (Rojo‐Vázquez et al. 2012). Their large size and higher numbers give rise to concerns of morbidity, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions (Ozdal et al. 2010; Rolfe et al. 1991; Rangel‐Ruiz et al. 2003).

Historically, paramphistomosis were deemed uncommon and harmless in temperate regions, but recent trends indicated an increasing prevalence of Calicophoron (C.) daubneyi in wetter areas of the Europe (Gordon et al. 2013). Biology of paramphistomes in the intermediate and final hosts as well as their ecology has become essential for developing an effective mitigation strategy (Huson et al. 2017). In Pakistan, where livestock and arable agriculture are vital to the economy, the rising trend of the prevalence and quantitative abundance of paramphistome infection is alarming. The adult paramphistomes are visually obvious on post‐mortem inspection (Malrait et al. 2015). Routine post‐mortem inspection of the slaughtered livestock offers valuable opportunities to collect samples of adult paramphistomes for their identification and understanding the associated pathological parameters (Ali et al. 2018) from which the impact of disease can be deduced (Tehrani et al. 2013). People are not considering paramphistomes as a major concern as these mostly inhabit rumens which are washed before selling in the market. However, the infection of ruminal flukes in other organs, for example, liver is not common (García‐Dios et al. 2024).

Abattoir based data describing fluke prevalence and their associated tissue damage is completely missing (Godara et al. 2014). This is a maiden study which describes the prevalence of paramphistome infection in the livers and rumens of the slaughtered ruminants evaluated through the histopathological examination of the infected organs. The results provide an endorsement of the extra‐ruminal infection of paramphistomes in livers. Hence, this highlighted the potential threat of paramphistomosis, particularly in conjunction with other infections, impacting nutrient absorption and overall metabolic health in ruminants affecting the economy of the resource‐poor stakeholders of the country.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Sampling Strategy

Between January and June 2024, a total of 384 ruminants slaughtered at an abattoir in the Narowal district, Punjab, Pakistan were examined for the presence of paramphistome infection. The sentinel surveillance was conducted and convenient non‐probability sampling (Thrusfield et al. 2018) was used to screen a total of 111 cattle, 64 buffaloes, 92 sheep, and 117 goats. Sheep and goats were considered young if they were under 9 and 12 months old, respectively, and adult when they were older than 9 months and 12 months respectively. Cattle and buffalo were categorised as young if they were under 24 months old and as adults if they were over 24 months. The age of the animals was determined via dental formula analysis and information provided by butchers and farmers.

Antemortem and Post‐Mortem Inspection of Animals

The ante‐mortem inspection parameters were recorded using the standard slaughterhouse, ante‐mortem inspection protocols. A prior formal consent from the owner of the animals was taken for inclusion in the study on a pre‐structured consent form including permission to take rumen and liver samples for examination. The whole gastrointestinal tract was located after exsanguination and then ligated. In order to look for flukes in the forestomach, the dorsal cranial and caudal ruminal sacs were opened. The livers of all animals were also carefully examined for presence or absence of paramphistomes.

The presence of paramphistomes in the forestomach or liver, sex and age (young or adult) of the animals were recorded. For histopathological analysis, we collected a total of 128 tissue samples, comprising 108 from animals infected with paramphistomes (28 from livers and 80 from rumens) and 20 from non‐infected animals (10 from livers and 10 from rumens) serving as controls (Tehrani et al. 2013). The study followed the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines of the University of Agriculture Faisalabad (UAF), Pakistan, and was approved by the Molecular Parasitology Laboratory of the UAF vide Letter No. MPL 621/11‐23.

Collection, Fixation and Transportation of Tissues

Liver and rumen tissue samples (1 cm3) were collected from paramphistome positive and negative animals (Hofmann and Schnorr 1982; Zitnan et al. 2008) and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin at a ratio of 1:10 (tissue to fixative) within 2 h of the slaughter. The samples were transported to the KBCMA College of Veterinary and Animal Sciences, Narowal, Sub‐campus University of Veterinary and Animal Sciences (UVAS), Lahore, Pakistan, for further analysis (transport duration not exceeding 1 h).

Preparation and Histopathological Examination of Tissues

The tissue samples were processed by dehydration using progressively stronger concentration of alcohol followed by cleaning with xylene, before embedding in the paraffin wax. Tissue blocks were subjected to slicing of 5 µm thicknesses using a microtome. Slices were positioned onto glass slides and were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stains (Suvarna et al. 2019) followed by their microscopic examination at 40× and 100× magnification (B‐150, Optika, Italy). Staining quality was routinely monitored, and control slides were included in each batch to ensure consistency.

Stained sections of rumen were examined to describe any histopathology using the standard guidelines. Photomicrographs were analysed using ImageJ software to measure the width and length of ruminal papillae, epithelial thickness, and the thickness of muscularis externa and tunica submucosa. The stained sections of hepatic tissues were examined to identify hepatic tissue disruption around the central vein and inflammatory cell infiltration. Dilation of central vein was also determined (Nobel 1997).

Data Management and Statistical Analyses

All histological assessments were performed in a blinded manner with respect to the infection status of the samples to avoid observers’ bias. Three to five non‐overlapping fields per section were randomly selected for morphometric measurements, and at least two sections per tissue sample were analysed for each animal. To ensure consistency, intra‐observer reliability was assessed by repeating measurements on 10% of randomly selected fields. Inter‐observer reliability was also evaluated by a second independent examiner.

The number of positive samples was divided by the total number of samples screened and multiplied by 100 to get the relative percentage (%) of paramphistome infection. Chi‐square tests were used to determine the effects of sex, age, and species on the likelihood of paramphistomosis. A one‐way ANOVA was used to process means and standard errors where significant differences were determined using Tukey's test to compare the means of histopathological parameters. Before applying ANOVA, data were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variances (Levene's test), with log‐transformation or non‐parametric tests used where assumptions were violated. Data were analysed using Minitab 17.0 software (Minitab Inc., Pennsylvania, USA). Associations having p values under 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The overall prevalence of paramphistome infection was 49.74%. Histological Examination of Livers

Hepatic tissues showed alterations in the histology with paramphistome infection. The normal radiating pattern of hepatocytes and sinusoids around the central vein was disrupted. Haemorrhages in the hepatic tissue and the blood vessels were congested. This was supported by the larger diameter (39.56 ± 2.72 µm) of central vein as compared to the non‐infected group (29.76 ± 3.15 µm). The infiltration area (%) of inflammatory cells (43.45 ± 7.9) and number of binucleated cells per mm (6.45 ± 0.06) showed a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the paramphistome infected animals compared to controls. Degenerative changes were evident in the infected animals, characterised by necrosis, hepatocellular vacuolation, and a reduced diameter of hepatocytes (Table 3, Figure 2).

Discussion

The current research highlights the value of abattoir data to shed light on the prevalence and impact of paramphistomosis in ruminants in the Narowal District of the Punjab province of Pakistan. Abattoir‐based studies to assess the impact of paramphistome infection require access to highly characterised samples and data (Tehrani et al. 2013). This is possible in countries where individual animal movement's records are accessible, but challenging in recourse‐poor countries like Pakistan. Consequently, few studies have been undertaken in the regions where paramphistomosis is most commonly seen; and a handful of investigations report their frequency and impact in these regions. The descriptions of paramphistome prevalence and histopathological changes associated with infection are informative, but the utility of the results is lessened by the inability to speciate the co‐infecting parasites (Ali et al. 2018). Morphological speciation of paramphistomes requires expertise and is potentially inaccurate (Odening 1983) which necessitates to apply next generation sequencing methods (Sargison et al. 2019) to describe the molecular epidemiology of co‐infecting fluke parasites. Patent paramphistome infections can be identified in live animals by faecal egg counts (Duignan et al. 2017); although interpretation is constrained by limited understanding of the relationship between egg counts, parasite burden, and pathology. The sensitivities of faecal egg counts for the identification of paramphistome in the forestomachs of Scottish cattle were 0.65 and 0.85 during the summer/autumn and winter months, respectively (Sargison et al. 2016). Furthermore, differentiation between the eggs of many different paramphistome species, and from those of Fasciola spp. (liver flukes) can be unreliable, leading to erroneous diagnoses or interpretation of results indicative of treatment failure (Gordon et al. 2013).

The overall prevalence of paramphistomosis in ruminants slaughtered in the Narowal district, Punjab, Pakistan was 50%; indicating a considerable parasitic burden on ruminant in this area. In 1997, the frequency of paramphistome infection at Sihala abattoir, Islamabad, Pakistan was about 75% in buffaloes and about 50% in cattle (Cheema et al. 1997). Overall prevalence of 17.5% of Paramphistomosis was reported in a study conducted in four different districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan (Rafiq et al. 2023). In Ireland, an apparent herd prevalence of 77.3% was determined (Martinez‐Ibeas et al. 2016). In India, Preethi et al. (2019) reported 42% prevalence of paramphistomosis in ruminants. Lower prevalence rates have been reported in other studies from neighbouring regions, for example, 40% in cattle in Iran (Khedri et al. 2015) and 17% in ruminants in Nepal (Pokharel and Sharma 2023). In northwestern Spain, only 14% (García‐Dios et al. 2024) and, in the United Kingdom, 13.5% (Busin et al. 2023) of the animals were positive for paramphistomes. Variations in occurrence across studies could be attributed to differences in environmental factors, local agro‐ecological conditions, extensive grazing, and livestock management practices relating to access to intermediate mud or water snail host and exposure to metacercarial challenge (Khedri et al. 2015; Rangel‐Ruiz et al. 2003). Frequency distribution may also vary due to the use of different diagnostic methods, or depending on the time of the year when samples were collected from recently infected animals.

The current study area reported higher frequency rates of paramphistomes which could be attributable to a relatively moderate temperature coupled with adequate rainfall throughout the year which is beneficial for the development of snails (intermediate hosts) as well as the external life cycle stages of paramphistomes (Jones et al. 2017; Kahl et al. 2021). The higher prevalence in our study is likely a result of the local agro‐ecological setting where extensive grazing systems coupled with wet to marshy ecosystems are favourable to intermediate hosts of the parasites, thereby facilitating persistent transmission. On the other hand, arid regions of Iran devoid of grazing systems reported a lower frequency (Khedri et al. 2015). Ireland's higher rates may be attributed to greater animal management activities along with persistent wetter weather, which may enhance the risk of transmission. In addition to environmental determinants, socio‐economic constraints that limit farmers’ access to veterinary services, irregular or inappropriate deworming regimens, and uncontrolled animal movements may influence the epidemiological patterns observed across different regions (Kitessa et al. 2023).

In this study, the host species had a statistically non‐significant association with paramphistomosis which is not different from the results published elsewhere like in Pakistan (Alvi et al. 2022; Khan and Maqbool 2012); in India (Kaur et al. 2009; Preethi et al. 2019); in Iran (Khedri et al. 2015); and in Ethiopia (Sirika et al. 2022). Similar differences between the frequency of paramphistome infections in cattle and sheep were reported in Turkey (Ozdal et al. 2010), albeit at a much lower overall prevalence. Nevertheless, a higher occurrence was observed in buffalo and cattle than in sheep (Al‐Gaabary et al. 2009). This may reflect the practice of taking buffalo and cattle to wallow in ponds potentially harbouring water snails, and to acquire metacercaria when grazing around their margins (Hajipour et al. 2021). In our area, animals are more likely to freely graze in lowland snail‐infested areas or in communal pastures which might be different from managed grazing systems (restricted or rotational basis) in Europe. This might contribute to explain the relatively higher magnitude of parmphistomosis in ruminant population of our country in all species. There is also the possibility of species‐specific variability due to variable ruminal physiology, immunity, and susceptibility to infection. along with management practices such as frequencies of deworming and grazing schedules. Although no association with host species was found, it is possible that individual physiological or immunological changes within host species could affect susceptibility and alteration in infection dynamics (Mabbott 2018). Studies integrating immunological profiling and induced infections would aid in understanding these species‐specific differences and their consequences on epidemiology and control of the parasites (Martins‐Duarte 2025).

Paramphistomosis was significantly associated with the sex of the host, with a prevalence of 63% in female animals and 42% in male animals similar to earlier reports from Egypt (Al‐Gaabary et al. 2009), Algeria (Titi et al. 2010) and Pakistan (Rizwan et al. 2023). Higher prevalence in female animals could be attributed to lowered immunity of undernourished animals during pregnancy and lactation (Albery et al. 2020). However, Preethi et al. (2019) reported a non‐significant association between sex and paramphistome infection. Such variation may be due to differences in physiological status (e.g., pregnancy, hormonal influences, lactation, etc.), management practices (e.g., preferential grazing access), geographical conditions, or sample sizes. Often in traditional systems, males are given better shelter or more care which keeps them in a protected environment, while females graze in more contaminated environments. More extensive and controlled epidemiological studies to separate biological phenomena from management‐related impacts of sex on the prevalence of parasites are direly needed.

The higher frequency of paramphistomosis in adults than in younger animals is similar to other studies (Paul et al. 2011; Sirika et al. 2022). However, Preethi et al. (2019) found significantly higher prevalence in younger animals as compared to adults. Calicophoron daubneyi were identified from the forestomachs of 29% of about 18‐ to 24‐month‐old cattle slaughtered in a Scottish abattoir (Sargison et al. 2016). In adult animals, paramphistomosis are cumulative; hence prevalence identified during abattoir‐based studies may represent both recent infection and burdens established over previous years (González‐Warleta et al., 2013). Older animals may also have more access to communal grazing, where levels of miracidial challenge may be higher. Greater opportunities for paramphistome infection with age may also account for the higher frequency in female animals, which are generally older when slaughtered.

The impact of paramphistome infection may be confounded by co‐infections with Fasciola spp. which have similar intermediate host, environmental and climatic requirements (Dorny et al. 2011). Multivariable regression analysis did not inferred statistically significant effect of adult C. daubneyi in the forestomachs of cattle on lifetime growth rates, when adjusting for other management factors and F. hepatica co‐infection in the United Kingdom (Sargison et al. 2016). Interestingly, no case of Fasciola spp. was detected in our samples. The absence of Fasciola spp. in our study area suggests possible regional differences in the ecology of paramphistomes as compared to Fasciola spp., transmission dynamics, underscoring the need for wider spectrum of surveillance of trematode co‐infections.

Statistically, the study month (season) was not shown to be significantly associated with the occurrence of paramphistomosis which is not different from earlier findings reported from Iran (Khedri et al. 2015), and Turkey (Ozdal et al. 2010). In contrast, a Mexican study indicated seasonal peaks of infection, particularly during rainy seasons when ecological conditions are suitable for parasite or intermediate host's development (Rangel‐Ruiz et al. 2003). In another study in Iran, the higher magnitude of infection of Paramphistomum spp. was found during July and August months as compared to those of October and November (Tehrani et al. 2013). These regional contrasts or variable findings might reflect influences of climatic differences on the seasonality of metacercarial challenge. The Narowal district has a semi‐stable pattern of climate throughout the year, in which seasonal variations in metacercarial challenge may not be pronounced. Alternatively, regional differences might be attributable to the variation of age groups of slaughtered animals along with factor of their seasonal exposure to metacercarial challenge (Khan et al., 2019).

The histopathological evaluation in the current study demonstrates alterations in the tissues of paramphistome‐infected animals. Substantial reductions in thickness of ruminal epithelia, length and width of ruminal papillae, and thickness of muscularis externa reported in our investigation have also been observed in UK cattle and sheep infected with C. daubneyi (Busin et al. 2023). It is commonly assumed that the attachment of several hundred or more adult flukes by their ventral suckers in the forestomach of the infected animals may cause damage to the ruminal papillae (Fuertes et al. 2015). However, denudation of the papillae occurred in many animals even in the absence of paramphistome infection might be putatively associated with ruminal acidosis and dietary changes. In another study, changes in the shape and length of the ruminal papillae have been described related to the presence of adult paramphistomes (Fuertes et al. 2015). Nevertheless, the structural abnormalities evidenced by the histopathological findings of this study might have impaired nutrient absorption and efficiency of digestion; negatively affecting the health and productivity of the animals (Strydom et al. 2023). The pathological changes in the hepatic tissues included haemorrhage, enlarged central hepatic veins, and the presence of many inflammatory cells are consistent with previous report (Khatoon et al. 2003). Some of the histopathological observations such as the increase in binucleated cells and the decrease in diameter of the hepatocytes also imply that regeneration is occurring in the face of ongoing parasitic burden. These changes highlight the known potential for paramphistomes in the liver to cause production loss (Hanna et al., 1988; Chaudhry et al. 2017).

Conclusions and Recommendations

The study provided a maiden data on the systemic effect of the paramphistomes extended beyond the rumen and involving hepatic tissues in the ruminants brought to the slaughterhouse of Narowal district with a significantly pronounced infection in older and female animals, suggesting potential age‐ and sex‐related susceptibility. The findings suggested that paramphistome infection can result in extensive histological damage, particularly in the liver, as evidenced by increased cellular infiltration, central hepatic vein enlargement, and degeneration of hepatocytes. Histopathological examination revealed endothelial thinning and structural alterations in ruminal papillae and muscularis externa layers, as well as pathological changes in liver suggested that paramphistomes in the ruminants may cause sub‐clinical liver damage, hence potentially disrupting the nutrient absorption and metabolic status.

It is suggested that future studies should emphasise the need for molecular identification of paramphistomes that may improve diagnostic accuracy and understand species‐specific pathogenicity. Seasonal dynamics of paramphistomosis, distribution of intermediate hosts, environmental factors, and patterns of anthelmintic resistance associated with paramphistome infections are needed to be explored. The integration of routine histopathology in slaughterhouse surveillance can serve as an early warning system for parasitic infections in the endemic regions, which can help understanding the transmission dynamics of paramphistomosis; hence, assisting in policy making for an effective and sustainable paramphistome control measures in ruminants.
