Neonatal digital twins enable personalized antibiotic dosing for treating suspected sepsis
Authors: Margaret Y Sui, Kyra L Rosen, Ariel Yuhan Ong, Joseph C Kvedar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal bloodstream infections remain a challenging medical problem to treat due to difficulties in determining appropriate antibiotic dosing. Standardized medical guidelines are often unable to account for the complexity of changing neonatal organ sizes and other concurrent medical treatments that impact antibiotic clearance rates. Digital twin models that successfully capture these features of neonatal physiology alongside bacterial evolutionary dynamics may allow for more personalized antibiotic dosing regimes.Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Neonatal sepsis is a major cause of preventable neonatal mortality worldwide. Most causes of sepsis are bacterial, and treatment requires balancing antibiotic strength with the need to protect healthy human flora and prevent antibiotic resistance1,2. Dosing antibiotics in neonates is especially challenging because antibiotic pharmacokinetics (PK, what the drug does to the body) and pharmacodynamics (PD, what the body does to the drug) are dependent on underlying physiology like renal clearance, which differs widely between neonates depending on gestational and postnatal age1.

Many current approaches extrapolate studies in adults to determine neonatal dosing; however, the physiology between neonates and adults is dramatically different. Although the United States Food & Drug Administration (FDA) has warned against this extrapolation, the scarcity of neonate-specific studies has forced physicians to continue to do so for many antibiotics, including amikacin3. Amikacin is an antibiotic primarily eliminated by the kidneys that is intentionally dosed to have peaks and troughs in its levels over time. The peaks are carefully titrated to maximize antibiotic effectiveness, and the troughs are intended as breaks to minimize side effects like hearing loss and kidney damage4. For appropriate dosing, bloodstream amikacin levels are measured at discrete timepoints. Since organ sizes and function change rapidly at the neonatal stage, PK and PD do not remain constant5.

Furthermore, the bacterial population within a neonate’s bloodstream changes over time depending on antibiotic levels. When antibiotic levels are low, antibiotic-susceptible bacteria can outcompete resistant strains because maintaining antibiotic resistance is energetically costly6,7. Adaptive antibiotic therapy regimes consider these evolutionary dynamics to improve clinical outcomes8,9.

Prunella et al. develop digital twins (virtual models) of neonates of varying ages and physiologies that predict bacterial loads in response to different antibiotic dose formulations10. Broadly speaking, digital twins refer to simulations of biological processes powering the body to create an in silico replica of the patient. These replicas can then be used to predict outcomes in response to specific medical treatments11,12. In addition to training on data to learn patterns, digital twins integrate underlying mechanistic physiology and physics. Rather than focusing on a single snapshot in time, digital twins also dynamically update parameters over time to capture evolving physiology. Prunella et al. built an optimizer on top of their digital twins to formalize the best dosing strategy based on individual neonate characteristics.

Combining models to create digital twins that capture bacterial population response

Prunella et al’s digital twin approach involved combining two models. The first model is a mechanistic PK-PD model that predicts antibiotic distribution into various body compartments: heart, kidneys, remainder tissues, and serum (blood) based on amikacin dose and frequency4. The second model is an evolutionary model that predicts bacterial antibiotic susceptibility and resistance over time in response to the antibiotic levels13–15. Together, these two models enable the prediction of both antibiotic levels and bacterial population over time10.

Considering time-varying physiological changes in neonates may improve antibiotic dosing

Since neonates grow quickly and have complex, changing medical status, antibiotic dosing strategies must also consider these changes. In particular, one parameter that changes rapidly and has a significant influence on amikacin levels is the renal clearance rate or the rate of removal of amikacin via the kidneys16,17. To model changes in neonatal renal clearance rate over time, Prunella et al. trained a type of deep learning model called a long-short term memory (LSTM) neural network on three sets of neonatal patient data for neonates of various gestational and postnatal ages who were either undergoing inotropic/ibuprofen coadministration, therapeutic hypothermia, had perinatal asphyxia previously, or had acute kidney injury18–22. They show that this deep learning-based model outperformed prior models in predicting renal clearance rates, as measured by a mean average percentage error reduction (MAPE) of 35–44%10. When combined with their other models, Prunella et al’s strategy of using deep learning to dynamically predict the renal clearance rate improved their digital twins models’ accuracy for predicting antibiotic levels and bacterial composition.

Considerations for optimizing antibiotic dosing strategies using these digital twins

These digital twin models capture changes in physiology over time and enable in-silico exploration of various antibiotic dosing strategies in real patients. To obtain concrete dosing strategies from these digital twins, Prunella et al. built a drug dosage optimizer that follows clinical guidelines and (1) determines antibiotic dosing to achieve high antibiotic efficacy by maintaining a peak concentration that is at least eight times the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and (2) keeps interdose administrations within 48hours16. Since renal toxicity is not incorporated into this optimizer, regimes that achieve an excessively high maximum concentration were penalized as overexposure. Optimization using these constraints yields antibiotic dosing strategies that are able to meet antibiotic PK-PD goals, satisfy safety levels, and maintain control over resistant bacteria levels.

Deployment of digital twins for personalized antibiotic dosing in neonates

Digital twin models that predict future neonatal physiology and bacterial resistance in response to different antibiotic dosing strategies are an important next step in enabling safer and more effective sepsis treatment for individual neonates. However, it is important to keep in mind the approximations that each model makes. For instance, this current model approximates amikacin clearance as largely dependent on creatinine filtration rate, which would make the model inaccurate for patients on dialysis. In further iterations of the digital twin model, concurrent treatment with other renal function-altering drugs should be tested.

Additionally, before models like this can be used widely to inform clinical decisions, they need to be validated in real-world neonate cohorts across hospitals. Differences in the laboratory testing procedures at different hospital sites can affect physiological parameter measurements. For instance, percentage deviations in amikacin measurements ranged from −7.5% to 6.6% between hospitals in one recent study23, and such variations must be accounted for before digital twin models can be deployed widely and made clinically useful. As more such models and approaches become increasingly sophisticated, it will be necessary to consider how to test and regulate these digital twin models to determine appropriate clinical use and existing model limitations11. It is important to note that overreliance on such models as a substitute for clinical judgment could also be dangerous, as such models likely do not accurately model rare physiological states that askew model assumptions on amikacin distribution, bacterial eco-evolutionary behavior, and renal function.

With these caveats in mind, digital twins remain a crucial next step in the personalization of medicine. In a world where we are generating increasing data from patients, digital twins could use these datasets to create a digital replica of individuals in a given snapshot in time that could be used to predict how the body will respond to specific treatment options. Such an approach could guide difficult clinical decisions and result in better outcomes for individual patients. In the case of this specific amikacin digital twin, Prunella et al. show that in the two cohorts tested with their optimized antibiotic regimes, there was stronger bacterial clearance and superior resistance suppression. Neonatologists in the future could ideally input neonate data into the model—like gestational age, postnatal age, and other diagnoses and key medications—to obtain an antibiotic dosing regime personalized to that specific neonate that satisfies clinical requirements. This approach is not specific to amikacin, and the digital twin approach developed by Prunella et al. could be adapted to other antibiotics such as gentamicin. These optimizer generated antibiotic regimes could serve as a starting point that could guide clinical decision-making and help alleviate physician overwork. While not a substitute for clinical decision-making, digital twin optimizers hold promise as helpful tools that can guide challenging clinical scenarios.

Conclusion

Neonatal physiology is complicated by rapid changes in organ sizes and function, as well as concurrent therapeutic interventions. Optimal antibiotic dosing strategies consider these physiological changes in order to achieve efficacious antibiotic levels without dosing at excessively high levels that promote toxicity or increase the proportion of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Digital twin models that capture these complexities enable in-silico determination of antibiotic regimes that are personalized for each neonate’s complex physiology. With the advent of better computational tools and digital models, thoughtful deployment of personalized approaches may address unmet needs and improve patient outcomes.
