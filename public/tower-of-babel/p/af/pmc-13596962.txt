Use of Operative Video Technology in Surgical Education: A Systematic Review
Authors: Emalee J. Burrows, Joachim Starup-Hansen, Danyal Z. Khan, Yihui Cheng, Maryam O. Hussain, Simon C. Williams, Danail Stoyanov, Hani J. Marcus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To explore the use of real-life operative videos for teaching surgical procedures and evaluate how these compare to alternative teaching methods.Background:Surgical trainees face increasing challenges in training due to evolving working patterns and the growing complexity of surgical procedures. As a result, it is becoming increasingly difficult to develop operative competence. To address such challenges, new training tools must be developed to meet the demands of contemporary surgical practice.Methods:We searched 5 databases (PUBMED, EMBASE, SCI-Expanded, ERIC, and CENTRAL) up to December 2024 for operative videos used in teaching surgical procedures. Articles were assessed on video format, editing method, and user interaction type. Educational impact was evaluated according to self-reported outcomes, knowledge acquisition, skill demonstration, clinical behaviors, and clinical outcomes.Results:Out of 35 included articles, 24 compared the educational benefit of operative videos against interventions that did not utilize real-life intraoperative content or baseline scores. Significantly favorable findings were demonstrated in 20 studies, including self-reported outcomes (60%), knowledge acquisition (64%), skill demonstration (66%), and clinical behaviors (100%). Ten studies compared types of operative video interventions. Trainees benefited more from 3-dimensional (n = 5) or virtual reality (n = 2) platforms compared to 2-dimensional formats. Those trained with videos editing key steps (n = 1) and applying cognitive task analyses (n = 1) performed better than with unstructured narration; and those incorporating interaction with the video outperformed those without (n = 2).Conclusion:Growing evidence supports the use of operative videos for surgical training, though further research is needed to determine the optimal formats and their impact on patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The European working time directive1 or the 80-hour work week in America,2,3 combined with the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic,4 increased subspecialization, and a shift towards nonsurgical or minimally invasive treatments (including endovascular coiling and stereotactic radiosurgery),5 may contribute to reduced training exposure. This evolving landscape has made the acquisition of operative experience increasingly difficult, with a reduction in the number of trainee-performed operations,4 reduced competency achievement,6 and increased patient morbidity.7 Emphasis on the application of new technologies8 and out-of-workplace educational methods has been highlighted as an alternative,9 of which the utility of surgical videos in learning surgical skills has become increasingly apparent6 including online video platforms such as WeBSurg.10

Operative videos, such as those captured by laparoscopes, endoscopes, operating microscopes, or smart theater set-ups, are increasingly viewed as a rich source of data to be exploited.11 Recent reviews have examined the use of video in surgical education with encouraging results.12–14 In particular, surgical video may offer a more widely distributable and cost-effective method of education, where critical events can be reviewed and replayed, offering a benefit over the traditional single intraoperative experience.14 Care should be taken, however, in how such data is collected and distributed.15

The aim of this study is thus to explore the use of real-life operative videos in surgical education and evaluate how these compare to alternative teaching methods. Moreover, the review aims to evaluate the manner in which the implementation of operative video-based teaching is most effective, summarizing the use of various video formats, editing methods, and types of user interaction with the video.

METHODS

Search Strategy

We utilized the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.16 The review was registered on PROSPERO on 28/02/2024 (Registration ID: 513439).

Inclusion and Outcomes

Articles were included if they utilized videos of a surgical nature, defined as audiovisual material of human operations or cadaveric dissections, where the aim was to measure their effect on training for a surgical procedure. The surgical video must form the majority of the educational intervention. Reviews, case reports, or articles where the full text was not available were excluded.

Information and Search Strategies

An online search was performed of PUBMED, EMBASE, SCI-Expanded, CENTRAL, and ERIC (Supplementary Table 1, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A638). Articles were retrieved from inception to December 2024.

Study Selection, Data Extraction, Data Items

Study screening was performed by 2 authors independently (E.B, Y.C.). Title and abstract screening was performed to remove ineligible studies, followed by full-text review. Data extraction was performed via a predefined form, including risk of bias assessment, into Excel (Microsoft Corp., USA) (Supplementary Table 2, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A638) (E.B.). Values were extracted for author, year, study design, participant characteristics, intervention details, and outcome measures. Outcome measures included participants’ self-reported learning outcomes, knowledge acquisition, skill demonstration, behaviors in a clinical environment, or patient/healthcare outcomes.

Assessment of Study Quality

Quality appraisal was performed using the Modified Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument (MMERSQI) tool.17 Domains are scored with section-dependent weightings over a common denominator of 100. The MMERSQI tool was chosen due to its superiority in considering the levels of mastery of Miller pyramid.18 It incorporates the significance of the different phases of learning, and the levels of importance of outcome measures.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

Findings are presented descriptively in accordance with reporting guidelines.16 Categories compared video format, editing method, and user interaction. These were based on previous studies assessing simulation in general for healthcare education.19,20 Outcomes were evaluated categorically utilizing a variation of McGaghie translational outcomes21 and key components of the MMERSQI framework.17 ChatGPT 5.3 was use to assist with editing and grammar with all output reviewed and verified by the authors.

Operative Video Versus Nonoperative Video

Twenty-four studies assessed trainee outcomes with operative videos compared to groups that were not trained with real-life operative video content, including text, lectures, animations, or pre-interventional cohorts. Trainees’ self-reported learning outcomes were improved in 3 studies,26–28 1 study found no difference,22 1 study favored the nonoperative video intervention,24 and 2 studies did not calculate significance.29,23 Nine studies demonstrated improved knowledge retention,22,24,30–36 4 demonstrated no significant difference,27,28,23,25 and 1 favored not training with operative videos.37 Skill demonstration was favorable in 4 studies,27,38–40 nonsignificant in 1,29 and unfavorable in 1,37 with 1 not reporting significance.41 Performance improvements in the real-life clinical setting, such as the number of operative errors or technical skills performance ratings, were found in all 5 studies.28,42–45 One study assessed patient/healthcare outcomes,45 although significance was not calculated.

Type of Operative Video Intervention

The use of operative videos for educational purposes varied substantially in both structure and educational design. Interventions were categorized into 3 key domains including: (1) video format [2-dimensional (2D), 3-dimensional (3D), or virtual reality (VR)], (2) editing method (unstructured narration, step-based frameworks, or cognitive task analysis), and (3) degree of user interaction (passive viewing or interactive engagement).

In 11 studies, trainees in both groups were exposed to different types of operative video interventions. The control groups incorporated real-life operative video content such as 2D operative videos, operative videos with unstructured narration, or watching operative videos without interactive components. Intervention groups included applied step frameworks (n = 1), cognitive task analyses (CTA) (n = 1), 3D operative videos (n = 5), VR operative videos (n = 2), or operative videos with some interactive component (n = 2).

Trainees experienced significantly better self-reported learning outcomes with the above interventions in 2 studies;46,47 1 found no difference.48 Trainees’ theoretical knowledge improved in 8 studies,46,48–54 and 1 reported no change.47 Skill performance improved in 1 study,51 with 1 showing no change,53 and skill performance in the real-life clinical setting improved in 2 studies.55,56

Comparing Different Types of Operative Video Format

Video format was assessed according to the method of visualization of the operative video content, including standard 2D recordings, enhanced 3D visualization, or immersive VR. Each format differed in its ability to convey spatial relationships, depth perception, and the context of the procedure, and was analyzed to evaluate the respective impacts of these formats on surgical learning.

Operative Videos Versus Animated VR

In this group, operative videos were compared to animated VR simulations, which did not incorporate real-life operative videos (Table 2). Zaid et al29 found similar Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) (P = 0.59) and operative time (P = 0.9). Operative video was easier to use (P = 0.032) and improved laparoscopic cholecystectomy knowledge (84.2% vs 76.6%; P = 0.042).24 Yang et al30 similarly reported video viewing with steps to improve Calot triangle dissection knowledge (mean: 15.9 ± 5.2 SD vs 11.9 ± 6.8 SD; P = 0.038), and step knowledge (mean: 11.2 ± 4.8 SD vs 8.1 ± 4.5 SD; P = 0.04), but not anatomical recognition or error awareness. Lohre et al37 performed a verbal knowledge and OSATS assessment, and in contrast found improvement with animated VR [mean difference: 1.9, 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.1–3.3, P = 0.03 and 6.9, 95% CI, 3.3–9.7; P < 0.001, respectively).

3D Operative Video

3D operative videos were videos that either allowed the operator to view the surgery in 3D via computer software, incorporated multiple views, or added 3D animations to demonstrate spatial orientation (Table 3). Benlice et al31 reported that cadaver videos overlaid with 3D animation performed better in knowledge tests compared to textbooks (mean: 16.25, 95% CI, 15.10–17.35 vs 12.16, 95% CI, 11.03–13.27; P < 0.01) and animation only (mean: 14.45, 95% CI, 13.43–15.46; P < 0.01). Similarly, Xue et al54 found operative video with 3D animations also increased knowledge compared to surgical videos alone (mean difference: 2.464, 95% CI, 1.29–3.68; P < 0.001) or textbooks (mean difference: 3.80, 95% CI, 2.64–4.96; P < 0.001). Other studies compared 3D operative video interventions to 2D operative video counterparts, finding improved knowledge (P < 0.05).48–51 This included theoretical knowledge (mean difference: 7%, 95% CI, 1–13; P = 0.002),49 topographical understanding (mean difference: 16%, 95% CI, 10–22; P < 0.0001),49 and anatomical knowledge (P < 0.05).50 Wang et al51 found Global Rating Scale (GRS), femoral nail angle, and entry point accuracy improved by 3.5% (P = 0.046), 29% (P = 0.037), and 18% (P = 0.204), respectively, compared to a single point-of-view.

Use of Operative Video in VR

Here the operative video was integrated into the VR platform (Table 4). Compared to text format, Ro, et al32 showed VR improved knowledge (mean: 5.17 ± 1.29 SD vs 4.59 ± 1.4 SD; P = 0.015), although Arents et al25 reported no difference in open ended (P = 0.68) or multiple choice quizzes (P = 0.8). Kim et al46 compared to the same video in 2D format with improved patient management (mean difference: 1.7, 95% CI: 0.35–3.05; P = 0.014) and knowledge [median score: 42, 37–48 interquartile range (IQR), vs 36, 32–40 IQR; P < 0.001). Harrington et al47 also reported higher engagement (P < 0.01) and fewer task unrelated thoughts (P < 0.01).

Comparing Different Editing Methods of Operative Videos

Editing methods were assessed according to the degree of instructional structuring applied to the videos. This ranged from simple observation to more complex annotations, including highlighting predefined procedural steps or cognitive frameworks.

Absent or Unstructured Narration of Operative Videos

Observation involved watching a video with or without unstructured narration, where no analysis or proforma was designed previously (Table 5). Yee et al27 reported greater confidence (median score: 75 vs text 32; P = 0.043), although Reck-Burneo et al26 found no difference (P = 0.79). In the simulated setting, Morin et al38 reported greater GRS scores (median score: 6, 4–9 IQR vs standard course 10, 9–12 IQR; P < 0.001) and a reduction in required assistance (1, 1–2 IQR vs 3, 2–4 IQR; P < 0.001). Yee et al27 also observed fewer errors during simulations (median errors: 1.33, 0.33–2.33 IQR, vs text 4.00, 4.00–4.67 IQR; P = 0.043). Mendez et al,42 in the clinical setting, noted a 55% reduction in errors (total preintervention: 91 vs 41 postintervention; P < 0.05) and a 64% reduction in staff takeovers (total preintervention: 66 vs 24 postintervention; P < 0.05).

Step Frameworks

Simple step frameworks were where formal senior review was undertaken to analyze key steps and generate a predefined step proforma which was then used to edit the video (Table 6). Step-based operative videos improved surgical knowledge (mean difference from baseline: 37%; 95% CI, 2.7–4.8; P = 0.001)36 including photo based (mean preintervention score: 33.2 ± 18.0 SD vs 66.7 ± 9.7 SD postintervention; P < 0.00001)33 and clinical knowledge (mean: 68.0% ± 2.01 SD, vs lecture 58.0% ± 1.85 SD; P = 0.043).34 In the clinical setting, Hamour et al43 and Crawshaw et al44 also demonstrated reduced errors (mean: 8.8 preintervention vs 4.5 postintervention; P < 0.05) and improved GRS (mean score: 46.8 vs usual preparation, 42.3; P = 0.002), respectively. Guerlain et al52 compared viewing each step three times from three operations versus the videos in sequence. They found significant improvement in perceptual (mean: 0.94 ± 1.34 SD vs 0.07 ± 1.33 SD; P < 0.05) and procedural knowledge (mean: 0.50 ± 0.63 SD vs 0.07 ± 0.92 SD; P < 0.05).

Cognitive Task Analysis and Evaluation

CTA involved editing the video with integration of step frameworks, a cognitive map of key decision-making points, and critical error points (Table 7). Nahas et al41 found a greater number of steps performed accurately (mean: 12 vs pictorial instruction booklet 10). Bhattacharyya et al39 and Bhattacharyya et al40 reported improved Arthroscopic Surgical Skill Evaluation Tool scores (mean difference: 8.9, 95% CI, 7.6–10.1 vs usual preparation; P = 0.002) and Touch Surgery scores (vs operative manual, P < 0.001), respectively. In the clinical setting, von Strauss Und Torney et al45 demonstrated 30% fewer microcomplications (95% CI: 19–40%; P < 0.05) versus usual preparation, estimating savings of €149 per case. Logishetty et al55 found a reduction in operative time (mean minutes: 38, 95% CI, 34–42, vs operative manual plus videos, 28, 95% CI, 23–32; P < 0.005), errors (mean: 49, 95% CI, 44–54 vs 29, 95% CI, 25–33; P < 0.05), and prompts required (mean: 25, 95% CI, 22–29 vs 13, 95% CI, 10–15; P < 0.05).

Comparing Different Types of User Interaction With Videos

Interactive videos included any intervention that required the participant to interact with or respond to the video material, either through interspersed questions, navigational steps, or rating videos (Table 8). Interactive operative videos demonstrated reduced study time (mean minutes: 105 ± 2 4 SD)28 compared to textbooks (mean minutes: 122 ± 30 SD, P < 0.001), improving self-reported anatomy knowledge (88%) and decision-making (67%)23 compared to lectures. Schmitz et al,22 Friedl et al,28 and Friedl et al35 found improvement in knowledge acquisition (P ≤ 0.0001). In the clinical environment, Friedl et al28 found that with interactive multimedia, operative task performance improved (mean score: 82.9% ± 10% SD vs printed material 64.7% ± 12% SD, P < 0.0001). Two studies compared the same video without quiz questions56 or checklist ratings.53 De La Garza et al53 reported improved quiz performance (mean score: 8.3 ± 1.1 SD, vs 7.1 ± 1.3 SD; P ≤ 0.001), and Kowalewski et al56 in the clinical environment, improved OSATS (67.0 ± 11.5 SD vs 59.1 ± 14.0; P = 0.034) alongside fewer errors (3.5 ± 1.9 SD vs 4.7 ± 2.0 SD; P = 0.027).

DISCUSSION

The aim of this review was to explore the use of operative videos in surgical procedure education to determine not only their effect but also to identify key components to consider when implementing them as an educational tool (Table 9).

Principal Findings

Significantly improved outcomes were seen in 20/24 studies in favor of the operative video intervention over nonoperative video. These findings demonstrated significance across self-reported outcomes (3/5 studies), knowledge acquisition (9/14 studies), skill demonstration (4/6 studies), and behaviors in a clinical environment (5/5 studies) with favorable cost-effectiveness results (1/1). Interventions varied substantially in format, editing method, and interactivity, ranging from unstructured recordings to step annotations and CTA, delivered on 2D, 3D, or VR platforms. More structured editing and interactive approaches consistently demonstrated improvement in knowledge, skill, and in some studies, performance in the clinical environment, with lesser impact from passive unstructured viewing. Similarly, more advanced visualization formats (3D or VR) generally improved spatial understanding and procedural knowledge. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring video-based interventions to include structured guidance, interactivity, and immersive formats to maximize educational benefit.

Findings in the Context of the Literature

Overall, findings agreed with previously reported systematic reviews on video use for surgical education.12–14,57 To our knowledge, this is, however, the first systematic review that describes the use of operative video for surgical procedure education. This work expands on previous reviews by focusing solely on the use of real-life intraoperative video for more complex procedures. Other meta-analyses summarizing video use for surgical skills include that by Mao et al58 which compared video interventions only for basic surgical skills, and found no significant difference.

Operative videos and their 3D or VR formats may enhance spatial orientation.59 For example, Prinz et al,49 compared 3D operative video to 2D operative video and found more significant changes in topographical compared to theoretic understanding. This positive finding extended to skills outcomes.51 This is consistent with literature correlating visuospatial perception with surgical training level,60 and performance.61 In the setting of the above, nonoperative VR elicited the only two studies that reported significant benefit over the operative video intervention, which may be in part due to the practical component of VR contributing to increasing motor skills competence, a phenomenon well described in the literature.62,63 This was, however, only assessed in simulated models, and extrapolation to real-life patients, where the intraoperative component is hypothesized to be particularly crucial, needs to be considered. This is supported by studies synthesizing such operative videos into the VR platform, where universally improved outcomes were seen, although this was not explicitly compared to nonoperative VR, or assessed for behaviors in the clinical environment, which limits our interpretation. The relevance of 3D and VR format to education has been described with conflicting results; particularly, comments suggest 3D platforms may cognitively overload novice trainees and actually lead to worsened outcomes.61 This was not seen in our review; however, it is a consideration where 2D options may have a lower cost, greater familiarity, and ease of use.

The optimal method of editing operative videos in surgery is unclear owing to minimal comparisons of methods. Despite this, the studies examined in this review do offer a number of evidence-based options and are relatively resource-effective, ranging from simple narration to simple step frameworks and cognitive task analyses. CTA forms the most comprehensive method and expands on simple narration and simple step frameworks by adding key error and decision points. CTA for surgery has been described in the literature with impacts not only on procedural knowledge but also extending to technical performance,64 which agrees with our findings that include improved skills39,40 and also clinical behaviors.45 Step and CTA editing may be less effective for simpler procedures, as seen by Chhaya et al50 finding no significant improvements in their extraocular scleral buckle procedure subgroup compared to more complex intraocular procedures. It may be especially useful, however, in time-pressured situations, complex, or rare cases.65

The interactive components appeared effective, including navigating through tasks, interspersed quiz questions, and rating videos. Of note, the interaction should place emphasis on the dynamic interplay between instructional events, actions, and cognitive tasks66 where characteristics of the learner and their learning outcomes should also play an important role.67

Heterogeneity and poor description of the control groups limited comparability both within and between each of the different types of video format, editing methods, and interaction types. In particular, there was limited comparison of step frameworks versus CTA, or VR versus 3D. Thus, it is clear each of the interventions is more effective than baseline text or their control counterparts, but limited studies explicitly compared the extent to which these more complex methods impacted learning outcomes. In the literature, the level to which more advanced or complex methods may improve teaching outcomes in surgery is largely equivocal68,69 with the suggestion that as long as the key constructs of the task are accurate to reality, the intervention will offer training of the most relevant aspects of the skill.70

Strengths and Limitations

Intervention and control groups were not well described or standardized. This lack of description may mean studies were not filtered into the search, and important characteristics of videos affecting outcomes may not have been reported. Outcomes were also heterogeneous, with a large majority of particularly knowledge outcomes assessed using unvalidated tools. Following McGaghie translational outcomes ranking, there was also limited higher-level outcomes. Extrapolation of lower levels is questionable71 although some high-quality studies do show promising results.72,73 The generalizability of the review is, however, fair and strengthened by a majority of studies conducted as RCTs (mean MMERSQI study design 18.1/23 ± 5.8 SD), and results being largely homogeneous. Nonsignificant results may have been due to the lack of sample size calculations (mean MMERSQI sample calculation 1.0/3 ± 1.4SD), small sample sizes (mean 57.5 ± 48.6 SD) or moderate levels of attrition (mean MMERSQI response rate 3.6/4 ± 0.9 SD). This makes it challenging to ascertain if there is indeed no effect in these studies or if they are simply underpowered. Without these calculations, there is a much higher risk of type-2 error.

CONCLUSIONS

It is clear that digital technology is rapidly advancing and will likely play a significant role in surgical education in the future.74 From this review, we highlight the role of operative videos in developing educational resources for teaching surgical procedures. In particular, we examined key features of video format, editing method, and interaction. Operative videos showed a greater impact on training of surgical procedures compared to nonoperative videos with 3D, VR, step editing, cognitive task editing, and interaction with video also outperforming their 2D, absent or unstructured narration, or non-interactive counterparts. Further research, however, is needed on the most effective implementation of such operative videos, and in particular, the optimal combination of the above features for cost-effectiveness and educational benefit.
