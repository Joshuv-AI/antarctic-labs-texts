Aldosterone and the Broken Clock: Restoring Circadian Blood Pressure Rhythm in Primary Aldosteronism
Authors: Cheng‐Hsuan Tsai
Published: 2026

Historically, primary aldosteronism (PA) was considered a rare cause of hypertension, often overlooked in routine clinical practice. However, contemporary evidence has redefined PA as the most common form of specifically treatable secondary hypertension, affecting up to 25% of patients with hypertension. 1 Beyond its pressor effects, aldosterone excess exerts direct deleterious impacts on target organs, leading to disproportionately high rates of stroke, myocardial infarction, and atrial fibrillation compared with essential hypertension with similar blood pressure (BP) levels. 2 , 3 , 4 Previous studies have highlighted the systemic nature of mineralocorticoid receptor overactivation, which extends far beyond fluid retention to include vascular remodeling and autonomic dysfunction. 5 , 6 These multisystemic effects suggest that the traditional focus on average BP may overlook more subtle, yet clinically significant, physiological derangements such as the disruption of the circadian BP architecture.

Circadian BP variation, characterized by a physiological decline during nighttime, is a fundamental feature of cardiovascular homeostasis. 7 The loss of nocturnal BP dipping has consistently been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, independent of average BP levels. 8 Although ambulatory BP monitoring has reinforced the prognostic importance of these patterns, the biological mechanisms underlying rhythm disruption in secondary hypertension remain partially veiled. Among these, PA represents a compelling model of endocrine chronopathy, where hormonal excess directly perturbs the body's internal timing.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Liang and colleagues provide a comprehensive analysis of circadian BP regulation in a large PA cohort. 9 By integrating cross‐sectional biochemical stratification with longitudinal interventional data, the authors provide robust evidence that aldosterone excess is not merely a pressor, but a potent disruptor of the cardiovascular clock. Using data from 681 patients, the investigators demonstrate a clear inverse, dose‐dependent relationship between plasma aldosterone concentration and nocturnal systolic BP decline. This dose–response effect suggests that aldosterone acts as a key mediator of the nondipper phenotype, a pattern notoriously prevalent in PA compared with essential hypertension.

The study's longitudinal component is particularly illuminating. Following selective adrenal artery embolization, 10 a minimally invasive intervention, patients who achieved biochemical success (normalization of aldosterone) showed a significant restoration of nocturnal dipping. Notably, this rhythm recovery was markedly absent in those with persistent aldosterone elevation, even if their office BP improved. This finding serves as a critical physiological probe, isolating aldosterone as the primary culprit in rhythm dysregulation. It suggests that clinical success, traditionally defined by BP reduction alone, may be an insufficient goal if the underlying endocrine chronopathy remains uncorrected. However, the path to clinical implementation faces challenges. Although selective adrenal artery embolization offers a unique window into PA pathophysiology, its role relative to the surgical gold standard or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist therapy requires further definition. 11 Furthermore, the reliance on 24‐hour ambulatory BP monitoring, though precise, remains a burden for long‐term patient monitoring.

Looking forward, this work opens several avenues for investigation. First, can mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist therapy achieve similar BP rhythm remodeling as mechanical interventions? Second, as we move toward personalized medicine, the integration of wearable technologies may allow for continuous, less invasive monitoring of these biological clocks. Finally, mechanistic studies exploring mineralocorticoid receptor signaling within central and peripheral clock networks are needed to fully elucidate these therapeutic targets.

In summary, Liang and colleagues provide compelling evidence that aldosterone excess disrupts circadian BP regulation in a reversible manner. Their findings reframe PA as a disorder not only of pressure and volume, but of biological timing. By highlighting biochemical remission as the cornerstone of rhythm restoration, this study advances our understanding of endocrine hypertension and demands renewed attention to the temporal dimensions of cardiovascular health.
