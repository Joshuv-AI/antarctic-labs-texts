Molecular identification of bat fly species and associated Bartonella bacteria from Lopburi and Sa Kaeo Provinces in Thailand
Authors: Vadeeporn Rattananupong, Wachareeporn Trinachartvanit, Sara Bumrungsri, Abdulloh Samoh, Visut Baimai, Arunee Ahantarig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBat flies harbour several species of bacteria, including Bartonella. We examined 50 bat flies collected from five species of cave-dwelling bats (Eonycteris spelaea, Hipposideros larvatus, Taphozous theobaldi, T. melanopogon and Rousettus sp.) in Lopburi and Sa Kaeo Provinces, Thailand. Four bat fly species in Streblidae (Brachytarsina cucullata, n = 18; B. macrops, n = 6; Megastrebla gigantea, n = 2; and Raymondia sp., n = 7) and one species in Nycteribiidae (Eucampsipoda latisterna, n = 17) were detected. The results of phylogenetic analyses of the COI, COII, 16S rRNA, and 18S rRNA gene sequences of these samples supported their morphological characteristics. They also provided the first molecular identification of these bat fly species and a new record of M. gigantea in Thailand. Molecular analyses of Bartonella from these bat flies using multigene analysis of five target markers (gltA, ITS, rpoB, nuoG and ftsZ) revealed a bacterial prevalence of 48%. Phylogenetic analyses identified six Bartonella species groups. They were related to those previously reported from Thailand and elsewhere in Asia and Africa. Interestingly, one group clustered with the potentially zoonotic species Bartonella rousetti found in Nigeria and Zambia. These findings highlight the rich biodiversity, particularly in terms of parasite–vector–host relationships, in the tropical climate of Thailand and Southeast Asia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41591-3.Keywords: Bat fly, Bartonella, Cave-dwelling bat, ThailandSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Microbiology, Molecular biology, Zoology

Introduction

Bats (Chiroptera) are recognized as among the most ecologically and evolutionarily diverse groups of mammals1,2. They perform a wide range of vital ecosystem services, including pollination, seed dispersal and insect population control, all of which contribute to ecosystem stability1. Their biological traits, such as gregarious roosting behaviour and occurrence across diverse habitats, including limestone caves, tree hollows and abandoned buildings1, result in bats serving as natural reservoirs for microorganisms, including pathogenic agents such as viruses (Ebola, Nipah and multiple coronaviruses) and bacteria (Bartonella mayotimonensis and Candidatus Mycoplasma haemohominis)3–6. Moreover, high intra- and interspecific contact rates in bat colonies may facilitate the rapid transmission of pathogens and maintain acute-immunizing infections7.

Bat flies of the families Nycteribiidae and Streblidae are blood-feeding ectoparasites that live mainly on bat hosts8. However, bat fly host specificity varies considerably. Some bat fly species are highly host specific, for example, Noctiliostrebla traubi on Noctilio leporinus and Paratrichobius dunni on Uroderma bilobatum9. In contrast, other bat fly species inhabit a broader range of hosts, making it more difficult to study their behaviour and ecosystem interactions9. Systematic studies of bat flies in Thailand are scarce10. More importantly, bat flies are considered potential vectors of microorganisms, including pathogens; for example, Polychromophilus spp. are transmitted by nycteribiids2. Current knowledge of bat flies and their association with microorganisms in tropical regions of Southeast Asia remains limited2,11. In this context, molecular detection of Bartonella bacteria in bat flies has been reported elsewhere only in Malaysia12. In Thailand, there are only two previous reports of the molecular detection of Bartonella spp. from bat blood13,14.

Bartonella are gram-negative, facultative intracellular bacteria. Bats and bat flies harbour many varieties of Bartonella spp., indicating complex host–vector relationships and the potential emergence of novel bacterial strain types11,15. Several Bartonella spp. have been implicated in emerging human diseases16. Among these, B. mayotimonensis, which causes endocarditis in humans3,4, and the potentially zoonotic B. rousetti are associated with bats17. Studies in Africa have shown that Bartonella spp. found in bats and bat flies exhibit notable phylogenetic relationships17. Additionally, several species of Bartonella detected in other animals have been recognized as causing zoonotic diseases, e.g., cat scratch disease (B. henselae), Carrion’s disease (B. bacilliformis) and trench fever (B. quintana)16. In Thailand, B. tamiae was first detected in humans18 and later in rodents and ectoparasites (chigger mites and ticks)19. In addition, Candidatus Bartonella thailandensis was discovered in rodents20.

However, the vectorial role of bat flies in Bartonella bacterial transmission remains poorly understood in Southeast Asia, including Thailand. In this report, the first molecular identification of morphological species of bat flies and the diversity of Bartonella bacteria in certain areas of Thailand are presented. Their phylogenetic relationships are also described.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (SCMU-ACUC), Faculty of Science, Mahidol University, Thailand (approval number MUSC 64-018-567). All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations for the care and use of animals. Furthermore, all methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Bat fly collection and species identification

In 2022, a total of 50 bat flies were collected from 17 cave-dwelling bats at four sites in Thailand. These were three caves in Lopburi Province (central region), i.e., Khao Wong (LW; n = 38; 15°2’36"N, 101°18’48"E), Bot Yawas (LB; n = 5; 15°1’59"N, 101°19’19"E), and Khao Chan (LC; n = 4; 14°58’46"N, 101°18’42"E), and one cave in Sa Kaeo Province (eastern region close to the Cambodian border), i.e., Khao Chakan (SC; n = 3; 13°50’19"N, 102°9’48"E). All bat capture and handling procedures were conducted under the supervision of a bat specialist, Associate Professor Dr. Sara Bumrungsri. Bats were captured using mist nets21 set up at cave entrances after sunset. Mist nets were continuously monitored during capture to prevent overcrowding and to minimize the risk of injury. Captured bats were carefully disentangled and placed individually in clean, breathable cotton bags. Bat flies were collected directly from their bat hosts using sterile forceps, transferred into 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tubes and preserved in 70% ethanol for subsequent analyses. After species identification22, the bats were promptly released in front of the cave. The holding time for bats during identification and bat fly collection did not exceed one hour to minimize stress to the animals. No bats were injured or killed during this study. Morphological identification of the bat flies was performed under a stereomicroscope using standard taxonomic keys23–25 for molecular analysis in the laboratory at Mahidol University.

DNA extraction

All 50 individual bat fly samples were surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol followed by 10% sodium hypochlorite and rinsed three times in sterile distilled water. DNA was extracted individually using the QIAamp DNA Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany)26 and stored at -20 °C.

PCR amplification and DNA sequencing

Multigene analyses were performed for bat fly species identification, Bartonella detection and identification. For bat flies, four target genes were used, including three mitochondrial genes—cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI)27, cytochrome c oxidase subunit II (COII)28, and 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA)29—and one nuclear gene, 18S ribosomal RNA (18S rRNA)28, for species identification. For Bartonella detection and identification, all 50 bat fly samples were analysed using five genetic loci, namely, citrate synthase (gltA)30, internal transcribed spacer (ITS)31, RNA polymerase subunit beta (rpoB)32, NADH dehydrogenase gamma subunit (nuoG)33, and cell division protein FtsZ (ftsZ)34. Primary screening was performed using gltA and ITS, followed by confirmation with rpoB, nuoG, and ftsZ. Primer sets used for each locus are listed in Table 1. These analyses followed published multilocus genotyping recommendations35.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed using a SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA) with initial denaturation (94 °C, 5 min), followed by 35 cycles (94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50–55 °C for 30 s depending on the primer sets, 72 °C for 1 min) and a final extension (72 °C, 10 min). Each run included positive and negative controls. PCR products were visualized on 1% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide at a final concentration of 0.5 µg/mL36, purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) and sequenced by the Sanger method (Macrogen, South Korea).

Phylogenetic analysis and pairwise comparisons of gene sequences

Nucleotide sequences were trimmed and assembled using MEGA X37 and compared to reference sequences in GenBank using BLAST (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST) to confirm the identity of the bat fly and Bartonella species. Multiple sequence alignments were performed with related sequences using the ClustalW algorithm in MEGA X, and pairwise identity percentages were calculated in BioEdit v7.238. Diagrams of pairwise comparisons for bat fly species and Bartonella species groups were prepared using Microsoft PowerPoint 365.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed to examine the relationships among the bat flies and Bartonella bacteria detected in this study. For the bat flies, the models used were COI (GTR + G+I), COII (GTR + G+I), 16S rRNA (GTR + G) and 18S rRNA (T92 + G). For Bartonella, the models used were gltA (T92 + G), rpoB (HKY + G), nuoG (T92 + G), ftsZ (TN93 + G) and the ITS region (K2 + G + I). Bootstrap values were calculated using 1,000 replicates. The alignment lengths for the bat fly genes were as follows: COI (609 bp), COII (595 bp), 16S rRNA (476 bp) and 18S rRNA (1,437 bp). For Bartonella, the alignment lengths were as follows: gltA (303 bp), rpoB (395 bp), nuoG (293 bp), ftsZ (925 bp), and the ITS region (295 bp). In addition, maximum likelihood trees were constructed based on concatenated sequences of four loci (3,117 bp) for bat fly species and five loci (2,211 bp) for Bartonella species.

Results

Bat fly species

All 50 bat fly samples were morphologically identified as five distinct species belonging to four genera across two families: Streblidae: Brachytarsina cucullata (Bc: n = 18, nos. LW8.1, LW14-25, LW31-33, LW40-41); B. macrops (Bm: n = 6, LW8.2, LB3-5, LB7, LB9); Megastrebla gigantea (Mg: n = 2, LW3, LW30); Raymondia sp. (Rsp: n = 7, LC1-4, SC3-5); and Nycteribiidae: Eucampsipoda latisterna (El: n = 17, LW1-2, LW4-7, LW9-11, LW26-29, LW35-36, LW38-39). They were collected from five species of cave-dwelling bats, namely, Eonycteris spelaea (Es), Hipposideros larvatus (Hl), Taphozous theobaldi (Tt), T. melanopogon (Tm) and Rousettus sp. (Rs).

Five bat fly species were identified based on their morphological characteristics (Fig. S3). Brachytarsina cucullata (body length < 2 mm) has a dark head and a very large triangular postvertex39(Fig. S3a), whereas B. macrops (1.9–2.1 mm) resembles it but has a darker head and an almost circular postvertex23(Fig. S3b). The dorsal view of Raymondia sp. shows a slightly quadrangular head (Fig. S3c). Megastrebla gigantea (4.2–4.6 mm) is distinguished by a dark, heavily pigmented head that contrasts with the reddish-brown body and a postvertex that is typically triangular but sometimes broadly ovoid25,39(Fig. S3d). Eucampsipoda latisterna is recognized based on Theodor’s (1967) diagnostic characteristics, including short, broad claspers bearing a row of spines that become longer and slenderer towards the base, with a few minute spines near the tip24(Fig. S3e).

For molecular characterization of these species of bat fly, multigene analysis involving four genes commonly used for genetic markers (COI, COII, 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA) was employed. The results of phylogenetic analyses based on these gene sequences revealed interspecific genetic differences, providing the first molecular identification of these bat flies and confirming that they are valid morphological species23–25. All the bat fly sequences from these analyses have been deposited in GenBank, and their accession numbers are summarized in Table S1.

Phylogenetic analyses of bat flies

Bat fly species were molecularly confirmed, and phylogenetic trees of four genes with respective diagrams were generated. Phylogenetic analysis based on the COI gene and the respective diagrams are depicted in Fig. 1a, b. Pairwise comparisons of the gene sequences revealed that the five bat fly species were quite distinct, with identities ranging from 78.79 to 87.7% (Table S3). For example, B. cucullata LW15 and B. macrops LB3 shared 85.08% identity, whereas B. cucullata LW15 and M. gigantea LW3 shared 87.70% identity (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, B. macrops LB3 and B. cucullata LW15 were 100% identical to Brachytarsina sp. 2 and sp. 3, respectively, previously reported from Thailand40. However, we observed gene sequences from a previous report in Thailand40 that demonstrated that Brachytarsina sp. 2 and sp. 3 shared 84.66% identity. Thus, it was most likely that Brachytarsina sp. 2 and sp. 3 actually belonged to B. macrops and B. cucullata, respectively. Interestingly, M. gigantea LW3 and B. cucullata LW15 were 100% and 87.70% identical, respectively, to reported Brachytarsina sp. C from Hong Kong41, while B. cucullata shared 90.48% identity with B. amboinensis from China42. Moreover, we observed that the gene sequences of B. amboinensis from China and Brachytarsina sp. C from Hong Kong shared 91.46% identity. These data suggest that Brachytarsina sp. C should be placed with M. gigantea in the genus Megastrebla (Fig. 1a, b). In addition, Raymondia sp. SC5 was clearly related to Raymondia sp. BF1 from Thailand (99.51% identity), whereas it differed from Raymondia sp. A from Hong Kong (94.58% identity). Nevertheless, E. latisterna LW9 shared 100% identity with Eucampsipoda sp. from Pakistan43, which was listed as E. africana in GenBank (Fig. 1a, b).

Phylogenetic analysis based on the 18S rRNA gene and the respective diagrams (Fig. 2c, d) also revealed results similar to those obtained for the three genes mentioned above. The pairwise comparison identity among the five bat fly species ranged from 92.60 to 98.24% (Table S3). Additionally, Raymondia sp. The concatenated phylogenetic tree based on four genes revealed five distinct species of bat flies in two families, i.e., Streblidae (B. cucullata, B. macrops, M. gigantea and Raymondia sp.) and Nycteribiidae (E. latisterna) (Fig. S1). The results strongly supported the data obtained from mitochondrial (COI, COII and 16S rRNA) and nuclear (18S rRNA) genes, which confirmed the morphological species of the bat fly under examination (Fig. S1).

Molecular identification of Bartonella bacteria

Molecular detections and identifications for Bartonella bacteria from 50 bat flies using multigene analysis involving five genetic markers (gltA, rpoB, nuoG, and ftsZ genes and the ITS region) yielded 24 positive samples from four bat fly species, namely, B. cucullata (n = 10), E. latisterna (n = 7), B. macrops (n = 5) and Raymondia sp. (n = 2), while M. gigantea yielded negative results. All Bartonella sequences obtained in this study have been deposited in GenBank, and their accession numbers are provided in Table S2.

Multilocus phylogenetic analyses of these gene sequences revealed six distinct Bartonella species groups (Nos. 1–6) on the basis of the cut-off criteria for 4 genes, i.e., gltA (96.00%), ITS (99.80%), rpoB (95.40%) and ftsZ (97.90%), except for the nuoG gene, as proposed by La Scola et al. (2003)44. Notably, molecular detection of Bartonella bacteria using the ftsZ gene yielded only three positive Bartonella species groups, i.e., Groups 1, 5 and 6.

The six Bartonella species groups included Group 1 (n = 15: LW8.1, LW8.2, LW14-19, LW23, LW25, LW31, LB3, LB4, LB7, LB9), Group 2 (n = 4: LW1, LW2, LW26, LW35), Group 3 (n = 1: LW5), Group 4 (n = 1: SC3), Group 5 (n = 1: SC5), and Group 6 (n = 2: LW6, LW7). Bartonella species Group 1 was detected in B. cucullata (Bc) and B. macrops (Bm), whereas Groups 2, 3 and 6 were found only in E. latisterna (El), and Groups 4 and 5 were found only in Raymondia sp. (Rsp) (Fig. 3). Samples from each Bartonella species group were randomly selected as representatives for molecular analyses, i.e., Group 1 (LW15, LB3), Group 2 (LW2, LW26), Group 3 (LW5), Group 4 (SC3), Group 5 (SC5) and Group 6 (LW6, LW7), as shown in the following details (Fig. 3).

The concatenated phylogenetic tree constructed from the five target markers (gltA, rpoB, nuoG, and ftsZ genes and the ITS region) strongly supported the existence of six Bartonella species groups (Nos. 1–6) (Fig. S2). The results revealed that Bartonella species Groups 1, 4 and 5 were related to the Bartonella strains previously detected in bat blood from Thailand (Fig. S2). Groups 1 and 5 were also related to the Bartonella strains previously detected in bat blood from Kenya and Vietnam, respectively. In addition, Groups 3 and 6 were related to the Bartonella strains previously detected in bat flies from China. Moreover, Group 6 was related to the Bartonella strain from bat flies in Egypt. Group 2 was related to B. rousetti strains detected in both bat blood and bat flies from Africa (Fig. S2). Intergroup gene sequence identities among the four bat fly species ranged from 64.80 to 89.87% (Table S4), well below the cut-off criteria for species delineation of La Scola et al. (2003)44. The data indicated that each group represented a distinct lineage. Collectively, these findings highlight the substantial genetic diversity among the six Bartonella species groups, reinforcing their recognition as separate lineages.

Discussion

Bat flies of the Streblidae and Nycteribiidae families are obligatory haematophagous ectoparasites of bats and exhibit a high degree of host specificity and evolutionary relationships with their bat hosts. These insects rely on bats not only for blood meals but also for reproduction and dispersal, forming a tightly linked host‒parasite association in certain ecological settings, especially in Africa17,45, the Middle East46 and Asia13–15,41,47. In Southeast Asia, the species diversity and distribution of bat flies remain underexplored, and precise species identification has been hindered by morphological convergence and cryptic or sibling species10,23,25.

Two decades ago, molecular analysis of nycteribiid and streblid bat flies using four genes (18S rDNA, 16S rDNA, COII and cytB) revealed geographically driven evolutionary divergence, with distinct clades corresponding to Old World and New World species48. Our study contributes significantly to the understanding of the species diversity of bat flies in Thailand in that molecular analyses involving COI, COII, 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA genes, integrated with morphological characterization, have enabled the updating of taxonomic keys25. These integrative approaches yielded a more reliable identification of several taxa of bat flies, particularly those previously assigned with uncertainty. For instance, we were able to verify the occurrence of five morphological species in Thailand, i.e., B. cucullata, B. macrops, M. gigantea, Raymondia sp. and E. latisterna, and elucidate their phylogenetic relationships. Thus, B. cucullata, B. macrops and M. gigantea belong to the same clade within the Streblidae family, whereas Raymondia sp. belongs to a different clade, although its morphological species status has not yet been formally described. E. latisterna of the family Nycteribiidae clearly belonged to a distinct clade (Fig. S1). Moreover, we classified the species status of M. gigantea (Streblidae). This species was previously reported under the name “Brachytarsina sp. C” from Hong Kong41 on the basis of morphological characteristics described in Jobling (1951)23. Two decades later, Maa (1971)25 extensively revised the taxonomy of the genus Brachytarsina on the basis of the morphological characteristics of body size and other details, reclassifying Megastrebla as a new genus separating it from Brachytarsina25. In addition, the author described M. gigantea collected from three taxa of bats, namely, Dobsonia praedatrix, D. moluccensis magna and D. peronii sumbana from New Britain, New Guinea and Indonesia, respectively. Therefore, we employed the updated taxonomic key of Maa (1971)25 for the morphological identification of M. gigantea, which was subsequently confirmed by multigene analysis. However, M. gigantea (LW3) collected from bats (E. spelaea and Rousettus sp.) in Lopburi Province, Thailand, showed 100% concordance with Brachytarsina sp. C from Hong Kong on the basis of the COI gene available from GenBank41. Hence, our results strongly suggest that M. gigantea is likely conspecific with Brachytarsina sp. C. Furthermore, the M. gigantea reported herein is a new record for Thailand according to the current species checklist of bat flies10.

Additionally, B. macrops (LB3) and B. cucullata (LW15) found in central Thailand were conspecific with Brachytarsina sp. 2 and Brachytarsina sp. 3, respectively, from western Thailand (Kanchanaburi Province)40. Raymondia sp. (SC3 and SC5) was closely related to Raymondia sp. BF1 from Thailand49 and Raymondia sp. A from Hong Kong41, whereas E. latisterna (LW9) in central Thailand was closely related to Eucampsipoda sp. from Pakistan43. These findings suggest that these species of bat flies occurring in Asian regions share evolutionary histories and ongoing gene flow.

Beyond their ecological role as ectoparasites, bat flies are increasingly recognized as potential vectors of pathogenic microorganisms, including Bartonella spp. Molecular detection and phylogenetic analyses of Bartonella species have been performed mainly on bat flies or bat blood13–15,47. In our study, a comprehensive molecular survey of Bartonella spp. in the five species of bat flies described above using multigene analysis targeting the gltA, rpoB, nuoG, and ftsZ genes and the ITS region is presented. The phylogenetic relationships revealed that the six Bartonella species groups were distributed in three clades. Thus, Groups 1 and 2 clustered in one clade, whereas Groups 4, 5 and 6 formed another distinct clade. Group 3 presented a distinct genetic distance from the other two clades (Fig. S2). Bartonella species Groups 1, 4 and 5 showed high sequence identity to those strains previously detected in bat blood from Thailand13,14 and Vietnam47, suggesting that they might be transmitted in Thailand and Southeast Asia. However, Groups 3 and 6 were detected in the bat flies as new records for Thailand, and they are closely related to those found in China15 and Egypt46. Surprisingly, Group 2 showed very close phylogenetic relationships with Bartonella rousetti, a potential pathogenic species associated with zoonotic infections in Africa17,45. Our findings indicated the possibility of zoonotic transmission of Bartonella species Group 2 via bat flies (E. latisterna) parasitizing bat hosts (E. spelaea and Rousettus sp.), which are commonly found in karst caves adjacent to agricultural fields in central Thailand.

Interestingly, B. rousetti, formerly documented as Bartonella sp. genogroup R, was first detected in the blood of the bat Rousettus aegyptiacus in Kenya, although it was not proposed as a novel Bartonella species at that time50. Eight years later, in Nigeria, similar strains of Bartonella were detected in both the blood of R. aegyptiacus and in bat flies (Eucampsipoda africana), while antibodies against Bartonella were detected in humans17. This led to the description of a new bacterium, Bartonella rousetti, named after the genus of its bat host17. B. rousetti was subsequently detected in the same bat and bat fly species in Zambia45. These data suggest that B. rousetti may be more widespread among African fruit bats than previously reported17,45,51.

Previous studies have shown variable prevalence rates of Bartonella infection in both bats and their ectoparasitic bat flies across different regions. In bats, Bartonella bacteria were detected in 12.43% (22/177) of individuals in Nigeria17, 36.56% (34/93) in Thailand14, and 25.49% (117/459) in Thailand13. In contrast, higher prevalence rates were reported in bat flies, including 47.37% (9/19) in Zambia45, 52% (13/25 pools) in China15, and 66.67% (26/39 pools) in Malaysia12. In the present study, Bartonella bacteria were detected in 24 of 50 bat flies (48%). This prevalence is comparable to that in previous reports from bat flies and is notably higher than that in most records from bats. The consistently higher prevalence observed in bat flies likely reflects their role as biological vectors and potential reservoirs of Bartonella bacteria.

The distinct Bartonella groups detected in this study exhibited host-associated clustering patterns that were consistent with previous findings. Group 1, detected from Brachytarsina cucullata and B. macrops parasitizing Taphozous theobaldi and T. melanopogon, respectively, clustered with Bartonella genogroups Tm and G2, which were previously reported from T. melanopogon13,14, indicating host specificity among Brachytarsina bat flies and their Taphozous bat hosts. Groups 4 and 5, detected from Raymondia sp. parasitizing Hipposideros larvatus, also showed close relationships with previously defined Bartonella genogroups within Hipposideros bats. Group 4 was closely related to genogroup H2 reported from H. larvatus bats14 and genogroup G4 from H. kunzi bats13, whereas Group 5 was related to genogroup H1 from H. armiger bats14 and genogroup G5 from Rhinolophus coelophyllus bats13. These findings suggest the host specificity of Bartonella lineages associated with Raymondia bat flies and their Hipposideros bat hosts. In contrast, Groups 2, 3, and 6, associated with Eucampsipoda latisterna, which parasitize Eonycteris spelaea and Rousettus sp., were related to Bartonella lineages previously reported from Eucampsipoda bat flies collected from Rousettus aegyptiacus and Eucampsipoda species in Africa and Asia15,17,45,46, suggesting broader host associations between Eucampsipoda bat flies and their Pteropodidae bat hosts. Although the number of samples was limited, these data suggest possible host-specific associations in bat fly–bat systems.

In conclusion, six distinct Bartonella species groups found in the four bat fly vectors parasitize five species of cave-dwelling bats in central and eastern Thailand, reflecting the rich biodiversity in this tropical region. Such diverse species of bat flies and Bartonella spp. appear to suggest a dynamic evolutionary landscape of Bartonella bacteria, bat fly vectors and bat hosts, indicating the possibility of coevolution or host-switching events facilitated by their close ecological relationships11,41. Further research on Bartonella species in association with bat fly vectors is needed to better understand the dynamic transmission, zoonotic potential and evolutionary relationships of hosts, vectors, and microorganism in Thailand and Southeast Asia.
