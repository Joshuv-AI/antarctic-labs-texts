Chicken interleukin-26: characterization and antigen capture enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
Authors: Hyoyoun Nam, Youngsub Lee, Minsung Park, Hyun S. Lillehoj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interleukin-26, a cytokine belonging to the IL-10 family, is an inflammatory mediator in mammals and has been shown to modulate T cell proliferation and gene expression in avian species. Given the limited understanding of chIL-26′s function at the protein level, our objective was to produce specific mAbs to chIL-26 and create an immunoassay to investigate its biological significance in chickens. We generated five anti-chIL-26 monoclonal antibody clones by using recombinant chIL-26 as an immunogen in mice. Western blot and indirect ELISA analyses confirmed that all five mAbs specifically recognize the recombinant chIL-26 protein. Using a pairing assay, we determined that the combination of capture antibody #10C7 and biotinylated detection antibody #8A5 was the most effective pair for a sandwich ELISA for chIL-26 detection. To further validate a newly developed antigen-capture ELISA chIL-26 detection, we first stimulated chicken macrophage cells with three agonists—Lipopolysaccharides, polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid, and Resquimod-848 at various concentrations. Second, qRT-PCR was used to confirm that LPS induced significant chIL-26 expression. The changes in chIL-26 production over time was monitored using a monoclonal antibody (mAb) combination (#10C7 and biotinylated #8A5). Antigen capture ELISA revealed a significant increase in IL-26 protein secretion, which peaked at 24 hours post-stimulation. This pattern mirrored the expression of chIL-26 mRNA, as detected by qRT-PCR. This assay was also successfully used to detect the changes in IL-26 levels in the serum of chickens infected with Eimeria maxima (E. maxima) and E. tenella. Compared to the uninfected chickens, IL-26 levels in E. maxima-infected chickens began to increase from 1-day post-infection (dpi), peaked at 3 dpi, and then rapidly decreased. In contrast, IL-26 levels in E. tenella-infected chickens peaked at 7 dpi. All newly produced mAbs specific for chIL-26 effectively neutralized the function of IL-26, as measured by cell proliferation assays and IL-1β expression using qRT-PCR analysis. These new anti-chIL-26 mAbs and the antigen-capture ELISA will be valuable tools for further dissecting the role of chIL-26 in poultry inflammatory responses and diseases.

Introduction

Interleukin-26 (IL-26), initially identified as a product of activated or transformed T cells, is now recognized as a novel inflammatory mediator, particularly associated with Th17 and NK cell populations (Donnelly et al., 2010). IL-26 is a relatively poorly characterized member of the IL-10 cytokine family which includes IL-10, IL-19, IL-20, IL-22, and IL-24. Structurally characterized as a 19-kDa α-helical protein, IL-26 is uniquely amphipathic and lacks a known murine homolog, posing challenges for conventional in vivo modeling (Larochette et al., 2019).

IL-26 exerts its biological effects through a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of IL-20RA and IL-10RB, leading to the activation of the Jak-STAT3 signaling cascade which induces the expression of a range of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, GM-CSF, TNF-α, and various interferons, as well as IL-8 and IL-10 in epithelial cells (Sheikh et al., 2004; Hör et al., 2020; Larochette et al., 2019). In addition to receptor-mediated signaling, IL-26 directly binds to the extracellular DNA, facilitating intracellular inflammatory responses and amplifying immune activation (Totter et al., 2020). Although IL-26 has not been widely implicated in cancer, recent vivo screening has revealed its unexpected role in promoting mammary engraftment and lung metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer, suggesting a previously unrecognized link between IL-26 and tumor progression (Totter et al., 2020).

In avian species, chicken IL-26 (ChIL-26) shares moderate sequence homology with its mammalian counterparts and is functionally expressed in T cells (Truong et al., 2016). ChIL-26 has been shown to modulate T cell proliferation and induced proinflammatory gene expression across various chicken cell lines, including T cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts (Truong et al., 2016). Given these findings, further investigation at the protein level will be necessary to better understand the biological significance of ChIL-26 in poultry in normal and inflammatory responses.

Accordingly, the objective of this study was to produce monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that specifically recognize chicken IL-26 that will enable a more comprehensive characterization of IL-26 function and, furthermore, to develop an immunoassay-based research tool to elucidate its role in health and disease in chickens.

Materials and methods

Recombinant IL-26

Purified recombinant chicken IL-26 protein was expressed in Escherichia coli using the previously described methods (GenScript Inc., Piscataway, NJ, USA) (Nam et al., 2025). The complete sequence encoding chIL-26 was cloned into the pET-30a expression vector, which includes an NH2-terminal polyhistidine epitope-tag using the primers containing NdeI and HindIII restriction sites (477 amino acids amino acids with molecular weight = 17,627.8). E. coli strain BL21 star (DE3) cells (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY) were then transformed with the chIL-26 recombinant plasmid for recombinant protein expression.

Production of mouse chIL-26 mAbs

The recombinant IL-26 was utilized as an immunogen to produce chIL-26-specific monoclonal antibodies in mice (Genscript Inc., Piscataway, NJ) (Lee et al., 2024; Nam et al., 2025). Hybridoma cells producing antibodies against chicken IL-26 were selected and grown in a large flask for purification using protein G affinity chromatography as described (Nam et al., 2025). To ensure specificity, hybridoma clones exhibiting the highest optical density (OD) values against chIL-26 in ELISA were selected and subjected to two rounds of single-cell cloning. These clones were then expanded in large flasks, and the mAbs were purified via Protein G affinity chromatography (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL). In brief, hybridoma supernatants were incubated with the Protein G agarose column overnight at 4°C, and bound antibodies were eluted using Pierce™ IgG Elution Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA). The concentration of each purified chIL-26 mAbs was measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Immunoblotting analysis

The recombinant IL-26 protein (1 µg) in sample buffer containing 10% 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, USA) and 4X Laemmli sample buffer (Bioworld, USA), was heated for 5 minutes at 95°C. Subsequently, 20 µl of the mixture per lane was loaded onto Mini-PROTEAN® precast gels (Bio-Rad, USA) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane using the Trans-Blot® Turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad, USA). The membranes were blocked with Superblock Blocking Buffer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) and incubated with 1 µg/ml of anti-chIL-26 mAbs (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., USA) diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer, the membranes were visualized using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, add more product source) and detected using ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, CA). All incubation steps were carried out on a shaker, and the membranes were washed with PBS-T following each incubation.

Detection of chIL-26 by indirect ELISA

Flat-bottomed 96-well High-binding plates were coated with recombinant chIL-26 protein at concentrations of 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 µg/ml in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C. To prevent non-specific binding, the plates were blocked with SuperBlock Blocking Buffer (Thermo Scientific, IL, USA) for 1 hour. Subsequently, the blocked plates were incubated for 2 hours with anti-chIL-26 monoclonal antibodies (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7) at a concentration of 1 µg/ml. Immunoreactivity was assessed by incubating for 1hr by HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson Immuno Research) diluted 1:5000 in blocking buffer. The antigen-antibody interaction was visualized using 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and the reaction was terminated with 2 N H2SO4 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). All incubations were performed at room temperature on a shaker, washed five times with PBS-T between steps, and OD at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bioteck, Winooski, VT).

Development of antigen capture ELISA

The pairing assay was conducted using anti-chIL-26 monoclonal antibodies (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7) as capture antibodies, while biotinylated anti-chIL-26 mAbs (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7), labeled with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-Biotin™ reagent (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA), served as detection antibodies, as previously described (Nam et al., 2025). For biotinylation, 1 mg of each mAb was incubated with a 10 mM solution of the biotin reagent for 1 h at room temperature. The mixture was then desalted using Zeba Spin Desalting Columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL). Briefly, flat-bottomed 96-well High-binding plates were coated with anti-chIL-26 mAbs at a concentration of 1 µg/ml and incubated at 4°C overnight. The following day, the plates were blocked with SuperBlock Blocking Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL) for 1 hour, followed by incubation with 1 µg/ml of recombinant IL-26 protein for 2 hours. Next, the plates were exposed to 1 µg/ml biotin-labeled anti-chIL-26 mAbs for 1 hour and subsequently conjugated with avidin-HRP (1:5,000) for another hour. After each incubation step, the plates were washed five times with PBS-T. The antigen-antibody interaction was detected using avidin-HRP secondary antibody diluted 1:5,000 in blocking buffer. The visualization of the reaction and absorbance assays were conducted as previously described (Nam et al., 2025). Based on the findings of the pairing assay, the antibody pair exhibiting the highest OD value was chosen for further investigation of IL-26 levels in HD11 cell supernatant and serum samples from Eimeria-infected chickens.

Expression of IL-26 in chicken HD11 cells

Chicken macrophage cells (HD11) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Hyclone, USA), supplement with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Sigma, USA) at 41°C in a humidified incubator with atmosphere of 5% CO2. Briefly, the cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells. The cells were incubated for 24 hours and then stimulated with three different agonists at three different concentrations: Lipopolysaccharides from E. coli O55:B5 (LPS; Sigma, USA) at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 µg/ml), polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly I:C; EMD Millipore Corp., USA) at 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 µg/ml, and Resquimod-848 (R-848; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 µg/ml for an additional 24 hours. The medium alone was used as a vehicle control. After stimulation with each agonist, the cells were harvested for qRT-PCR analysis, while the supernatant was collected for capture ELISA assay. Based on the result of the stimulation assay, the agonist that elicited the highest IL-26 production was chosen for additional investigation. Following the agonist comparison in HD11 cells, LPS was identified as the most effective stimulus for IL-26 secretion. HD11 cells were subsequently treated with LPS at a concentration of 1 µg/ml, and both the cell supernatant and cells were collected at distinct time points (0, 5, 24, and 48 h). Post-stimulation, the isolated cells were subjected to qRT-PCR analysis, while the supernatant was utilized for antigen capture ELISA. At each time point, six samples were collected, with each sample consisting of three replicates derived from two independent experiments.

Monitoring changes in IL-26 levels using antigen-capture ELISA

Based on agonist screening in HD11 cells, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was identified as the most effective stimulator of IL-26 secretion. HD11 cells were treated with LPS at a concentration of 1 µg/ml, and both supernatant and cells were collected at specified time points (0, 5, 24, and 48 h). Following stimulation, separated cells were subjected to qRT-PCR analysis, while supernatants were processed for antigen capture ELISA. At each time point, six samples were collected, with each sample consisting of three replicates from two independent experiments.

To assess the circulating IL-26 levels during coccidiosis, 60 serum samples were obtained from male broiler chickens (Ross 708) experimentally infected with E. maxima and E. tenella. These samples, derived from a previously approved study by the Beltsville Agricultural Research Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (#20-015), were analyzed for IL-26 using a newly developed antigen capture ELISA using the #10C7 and #8A5 antibody pair (Lee et al., 2024). IL-26 quantification in HD11 cells and serum from Eimeria-infected chickens was done as follows: high-binding 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with the capture antibody (#10C7) at a concentration of 1.0 µg/ml in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer. The plates were subsequently blocked with SuperBlock Blocking Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL) for 1 h at room temperature (R/T). Supernatants from HD11 cells (6 samples/time point) and serum samples (6 samples/time point), diluted 1:1 in PBS, were applied to the wells and incubated for 2 h at R/T. Following this, the plates were incubated with the biotinylated detection antibody (#8A5) at 1.0 µg/ml for 1 h at R/T. Finally, avidin-HRP conjugate (diluted 1:5,000 in PBS) was added for 1 h at R/T. All washing, incubation, reaction development, and visualization steps were performed under conditions identical to those used in indirect ELISA assays.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated from HD11 cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA). The RNA concentration and yield were measured with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technology, USA) at 260/280 nm. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed in a 25 µl reaction volume using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen Inc., Valencia, CA), and the resulting cDNA was diluted 1:5 in RNase-free water. The cDNA was stored at −20°C until PCR analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (PowerTrack, Applied Biosystems, Vilnius, Lithuania), PCR amplification was performed with the following IL-26 primers: forward 5′-AATGCCTGTCTTCCGTGTG -3′ and reverse 5′-TCATTGATGGCCTTGTAGACC -3′. The Applied Biosystems QuantStudio3 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was employed. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the transcripts were normalized to GAPDH (forward 5′-GGTGGTGCTAAGCGTGTTAT-3′ and reverse 5′-ACCTCTGTCATCTCTCCACA-3′) using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Schmittgen et al., 2000).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the commercial Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) (Kim et al., 2017). HD11 cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 1 mL of culture medium and incubated for 24 hours. The cells were then divided into eight groups and exposed to the following conditions: a control group (medium only), a group treated with recombinant chIL-26 (500 ng/mL), and five groups pre-incubated with recombinant chIL-26 (500 ng/mL) in combination with specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (each at 2 µg/mL): #5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7. Additionally, one group was pre-incubated with recombinant chIL-26 (500 ng/mL) and chicken IL-12 mAb (#10G10F4, 2 µg/mL), which had been used in a previous study as controls (Lee et al., 2024). Following a 24-hour incubation at 37°C, cells were treated with 20 µL of CCK-8 solution per well and further incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an automated microplate spectrophotometer (Bioteck, Winooski, VT). Two independent experiments were performed, each consisting of three replicates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test in GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Prism, San Diego, CA). Results are expressed as mean ± SD, with *p < 0.05, ** p < 0.005, *** p < 0.0005, and **** p < 0.0001 considered statistically significant when compared to control group.

Results

Development of hybridomas secreting chIL-26 mAbs

After initial screening of hybridomas for their specific binding activity to chIL-26 protein, five anti-chIL-26 mAbs clones (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7) were selected and purified for further characterization. Each mAb demonstrated specific recognition of recombinant chIL-26 protein with a molecular weight of approximately 18 kDa, as evidenced by a single band in western blot analysis (Fig. 1A). The recombinant IL-26 protein showed the highest binding efficiency at 1 µg/mL, and the indirect ELISA showed a dose-dependent pattern (Fig. 1B). Among the tested mAbs, three antibodies, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7, showed highest OD values with recombinant IL-26 protein at 1 and 0.5ug/ml dose ranges. Notably, #7H10 mAb showed a higher OD value compared to other mAbs at lower concentrations (0.25 and 0.125 µg/mL). Pairing assay for antigen capture ELISA

Based on the results of the pairing analysis, the optimal combination selected for further investigation was the capture antibody #10C7 mAb paired with the detection antibody biotinylated #8A5 mAb (Fig. 2). Both #5D11 and #10C7 mAb exhibited consistently high binding affinity when used as capture antibodies, while the biotinylated #8A5 mAb demonstrated strong OD values as a detection antibody. The detectable range for this assay combination was 0.5–1,500 ng/mL (data not shown). Given the second-highest binding affinity observed for the combination of #5D11 as the capture antibody and biotinylated #8A5 as the detection antibody, this pairing combination was used for confirmatory analysis following the evaluation of the #10C7-biotinylated #8A5 combination. As shown in Fig. 3A, LPS stimulation led to a significant increase in IL-26 mRNA expression at all concentrations, with the highest level observed at concentration of 1.0 μg/mL (p < 0.0001). Although poly I:C treatment showed increased chIL-26 levels at a concentration of 0.5 μg/mL, while stimulation with R-848 resulted in a slight increase at concentrations of 1.0, and 2.0 /mL, these differences were not statistically significant compared to untreated control values. Temporal changes in secreted IL-26 mRNA levels in HD11 cells following LPS stimulation (1.0 μg/mL) were assessed at 5, 24, 48 hours post treatment (Fig. 3B). A modest increase was detected at 5 hours (p < 0.05), followed by a peak in IL-26 expression at 24 hours (p < 0.0001), after which levels declined at 48 hours. Fig. 3D illustrates the secretion profile of IL-26 from HD11 cells stimulated by LPS over 48 hrs as quantified using a newly developed antigen-capture ELISA assay incorporating capture mAb #10C7 and biotinylated #8A5 as the detecting mAb. The secretion pattern mirrored the mRNA expression trend, with a slight elevation at 5 hrs (p < 0.05), a sharp increase at 24 hrs (p < 0.0001), and a subsequent decline at 48 hrs post-stimulation. Changes in IL-26 Levels in Eimeria-infected chickens

As illustrated in Fig. 4, serum IL-26 levels in broiler chickens were assessed during infection with E. maxima and E. tenella using a newly developed sandwich ELISA, with the unlabeled monoclonal antibody #10C7 as the capture antibody and biotinylated #8A5 as the detection antibody. Following infection with E. tenella, serum IL-26 levels were significantly increased at all time points tested compared to the uninfected control group. IL-26 levels increased markedly at 3 days post-infection (dpi), showing a slight decline at 5 dpi, but peaked at 7 dpi (p < 0.001). Similarly, in chickens infected with E. maxima, IL-26 levels remained significantly elevated throughout the infection period compared to the uninfected controls. However, unlike E. tenella-infected chickens, IL-26 levels in E. maxima-infected chickens were notably higher at 3 dpi (p < 0.001) and gradually declined over time. Notably, this pattern bears a strong resemblance to the IL-34 production dynamics observed in E. tenella- and E. maxima-infected chickens as we described in our previous study (Nam et al., 2025). Neutralization effect of IL-26 by anti-IL-26 mAbs

The neutralizing effect of IL-26 mAbs on HD11 proliferation was evaluated using HD11 cells after stimulating with rchIL-26 using a cell proliferation assay with the CCK-8 kit (Fig. 5A). Stimulation with rchIL-26 significantly increased cell proliferation (p < 0.0001) compared to medium control. However, pre-incubation of rchIL-26 with anti-chIL-26 mAb (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7) resulted in markedly reduced proliferation (p < 0.0001) compared to the non-treated control group. These findings indicate that five mAbs effectively neutralized the biological activity of recombinant chIL-26. In contrast, cells treated with recombinant chIL-26 pre-incubated with the chIL-12 mAb (#10G10F4) exhibited proliferation levels comparable to those stimulated with recombinant chIL-26 alone, suggesting a lack of neutralizing effect. The neutralizing effects of the mAbs detecting chIL-26 were further validated using qRT-PCR analysis, specifically using expression of chicken IL-1β cytokine in HD11 cells (Fig. 5B). Stimulation with recombinant chIL-26 significantly upregulated IL-1β expression (p < 0.0001) compared to the medium control. However, pre-incubation of recombinant chIL-26 with five anti-chIL-26 mAb clones (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7) resulted in a marked reduction in IL-1β expression relative to stimulation with recombinant chIL-26 alone. Discussion

Interleukin-26 (IL-26) has been identified as a member of the IL-10 cytokine family, specifically within the IL-20 subfamily (Gowhari Shabgah et al., 2022). Although the precise biological role of IL-26 in chickens needs to be better studied, this study showed that the growth of HD11cells was enhanced upon IL-26 stimulation in vitro suggesting that poultry IL-26 stimulates immune cell differentiation in a manner analogous to its function in humans.

IL-26 is recognized as a pro-inflammatory cytokine, and its interaction with the heterodimeric receptor complex—comprising IL-10R2 and IL-20R1—on epithelial cells induces the release of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 (Hor et al., 2004; Sheikh et al., 2004). Notably, monocytes and macrophages are capable of responding to IL-26 to produce inflammatory cytokines, even though they express only the IL-10R2 subunit of the receptor complex (Corvaisier et al., 2012). Previous studies have shown that IL-26 is expressed in various immune cell types, including monocytes and macrophages, with its expression significantly upregulated upon stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and IFN-γ (Larochette et al., 2019). In line with these findings, our study demonstrated a marked increase in IL-26 secretion from HD11 cells following LPS stimulation.

In this study, we provide the first evidence that recombinant chIL-26 induces IL-1β expression in avian macrophage HD11 cells, mirroring the pro-inflammatory activity observed in human macrophages. However, further investigation is required to elucidate the underlying signaling mechanisms.

Availability of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that specifically recognize the recombinant chIL-26 protein (#5D11, #6C3, #7H10, #8A5, and #10C7) and the chIL-26 specific ELISA will enable the monitoring of IL-26 secretion in chickens infected with pathogens, serving as valuable immunological tools for detecting IL-26 in various immunological studies in chickens. The validity of this newly developed ELISA was further supported by its ability to detect changes in serum IL-26 levels in chickens infected with E. maxima and E. tenella, underscoring its applicability in avian disease models. Interestingly, IL-26 production exhibited distinct temporal patterns depending on the Eimeria species. The earlier peak in E. maxima infection (3 dpi) likely reflects its broad tissue tropism in the small intestine and rapid induction of innate-like Th17 responses. In contrast, the delayed peak in E. tenella (7 dpi) may be attributed to its deep invasion into the cecal submucosa and the severe tissue damage occurring during the later stages of its life cycle, possibly involving different immune evasion strategies.

Crucially, the utility of these newly developed mAbs extends beyond simple detection to the functional modulation of IL-26 activity. Stimulation of HD11 cells with recombinant chIL-26 led to increased IL-1β expression, and this effect was effectively neutralized by the chIL-26 mAbs, as evidenced by reduced cytokine levels. These findings confirm the functional neutralization capacity of the newly developed antibodies. More importantly, as demonstrated in this study, the induction of IL-1β by IL-26 suggests that this cytokine plays a pivotal role in potentiating early host defense mechanisms by stimulating the innate immune system. Consequently, future studies are warranted to determine whether IL-26 acts as an upstream regulator that triggers the pathological cascades associated with autoimmune diseases.

Taken together, these novel immunological reagents offer valuable tools for dissecting the role of chIL-26 in poultry inflammatory responses.
