Pathogenic bacteria exploit transferrin receptor transcytosis to penetrate the blood–brain barrier
Authors: Zhihui Cheng, Yangyang Zheng, Wen Yang, Hongmin Sun, Fangyu Zhou, Chuangjie Huang, Shuwen Zhang, Yingying Song, Qi’an Liang, Nan Yang, Meifang Li, Bin Liu, Lu Feng, Lei Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificancePathogenic bacteria can reach the brain to cause meningitis resulting in high mortality and morbidity. The brain is particularly protected and isolated from the rest of the body by the blood–brain barrier (BBB). How these bacteria penetrate the BBB remains poorly understood. We demonstrate a penetration mechanism employed by three major meningitis-causing bacterial pathogens, Streptococcus pneumoniae, group B Streptococcus, and neonatal meningitis Escherichia coli. Central to this mechanism is the fusion of bacteria-containing vesicles with transferrin receptor (TfR) vesicles to couple the bacterial penetration of the BBB to TfR transcytosis. These findings provide insights into the development of broad-spectrum approaches against bacterial meningitis and shed light on strategies for using TfR transcytosis to deliver therapeutics across the BBB.

Bacterial meningitis, a devastating disease occurring worldwide, remains a major cause of high mortality and morbidity (1). Pathogenic bacteria circulating in the blood penetrate the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and invade the brain, where they then multiply and induce the release of proinflammatory factors and toxic compounds, which in turn lead to meningitis (2). Neonatal meningitis Escherichia coli (NMEC) and group B Streptococcus (GBS) are the two major causative agents of neonatal meningitis, and Streptococcus pneumoniae (SPN) is the major causative agent of bacterial meningitis after the neonatal period (2). The mechanisms by which pathogenic bacteria penetrate the BBB, the prerequisite for them to cause meningitis (3), remain poorly understood.

The human BBB is composed of human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs). The unusually low rate of transcytosis allows the BBB to protect the brain from bloodborne agents, including pathogens and toxins (4, 5). Following circulation in the bloodstream and adherence to the apical side of the BBB, pathogenic bacteria exploit different strategies to penetrate the BBB to reach its basolateral side (3, 6). SPN, GBS, and NMEC can penetrate the BBB through the transcellular pathway by invading and crossing through HBMECs (7–10). After invasion, NMEC, GBS, and SPN reside in membrane-bound vesicles (8, 11, 12); however, the mechanisms by which these bacteria-containing vesicles (BCVs) are trafficked from the apical side to the basolateral side, especially with the low rate of transcytosis in HBMECs, remain unknown.

Transferrin receptor (TfR) transcytosis is one of the few active transcytosis pathways in HBMECs (13, 14). TfR is highly expressed at the BBB and binds the iron-binding protein transferrin (Tf) in the blood, internalizes with endocytic vesicles through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and transcytoses iron across the BBB (15).

In this study, we present evidence showing that the infection with NMEC, GBS, and SPN, activates the same fusion process of BCVs with TfR vesicles, which enables those bacteria to couple their penetration of BBB to TfR transcytosis, and illustrate the details of this process in HBMECs and mouse models. This vesicle fusion is temporally and spatially ensured by a unique modification of TRAF3 downstream of the Toll-like receptor (TLR)–TRAM–TRIF pathway, the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA–exocyst subcomplex I (SC I) complex on BCVs, and the sequential tethering of SC I through the interaction of SEC6 with the soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor activating protein receptor (SNARE) protein SNAP23 on TfR vesicles. This vesicle fusion in response to innate immune signaling coupling bacterial expulsion to the intracellular trafficking of TfR vesicles represents a HBMEC-specific cellular immune defense mechanism that is exploited by major meningitis-causing bacterial pathogens to traverse the BBB and infect the brain.

Results

Intracellular Trafficking of TfR Is Necessary for Pathogenic Bacterial Transcytosis of HBMECs.

Since TfR colocalizes with BCVs in NMEC-infected HBMECs (7), we first confirmed that it also colocalizes with BCVs in GBS- or SPN-infected HBMECs. Confocal microscopy showed that 70%, 71%, and 73% of intracellular NMEC-, GBS- and SPN-containing vesicles were colocalized with TfR at 4 h p.i. (2 h before bacterial transcytosis becomes evident), respectively (Fig. 1A). Using a polarized HBMEC monolayer with tight junctions in Transwells in vitro (16), we then showed that silencing TfR (96% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) significantly reduced the numbers of all three bacteria that are transcytosed (to the basolateral side of the cell) or exocytosed (to the apical side of the cell) (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) but did not affect bacterial endocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Silencing ACAP1 (70% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), which promotes endocytic TfR recycling to the cell membrane (17), significantly reduced bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis but did not affect bacterial endocytosis (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). Thus, bacterial traversal is related to TfR intracellular trafficking in HBMECs.

The Binding of SNAP23 and SEC6 of Exocyst Subcomplex I Tethers BCVs to TfR Vesicles to Initiate Their Fusion.

NMEC and TfR enter HBMECs through two distinct endocytic pathways and thereafter reside in different endocytic compartments (2, 18, 19). We first confirmed that TfR on BCVs is endocytosed from the cell membrane by showing that membrane TfR colocalizes with BCVs at the similar rate as to total TfR (Fig. 2A) and that the inhibition of protein synthesis in HBMECs prior to NMEC infection had no effect on the colocalization of TfR with BCVs (Fig. 2B). Therefore, BCVs and TfR vesicles must have fused in HBMECs after NMEC infection, resulting in the colocalization of BCVs with TfR.

SNAREs, localizing to distinct organelles, mediate vesicle tethering and fusion (20). Proteomic analysis on BCV fraction of HBMECs infected with NMEC found SNARE proteins SNAP23, VAMP3 and 8, and syntaxin 4 and 5 (Dataset S1). Among these proteins, silencing of only SNAP23 (84% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) significantly reduced the colocalization of TfR with BCVs (Fig. 2C). Confocal microcopy showed that 71% of BCVs were colocalized with SNAP23, and these SNAP23-positive BCVs were in the cytosol (Fig. 2D). Both TfR and SNAP23 were detected in the BCV fraction, although the amount of TfR was significantly reduced when SNAP23 was silenced (Fig. 2E). Silencing SNAP23 also significantly reduced bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Thus, SNAP23 is involved in the fusion of BCVs with TfR vesicles.

We next found that SEC6 is involved in this vesicle fusion via an interaction with SNAP23. SNAP23 interacts with SEC6, VAMP2, 3, and 8, and syntaxin 2, 3, 4, and 5 under different conditions (21–23). As silencing of VAMP3, 8 and syntaxin 4, 5 did not affect the colocalization of TfR with BCVs (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C) and VAMP2 and syntaxin 2 and 3 were not involved (Dataset S1), we focused on SEC6. Western blot analysis showed that the interaction between SEC6 and SNAP23 was significantly increased in infected HBMECs (Fig. 2G). Confocal microscopy showed that 72% of intracellular BCVs were colocalized with SEC6 and that these SEC6-positive vesicles were in the cytosol (Fig. 2H). Silencing SEC6 (66% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S2D) significantly reduced the colocalization of TfR with BCVs (Fig. 2I). The amount of TfR was significantly reduced in the BCV fraction isolated from SEC6-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 2J). The role of SEC6 in vesicle fusion was further confirmed by the finding that NMEC transcytosis and exocytosis were significantly reduced in SEC6-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 2K and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E).

SEC6 is a subunit of SC I, and SC I on vesicles binds to SNARE proteins on target membranes to initiate vesicle tethering before fusion (24). We then confirmed the presence of SC I by showing that other subunits of SC I, SEC5, and SEC8 are colocalized with BCVs and found that this colocalization was significantly reduced in SEC6-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 2L). Since SNAP23 and the exocyst complex are usually located on opposite membranes to mediate the fusion of vesicles with the cell membrane via reciprocal tethering (22), SEC6 and SNAP23 are likely uniquely located on BCVs or TfR vesicles before fusion. SNAP23 was detected on purified TfR vesicles before and after NMEC infection, while SEC6 was detected on the vesicles only after infection (Fig. 2M). Silencing SNAP23 had no effect on the colocalization of SEC6 with BCVs, while silencing SEC6 significantly reduced the colocalization of SNAP23 with BCVs (Fig. 2N). These results indicate that SEC6 in SC I on BCVs binds to SNAP23 on TfR vesicles to tether these two types of vesicles and initiate their fusion.

The Small GTPase RalA Interacts with SEC6 to Recruit SC I to BCVs.

Small GTPases recruit the exocyst complex, including SC I, to vesicles and facilitate their tethering to target membranes (25, 26). Coimmunoprecipitation and proteomic analysis showed that small GTPase RalA interacted with SEC6 only after NMEC infection (Dataset S2). Confocal microscopy showed that 75% of BCVs were colocalized with RalA in NMEC-infected HBMECs at 4 h p.i. (Fig. 3A). The colocalization of TfR and SNAP23 with BCVs and the amount of TfR and SNAP23 in the BCV fraction were significantly reduced (Fig. 3 B and C), when RalA was silenced (55% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). RalA but not SNAP23 was enriched in the BCV fraction isolated from SEC6-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 3D). In addition, the transcytosis and exocytosis of NMEC were significantly reduced in RalA-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B).

Proteomic analysis showed that the subunits of SC I, SEC5, SEC6, and SEC8, interacted with RalA only after NMEC infection (Dataset S3). The colocalization of SEC5, SEC6, and SEC8 with BCVs and the amounts of SEC5, SEC6, and SEC8 in the BCV fraction were significantly reduced when RalA was silenced (Fig. 3 F and G). Western blot analysis showed no difference in the level of binding of RalA to SEC6 between SEC5- or SEC8-silenced HBMECs and control cells; however, the binding of RalA to both SEC5 and SEC8 was significantly reduced in SEC6-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 3H). Thus, RalA is involved in vesicle fusion by interacting with SC I through the direct binding of SEC6. Because SEC6 also interacts directly with SNAP23 on TfR vesicles, we conclude that SEC6 plays a crucial role in the tethering of BCVs to TfR vesicles.

TRAF3 Activates RalA via RCC2 to Recruit SC I.

RalA can interact with a number of effectors (27). Small GTPases, including RalA, recruit SC I through direct binding with SEC5 (28). Therefore, it is striking that RalA deposited on BCVs precisely recruited SC I through interaction with SEC6 among its effector pool, which includes SEC5 (Fig. 3 G and H and Dataset S3). We reasoned that the mechanisms by which signals emanate from BCVs and orchestrate the communication between effector proteins to mobilize RalA to BCVs achieve the precise recognition and selection of SEC6 by RalA. We first investigated the mechanism by which RalA is mobilized to BCVs. Ral guanine nucleotide exchange factors (RalGEFs), including RCC2, bind to and activate RalA by promoting its transition from a GDP- to a GTP-bound state (27). RCC2 was found to interact with RalA only after NMEC infection (Dataset S3). Confocal microscopy showed that 72% of BCVs were colocalized with RCC2 in NMEC-infected HBMECs at 4 h p.i. (Fig. 4A). NMEC infection caused a marked increase in GTP binding to RalA (Fig. 4B). Silencing RCC2 with siRNA (75% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) markedly reduced the amount of GTP-bound RalA and the colocalization of both RalA and TfR with BCVs (Fig. 4C). The amounts of RalA and TfR were significantly reduced in the BCV fraction isolated from RCC2-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 4D). Thus, RCC2 on BCVs specifically recruits and activates RalA.

Then, we investigated the mechanism by which RCC2 is deposited on BCVs. By proteomic analysis, TRAF3 was found to be immunoprecipitated with RCC2 only after NMEC infection (Dataset S4). We first confirmed that TRAF3 is anchored on BCVs before it interacts with RCC2 by showing that silencing RCC2 did not affect the colocalization of TRAF3 with BCVs (Fig. 4E) and that the colocalization of TRAF3 and TfR increased significantly after NMEC infection (Fig. 4F). We next confirmed that TRAF3 mediates the recruitment of RCC2 and RalA to BCVs. Silencing TRAF3 (85% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S4B) markedly reduced the colocalization of RCC2 and RalA with BCVs (Fig. 4G). The amounts of RCC2 and RalA were significantly reduced in the BCV fraction isolated from TRAF3-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 4H). Thus, TRAF3 recruits RCC2 to BCVs on NMEC infection. Then, we confirmed that the interaction between TRAF3 and RCC2 on BCVs determines the specific binding of RalA to SEC6. RalA and SEC6 do not interact with each other when they are not located on BCVs, indicating that only RalA activated by RCC2 on BCVs can interact with SEC6 (Figs. 3 F and G and 4C and Dataset S3). Silencing TRAF3 or RCC2 reduced the colocalization of SEC5, SEC6, SEC8, and TfR with BCVs compared to that in control siRNA-treated HBMECs (Fig. 4I). The amounts of SEC5, SEC6, SEC8, and TfR were significantly reduced in the BCV fraction isolated from TRAF3- or RCC2-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 4J). In addition, the transcytosis and exocytosis of NMEC were significantly reduced in TRAF3- or RCC2-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 4K and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Thus, the complex of TRAF3, RCC2, and activated RalA on BCVs enables the recruitment of SC I via specific binding of SEC6.

We then examined the phosphorylation of RalA, since both GTP binding and phosphorylation are required for the full and situation-adapted activity of RalA (29). RalA is phosphorylated at serine residues (S183 and S194) (29) and the levels of phosphorylation at S183 and S194 of RalA in NMEC-infected HBMECs were significantly higher than those in uninfected cells (Fig. 4L). We then substituted S183 or S194 in RalA with arginine, and western blot analysis showed that SEC6 was immunoprecipitated with WT RalA-Flag but not with mutant RalA (S183A or S194A)-Flag in HBMECs after infection (Fig. 4M). Overexpression of WT RalA significantly increased but overexpression of mutant RalA reduced the transcytosis and exocytosis of NMEC (Fig. 4N and SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Transgenic mice expressing mutated RalA (S194A) had significantly reduced bacterial titers in cerebrospinal fluid after tail vein infection compared to those in WT mice (Fig. 4O). Thus, the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex on BCVs activates RalA to recruit SC I.

Polyubiquitination of TRAF3 at K168 and K181 Triggered by the TLR4 Signaling Pathway Is the NMEC BCV Selection Signal for TfR Vesicles.

We have now shown that NMEC infection triggers the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex on BCVs. TRAF3 can function as a molecular rheostat, coordinating distinct subsets of innate signaling events depending on the different ubiquitin modifications that it experiences in different types of cells (30, 31). We hypothesized that TLR4 signals emanating from NMEC BCVs activate TRAF3 in a HBMEC-specific manner and trigger its interaction with RCC2. TLR4 recognizes LPS of gram-negative bacteria (32). We first showed that TLR4 activated by NMEC infection contributes to vesicle fusion. Seventy-three percent of BCVs were colocalized with TLR4 in NMEC-infected HBMECs at 4 h p.i. (Fig. 5A). Silencing TLR4 (65% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) or infecting HBMECs with the NMEC mutant ΔmsbB, which lacks LPS, markedly reduced the colocalization of TfR with BCVs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Silencing of TLR4, inhibition of TLR4 activation with a specific inhibitor, TAK-242, or infection with ΔmsbB significantly reduced bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C).

To examine whether activation of the TLR4 signaling pathway initiates the recruitment of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex to BCVs, we silenced TLR4 in HBMECs or infected HBMECs with ΔmsbB and found that the colocalization of TRAF3, RCC2, and RalA with BCVs and the amounts of these proteins in the BCV fraction were significantly reduced (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D and C). Moreover, the colocalization of SEC6 and SNAP23 with BCVs and the amounts of these two proteins in the BCV fraction were markedly reduced under the two conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D and C). Thus, activation of TLR4 on BCVs triggers TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex formation.

To confirm that TLR4 activates TRAF3 through the TRAM–TRIF but not the TIRAP–MyD88 signaling pathway, we silenced TRAM, TRIF, or TIRAP (85%, 65%, or 61% reduction, respectively, SI Appendix, Fig. S6A) and found that the colocalization of BCVs with TfR was markedly reduced only in TRAM- or TRIF-silenced HBMECs (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). In addition, the amounts of TRAF3, RalA, and TfR were significantly reduced in the BCV fraction from these cells (Fig. 5 D and E). The transcytosis and exocytosis of NMEC were significantly reduced in TRAM- or TRIF-silenced HBMECs compared to control siRNA-treated HBMECs (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Gene-deficient TRIF−/− mice showed significantly reduced bacterial titers in the cerebrospinal fluid after tail vein infection (Fig. 5G). Therefore, TLR4-initiated and TRIF- and TRAM-mediated signals emanating from BCVs lead to the recruitment and activation of TRAF3 to induce the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex.

Next, we infected HBMECs with nonpathogenic E. coli K-12 or coinfected with E. coli K-12 and NMEC and found that E. coli K-12 could not enter nor cross through HBMECs under these two conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). We isolated E. coli K-12 after incubation with HBMECs and found that TRAM, TRIF, and TRAF3 were absent in the bacterial fraction (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). Then we incubated LPS-coated latex beads with HBMECs and found that colocalization of TRAM, TRIF, and TRAF3 with latex beads could not be detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). These data indicate that only TLR4 signals emanating from BCVs lead to the recruitment and activation of TRAF3 via TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway in HBMECs.

TRAF3 activated by the compartmental TLR4–TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway can interact with Ral guanine nucleotide dissociation stimulator (RalGDS) or TBK1 due to K33-linked polyubiquitination at K168 or K63-linked polyubiquitination at unidentified residues of TRAF3, respectively (30, 31). Therefore, we hypothesized that the interaction of TRAF3 with RCC2 preceding the fusion of BCVs and TfR vesicles does not occur simply through the detection of bound TRAF3 molecules on BCVs but rather through the detection of a unique polyubiquitinated form of TRAF3. Since the zinc finger motifs at the N-terminus of TRAF3 are responsible for its interaction with RalGEFs (30), we substituted every lysine residue in this region (K138, K154, K156, K160, K168, and K181) with arginine and found that overexpression of TRAF3 K168R or K181R in HBMECs significantly reduced bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (Fig. 5H and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). We then substituted each lysine of ubiquitin (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63) with arginine and found that overexpression of ubiquitin K33R in HBMECs significantly reduced bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Overexpression of TRAF3 K168R or K181R or ubiquitin K33R significantly reduced the colocalization of RCC2, RalA, and TfR with BCVs (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Thus, K33-linked polyubiquitination at K168 and K181 of TRAF3 triggered by the TLR4 signaling pathway results in the specific recruitment of RCC2 to BCVs and the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex.

Collectively, these findings reveal that upon NMEC infection, TLR4 signals emanating from BCVs recruit TRAF3 and activate K33-linked polyubiquitination at K168 and K181 of TRAF3, leading to the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex on BCVs. RalA in this complex recruits SC I through binding to SEC6 that then interacts with SNAP23 on TfR vesicles and results in selective tethering of BCVs to TfR vesicles (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

GBS and SPN Exploit the TLR2–TRAF3–RalA–SEC6–SNAP23 Axis to Hitchhike on TfR Transcytosis and Penetrate the BBB.

We next investigated whether the TLR–TRAF3–RalA–SEC6–SNAP23 axis is also exploited by GBS and SPN to mediate the fusion of BCVs with TfR vesicles and penetrate the BBB. TLR2 recognizes peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid (LTA), the major pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) of gram-positive bacteria (33), and compartmental TLR2 signals can activate the TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway (34). Since compartmental TLR4 signals on NMEC BCVs trigger a unique form of polyubiquitination of TRAF3 through the TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway, we investigated whether the TLR2 signals emanating from GBS or SPN BCVs activate TRAF3 through the TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway in HBMECs. More than 65% of GBS or SPN BCVs were colocalized with TLR2 or TRIF (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). Silencing TLR2 (79% reduction, SI Appendix, Fig. S10C) or TRIF in HBMECs significantly reduced the colocalization of TRAF3 with BCVs, as well as bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D–F and Fig. 6A). We infected HBMECs with nonpathogenic Staphylococcus epidermis and found that S. epidermis could not enter nor cross through HBMECs (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B). We isolated S. epidermis after incubation with HBMECs and found that TRAM, TRIF, and TRAF3 were absent in the bacterial fraction (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). Then, we incubated LTA–coated latex beads with HBMECs and found that colocalization of TRAM, TRIF, and TRAF3 with latex beads could not be detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). These results indicate that TLR2 signals emanating from GBS or SPN BCVs lead to the recruitment and activation of TRAF3 through the TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway and are involved in bacterial intracellular trafficking.

We next showed that TRAF3 activated by TLR2 signals undergoes the same mode of polyubiquitination as TRAF3 activated by TLR4 signals in NMEC-infected HBMECs. More than 65% of GBS or SPN BCVs were colocalized with RCC2, RalA, SEC5, SEC6, and SEC8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B), and the amounts of these proteins were significantly reduced in the BCV fraction isolated from HBMECs silencing TRAF3, overexpressing ubiquitin K33R, or overexpressing the TRAF3 K168R or K181R mutant compared to control cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). The involvement of RCC2, RalA, and SEC6 in vesicle fusion was confirmed by the finding that silencing these proteins significantly reduced the colocalization of TfR with BCVs, as well as bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A–C and Fig. 6B). Then, we confirmed the roles of SNAP23 by showing that silencing SNAP23 significantly reduced the colocalization of TfR with BCVs, as well as bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 A–C and Fig. 6C). Therefore, in HBMECs, GBS and SPN infection activates TLR2 signaling on BCVs, resulting in the same form of polyubiquitination of TRAF3 induced by NMEC infection through the TRAM–TRIF signaling pathway. This unique form of polyubiquitination leads to the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA–SC I complex on BCVs, which results in vesicle tethering through the interaction of SEC6 in SC I with SNAP23 on TfR vesicles and, presumably, the vesicle fusion that enables bacterial hitchhiking on TfR transcytosis and penetration of the BBB (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This mechanism was further confirmed by comparing transcytosis of GBS and SPN across the BBB in our meningitis mouse model. TRIF−/− mice and mice expressing RalAS194A showed significantly reduced bacterial titers of GBS or SPN in the cerebrospinal fluid after tail vein infection (Fig. 6 D and E).

Discussion

The fusion of BCVs with TfR vesicles to expel intracellular bacteria may represent a HBMEC-specific defense mechanism. However, the leading meningitis-causing bacteria exploit this mechanism to penetrate the BBB. As a protective cell-autonomous immune response, nonimmune cells use the subcellular trafficking mechanism to immediately exocytose BCVs back to the extracellular milieu or shuttle them to the lysosome, where bacteria are digested or exocytosed (35, 36). Meningitis-causing bacteria have evolved mechanisms to prevent the fusion of BCVs with lysosomes in HBMECs (7, 37); thus, HBMECs must rely extensively on exocytosis to clear the invaded bacteria. TfR trafficking is one of the most active intracellular trafficking pathways and constantly shuttles TfR back to the cell membrane (38); it is reasonable that HBMECs select TfR trafficking for bacterial expulsion. Here, we found that innate immune responses trigger the fusion of BCVs with TfR vesicles to couple bacterial transcytosis and exocytosis with TfR trafficking. Only a small portion of internalized TfR as well as bacteria are transcytosed across the BBB (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) (38). Although some bacterial pathogens have gained the ability to counteract host immunity in the brain and immune responses against these bacteria in the brain can be substantial (39, 40), it is not surprising that only a small number of bacteria invading the brain can lead to meningitis (Figs. 4O, 5G, and 6 D and E).

We demonstrated a mechanism which ensures that only vesicles loaded with bacteria can fuse with TfR vesicles in HBMECs. The accuracy of this mechanism is authenticated by three factors: the unique polyubiquitination of TRAF3 on BCVs, the formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA–SC I complex on BCVs, and the interaction between SEC6 in SC I and SNAP23 on TfR vesicles. We showed that compartmental TLR2 and TLR4 signals on BCVs trigger a previously unknown process of K33-linked polyubiquitination at K168 and K181 of TRAF3 in HBMECs. Polyubiquitinated TRAF3 recruits RCC2 and then RalA and SC I, unlike its known functions when modified by other forms of polyubiquitination (30, 31). The formation of the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex on BCVs ensures the recruitment of SC I through the interaction of RalA with SEC6. SC I primarily tethers intracellular vesicles to the cell membrane prior to fusion (41), and the recruitment of SC I has been demonstrated to be mediated only by the direct interaction of SEC5 with Ral GTPases, including RalA (24). In HBMECs, we found the major subunits of SC I, SEC5, SEC6, and SEC8, on BCVs (Dataset S1); however, SEC6 was directly bound to RalA on BCVs, and this binding recruited SC I to BCVs, suggesting that SEC6 has a distinct function endowed by the TRAF3–RCC2–RalA complex. The presence of SEC6 on BCVs authenticates the specific tethering to TfR vesicles by interaction with SNAP23.

SPN interacts with host PAFR, pIgR, and PECAM1 to adhere to the cell membrane and enter HBMECs (2). CaspR1 and Ecgp96 are host receptors to promote NMEC binding and invasion (3). Inflammation leads to an upregulated expression of receptors, which may include pIgR, PECAM1, on the plasma membrane of the apical side of HBMECs (42). It remains to be investigated whether TLR signals from the cell membrane facilitate bacterial entry by upregulation and activation of these receptors. NMEC and GBS initiate macropinocytosis to enter HBMECs (18, 43). SPN can enter HBMECs via three pathways, including clathrin and caveolae-mediated endocytic pathways and a dynamin-independent pathway, and form different BCVs (44). We showed that silence of TfR had weaker effect on SPN transcytosis compared to NMEC transcytosis (Fig. 1B). It is possible that SPN might also use other mechanisms, besides hitchhiking on TfR transcytosis, to penetrate the BBB.

We showed that NMEC, GBS, and SPN, major causative pathogens of bacterial meningitis during or after the neonatal period (2), have employed the same process within HBMECs to penetrate the BBB. To cause meningitis, these bacteria have to overcome host immune system to colonize in different parts of the body, reach the bloodstream, and survive and replicate within the blood before they enter HBMECs (2, 3). Aging is associated with profound changes in the human immune system (45), and it is highly likely that NMEC, GBS, and SPN employ different strategies to colonize, survive, and replicate and thus cause meningitis in people of different ages.

The BBB constitutes an obstacle for the delivery of therapeutic drugs into the brain (38). As one of the most active transcytosis pathways in HBMECs, TfR transcytosis has been extensively studied for drug transport, and some approaches using TfR-targeting antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates for delivery across the BBB have shown promising results in clinical trials (13, 38, 46). As a result of host and pathogen coevolution, major pathogenic bacteria exploit the same TfR transcytosis pathway to traverse the BBB. Unlike approaches that have been used to bind therapeutics to TfR, pathogenic bacteria remain in BCVs that fuse with TfR vesicles and thus hitchhike on TfR intracellular trafficking. The strong binding of therapeutics to TfR is a signal for the cell to target these molecules for degradation in the lysosome, which hinders TfR-targeted brain drug delivery (47). We found that in HBMECs, certain endocytic compartments fuse with TfR vesicles to hitchhike on TfR transcytosis and transport substances within these compartments across the BBB without TfR binding. By illustrating the mechanism and details of this vesicle fusion, our findings shed light on the development of novel approaches for delivering therapeutic drugs into the brain.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial strains were cultured in Luria–Bertani broth, Todd-Hewitt broth, or Tryptose Soya broth, respectively. HBMECs were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 10% Nu-Serum, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., GemPharmatech, or obtained from Nankai Animal Resources Center. Primers, siRNAs, shRNA, reagents, and experimental methods, including bacterial invasion assay, Transwell assay, immunofluorescence microscopy, isolation of TfR vesicles and intracellular BCVs, coimmunoprecipitation analysis, western blotting analysis, RalA activation assay, in vivo bacterial penetration of the BBB, quantification, and statistical analysis are described in Datasets S5 and S6 and SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
