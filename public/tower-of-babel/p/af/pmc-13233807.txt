Drivers of disparities in asthma exacerbation and healthcare utilization: a quantitative analysis of neighborhood deprivation in Southern California, United States
Authors: Stanley Xu, Fagen Xie, Daniel A. Powers, Eric J. Puttock, Michael Schatz, William M. Vollmer, Robert S. Zeiger, Wansu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWhile prior studies showed lower socioeconomic status was associated with worse acute asthma exacerbations (AAE) and asthma-related emergency department/urgent care (ED/UC) visits, few have quantified contributions of risk factors. We aim to identify and quantify risk factors driving these disparities across levels of neighborhood deprivation.MethodsWe conducted a cohort study of 79,562 adults with mild asthma in Kaiser Permanente Southern California, United States, during 2013–2018, with follow-up through 2019, comparing participants in Neighborhood Deprivation Index (NDI) quintiles five (Q5, most deprived) and one (Q1, least deprived). Outcomes included AAE and asthma-related ED/UC visits. We employed Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition to quantify contributions of 14 risk factors to these disparities.ResultsIndividuals in NDI Q5 had a 1.5% higher rate of AAE and a 3% higher rate of ED/UC visits than those in Q1. The 14 risk factors explained 61.2% of the AAE disparity and 52.3% of the ED/UC disparity. Race and ethnicity contributed most, 33.1% of the AAE disparity and 22.8% of the ED/UC disparity, followed by body mass index, prior asthma exacerbations, and exercise levels.ConclusionsThese results underscore the need for targeted interventions addressing socioeconomic and behavioral factors to reduce asthma-related health disparities, particularly in disadvantaged populations.

Introduction

Worse asthma exacerbations and asthma-related healthcare utilization are consistently linked with lower socioeconomic status (SES) (Redmond et al., 2022). Individuals with lower SES experienced poorer asthma control and increased use of emergency healthcare services (Bacon et al., 2009). National data from the National Health Interview Survey and the National Vital Statistics System (2006–2018) indicated that asthma prevalence, attacks, emergency department and urgent care (ED/UC) visits, and mortality were higher among racial and ethnic minorities, low-income individuals, and residents of small or non-metropolitan areas (Pate et al., 2021). Income is also a risk factor for asthma exacerbations and reduced lung function (Cardet et al., 2018).

While prior studies have established the association between socioeconomic factors and asthma outcomes, Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition quantifies how individual risk factors contribute to disparities. The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition partitions observed differences in outcomes between two or more groups into components attributable to differences in the distribution of risk factors and differences in their effects (Oaxaca, 1973; Blinder, 1973; Jann, 2008). Rashmi et al. (2021) used decomposition analysis to study asthma prevalence in India, identifying wealth and urban residence as key contributors. Sarpong and Miller, 2013 applied this method to explain racial disparities in asthma controller medication use, identifying both enabling and predisposing characteristics as major drivers.

Poor Neighborhood Deprivation Index (NDI) was previously associated with increased rates of acute asthma exacerbation (AAE) and greater ED/UC utilization in adults with mild asthma (Xu et al., 2024). Addressing these disparities requires identifying key contributing risk factors.

The objective of the current study is to assess how risk factors identified in a previous study contribute to differences in AAE and asthma-related healthcare utilization between the most deprived (NDI quintile 5, bottom 20%) and the least deprived (NDI quintile 1, top 20%) groups among adults with mild asthma in Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) during 2013–2018.

Methods

Study design and population

We established a cohort of adults with mild asthma as previously described (Xu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) using administrative data from KPSC, an integrated healthcare system in Southern California, United States, during 2013–2018, with follow-up through 2019. The demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of KPSC patients closely mirror those of the broader Southern California population (Davis et al., 2023, StataCorp, 2023).

We first identified adults with mild asthma using diagnosis, medication use, and healthcare utilization criteria. Eligible individuals had at least one inpatient or outpatient encounter with asthma listed as a primary or secondary diagnosis, were aged 18–85 years, and had low baseline treatment intensity defined as no more than one dispensing of a controller medication (including inhaled corticosteroids, ICS/LABA combinations, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and LAMA) and no more than two short-acting β-agonist (SABA) canisters in the prior year.

To capture a real-world population with low disease severity and limited baseline treatment intensity, we further excluded individuals with more than one asthma exacerbation, any asthma-related hospitalization, or a diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in the prior year, and required continuous health plan enrollment for at least one year before and after cohort entry.

For patients with multiple qualifying encounters, we randomly selected a single visit as the index visit. Mild asthma was defined based on medication use and healthcare utilization in the year prior, and outcomes were assessed in the subsequent year. A random index visit reduces selection bias and temporal variation.

Patients' addresses at the index visit were matched to 2020 Census Tract IDs, assigning each individual an NDI score based on level of deprivation from 2014 to 2018 (Andrews et al., 2020). Individuals were then categorized into NDI quintiles (Q), with higher quintiles indicating greater socioeconomic deprivation. A total of 79,562 patients residing in either NDI Q1 or Q5 were included in the analysis.

We selected the 2013–2018 study period, with follow-up through the end of 2019, to avoid COVID-19 disruptions. Although more recent data are technically available, both our cohort definition and outcome measures rely on healthcare utilization patterns substantially altered during the pandemic (Xu et al., 2021).

Measures

Study outcomes

The two not mutually exclusive study outcomes, described in detail previously (Xu et al., 2024), were the occurrence of AAE and asthma-related ED/UC visit within one year following the index visit. An AAE was identified as a hospitalization, ED visit, or hospital-based observation with a principal discharge diagnosis of asthma or wheezing, or (2) a principal diagnosis related to specific respiratory conditions, with either acute asthma exacerbation or status asthmaticus listed as a secondary diagnosis. Additionally, AAE included instances of systemic corticosteroid administration where asthma was recorded as the principal or primary encounter diagnosis, or when associated with a systemic corticosteroid order. An ED visit meeting diagnostic criteria for asthma exacerbation was counted as an AAE regardless of whether systemic corticosteroids were administered. Asthma-related ED/UC utilization was defined as any ED or UC visit with asthma or wheezing as the principal or primary diagnosis, or other specific respiratory conditions as the principal or primary diagnosis with asthma in any secondary coding position. ED/UC visits meeting criteria for AAE were classified as AAEs, while ED/UC utilization was analyzed separately to capture broader asthma-related healthcare use beyond exacerbation events.

Risk factors

The comparison was between individuals in NDI Q1 (least deprived) to those in NDI Q5 (most deprived). We selected 14 risk factors previously associated with AAE and/or asthma-related ED/UC visits: age, sex, race and ethnicity, minutes exercised/week, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), asthma/wheezing ED visits in prior year, asthma/wheezing UC visits in prior year, asthma exacerbation in prior year, chronic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, use of insulin, nasal polyp, and eosinophil count (Xu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). These factors were derived from KPSC Research Data Warehouse (Chen et al., 2023). Race and ethnicity data were primarily collected through patient self-report (Derose et al., 2013). Continuous variables (e.g., age, exercise, eosinophil counts) were categorized using clinically relevant cut points.

Statistical analysis

We summarized the characteristics of the individuals in NDI Q1 and Q5 using frequencies (%) and compared differences in AAE and ED/UC rates to quantify the disparity between the two groups. We fit separate multivariable logistic regression models to evaluate the associations between each of the 14 risk factors with AAE and asthma-related ED/UC visits. Models were stratified by NDI group (Q1 and Q5), and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. These models also provided the estimated coefficients and predicted probabilities used as inputs for the subsequent decomposition analyses.

To quantify the contributions of risk factors to disparities between groups, we applied Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition to partition differences in outcomes into components attributable to differences in the distribution of risk factors (explained component) and differences in their effects (unexplained component) (Oaxaca, 1973; Blinder, 1973; Jann, 2008; Rashmi et al., 2021). Because outcomes were binary, the decomposition was conducted on the scale of predicted probabilities using a nonlinear extension. Yun's linearization approach was used to estimate covariate-specific contributions (Yun, 2024).

In multivariate decomposition analyses of binary outcomes AAE and ED/UC, NDI Q1 served as the reference group. The overall difference in the outcome (∆G), defined as the difference in average predicted outcomes between groups, was partitioned into explained (E) and unexplained (U) components, reflecting differences in covariates and their associations with the outcome, respectively. For each risk factor k, its contribution (Ek) to ∆G is scaled by 1000 for interpretation, and the percentage explained was calculated as Ek/∆G ×100. Positive percentages indicate the extent to which the outcome in NDI Q5 would decrease if its covariate distribution matched that of NDI Q1, while negative values indicate the extent to which it would increase.

For categorical risk factors with more than two levels, coefficients were constrained to sum to zero in the logit models to ensure invariant decomposition results to the choice of reference group.

All analyses were conducted using Stata with the mvdcmp module (Powers et al., 2011; Stata, 2023). This study was approved by KPSC Institutional Review Board with waiver of informed consent and was conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the protection of human subjects, including safety and privacy.

Results

Demographic and health-related characteristics

Table 1 presents demographic and clinical characteristics of individuals in NDI Q1 and Q5. Individuals in NDI Q5 had a higher proportion of adults aged 18–24 (23.6%) compared to NDI Q1 (18.0%). NDI Q5 had a higher proportion of Hispanic (53.2%) and Black (22.6%) individuals, whereas NDI Q1 included a greater proportion of Non-Hispanic White (57.4%) and Asian/Pacific Islander (13.9%). Asthma/wheezing ED visits in the prior year were more frequent in NDI Q5 (5.5%) than Q1 (3.2%), as were prior asthma exacerbations (22.6% vs. 19.4%). Regarding health behaviors and comorbidities, individuals in NDI Q5 were more likely to be obese (52.5% vs. 33.0%), less likely to engage in ≥271 min of weekly exercise (11.6% vs.17.3%), and more likely to report no exercise (44.0% vs. 31.9%). Discussion

This study provides important insights into disparities in asthma outcomes. Our findings align with prior research showing that lower SES is associated with worse asthma outcomes (Bacon et al., 2009; Redmond et al., 2022). Race and ethnicity were the largest contributor, explaining 33.1% of the difference in AAE rates and 22.8% of the difference in ED/UC visits. These findings align with prior studies reporting that Black and Hispanic individuals often face greater asthma-related morbidity due to a combination of biological, environmental, and systemic factors (Forno and Celedon, 2009; Grant et al., 2022; Lovinsky-Desir et al., 2024; Holsey et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2024). Addressing disparities in social determinants of health in Black and Hispanic individuals would likely mitigate the disparities in asthma outcomes. These findings may be generalizable to other regions with similar socioeconomic gradients and healthcare utilization patterns.

BMI was another key driver, contributing 17.6% of the observed difference in AAE rates and 7.4% of the observed difference in asthma-related ED/UC utilization. Obesity has been consistently linked to worse asthma control, likely through both mechanical and inflammatory pathways that worsen airway function (Shore, 2008; Dixon and Poynter, 2016), suggesting weight management could reduce disparities in AAE.

Prior asthma exacerbations accounted for 10.2% of the difference in AAE and 1.9% of the difference in asthma ED/UC utilization, indicating higher risk of future adverse outcomes. This underscores the importance of early, sustained management including medication adherence and follow-up to prevent recurrent exacerbations (Global Initiative for Asthma Management and Prevention, 2022).

Although prior asthma exacerbation rates appear high for a population with mild asthma, this reflects the study's definition of mild asthma based on treatment intensity rather than absence of exacerbations. Individuals with frequent exacerbations or asthma-related hospitalizations were excluded, while a single prior asthma exacerbation was permitted, reflecting the heterogeneity of mild asthma in real-world settings. The observed prevalence of prior asthma exacerbations may therefore reflect suboptimal management or barriers to care rather than misclassification of asthma severity. Importantly, prior validation work based on chart review in a subset of cases demonstrated approximately 90% confirmation of asthma exacerbations, supporting the validity of outcome ascertainment.

Age may influence asthma outcomes in complex and divergent ways, potentially reflecting differences in healthcare-seeking behaviors or underlying disease mechanisms. While age has been consistently linked to asthma exacerbations in prior studies, its inverse relationship with asthma-related ED/UC utilization in our analysis aligns with literature suggesting that older adults may be more likely to manage asthma exacerbations in outpatient settings or may face different thresholds for emergency care. In contrast, biologic markers such as eosinophil counts, despite being associated with asthma severity in previous studies, did not explain disparities between the NDI Q1 and Q5 groups in our study, likely due to their similar distribution across the two groups.

Interestingly, chronic rhinitis and chronic sinusitis, although common asthma comorbidities, contributed little to the observed disparities, possibly because their prevalence and impact on asthma outcomes did not vary meaningfully by socioeconomic context. The minimal contribution of insulin use and nasal polyps may reflect their low prevalence or weak associations with outcomes examined. These findings may suggest that disparities in these asthma outcomes are more likely to be driven by modifiable behavioral factors (e.g., BMI and physical activity) or environmental factors than by biological factors such as chronic sinusitis or nasal polyps.

The findings of this study have important implications for designing targeted interventions to reduce asthma disparities. Race and ethnicity as major contributors highlight the need for culturally tailored asthma management programs that address systemic inequities in healthcare access and quality. Furthermore, addressing obesity through health education programs or integrating weight management strategies into primary care for patients with asthma could be an effective approach to improve outcomes in socioeconomically deprived populations. The role of exercise underscores the importance of providing safe and accessible environments that promote physical activity in low-income neighborhoods.

It is possible that neighborhood deprivation is intricately linked with disparities in asthma outcomes, partly through its influence on mediating factors. Prior healthcare utilization, which can be affected by living in a deprived neighborhood, may serve not only as a confounder but also as a mediator, wherein increased prior utilization partly channels the effect of NDI on future outcomes. Similarly, obesity, a modifiable risk factor associated with neighborhood deprivation, may mediate the relationship between NDI and asthma outcomes. Recognizing these factors as mediators, rather than confounders, clarifies how the effects of NDI are transmitted through pathways that are potentially modifiable, and may inform the development of targeted interventions for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.

Reliever and controller medication use were not included as explanatory variables in the decomposition analysis. Asthma medication use was intentionally restricted as part of the cohort definition (≤1 controller dispensing and ≤ 2 short-acting β-agonist canisters in the year prior to cohort entry) to identify individuals with mild asthma and relatively homogeneous baseline treatment intensity. As a result, baseline medication use varied minimally across NDI quintiles and was unlikely to explain observed disparities in asthma exacerbations or healthcare utilization. Moreover, reliever and controller use may reflect access to care, prescribing practices, or adherence behaviors that are themselves influenced by socioeconomic context and may lie on the causal pathway between neighborhood deprivation and asthma outcomes.

Our study has several strengths. First, we used a large, diverse cohort from a healthcare system, enhancing the generalizability of our findings to similar populations. Second, the application of a modified Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition analysis allowed us to disentangle the contributions of specific factors to observed disparities, providing a nuanced understanding that extends beyond traditional regression analyses. Furthermore, the inclusion of detailed clinical risk factors enabled us to assess the impact of both demographic and clinical risk factors on asthma outcomes. This study has limitations. Its population consisted of insured individuals within a healthcare system, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to uninsured populations or other settings. Additionally, unmeasured behavioral factors such as medication adherence, exposure to environmental triggers, and health literacy were not available. These factors may explain some of the unexplained disparity in outcomes. The observational nature of this study also precludes causal inferences.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the significant role of neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation in asthma outcomes, with race and ethnicity, obesity, and prior exacerbations being key contributors to disparities in AAE and asthma-related health care utilization. Targeted interventions addressing these factors are essential to reducing asthma-related inequities and improving health outcomes among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.
