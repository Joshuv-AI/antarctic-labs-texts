Epidemiology, resistance patterns, and outcomes of candidemia acquired in Italian intensive care units: insights from the GIVITI network (2020–2024)

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis multicenter study focuses on the latest (2020-2024) trends in candidemia in 155 Italian ICUs, stressing its frequency and lethality, with a shift toward non-albicans, fluconazole-resistant species, namely C. parapsilosis. In this study, resistance to fluconazole prolonged ICU stays without increasing mortality, while non-catheter-related episodes had a more severe prognosis.RationaleCandidemia is the leading fungal bloodstream infection (BSI) in the ICU and carries high mortality. After the COVID-19 pandemic, non-albicans, fluconazole-resistant species increased in Europe, yet contemporary ICU-acquired data are scarce. The primary outcome of this study was to evaluate the incidence of ICU-acquired candidemia per 1,000 “at risk” ICU admissions (ICU length of stay [LOS] ≥ 48 h). Secondary analyses compared ICU-acquired candidemia with ICU-acquired bacterial BSI and assessed species distribution, fluconazole susceptibility, and outcomes by fluconazole resistance and by source of infection.MethodsData were drawn from the PROSAFE network, a prospective multicentric observational project conducted across 192 Italian ICUs (2014–2023).ResultsAmong 179,590 ICU admissions, 95,662 were at risk. ICU-acquired candidemia occurred in 373 patients (3.9‰ of at-risk admissions). Compared with ICU-acquired bacterial BSI, candidemia patients had longer intra-ICU and hospital LOS (32 vs 24 days and 44 vs 36 days, respectively; p < 0.001) and higher mortality (intra-ICU 41.3% vs 28.7%; intra-hospital 50.3% vs 34.1%; p < 0.001). Candida albicans was isolated in 47.2% of episodes, followed by Candida parapsilosis at 33.0%; the latter is often fluconazole-resistant (53.7%). Mortality did not increase in fluconazole-resistant episodes, but ICU LOS did (36 vs 31 days; p = 0.016). Non-catheter-related infections (197/373, 52.8%) had higher intra-hospital mortality rates (57.0% vs 42.9%; p = 0.007).ConclusionsICU-acquired candidemia in Italy remains frequent and lethal, with a worrisome shift in epidemiology and resistance; the latter prolongs ICU stay without increasing mortality. Non-catheter-related episodes had a poorer prognosis. Findings support enhanced surveillance, catheter care, and earlier antifungal therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05886-1.

Background

Candida spp. bloodstream infection (BSI), or candidemia, is the most common fungal infection in the intensive care unit (ICU) and a leading cause of sepsis in critically ill adults. Its incidence in the ICU is up to tenfold higher than in non-critical wards, and it remains associated with mortality rates sometimes exceeding 40% despite modern care [1].

Estimates across Europe vary (5.5–10 episodes/1,000 ICU admissions) owing to geography and heterogeneous case definitions, as some investigations include all candidemia episodes in the ICU, while others restrict their analysis to ICU-acquired cases only [2–4] When only infections developing ≥ 48 h after admission are considered, incidence stabilizes around 5.5–6.7 per 1,000 at-risk admissions [5–7]. Despite infection control efforts, a significant increase in ICU-acquired candidemia episodes was registered in Europe both before and during the COVID-19 pandemic [8–10].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, European ICUs recorded higher rates of candidemia, mostly sustained by fluconazole-resistant non-albicans strains, namely Candida parapsilosis (C. parapsilosis), Candida glabrata (C. glabrata), and more recently Candida auris [11, 12]. Italy faced particular challenges related to severe healthcare resource strain and heavy antimicrobial usage, fostering the emergence of resistant strains [13].

Typical patients with candidemia are older men with multiple comorbidities, especially cardiovascular, renal, or hepatic failure, often undergoing central venous access device (CVAD) placement, parenteral nutrition, abdominal surgery, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial treatments [14].

Moreover, appropriate antifungal therapy is often delayed because of nonspecific symptoms and shifting epidemiology [15, 16]. As a result, mortality rates remained persistently above 30% in the last decade, reaching up to 71% at 30 days of follow-up, disregarding the antifungal agent used or the resistance profile of the isolate, according to recent studies [17–20].

Recent post-pandemic data on ICU-acquired candidemia are scarce in Europe, despite its clinical relevance [21]. In this study, we provide an updated national overview of ICU-acquired candidemia leveraging data from more than 150 Italian ICUs over the past five years.

The primary objective was to estimate the incidence of ICU-acquired candidemia per 1,000 at-risk admissions. Secondary objectives were 1) to compare clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with ICU-acquired candidemia vs. bacterial BSI, 2) to describe species and resistance trends in ICU-acquired candidemia, 3) to compare clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with ICU-acquired fluconazole-resistant vs. fluconazole-susceptible candidemia, and 4) to compare clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with catheter-related (CR) vs. non-CR ICU-acquired candidemia.

Methods

Study design

This study is a multicenter, retrospective, observational analysis from the PROSAFE initiative (PROmoting patient SAFEty and Quality Improvement in Critical Care) [22].

GiViTI network

The Italian Group for the Evaluation of Interventions in Intensive Care Medicine (in Italian Gruppo Italiano per la Valutazione degli Interventi in Terapia Intensiva or GiViTI) is an established research network focused on assessing and improving ICU care quality, involving > 200 ICUs in Italy. Data collection is prospective in the ICUs participating in the GiViTI network and based on the software PROSAFE, developed thanks to an international initiative.

Data entry is performed by at least 3 ICU physicians per center using an electronic case report form (eCRF) synchronized with a central server for validation.

Ethical statement

The Margherita-PROSAFE Project was approved by the Liguria regional ethics committee (approval n. 381REG2015) and by all participating sites. The study was conducted in agreement with the Declaration of Helsinki and with European and national regulations.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adults admitted to ICUs from January 1 st, 2020, to December 31 st, 2024, with an ICU length of stay (LOS) ≥ 48 h were included. Only the first episode of BSI was analyzed. Exclusion criteria were ICU LOS < 48 h, positive blood culture (BC), or diagnosis of infective endocarditis within the first 48 h of ICU stay.

Data collection

Retrospective analyses required authorized personnel currently part of the GiViTI project and employed at the Mario Negri Institute to retrospectively access the PROSAFE dataset for data collection and statistical analyses purposes. Collected variables include ICU- and patient-level characteristics. ICU data include the number of ICU admissions and geographical regions. Patient data included demographics, ward of origin, admission indication, SOFA and SAPSII scores, comorbidities, ICU procedures, and treatments. Microbiological data covered identified pathogens and fluconazole resistance. Clinical outcomes were intra-ICU and intra-hospital mortality, and LOS.

Definitions

Candidemia was defined as ≥ 1 positive BCs for Candida spp., from either venipuncture or peripheral and central lines, with clinical signs or symptoms of infection. This differs from the later FUNDICU 2024 consensus, which requires venipuncture BC positivity [23].

Bacterial BSI required either one BC positive for a bacterial pathogen or 2 or more BCs growing bacteria of the common skin flora (coagulase-negative staphylococci, Micrococcus spp., Propionibacterium acnes, Bacillus spp., and Corynebacterium spp.) in a patient with signs/symptoms of infection.

Infections occurring ≥ 48 h after ICU admission were considered ICU-acquired. The definition of “at risk” patient implied an ICU stay of ≥ 48 h and thus susceptibility to ICU-acquired infections. In particular, ICU-acquired candidemia was defined as such when BC positivity for Candida spp. occurred in a patient in the ICU after a minimum ICU stay of 48 h prior to candidemia onset.

Resistance to fluconazole was defined according to antifungal susceptibility testing performed using CLSI M60 or EUCAST fluconazole breakpoints, according to the version available at the time of data collection [24, 25]. Catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) was identified by the attending ICU physician per GiViTI criteria: a) culture from CVAD and catheter tip, with quantitative method ≥ 103 colony forming units (CFU)/mL OR semi-quantitative method > 15 CFU, b) differential time to positivity between BCs from CVAD and peripheral site, or c) positive culture from CVAD and pus at the catheter insertion site [26].

Statistical analysis

To ensure data integrity, cross-variable checks were performed during data collection, and inconsistent or missing data were reported in the eCRF. For each ICU, we considered only data of patients admitted in a period (whose length depends on the cardinality of the admissions) where at least 90% of patients’ records were complete [22].

Centers with a reported occupancy rate of less than 50% or with significant heterogeneity in monthly admissions received queries. Data from ICUs with at least 4 months of valid data, according to the above-mentioned criteria, were analyzed.

Continuous variables are medians (Q1–Q3); categorical variables n (%). Differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The incidence rate of ICU-acquired candidemia was calculated as the number of events per 1000 ICU admissions among included patients who were potentially susceptible to ICU-acquired infections.

The percentage of missing data was reported in the descriptive analysis. No imputation methods were applied.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3, with a significance threshold of 0.05.

Results

Epidemiology of candidemia in Italian ICUs

From 179,590 ICU-admitted patients in 155 centers between, 164,324 were included in the analyses, and 95,662 met at-risk criteria. A total of 1,559 patients experienced at least one episode of candidemia, either at ICU admission or during ICU stay, with a cumulative incidence of 8.7‰ ICU admissions. The patients with ICU-acquired candidemia were more frequently males (262/373, 67.9%), with a median age of 66 years (58.0, 74.0).

In the candidemia group, 33.8% (126/373, 33.8%) of patients were admitted from emergency wards and 29% from medical wards (108/373). The Body Mass Index (BMI) was 30 or more in 102 patients (27.2%). Cardiologic conditions (249/373, 66.8%) and diabetes (90/373, 24.1%) were the most frequently encountered comorbidities in the ICU-acquired candidemia group. These, alongside immunosuppression, (56/373, 15.0%), were more frequently observed (p = 0.024, 0.021, and 0.010, respectively) compared to patients with ICU-acquired bacterial BSI.

Median SOFA at admission was 8.0 (6.0, 11.0) in the ICU-acquired candidemia group and 7.0 (5.0, 10.0) in the ICU-acquired bacterial BSI group, and median SAPSII scores were 45.0 (35.0, 57.0) and 40.0 (33.0, 50.0), respectively. Both scores were significantly higher in the ICU-acquired candidemia group (p < 0.001).

At admission, a third of patients who later developed ICU-acquired candidemia (135/373, 36.2%) had a respiratory infection. Furthermore, at admission, higher prevalences of respiratory (p < 0.001), central nervous system (p = 0.045), intra-abdominal (p < 0.001), and skin and soft tissue (p = 0.033) infections were observed in ICU-acquired candidemia patients.

Half of patients with ICU-acquired candidemia (177/373, 47.5%) received empirical antimicrobial therapy at ICU admission vs. less than a third (1,030/3,464, 29.7%) in the ICU-acquired bacterial BSI patients’ population (p < 0.001). Moreover, patients with candidemia were significantly more likely to receive hemodialysis and parenteral nutrition (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively).

ICU LOS was prolonged among candidemia patients, with a median of 32.0 days (20.0, 46.0) versus 24.0 days (15.0, 37.0) in ICU-acquired bacterial BSI patients (p < 0.001). Similarly, median intra-hospital LOS was also longer among candidemia patients (44.0 days, [27.0–65.0] vs 36.0 days, [23.0–55.0], p = 0.001). Intra-ICU mortality was higher in the candidemia group (154/373, 41.3%) compared to those with ICU-acquired bacterial BSI (993/3,464, 28.7%; p < 0.001). Comparably, intra-hospital mortality was higher in patients with candidemia (185/373, 50.3%) than in the ICU-acquired bacterial BSI group (1160/3,464, 34.1%, p < 0.001).

Microbiology of Candida spp. isolates, fluconazole resistance trends, and patients’ characteristics and outcomes comparison in fluconazole-resistant and susceptible ICU-acquired candidemia episodes

Fluconazole susceptibility testing was available for 207 of Candida spp. isolates (207/388, 53.4%), and C. albicans was susceptible to fluconazole in over 90% of cases (81/89, 91.0%), while C. glabrata and C. parapsilosis displayed higher rates of resistance (9/22 and 44/82, 40.9% and 53.7%, respectively).

The prevalence of fluconazole resistance in retrieved Candida spp. isolates was the highest in the years 2022 and 2024 (16/42 and 9/25, 38.1% and 36.0%, respectively).

Patients with ICU-acquired candidemia episodes had mixed bacterial-fungal etiology in 31.6% of cases (118/373), and 157 bacterial isolates were retrieved overall, most frequently coagulase-negative staphylococci (or CoNS), Klebsiella spp., and Pseudomonas spp. (32/157, 27/157, and 25/157, 20.4%, 17.0%, and 15.9%, respectively). Baseline characteristics did not differ in the fluconazole-resistant and -susceptible Candida spp. ICU-acquired candidemia groups. Less than 5% of total ICU-acquired candidemia patients for whom susceptibility testing was available were receiving empirical antifungal coverage at the time of BC positivity (6/197, 3.0%). Intra-ICU LOS was significantly longer in the fluconazole-resistant ICU-acquired candidemia group (36.0 days [22.8, 59.0]) compared to fluconazole-susceptible infections (31.0 days [20.0, 42.0], p = 0.016). Overall intra-ICU and intra-hospital mortality rates were 42.6% (84/197) and 51.8% (101/197) among ICU-acquired candidemia patients with available susceptibility testing, and mortality estimates did not differ between groups (p = 0.827 and p = 0.512, respectively).

Characteristics and outcomes of patients with catheter vs. non-catheter sources of ICU-acquired candidemia episodes

Of the total ICU-acquired candidemia episodes, almost half (176/373, 47.2%) were flagged by the attending physician as catheter-related (CR) events. This was the most frequent source of ICU-acquired candidemia in the years 2021 and 2022 (62/123 and 38/75, 50.4% and 50.6%, respectively).

Baseline characteristics did not differ among patients with CR and non-CR ICU-acquired candidemia. Wards of origin were significantly different (p = 0.007), and patients with CR sources of ICU-acquired candidemia came more frequently from medical wards and the emergency department (34.1% vs. 24.4% and 36.9% vs. 31.0%, respectively), while surgical or high-intensity wards predominated in the non-CR candidemia group (21.3% vs. 14.2% and 11.2% vs. 3.4%, respectively). Targeted antibacterial treatment at candidemia onset was more frequent in the CR ICU-acquired candidemia group (37.5% vs. 27.9%, 65/176 vs. 55/197 patients, p = 0.048), and intra-ICU LOS was significantly higher in patients with CR candidemia (34.0 [21.0, 53.2] vs. 30.0 [18.0, 43.0], p = 0.038), but intra-hospital LOS did not differ (46.0 [30.0, 66.0] vs. 41.0 [26.0, 65.0], p-value = 0.326). On the other hand, the non-CR source of ICU-acquired candidemia was associated with significantly higher intra-ICU mortality (46.7% vs. 35.2%, 92/197 vs. 62/176, p = 0.025) and intra-hospital mortality (57.0% vs. 42.9%, 110/197 vs. 75/176, p = 0.007) rates.

Discussion

Summary of findings

In this nationwide analysis of 155 Italian ICUs (2020–2024), we observed overall candidemia and ICU-acquired candidemia incidences of 8.7‰ ICU admissions and 3.9‰ at-risk ICU admissions, respectively. Findings are aligned with European studies reporting overall candidemia incidences of 5.5–10‰ ICU admissions and ICU-acquired candidemia incidences of 5.5–6.7‰ at-risk admissions (2,4). Furthermore, we observed a substantial mortality burden, with overall intra-ICU and intra-hospital mortalities of 41% and 50%, respectively. Morbidity was also considerable, with median intra-ICU LOS exceeding 30 days, significantly longer compared with ICU-acquired bacterial BSIs (intra-ICU LOS + 8 days; intra-hospital LOS + 8 days). C. albicans (183/388, 47.2%) and C. parapsilosis (128/388, 33.0%) were predominant, the latter with > 50% fluconazole resistance. Lastly, differences in the source of infection shaped clinical outcomes, with non-CR episodes displaying the highest mortality (57.0%).

In this study, we deemed the ICU-acquired infection definitions more relevant and suitable to inform clinical practice and preventive measures, hence the choice of ICU-acquired candidemia. Candidemia represented 10% of ICU-acquired BSIs, and we observed 3.9 events per 1,000 admissions at risk (3.9‰), slightly lower than in the studies from Bassetti et al. and Bougnoux et al., conducted in Europe and France, despite the burden caused by the COVID-19 pandemic in Italy, highlighting the results obtained in the fields of antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention and control in the last decade [5, 6].

Incidence peaked in 2021 at 0.6%, plausibly reflecting the well-documented COVID-19 pandemic-related strain on healthcare systems [1].

The higher mortality and prolonged LOS observed in ICU-acquired candidemia cases, compared to bacterial BSI, are likely multifactorial but appear closely linked to the greater burden of comorbidities and clinical severity at ICU admission in this group. As shown in Table 1, patients with candidemia more frequently had cardiometabolic disease, immunosuppression, and the need for hemodialysis or parenteral nutrition, features that align with the known risk profile for invasive candidiasis and may partially explain their worse outcomes [14].

We confirm in a large national cohort the available evidence that considers candidemia a distinctly high-risk BSI phenotype in critical care [27].

Across almost 400 Candida isolates, C. albicans remained predominant (47.2%), with substantial C. parapsilosis (33.0%) and C. glabrata (11.6%) rates, while C. auris was rare. This microbiological profile closely mirrors findings from other European and non-European cohorts, reinforcing the prominence of these fungal pathogens—now recognized as priority species—in the global epidemiological landscape [28]. Mixed bacterial-fungal infections were frequently observed, in 30% of cases, often with Gram-negative pathogens and enterococci, suggesting a sizeable gastrointestinal/abdominal source signal, mediated either by surgery, systemic conditions (e.g., immunosuppression, hypoalbuminemia), or low cardiac output states, fostering bacterial and fungal translocation [29].

This stresses the importance of optimal surgical, fluid, and comorbidity management, as well as diagnostic sampling, in patients with an evocative risk profile.

Overall fluconazole resistance peaked in 2022 and 2024 (36–38%), driven by C. parapsilosis (53.7%) and C. glabrata (40.9%), consistently with post-pandemic dissemination in Europe [10, 13]. These findings mandate empirical echinocandin treatment in critically ill adults with suspected candidemia, with early de-escalation guided by species and susceptibility testing [24]. Of note, only 3% of patients were receiving empirical antifungal therapy at the time of candidemia onset, and this > 24–48-h lag raises concern for undertreatment in a high-risk population [24]. Moreover, in this study we did not collect data on echinocandin resistance, which, although rare in Europe and Italy (typically < 3% for C. albicans), has been reported to have risen in C. glabrata during the 2020 COVID-19 ICU surge, likely linked to echinocandin overuse [30, 31].

Mortality did not differ by fluconazole susceptibility in our cohort, consistent with multicenter Italian data in C. parapsilosis BSIs [17], and ICU LOS was increased in fluconazole-resistant infections (36 vs 31 days). Nearly half of the episodes were classified as CRBSIs by the attending physician, and non-CR candidemia had significantly higher intra-ICU and intra-hospital mortality rates, with shorter intra-ICU LOS compared to CR cases. The mortality gradient favors a role for vascular device source control, whereas non-CR cases likely reflect deeper or disseminated foci with fewer rapid control options [27]. Conversely, the longer LOS in the CR-candidemia group may be explained by the well-studied delayed onset of nosocomial CRBSIs, often occurring after 10–15 catheter-days [32].

Limitations include retrospective design, which may introduce misclassification bias, and lack of data on antifungal timing, appropriateness, candidemia complications, catheter tip or peritoneal fluid cultures, and infection source control in both CR and non-CR cases (see Supplementary Table S6 for study variable definitions). Susceptibility testing was incomplete and not uniform across centers. The FUNDICU 2024 criteria were not applied, possibly inflating incidence; however, in critical care practice, even a single positive central-line culture typically warrants antifungal treatment. Lack of echinocandin susceptibility and biomarker results (β-D-glucan) also limits interpretation. Strengths of this study are its large multicenter sample, standardized data collection protocols guaranteeing data quality, and the focus on strictly ICU-acquired episodes. Comparison with bacterial BSI clarifies the specific burden attributable to fungal infections. Finally, the study offers updated epidemiological insights into species distribution and fluconazole resistance trends after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Looking ahead, prospective ICU surveillance with treatment, diagnostic, and outcome data may guide future risk stratification. In fact, the recent trend in candidemia is to identify patients who may benefit from shorter or early oral treatment regimens, such as CR candidemia cases that align with the novel uncomplicated candidemia definition [33]. Thus, platforms such as the GiViTI network are ideal for patient profile clustering and observational studies on pressing topics to better inform ICU practice, namely the impact of shorter antifungal courses, as suggested by Vena et al. [34], or trends in echinocandin resistance in light of the recent C. auris outbreaks in Europe, and Italy in particular [35].

Lastly, such datasets could be valuable for AI model training and validation in an effort to improve patient care and outcomes. AI-assisted prediction, rapid mycology pipelines, and improved risk stratification, in fact, hold the potential to mitigate the ≥ 24–48-h treatment gap we highlighted.

Conclusions

ICU-acquired candidemia remains a major burden in Italian critical care, with high incidence, prolonged stays, and mortality rates exceeding 50%. The shift toward fluconazole-resistant C. parapsilosis and C. glabrata complicates clinical management. Outcomes are consistently worse than bacterial BSIs, and fluconazole resistance is associated with increased LOS but not death risk. Catheter-derived ICU-acquired candidemia is a very common yet preventable infection source, and its outcomes are more favorable than primary episodes.

Sustained surveillance, antifungal stewardship, and ICU-specific infection control measures are essential to curb resistance and improve outcomes in the ICU.
