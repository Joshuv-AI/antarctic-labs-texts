A Phase I Study to Evaluate the Safety, Tolerability, and Pharmacokinetics of Highly Selective NaV1.8 Inhibitor JKN2306 in Healthy Chinese Subjects
Authors: Ying Yang, Mai Han, Gang Cui, Dan Zhang, Wei Wu, Na Mi, Jiangli Jin, Shuxiao Zhang, Xin Zhang, Junjie Li, Chunyan Xiao, Jing Wang, Chaoyun Gou, Chong Su, Jintong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTJKN2306 is a novel, highly selective Nav1.8 inhibitor under development for the treatment of acute pain. This first‐in‐human study aimed to evaluate its safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and food effect (FE). This was a single center, randomized, double‐blind, placebo controlled, single ascending dose (SAD) and FEstudy in healthy Chinese subjects. In the SAD part, 44 subjects were randomized to receive a single oral dose of JKN2306 (10, 30, 100, 200, or 400 mg) or placebo. The FE part was a sequential, two‐period crossover study in 14 subjects from the 200 mg SAD cohort, who received a single 200 mg dose under fasting and fed conditions. Forty‐four subjects were enrolled and completed the study. JKN2306 was safe and well‐tolerated across all doses. Treatment‐emergent adverse events were mostly Grade 1, with no serious adverse events or dose limiting toxicities. No clinically significant effects on QTc interval were observed. JKN2306 was rapidly absorbed, with T max of 1.50–2.50 h. The t 1/2 ranged from 13.93 to 29.82 h. Exposure (C max and AUC) increased approximately linearly with dose over the 10–400 mg range. Food intake delayed the median T max by 1 h and reduced C max by about 17%, but had no significant effect on AUC. JKN2306 exhibits a favorable safety and tolerability profile and predictable linear PK in healthy subjects. Food has a modest effect on the rate but not the extent of absorption. These findings support the further clinical development of JKN2306 for the treatment of acute pain. Introduction

Pain is a fundamental sensory experience that fulfills a critical function: it signals bodily injury and triggers the body's innate self‐preservation responses [1]. Nevertheless, moderate to severe pain can lead to unwarranted suffering that necessitates targeted pain management interventions [2, 3, 4]. Despite the availability of multiple treatment modalities, a significant portion of the population still experiences inadequate relief from acute and neuropathic pain [5]. For moderate‐to‐severe pain, opioids continue to be widely prescribed, given their potent analgesic effects mediated by the activation of μ‐opioid receptors in the brain and spinal cord [6]. Although highly effective in pain relief, the clinical utility of opioids is constrained by their notable adverse effects, including sedation, respiratory depression, tolerance, dependence, and addiction [7]. Concerns over analgesic‐associated adverse effects, coupled with the paucity of viable analgesic options, have driven the pursuit of novel pharmacological agents characterized by enhanced efficacy and an improved adverse effect profile. The recent identification of the pivotal role of the voltage‐gated sodium channel NaV1.8 in acute pain pathophysiology has further stimulated research focused on the development of NaV1.8‐selective inhibitors [8]. Peripheral pain, encompassing acute (nociceptive), neuropathic, and inflammatory subtypes, arises from the excessive firing of peripheral sensory neurons that innervate bodily tissues, including dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons [9]. Three subtypes of peripheral voltage‐gated sodium channels, namely NaV1.7, NaV1.8, and NaV1.9, have been identified as key regulators of firing in peripheral pain‐signaling neurons [9]. Notably, none of these channels are expressed in the brain, thereby eliminating the risk of addiction; as such, they represent promising therapeutic targets for non‐opioid analgesia. Among these three voltage‐gated sodium channels, NaV1.8 exhibits depolarization resistance, mediates the majority of current during the rising phase of action potentials, and drives repetitive neuronal firing in response to depolarizing stimuli, regarded as a promising target for treating pain [1, 10]. Vertex Pharmaceuticals' Journavx (suzetrigine), an oral, non‐opioid analgesic highly selective for NaV1.8 versus other NaV channels (NaV1.1–1.7, NaV1.9), received FDA approval on January 30, 2025, for adult moderate‐to‐severe acute pain [11]. The regulatory approval of suzetrigine provides clinical proof of concept for NaV1.8 as a viable and effective target for analgesic intervention.

JKN2306 is a novel, highly selective NaV1.8 inhibitor developed by Joincare Pharmaceutical Group Industry Co. Ltd. This compound exerted no significant inhibitory effect on NaV 1.1–1.7 channels and exhibited potent and selective inhibitory activity against the NaV 1.8 channel, with a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of approximately 3 nM. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that JKN2306 exerts robust analgesic efficacy in rat models of neuropathic pain and postoperative pain, accompanied by a favorable safety profile following long‐term administration. Specifically, no significant effects of JKN2306 were observed on the respiratory, central nervous, or cardiovascular systems in preclinical studies. National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) clinical trial approval letter of JKN2306 tablet was obtained on January 20, 2025. To support further clinical development, we conducted this phase I study to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and PK characteristics of JKN2306 tablets in healthy Chinese subjects. The results of this study will provide critical data for further clinical development.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single‐center, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled phase I study conducted at China‐Japan Friendship Hospital (Beijing, China) from Feb 2025 to Jul 2025. The study consisted of two parts: a single ascending dose (SAD) study and a food effect (FE) study. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (EC) of China‐Japan Friendship Hospital (ethics approval number: YW2024‐073‐01) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines, and relevant Chinese regulatory requirements. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to study participation. The study design is given in Figure 1.

The scheme of study design.

Study Population

Healthy Chinese male and female subjects aged 18–55 years with a body mass index (BMI) of 19.0–28.0 kg/m2 (males ≥ 50 kg, females ≥ 45 kg) were eligible. Key inclusion criteria included good general health (confirmed by medical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, and electrocardiogram [ECG]), no fertility plans for 90 days after the last dose, and willingness to comply with study procedures. Exclusion criteria included a history of severe diseases, hypersensitivity to study drugs or their components, recent participation in other clinical trials, use of drugs that affect liver metabolism within 28 days, and abnormal ECG findings (e.g., QTcF ≥ 450 ms in males, ≥ 470 ms in females) etc.

Study Drugs

JKN2306 tablets (specifications: 10 and 50 mg) and matching placebos were provided by Joincare Pharmaceutical Industry Group Co. Ltd. (Shenzhen, Guangdong, People's Republic of China). The drugs were stored at ≤ 25°C in a shaded, sealed container. The placebo was identical to JKN2306 tablets in appearance, weight, and smell but contained no active ingredients.

Study Procedures

Randomization and Blinding

This study was designed as a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial. Subjects were sequentially enrolled into five ascending dose cohorts: 10, 30, 100, 200, and 400 mg. Within each cohort, participants were randomly assigned to receive either JKN2306 or a matching placebo at predefined ratios (2:1 for 10 mg, 3:1 for 30 mg, 100 mg, 400 mg, and 6:1 for 200 mg). The random numbers for subjects in each cohort were generated by a statistician independent of the study using SAS version 9.4 to create randomization tables, and a separate randomization scheme was generated for each dose group. To maintain the blinding, JKN2306 and the placebo were identical in appearance and packaging. All investigators and participants remained blinded to treatment assignments throughout the trial, with drug coding and allocation managed by unblinded personnel independent of the study.

Single Ascending Dose Study

Based on the results of nonclinical in vitro and in vivo studies, and using the methods of no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL), pharmacologically active dose (PAD), and minimal anticipated biological effect level (MABEL), the maximum recommended starting dose (MRSD) of JKN2306 was determined to be 10 mg, the clinical effective dose range is predicted to be 100–300 mg, and the maximum clinical dose supported by the conducted nonclinical toxicology studies is 1200 mg. On the basis of nonclinical efficacy and safety results, combined with the specification of JKN2306 tablets, the dose range was set as 10–400 mg in the SAD study. Subjects were randomized into five dose cohorts: 10 mg (n = 6: 4 JKN2306, 2 placebo), 30 mg (n = 8: 6 JKN2306, 2 placebo), 100 mg (n = 8: 6 JKN2306, 2 placebo), 200 mg (n = 14: 12 JKN2306, 2 placebo), and 400 mg (n = 8: 6 JKN2306, 2 placebo). Subjects were admitted to the clinical research unit on the day before dosing (D‐1) and fasted for at least 10 h prior to dosing. On D1, subjects received a single oral dose of JKN2306 or placebo with 240 mL of warm water. Blood samples for PK analysis were collected at pre‐specified time points: within 1 h pre‐dose, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, 120, 168 h post‐dose. Safety assessments (physical examination, vital signs, laboratory tests, ECG, and adverse event (AE) monitoring) were performed throughout the study. To ensure a robust cardiac safety assessment, ECGs were performed in triplicate at time points meticulously synchronized with PK blood sampling (pre‐dose, and 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h post‐dose) in the 100–400 mg cohort. This schedule was designed to capture the peak plasma concentration and the subsequent elimination phase, providing sufficient data for C‐QTc modeling. The inclusion of a 400 mg cohort ensured that the study assessed a supratherapeutic exposure level, in accordance with ICH E14 regulatory guidance. Subjects in the 10, 30, 100, and 400 mg cohorts were discharged on D8, while subjects in the 200 mg cohort continued to the FE study.

Food Effect Study

The FE study was a two‐period crossover design conducted in the 200 mg dose cohort of the SAD study. After a 7‐day washout period, subjects received the same dose of JKN2306 or placebo under high‐fat meal conditions. The total calorie content of a high fat meal is approximately 800 to 1000 kcal. Among them, protein provides about 150 kcal, carbohydrates provide about 250 kcal, and fat provides about 500 to 600 kcal. Subjects consumed the meal within 30 min, and dosing occurred 30 min after the start of the meal. Blood sample collection and safety assessments were identical to the SAD study. Subjects were discharged on D15 after completing the last safety assessment.

Bioanalytical Methods

Plasma concentrations of JKN2306 were determined by a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method. The analytical column was a C18 column with mobile phases consisting of 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution. The chromatographic separation was performed on a chromatographic system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a XDB C18 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm, Agilent, USA). The detection was carried out using a Triple Quad 5500 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) equiped with an electrospray ionization source operating in the positive ion mode. The reference standard JKN2306 and the internal standard d4‐JKN2306 were provided by Joincare Pharmaceutical Group Industry Co. Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). HPLC‐grade acetonitrile were purchased from Sigma (USA), and formic acid was obtained from TCI (Japan). Ultrapure water was generated using a Milli‐Q water purification system (Millipore, France). The linear range was 3.00–3000 ng/mL, with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 3.00 ng/mL. The intra‐batch and inter‐batch precision (CV%) were ≤ 2.8% and ≤ 3.1%, respectively, and the accuracy (bias%) ranged from −4.9% to 2.8%, meeting the acceptance criteria of ICH M10 guidelines.

Safety Analysis

SAS version 9.4 was used for statistical analysis. Safety data were summarized using descriptive statistics. The safety population consisted of all subjects who received any amount of the study drug or placebo and had at least one post‐dose safety assessment. The summaries of TEAEs for the subjects assigned to placebo were pooled across all dose cohorts and treatment periods. AEs were coded using MedDRA 27.1 and classified by system organ class (SOC) and preferred term (PT). The severity of AEs was graded according to CTCAE 5.0 (mild, moderate, severe). Safety assessments included AEs, laboratory tests (blood routine, biochemistry, coagulation function, urinalysis), vital signs, physical examinations, and ECGs. For the C‐QTc relationship analysis, linear mixed‐effects models were applied to evaluate the association between JKN2306 plasma concentrations and ΔΔQTc.

Pharmacokinetic and Statistical Analysis

PK parameters were calculated using non‐compartmental analysis with Phoenix WinNonlin 8.4 software. Key PK parameters included maximum plasma concentration (C max), time to reach C max (T max), area under the concentration‐time curve from dosing to the last quantifiable concentration (AUC0‐last), area under the concentration‐time curve from dosing to infinity (AUC0‐inf), terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2), apparent total clearance (CL/F), and apparent volume of distribution (Vz/F). Dose proportionality was evaluated using the power model for C max, AUC0‐last, and AUC0‐inf. Dose proportionality was assessed using the power model: ln(PK parameter) = α + β × ln(dose). Dose proportionality was concluded if the 90% CI of β was entirely contained within the pre‐specified critical interval calculated as [1 + ln(θ L)/ln(r), 1 + ln(θ H)/ln(r)], where θ L and θ H were the lower and upper limits of the acceptable exposure ratio and r was the ratio between the highest and lowest doses. Given the 10–400 mg dose range in this study, r was 40. The pre‐specified θ L and θ H were 0.70 and 1.43 for C max, corresponding to a β acceptance interval of 0.90–1.10, and 0.80 and 1.25 for AUC0‐last and AUC0‐inf, corresponding to a β acceptance interval of 0.94–1.06. For the FE study, the primary PK parameters AUC0‐last, AUC0‐inf, and C max were log‐transformed and analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model. The geometric mean ratio (GMR) and 90% confidence interval (CI) of PK parameters (fed vs. fasting) were calculated; a 90% CI within 80.00%–125.00% was considered no significant FE.

Safety and Tolerability

Safety analysis was based on the safety analysis set (SS). All 44 successfully enrolled subjects in this study were included in the SS. Among these 44 subjects, 18 (40.9%, 18/44) reported 21 adverse events (AEs), all of which were treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Additionally, 16 subjects (36.4%, 16/44) experienced 17 TEAEs related to the study medication. All AEs were graded as mild to moderate (Grades 1 and 2), with no severe AEs (Grade 3 or above) reported. No serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred during the study, and there were no TEAEs leading to treatment interruption, discontinuation, study withdrawal, or death.

Single Ascending Dose Study

Among the 44 subjects in the SAD study, 16 (36.4%, 16/44) reported 17 TEAEs, and 14 (31.8%, 14/44) experienced 14 TEAEs related to the study medication. Common adverse reactions (classified by PT with an overall incidence ≥ 1.0%) more frequent in the JKN2306 cohort than in the placebo (JKN2306 cohort vs. Placebo cohort) included alanine aminotransferase elevation (5.9% vs. 0%), neutropenia (5.9% vs. 0%), hyponatremia (2.9% vs. 0%), ECG T‐wave abnormality (2.9% vs. 0%), sensory disturbance (2.9% vs. 0%), and cheilitis (2.9% vs. 0%). Notably, neutropenia, ECG T‐wave abnormality, sensory disturbance, and cheilitis occurred only in the 10 mg and 30 mg dose cohorts, not in the high‐dose cohorts (100–400 mg).

Thirteen subjects (29.5%, 13/44) had 14 Grade 1 TEAEs per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), of which 11 (25.0%, 11/44) were related to the study medication (11 cases). Three subjects (6.8%, 3/44) reported 3 Grade 2 TEAEs, all deemed related to the study medication: one case/episode of hypertriglyceridemia (200 mg cohort), one case/episode of neutrophil count decreased (10 mg cohort), and one case/episode of cheilitis (10 mg cohort).

Laboratory parameters that were normal or abnormally insignificant at baseline but became clinically significant after treatment included absolute neutrophil count, white blood cell count, alanine aminotransferase, sodium, blood glucose, triglycerides, and urinary ketone bodies. No obvious dose dependence of these laboratory abnormalities was observed across JKN2306 dose cohorts and the placebo cohort. All subjects had normal or clinically insignificant vital signs and physical examination results. Only 1 subject (2.3%, 1/44) in the 30 mg cohort (16.7%, 1/6) had a clinically significant ECG change, reported as a Grade 1 TEAE of ECG T‐wave abnormality (possibly related to the study medication). No clinically significant ECG changes were reported in the high‐dose cohorts (100–400 mg). No clinically meaningful trends were observed in laboratory tests, vital signs, physical examinations, or ECGs.

Throughout the study, no clinically significant T‐wave changes or U‐waves were detected in any subject's C‐QT ECG. QT intervals were corrected using both Fridericia's formula (QTcF) and individual subject‐specific correction methods, with subsequent modeling analysis showing better fit with Fridericia's correction. Changes in QTcF and individually corrected QT (QTcI) post‐administration were not significantly imbalanced across dose cohorts and showed no obvious dose dependence. For both correction methods, the upper limit of the 90% CI for ΔΔQTc corresponding to the geometric mean C max in the highest dose cohort (400 mg) did not exceed 10 ms. It is anticipated that JKN2306 has no significant effect on QT interval prolongation at doses up to 400 mg.

Food Effect Study

Among the 14 subjects in the FE study, 6 (42.9%, 6/14) reported 9 AEs, all of which were TEAEs. Additionally, 4 subjects (28.6%, 4/14) experienced 5 TEAEs related to the study medication. The only drug‐related TEAE (classified by PT with an overall incidence ≥ 1.0%) more frequent in the JKN2306 cohort than in the placebo (total across both phases, JKN2306 cohort vs. placebo cohort) was urinary tract infection (8.3% vs. 0.0%). Five subjects (35.7%, 5/14) had 7 Grade 1 TEAEs, of which two subjects (14.3%, 2/14) had three cases related to the study medication. Two subjects (14.3%, 2/14) reported two Grade 2 TEAEs, both deemed related to the study medication: one case of hypertriglyceridemia (200 mg cohort, fasting phase) and one case of urinary tract infection (200 mg cohort, fed phase).

In the FE study, laboratory parameters that were normal or abnormally insignificant at baseline but became clinically significant after the first period of administration in the 200 mg cohort included sodium, blood glucose, triglycerides, and urinary ketone bodies, after the second period, such parameters included sodium, triglycerides, urinary ketone bodies, and urinary red blood cell count. No notable abnormalities were observed in laboratory tests, vital signs, physical examinations, or ECGs.

Food Effect Study

Compared with fasting administration, fed administration of 200 mg JKN2306 delayed the median T max from 2.50 to 3.50 h. The C max was reduced by about 17% (GMR: 83.39%, 90% CI: 74.20%–93.72%), with the 90% CI falling outside the range of 80.00%–125.00%, which indicates a significant FE on C max. While AUC0−last (GMR: 103.07%, 90% CI: 92.54%–114.80%) and AUC0−inf (GMR: 102.99%, 90% CI: 92.48%–114.68%) both 90% CI was within the range of 80.00%–125.00%, demonstrating the absence of a FE on AUC exposure parameters. The t 1/2 was similar between the two states (18.73 h vs. 17.91 h). (Figure 4, Tables 7 and 8).

Discussion

Acute pain management remains a significant clinical challenge worldwide [12]. Currently available analgesics, primarily nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids, are often limited by their side effects, such as gastrointestinal toxicity, cardiovascular risks, or the potential for addiction and respiratory depression [10, 13]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel, non‐opioid therapeutic agents with improved safety profiles. Voltage‐gated sodium channels play a pivotal role in the initiation and conduction of pain signals [14]. Among them, NaV1.8, which is highly expressed in peripheral sensory neurons (nociceptors), has emerged as a promising target for analgesia [15]. This Phase I study represents the first‐in‐human (FIH) clinical evaluation of JKN2306, a novel and highly selective NaV1.8 inhibitor. The results demonstrate that JKN2306 is safe, well‐tolerated, and possesses a favorable PK profile following single oral doses ranging from 10 to 400 mg in healthy Chinese subjects. These findings provide a critical foundation for dose selection in subsequent Phase II trials for acute pain management, and the first clinical evidence of its potential as a next‐generation analgesic.

In terms of PK characteristics, JKN2306 was rapidly absorbed with a T max of 1.50–2.50 h, which supports its potential to exert a rapid analgesic effect. The t 1/2 of 13.93–29.82 h suggests that JKN2306 has a long elimination half‐life, supporting a potential once‐daily or twice‐daily administration regimen in subsequent clinical trials, which is highly advantageous for improving patient compliance. Furthermore, the study confirmed dose proportionality for C max, AUC0‐last, and AUC0‐inf within the 10–400 mg range. Although the increase in AUC0‐last and AUC0‐inf was slightly higher than the dose increment, the overall exposure increased in a predictable linear manner. This linearity simplifies dose escalation and safety monitoring in future clinical applications, which is favorable for dose selection in phase II studies.

The FE study showed that high‐fat meals delayed T max and reduced C max by about 17% but had no significant impact on AUC. This finding suggests that JKN2306 can be administered with or without food, improving patient compliance. The reduction in C max may be attributed to delayed gastric emptying and reduced absorption rate caused by high‐fat meals, which is a common phenomenon in oral drug absorption. However, the lack of effect on AUC indicates that the total amount of drug absorbed is not affected by food, which is clinically relevant for patients who may take the drug with meals. These results imply that JKN2306 can be administered regardless of food intake, providing flexibility for patients who may need to take the medication during or between meals.

Suzetrigine, the first FDA‐approved NaV1.8 inhibitor for the treatment of moderate‐to‐severe acute pain, provides important clinical validation of NaV1.8 inhibition as a non‐opioid analgesic strategy [11]. However, direct extrapolation of the clinical efficacy, effective exposure threshold, or exposure‐response relationship of suzetrigine to JKN2306 should be interpreted with caution, because the two compounds differ in chemical structure, in vitro potency, plasma protein binding, metabolite activity, and tissue distribution. In preclinical electrophysiological assays, JKN2306 showed potent inhibition of human NaV1.8, with IC50 values of 3.001 nM, and showed no significant inhibition of NaV1.1‐NaV1.7 channels. By comparison, according to the FDA integrated review [11], suzetrigine inhibited NaV1.8 with an IC50 of approximately 0.7 nM. Its major human metabolite M6‐SUZ was also pharmacologically active, with an IC50 of approximately 0.5 nM against NaV1.8 and 2.5 ± 0.20 nM against human DRG TTX‐resistant currents. Numerically, suzetrigine appears to be approximately four‐fold more potent than JKN2306 based on NaV1.8 IC50 values; however, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously because the assays, channel states, cellular systems, and metabolite contributions were not identical between the two development programs. Plasma protein binding also differs between the two compounds. JKN2306 showed high plasma protein binding in humans, with an estimated unbound fraction of approximately 2.1%, corresponding to approximately 97.9% binding. In contrast, the FDA review reported plasma protein binding of approximately 99% for suzetrigine and 96% for M6‐SUZ in humans. Therefore, total plasma concentrations are not directly comparable across compounds, and interpretation of pharmacologically relevant exposure should consider unbound drug concentrations as well as active metabolite contribution. Tissue distribution data further support caution in cross‐compound extrapolation. In rats given a single oral dose of JKN2306 at 30 mg/kg, the compound distributed rapidly into tissues within 30 min. The highest tissue exposures were observed in the gastric wall and liver, with AUC0‐last values of 395 000 and 380 000 ng*h/g in male rats, and 528 000 and 459 000 ng × h/g in female rats, respectively. Most brain concentrations were below the LLOQ, and no comparable cerebrospinal fluid, spinal cord, or DRG free exposure data are currently available for JKN2306. By contrast, suzetrigine distribution was specifically evaluated in CNS‐ and pain‐relevant matrices in nonclinical studies. In male cynomolgus monkeys given a single oral dose of suzetrigine 3 mg/kg, total concentrations at 4 h were 612 ng/mL in plasma, 2010 ng/g in brain, 1430 ng/g in spinal cord, and 1590 ng/g in DRG for suzetrigine; corresponding values for M6‐SUZ were 186 ng/mL, 209 ng/g, 200 ng/g, 5.29 ng/mL, and 266 ng/g, respectively. When expressed as mean free tissue concentration‐to‐free plasma ratios at 4 h, the ratios for suzetrigine were 1.28 in brain, 0.721 in spinal cord, 0.692 in CSF, and 0.738 in DRG; corresponding ratios for M6‐SUZ were 0.291, 0.105, 0.430, and 0.281, respectively. Taken together, suzetrigine should be considered as clinical validation of the NaV1.8 target rather than as a quantitative predictor of the analgesic efficacy, effective dose, or effective exposure threshold of JKN2306. The present FIH study demonstrated that JKN2306 had favorable safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic characteristics in healthy Chinese subjects, which supports further clinical development. Nevertheless, the analgesic efficacy and exposure‐response relationship of JKN2306 should be independently established in proof‐of‐concept and confirmatory clinical trials in patients with acute pain.

Regarding safety, JKN2306 was well‐tolerated at all doses tested. All TEAEs were mild to moderate in severity, and no serious AEs or deaths occurred. The most common AEs were hypertriglyceridemia, alanine aminotransferase elevation, and neutropenia, which were transient and resolved without intervention. Notably, no dose‐dependent increase in AE incidence was observed, and no clinically significant abnormalities in laboratory tests, vital signs, or ECGs were detected. The rigorous design of the ECG monitoring schedule, which closely matched the PK profile, allowed for a high‐resolution analysis of the C‐QTc relationship. By evaluating doses from 100 to 400 mg, this study demonstrated that even at supratherapeutic exposures significantly higher than the intended clinical dose, JKN2306 did not exhibit a signal for clinically relevant QTc prolongation. Extensive C‐QTc modeling analysis showed that the upper limit of the 90% CI for △△QTc did not exceed 10 ms at the highest dose (400 mg), and this confirms that JKN2306 has no significant risk of QT interval prolongation. The C‐QTc results suggest that JKN2306 has no significant cardiac safety risks, which is consistent with its high selectivity for Nav1.8 and minimal off target activity on cardiac sodium channels. In this study, none of the dose cohorts met the dose escalation termination criteria. Overall, TEAEs observed in this study were predominantly CTCAE Grade 1, and all TEAE outcomes during follow‐up were recorded as recovered, resolved, or stable.

Compared with other NaV1.8 inhibitors in clinical development, such as Suzetrigine, JKN2306 exhibits a similar safety profile with no unexpected AE.11 With reference to the reported safety outcomes of Suzetrigine, the common AEs (incidence ≥ 3%) in the Suzetrigine cohort from the pooled Phase III clinical trials in postoperative patients included gastrointestinal disorders (constipation, nausea, vomiting), nervous system disorders (headache, dizziness), and skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders (pruritus). Among these events, the incidences of gastrointestinal disorders (constipation, nausea, vomiting) and nervous system disorders (headache, dizziness) in the suzetrigine cohort were all lower than those in the placebo cohort.

TEAEs (incidence ≥ 1%) with an incidence higher in the Suzetrigine cohort than in the placebo cohort were as follows: pruritus, muscle spasms, elevated blood creatine phosphokinase, and rash. None of the above mentioned common TEAEs associated with the Suzetrigine were observed in this study. Based on the safety profile of the NaV1.8 inhibitor, subsequent clinical trials of JKN2306 will pay attention to the occurrence of skin disorders (primarily rash), the abnormal levels of blood creatine phosphokinase, muscle spasms, nervous system disorders (e.g., dizziness, headache) and gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., constipation, nausea, vomiting) in subjects.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, as a FIH Phase I trial, this study was conducted in a relatively small population of healthy volunteers rather than patients with acute pain, while this approach allows for the assessment of the drug's basic safety and PK profile. Second, this study only evaluated the SAD and FEs of JKN2306. Given its long terminal half‐life observed in this study, multiple‐ascending dose (MAD) trials are warranted to further characterize its steady‐state kinetics and long‐term safety profile. Third, the study population was limited to healthy Chinese subjects. Considering potential inter‐ethnic differences in drug metabolism and response, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic cohorts needs to be validated in future global clinical trials.

Conclusion

This phase I study demonstrates that JKN2306 tablets are safe and well‐tolerated in healthy Chinese subjects at doses of 10–400 mg. The drug exhibits linear PK characteristics and no clinically significant FE. These findings support the further development of JKN2306 for the treatment of acute pain.
