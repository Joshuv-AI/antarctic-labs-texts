Mathematical modeling of planar cell polarity: principles, approaches, and open questions
Authors: Mohd Suhail Rizvi, Mohit Kumar Jolly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlanar cell polarity represents a fundamental mechanism by which cells within epithelial sheets align their orientation, enabling coordinated tissue morphogenesis and function. Disruption of PCP leads to developmental defects and disease, highlighting the importance of understanding its establishment and maintenance. While experimental studies have identified key protein molecules that drive PCP, mathematical and computational modeling have become indispensable in connecting molecular interactions to tissue-level outcomes. Over the last couple of decades, diverse approaches, such as agent-based models, Cellular Potts frameworks, Petri nets, continuum theories, and phenomenological models, have been developed to capture distinct aspects of PCP dynamics. These frameworks allow systematic exploration of nonlinear feedback, intracellular and intercellular signaling, and the influence of geometry and mechanics, and noise on polarization. This review summarizes these mathematical and computational developments in PCP modeling, emphasizing methodological assumptions, insights gained, and open challenges. By bridging experiment and theory, PCP modeling advances both mechanistic understanding and predictive capacity for tissue-scale organization.Subject terms: Biophysics, Cell biology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Systems biology

Introduction

Cell polarity in epithelial tissues, characterized by asymmetric organization of proteins inside the cells, is essential for epithelial development and function. At the cellular scale, the polarity is known to emerge from several mechanisms, such as Golgi polarity1, front-rear polarity2, and chiral cell polarity3, that contribute to diverse cellular functions across different contexts. Given the two-dimensional topology of epithelium, at the tissue scale, cells have two polarity axes- apicobasal polarity establishes apical and basal domains within individual cells4, while planar cell polarity (PCP) refers to the coordinated alignment of polarity across the plane of a tissue, perpendicular to the apical-basal axis5,6 (see Fig. 1A). This organizational principle underlies the emergence of highly ordered structures and collective cellular behaviors that are critical for normal development and physiology7. Unlike apical-basal polarity, which compartmentalizes at sub-cellular levels, PCP establishes directional cues that span across groups of cells in a coordinated manner, ensuring that multicellular tissues exhibit coherent orientation and function. By providing directional axes that orient cytoskeletal and adhesive machinery, PCP acts as a cellular compass steering morphogenesis, physiology, and tissue repair.

Role of PCP in morphogenesis

PCP, first described in Drosophila melanogaster through the observation of uniform orientation of wing hairs and sensory bristles8, has been recognized as a conserved feature across animal systems9 as well as plants10. In vertebrates, PCP governs processes as diverse as neural tube closure11, cochlear hair bundle orientation12, limb elongation13, kidney development14, and ciliary alignment15 in multiciliated epithelia. Its influence extends from large-scale morphogenetic events, such as convergent extension movements16 during gastrulation-to fine-tuned tissue patterning, exemplified by the precise alignment of mammalian hair follicles17. Its importance is arguably best highlighted in disease. Mutations disrupting PCP genes are strongly associated with neural tube defects18, one of the most common and severe birth defects in humans. Aberrant PCP signaling also contributes to Robinow syndrome19, skeletal dysplasias20, and idiopathic scoliosis21. Recently, the PCP has also been reported to play a crucial role in the development of planarian flatworms22.

Beyond development, PCP dysfunction impairs homeostasis of epithelia, compromising airway clearance through defective ciliary beating and perturbing epidermal renewal23. Such links underscore that PCP is not only vital during embryogenesis but also for maintaining adult tissue integrity. Some of these functions of PCP in embryogenesis and disease, are due to its role in coordinated cell motility24,25. During convergent extension, cells polarize mediolaterally and intercalate directionally26,27, processes that fail when PCP components are disrupted. In the mammalian epidermis, PCP aligns cell divisions and orients cytoskeletal remodeling28, ensuring patterned hair follicle emergence29. In the trachea and brain ventricles, PCP coordinates the alignment of motile cilia, allowing directional fluid flow essential for respiratory function and cerebrospinal fluid dynamics30. Its disruption reveals how even subtle losses in polarity can propagate into major defects in organ shape and function5.

PCP biology

The establishment of PCP involves the integration of global orienting cues with local molecular interactions that yield asymmetric protein distributions across cells. These asymmetric patterns are propagated across tissues, ensuring coherent alignment. Molecularly, PCP signaling is orchestrated through two inter-connected systems- the core PCP module and the Fat-Dachsous-Four-jointed (Ft-Ds-Fj) module (see Fig. 1B).

Players involved: two modules

The core pathway is highly conserved and comprises transmembrane and cytoplasmic proteins that segregate asymmetrically within cells. Key transmembrane components of the core module include- Frizzled (Fz), a seven-pass transmembrane Wnt receptor31, Van Gogh (Vang; also Strabismus), a four-pass transmembrane protein32, Flamingo (Fmi, also CELSR in vertebrates), an atypical cadherin that forms homodimers across cell junctions33. The cytoplasmic effectors include Disheveled (Dsh), recruited to the cortex via Fz34, Prickle (Pk), recruited with Vang35, and Diego (Dgo), binds Dsh and stabilizes Fz complexes36. The asymmetric localization of these proteins has been proposed to take place via mutual exclusion37, positive feedback (e.g., clustering of like complexes)38, negative feedback (e.g., antagonism between distal and proximal complexes)39, and asymmetric interactions (interaction of Fz-Fmi in one cell with Fmi in neighboring cell)40. Together, these interactions result in asymmetric cortical complexes with Fz-Dsh-Dgo and Vang-Pk complexes localizing on two opposing ends of the cells (distally and proximally, respectively), with Fmi on both sides bridging cell-cell contacts. Despite the tightly controlled interactions, the core protein complexes at the cell-cell junctions have been shown to be not rigid stoichiometric assemblies but allow robust polarization across variable compositions41.

The second PCP module involves large atypical cadherins Fat (Ft) and Dachsous (Ds), and the Golgi-resident kinase Four-jointed (Fj)42. Ft and Ds interact heterophilically across junctions, while Fj modulates their binding affinity by phosphorylation43. As opposed to the core proteins that are usually expressed uniformly across the tissue, Ds and Fj are expressed in opposing tissue gradients that generate asymmetric Ft-Ds distributions on the cell membrane44. Similar to the core module proteins, for Ft-Ds-Fj module intra- and inter-cellular protein interaction mechanisms of mutual exclusion and feedback have been proposed.

Local interactions and global cues

PCP establishment relies on intricate molecular interactions among the proteins described above to generate and propagate asymmetry across tissues. In the core PCP module, Fmi forms homodimers across cell junctions, facilitating intercellular communication33. Intracellularly, Fz recruits Dsh, while Dgo binds to Dsh, promoting distal clustering. Conversely, Vang and Pk interact to stabilize proximal complexes36. These assemblies are mutually exclusive due to competitive inhibitory interactions9. For example, Pk and Dgo compete for binding to Dsh and Vang, reinforcing asymmetry. Positive feedback amplifies local clustering, while endocytic trafficking removes mislocalized components, refining polarity. Thus, these interactions are inherently local in nature, and can give rise to local ordering that may not necessarily result in global polarity38. More recently, the role of cell-intrinsic signaling, as opposed to the mechanism based on limited protein pool or polarized transport, has been demonstrated45.

To connect local polarization to the global tissue axis, the Ft-Ds-Fj module acts through expression gradients of Ds and Fj46. The heterophilic affinity between Ft and Ds results in Ft-Ds heterodimers at cell–cell interfaces that are also not isotropically localized along the cell periphery. This local polarity in Ft-Ds heterodimers, coupled with tissue scale expression gradients, results in a global polarity, aligned with the tissue axis47.

Interaction between local and global modules

The debate over the mechanism of interaction between these two modules is still not settled48. One mechanism of interaction between two groups is via the PKSple isoform that can physically interact with Ds49. Another potentially indirect mechanism of interaction is via microtubules. The Ft-Ds-Fj driven global polarity has been known to orient microtubule arrays, thereby biasing the trafficking of core PCP components (e.g., Fz-Dsh vesicles toward microtubule plus ends)50. Thus, the Ft-Ds system imparts tissue-wide polarity by influencing the initial anisotropic distribution of core PCP proteins, which is then amplified and stabilized through local feedback mechanisms. As far as the current understanding of the PCP biology goes, the exact nature of the interaction between the two modules is tissue dependent48.

Approaches of study and role of mathematical modeling

PCP has been extensively studied using experimental animal models, such as Drosophila and vertebrate systems, as well as in vitro cell culture assays51 that reveal molecular interactions at high resolution. While these experimental approaches have uncovered key players and pathways, an additional and complementary strategy is mathematical modeling, which provides a means to link molecular interactions with emergent structures. Mathematical modeling, a cornerstone of systems biology, offers an invaluable research methodology to systematically combine the complex molecular interactions into predictive principles of cellular and tissue organization and function52–55. In PCP research too, mathematical (described using differential equations) and computational (described using rule-based description) modeling has provided mechanistic insights into molecular interactions at sub-cellular and inter-cellular levels and their role of PCP establishment and maintenance that were not possible by experiments alone. Since it has been nearly a decade since the last dedicated reviews on mathematical modeling of PCP56,57, the field has advanced with new modeling approaches and insights. Here, we are attempting to present an updated overview of PCP modeling approaches, highlighting key insights in PCP, persistent challenges, and open questions shaping future investigations.

Modeling approaches

With the first mathematical model for PCP, proposed in 200537, mathematical modeling has been extensively employed to test how molecular interactions drive PCP. Since then, diverse frameworks have emerged, each suited to probing distinct mechanistic questions underlying PCP establishment and maintenance. Agent-based models (ABMs) capture individual cell behaviors and stochasticity, cellular Potts models (CPM) emphasize cell shapes and adhesion, Petri net models formalize interaction networks and concurrency, continuum models provide analytical insights into pattern stability, and phenomenological models distill key principles into simplified rules (Fig. 2). In the following, we describe these approaches and how they have been applied to study PCP. Existing modeling frameworks are formulated based on the interactions within either the core module or the global module, or are constructed in a module-agnostic manner applicable to either. To our knowledge, no modeling study to date has explicitly simulated the crosstalk or direct interactions between these two modules. Therefore, in the following sections, we provide a description of the models in the form of general protein interactions that can be interpreted in the context of either the core or global module proteins.

Cellular Potts model

The CPM is a lattice-based computational framework for simulating collective cell behaviors, where cells occupy multiple lattice sites and evolve by energy-minimizing dynamics58. Given the detailed spatiotemporal description of protein distribution within a cell, this approach captures the information at the sub-cellular spatial resolution level. Widely applied in cancer biology, CPM captures tumor growth59, invasion59,60, and cell-cell61, or cell-matrix60,62,63 interactions, linking microscopic rules to emergent tissue-level patterns. This approach has also been used to study PCP dynamics64,65.

This modeling approach has been applied to study the role of the core PCP module and has shown that robust PCP can arise from asymmetric Flamingo-based cadherin bridges that enable neighboring cells to compare Frizzled activity. Further, a weak and transient tissue scale expression graident (for example, in Frizzled expression) can locally amplify the long-range polarity64. This also highlights that any global cue need not be permanent for PCP establishment. In other work based on the CPM, the requirement of global cue has been shown to be not required, provided that a small initially polarized region is coupled to uniform tissue growth and proliferation65. Together, these studies suggest that long-range PCP can be achieved either through transient upstream biases interpreted by intercellular feedback or through growth-mediated propagation of local polarity states in the absence of global cues.

Petri net model

Another PCP modeling approach uses Petri net which is a graphical and mathematical modeling tool used to represent distributed, concurrent, and stochastic systems66. In the context of PCP, it consists of places (usually cell membrane compartments obtained by dividing a hexagonal cell into seven regions), tokens (the proteins molecules involved in PCP), and transitions (binding, unbinding, and dimerization events that change these molecular states). In one study, hierarchical colored Petri nets (HCPNs), an advanced form of Petri nets, were used to study the PCP dynamics in Drosophila wing67. In this form of Petri Nets, the “colored” tokens allow multiple molecular species to be tracked in a single model, and “hierarchical” structure lets the system be broken down into modules. With this encoding of the PCP machinery, the HCPN can be executed computationally using specialized Petri net simulation tools68–70. This yields trajectories of how proteins redistribute and polarize across cells. This computational modeling approach differs considerably from the agent-based modeling where differential equations are used to describe the system dynamics. In contrast, the rule-based HCPN captures discrete molecular interactions, stochastic events, and modular intercellular coupling, enabling multiscale modeling of polarity. This makes HCPNs more flexible for complex, spatially organized PCP processes. The works based on this modeling approach have reproduced the known experimental observations, such as domineering non-autonomy67. Despite the sophisticated description of hierarchical interactions among different components, this approach has not been very popular in the study of the PCP.

While both CPM and Petri net models can resolve subcellular dynamics, their high computational demands make scaling them to large tissues challenging. Therefore, a modeling framework that captures only the essential subcellular information required for PCP dynamics is needed.

Agent-based models

ABMs, one of the most popular modeling approaches to study PCP, are computational frameworks that simulate complex systems by representing individual entities, or “agents” (in the case of PCP, cells), with rules governing their behavior and interactions71,72. By linking evolution of the state of the agent through local rules, ABMs reveal emergent patterns at the population or tissue level. In biology, they have been widely applied to model collective cell migration73, immune system dynamics74, tumor growth75, microbial ecosystems76, and ecological interactions77. Their strength lies in linking microscopic information to macroscopic outcomes, allowing exploration of stochasticity, adaptation, and spatial organization. In the context of PCP, most of the mathematical and computational modeling works have utilized this approach in one and two dimensions (37,39,78 to name a few). In this modeling framework in 1D, cells are represented as agents with two ends (left and right), with the left end of one cell interacting with the right end of its neighbor. In two dimensions, this gets extended to cells with six degrees of freedom (for a regular hexagonal lattice), where the cell–cell interaction takes place at the shared interfaces. Summary of the agent based modeling of PCP and the biological insights provided by the models

Despite the success of ABMs in PCP, their applicability to the actual tissue scale spatial domains still remains limited (though not impossible with sufficient resources). Also, ABMs, being mainly computational in nature, do not provide any analytical insights into the PCP.

Phenomenological models

Another approach to study PCP relies on a phenomenological description based on coarse-graining of the molecular details of PCP proteins into effective fields and energy functionals86,87. The tissue level polarity patterns are obtained by minimizing the energy under appropriate constraints. This approach is influenced by the modeling paradigm used for some physical systems, such as magnetization (see section on Relation with other physical systems for more details), that rely on equilibrium thermodynamic description. One apparent advantage of this approach is that it intentionally discards biochemical detail to retain clarity and a small parameter set for qualitative comparison with experiments. Therefore, it can be easily deployed for studying large-scale polarity alignment and disorder-order transitions in tissues, as well as how tissue-level cues compete with local interactions to establish long-range coordination. However, this advantage is arguably not very useful in providing any mechanistic insight into the emergence of PCP. Therefore, a modeling approach that can not only probe mechanistic questions but also handle large tissue-scale spatial domains can be very appealing for the study of PCP.

Continuum theory

Continuum theory models describe biological or physical systems using continuous fields governed by partial differential equations, approximating discrete entities as smoothly varying densities. Widely applied in fluid dynamics, materials science90, and developmental biology, they capture large-scale emergent behavior. A classic example is Turing’s reaction-diffusion model, explaining spontaneous pattern formation like stripes or spots91. In a recent work, the continuum theory approach has been used to study PCP dynamics in one81 and two dimensional92 models of epithelial tissues. This approach considers the PCP as a continuum field obtained by averaging over multiple cells. It has to be noted here that at the mechanistic level, the ABMs and continuum models can be similar. In fact, one can convert ordinary differential equation-based ABMs to a corresponding partial differential equation-based description in the continuum limit. The main advantage of this approach is its analytical tractability that is not usually present in ABMs that are computational in nature. Analytical tractability enables the identification of steady states, assessment of their stability, and determination of parameter ranges for polarized tissue states, thereby revealing conditions that lead to global alignment or loss of polarity. This approach can also be naturally extended to non-planar geometries and topologies. However, the advantages of the continuum theory come at a cost, that is, its lack of capturing cellular levels of details which are usually observed and reported in experimental literature.

Effects of modeling approaches

Does model dimensionality matter in PCP?

In statistical physics, it is known that criticality, characterized by a qualitative change in the system behavior from polarized to unpolarized, strongly depends on the number of dimensions in which the system exists93. For example, in one dimension (1D), many models (for instance Ising model94) cannot sustain long-range order, so no true phase transition occurs. In two dimensions, critical phenomena emerge but with unique behaviors, while in three or more dimensions, the system exhibits more familiar continuous transitions with universal critical exponents95. Thus, dimensionality fundamentally shapes how order and fluctuations interact96. This suggests that in PCP, too, the transition between unpolarized and uniformly polarized tissue state is likely to be dependent on the model dimensions (Fig. 3).

Although all of the modeling approaches described in the previous sections can be implemented in one and two dimensions, many modeling studies have examined PCP dynamics in a one-dimensional setting (despite epithelial tissues being inherently two-dimensional), making important to consider how the dimensionality of the model influences the results39,78,79,97,98. Specifically, these works have explored the role of intercellular interactions, tissue-level protein expression gradients, unidirectional and bidirectional signaling (reciprocal vs non-reciprocal interactions, in statistical physics parlance, also see section on Relation with other physical systems) on PCP establishment and maintenance. Given the computational nature of these works, it needs to be seen, at least in some of these cases, if there is any change in the results if the model is translated to two dimensions. Notably,99 provided a rare experimental counterpart by demonstrating PCP establishment in the one-dimensional notochord of Ciona. This work revealed that local cell-cell interactions and global cues jointly coordinate polarity, making this system uniquely suited to test hypotheses generated from one-dimensional modeling studies.

Simulation domain: how big is big enough?

Similar to the model dimensions, the domain size used in the computer simulation of the model can also have a significant effect on the outcome. This is well acknowledged in statistical mechanics100,101. Therefore, it is crucial to notice the system size reported in the modeling studies to assess the robustness of the proposed mechanisms for the PCP. As expected the Cellular Potts and Petri net models, given their large degrees of freedom, are not well equipped to simulate large tissue domain, as opposed to agent based, phenomenological, and continuum modeling frameworks where large system sizes can also be simulated. While simulations using domain sizes comparable to those of a particular biological tissue are appropriate from a developmental perspective, they are often less rigorous from a mathematical or physical standpoint in delineating the effects from the boundary (or system size) and biological factors. Importantly, tissue size itself impacts (Fig. 3), as polarity can be established early when boundary signals alone may be sufficient, but not in large tissues38. In large tissues, however, in the presence of non-local intracellular interactions, the boundary signals can generate collective PCP82. A recent modeling work by65 shows that in small tissues, boundary effects strongly impact polarization, often destabilizing global alignment. As system size increases, long-range order becomes more robust, with feedback mechanisms stabilizing uniform polarity across the tissue. However, very large domains result in the emergence of complex collective structures, such as swirling patterns, reflecting sensitivity to noise and heterogeneity. Thus, attention to domain size is critical for reconciling biological relevance with physical and mathematical rigor in PCP modeling.

How does the choice of model shape our understanding of PCP?

The choice of modeling framework strongly influences the scope and resolution (time and space) of PCP studies (Fig. 2). Potts models provide the highest spatial detail, capturing subcellular protein distributions and fine-grained interface dynamics, but they are computationally expensive and thus limited to smaller systems. Agent-based, Petri net, and phenomenological models simplify this by representing cells and their interfaces as discrete agents, allowing efficient study of tissue-level interactions, albeit without subcellular resolution. At the other extreme, continuum models treat tissues as continuous media, capturing large-scale polarization patterns across thousands of cells, though they lose cell-level details. Thus, each approach offers a distinct trade-off between resolution and scalability.

Apart from the differences in the length and timescales considered in different modeling approaches, the mathematical models also differ in biological regulations. These difference are in terms of the intra-cellular38,82 and inter-cellular81,97 interactions among the proteins involved80,81,92, PCP establishment through protein binding-unbinding or protein-complex binding-unbinding38,39,78,79. Modeling works, by considering such biologically contrasting mechanisms, have proposed different mechanistic models of PCP requiring experimental validation.

Effect of biological factors

Intracellular interactions: necessary or not?

Despite significant progress in understanding PCP, the role of intracellular interactions remains unresolved, particularly regarding whether they are necessary and/or sufficient for PCP establishment. While it is clear that intracellular interactions alone cannot account for long-range polarity ordering, their precise necessity has yet to be definitively established (Fig. 3). Even recently, the experimental reports on the requirement of cell-autonomous interactions for PCP establishment have presented contrasting conclusions (102 versus103).102 argues that intercellular interactions are necessary and sufficient for PCP, which is in contrast to the findings by103, which shows cell-autonomous mechanisms to be sufficient. Of course, one possible explanation for these contrasting mechanisms can be attributed to the different model systems used in these works. Mathematical modeling, therefore, can bring a unifying framework to systematically compare these experimental findings, explore how variations in cellular or tissue context alter PCP establishment, and predict the parameter regimes under which one mechanism may be favored over the other. The role of intracellular interactions in reinforcing or amplifying asymmetry within the core module is well established9. However, whether these interactions are strictly necessary for PCP establishment, and under what conditions their necessity arises, remains to be comprehensively investigated. In a computational work by ref. 82 it is shown that asymmetric localization of PCP proteins within individual cells is central to amplifying polarity cues. Intracellular interactions become necessary when external signals are weak, variable, or noisy. Under such conditions, they are essential for amplifying small polarity cues into stable asymmetric states, something that weak intercellular mechanisms alone cannot achieve.

Can cell geometry guide PCP?

In the work, by ref. 82 an agent-based modeling approach is followed to study the effect of intracellular interactions and cell shapes on PCP. Based on the core module of the PCP, the model assumes finite pools of membrane proteins (Frizzled, Vang, etc.) and includes cooperative cytoplasmic interactions that can act locally or nonlocally across junctions. Here, the notion of interactions being “local” or “nonlocal” is determined by the protein diffusion length scale relative to the cell size. Given that the interactions between the proteins (or protein complexes) bound to the membrane are via the protein molecules present in the cytoplasm, the interactions are “nonlocal” if the diffusion length scale is comparable (or greater) to the cell size. If the cell size is much larger, the interactions remain localized. A central finding is that nonlocal intracellular interactions (small cell dimensions) are essential for robust polarization, especially in geometrically irregular tissues. Importantly, tissue elongation was predicted to orient polarity perpendicular to the axis of elongation, in line with experimental observations in systems like the mammalian cochlea and skin42,104. The study shows that cell shape anisotropy directly couples to polarity orientation, providing a mechanistic explanation for how geometry guides tissue-wide alignment. This work also highlights how geometric disorder destabilizes polarity in the absence of nonlocal interactions and predicts conditions where elongation acts as a global orientational cue. Thus, the study establishes a crucial link between cell geometry and PCP robustness, emphasizing the interplay between intracellular signaling and tissue-scale morphology.

Does protein expression gradients act only as a global cue?

Using mathematical modeling, the tissue-level expression gradients of proteins, specifically of Fj and Ds, have been explored to act as the global cue for alignment of the polarity established through local interactions39,78,79. Using an agent-based model, in the work39, it is shown that in PCP, Ft-Ds heterodimers exhibit collective polarization through local cooperative and inhibitory interactions, enabling cells to sense weak gradients and propagate polarity across tissues. Using a similar modeling framework (albeit with different assumptions about molecular interactions),78 further generalized the principles of gradient interpretation. Their work demonstrated that molecular bridges formed by heterodimeric interactions (such as between Ft and Ds) are fundamentally more effective than homodimeric ones in generating robust, amplifiable polarity. A key insight from their model is the trade-off between gradient steepness and tissue-wide coordination. Shallow gradients produce more uniform polarity across a tissue, whereas steeper gradients, while enhancing the polarity within a cell, can introduce variability in tissue-scale polarization. But is generating coordination of PCP at the tissue scale the only role of these gradients? In a recent modeling work using agent-based and continuum approaches, it has been shown that the tissue-wide expression gradient (even in a single protein) plays a dual role81. In addition to being the global cue for tissue-wide polarity coordination, it also enhances the stability of the polarized state against localized or stochastic perturbations.

How do localized perturbations affect tissue-level PCP?

Localized perturbations or disturbances in the PCP context come in two flavors: first, gain/loss of a PCP protein in a small region of tissue; and second, irregularities in protein expression levels in the tissue. The first of these is realized experimentally by clonal disruptions in the expression levels of the proteins48,105 and in mathematical models by setting protein expression levels to a low (in case of loss) or high (in case of gain) levels in a small region with the simulation domain37,78,81. In this case, in addition to the loss of PCP in the affected region of the tissue (clone), the polarity of the surrounding cells is also disrupted, usually in a direction that no longer aligns with the tissue axis. This phenomenon has been labeled domineering nonautonomy, and its exact effect on the surrounding tissue depends on the loss/gain of the specific protein.

The second type of local irregularity in protein expression levels is naturally present in any tissue, as all cells cannot have exactly the same protein levels or a ‘smooth’ gradient (which is usually an assumption in most modeling work). To assess the effect of this source of disturbance, a recent work81 considered the expression level of one of the two proteins (Ft and Ds) to have ‘quenched’ noise in the tissue. The effect of such spatially varying but temporally fixed noise has been explored in other contexts in statistical mechanics106. In this setting, it is shown that the spatial ‘quenched’ noise mainly affects the overall PCP coordination in the tissue with little to no effect on the polarity of individual cells (Fig. 3). The loss in the tissue-level coordination is also smaller in the presence of the expression gradients.

What is the role of stochasticity in PCP?

In addition to spatial variability, stochasticity also arises temporally from dynamic noise. This refers to the inherent randomness in biochemical processes, such as the binding and unbinding of proteins within a cell or at cell-cell interfaces. This effect is usually considered in the models by incorporating noise terms in the dynamical equations38,81. In these works, it has been shown that, in the absence of an external global cue (such as a gradient), swirling patterns emerge from dynamic noise. At small noise amplitudes, all the cells in the tissue remain polarized in the same direction. With an increase in the noise amplitude, patches of polarization are formed, and the number of such patches increases with a further increase in the noise. Therefore, at a high strength of dynamic noise, the coordinated tissue polarity is lost (Fig. 3).

Relation with other physical systems

There are several examples in statistical physics where large-scale alignment or order emerges from local interactions. Some of them include the Ising and Potts models for discrete spins on a lattice107, the XY model for continuous planar spins108, and the Vicsek model109 and Toner-Tu theory110,111 for the polar flocking and hydrodynamics of active matter. These systems offer a powerful theoretical perspective for analyzing PCP, as they also rely on local interactions to achieve global order (Fig. 3).

In previous works, the PCP has been looked at from the perspective of these frameworks. For example, in the phenomenological model described earlier, the cell polarity is considered to be analogous to the magnetization, and the global cue is interpreted as the externally applied magnetic field86,87. Similarly, this analogy between magnetization and PCP is further invoked in the work by ref. 38, where the stochastic effects are argued to be similar to those of the temperature in classic magnetization models.

It should be noted that among these, the Ising, Potts, and XY models describe equilibrium statistical mechanics, whereas the Vicsek and Toner-Tu models capture non-equilibrium statistical mechanics. From this perspective, it is also important to identify the equilibrium versus non-equilibrium nature of the PCP models. Since phenomenological models86, CPM62, are based on potential energy minimization, they present PCP as an equilibrium phenomenon.

For other modeling frameworks that rely on protein-protein interactions, the equilibrium or non-equilibrium nature of the PCP is to be determined from the details of those interactions. For instance, the model proposed in ref. 97 incorporates non-reciprocal interactions between proteins in adjacent cells, which inherently places the description of PCP outside the realm of equilibrium thermodynamics. The active agent models of Vicsek and Toner-Tu theory do present similar qualitative pictures (with global polarized state, swirling patterns, etc.) as seen in PCP phenotypes. But it needs to be seen if the non-equilibrium character of these models also extends to the PCP. Despite these visual similarities, PCP fundamentally differs from these flocking models due to the absence of any advective transport of the agent (a cell, in the case of PCP), at least not in a static tissue. But this does not prevent the advection of polarity information across the tissue in the PCP. In motile tissues, however, this distinction may blur, and the similarities between PCP and active flocking systems may become more pronounced. Therefore, the fundamental nature of PCP, whether it is best conceptualized as an equilibrium or a far-from-equilibrium system, remains a pivotal open question, whose resolution hinges on accurately characterizing the microscopic interactions.

Challenges and open questions in PCP modeling

Nature of molecular interactions underlying PCP

As described in the Modeling approaches section, different modeling approaches for PCP are based on specific assumptions about molecular interactions. For instance, some works consider protein binding-unbinding at cell membranes as the building block for the model, others explicitly consider heterodimer or homodimer interactions at cell-cell interfaces, and others consider non-local intracellular interactions among the proteins involved. Despite these fundamental differences at the molecular levels, almost all of the models are able to recapitulate the experimental observations, at least qualitatively. This begs a question - exactly which molecular mechanism is responsible for PCP establishment? As far as we know, there has been no work to distinguish the contribution of the underlying assumptions about the molecular interactions in PCP. It is undoubtedly challenging to infer the nature of molecular interactions from observations at the cellular or tissue level, since these do not provide spatial resolution at the molecular scale.

It will require a concerted effort, combining targeted experimental perturbations with iterative mathematical modeling and quantitative comparison between the two to resolve this. Specifically, models must generate testable, quantitative predictions that are unique to their underlying assumptions, which can then be validated or refuted by high-resolution spatiotemporal data from experiments.

Interactions between two modules: series versus parallel action

As described earlier, the relationship between the Ft-Ds module and the core module can be argued as either sequential (series) or independent (parallel), with experimental evidence supporting both views. In favor of a series model, studies in the Drosophila eye show that gradients of Ds and Fj establish Ft-Ds asymmetry, which is transferred to core protein asymmetry via PkSple isoform.

In contrast, support for a parallel model comes from the wing and abdomen, where the pathways can function independently. Uniform overexpression of Ds can rescue trichome orientation, and Ft or Ds overexpression can repolarize neighboring cells even without Fmi or Fz, showing core-independent action.

The arguments supporting both mechanisms are primarily experimental, as there has been no modeling effort explicitly assessing their relative roles and outcomes (Fig. 3). This may be because most modeling studies have focused on either the core module or the Ft-Ds module in isolation. It is therefore timely for the modeling community to integrate all players into a unified framework to rigorously test whether these pathways act in series, in parallel, or through a hybrid logic.

Link with tissue mechanics

One of the most important open questions in PCP modeling is how polarity is coupled to tissue mechanics, a connection that has been repeatedly observed but remains poorly captured in theoretical frameworks57. This is mainly due to the fact that mathematical models of PCP consider the timescale of tissue mechanics to be much larger than that for PCP and assume a static tissue morphology. The coupling between the two processes is bidirectional: PCP affects tissue mechanics, and tissue mechanics affects PCP. Between these two, the role of PCP (or rather PCP proteins) on tissue mechanics has been explored using mathematical models (a few examples are refs. 112–114) to show the role of Ft and Ds in tissue morphogenesis, given their dual roles regulating cell-cell adhesion and tumor suppression.

The effect of tissue mechanics/flows on PCP dynamics has also been investigated in some specific contexts87,115–118. In these works, the cell flows are identified to be the drivers of the emergence of tissue-scale coordinated polarity115–117, or to be competing with the polarity in the global-module proteins118. Apart from flow-driven PCP regulation, the work by ref. 87 identifies the role of mechanical tension between normal and PCP-mutant cells to be the regulatory mechanism to minimize the disruptive impact of the PCP mutant cells as topological defects in the epithelium. These works show the role of tissue mechanics at different time scales- in PCP emergence (regulated by cell flows at shorter time scales) and its maintenance (regulated by line tensions between cells). Exploring the bidirectional coupling between tissue mechanics and PCP,119 have demonstrated that PCP proteins and anisotropic tissue stress drive the crystalline packing of cone photoreceptors in the zebrafish retina. Quantitative live imaging of the Xenopus neural plate has revealed that the dynamic localization and turnover of PCP proteins are tightly coordinated with actomyosin-driven junction contraction, linking molecular polarity directly to the mechanical forces of convergent extension120.

The challenge now is for the modeling community to develop general frameworks that integrate mechanics and polarity within a single dynamical system for multiple (Fig. 3). Vertex models of epithelial packing that capture emergent flows and stresses, and continuum models, provide promising starting points, but they must be coupled to explicit PCP molecular asymmetries. Only then can we rigorously ask whether mechanics serves as a parallel cue, a downstream consequence, or an overriding input to molecular pathways.

Effect of organ shape and topology

While we now appreciate that PCP signaling is a fundamental architect of epithelial organization, its specific role in adapting to complex organ morphology presents an open frontier. For instance, how does the intricate topology of a branching duct system actively instruct and constrain PCP protein localization? The finding that apical restriction of Vangl in pancreatic ducts is essential for maintaining epithelial integrity121 offers a clue, but it challenges us to understand the interplay between form and function.

Furthermore, can we truly generalize these PCP mechanisms across vastly different tissue types? The equally critical role of PCP in directing crypt positioning for embryo implantation in the uterus122 suggests the role of geometric cues. This presents the central challenge in mathematical modeling of PCP- to move beyond isolated observations and forge an integrated mathematical and computational framework that explicitly links subcellular PCP molecular interactions, macroscopic organ geometry, and active tissue (Fig. 3). Only through such an effort can we identify the common features of PCP in different contexts, and ultimately reveal how individual molecular factors are harnessed to sculpt biological form and function.

Conclusion

In summary, mathematical modeling has been indispensable in unraveling the complexity of PCP, linking molecular interactions to emergent tissue-scale organization. A variety of approaches, each built on distinct assumptions, have yielded complementary insights into polarity establishment, robustness, and multicellular outcomes. Nonetheless, important challenges remain in both the biology and physics of PCP. Addressing these will require close integration of modeling with quantitative experimentation, fostering multidisciplinary efforts that can ultimately deliver predictive frameworks for understanding and regulating PCP in development, physiology, and disease.
