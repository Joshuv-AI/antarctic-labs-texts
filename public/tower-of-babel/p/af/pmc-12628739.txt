Navigating cultural diversity in diabetes care: Insights from Australian healthcare professionals
Authors: Diane Gargya, Vincent Chan, Thilini Thrimawithana, Kimmi Keum Hee Ko, Ieva Stupans, Barbora de Courten, Chiao Xin Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo address the growing challenge of diabetes management in culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations, this study explores healthcare professionals' (HCPs) knowledge, attitudes, and practices on providing culturally appropriate diabetes care to people with diabetes of CALD backgrounds. With diabetes disproportionately affecting CALD populations, understanding how care can be tailored to socio‐cultural context is critical for improving diabetes outcomes.MethodsA qualitative design was employed, using semi‐structured interviews with Australian HCPs involved in diabetes care. Eleven participants were purposively sampled to ensure diverse professional perspectives, including general practitioners, endocrinologists, nurses, pharmacists, dieticians, and a podiatrist. Data collection, conducted between April and October 2024, included face‐to‐face and virtual interviews. Reflexive thematic analysis, informed by critical realist ontology, was used to identify themes.ResultsThematic analysis identified four themes: (1) The Compassionate Carer, focusing on empathy and building trust to provide individualised care; (2) The Lifelong Learner, emphasising cultural humility and the importance of ongoing learning; (3) The Person‐Centred Provider, highlighting shared decision‐making and flexible, person‐driven approaches; and (4) The Culturally Attuned Provider, addressing the need for HCPs to understand the contexts of people with diabetes. HCPs demonstrated a commitment to continuous learning, adaptability, and respectful communication tailored to individuals' unique cultural contexts, with health technology supporting active engagement.ConclusionThis study highlights the critical role of compassion, cultural humility, and understanding in providing culturally responsive diabetes care. Supporting HCPs through ongoing learning and reflective practice, while fostering empathy and humility, is essential. Insights generated underscore the need for structural support, including organisational flexibility, interpreters, culturally tailored resources, and digital tools to improve access and communication. Aligning system‐level support alongside HCPs' commitment to person‐centred care is essential for providing equitable and sustainable diabetes care for CALD populations.

INTRODUCTION

Australia is widely regarded as one of the world's most multicultural countries. The Australian Bureau of Statistics identifies culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations by factors such as country of birth, languages spoken at home, English proficiency, and parents' country of birth or religious affiliation. 1 Currently, 31.5% of Australians are born overseas, and 22.8% speak a language other than English at home. 1 , 2 Of these, 3.4% report speaking English either not well or not at all. 1 Census data from 2021 indicates a higher prevalence of diabetes among people born overseas (7.0%) compared with those born in Australia (4.7%). 1 Living with diabetes often affects quality of life due to the ongoing burden of management and associated complications. 3 , 4 , 5

Australia's healthcare system includes both public and private providers, offering universal coverage through Medicare, which subsidises hospital and primary care services. However, CALD communities often experience unique barriers in accessing the healthcare system and engaging with treatment, impacting their quality of life. 6 These challenges highlight the importance of culturally responsive diabetes care, which considers cultural backgrounds, language, health literacy, and individual experiences. 7 This approach is particularly important for diabetes self‐management education and support (DSMES) programmes, which aim to equip people with diabetes with essential knowledge, skills, and motivation. 4 , 5 Evidence suggests that culturally responsive care improves engagement and self‐management among people with diabetes and leads to better glycaemic control compared with standard care. 7 , 8 For example, Diabetes Victoria's (Australia) culturally adapted Life! Programme delivers interventions in participants' preferred languages and cultural dietary practices, resulting in high retention rates and improved health outcomes. 9

Healthcare professionals (HCPs) play a crucial role in supporting self‐management of people with diabetes. Their attitudes, communication skills, and ability to provide culturally responsive care can influence health outcomes. 8 , 10 HCPs must understand different cultural perspectives, recognise their own biases, and continually develop cultural competence and humility. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 By integrating the foundational elements of cultural competence– cultural desire, awareness, knowledge, skills, and encounters, 13 with a lifelong practice of self‐reflection, openness, respect, and a commitment to equity and partnership, 12 this approach equips HCPs to provide clinically effective and culturally responsive diabetes care. 14 , 15

Most research on diabetes care for CALD populations in Australia focuses on the perspectives of people with diabetes, with limited insight into HCP experiences. 16 , 17 Exploring the nuanced interactions between HCPs and people from CALD backgrounds in community and outpatient settings can generate insights that may contribute to a culturally responsive approach to DSMES delivery and inform adaptable communication and care strategies. This study aims to explore HCPs' perspectives on essential knowledge, attitudes, and practices for effective diabetes care among people from CALD backgrounds. It also identifies potential strategies for culturally responsive, person‐centred, and inclusive diabetes care interventions.

THE METHODOLOGY

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative approach underpinned by a critical realist ontology and a contextualist epistemology. Critical realism assumes that reality exists independently of human perception, but our understandings of it are always mediated by language, culture, and social context. 18 , 19 Reality is therefore singular, yet knowledge of it is necessarily partial and situated. HCPs' views on providing diabetes care to people from CALD backgrounds were understood as reflecting both their lived experiences and the broader social and systemic structures shaping practice. Complementing this, a contextualist epistemology recognises that knowledge cannot be separated from the knower; it is co‐constructed through the interaction between researcher and participant. 18 , 20 Research knowledge is viewed as local, provisional, and reflexively produced, shaped by both the research context and the interpretive role of the researcher. 18 , 20

Within this approach, a semi‐structured format was chosen for its ability to capture rich, detailed insights into HCPs' experiences and practices in providing diabetes care to individuals of CALD backgrounds. 21 It facilitated participant reflection, exploration of contextual factors, and open‐ended dialogue, aligning with the study's focus on capturing personal and contextualised accounts of healthcare provision. 18

The interview guide, developed collaboratively by the research team and informed by existing literature, 22 , 23 was refined through pilot testing with two HCPs to ensure relevance and clarity. Iterative revisions were made throughout the study to refine the focus and coverage (Appendix S1).

Participants

Participants were recruited through professional networks, social media, and direct interactions. Snowball sampling was encouraged to broaden recruitment beyond the researchers' immediate professional networks. Recruitment materials emphasised the voluntary and confidential nature of study participation, without any obligation to take part, to reduce the risk of perceived pressure or social desirability bias. Purposeful sampling targeted HCPs directly involved in the diabetes annual cycle of care, 5 prioritising those with specialised roles in diabetes management. Eligibility criteria included (1) being an Australian registered/recognised HCP, (2) being involved in diabetes care within the past 12 months, and (3) experience providing care to people from CALD backgrounds and/or carers.

Of 19 expressions of interest, 14 HCPs were interviewed. Three participants were excluded due to their focus on practice with First Nations populations, which fell outside the study scope. The final dataset included 11 HCPs representing a diverse range of professions (Table 1). Most participants practised in the greater Melbourne area and private settings, with over half having more than 10 years of professional experience. Sample size was guided by the concept of information power, whereby adequacy was determined not only by the number of participants but also by their professional relevance and specificity to the study aim. 24 The inclusion of HCPs from different professional backgrounds was intentional, as these practitioners collectively contribute to the annual cycle of diabetes care in primary care settings.

Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with written and verbal consent obtained. Ethical approval was granted by the University HREC (project number 26481).

Dataset generation

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted from April to October 2024 using the study interview guide. Interviews were audio‐recorded and averaged 50 min in length (range 30–60 min). To accommodate participants' schedules, interviews were conducted in person (n = 1), video calls (n = 8), and phone calls (n = 2). At the start of each interview, participants were reminded that there were no right or wrong answers and were encouraged to share their experiences, insights, and perspectives openly.

All interviews were conducted by the first author (DG), a registered pharmacist, credentialled diabetes educator, and a first‐generation immigrant with 15 years of community healthcare experience. Transcription was completed using Microsoft Teams and Word (North Sydney, Australia) services, followed by a thorough review and correction by DG for accuracy. To maintain participant confidentiality, all identifying information was replaced with coded identifiers. Two participants reviewed their transcripts; no corrections or feedback were provided.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed an inductive approach, focusing on participants' lived experiences within diverse context. 18 Reflexivity was integral to the process, acknowledging how the researchers' professional backgrounds as healthcare practitioners, alongside their experiences as first‐ or second‐generation immigrants, collectively shaped data interpretation. This reflexive stance facilitated nuanced, contextually informed insights, balancing practical understanding with broader cultural and healthcare considerations.

Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) guided the analysis process, 18 following its six iterative phases (Appendix S1). DG familiarised herself with the dataset by listening to audio recordings and re‐reading transcripts, noting initial insights. Initial coding began on hardcopy transcripts to encourage deep engagement, then transitioned to digital tools, Microsoft Word for efficiency and Miro board (San Francisco, United States of America) for visualisation. Over hundred code labels were generated to capture ideas, concepts, or meanings.

Provisional themes were iteratively developed and refined to identify patterns of shared meaning, guided by the research question and the first author's insights. To enhance the richness and depth of interpretation, the research team (CL, TT and VC) independently read and analysed selected transcripts to encourage reflexivity and contribute diverse perspectives of data interpretation. Collaborative discussions among the research team (DG, CL, VC, TT, and IS) further refined themes, ensuring coherence with the data and alignment with the study aims.

Themes and subthemes were finalised, with illustrative quotes selected to demonstrate analytic points (Appendix S1). Finally, this qualitative analytic study is reported following the Reflexive Thematic Analysis Reporting Guidelines (RTARG) 25 (Appendix S1).

RESULTS (THE ANALYSIS)

Data analysis led to the development of four intersecting main themes on HCPs' perspectives on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices essential for effective and responsive diabetes care for people from CALD backgrounds—the compassionate carer, the lifelong learner, the person‐centred provider, and the culturally attuned provider (Figure 1).

The compassionate carer (attitudes)

This theme embodied the HCP participants' attitudes of empathy, sensitivity, and collaboration when providing culturally responsive diabetes care. It comprised three interrelated subthemes: empathetic understanding, interpersonal awareness, and a collaborative mindset. These reflected the nuanced approach grounded in understanding people's unique circumstances, fostering trust, and providing personalised care.

The ‘empathetic understanding’ subtheme reflected the HCP's awareness of and connection to the experiences of people with diabetes, enabling them to provide compassionate and empathetic care. It underscored the importance of moving beyond superficial interactions to understand and engage with the person's emotions and circumstances. Participants emphasised the need to recognise the complex layers of struggles the person with diabetes might face.There's all these […] layers in there that you just don't know at times […] She's probably struggling to make ends meet […] but they are proud people and so they won't come across […] they don't want to admit to you that they're actually having problems. The lifelong learner (attitudes)

The second theme, ‘the lifelong learner’, embodied the HCPs' commitment to the process of cultural competence as a continuous journey. This theme included four subthemes: cultural humility, cultural desire, dynamic learning, and reflective practice. Collectively, these reflected HCPs' openness to interactions, cultural explorations, and self‐reflection to meet the shifting diabetes care needs of people from CALD backgrounds.

‘Cultural humility’ was a recognition of the HCPs' knowledge limitations and embracing people with diabetes as partners in learning. It reflected the understanding that cultural competence cannot be achieved as a fixed goal but is a lifelong process requiring openness and humility. While assumptions might sometimes be unavoidable, they must be recognised and confirmed.You can't just assume […] you ask them, you don't assume that they eat a full carb diet […] You have a bias in your head. But then you also have to confirm it […] just see if it holds true for that person or not. (HCP 7)

You can't just assume […] you ask them, you don't assume that they eat a full carb diet […] You have a bias in your head. But then you also have to confirm it […] just see if it holds true for that person or not. (HCP 7)

It also involved recognising the person's autonomy and supporting person‐centred goals. HCPs respected the choices of people with diabetes, addressing potential power imbalances, while offering education and guidance.They've lived a lot of life, and they've got a lot of knowledge themselves. It's up to them if they want to take your education, take your advice and implement that into their own lifestyle. (HCP 5)

They've lived a lot of life, and they've got a lot of knowledge themselves. It's up to them if they want to take your education, take your advice and implement that into their own lifestyle. (HCP 5)

The subtheme of ‘cultural desires’ captured the intrinsic motivation and openness of HCPs to learn about diverse cultures. Their willingness and curiosity came from an appreciation of diversity and the belief that understanding the person's cultural contexts improve health care and clinical practice.I love learning about the different cultures, and I find it fascinating. But all that information helps us tailor and give them more accurate advice, to support them better than generic (information). (HCP 2)

The strong intention to engage with cultural diversity fostered the foundation for ‘dynamic learning’, leveraging cultural encounters as ongoing opportunities for growth.

‘Dynamic learning’ highlighted how HCPs described the learning opportunity from each encounter with the person with diabetes, adapting practices based on their experiences and individual observations. This iterative and contextual learning process evolved through exposure to diverse populations.It takes a lot of time […] you just observe. The person‐centred provider (practices)

The theme ‘the person‐centred provider’ highlighted the importance of tailoring care to individual needs through collaborative and flexible approaches. It consisted of three subthemes: shared decision making, active partnerships with people, and different modalities of care. Together, these reflected the dynamic interplay between HCPs' clinical expertise and the lived experiences, values, and preferences of people with diabetes.

The subtheme ‘shared decision‐making’ was a key element of a person‐centred approach, involving HCPs collaboratively developing care plans with people with diabetes. It respected their autonomy, integrated their goals, and aligned care with their values. This approach moved beyond rigidly following protocols to co‐creating clinically sound and personally relevant strategies. It involved HCPs sharing accessible, simplified medical information and concepts that were practical and relatable to the people with diabetes.It's all about relating the information to something that the patient can understand […] giving practical recommendations to the patient, things that they can actually inculcate. (HCP 4)

It's all about relating the information to something that the patient can understand […] giving practical recommendations to the patient, things that they can actually inculcate. (HCP 4)

By balancing professional guidance with the agency of people with diabetes, care aligned with their informed choices, ensuring practical and individualised outcomes.

‘Active partnerships with people’ focused on empowering people with diabetes to take an active role in their care. By fostering open dialogue and creating safe spaces for people with diabetes to share their perspectives and experiences, HCPs strengthened therapeutic partnerships. Through active listening and thoughtful integration of their lived experiences, HCPs further enhanced the personalisation of care plans.You tell me your situation, tell me what you're doing, tell me what works for you, tell me your background and what you've been doing and how you do it in your family, your culture […] letting them have that space to share their knowledge. The culturally attuned provider (knowledge)

The theme ‘the culturally attuned provider’ underscored the value of contextual knowledge in providing responsive and inclusive care. This theme comprised four subthemes: immigrant life, language, cultural practices and social norms, and health and digital literacy. These dimensions reflected the importance of understanding the broader cultural and social contexts in which people with diabetes manage and live with diabetes.

Understanding ‘immigrant life’ and the lived experiences of people with diabetes enabled HCPs to address unique cultural and individual needs, especially for those from disrupted healthcare journeys in war‐torn or underdeveloped regions.They don't know when their birthday is. GENERAL DISCUSSION

This study offers insights into the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of Australian HCPs in providing culturally responsive diabetes care to people from CALD backgrounds. Effective care for CALD populations required compassion, adaptability, and a knowledge‐driven approach. This involved empathy, ongoing learning, person‐centred approaches, and a deep understanding of cultural diversity. These insights emphasised the need for targeted professional development, organisational support, and system change to enhance culturally responsive healthcare delivery.

Expanding knowledge: Cultivating dynamic cultural and clinical competence

HCPs needed a nuanced understanding of the cultural and social contexts influencing diabetes management among people from CALD backgrounds. 13 In this study, HCPs recognised that being aware of immigrant experiences, language preferences, cultural beliefs, and varying levels of health literacy supported their ability to provide responsive diabetes care. These findings aligned with those reported by HCPs from Europe 26 and the United States 17 who also emphasised the value of cultural knowledge in supporting cross‐cultural care practices.

The theme ‘The culturally attuned provider’ highlighted the need for maintaining dynamic cultural knowledge, knowledge that adapted to the shifting needs and contexts of people with diabetes. This included actively developing cultural awareness, refining practical skills, seeking cultural encounters, and being motivated by a genuine desire to improve care quality and equity. 13 , 16 For HCPs in this study, clinical encounters with people from CALD backgrounds provided key learning opportunities. This experiential learning, also observed among Swedish HCPs, was central to developing cultural competence. 17 Practice settings with a high proportion of people from CALD backgrounds, as was the case for many HCPs in this study, facilitated continuous cultural encounters. Campinha‐Bacote's model 13 supported this, identifying such encounters as the foundation for developing cultural competence. The process begins with acquiring cultural knowledge and develops through reflection, skill refinement, and practice. 13 These encounters fostered curiosity and self‐awareness, encouraging HCPs to examine personal biases and value the richness of people with diabetes' cultural perspectives. For example, learning about diverse dietary practices helped refine care strategies and supported individualised, respectful care. 22

The ongoing learning described by HCPs reaffirms that cultural competence should not be viewed as a static end point or a checklist of attitudes and skills. 27 , 28 By integrating attitudes of cultural humility and compassion, HCPs adopted a more dynamic and sustainable approach. 12 , 15 System‐level changes, such as organisational flexibility with sufficient time and resources, could further support this commitment to learning and providing culturally responsive care. 26

Shifting attitudes: Promoting cultural humility and compassion

The dynamic nature of cultural competence relied on continuous self‐reflection, alongside compassion and cultural humility. 12 , 13 , 28 These attributes enabled HCPs to engage effectively with people from CALD backgrounds, build trust, and foster strong person‐provider partnerships. 25

The theme ‘The Compassionate Carer’ demonstrated how compassion, combined with cultural humility, helped HCPs address biases and form meaningful connections with individuals. 12 , 29 Compassion involved acknowledging and addressing suffering through meaningful action, extending beyond clinical tasks to consider the individuals' broader physical, emotional, and social realities influencing their well‐being. 29 In this study, empathy for the personal experiences and emotions of people with diabetes, along with interpersonal awareness of both explicit and implicit needs, was central to this approach; while their collaborative mindset reflected an openness and optimism toward individuals, a genuine desire to help, care, and work alongside them. This attitude enabled HCPs to build trust and foster mutual respect.

‘The Lifelong Learner’ theme reflected HCPs' commitment to self‐reflection and continuous development, driven by cultural desire– the intrinsic motivation to meaningfully engage with diverse communities. 13 Cultural awareness often served as a catalyst for this learning, 13 prompting HCPs to question their assumptions and re‐examine their cultural lens. This growing awareness, in turn, nurtured cultural desires, reinforcing their motivation to continue learning and deepening their engagement with diverse individuals. This was evident in HCPs' willingness to question their assumptions, reflect on biases, and consistently respect the autonomy of people with diabetes during clinical encounters. Such openness supported a dynamic process of learning with and from individuals. Together with self‐reflection, these attitudes embodied core elements of cultural humility, reinforcing its value in providing effective and responsive diabetes care. 12 , 28 , 29

Incorporating reflective practices into professional development, 28 such as journaling and peer discussions, might help HCPs critically evaluate and enhance cultural humility. 30 While compassion and humility were essential, they could not address system barriers alone. 7 , 26 Individual‐level efforts must be supported by organisational changes, embedding cultural awareness across all levels of the health system, redesigning models of care to allow time for building trust and relationships with people with diabetes, and addressing structural inequities in service access. 14

Enhancing practices: Tailoring care to meet diverse needs

Effective diabetes care for people from CALD backgrounds required adaptive, person‐centred practices that respected individual cultural contexts and preferences. National and international diabetes management guidelines emphasised the need for tailored education and self‐management support, sensitive to the individual's cultural, socioeconomic, and literacy background. 4 , 5 Acknowledging their lived experiences and personal goals was essential to fostering sustainable behaviour change and improved outcomes. 9 , 23

The theme ‘The person‐centred provider’ aligned with key elements of person‐centred care, including shared decision making, strong person‐provider relationships, and a broad understanding of care context. 31 HCPs viewed people with diabetes as experts in their own lives. Research supported this approach, showing that directly asking about the cultural needs and preferences of people with diabetes reinforced shared care planning and mutual respect. 17 Previous reviews found that HCPs who embraced this practice were better able to navigate cultural nuances, develop trust‐based relationships, and tailor care to individual needs, core aspects of effective health care participation and involvement. 31 These behaviours reflected a broader shift toward recognising people with diabetes as active participants in care and ensuring that clinical interactions were informed by their values, preferences, and lived experiences. 17

The mutual exchange of knowledge and expertise between people with diabetes and HCPs played an important role in building trust, strengthening engagement, and overall care quality. 32 These strategies reflected the concept of cultural skill, a core construct of Campinha‐Bacote's model, 13 involving the application of relevant cultural knowledge to provide clinically effective and culturally appropriate care. This study reaffirmed that health practices must constantly adapt to the changing needs and contexts of people with diabetes. 16

Flexibility in communication and care strategies was fundamental to facilitating engagement of people with diabetes. HCPs used multimodal communication strategies, including visual aids, interpreter services, and culturally tailored materials, to address language and literacy barriers. 6 , 26 Digital tools were also leveraged to empower individuals, reflecting the growing role of digital health technology in enabling personalised and accessible healthcare. 11 , 32 This adaptable approach was consistent with previous studies that highlighted the importance of adaptable care models when supporting CALD populations. 7 , 16 , 26

Though person‐centred approaches were often viewed as time‐consuming, the long‐term benefits of improved engagement and outcomes often justified the initial investment of time and resources. 17 , 32 Practical tools, such as culturally tailored educational materials, digital tools, and interpreters employed both in this study and others supported communication and engagement. 6 , 7 To sustain the engagement of people with diabetes and effectively integrate culturally responsive care into everyday practice, healthcare systems must allow for structural flexibility. 6 , 26 , 31 , 32

Respecting and understanding cultural diversity required more than awareness of the differences; it involves integrating an attitude of cultural humility alongside an expanding cultural knowledge. These qualities enabled HCPs to respond to individuals' unique cultural contexts with respect, sensitivity, and competence. In this study, HCPs' collaborative approach to person‐centred care was driven by compassion and a commitment to working with people with diabetes as active partners in their care. Their genuine sense of selflessness, paired with a dedication to continuous learning, allowed them to adapt and respond effectively to the evolving needs of CALD populations.

This study highlighted the need for healthcare systems to support culturally responsive diabetes care through ongoing training, reflective practice, and appropriate resources. Clinically, it reinforced the importance of compassionate, person‐centred care that is adaptable to diverse cultural needs. System‐level support, including access to interpreters, culturally tailored materials, and digital tools, was essential to ensure equitable and effective care for CALD populations.

While these insights might be relevant to other multicultural healthcare settings, their transferability depended on local healthcare structures, funding models, and policy environments. Principles such as compassion, cultural humility, and person‐centred care are broadly relevant but must be adapted to align with the specific healthcare system context.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that contextualised its interpretations. The small, purposive sample of HCPs involved in diabetes care for CALD populations ensured data relevance and depth but may limit the transferability beyond similar metropolitan practice settings. While purposive sampling supported disciplinary diversity, recruitment was limited to HCPs who self‐identified as having experience with CALD populations. This may have introduced selection bias towards those more engaged or interested in cultural issues, potentially overlooking the perspectives of those less confident or supported in cross‐cultural care. The use of self‐reported data may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants may have naturally emphasised positive aspects of their practice. The study did not include triangulation through interviews or observational data with people with diabetes, which may be considered in future research. Some participants were known to the first author, which may have influenced responses; however, this familiarity also helped build rapport and encourage openness. A reflexive approach was used throughout the study, including regular discussions among the research team to reflect on how researcher perspectives may have shaped data collection and analysis. Despite these limitations, the study contributes valuable insights and underscores the importance of reflexivity in qualitative research.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the importance of compassion, cultural humility, and cultural understanding in providing person‐centred care to CALD communities. Supporting HCPs through ongoing learning and reflective practice, while fostering attitudes of empathy and humility, is essential. It also points to the need for healthcare systems to strengthen support structures, including the use of interpreter services, culturally appropriate resources, and digital tools to improve access and communication. These supports can help build trust, support engagement, and contribute to better health outcomes. Aligning system‐level support alongside HCPs' commitment to person‐centred care is essential for providing equitable and sustainable diabetes care for CALD populations. Further research should explore people from CALD backgrounds' experiences and assess the impact of culturally responsive training and care models across different practice settings to inform service and policy development.
