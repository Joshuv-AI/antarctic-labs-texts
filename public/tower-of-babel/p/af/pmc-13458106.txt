Quantifying the impact of time-delayed reporting and supportive care in measles outbreak: A simulation approach for Texas, USA
Authors: Chidozie Williams Chukwu, Ousmane Koutou, Moussa Ouédraogo, Dawit Denu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines how time delays in vaccination response, case reporting, and access to supportive care influence measles transmission during the recent outbreak in Texas, USA. We develop a delay-based compartmental model that captures both biological and operational features of measles control and calibrate it using Texas reported Measles incidence data from January 20 to May 27, 2025. Analytical and numerical results show that delays in reporting and intervention can substantially weaken outbreak control, allowing transmission to persist even in highly vaccinated populations. In contrast, timely case detection, improved vaccination uptake, and access to supportive care significantly reduce transmission and can drive the effective reproduction number below unity. These findings highlight the critical role of minimizing reporting and healthcare delays alongside sustained immunization efforts to prevent measles resurgence and support elimination goals in Texas and similar settings.Keywords: measles, time delay, supportive care, sensitivity analysis, numerical simulations

1. Introduction

Measles remains a major public health concern, even in countries with established vaccination programs. Despite being preventable through effective immunization, the disease continues to cause outbreaks due to various social, behavioral, and logistical challenges. In recent years, the United States has experienced a rise in measles cases, with Texas among the most affected states.1,2 This resurgence highlights the need to comprehensively understand the factors driving measles transmission and control failures.

Several factors have driven the resurgence of measles in the U.S., including declining vaccination rates, vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, and increased global mobility.3,4 Despite the national elimination of measles in 2000, cases have sharply risen by 2025. As of mid-year 2025, over 1,300 confirmed measles cases had been reported across 38 states and Washington, D.C., marking the highest annual total in more than 30 years.1,5 Most of these cases ( 92−95% ) occurred in unvaccinated individuals or those with unknown vaccination status.1,6 Texas has emerged as the epicenter of the outbreak. Beginning in late January 2025, the West Texas outbreak has now accounted for more than 750 confirmed cases, leading to at least two child deaths.5,7 The outbreak originated in Gaines County, where measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination rates have declined from nearly 97% in 2019 to about 94% in 2024 among kindergarteners, with some tight-knit communities reporting exemption rates as high as 14−20% . 8 These trends have created immunization gaps that facilitated onward spread to neighboring states such as New Mexico, Oklahoma, and Kansas. 1 This unprecedented resurgence is largely attributed to clusters of vaccine refusal, weakened trust in public health messaging, funding cuts to immunization programs, and delays in case reporting that hampered early containment efforts. 6

County-level reporting delays and logistical challenges in Texas further exacerbated the outbreak response. In general, these developments underscore the critical need to address behavioral and systemic barriers to vaccination to prevent localized outbreaks and preserve national measles elimination status.

Early detection and rapid reporting of measles cases are crucial for effective outbreak control. Delays in diagnosis or reporting to public health authorities can allow the disease to spread unchecked, particularly in highly connected populations. Furthermore, timely access to supportive care significantly affects outcomes, particularly for vulnerable populations such as children and the immunocompromised. 9 Modeling studies have shown that even short delays in public health interventions can dramatically increase the size and duration of outbreaks. 10

Mathematical modeling has played a critical role in understanding measles dynamics and informing public health strategies. Traditional compartmental models, such as the Susceptible - Infectious- Recovered (SIR) and Susceptible - Exposed - Infectious- Recovered (SEIR) frameworks, have been widely applied to study transmission, evaluate vaccination campaigns, and assess outbreak scenarios.11,12 More recent models incorporate complexities such as behavioral responses, heterogeneous contact patterns, vaccine hesitancy, and delays in diagnosis or vaccination.13,14 However, many models still rely on national-level data, limiting their applicability to regional outbreaks such as those in Texas. In early 2025, measles resurged in the state of Texas, providing a stark example of how region-specific dynamics can influence outbreak trajectories. The Texas Department of State Health Services (DSHS) confirmed that since late January, more than 700 cases have been reported across more than 30 counties, with over two-thirds of the infections occurring in children and adolescents. 15 Central to the outbreak was the rural South Plains/West Texas region (notably Gaines County), where low MMR vaccination coverage and closely knit communities allowed for rapid spread. The outbreak caused a significant burden: dozens of hospitalizations and at least two fatalities were reported among school-aged children, all of whom were unvaccinated and without known underlying conditions.16,17 These developments underscore the importance of regional modeling approaches that incorporate local reporting and care delay characteristics. The outbreak in Texas illustrates how delays in case reporting, combined with suboptimal supportive care in rural settings and pockets of under-vaccinated populations, can amplify transmission of a pathogen as contagious as measles. Within the framework of compartmental modeling, incorporating these specific features (geographic clustering, under-immunized groups, and reporting lags) allows for more realistic and actionable insights, particularly when evaluating control strategies at the state or county level rather than relying solely on national-level averages.16,18 This paper builds on existing work by integrating delay dynamics and localized parameters to better understand measles spread and control in Texas, USA. In this study, we formulate and analyze a compartmental model to assess the role of time delays in case reporting and supportive care in measles outbreak dynamics. Some of the current literature modeling similar disease dynamics and using different methodologies can be found in Refs. 19 and 20.

This paper contributes to the measles modeling literature by developing a time-delay compartmental framework that jointly incorporates biologically motivated delays and operational public-health delays within a single, data-calibrated model. In contrast to existing measles models that typically examine vaccination or treatment delays in isolation, the present study explicitly integrates vaccination-response delay, disease-progression delay, and reporting delay through a dedicated compartment of delayed reported infectious cases (Qu) , alongside a distinct supportive-care compartment. This structure enables a unified assessment of how delayed immunity development, late case detection, delayed reporting, and access to supportive care interact to shape outbreak dynamics. Furthermore, the model is calibrated using recent Texas-specific measles incidence data from the 2025 outbreak, allowing for regionally relevant parameter estimation and policy interpretation. The analytical decomposition of the reproduction number into contributions from infectious and delayed reported infectious cases, combined with sensitivity analysis of delay parameters, provides new quantitative insight into how moderate reporting delays captured explicitly via Qu , can undermine outbreak control even under high vaccination coverage. These results extend prior delay-based measles models by highlighting the coupled epidemiological and operational mechanisms driving measles resurgence in a real-world setting.

The outline of this manuscript is as follows: The model formulation and assumptions are given in Section 2, while the control reproduction number Rc is examined using the next-generation matrix approach, and the local stability of the disease-free equilibrium is examined in Section 3. In Section 4, numerical simulations demonstrate that reporting delays can significantly compromise outbreak control, whereas integrating timely diagnosis, effective vaccination, and supportive care can substantially reduce disease burden. Also, parameter estimates are based on epidemiological data, and the model is calibrated using recent measles incidence in Texas. Forecasting results indicate that optimizing these interventions concurrently is critical for preventing future measles outbreaks. The paper concludes in Section 5 with a brief discussion and directions for future work.

2. The model

2.1. Model motivation

Mathematical modeling is crucial in understanding and predicting the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases such as measles. Given the complexity of measles epidemiology, including vaccination effects, varying infectious stages, and transmission-to-infectiousness delay and reporting, the development of such a model is essential. In particular, our model incorporates a time delay τi for i=1,2 to reflect the biological latency in disease progression supported by 10 and τ3 representing reporting (behavioral) time delay(lag). We incorporate these delays to account for when individuals become infectious and seek medical attention or receive supportive care. This delay is particularly important for measles, as it affects both the transmission dynamics and the timeliness of control interventions. The compartment Qu specifically represents infectious individuals who have been reported or received supportive care after a delay. The inclusion of vaccination stages, temporary immunity, and delayed reporting enables a more accurate and realistic depiction of disease dynamics, allowing for improved forecasting and evaluation of control strategies, such as immunization campaigns, early detection, and healthcare response systems. This formulation is particularly relevant when delayed intervention or late case detection significantly impacts the control of outbreaks.

The delay parameters incorporated in the model represent distinct epidemiological mechanisms. The parameter τ 1 corresponds to the biological latent period, namely the average time between infection and the onset of infectiousness. For measles, this latent period is well documented and typically ranges from 10 to 14 days. The parameter τ 2 represents a vaccination-related delay associated with the development of partial or full immune protection following vaccination. This delay captures the time required for an effective immune response to develop after vaccine administration and is therefore mechanistically distinct from the intrinsic disease latency represented by τ 1 .

The parameter τ 3 represents a supportive-care access delay, corresponding to the average time between an individual’s entry into the unreported infectious class and subsequent admission into the supportive-care compartment. Unlike τ 1 and τ 2 , which are associated with biological processes, τ 3 reflects healthcare-seeking behavior, treatment accessibility, and operational delays within the healthcare system. It is important to distinguish τ 3 from the parameter ω. While ω governs the rate at which infectious individuals leave the infectious compartment and enter either the unreported infectious pathway or the supportive-care pathway, τ 3 determines the average delay experienced by individuals in the unreported infectious class before receiving supportive care. Thus, ω controls the flow intensity between compartments, whereas τ 3 captures the temporal lag associated with access to treatment. Due to data limitations, τ 3 is generally not directly identifiable from incidence data alone and is therefore informed by epidemiological evidence and plausible ranges reported in the literature.

Humans are recruited at birth, represented by the parameter π , and become susceptible to measles at birth. These susceptible individuals may receive a first dose of the MMR vaccine at a rate ϕ1 ,10,21 and, depending on vaccine acceptance, may subsequently receive a second dose at a rate ϕ2 . Individuals who receive only one dose of the vaccine may still become exposed, albeit at a reduced susceptibility rate, as the vaccine confers partial immunity to the measles virus. Vaccine efficacy is modeled as a reduction in susceptibility among vaccinated individuals, captured by the parameter ξ∈[0,1] , expressed per day. Meanwhile, susceptible individuals may also become exposed through contact with infectious individuals in the I class or delayed reported infectious individuals in the Qu class. This results in a potentially imperfect (or defective) contact that leads to successful transmission. The corresponding force of infection is defined as βS(I+ηQu) , where η represents the relative infectiousness of individuals in the Qu class compared to those in the I class. Exposed individuals may experience a delay before becoming infectious.

Furthermore, a proportion p of infectious individuals seek treatment and move to the supportive care compartment C at a rate ω , while the remaining fraction (1−p) transitions to the class Qu of reported individuals with delay. Individuals in Qu experience a delay in reporting their status, represented by the delay parameter τ3 . Individuals in the Qu and C classes recover at rates γ1 and θ , respectively. We assume a general natural mortality rate μ for all compartments. Additionally, there is a disease-induced mortality rate δ that applies only to individuals in the infectious class I . This induced mortality is considered negligible for individuals in the Qu and C compartments and is therefore excluded from those classes.

The compartment Qu represents individuals who have been identified or reported after a delay but are not necessarily fully isolated from the population. In practical outbreak settings, particularly during large-scale epidemics, case reporting does not immediately imply strict isolation or complete removal from transmission pathways. To capture this distinction, individuals in Qu are assumed to retain residual transmissibility, which is modeled through the modification parameter η∈[0,1] . A value η<1 reflects reduced, but nonzero, transmission due to partial isolation, behavioral changes, or reduced contact rates following detection. When η=0 , reported individuals are fully isolated and do not contribute to transmission. This formulation reflects real-world conditions in which delays in isolation, imperfect compliance, or healthcare system constraints allow continued, albeit reduced, transmission from detected individuals.

The basic reproduction number R0 is the average number of secondary infections caused by a single infectious individual introduced into a completely susceptible population, in the absence of any interventions or control measures, and when control measures (e.g., vaccination, masks, distancing) are already in place, it becomes the controlled reproduction number Rc . We note that the Rc is mathematical rather than operational. A reduction (increment) in Rc will imply a reducing (increasing) in the basic reproduction number, denoted as R0 value. The quantity Rc represents a model-derived effective reproduction number that incorporates both biological transmission processes and operational factors, including reporting delays. While Rc retains a threshold-type interpretation (i.e., Rc<1 indicates a decline in transmission), it should not be interpreted as a direct replacement for the classical basic reproduction number R0. In particular, components related to reporting delays influence the timing and structure of observed transmission rather than the pathogen’s intrinsic transmissibility. As a result, Rc reflects a composite effect of epidemiological and operational mechanisms. Therefore, its use for direct policy thresholds should be approached with caution, especially when interpreting interventions that affect reporting or detection processes rather than underlying transmission dynamics.

4. Numerical simulations

In this section, we carry out numerical simulations. We first describe the model parameterization in the next subsection.

4.1. Parameter calibration

We parameterize our model using the measles incidence in Texas, USA, from January 20, 2025, to May 27, 2025. The measles MMR vaccine is approximately 93% effective after one dose and 97% effective after two doses. These efficacy rates are based on large population studies and are widely used in epidemiological modeling and clinical recommendations. 29 Therefore, we assign ξ=0.93 (dimensionless). The parameter μ represents the natural mortality rate, which is based on the general life expectancy in the U.S., approximately 76.5 years. Therefore, we have μ=176.5×365 day −1 . 30 The disease-induced death rate δ is based on an estimated 1 to 3 deaths per 1,000 reported cases, 31 corresponding to: δ∈[11000,31000] . Assuming an average infectious period of 7 days, the induced mortality rate per day is then: δ∈[0.0017,0.0037]≈[0.000143,0.000429]day−1 . Since the natural recovery rate from measles infection (without supportive care) is given by 18 per day, 32 we assign comparatively lower rates for recovery through supportive care and for delayed reported infectious individuals. Specifically, we assume that the recovery rate with supportive care is θ∈[18×3,18] . Additionally, since the transmission-to-infectiousness latency period for measles infection is between 7-14 days, 9 we carefully choose and assign τ1=7,τ2=10 , and τ3=12 days, respectively. Although the remaining parameters are taken from existing literature, following the epidemiological transmission pattern for measles, parameters such as contact rate, vaccination rates, recovery rates, and rate of entering supportive care compartments are fitted using our data set.

Thus, the results indicate that larger transmission-to-infectiousness delays intensify outbreak severity by increasing the burden of infectious, unreported, and confirmed cases. 5. Discussion and conclusion

5.1. Discussion

This study developed and analyzed a time-delay compartmental model to examine how vaccination, supportive care, and reporting delays shape measles transmission in Texas, USA. The inclusion of delay parameters τi(i=1,2,3) allowed for a more realistic representation of temporal gaps in disease progression, vaccination response, and reporting efficiency. Model calibration using recent Texas measles data produced a strong fit between simulated and observed trends (MSE=8.7431,MAE=2.3769 , RMSE=2.9569) , indicating that the model effectively captures the dynamics of measles spread in the region. Simulation outcomes revealed that time delays have a significant impact on the trajectories of epidemics. In particular, longer vaccination delays (τ1) postpone the development of immunity, resulting in delayed peaks in infection. Delays during the infectious period ( τ2 ) extend the duration of active transmission, while behavioral delays ( τ3 ) sustain low-level transmission and slow the fade-out of the epidemic. However, improved reporting rates ( ω ) and access to supportive care ( C(t) ) can offset these effects, accelerating disease decline. The contour analysis of Rc,τ showed that timely vaccination, efficient isolation, and reduced reporting lag collectively push the effective reproduction number below unity, ensuring epidemic control. In addition, the local sensitivity analyses indicate that transmission-related parameters, particularly β , ξ , and η , exert the strongest influence on Rc . In contrast, delay parameters ( τ1 , τ2 , τ3 ), while epidemiologically meaningful, have comparatively smaller quantitative effects. Therefore, although reducing delays in reporting and intervention can enhance outbreak control, their impact is secondary to interventions that directly reduce transmission, such as vaccination and contact reduction. The role of delays should thus be interpreted as complementary rather than dominant drivers of measles dynamics.

5.2. Model limitations

Despite its robust formulation, the model has several limitations. First, it assumes homogeneous population mixing, overlooking the spatial and social heterogeneities that characterize measles outbreaks in large states such as Texas. Second, the time delays were modeled as fixed constants, whereas they are likely to vary across healthcare infrastructure and reporting systems. Third, stochastic variability, waning immunity, and age-specific factors, which are important for measles dynamics, were not included. Additionally, underreporting or reporting lags in surveillance data can affect the accuracy of parameter estimation. Also, an important counterintuitive outcome of the model is that higher mortality can lead to a reduction in the number of infectious individuals by shortening the time they remain infectious. While this may appear to suggest improved control, the decline is driven by premature removal through death rather than by vaccination, early detection, or treatment. This highlights the need for caution when interpreting reductions in cases that arise from mortality-driven mechanisms rather than effective public health interventions. Nonetheless, the model offers valuable qualitative insights into the mechanisms by which delayed interventions influence outbreak duration and control. Lastly, due to the complexity of the model and the simultaneous estimation of multiple parameters, practical identifiability remains a limitation. Different parameter combinations may yield similar fits to the observed data, particularly when delays are fixed and only incidence data are available. While the use of multiple initializations and goodness-of-fit metrics improves robustness, we acknowledge that parameter estimates may not be unique. This limitation should be considered when interpreting quantitative results. Future work will incorporate formal identifiability analysis and Bayesian inference approaches to better quantify parameter uncertainty.

5.3. Comparison with existing literature

Our findings are consistent with prior studies on measles delay models and contribute new insights for the Texas context. Al-Darabsah et al. 10 demonstrated that time delays in vaccination and treatment can sustain transmission cycles and delay epidemic extinction. The present study extends that framework by integrating supportive care and explicit reporting delays, offering a more operationally relevant model for outbreak response. Similarly, studies by Aldila et al.36,37 emphasized the importance of optimizing intervention timing in infectious disease control. For the U.S. and particularly Texas, few measles models have explicitly incorporated time-delay dynamics calibrated to local data. The current model bridges this gap by combining a mechanistic delay structure with data-driven parameter estimation, providing realistic insights into epidemic behavior in the presence of reporting and vaccination lags. Reports from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1 and the Texas Department of State Health Services 38 further support these findings, emphasizing that delayed case reporting and vaccination uptake have played a significant role in recent measles resurgences. 5.4. Future work and concluding remark

Future extensions of this work include the development of stochastic, age-structured, or spatially explicit models to account for demographic and regional heterogeneity. Integrating mobility data, behavioral dynamics, and real-time surveillance information would enhance predictive accuracy and applicability. Moreover, employing Bayesian inference or hybrid machine-learning approaches could improve parameter uncertainty quantification. In addition, a natural extension of the present work is to formulate a fractional-order as well as using Lie group versions of the model in order to capture potential memory and hereditary effects in measles transmission and intervention response. Such a formulation would allow for a systematic comparison between integer-order and fractional-order dynamics, with particular emphasis on how memory effects influence stability properties, reproduction numbers, and long-term disease persistence.

We note that the proposed model contains multiple epidemiological and intervention-related parameters, some of which are estimated from available data. As in many structured delay differential equation models, this raises potential structural and practical identifiability issues, particularly when incidence data are limited. While several parameters were fixed based on existing literature, different parameter combinations may reproduce similar epidemic trajectories. Consequently, the model’s primary purpose is to provide qualitative insight into the effects of vaccination, delayed reporting, and supportive care on measles dynamics, rather than precise estimation of all parameters. A formal identifiability and uncertainty analysis is left as future work. Finally, extending the framework to a cost-effectiveness setting would enable policymakers to assess the relative benefits of timely vaccination, supportive care, and reporting interventions in controlling measles and similar diseases.

This study highlights the crucial role of time delays in influencing the outcomes of measles transmission. Even moderate delays in vaccination, infectious progression, or reporting can prolong outbreaks and sustain transmission. Conversely, improving reporting efficiency, reducing healthcare delays, and increasing vaccination coverage can rapidly drive the effective reproduction number below one, halting transmission. For Texas and similar regions, these results highlight the urgent need for integrated and timely interventions to maintain measles elimination. The model presented here serves as a quantitative foundation for understanding delay-driven epidemic behavior and offers a practical decision-support tool for public health planning.46–49
