Incorporating economic considerations in clinical practice guideline development: a scoping review
Authors: Lungiswa Nkonki, Funeka Bango, Pamela Vorster, Dachi Arikpo, Gerald Manthalu, Amanda Brand, Tamara Kredo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntegrating economic evidence into clinical practice guideline (CPG) development is key to optimally allocating finite healthcare resources. Economic analysis contributes to decisions that enhance population health by investing in effective and affordable care. However, the absence of an accepted approach for using economic evidence to guide decisions in CPGs is challenging. This study, part of the Global Evidence, Local Adaptation (GELA) project in Malawi, Nigeria, and South Africa, examines and summarises available methodological guidance in the literature for including economic evidence in CPG development.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), World Health Organization (WHO), and Guideline International Network (GIN) libraries from 2013 to 2023 for English-language guidance documents that provided methodological recommendations on integrating economic evidence in CPG development. We also searched ten websites of key guideline development institutions, professional societies, and health technology assessment agencies. Drawing on the various recommendations in eligible guidance documents, we present a summary of suggested approaches.ResultsWe identified 168 guidance documents; after excluding 151 and assessing 17 full-text documents, eight were eligible. Available guidance was categorised into four key processes: assessing the need for economic evidence, defining priorities, outlining the scope of the analysis, and assessing the evidence. Key methodological challenges identified included managing the nuances of building whole disease models and using results from economic evaluations with differing underlying assumptions to inform recommendations. We also identified critical actions for incorporating economic evidence in final guideline recommendations in three areas: methods, presentation of results, and the judgment of cost-effectiveness.ConclusionA transparent and structured process for integrating economic evidence into guideline recommendations is essential for ensuring the relevance of decisions to the implementation context and enhancing confidence in the process. Our findings may support healthcare professionals, guideline developers, health economists, and policymakers involved in CPG development.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14306-7.

Introduction

Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) promote evidence-based and cost-effective healthcare by guiding clinical decisions and optimising health budget use. They are systematically developed recommendations intended to support clinicians in making appropriate care decisions and help health systems use resources efficiently [1]. Typically, CPGs are developed by government agencies or professional societies. Their objectives may differ across settings, for example, standardising clinical practice in countries such as the United Kingdom or informing services under Universal Health Coverage (UHC) in countries like Brazil [2].

Importantly, CPGs operate in conjunction with other evidence-informed processes. Health technology assessment (HTA) provides the technical appraisal of technologies, with emphasis on cost-effectiveness and budget impact. The benefit package design translates both CPG and HTA evidence into coverage decisions about which services are financed under health systems. Although these processes share overlapping methods, they differ in scope, audience, and outputs. HTA is directed at policymakers, CPGs at clinicians, and benefit design at financing decisions [3, 4]. Together, they form complementary layers of decision-making. Their comparative roles and inter-relationships are further detailed in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Growing global health system challenges, including constrained economic resources and rising costs, have increased interest in rigorous CPG development to support efficient healthcare spending to bridge the gap between clinical care and economic realities [5]. Ensuring quality care is crucial for achieving UHC [6]. Yet many countries, particularly low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), struggle with avoidable mortality, morbidity, and economic losses [7]. In such contexts, CPGs play a key role in directing health investments [2]. However, CPGs that omit economic considerations are inadequate for context-specific decision-making and risk inefficient use of limited resources [8]. Therefore, the need for CPG development to be informed by economic evidence, in addition to evidence around anticipated benefits and harms and qualitative considerations, is increasingly recognised. Evidence on economic implications is explicitly included in structured and transparent decision-making approaches, such as the GRADE Evidence-to-Decision (EtD) framework [9]. However, while the importance of incorporating economic evidence in CPG development is widely recognised, approaches to finding or generating appropriate evidence and its consideration in CPG development remain inconsistent [10].

Approximately 75% of countries with formal health decision-making processes report using HTA to inform CPG development, indicating that many jurisdictions, including high-income and some middle-income countries, have mechanisms to incorporate economic evidence into guideline development [11]. Brazil illustrates how this can be institutionalised in a middle-income setting; its Ministry of Health works through the national HTA agency, CONITEC, to ensure that cost-effectiveness and budget impact evidence inform the interventions included in public health guidelines. In contrast, established standards for linking economic evidence to CPG development are common in high-income settings, whereas many LMICs often rely on ad hoc or less formalised processes. Governance processes also differ, with some countries having formal guideline oversight, while others lack formalised coordination [12].

When economic considerations are incorporated into national or international CPGs, such as those from the World Health Organization (WHO), there is limited guidance on how to adapt this evidence to diverse contexts with varying healthcare needs and budgets [13]. A lack of contextualisation can lead to unintended consequences. If these consequences include misjudging the resources needed to implement a recommended new practice, countries may struggle to adopt necessary interventions effectively. In LMICs, this is further compounded by multiple barriers to producing and using high-quality economic evidence, including the scarcity of context-relevant data and limited capacity to conduct or interpret economic analyses [13, 14]. Enabling factors include greater stakeholder acceptance of economic evidence, improving economic literacy among guideline developers, and offering more explicit methodological guidance. These factors highlight the need for comprehensive, standardised guidance on systematically incorporating economic evidence into the development of CPGs [13].

This scoping review aimed to address the lack of explicit methodological guidance by exploring existing literature on practical frameworks and best practices for integrating economic evidence into CPGs. Furthermore, the scoping review foregrounded work on incorporating economic evidence into CPG development within the Global Evidence, Local Adaptation (GELA) project. This project aimed to maximise the impact of research on poverty-related diseases by strengthening the capacity of researchers and decision-makers to adapt global evidence for locally relevant newborn and child health CPGs in Malawi, Nigeria, and South Africa [12]. In Nigeria and Malawi, no standardised processes exist, whereas South Africa has a developing guideline infrastructure that is increasingly linked to HTA processes, with varying consideration of economic evidence.

This study employed a scoping review methodology to examine the range and nature of available guidance on incorporating economic evidence in CPG development. Findings were intended as a resource for researchers and guideline developers, particularly in LMICs, clarifying existing guidance while highlighting areas where methodological development is needed.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was guided by the methodological frameworks for scoping reviews outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis [15] and the paper by Arksey & O’Malley (2005) [16]. In addition, we applied the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) to guide our reporting [17]. Our protocol was published in the public domain (https://zenodo.org/records/12705260).

Search strategy

We systematically searched for both peer-reviewed published articles and grey literature. For published literature, we searched the MEDLINE database (via PubMed) for articles published between June 2013 and June 2023. As this scoping review was intended to inform further work in GELA, we had to follow a rapid review methodology and ensure an acceptable trade-off between responsiveness and rigour. To ensure this is achieved, we restricted our search to one database. The selection of this database was informed by our initial scoping, which indicated that key papers on this topic were published in health services and policy journals, which are well covered by MEDLINE. We developed a search strategy to identify relevant articles in collaboration with an information specialist. Text Words as well as Medical Subject Headings and Subheadings were used to search for concepts related to economic evaluation (“Costs and cost Analysis” and related terms), CPGs (“Practice guidelines as topic” and related terms), and methods (“Methods” and related terms). The search string for MEDLINE is shown in Supplementary Appendix 2. We performed iterative testing of the search terms during the protocol stage, ensuring our search strategy retrieved benchmarked eligible guidance documents. In addition, we searched the websites of guideline repositories (WHO and Guidelines International Network (GIN)) and key guideline development institutions, professional societies, and Health Technology Assessment (HTA) agencies for grey literature guidance documents. These included the United Kingdom’s National Institute for Health Care Excellence (NICE), the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) of Australia, US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), International Society for Pharmacoeconomic and Outcomes Research (ISPOR), Guide to Economic Analysis and Research (GEAR), The Health Intervention and Technology Assessment Program (HITAP) of Thailand, The Centre for Outcomes Research and Economic Evaluation for Health (CORE2-Health) of Japan, the National Committee for Technology Incorporation (CONITEC) of Brazil, and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE).

Inclusion criteria

As this scoping review did not target any specific population or participant group, study inclusion was guided by a modified Population, Concept, Context (PCC) framework shown in Table 1 [15]. Only guidance documents published within the last ten years were considered, given the rapid progress of methods and approaches for CPG development [5]. Where various versions of the same guidance document existed, the most recent version was used. To align with the capabilities of the review team and ensure consistency in the interpretation of the literature, only guidance documents published in English were considered.

Screening and study selection

For the database search, results were imported into EndNote 21, a reference management software, and duplicates were removed. The screening process consisted of two stages: title and abstract screening and full-text screening. Two authors independently screened each article identified through the MEDLINE (PubMed) search against the eligibility criteria using Covidence, a web-based screening tool. Documents retrieved from the grey literature were exported to a Microsoft Excel 2023 spreadsheet. Since abstracts are usually unavailable in grey literature, titles and tables of contents were reviewed by two authors based on the relevance to the research question and the availability of methodological details. Titles that appeared relevant were moved into full-text screening in Excel. Conflicts from all searches were resolved through a deliberative approach and by consulting a third reviewer if necessary.

Data extraction

To systematically capture relevant information on methods for incorporating economic evidence into CPGs, we developed a data extraction tool on Microsoft Excel 2023. The tool was informed by established frameworks for economic evaluations and practical considerations for CPG development [18, 19]. Supplementary Appendix 3 contains the final version of the tool. The tool’s key domains included general study information and guidance characteristics, key steps for integrating economic considerations into CPG development, analytical methods, and applications for contextual adaptation and decision-making. Four reviewers piloted the tool on a subset of studies (25%) to assess clarity and completeness. Feedback from the pilot was used to revise the tool, enhancing its usability and consistency, before full data extraction.

One reviewer charted data from the included guidance documents and extracted information using the finalised data extraction form. A second reviewer reassessed the data extraction for accuracy and quality control. Discrepancies or disagreements were resolved by discussion or consulting a third reviewer.

Data synthesis and presentation

Given the methodological diversity and qualitative nature of the included guidance documents, we conducted a narrative synthesis to summarise key patterns. We used a combined deductive-inductive approach. First, we organised the extracted data into broad categories of interest deduced from the literature, which allowed for comparison across the different guidance documents. We then employed an iterative and inductive approach to identify common themes based on the dominant methodological approaches reported in the included guidance on incorporating economic evidence in CPGs. Our analysis focused on the content of the guidance documents relevant to CPG development. We did not attempt to disentangle the origin of specific methodological standards, but rather to synthesise what the documents collectively present to guideline developers.

Assessment of reporting completeness

We evaluated the completeness of reporting for each document by determining whether the key methodological topics and items from the identified themes were clearly described. In particular, we focused on whether the guidance offered sufficient direction to clinical guideline development teams on how to practically incorporate economic evidence. We were also interested in whether health economists were provided with methodological guidance on how the analysis should be conducted in practice. This approach enabled us to systematically compare coverage across the included guidance documents, thereby highlighting variations in the level of detail provided for the various aspects. One reviewer assessed the reporting completeness, while a second reviewer cross-checked all the documents to ensure consistency. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. We evaluated completeness using a scoring system that assigned one point for each item reported.

Results

We identified a total of 168 guidance documents, consisting of 128 peer-reviewed articles and 40 grey literature documents, respectively. After eligibility screening, we included eight guidance documents, including three peer-reviewed articles and five documents from the grey literature (Fig. 1). Records were mainly excluded because they did not explicitly address the incorporation of economic evidence in CPG development. Other reasons included a lack of formal guidance and being inaccessible in full text.

Following the deductive categorisation, an inductive analysis identified common themes related to the process (the overall structured sequence of activities) and methods within the process for incorporating economic evidence into CPGs. The findings were synthesised into key themes: (1) processes for incorporating economic evidence into CPGs, (2) methodological considerations for conducting economic analyses, (3) steps for including economic evidence in the final recommendations, and (4) methodological challenges. However, the extent to which each guidance document provided comprehensive coverage of the identified themes varied. While some guidance documents offered extensive coverage of multiple themes, others focused only on a subset of themes, omitting certain aspects. Because process and methods guides often cross-reference each other, our synthesis emphasised the content accessible to guideline developers rather than attributing each methodological detail to its source. Presentation of technical economic evaluation methods varied. Documents for technical analysts included detailed methodological standards, while those aimed at policy committees emphasised the structure of the actual processes. The document completeness analysis evaluated the extent to which each document addressed the various items within the identified themes. Table 2 provides the list of the topics that were identified.

Processes for incorporating economic evidence into CPGs

Five of the eight guidance documents we included outlined the process for incorporating economic evaluation evidence [20–24]. We identified four steps: (1) assessing the need for economic evidence, (2) defining priorities, (3) defining the scope of the analysis, and (4) assessing available evidence (Table 3).

Guidance on methodological approaches and techniques for incorporating economic evaluation evidence into CPGs

• Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is recommended as the key analytical technique [18, 21, 23]

• Cost-utility analysis (CUA) is recommended for whole disease models [24]

• The time horizon must be long enough to reflect all-important differences in the costs and outcomes of the interventions under consideration [18–21]

• Lifetime horizon for whole disease models [24]

• Ensure adequate follow-up periods, which may vary by health outcome [25]

• Costs should relate to the perspective used and should be valued using the prices relevant to that perspective [18, 21]

• Costs borne by people using services and the value of unpaid care may also be included if they contribute to outcomes [18, 21]

• Select data sources that most closely reflect the opportunity cost, given the perspective of the analysis [20]

• Consider resource requirements for implementing recommendations across the NHS [23]

• Systematically identify, measure, value, and report all relevant resources consumed or saved as a result of the implementation of the intervention under consideration [20]

• Value relevant resources identified for all sectors in monetary terms. If not possible, present the relevant resources identified in an Impact inventory table for economic evaluations for consideration by decision-makers [20]

• Resource use data can be obtained from existing guidelines on the subject/disease being modelled [24]

• Report resource use in natural units in the evidence profile [25]

• Quality-adjusted life years (QALY) are preferred [18, 19, 21, 24]

• The EQ-5D is the preferred choice for measuring health-related quality of life [18, 19]

• Use the ASCOT instrument for measuring social care-related quality of life [21]

• Use the ICECAP instrument for measuring capability [21]

• Undertake a cost-effectiveness analysis. The guideline development group (GDG) should discuss cost-effectiveness in parallel with general effectiveness when formulating recommendations [21]

• All economic analyses should be validated by systematic checking of model formulae and inputs by a second modeller, sensitivity analysis, ensuring that the model results are plausible and can be explained, comparing endpoints from the model with source materials [21]

• Estimate incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) and, if necessary for interpretation, calculate the net monetary benefits or net health benefits for both reference case analyses [20]

• For analyses with more than two interventions, a sequential analysis of cost-effectiveness should be conducted following standard rules for estimating ICERs, including the exclusion of dominated interventions [20]

• Costs and health effects should be discounted at the same rate [18, 20, 21, 24]

• Alternative analyses using rates of 1.5% for both costs and health effects may be presented alongside the reference-case analysis in specific circumstances, like exploring the impact of uncertainty [19, 21]

• The impact of uncertainty in the discount rate should be assessed by comparing the results of the reference cases to those from non-reference case analyses, using discount rates of 0% and 3% per year [20]

• A context-appropriate discount rate should be used [25]

• Establish the equity dimensions and goals that should be included in the economic evaluation of the program under consideration [20]

• Analyses that incorporate relevant equity concerns should accompany the reference case analysis (e.g., distributional cost-effectiveness analysis, extended cost-effectiveness analysis, or other emerging methods), and be presented alongside the reference case [20]

• Report equity considerations relevant to specific topics and how these were addressed in economic evaluation [18, 21]

• The impact of the recommendation on equity groups based on age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation must be considered [23]

• Use standard and appropriate procedures for characterising uncertainty (parameter, structural, technology-specific), e.g., deterministic or probabilistic [19–21] value-of-information analysis, scenario analyses, Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) and cost-effectiveness acceptability frontiers [CEAFs] [20, 24]

• Explore methodological uncertainty by comparing the reference case results to those from a non-reference case analysis that deviates from the recommended methods to ascertain the impact of methodological differences [20]

Considerations for including economic evidence in the final recommendations

Four of the eight guidance documents we included outlined steps for including economic evidence in final CPG recommendations [22–25]. Guidance on the steps for including economic evidence in the final recommendations included considerations in three areas: (1) methods, (2) presentation of results and (3) judgement of cost-effectiveness.

Methods

Following best practices for economic evaluation, the guidance cautions against the double-counting of costs and benefits, especially when using a cost-consequence analysis [22]. In addition, it is advised that changes in productivity should not be included in evidence profiles as a measure of resource use [27]. The GRADE approach aims to standardise the consideration of resource use in CPG development. Given the significant variation in methods for estimating productivity costs, excluding these helps maintain methodological consistency across different studies, ensuring a focus on direct healthcare costs rather than broader economic considerations and implications. Notably, evidence should focus on best estimates of resource use and not on costs, as costs are a function of both resources expended and the cost per unit of resource [23].

Presentation of results

The presentation of all evidence using tables that include details of data sources is encouraged [20]. Costs must be disaggregated by health state and resource category. In addition, natural units should be used to estimate resource use, as reporting only total costs across broad categories of resource expenditure may leave users without the information required to judge whether estimates of unit costs apply to their setting. Finally, evidence profiles and a summary of findings tables should indicate whether the net benefits are worth the incremental costs. They should always present resource use, not just monetary values [23].

Judging cost-effectiveness

In line with standard economic evaluation guidance, an intervention that dominates the alternatives should be recommended. However, when one intervention is more effective and costly than another, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) should be considered. In addition, when formulating recommendations, cost-effectiveness should be discussed alongside the evidence on general effectiveness [22].

Methodological challenges

The cited methodological challenges broadly relate to economic modelling. This is primarily attributed to the demands of building disease models [27]. For the inclusion of economic evidence into CPG recommendations, using previous economic evaluation studies and pooling resource estimates from different studies should be carefully considered, as it can be controversial and may not be appropriate; therefore, modelling may not be helpful [23]. This guidance is based on the general conduct of economic evaluation studies, where assumptions that differ substantially from those of guideline developers are often made, using evidence on effects and resource use derived from primary research-based sources already summarised in the evidence profile [27]. It is advisable only to present pooled estimates of resource use when the outcome under consideration has a common meaning across the studies involved in the analysis.

Discussion

A framework for guiding the use of economic evidence in CPGs can ensure transparency, credibility and consistency in decision-making. In addition, a consistent approach provides transparent guidance for stakeholders, including policymakers, clinicians, and payers, on how economic evidence is incorporated into clinical recommendations [28]. This scoping review examined the available peer-reviewed and grey literature to guide the integration of economic evidence into CPG development. The findings highlight both process- and method-related considerations for using economic evidence in CPG development. In addition, the findings highlight various critical aspects to consider, including details for incorporating economic evidence into the final recommendations and methodological challenges relevant for guideline developers.

Our findings reveal a variation in the comprehensiveness of the frameworks presented by the organisations included in the scoping review. Some organisations provide more comprehensive and structured frameworks for integrating economic evidence into CPGs [20, 21, 25]. While the included guidance documents differ in their approaches, our synthesis identified four critical steps to follow when incorporating economic evidence into CPGs. The findings indicated that the process for incorporating economic evidence in guideline recommendations must begin with establishing the need for economic evidence. Some guideline development groups routinely consult a health economist. However, many systems also use predefined triggers to signal when in-depth economic analysis and the involvement of a health economist are required. This highlights the need for improved health economics capacity within guideline development groups in LMICs, as well as a range of capacities that require development along the evidence-to-policy continuum [28, 29]. While economic evidence for many LMICs has grown, local capacity for generating economic evidence in sub-Saharan Africa is still limited [30].

Guidance on methods for conducting economic analyses recommended cost-effectiveness analysis as the primary key analytical technique and cost-utility analysis for comprehensive disease models. Other considerations on methods included various steps, such as ensuring that the time horizon is long enough to reflect all important differences in the costs and outcomes of the interventions under consideration. Considerations on costing methods related to the perspective, data sources, and reporting of the costs. Guidance on health outcomes focused on the tools for measurement. Other guidance on methods focused on discounting, dealing with uncertainty, equity considerations, and interpreting the results of economic analyses. These align closely with national HTA reference cases, indicating that CPG guidance is directly based on established HTA frameworks. These methodological considerations are critical as methodological heterogeneity can affect the synthesis of different studies in reviews, undermining the consistency of economically informed recommendations.

Only half of the included guidance documents explicitly stated how economic considerations should be applied during recommendation formulation. Where this was addressed, three considerations emerged. Firstly, regarding the procedure for developing final recommendations, evidence should focus on the best estimates of resource use rather than on costs, which are a function of both the resources expended and the cost per unit of resource. Secondly, when presenting results, natural units should be used to estimate resource use, as reporting only total costs across broad categories of resource expenditure may leave users without the necessary information to determine whether estimates of unit costs are applicable to their specific setting. Thirdly, evidence profiles and summary of findings tables should explicitly specify whether the net benefits are worth the incremental costs.

The judgment of cost-effectiveness should be made following the standard decision rule, wherein an intervention that dominates the alternatives should be recommended, or the ICER should be used when an intervention is more effective and costly than another. Such ICERs are usually assessed against a predefined threshold. However, the absence of context-specific thresholds in many LMICs poses a challenge to consistent decision-making. Reliance on generalised benchmarks, such as the 1–3 times GDP per capita, can lead to recommendations which do not serve country-specific budgetary constraints and health priorities [31].

Our analysis indicates that economic evidence in CPGs and HTA is methodologically aligned, but their functions and audiences differ. While HTA processes are policy-oriented, often requiring full de novo models, CPGs are intended for clinicians and require simplified presentation of findings for multi-disciplinary panels. In CPGs, recommendations may be stratified by patient subgroup or health system level, which is not a common approach in HTA.

Various methodological challenges were identified. One of the main challenges is using results from economic evaluations with differing underlying assumptions to inform CPG recommendations. This becomes especially challenging when results are used to inform recommendations across different contexts, raising questions about the transferability of health economics data [32]. Variations in data sources, quality and assumptions can lead to different conclusions, even for the same alternatives. While adaptations are sometimes possible, it is essential to use context-specific resource use estimates, which requires transparency in the methods of the source economic evaluation [33]. However, local cost data and resource utilisation might not be available in resource-limited settings, which might limit the integration of economic evidence. Only one document provided explicit guidance on contextualisation of external evidence, highlighting a significant gap for LMICs where local cost data are scarce and data from high-income settings are rarely relevant. Efforts to improve the collection of contextual data are critical to facilitating the widespread use of economic evidence in CPG development in these settings [34]. Otherwise, interim strategies such as transferability checklists and scenario analyses may support guideline developers until formal guidance becomes available.

Guidance documents differed on the timing of introducing economic evidence within the CPG decision-making process. Some suggest that economic considerations be introduced in parallel with the appraisal of clinical evidence, while others recommend deferring economic considerations until after clinical effectiveness is established. Considering the economics of interventions early may ensure recommendations are implementable and financially feasible. Frameworks that include economic considerations as one of the explicit domains can provide transparency, without overshadowing clinical evidence [9]. While insufficient use of economic evidence risks unaffordable recommendations, overemphasising economics may undermine equity; however, available frameworks such as GRADE EtD are designed to balance these concerns.

While this scoping review identified key considerations and methods, it also highlighted variation in available guidance. A universally accepted framework could offer structure, consistency and transparency for guideline developers. Such a framework should not only adopt HTA methods, but also address CPG-specific needs, including presentation for non-economist audiences. Strengthening economic expertise in guideline panels would also ensure appropriate interpretation of economic evidence, thereby improving the quality, consistency and application of economic evidence in CPGs [28]. Governance alignment between HTA and CPG processes is also essential. Misaligned processes can create conflicting signals for policymakers and clinicians, especially in LMICs, where effective governance can be challenging [35, 36]. Coordinated governance reduces duplication and strengthens the legitimacy of economic evidence in health decision-making.

These findings are therefore particularly relevant for the three GELA countries. While Nigeria and Malawi lack standardised processes, South Africa has a developing guideline infrastructure increasingly linked to HTA, with varying consideration of economic evidence. In South Africa, the growing integration between the Essential Medicines List, Standard Treatment Guidelines, and HTA processes provides a strong foundation for systematically incorporating economic evidence. To advance progress, efforts should focus on strengthening transparency and ensuring that guideline panels consistently document when and how economic evidence informs recommendations. In Nigeria, the increasing use of WHO and GRADE-EtD approaches to CPG development has heightened the need for practical methods for incorporating economic evidence. A useful entry point would be adapting existing high-quality CPGs to the Nigerian context, which would provide opportunities to integrate contextual economic evidence into recommendations. Additionally, capacity strengthening for producing and utilising economic evidence needs to be intensified. This would include training and generating local data to support the production of locally relevant economic evidence.

In Malawi, economic evidence is already being applied in HBP design; however, its focus does not extend to the Malawi Standard Treatment Guidelines, and disease-specific CPGs remain fragmented across multiple structures. The Malawi Ministry of Health recently approved the use of HTA for new health technologies, which could harmonise processes and open the way for systematic use of economic evidence in CPG development.

This review has some limitations. Given the study’s aim, the scope was broad. Yet, since we included documents from the grey literature, we may have excluded some guidance, especially those not published in English. Our analysis focused on the content of the guidance documents relevant to CPG development. We did not attempt to disentangle the origin of specific methodological standards, but rather to synthesise what the documents collectively present to guideline developers.

Additionally, we did not employ traditional measures to evaluate the quality of guidance documents, which could limit conclusions regarding the value of individual guidance. However, the scoping review aimed to map and describe guidance on including economic evidence in CPGs, providing a compiled list of resources. Therefore, we aimed to be as inclusive and comprehensive as possible and did not intend to assess the merits of guidance documents in terms of rigour.

Conclusion

Many systems have begun linking economic evidence with the development of CPGs. However, available guidance differs in scope and detail. Future research should focus on standardising approaches, clarifying triggers for economic analysis, and building capacity among guideline developers. We anticipate that improving these processes will result in more consistent integration of economic evidence in CPG development, particularly in LMIC settings.
