Harm reduction approaches for the use of benzodiazepines: a scoping review
Authors: Caity Morrison, Isabella Natale, Antigone Branchflower, Craig Harvey, Robert M. Lundin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBenzodiazepines are widely prescribed but are associated with significant risks, particularly when used long-term. The anxiolytic and hypnotic properties of these medications increase their risk of dependence, which can lead to nonmedical and illicit use. Illicit use further compounds these harms, particularly with the emergence of potent novel benzodiazepines on the unregulated market. While tapering remains the standard treatment, not all individuals seek discontinuation. In such cases, harm reduction becomes a key approach to minimise associated risks. This review aimed to identify and synthesise existing harm reduction approaches for people using benzodiazepines.MethodA systematic search was conducted across four databases, PsycINFO (n = 183), MEDLINE (n = 345), Web of Science (n = 382), and Embase (n = 940), following the PRISMA guidelines. Searches were carried out between February 14 and March 30, 2024, using terms related to harm reduction and benzodiazepines. The search was re-run on July 7, 2025, using the same strategy across all four databases.ResultsThirty-five studies were included and grouped into the following themes: direct interventions (n = 16), policy approaches (n = 9), and population-specific approaches (n = 10). Among direct interventions, drug checking was the most frequently reported approach, with advanced techniques improving the detection of novel benzodiazepines and prompting safer use practices. Benzodiazepine agonist prescribing during the COVID-19 pandemic has yielded positive outcomes; conversely, policy responses such as rescheduling and prescribing changes indicated mixed results. While some studies reported reduced use and improved treatment engagement, others highlighted unintended consequences that may displace or exacerbate harm. Specific populations, such as young people, those who inject benzodiazepines, and members of online communities, highlight the diverse demographics of people who use benzodiazepines and emphasise the importance of developing tailored responses to address unique needs.ConclusionDrug checking emerged as the most widely reported harm reduction approach for benzodiazepine use, with consistent positive outcomes across studies. Prescribing and policy interventions demonstrated variable impacts, often influenced by broader systemic factors. Critically, a clear gap remains in harm reduction approaches for those not seeking treatment, highlighting a need for inclusive, flexible and pragmatic responses. There is also a need for more robust evaluation of harm reduction interventions to strengthen the evidence base and inform practice.

Introduction

Benzodiazepines are a commonly prescribed class of depressant medications [1] and are considered essential medicines by the World Health Organisation [2]. Primarily prescribed for anxiety, panic and sleep disorders, and substance withdrawal; these medications hold anxiolytic, hypnotic, muscle relaxant, and anticonvulsant properties [3]. Nonmedical use of benzodiazepines, defined as use that deviates from prescribed dosing or use without a prescription via medication diversion or other illicit sourcing [4], is driven by various motivations, including relaxation, assistance with sleep, pain management and the modulation of other drug effects [5–7].

Recent prevalence data from the 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health in the United States (N≈283,458 participants aged ≥ 12 years) estimate that approximately 1.3% of the US population engaged in nonmedical use of benzodiazepines in the past year [8]. Similarly, in Australia, the 2022–2023 National Drug Strategy Household survey (N = 21,500) estimated 1.6% of people aged over 14 years had used tranquiliser/sleeping pills (including benzodiazepines) nonmedically in the past year [9]. While prescribed benzodiazepine rates in Australia have mostly declined between 2012 and 2022, data from the 2020–2021 Australian Illicit Drug Data Report indicate that the detection of prescription pharmaceuticals at the Australian border has increased by 6% since 2011–2012, with benzodiazepines accounting for 64% (n = 902) of the 1,337 prescription pharmaceutical border detections [10]. In Europe, the 2017 Nonmedical Use of Prescription Drugs (NMURx) survey (representing approximately 260 million European adults) reported varying past-year nonmedical benzodiazepine use across Spain (3.9%), Italy (2.4%), France (1.7%), Germany (1.0%), and the United Kingdom (0.9%) [11].

Benzodiazepines are associated with significant health risks and adverse effects in both prescribed and nonmedical use contexts. Clinical guidelines consistently recommend time-limited use at the lowest effective dose [12], however, evidence suggests that up to one-third of prescribed benzodiazepine use may continue long-term [13]. Prolonged use can lead to various harms, including dependence, cognitive decline, and potentially life-threatening withdrawal [14]. Among people who inject drugs, concurrent benzodiazepine use is associated with increased dependence severity, syringe sharing, sexual risk behaviours, and nonfatal overdose [15]. Fast-onset, short-acting benzodiazepines such as midazolam and flunitrazepam are particularly associated with anterograde amnesia, especially when combined with alcohol or other substances, potentially increasing vulnerability to criminal victimisation and, to a lesser extent, criminal activity [15]. The most severe risk is fatal overdose, particularly when combined with other central nervous system depressants, such as opioids [16].

In Australia, the rate of drug-induced deaths associated with benzodiazepines increased from 1.9 per 100,000 people in 1997 to 2.7 deaths per 100,000 people in 2022, representing a 42.1% increase [17]. During the same period in the UK, deaths involving benzodiazepines increased from 9.7 deaths per million in 1997 to 17.8 deaths in 2022, representing an 83.5% increase [18]. In a similar period in the United States, there were 1145 deaths associated with benzodiazepines in 1999, compared with 10,964 deaths in 2022, representing an eightfold increase in this period [19].

A complicating factor in benzodiazepine consumption is the proliferation of counterfeit products on the illicit drug market. These counterfeits, often manufactured to mimic pharmaceutical branded products (e.g., 'Xanax'), can be difficult for consumers to distinguish from legitimate pharmaceuticals [20], creating additional challenges for harm reduction efforts. Analyses of seized counterfeit tablets have been found to contain varying quantities of benzodiazepines, novel benzodiazepines, and other substances, including fentanyl and other synthetic opioids [21], presenting significant overdose risk.

“Novel benzodiazepines” is a term that encompasses a range of compounds, including newly synthesised substances structurally similar to existing benzodiazepines, as well as older compounds that were never approved, discontinued, or withdrawn from the pharmaceutical market, often due to concerns such as high potency, toxicity, or misuse potential. Often marketed as 'designer benzodiazepines' or 'research chemicals' [22], these substances typically produce effects similar to, or stronger than, more well-known benzodiazepines, and are often developed or re-emerge in attempts to circumvent legal controls [21, 23]. The presence of these drugs has increased significantly since they first appeared on online drug markets in 2007, with 36 novel benzodiazepines reported to the EU early warning system between 2007 and 2023 [24]. These substances pose additional risks due to their largely unknown safety profiles and potency and are frequently detected in counterfeit pills [22].

Analyses indicate that novel psychoactive substances (NPS), including benzodiazepines, are widely available for purchase online [5, 25]. Drug cryptomarkets, operating on the darknet, are an overlay of the internet accessible only via specialised software and facilitate the anonymous sale of illicit drugs through online marketplaces [26]. Since the 2011 launch of the Silk Road, numerous such platforms have emerged, and despite significant law enforcement efforts, cryptomarkets continue to play a persistent role in the global drug trade [25, 26]. A recent analysis of 14 drug cryptomarkets (1st February 2024 to 31st January 2025) revealed that benzodiazepines comprised the third-largest percentage (8.1%) of all drug listings [27]. The growing presence and easy availability of novel benzodiazepines only add complexity to efforts aimed at reducing benzodiazepine-related harm.

Harm reduction is grounded in principles of justice and human rights. It includes policies, programs, and practices designed to minimise the negative health, social, and legal consequences associated with drug use, as well as the impact of drug laws and policies [28]. At its core, harm reduction promotes positive change by working collaboratively with people who use drugs without judgment, coercion, discrimination, or the requirement of abstinence in order to receive support [28]. Although there is a broad understanding and agreement on what harm reduction means, no single definition is universally adopted [29]. Harm reduction approaches have been successfully applied across numerous substance use contexts, including sterile injecting equipment provision [30], safer inhalation pipe provision for crack cocaine [31] and methamphetamine [32], opioid agonist treatment [33], nicotine replacement therapy [34], and naloxone provision for opioid overdose response [35]. Harm reduction initiatives for illicit drug use are well evidenced and offer cost-effective, pragmatic solutions [36].

Current treatment approaches for benzodiazepine dependence primarily involve deprescribing through initial stabilisation and/or conversion to prescribed benzodiazepine followed by stepwise dose reduction with the goal of eventual abstinence [14, 37]. A reduction plan often includes replacing short-acting, more potent benzodiazepines (e.g., alprazolam) with longer-acting options (e.g., diazepam) guided by standardised dose equivalence protocols [37]. However, traditional dose equivalence tools prove challenging for novel and counterfeit benzodiazepines owing to their largely unknown composition and relative potencies, complicating treatment planning. In parallel, regulatory responses, such as medication rescheduling, have been used to address broader issues related to benzodiazepine harm and dependence [38].

Despite the success of harm reduction in the context of other substances, few benzodiazepine-specific approaches are available for harm reduction services. Similarly, drug and alcohol treatment services tend to focus on cessation and deprescription, which are options that may not address the needs of those not seeking to stop using benzodiazepines in the short term. Previous benzodiazepine reviews have focused on epidemiology [39], benzodiazepine prescribing outcomes for opioid agonist treatment patients [40], and deprescribing of benzodiazepines [41, 42], highlighting the need for an evaluation of harm reduction in the context of benzodiazepines. This scoping review aims to identify literature referencing harm reduction approaches related to benzodiazepine use. A secondary objective is to thematically categorise the included studies and provide a narrative synthesis summary of current approaches in this area.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across four databases: PsycINFO (EBSCO, n = 183), MEDLINE (PubMed, n = 345), Web of Science (Clarivate, n = 382), and Embase (EMBASE, n = 940), in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. The search commenced on 14 February 2024 and concluded on 30 March 2024. To ensure currency of results, the search was rerun on 7 July 2025 using the same databases and search strategy; PsycINFO (n = 230), Web of Science (n = 482), PubMed (n = 444), and Embase (n = 1195). Search rerun duplicates were removed in two stages (n = 218 + 127), leaving 249 unique records. These were screened by title and abstract, with 17 selected for full-text review. Of these, 7 met the inclusion criteria and were added to the final synthesis.

The search strategy used combinations of terms related to “harm reduction” (including overdose prevention, prescription misuse, and diversion) and benzodiazepines, including both generic and novel substances: diazepam, alprazolam, temazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam, oxazepam, clobromazolam, bromazolam, etizolam, flualprazolam, clonazolam, estazolam, nordiazepam, and “novel benzodiazepine.” This list was collaboratively developed based on substances commonly prescribed, misused, or implicated in overdose at the time of review. No publication date limits were applied to ensure capture of both historical and contemporary harm reduction strategies, allowing for the identification of interventions that may reflect the evolution of current practices. A breakdown of search terms and results is available in Appendix 1. The PRISMA flowchart was generated using the PRISMA 2020 tool [43]. Reference lists of included full-text papers were also screened for additional relevant studies. No filters or limits were applied to retrieved records during screening.

Study selection criteria

The full-text selection procedure is summarised in Fig. 1. Duplicate records were identified and removed using Zotero Reference Manager (v6.0.37). Titles, abstracts, and keywords were screened independently in duplicate by four reviewers (IN, CM, CH, RML) using Rayyan (Rayyan 2024) with blinded voting. An initial screening of 40 studies was conducted independently by all reviewers to pilot the inclusion criteria. The team then met to discuss the results, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency in the application of eligibility criteria. This calibration process was completed before continuing with the full screening of all identified records. Consequently, the review was reframed as a scoping review to better align with the goal of mapping existing literature and identifying knowledge gaps, rather than synthesising intervention effectiveness.

Results

The identification and screening process is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart below (Fig. 1). The search process identified 2351 studies in which 1048 duplicates were removed. A total of 1303 abstracts were screened for relevance, with 88 papers selected for full-text review. The review led to the exclusion of 53 papers that either did not include harm reduction strategies (n = 39) or were not relevant to benzodiazepines (n = 14), leaving 35 papers for study inclusion.

The included papers were thematically grouped by the type of harm reduction approach or target population. The main identified themes were direct interventions (n = 16), policy (n = 9), and population-specific approaches (n = 10). Where relevant, additional subthemes have been described.

The studies involved the following locations: Canada (n = 8), the United States (n = 8), the United Kingdom (n = 4), Thailand (n = 2), Switzerland (n = 2), Australia (n = 4), Sweden (n = 1), Ireland (n = 1), Wales (n = 1), multiple locations (n = 2) and global/online studies (n = 3). Methodologically, the studies were predominantly quantitative (n = 20), with the remaining being qualitative (n = 1), reviews (n = 4), mixed methods (n = 2), and a policy analysis (n = 1). A summary of the extracted data is presented below. The full table of results is provided in Appendix 1.

Direct interventions

Drug checking

Drug checking emerged as the most widely used harm reduction strategy, with nine studies employing various methods for drug sample analysis. These methods range from laboratory analyses to portable point-of-care methods. Techniques include paper spray mass spectrometry (PS-MS) [44–46], gas chromatography‒mass spectrometry (GC‒MS) [45, 47, 48], liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (LC‒MS) [47], miniature paper capillary spray ionisation tandem mass spectrometry (PCSI‒MS/MS) [46], nuclear magnetic resonance [46], Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) [46, 48, 49], handheld Raman spectroscopy [50], fentanyl test strips (FTS) [48, 51, 52], and benzodiazepine immunoassay strips [48].

Mass spectrometry-based methods were found to be effective in detecting low concentrations of substances such as etizolam and carfentanil. The PCSI-MS/MS system successfully identified etizolam in 11 out of 15 samples where both benzodiazepine test strips and FTIR methods failed to detect its presence [46]. Similarly, portable GC‒MS outperformed infrared spectroscopy, identifying 62% of samples containing carfentanil and 36% of samples containing etizolam, with even higher success rates (78%) for samples containing etizolam concentrations above 3% by weight [45]. In contrast, infrared spectroscopy could only detect etizolam in 9% of the etizolam-containing samples [45]. Most studies have identified novel benzodiazepines, including etizolam, flualprazolam, and flubromazolam, present in samples that are expected to be other drugs [44, 45, 47–50]. Etizolam is the most commonly detected novel benzodiazepine across multiple sites [44–47], with point-of-care techniques such as PS-MS being able to detect very low levels of etizolam that other methods such as FTIR miss [44, 46]. A recent laboratory analysis of internet-purchased samples of etizolam, flubromazepam, and pyrazolam highlighted significant variability in drug content both within and across batches. Some etizolam tablets contained up to 20 times the recommended clinical dose (1–4 mg), and none contained etizolam as a lone compound [53]. Numerous studies found novel benzodiazepines were most often detected in samples expected to be opioids [44, 47–49]. The presence of fentanyl analogues and unexpected benzodiazepines in the same samples presented serious overdose risk concerns [47, 49]. In one study, benzodiazepine-type samples accounted for 41% of all samples associated with a negative outcome during the study period, such as adverse health events, extreme sedation, or overdose [47].

At times, participants opted for sample destruction after the test results differed from those of the expected substances [44]. They reported adopting other harm reduction practices, such as using smaller doses, avoiding solitary use, and carrying naloxone [44, 51]. Most participants who used FTS incorporated it into their daily routines because of their convenience [52]. A separate study revealed that, after using FTS, participants reduced their injection frequency, avoided solitary drug use, and decreased benzodiazepine use [51]. One study found that positive FTS results were linked to safer drug use behaviours over a 28-day period, including using smaller amounts, and having naloxone on hand. Particularly when FTS were used on benzodiazepines, which resulted in a 20-fold reduction in use after a positive FTS result [54].

Agonist treatment

Five studies examined the use of agonist treatment. Three studies focused on a "safer supply" of substances during the COVID-19 pandemic to support individuals in isolation [32, 55, 56]. The first study involved prescribing long-acting benzodiazepines at 50% of the patient’s usual dose for six residents with high-dose dependence (8% of the sample) [55]. The second study outlined the provision of 'up to 30 mg daily' to prevent benzodiazepine withdrawal for more than 70 residents; the specific type of benzodiazepine was not specified [56]. Both studies reported positive outcomes, with the first study finding low rates of adverse events (intoxication, diversion), no overdoses, and successful isolation completions. The second study revealed improvements in residents’ general health, behaviour, and compliance with public health measures, as reported by staff [56]. The third study explored harm reduction service provision across 34 cities during the pandemic, and highlighted the introduction and continuation of a prescribed benzodiazepine stabilisation protocol in Dublin [32].

Two qualitative studies explored individuals’ perceptions of benzodiazepine use and treatment [57, 58]. The first study found participants preferred prescribed benzodiazepines over illicit use, citing benefits such as reduced costs, criminal activity, and the risk of contaminated pills [57]. Notably, some participants in this study reported treatment termination from their prescriber if nonmedical benzodiazepine use was suspected or disclosed. The second study found most participants saw the benefits of agonist treatment, such as reduced criminal behaviour and greater stability [58]. However, a subset of participants, particularly those with a history of hospitalisations and withdrawal attempts, felt that discontinuation was preferable to a maintenance approach, fearing that continued dependence would hinder their treatment decisions.

Policy

Rescheduling

Three studies examined the impacts of the rescheduling of medications, including benzodiazepines [59–61]. A 2021 review included five studies that explored the rescheduling of alprazolam in Australia from Schedule 4 to Schedule 8 in 2014 [59]. Four of the studies reported declines in alprazolam use, with reductions in prescribing, dispensing, overdose deaths, and calls to poison centres. However, the extent of substitution varied. Further restrictions in 2017, such as removing 2 mg pills from public subsidies and limiting pack sizes, had no substantial impact.

Two qualitative studies explored the rescheduling of temazepam gel capsules. One involved 100 people with long-term polydrug use in the UK, [61] where participants reported reduced injection of gel capsules but continued intravenous use with temazepam tablet formulations. The participants also reported increased criminal activity, higher drug prices, and substitution with riskier drugs such as flunitrazepam. Changes in the illicit drug market, such as the appearance of unfamiliar tablets and increased suspicion, desperation, and aggression, have been reported [61].

Another qualitative study centred on street-based drug use in Melbourne focused on the drug marketplace after the cessation of the temazepam gel capsule formulation in Australia in 2004 [60]. The study referenced individual harm reduction strategies used, such as waiting until the end of the day when at one's home to use temazepam to reduce the risk of losing money, losing drugs, or being arrested due to reduced vigilance. This study emphasised the need for harm reduction approaches to consider the pleasures people obtain from their drug use when responding to high-risk practices. The author suggested that reconsideration of the social and cultural values associated with harm reduction is warranted [60].

Prescribing

Three studies examined approaches to benzodiazepine prescription [62–64]. One study evaluated a ‘no benzodiazepine’ policy that was implemented across six outpatient mental health clinics in 2016 in the United States [62]. To reduce overdose deaths, the clinics removed benzodiazepines from the approved medication list and provided patients with individualised treatment plans, including psychoeducation, psychotherapy, slow taper, and inpatient detoxification. The study reported an 89% reduction in benzodiazepine prescriptions over two years, with no decrease in patient caseloads [62].

A retrospective analysis found 127 opioid substitution treatment (OST) patients receiving concurrent benzodiazepine agonist treatment had significantly longer treatment retention (72 months) than did those without (34 months) or with brief (51 months) benzodiazepine agonist treatment [65] In-treatment mortality was lower for patients receiving benzodiazepine agonist treatment (1.31/100 patient-years) than for those without (1.79/100). However, posttreatment mortality for patients receiving benzodiazepine agonist treatment increased by 450%, whereas it increased by 191% for those not receiving agonist treatment and by 125% for those receiving only brief benzodiazepine agonist treatment.

A 2018 review examined the risks, benefits, and alternatives for patients prescribed benzodiazepines when they were transitioning to a new prescriber, emphasising the challenges of following guidelines that recommend short-term use [64]. The review suggests that evidence of harmful side effects, nonmedical use, or diversion should lead to the safe discontinuation of benzodiazepines, whereas cases without clear harm can be managed individually, by monitoring, contracts, and tapering plans when patients refuse to stop despite being informed of the risks [64].

Toxicosurveillance

Three linked studies from Victoria, Australia, form a comprehensive toxicosurveillance project monitoring illicit drug signals among patients with drug-related toxicity [66–68]. The first study detailed the Emerging Drugs Network of Australia—Victoria (EDNAV) [68], a coordinated toxicosurveillance system integrating clinical and toxicological data across emergency departments, laboratories, and public health agencies. Using a risk-based framework, EDNAV facilitates timely detection of concerning drug signals and supports rapid harm reduction responses, including dissemination of alerts to the community. The second study, as part of the EDNAV project, examined 1112 emergency department presentations (2020–2022), identifying 183 cases (16.5%) involving novel benzodiazepines, predominantly in young males (median age 24) [66]. Twelve different novel benzodiazepines were detected, with clonazolam and etizolam most common. Nearly half of the cases involved multiple novel benzodiazepines, often combined with psychostimulants like methylamphetamine. Prescription benzodiazepines and psychotropic medications were also frequently co-detected, often without patient disclosure. High-risk cases led to targeted public health alerts [66]. The third study from EDNAV focused on drug toxicity at music festivals [67], reviewing 1603 medical presentations, of which 228 involved illicit drug toxicity. Blood samples from 24 severely affected patrons (median age 22) revealed polysubstance use, with toxicology confirming a median of four drugs per individual. MDMA, ketamine, and cocaine were most common, and nearly half of the samples contained NPS despite none reporting intentional use.

Population-specific approaches

Online drug forums

Three studies explored information exchange in online forums [69–71]. One study reported that users discussed optimal dosing for desired effects and safety, drug formulation alterations, routes of administration, and effect durations [69]. Although harm reduction information was shared, forum users also displayed a sense of superiority regarding their drug knowledge, which the authors suggested may promote risky practices. Two studies focused on reviews of drug vendors and strategies to reduce the harms of polysubstance use [70, 71]. The first study highlighted advocacy for home drug testing [70], whereas the second identified discussions on low dosing, controlling drug use frequency, and safer drug use at home [71].

People who inject benzodiazepines

Two studies examined midazolam injection in Thailand [72, 73]. The first study in Bangkok identified a need for four key harm reduction responses: the providing established groin injection-related advice, early care for soft-tissue infections, access to sterile supplies, and education on the risks of injecting alone [73]. The second study found that midazolam injection was linked to various complications and recommended improving methadone maintenance treatment to reduce compensatory use of midazolam, as well as implementing peer-based harm reduction programs [72].

Young people

Two studies included young people who used benzodiazepines [74, 75]. One study in New York City focused on young people (aged 18–30) engaged with harm reduction services [74]. Young participants differed from older individuals in terms of sociodemographics, health and social risk factors, and drug use patterns. This study identified specific drugs, including heroin, novel benzodiazepines, and nonprescribed methadone, are linked to increased overdose risk in youth. The key risk factors for overdose include homelessness, drug detoxification, and psychological distress. A later study reviewed harm reduction strategies for adolescents in public health settings [75]. The authors provided recommendations for healthcare practitioners, including educating adolescents and caregivers about counterfeit pills, securing medication storage, and distributing naloxone and FTS.

Women who use drugs

One study examined the gendered impacts of drug supply contamination in British Colombia during the COVID-19 pandemic. In response to increased overdose risk and gendered violence, with particularly attention paid to the risk of sexual assault in the context of benzodiazepine use, women developed informal peer networks to support and check on each other during this time [76].

Novel benzodiazepines

A rapid review focused on etizolam [77] suggests established harm reduction advice, such as avoiding the combination of benzodiazepines with other sedatives, remains relevant and increasingly important due to the growing prevalence of nonmedical benzodiazepine use.

Poison centre callers

One study evaluated the use of social workers in a telephone-based poison centre, where they assisted with tablet identification and overdose information [78]. During the 20-month study, social workers answered 17,616 tablet identification calls. Overdose prevention and drug safety information was provided to 37.7% of callers. The service reached 6635 individuals potentially at risk from nonmedical use of prescription medications. The intervention was found to be practical, feasible, and quickly implementable.

Discussion

This review identified existing harm reduction approaches for benzodiazepine use, grouping the included studies into three main categories: direct interventions (drug checking [n = 11], agonist treatment [n = 5]), policy (rescheduling [n = 3], prescribing [n = 3]), toxicosurveillance [n = 3]), and population-specific approaches (online drug forums [n = 3], people who inject benzodiazepines [n = 2], young people [n = 2], women who use drugs [n = 1], novel benzodiazepines [n = 1], and poison centre callers [n = 1]). Drug checking emerged as the most prevalent harm reduction approach, with the highest number of studies.

Growing evidence for drug checking

Drug checking has emerged as a critical and adaptive harm reduction response to the growing challenges posed by novel benzodiazepines. The shift of drug use discussions and transactions to online spaces [66–69] highlights how use of unknown compounds when alone at home can render some traditional harm reduction advice insufficient. In this context, drug checking offers essential information about the substances people intend to use, further informing their decision-making and use of harm reduction strategies [44].

Initially, established in Europe, drug checking has expanded across North America in response to the opioid crisis and fentanyl contamination in the drug supply [79]. A common finding across the included drug checking studies was the detection of unexpected substances. Etizolam was most frequently identified, and in some instances was co-detected with fentanyl, posing significant overdose risk [44]. Pills marketed as pharmaceutical benzodiazepines, such as Valium and Xanax, were also found to lack the expected active ingredients and/or contain unexpected substances [48, 50], further highlighting the value of drug checking services.

These findings demonstrate the necessity of tailored harm reduction advice, such as cautioning that naloxone may be ineffective for benzodiazepine overdose and exploring other strategies such as avoiding using alone, using a test dose, and keeping naloxone on hand [44]. Behavioural changes were evident, consistent with research on the impact of drug checking results on behaviour modification [79]. Behavioural modifications following drug checking intervention included disposal of adulterated samples, reduced benzodiazepine use, reduced injection frequency, and reduced solitary drug use [44, 51, 54].

Technological and analytical limitations remain a challenge, especially when detecting novel benzodiazepines at low concentrations. The trade-off between cost, speed, and accuracy proves difficult to balance. Low-cost tools such as FTS (~ $1 USD) provide rapid, albeit less specific, results [51], whereas more sophisticated methods such as PS-MS offer higher sensitivity at greater expense and complexity [44]. This indicates an ongoing need for innovation and investment to improve drug checking technologies that can meet accuracy and speed requirements when detecting emerging substances.

Unclear evidence for agonist treatment

The evidence on the effectiveness of agonist treatment for benzodiazepine dependence remains limited and methodologically diverse. The five included studies suggest some potential benefits, although the evidence is not as robust as that for opioid agonist treatments [33] or as extensively explored as potential methamphetamine agonist treatment options [80]. Notably, benzodiazepine agonist treatment during the COVID-19 pandemic has shown positive outcomes, such as successful isolation completion and improved health outcomes, with minimal adverse events [56]. These results reflect the broader trend of flexible harm reduction approaches during the pandemic [81].

Qualitative study participants generally preferred prescribed benzodiazepines over illicit sources due to reduced criminal activity, lower costs, and decreased exposure to contaminated substances [57, 58]. Some participants preferred a discontinuation approach to treatment [58], emphasising the importance of choice and individualised treatment.

Latent impacts of rescheduling

Several studies have examined the rescheduling of benzodiazepines, particularly alprazolam, and the gel-capsule formulation of temazepam [59–61]. Unintended consequences related to the restrictions of temazepam gel capsules emerged, including increased criminal activity, increased hostility and desperation in the drug market, and substitution with potentially riskier substances [61]. The rescheduling of alprazolam in Australia in 2014 followed coroner’s recommendations due to alprazolam involvement in overdose deaths [82]. The rescheduling led to a reduction in alprazolam use, prescriptions, and related poison centre calls [83]. However, street prices for alprazolam rose [83, 84], and while alprazolam-related deaths decreased by 60% (57 deaths in 2012 to 23 deaths in 2015), overall benzodiazepine overdose deaths rose by 49% in a similar period (160 deaths in 2009 to 238 deaths in 2015) [82]. This suggests that while the policy reduced some of the harm associated with alprazolam, it had a lesser impact on broader benzodiazepine-related mortality [82].

Limitations in current prescribing practices

Approaches to care for people prescribed benzodiazepines vary significantly, reflecting differences in how harm reduction is interpreted and applied. Some policies emphasise discontinuation, recommending rapid cessation in cases of misuse, which the authors defined as behaviours such as doctor shopping, early refills, or reports of lost prescriptions, even if this goes against the patient’s wishes [64]. A "no benzodiazepine" policy implemented in outpatient mental health settings incorporated individualised treatment plans but enforced cessation as a non-negotiable outcome [62]. While some approaches are framed as harm reduction, they may differ from core harm reduction principles commonly described in the literature, such as supporting patient autonomy, incremental progress, and continuity of care without punitive measures [85]. This highlights the variability in the operational definition of harm reduction and its application in clinical policies. Some policies may adopt the term “harm reduction” without fully embodying its foundational principles, reflecting a broader lack of consensus. Inclusion of an approach with this framing of harm reduction points to the range of perspectives and practices in the field. These discussions are valuable for fostering critical reflection and advancing understanding among clinicians and researchers, especially considering the often limited formal training in harm reduction within medical education [86].

A clear need for population-specific approaches

Benzodiazepine use is characterised by significant heterogeneity in populations who use them, patterns of use, types of risk, and settings for use. These range from street-based settings, communities facing unprecedented overdose crises, to health services, poison centres, and online marketplaces or forums. Harms associated with use span injecting-related infections [73], accidental injury, sexual assault, and gendered violence [76]. Anonymous online platforms facilitate access to supply, while simultaneously providing both harm reduction information and potential misinformation [69]. This diversity in contexts, risks, and user experiences underscores the need for population-specific, adaptive strategies to effectively reduce benzodiazepine-related harms.

Recommendations

Review findings suggest a need for flexible and individualised responses to benzodiazepine use, including case-by-case planning and risk–benefit analyses [58, 64, 74]. When working alongside people who use benzodiazepines, tailored responses should consider factors such as age, gender, route of administration, and concurrent substance use [72, 74, 75]. Providing a safer supply of prescribed benzodiazepines where appropriate may serve as a protective measure against the harms associated with illicit benzodiazepine use [49, 58, 61, 77]. Treatment-specific considerations include combining gradual dose reduction with therapy rather than dose reduction alone, and improving OST access and quality to reduce the compensation of low methadone dosing with benzodiazepines [72]. Peer-based harm reduction programs, informal peer networks, low threshold care and safer injecting information are also recommended [72, 76]. For younger populations, education on counterfeit pills and the provision of naloxone and FTS remain valid [75]. Established harm reduction strategies, such as using smaller test doses and not using alone, also remain applicable [49]. However, there is a clear need for tailored harm reduction strategies on the basis of drug checking results [47], anonymous access to drug checking [48], and increased awareness of novel benzodiazepines as adulterants [49]. Communication technologies, such as SMS, can further improve engagement and rapidly disseminate drug-related alerts [50, 78].

Limitations

This review notes limitations both in methodology and in the evidence found. The search strategy’s focus on the term ‘harm reduction’ may have excluded studies that employed approaches aimed at reducing harm without explicitly naming this term. Furthermore, the wide variety of terms used to describe novel benzodiazepines made it challenging to create an exhaustive search strategy. Both limitations may have led to the omission of relevant studies. Owing to the heterogeneity of the studies included, the evidence provided was of varying quality, and due to the nature of the studies, both risk of bias and meta-analyses were not conducted, and the intended review methodology was changed from systematic to scoping. The inclusion of any studies that used the term ‘harm reduction’ has led to the inclusion of some approaches, such as abstinence-focused treatment, that may not align with most conceptualisations of harm reduction. Finally, as this review focused on the academic literature available, it omits benzodiazepine-related harm reduction approaches by individual organisations, particularly peer-led organisations, such as peer-to-peer advice and related resources.

Conclusions

This review highlights drug checking as the most prevalent and pragmatic harm reduction strategy currently available for benzodiazepine use, particularly in the context of continuously emerging novel substances. Conversely, interventions related to prescribing and policy have shown mixed effects, often shaped by broader structural and systemic factors. There is a clear need for population-specific approaches, particularly for groups disproportionately affected by benzodiazepine-related harm. Critically, there is a notable absence of harm reduction interventions for individuals who are not seeking abstinence or formal treatment. This gap reflects a broader misalignment between existing service models and the realities of benzodiazepine use, particularly when it intersects with other structural vulnerabilities such as homelessness, stigma, and criminalisation. Future work should prioritise the development and evaluation of nonjudgmental, flexible interventions that meet people where they are. This includes expanding drug checking accessibility, exploring safer supply models, and codesigning services with people who use benzodiazepines. As the unregulated drug supply continues to evolve, so too must our harm reduction response.
