Dietary plant extracts reduce methane emission and modulate rumen microbial functionality in Merino lambs
Authors: Abiodun Mayowa Akanmu, Abubeker Hassen, Este van Marle-Köster, Festus Adeyemi Adejoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The formation of enteric methane from ruminants represents a significant loss of dietary energy that adversely affects growth and production while also contributing to the environmental footprint of livestock production through greenhouse gas accumulation. Phytogenic feed additives rich in bioactive compounds have been proposed as sustainable alternatives to synthetic additives for improving nutrient utilisation and reducing methane. This study evaluated the effects of Moringa oleifera, Jatropha curcas, and Aloe vera extracts on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, methane production, rumen fermentation in South African Mutton Merino lambs using an in vivo feeding trial while the microbial diversity and functionality was evaluated using shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Supplementation with Moringa and Jatropha improved dry matter and crude protein digestibility (P < 0.05). Methane emission decreased in all plant extract groups, with reductions of 17% (Jatropha), 9% (Moringa), and 12% (Aloe) relative to control (P < 0.05). Ammonia nitrogen concentrations were lower in supplemented groups, particularly Moringa and Aloe (P < 0.01), while volatile fatty acids and growth performance were unaffected. Metagenomic profiling revealed Bacteroidetes as the dominant phylum and showed enrichment of genes which may be associated with protein biosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism in Moringa and Jatropha lambs, aligning with improved digestibility and reduced methane emissions. Dietary inclusion of M. oleifera, J. curcas, and A. vera extracts reduced methane emissions and improved dry matter and crude protein digestibility without compromising growth. These results suggest that these phytogenic extracts can serve as sustainable feed additives to improve efficiency and mitigate environmental impacts in ruminant production systems.

Introduction

Ruminant livestock continue to face considerable scrutiny due to their contribution to anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, primarily enteric methane (CH₄), as well as methane (CH₄) and nitrous oxide (N₂O) emissions arising from manure management1,2. The environmental footprint associated with the global production of meat, milk, and hides has intensified advocacy for reductions in livestock numbers or decreased consumption of animal-derived products1. However, in the context of a growing human population and increasing demand for animal-sourced foods, a more pragmatic approach involves reducing emission intensity per unit of meat or milk produced. This can be achieved through strategies such as modulation of rumen fermentation, development of climate-smart genetics, and dietary manipulation, among others3. Beyond their contribution to global warming, methane emissions from the rumen also represent a loss of dietary energy to the host animal that could otherwise be partitioned toward productive functions4.

Central to efficient feed fermentation and methane (CH₄) production is the rumen microbial community, a complex ecosystem comprising bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and methanogenic archaea that degrade dietary carbohydrates into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), gases, and microbial protein; shifts in microbial composition and functional potential directly influence rumen fermentation pathways and fermentation end products5,6.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of medicinal plant supplementation, both in vitro and in vivo, in reducing enteric CH₄ emissions and improving feed utilisation in ruminants7–9. The biological activities of these medicinal plants are primarily attributed to their phytochemical constituents10, which function as defence mechanisms against invaders, grazers, and parasites. These effects are analogous to those of antibiotic growth promoters, which modulate rumen fermentation and subsequently enhance production efficiency11.

Therefore, strategies that target both the taxonomic composition and metabolic capacity of rumen microorganisms may confer dual benefits by mitigating wasteful gaseous emissions while simultaneously enhancing animal productivity6. Phytochemicals derived from Moringa oleifera (MO), Jatropha curcas (JA), and Aloe vera (AV) have been shown to exhibit strong antimicrobial activity against several pathogenic bacterial species that impair animal growth and performance12–15. Previous studies have reported that crude extracts of MO, JA, and AV reduce in vitro CH₄ production and improve in vitro organic matter digestibility at inclusion levels ranging from 25 to 50 mg/kg of feed7. Similarly, pre-treatment of feed substrates with Aloe vera extract resulted in significantly lower CH₄ emissions in vitro16.

Despite these promising findings, relatively few studies have validated these effects in vivo. In particular, there is a paucity of in vivo studies evaluating methanolic extracts of MO, JA, and AV as rumen modulators and their effects on rumen microbial diversity and functionality. Furthermore, a comprehensive characterisation of the rumen microbial ecosystem using shotgun metagenomic sequencing to concurrently assess taxonomic and functional shifts has not been reported for these medicinal plants in ruminant species. The hypothesis of the study is that dietary supplementation with the extract of MO, JA and AV will reduce enteric methane in vivo without affecting nutrient intake or growth. A further hypothesis is that the extract will result in a modification of the microbial diversity and functionality in the rumen of sheep. The outcome of these investigations will contribute to the development of sustainable strategies for reducing the environmental footprint of ruminant production systems. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of methanolic extracts of MO, JA, and AV on feed digestibility, growth performance, rumen microbial community structure and functional potential, and enteric CH₄ emissions in Merino lambs fed a total mixed ration (TMR).

Methodology

The study took place at the Experimental farm, Innovation Africa (IA) campus, University of Pretoria, South Africa, during the summer with average temperatures ranging from 18 °C to 39 °C. The study was carried out in compliance with the recommendations of the South African National Health Research Ethics Council and in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. The experimental procedure was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Pretoria, South Africa, with references number ECO-03014.

Collection, preparation, phytochemical analysis, and administration of plant extracts

The leaves of MO, JA and AV were harvested from the botanical gardens of the University of Ibadan, Nigeria. Samples were collected from 10 different plants of the same species and the harvested plant materials were identified and authenticated in the herbarium of the Department of Botany, University of Ibadan, Nigeria. Subsequently, the fresh foliage was imported to South Africa under refrigeration, facilitated by an importation permit issued by the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development, Pretoria, South Africa. The plant materials were stored under cold storage and subsequently subjected to phytochemical extraction in 100% methanol under gentle stirring for 96 h at 20℃, filtered through 150 μm aperture. The excess methanol was removed under a vacuum using a Rotorvapor Hie-VAP (Hiedolph, Germany) at 35℃ and the extract was subsequently freeze dried. The detailed extraction and reconstitution procedure were as documented previously15. Freeze dried crude extract was reconstituted into water at the point of drenching the animals. The actual dosage administered to each lamb was adjusted weekly based on the animals’ feed consumption in the preceding week and equivalent to extract concentration of 50 mg/kg feed DM based on previous studies on the efficacy of the MO, AV and JA extract7. Each lamb was drenched twice daily in two instalments corresponding to the time of feeding at 07:00 and 16:00, using a 20 mL drencher manufactured by NJ Philips, Somersby Australia.

Experimental design, growth performance and experimental feed

Forty (40), South African Mutton Merino male lambs, aged 4-month-old and with an average liveweight of 28.8 ± 0.4 kg sourced from the Innovation Africa (IA) research farm at the University of Pretoria, were used for trial. Before the commencement of the experiment, lambs received vaccinations (Multivax-P Plus) against a host of bacterial infections, deworming (Abamectin 0.2% m/v), and underwent wool shearing. Lambs were ranked based on body weight and subsequently grouped into five homogenous blocks. From each block, two lambs were randomly assigned to each of four dietary treatments in a randomised complete block design (RCBD). The pair of lamb from each block was housed in a pen thus 5 pen per treatment with 2 animals within each pen. All experimental lambs were provided with a total mixed ration (TMR) and contains approximately 42% roughage. The TMR was formulated to fulfil the nutritional requirements of growing lambs employing the AMTS formulation program in accordance with NRC (2017) guidelines. Following a 14-day adaptation period to the formulated diet, lambs were gradually introduced to the one of four extract treatment which include: T1 (TMR + distilled water as placebo), T2 (TMR + MO extract at 50 mg/kg DM feed), T3 (TMR + JA extract at 50 mg/kg DM feed), T4 (TMR + AV extract at 50 mg/kg DM feed). Adaptation commenced with a low dosage which was gradually increased to the recommended 50 mg/kg over additional 14 days.

The growth performance of the lambs was subsequently monitored for 75 days after the adaptation period. Rations were dispensed in the morning and afternoon, and refusals collected and weighed before each morning feeding. Corresponding doses of extract solution were administered in two equal parts at feeding time, while fresh water was provided ad libitum. Bi-weekly weighing of lambs was used to estimate average daily gain while average daily feed intake and feed conversion ratio were estimated based on feed consumption and body weight changes.

Nutrient digestibility, carcass, and methane emissions measurement

Following the conclusion of the growth study, one lamb from each pen was randomly selected (20 lambs in total) and transferred to individual metabolic cages and subsequently fitted with faecal bags for total faecal and urine collection. Lambs selected were those withbodyweight closest to the group mean. Each lamb was subsequently offered a fixed daily feed allowance of 1.8 kg, based on their previous week’s intake during the growth phase while feeding and drenching protocols remained the same. There was a 14-day adaptation period to the cages and faecal harnesses, followed by a 6-day collection period during which feed offered, refusals, faeces, and urine were collected, weighed and sampled daily (10%). Sub-samples collected daily were frozen for subsequent laboratory analysis. At the end of the digestibility collection, pooled sub-samples of feed, refusals and faeces were mixed thoroughly and then sampled for analysis. A portion of each of feed, refusals and faecal sample was weighed, and oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h for dry matter determination according to AOAC, protocol17, while a second portion was dried at 55 °C for 48 h, milled to pass a 1 mm sieve, and stored for chemical analysis. Urine samples collected in a plastic container containing sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) to prevent nitrogen volatilisation. Each day, a 10% aliquot was frozen at − 20 °C and then pooled together at the end of the study for nitrogen analysis.

For CH4 emission measurements, the 20 lambs used in the digestibility and nitrogen balance study were transferred to the open-circuit respiration chambers, in batches of four. Each batch represented a complete block (i.e., one lamb per treatment group). The chamber design has been previously described18 and consisted of powder-coated steel frames (25 × 25 mm) enclosed with PVC sheeting, yielding a chamber volume of approximately 5 m³. Ventilation was provided via extractor fans and door-mounted air gaps to maintain consistent airflow. Prior to animal trials, CH4 recovery rates were validated using standard volumes of CH₄, (Portagas, Pasadena TX 77503, USA) with calculated recovery efficiencies between 85% and 97%. Air samples were collected continuously from each chamber and duct system using peristaltic pumps and plastic tubing into deflatable Teflon bags over a 22.5 h every day while cleaning and feed provision was done within 1 h in each chamber. Each lamb rotated through different chambers during a five-day cycle to eliminate chamber effects while gas recovery rates were used to correct data for each cycle18. Methane concentration was quantified using gas chromatography (Model 8610 C GC Gas Analyser, SRI Instruments, Germany), equipped with a flame ionisation detector and a solenoid valve column packed with silica gel. Five gas samples were drawn from each Teflon bag and injected manually into the GC using a syringe and methane peaks were converted into concentration using standard curve generated with the Peak simple software.

The remaining 20 lambs from the growth trial (five per treatment group) were humanely stunned using a captive bolt pistol and immediately slaughtered by severing the major blood vessels in the neck to evaluate carcass yield and sampling of rumen content. Rumen fluid samples were collected within 7–10 min post-exsanguination, thoroughly mixed, and filtered to remove large feed particles. A previous work indicates that rumen microbial activity can remain stable for at least 30 min after sampling when samples are handled promptly and maintained under appropriate conditions19. Although rumen samples were collected from 20 slaughtered lambs, 16 representative samples (four per treatment group) were selected for shotgun metagenomic sequencing based on DNA quality and concentration. One portion of the rumen fluid was preserved for ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N) and another portion for volatile fatty acid (VFA) analysis using standard procedures18. Additional subsamples were stored in sterile container at − 20 °C for subsequent microbial analysis.

Data collection and analysis

Chemical analysis

The dry matter (DM, ID 934.01), organic matter (OM) and ash (ID 942.05) of the TMR, refusals and faeces were determined following standard procedure of AOAC17. Acid detergent fibre, neutral detergent fibre, and acid detergent lignin were determined using ANKOM 200/220 fibre analyser and Daisy incubator (ANKOM Technology, Fairport, NY, USA) according to the procedure of Van Soest et al.20. Heat stable alpha-amylase and sodium sulphite were incorporated in the NDF assay. Nitrogen was analysed with Leco (Leco TruMac N determinator, Leco Corporation, St. Joseph. USA). Ether extract was determined by extracting the sample with ether following the Tecator Soxtec (HT6) system according to AOAC procedures17.

Rumen fluid samples were analysed for pH (model PB-10/c, Sartorius, Germany), while the samples for ammonia nitrogen were analysed using the phenol-hypochlorite reagent spectrophotometric procedures. The VFA samples were analysed using a gas chromatography (Shimadzu GC-2010 Tracera; Shimadzu corp., Kyoto, Japan) with Barrier Ionization Discharge (BID) detector and fitted with a 30 m Inert Cap Pure Wax column (df = 0.25 μm, I.D.=0.25 mm). The handling steps and equipment conditions were as described in previous study18.

Rumen microbial analysis

Frozen rumen fluid samples were thawed at room temperature, and total genomic DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit (D4300; Zymo Research, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracted DNA was stored at − 20 °C until further processing. The purity and concentration of the DNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, South Africa) and a Qubit™ 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA), respectively. Agarose gel electrophoresis was also performed for additional quality verification, as previously described21.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed at Novogene Laboratories (Singapore). Library preparation was conducted using the Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina), beginning with 20–25 ng of genomic DNA. DNA fragmentation was carried out using a Covaris sonicator (Covaris Inc., Massachusetts, USA) to generate fragments of approximately 300 bp. Following end repair, A-tailing, and PCR amplification, libraries were quality-checked using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and quantified by qPCR prior to sequencing.

A total of 16 rumen samples were sequenced using paired end reads on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Sequencing generated approximately 46.7 GB of raw data across all samples, corresponding to an average sequencing depth of approximately 2.9 GB per sample. This sequencing depth provided adequate coverage for downstream taxonomic profiling and functional annotation of the rumen microbiome. Raw sequence reads were subsequently analysed using the MG-RAST server (version 4.0.3)22 for quality control and downstream analysis, including functional and taxonomic annotation. The bioinformatics workflow followed the approach previously reported23,24. Sequencing depths was evaluated at the project level, and detailed read-level metrics data are available in the NCBI SRA database under BioProject accession number PRJNA775821, covering all treatment groups.

Statistical analysis

Weekly body weight measurements were used to estimate individual average daily gain (ADG) and total feed consumption within each pen was averaged to estimate average daily intake of each lamb. All data collected were analysed using the Proc Mixed model of SAS (Version 9.4; SAS, Cary, USA). The model statement includes:

Results

There was no morbidity or mortality during the growth trial or the subsequent evaluation of digestibility and methane production. Table 2 shows the response of the growing lambs to medicinal extract supplementation. Dry matter intake differed (P < 0.05) among treatments, with animals in the MO, JA and AV groups consuming greater total DMI than those in the control group. However, when intake was expressed relative to metabolic body weight (kg DM/kg BW⁰·⁷⁵), no significant differences were observed among treatments (P > 0.05). Furthermore, extract supplementation did not affect NDF, ADF, CP and starch intake across the treatments. Dry matter, organic matter and crude protein digestibility differed by medicinal plant extract inclusion (P < 0.05) with lambs consuming JA, AV and MO having higher DM digestibility compared to the control animals. However, extract supplementation did not affect NDF and ADF digestibility across the treatments. All of MO, JA and AV reduced (P < 0.05) methane production in the animals when expressed in g/d and in relation to feed consumed (g/kg DDMI) and per bodyweight (g/kg BW) as seen in Table 3. Compared to the control, MO, JA and AV reduced daily methane production by 9%, 17% and 12%, respectively.

Discussion

The absence of treatment effects on DMI per unit of metabolic body weight indicates that the observed differences in absolute DMI were primarily associated with differences in body weight and growth performance rather than changes in feed intake regulation. Normalization of intake to BW⁰·⁷⁵ suggests that animals across treatments consumed similar amounts of dry matter relative to their metabolic requirements, implying no adverse effects of MO, JA, or AV supplementation on appetite or feeding behaviour.

An enhanced crude protein digestibility in MO and JA treated animals, may suggest enhanced proteolytic activity and nitrogen utilisation potentially driven by microbial proliferation or enzymatic activation stimulated by the plant secondary metabolites. The reduction in rumen ammonia nitrogen coupled with increased crude protein digestibility suggests improved nitrogen utilization, likely through decreased ruminal protein degradation and/or enhanced post-ruminal protein flow. The lack of significant changes in Proteobacteria and Cyanobacteria may indicate that these effects were achieved without major shifts in these bacterial populations, implying modulation of microbial activity or other functional groups rather than broad changes in community composition. The lower rumen ammonia nitrogen in the rumen may be indicative of a more efficient nitrogen turnover which is capable of reducing urine nitrogen loss associated with ruminants18.

These findings align with previous studies indicating that phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenolics, and anthraquinones can modulate microbial enzymatic systems, facilitating polysaccharide and protein breakdown25,26. Zhou et al.27 further reported that anthraquinones in AV and JA can act as redox mediators, enhancing lignocellulose depolymerisation. Increased CP digestibility have been observed in a previous study when MO leaf extract was added to the diets of lactating cows28. Therefore, the improved digestibility in this study may be attributed to a combination of increased nutrient availability, improved fermentation, and functional microbial shifts.

The significant reductions in CH4 emissions further support the role of these plant extracts in modifying rumen fermentation pathways. The observed trends are in line with a growing body of literature demonstrating the CH4 suppressing potential of these phytogenic feed additives1,29. The mechanism of enteric CH4 mitigation has been proposed to include one or combination of these processes: inhibition of methanogenic archaea, reduction in protozoal populations, or redirection of hydrogen towards alternative electron sinks such as propionate18,30–32.

Although the extracts resulted in significant reductions in methane production, this was not accompanied by significant changes in total VFA concentration or individual VFA proportions, including propionate. The absence of significant differences in propionate limits the ability to support hydrogen redirection towards alternative electron sinks as a primary mechanism in this study. While numerical trends in propionate were observed, these were not statistically significant. Hydrogen redirection towards propionate formation remains a plausible hypothesis; however, in the absence of direct measurements of hydrogen balance or significant shifts in fermentation end-products, this mechanism cannot be conclusively established. The proposed redirection of hydrogen mechanism remains speculative and can only be validated using hydrogen balance measurements and this warrants further investigation.

The microbial functional analysis in the current study revealed decreased relative abundance of genes associated with microbial stress response (e.g., mercuric resistance operon regulator), which may indicate reduced activity or viability of hydrogenotrophic methanogens under phytochemical pressure33. Although canonical methanogenesis genes like mcrA were not prominent, the suppression of microbial pathways linked to methanogen fitness aligns with the observed reduction in CH4 production.

In addition, the abundance of carbohydrate metabolic genes (e.g., uronate isomerase) showed considerable variability across treatments. While higher values were observed in MO and AV supplemented lambs, the absence of this gene in the JA treatment, indicates that this gene is not solely responsible for methane suppression across treatments. Although shifts in carbohydrate metabolism may contribute to altered rumen fermentation dynamics, direct attribution to hydrogen redirection or methane mitigation mechanisms remain unclear34. The observed reduction in CH₄ emissions may reflect coordinated changes in microbial functional activity rather than the effect of a specific carbohydrate metabolism pathway. Similar reductions in CH₄ emissions have been reported with AV supplementation in dairy cattle, often linked to its flavonoid and anthraquinones35 content, which may partly support the findings of the present study.

There is a lack of significant changes in VFA concentrations across treatments. However, ammonia nitrogen was significantly lower in MO and AV treatments. This could indicate more efficient nitrogen capture by rumen microbes, likely due to improved microbial growth and protein synthesis, a hypothesis supported by the elevated abundance of protein biosynthesis genes observed in JA and MO lambs (Fig. 2), and consistent with earlier findings28,36. From a sustainability perspective, lower rumen ammonia nitrogen concentrations may translate into improved nitrogen use efficiency and reduced urinary nitrogen losses, which are key contributors to environmental nitrogen pollution in ruminant systems. Although VFA profiles remained stable, the functional gene pattern in carbohydrate pathways (Fig. 2) and higher digestibility values (Table 2) may suggest improved fermentation efficiencyJ37. This indicates that, despite no change in VFA concentration, energy extraction from feed may have increased, particularly via enhanced monosaccharide and oligosaccharide degradation which was observed in other phytogenic supplemented rumen systems38.

The VFAs concentration in the rumen is not static and reflects a balance between production and absorption into the bloodstream39. The VFA absorption rate was not measured in the current study, and this may warrant further testing. The stable pH values across treatments indicate that the extracts did not result in any acidotic shifts in the rumen environment40.

The inclusion of MO, JA and AV extracts at the current doses did not depress growth, although some reports even suggest growth promoting benefits16,41. Previous in vitro findings showed increased digestibility32, whereas in the present in vivo trial improvements were only numerical. Recent studies42,43 support the integration of bioactive plant compounds in feedlot systems to enhance both productivity and environmental efficiency. Nevertheless, further testing variable doses is recommended considering potential for enhanced growth.

Microbial diversity results provide further insights into the underlying mechanisms. The dominance of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes is typical of high forage diets6,24. The lack of significant differences at the phylum level suggests that methane inhibition may not be directly explained by broad taxonomic shifts, but rather by more subtle changes in microbial activity or functional pathways. Although numerical variations in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio were observed and this ratio is often used as a proxy for energy harvesting efficiency44, its relevance remains unclear due to absence of significant treatment effects45. Previous work has shown that plant secondary metabolites such as saponins and tannins can directly affect methanogen abundance or indirectly modulate protozoal hosts29,46. The inclusion of plant extracts may enhance microbial resilience and ecological stability in the rumen. Increased microbial diversity is generally associated with improved feed conversion and metabolic plasticity, particularly under dietary interventions47.

While not a canonical methanogenesis marker, the mercuric resistance operon regulatory protein gene which was significantly reduced or fully suppressed in plant extracts treated lambs has been linked to cellular stress response, redox regulation, and detoxification mechanisms in methanogenic archaea48. The abundance of uronate isomerase, a gene associated with the conversion of carbohydrate intermediates to SCFA precursors which was significantly higher in MO and AV groups, which may reflect alterations in carbohydrate metabolism. However, direct links to propionate production or methane mitigation cannot be conclusively established. These findings mirror in vivo observations of slightly higher propionate levels and support prior reports linking uronic acid metabolism to energy yield optimisation49. Other functionally relevant genes, such as RNA polymerase sigma factor HrdD and xylose isomerase, were elevated in JA and MO treatments, suggesting increased transcriptional activity and carbohydrate conversion potential in treated groups. While classic methanogenesis genes like mcrA and hdr were not enriched in our dataset, the observed gene profile shifts are consistent with reductions in CH4 emissions (Table 3) and improvements in digestibility (Table 2). These results support the idea that plant secondary metabolites can modulate microbial gene expression toward less methanogenic and more energetically efficient states31.

At a broader functional level, the enrichment of protein biosynthesis and central carbohydrate metabolism pathways in JA and MO lambs mirrors the observed improvements in digestibility and reductions in CH4. These shifts suggest that phytochemicals may simultaneously enhance nutrient utilisation and reduce environmental impacts45,48,50. Taken together, the results suggest that secondary metabolites in MO, JA, and AV extracts modulate rumen microbial metabolism in ways that favour more efficient energy capture, greater microbial protein synthesis, and reduced methanogenesis50. This highlights the potential of these extracts as viable feed additives in sustainable ruminant production systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that supplementation with MO, JA and AV extracts reduced enteric methane emissions in Merino lambs without adversely affecting feed intake, growth performance, or carcass characteristics. Improvements in crude protein digestibility and reductions in rumen ammonia nitrogen suggest enhanced nitrogen utilisation and potential benefits for nutrient efficiency. The observed methane reduction is therefore likely the result of complex microbial and metabolic interactions rather than a single defined pathway. Further research incorporating targeted measurements of hydrogen balance, methanogenic activity, and dose-response relationships is required to elucidate the mechanisms involved and optimise the use of these plant extracts in ruminant production systems. Overall, these findings support the potential of medicinal plant extracts as sustainable feed additives for reducing the environmental footprint of ruminant production, while maintaining animal performance.
