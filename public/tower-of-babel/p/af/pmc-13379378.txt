High speed biaxial piezoelectric MEMS micromirror with varifocal tunability
Authors: Hunter Shillingburg, Daniel Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-speed control of light in 3D space is a rapidly advancing field because it enables unique applications in neurobiology, communications, laser micromachining, and augmented/virtual reality. Several technologies have been demonstrated to advance this field, such as complex spatial light modulators (SLMs) and intricate optical systems that independently control in-plane and out-of-plane beam steering. These systems face significant limitations in speed, data handling, and power dissipation, and their bulky optical components hinder integration into other systems. Here, we present a tip-tilt-curvature micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) scanner that overcomes these limitations by combining rapid lateral beam steering with ultra-fast axial focal tuning in a compact form factor of only a few millimeters. The device achieves lateral scan rates exceeding 10 kHz with axial focus modulation over tens of centimeters at operating frequencies exceeding 100 kHz. In benchmarking our device against other tip-tilt-curvature MEMS scanners, the one presented here demonstrates outstanding performance in combining lateral and axial scanning speeds. This performance, along with its compactness, establishes a new scalable architecture for dynamic three-dimensional control of light with high spatiotemporal resolution.

Introduction

The rate at which optical information can be precisely delivered, processed, and gathered in three-dimensional space is an integral part of many advancing fields of technology1,2. In biomedical imaging and microscopy, this enables further understanding of sub-cellular dynamics with ultra-fine, previously unresolvable time responses. It also prevents overexposure of photosensitive samples, which could cause photobleaching or phototoxicity in a sample, potentially leading to cell death or weakly measured signals. While multiphoton and confocal microscopy techniques have been drastically improving, the capabilities of the system are dwarfed by the limitations of the optics used for imaging volumes of samples3–5. On a similar note, the manufacturing of laser-micromachined goods requires efficient throughput, which depends on the speed and accuracy of production6–8. These systems often include a tight focal point to define micron-sized features, leading to a lower depth of field. This lower depth of field can cause surfaces with significant roughness to have processing defects, which can be remedied by fine-tuning the axial position of a beam during the micromachining process. The field of augmented reality (AR) demands high image refresh rates to reduce flickering, as well as high-definition detail and contrast within sleek, comfortable wearables9–11. A common concern with AR technology is a phenomenon known as the vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC)12, where the eyes may converge inward on an image, but due to displays being at fixed positions, the eyes cannot properly accommodate their focus onto the screen. This can lead to visual fatigue and headaches. Dynamically changing the focus of virtually generated AR objects can compensate for this accommodation failure and help alleviate VAC. And so, all such technologies can be seen to benefit not only from faster lateral scanning but also from higher-speed axial control.

Several technologies are currently aimed at addressing the challenge of rapid 3D optical information handling. One method utilizes SLMs to generate holograms and light patterns by locally changing the phase of a beam’s wavefront13–16. While SLMs enable unprecedented control of wavefronts1,17, they have the drawback of controlling the state of many thousands of individual pixels with speed and resolution only achieved with complex driving electronics. Another popular method used consists of separating the axial and lateral scanning components by using an SLM, micro-positioning stage, or laser beam scanning MEMS to perform a lateral scan and a varifocal optical element to handle the axial scanning. These varifocal optics can include higher-end liquid crystals, tunable acoustic gradient-index (TAG) lenses, piezoelectric stages, and adaptive optics such as varifocal MEMS mirrors18–22 and metalenses1,23–25. While these optical elements can attain fast axial scanning operation, additional components are required to expand their capabilities for modulating light in three dimensions, resulting in bulky systems with complex assembly requirements. For example, volumetric Lissajous confocal microscopy at an unsurpassed 3D scanning speed has been demonstrated by utilizing a TAG lens with a resonant mirror and a galvo scanner26. In this paper, a MEMS-based laser scanner is introduced that can change the effective focal point exceeding 100 kHz over tens of centimeters while performing lateral scanning at >10 kHz, providing a new tunable optical device for dynamic three-dimensional control of light with high spatiotemporal resolution.

In the following sections, a description of the MEMS design and fabrication process is first provided. Next, the lateral scanning capabilities of the device are characterized through modal analysis and frequency response plots utilizing both COMSOL Multiphysics simulations and experimental measurements taken with a digital holography microscope. Then, the axial capabilities are demonstrated by measuring how the mirror’s curvature and focal length change under several bias voltages. To understand how fast the mirror can change its radius of curvature, the temporal response of the mirror plate control under a low-frequency square wave was analyzed. A resonant AC frequency sweep was also performed with a wide bandwidth to provide a further understanding of the mirror plate’s speed characteristic. The final section is reserved for a demonstration of the device’s ability to move a 2D scan trajectory in three-dimensional space by adjusting the imaging plane’s position. Lissajous figures were generated and shown to focus and defocus when the mirror curvature is adjusted during a lateral scan. Last, a simple laser scanning confocal microscope was built and used to image a standard USAF target at two distinct focal planes, where 2D Lissajous figures were generated.

Results

MEMS design and fabrication

MEMS scanners have vast capabilities as tools for light modulation. These small optical elements have shown their use in Light Detection And Ranging (LiDAR)27–29, augmented/virtual reality displays30, and even in functionalizing smart headlights31,32. In order to attain faster generation of denser scanning patterns, the scanner design shown here was designed to have a small chip footprint/mirror size and tight torsional beams along the tilting axes.

Aside from the ground electrode, which connects directly to the silicon device layer, the electrodes and wires are isolated from the silicon with a pad oxide layer, while the metals covering the actuation regions are isolated with aluminum nitride. The isolated wires going to the mirror plate connect to the bond pads with traces that run along the inside of the outer actuators. A piezoelectric film, which undergoes mechanical strain and changes shape in response to an applied electric field, is utilized to control the scanner’s angular tilting and tipping, as well as tune its varifocal behavior. Applying voltages to the electrodes causes each of the isolated regions of the device to deform, resulting in an induced tipping of the mirror in each of the lateral directions. In the case of the mirror plate (which also has a piezoelectric layer), the curvature changes when a voltage is applied. Assuming no initial stress in a piezoelectric film, the amount of strain S induced by an electric field is linearly related to the piezoelectric coefficient matrix d of the material used and the electric field strength E, given as S=dE. This mode of actuation was chosen for several reasons. Firstly, the electric field across the film is greater in thinner films, allowing the required voltage to be reduced greatly by adjusting the film thickness. Further, since the actuation physics is electric field-based, the power consumed by controlling this scanner is greatly reduced. Moreover, since piezoelectric materials are becoming easier to deposit as fabrication processes mature, these materials have been undergoing an increase in utilization over the years34 and are expected to improve the efficiency of next-generation MEMS devices. In comparison, electrothermal actuators have been used extensively in MEMS research owing to their large actuation range with low voltage requirements; however, they may behave unpredictably at times due to environmental temperature fluctuations, and they have significant power consumption and slow millisecond-response times due to heat dissipation35. Electrostatic actuators are also widely used since they have simple fabrication and reliable control, but they are frequently limited in that they require a large chip footprint or high driving voltage to operate.

The MEMS scanner outlined in this paper uses aluminum nitride as its piezoelectric layer and aluminum as its metallic layer to deform the inner and outer gimbal actuators, as well as allow for tuning of the mirror’s curvature when biased with a voltage. This film stack is standard for the multi-user manufacturing process run by Science Corporation, known as PiezoMUMPs. Aluminum nitride was used largely due to the multi-user process standard, however its linearity, structural rigidity and ease of deposition give further reasons to rely on it as the piezoelectric film of choice. PZT is a common alternative due to its significantly larger piezoelectric coefficients (enabling larger deformation with lower voltage), however, its dependence on lead (Pb) and high deposition temperature requirement limit the material from being adopted into CMOS processes. To compensate for this, aluminum scandium nitride (AlScN) has emerged and is still undergoing much development (see ref. 36 for a recent comparative analysis of these three piezoelectric films). As the reference suggests, AlScN provides a higher piezoelectric coefficient than AlN while maintaining CMOS compatibility, but it requires careful attention to doping levels since it can change mechanical properties and lead to different crystal phases, and it is also more susceptible to impurities from scandium oxidizing to form Sc2O3.

As mentioned, aluminum nitride has a relatively low piezoelectric coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{31}\approx 6.5\,\mathrm{pC}/{\rm{N}}$$\end{document}d31≈6.5pC/N) compared to PZT (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{31} > 100\,\mathrm{pC}/{\rm{N}})$$\end{document}d31>100pC/N), meaning that higher driving voltages are required37. However, since this coefficient is correlated with the material’s polarizability, a larger piezoelectric coefficient results in larger hysteresis in their mechanical response, especially when switching between positive and negative voltages. As a result, aluminum nitride-based devices use simpler control schemes and bring about lower power dissipation, since there are fewer dielectric losses, and bipolar signals can be used without the DC offset required in PZT-based actuation38.

Lateral mode characterization

The frequency responses of the scanner for x- and y-axis rotation are shown in Fig. 2, measured using the stroboscopic feature of a LynceeTec digital holography microscope (DHM-R1000) with voltages ranging from 1 Vpp to 4 Vpp. These voltages are applied to only one actuator because the stroboscopic unit used for the measurements has only one output channel. Those responses were then plotted as a function of the total optical angle achieved. The linear resonant frequencies of the x-axis and y-axis modes were measured to be 10.05 kHz and 12.29 kHz with Q factors of 195.1 and 227.6 in air, respectively. This bears close similarity to the FEA simulations, which had x-axis and y-axis eigenfrequency modes at 10.705 kHz and 12.892 kHz. The mode shapes of both the simulated data (using COMSOL Multiphysics) and experimental data (reconstructed from the recorded hologram data) are shown in Fig. 3. Due to fabrication variations, small differences between the measured and simulated resonant frequencies are observed. Nonlinearity in the frequency response is due to the stiffening of the device torsional beams at high actuation velocity. These nonlinearities result in frequency-dependent amplitudes. However, they also widen the resonant-mode bandwidths, allowing frequency tuning in Lissajous figure generation. To test the maximum angular tilting limit of the scanner under resonant actuation, the scanner was set up in an optical system where a HeNe beam (Thorlabs HNL020LB, 632.8 nm) was directed towards the mirror plate and the reflected light towards a screen. For these measurements, the scanner was placed in a vacuum chamber, which was pumped down to 0.1 Pa to further push the angular tilting to its limit. A sinusoidal voltage signal was then applied to each actuator, and the optical tilting range was determined under multiple actuation voltages. When driving the scanner with a resonant 10 Vpp signal on each axis, the maximum optical angle achieved was 16°.

The DC tip tilt range of the MEMS was also measured using this microscope with voltages ranging from −20 Vpp to 20 Vpp applied to one actuator. From a −20 V to 20 V range, the x-axis has a range of about 0.1 deg with a slope of 2.3 mdeg/V, while the y-axis has a range of 0.16 deg with a slope of 4.1 mdeg/V. As is well known for this type of micromechanical devices, the DC ranges are quite small, so the MEMS was designed to actuate at resonance in order to obtain the dynamic response previously shown.

Although astigmatism is present and limits the smallest resolvable feature size, it can be reduced by optimizing the fabrication process to produce a more symmetric mirror or by proper optical system design, using either a pinhole to spatially filter out-of-focus light or an aspherical lens to align the focal points more precisely. This astigmatism and other aberrations created by the asymmetry of the mirror plate were quantified through the use of Zernike coefficients41.

Zernike polynomials are commonly used to decompose an optical wavefront into a weighted sum of well-defined aberration modes and form an orthonormal basis defined on the unit disk. Each term in the expansion, characterized by a radial order n and azimuthal order m, corresponds to a physically meaningful optical aberration (e.g., defocus, astigmatism, coma), and its associated Zernike coefficient quantifies the magnitude of that aberration in the measured surface. This representation allows for a systematic and quantitative identification of the dominant sources of wavefront error in an optical element, and is widely used in the characterization of mirrors, lenses, and other optical surfaces41. The Zernike coefficients are shown in Fig. 6, which includes a visualization of the mathematical shape at each index, such as tilting in the y- and x-directions corresponding to indices 1 and 2 (a full breakdown of the Zernike coefficient derivation and aberration mode shapes can be found in ref. 41). Piston (index 0) is shown but, in many cases, is ignored in any analysis, since it carries no useful information about the aberrations and is used only to better fit a measured surface to the Zernike mathematical model. The “defocus” term (index 4) directly relates to the radius of curvature (focal length) of a mirror plate. This index value decreases as the mirror is actuated from 50 V to -50V, indicating that it becomes more optically flat. The final aberration outlined in Fig. 6 is the oblique astigmatism (index 3), which is unwanted but is possibly related to the fabrication process (this astigmatism can also be seen in the elliptical phase profiles shown in Figs. 4 and 5). The response time at which the mirror can change its curvature was measured by switching the driving voltage on and off with a 1 Hz square wave while measuring the reflected light through a 100 µm pinhole placed at the mirror’s initial focal point (optical system shown in Fig. 7). The light intensity emerging from the pinhole was then measured with an avalanche photodetector (ThorLabs, APD410A). When the light is still in focus at the pinhole (0 V on the mirror plate), the photodetector reads a higher voltage signal. When a voltage is applied to the mirror, its curvature changes, and the focal point moves away from the pinhole; thus, the signal’s intensity drops as more light is blocked by the pinhole, resulting in a lower measured voltage at the photodetector. This method enables a high temporal resolution of 1 MHz in the measurement. As shown in Fig. 7, the mirror’s focal length exhibits rise times of 5 µs and fall times of 20 µs. This transient response was measured for Vac values of 5 V and 10 V. While a linear elastic system driven by a symmetric voltage step would be expected to exhibit identical rise and fall times, the observed asymmetry is consistent with the well-documented behavior of polycrystalline piezoelectric thin films and can be attributed to a time-dependent strain that follows the instantaneous elastic response and is characteristic of polycrystalline thin films. When a voltage step is applied (voltage ON), the piezoelectric strain has two components: a fast, quasi-instantaneous elastic deformation and a slower creep component that continues to develop over a timescale governed by the relaxation dynamics of grain boundaries and defect-dipole reorientation within the film. Lissajous figure focusing & defocusing

The imaging capabilities of the tip-tilt-curvature micromirror were studied using Lissajous patterns, where a Lissajous image was created at a specific focal length and was moved to a different focal length by the voltage applied to the mirror plate actuator. In brief, Lissajous figures are formed when two resonant signals are applied to each lateral axis, where their greatest common denominator (GCD) correlates with the time it takes to complete one cycle (the refresh rate), and the number of lobes at the edge (given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{x}=\frac{{f}_{x}}{{GCD}}$$\end{document}nx=fxGCD and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{y}=\frac{{f}_{y}}{{GCD}}$$\end{document}ny=fyGCD) indicates the overall pattern fill factor44–46. This allows for tuning of a Lissajous figure pattern by simply changing the driving frequencies, which is useful for tailoring an optical system to favor either higher refresh rates or denser patterns. Generating scan patterns with this technique is beneficial for image stabilization when exposed to environmental vibrations and disturbances30,47. Laser scanning confocal microscopy

To further demonstrate the capability of the tip-tilt-curvature MEMS scanner in a three-dimensional imaging system, a simple laser-scanning confocal microscope was set up that uses a polarized beam splitter to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio (see Fig. 10). The beam splitter polarizes the input beam into transmitted p-polarized light and reflected s-polarized light. The latter is sent to the imaging section of the microscope. This beam is first reflected off of the MEMS scanner used to form a 2D Lissajous figure. The reflected light from the scanner is then passed through a 4x microscope composed of three lenses, with focal lengths of 50 mm, 100 mm, and 25 mm, respectively. A quarter-wave plate between the lenses is used to rotate the polarization of the light 180°, allowing it to pass through the polarized beam splitter as p-polarized light instead of being reflected as it was before. This light is then focused into a 100 µm pinhole with a 50 mm lens to prevent any out-of-focus light from being recorded. Discussion

In order to make a quantitative comparison with previously published works48–53, a common figure of merit should be identified for tip-tilt-curvature scanners. The comparison outlined here was broken down into two components: an axial and a lateral scanning figure of merit. By plotting these two figures of merit against each other, a graph can be generated to summarize key metrics of reported 3D MEMS devices with tip, tilt, and varifocal control, as shown in Fig. 11. At the time of writing, the most recently published scanner with tilt-tip-curvature actuation was demonstrated by Haidarian et al. (ref. 52). Their scanner was demonstrated to attain an optical tilting range of 16° with a change in optical power range of 32 diopters when their mirror’s curvature is adjusted. It features a mirror diameter of 10 mm, which is hand-assembled onto PZT-based actuators, giving a chip area of 40 mm x 43 mm. The benchmarking comparison in Fig. 11 demonstrates that the scanner reported in this manuscript outperforms other architectures by achieving the highest resonant scanning speeds with reasonable actuation voltages (10 Vpp for lateral scanning and up to 50 Vdc for axial focusing). The scanner reported here also utilizes a simple monolithic fabrication method with CMOS-compatible materials, needing only five masks in a standardized semiconductor process. An additional benefit of using CMOS-compatible materials is that key aspects of the mechanical reliability of these devices are well understood. Early studies on resonant silicon microstructures demonstrated that single-crystal silicon structures can sustain cyclic loading at frequencies in the kHz range for tens of billions of cycles without measurable degradation when operated below the fracture stress55. The AlN piezoelectric layer offers additional advantages in this context: its chemical inertness and hardness make it intrinsically resistant to fatigue under periodic electromechanical loading. A systematic study of AlN thin films under cyclic loading found no clear signs of mechanical fatigue after 10,000 cycles at 83% of the fracture strength42. Furthermore, the established commercial use of AlN in bulk acoustic wave (BAW) filters and resonators operating at GHz frequencies for wireless communications, with trillions of cycles accumulated over device lifetimes, provides strong practical evidence of the material’s resilience under resonant loading conditions. The operating strains in our device at the reported drive voltages are well below the fracture strengths of both silicon and AlN. The demonstrated capabilities of the tip-tilt-curvature MEMS scanner reveal promising applications in brain research and AR/VR applications. For brain research, where neurons exhibit firing rates on the millisecond timescale and densities approaching 10⁵ neurons/mm³, this MEMS device is a viable solution for meeting the stringent spatial and temporal sampling requirements14,46. It will enable targeting individual neurons within predetermined 3D volumes at operational frequencies exceeding 10 kHz. Furthermore, the device’s integrated architecture, enabling simultaneous in-plane and out-of-plane beam positioning within a single chip, represents a significant advancement in system miniaturization, which is a key consideration for sophisticated neuroimaging applications. Of particular significance is the device’s curvature-modulation resonance at 141.3 kHz, which enables resonant scanning along all three axes at substantially reduced driving voltages, a critical advantage for power-constrained applications and long-term operational stability. For AR and VR applications in image generation, while the image’s focus can be tuned by adjusting the curvature of the scanner’s mirror plate, resolution and frame rate requirements place a heavy burden on the scanner’s lateral scanning metrics. Looking at this from a frequency perspective, an idealized fill factor of a Lissajous figure (related to the number of overlapping points) for a 1280 × 720 resolution at 60 Hz demands driving frequencies of >40 kHz for each axis56. While this MEMS design currently lacks that capability, it achieves desirable frequencies capable of the same resolution at 30 Hz with a 73% fill factor (see ref. 49).

Materials and methods

PiezoMUMPs process fabrication steps

The MEMS scanners were fabricated using the Science Corporation’s PiezoMUMPs process. The red line in Fig. 1a indicates a cross-section of the device, as shown in Fig. 1c. The process starts off a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer with a bulk thickness of 400 µm, a buried oxide layer of 1 µm, and a silicon device layer of 10 µm. Initially, a 0.2 µm pad oxide is grown and patterned. This can be used as an isolation layer for top-layer electrical connections without any actuation involved. Then, a 0.5 µm aluminum nitride (AlN) layer is deposited using reactive sputtering and patterned. A 1 µm metallic layer of aluminum is then e-beam evaporated onto the device and patterned. At this point, the device layer of silicon is patterned using reactive ion etching (RIE), and the bulk layer of silicon is patterned using a backside deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) process. Finally, a wet etch of the buried oxide is performed, releasing the MEMS. The MEMS chips were then attached and wire-bonded to a simple THT hybrid package to use with electronics in a breadboard or PCB.
