Failed induction of labor and associated factors among women receiving induction at public hospitals of Kembata zone in central Ethiopia
Authors: Arega Abebe Lonsako, Teshale Tilahun, Mulatu Wolde, Tariku Laelago, Tsehaynew Kasse, Asfaw Anulo, Addisu Girma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFailed induction of labour contributes to high caesarean section rates, posing risks to both maternal and neonatal health. Despite its clinical importance, limited data exists on the prevalence and determinants of failed induction of labour in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of failed induction of labour among women in public hospitals of central Ethiopia.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted across multiple health facilities. Data were extracted from the 386 randomly selected medical charts using a pre-tested checklist, then entered into Epi data version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Multivariable Logistic regression analysis was performed and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of failed labor induction was 21.2%, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 17–25%. Factors significantly associated with failed induction included age over 30 years [adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 6.78, 95% CI: 3.13–14.70], being a primigravida [AOR = 6.68, 95% CI: 3.10–14.40], a low Bishop score [AOR = 3.90, 95% CI: 1.62–9.39], and premature rupture of membranes [AOR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.42–7.54].ConclusionThe study identified a high rate of failed labour induction. Key factors contributing to failed labour induction included maternal age over 30 years, primigravida status, a low Bishop score, and premature rupture of membranes. Enhanced monitoring and follow-up are necessary to mitigate potential adverse effects on both maternal and fetal health.Keywords: Determinants, Induction of labour, Failed induction, Women, Ethiopia

Introduction

Failed induction of labor is the inability to achieve active labor (typically 4–6 cm cervical dilation with regular contractions) despite adequate attempts with induction methods such as oxytocin and/or membrane rupture within a defined time frame [1]. The definition of failed induction varies across guidelines. American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (ACOG) and Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine (SMFM) define it as the inability to achieve active labor (typically 6 cm dilation) despite adequate uterine contractions, with SMFM specifying a minimum of 24 h of oxytocin administration with membrane rupture [2, 3].

In contrast, the Federal Ministry of Health of Ethiopia [4], 2020 and St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, 2020 guidelines use a threshold of 4 cm dilation to define active labor, with failed induction occurring after a specified period of oxytocin infusion and/or mechanical methods, often within 24 h. While ACOG and SMFM focus on prolonged oxytocin use and amniotomy, FMOH and SPHMMC emphasize institutional protocols and adapt their definitions to local resource availability and clinical practices in Ethiopia [4].

Common methods for labor induction include pharmacological options such as prostaglandins and oxytocin infusion, as well as mechanical methods like balloon or Foley catheters and amniotomy [5]. Failed induction of labour (FIOL) is a significant contributor to obstetric complications. It is defined as the inability to achieve regular uterine contractions and cervical change after 6–8 h of maintaining the oxytocin dose, with artificial rupture of membranes if applicable [6].

Studies have shown that the prevalence of failed induction of labor varies worldwide. In United States 19.7% [7], Germany 37.7% [8], Nigeria 24.1% [9], Egypt 16% [10], Khartoum-Sudan 15.5% [11], However, in Ethiopia varies at regional level. 11.8% in Tigray [12], 19.7% in Dessie Referral Hospital [13], 21.4% in Jimma University specialized hospital [14], 20.5% in Adama hospital medical college [15] and 24.4% in University of Gondar Specialized Hospital [16], 17.3% in Hawassa referral hospital [17], in Worabe comprehensive specialized hospital 22.2% [18], and in Wolaita Sodo 26.5% [19].

Studies have identified several factors associated with failed induction of labor, including maternal age, gestational age, parity, Bishop score, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), post-term pregnancy, previous obstetric complications, and birth weight. These factors are commonly linked to failed induction [17, 20], and an increased risk of caesarean section and other operative deliveries [21].

Failed induction of labor can lead to several adverse outcomes for both maternal and neonatal health, including emergency caesarean sections, postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), hysterectomy, stillbirth, neonatal injuries, maternal death, and prolonged recovery periods [22–24]. Despite these significant risks, there is a scarcity of specific evidence on the prevalence and associated factors of failed induction of labor in Ethiopia, particularly in Central Ethiopia. While various studies have examined the general prevalence of failed induction, there remains a lack of detailed data specific to this region. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the prevalence and determinants of failed induction of labor in public hospitals of Kembata Zone in Central Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, setting and period

We conducted a multi-center retrospective cross sectional study in public hospitals of Kembata zone, Central Ethiopia. The zone is located at 340 km southwest of the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa, and 130 km from Hawassa. According to the 2016 census conducted by CSA the Zone has a total population of 1,080837 of whom about 536,676 are males & 544,161 are females. The Kembata zone has four governmental hospitals; Dr Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital, Doyogana Primary Hospital, Shinshicho Primary Hospital, and Angecha Primary Hospital. According to the Kembata zone HMIS 2024 report, these hospitals provided delivery services to 4593 women in six months and of these, 933 were conducted induction of labor. The women’s medical records document reviewed six months back from January 1- June 30, 2023, were extracted during October 11- November 10, 2024.

Populations

All women who received induction of labour in public hospitals of Kembata zone were considered as source population, while all randomly selected women who received induction of labour in public hospitals of Kembata zone during data collection were study population.

Eligibility criteria

Medical record charts of women who received induction of labor in public hospitals of the Kembata Zone were included in the study. Charts that lacked clear information regarding the induction of labor were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

We calculated the sample size by using a single population proportion formula with the following assumptions; the margin of error (d) = 5%, confidence level = 95% (Zα/2 = ± 1.96), design effect 1.5 and the proportion of failed induction of labour of 20.9% [20]. So, the final calculated sample size with a 10% attrition rate was 419.

All hospitals providing delivery services in the Kembata Zone were included in the study. A total of 820 women underwent labor induction in the study area, distributed as follows: 249 women at Shinshicho Primary Hospital, 171 at Doyogana Primary Hospital, 107 at Angecha Primary Hospital, and 293 at Dr. Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital. The sample was then proportionally allocated based on the number of women who underwent labor induction at each hospital: 127 women from Shinshicho Primary Hospital, 87 from Doyogana Primary Hospital, 55 from Angecha Primary Hospital, and 150 from Dr. Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital. Finally, using the list of medical record numbers for all women at each hospital as the sampling frame, a simple random sampling method was employed to select the sample (Fig. 1).

Operational definitions

Failed induction of labour

Is operationalized as the occurrence of birth by caesarean section delivery for the indication of failed induction of labour [6, 16, 25].

Bishop score

Is a tool used to evaluate the readiness of the cervix for labor. It is calculated based on five parameters: cervical dilation, effacement, consistency, position, and fetal station, with a maximum score of 13 points. Each parameter is assigned a score, and the total score helps predict the likelihood of a successful labor induction. A higher Bishop Score indicates a higher chance of a successful induction and vaginal delivery [26].

The induction protocol at study setting hospital involves using oxytocin doses tailored to parity

With primigravida receiving 22.5 IU/1L and multigravida receiving 11.25 IU/1L for induction. In pre-eclamptic mothers, the same dose is diluted in 500 ml of normal saline, starting at 10 drops/min and escalating by 10 drops every 30 min to a maximum of 40 drops/min. For maintenance (second bag) during augmentation, primigravida receive 11.25 IU/1L, and multigravida receive 5.5 IU/1L, ensuring safe and effective labor management.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were extracted from the selected medical charts using a pre-tested checklist, which was adapted from previous relevant literature [16, 27, 28],. This checklist was used to collect information on socio-demographic factors, obstetric history, reasons for induction, and induction methods. The data were sourced from maternity registration books, clients’ medical history sheets, induction sheets, labour follow-up sheets, partograph sheets, and operation summaries. Five BSc midwives were recruited as data collectors, and two MSc-level maternity and reproductive health supervisors oversaw the process to ensure accurate and efficient data extraction, as they are familiar with the subject matter.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, a two-day training session was provided to the data collectors and supervisors, covering the study objectives and data extraction methods. A pre-test was conducted on 5% of the sample size at Halaba Kulito General Hospital before the actual data collection to ensure the checklist was clear, error-free, and complete. Based on the pre-test results, necessary adjustments were made, and the revised tool was used for the actual data collection. During the data extraction period, supervisors and the investigator conducted close supervision and monitoring to maintain data quality. Daily evaluations were done to check data completeness and address any issues encountered during data collection. Finally, the supervisor and investigator reviewed all collected data for completeness, consistency, and clarity during data management.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were cleaned, coded, and entered into EpiData version 3.1 software, then exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarize the characteristics of the study participants, with the results presented in text, tables, and figures. A binary logistic regression model was used for bivariable analysis to identify associations between each independent variable and the outcome variable. Independent variables with p < 0.25 were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to control for potential confounders and identify actual associations. Multi-collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with none of the variables showing a VIF greater than 5. The model’s fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which was found to be insignificant (p = 0.591), indicating that the model was well-fitted. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and variables with p < 0.05 were considered significantly associated with failed induction of labor.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 386 medical records of women who underwent induction of labor were included in the study, with a response rate of nearly 92%. Almost two-thirds, 244 (63.2%), of the participants were age below 30 years. The minimum and maximum ages were 19 and 37 years, respectively, with a mean age of 31 years. Regarding the participants’ residence, more than half, 219 (56.7%), were rural residents, while 167 (43.3%) were urban residents.

Obstetrics related characteristics

Among the 386 women who underwent induction, nearly two-thirds, 245 (63.5%), were at term pregnancy, and 282 (73.1%) were multiparous. Of the 386 women, 272 (70.5%) had newborns with a birth weight between 2500 g and 3999 g. The minimum birth weight was 1400 g, and the maximum was 4900 g, with a mean of 3329.4 g. 225 (58.3%) had a Bishop score of less than 5. More than half, 186 (48.2%), had attended 1 to 4 ANC visits. Term pregnancies accounted for 245 (63.5%), while preterm pregnancies were 58 (15%). Additionally, 282 (73.1%) of the women were multiparous (Table 1).

Magnitude of failed induction of labour

In this study, the magnitude of failed induction of labour was 82 (21.24%) with a 95% confidence interval of (17.0 to 25.0). This suggests that approximately one in five inductions were unsuccessful (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with failed induction of labor

In the bivariate analysis, factors such as age (≥ 30 years), number of parities, pre-induction Bishop Score, antenatal care, premature rupture of membranes, intrauterine growth restriction, and birth weight of the newborn were identified as being associated with failed induction of labor, with a p-value < 0.25. These variables were considered candidates for multivariable analysis.

However, in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, four variables age (≥ 30 years), parity, Bishop Score, and premature rupture of membranes were found to be significantly associated with failed induction of labor at a 95% confidence level, with p < 0.05. The study found that the odds of failed induction of labor were significantly higher among certain groups. Mothers aged 30 years or older had six times greater odds of failure compared to those younger than 30 (Adjusted Odds Ratio: 6.78, 95% CI: 3.13, 14.70). Primigravida women had nearly six times the odds of failed induction compared to multigravida women (AOR: 6.68, 95% CI: 3.10, 14.40). Women with an unfavorable pre-induction Bishop score were four times more likely to experience failed induction compared to those with a favorable score (AOR: 3.90, 95% CI: 1.62, 9.39). Additionally, women with premature rupture of membranes had three times the odds of failed induction compared to those without it (AOR: 3.27, 95% CI: 1.42, 7.54) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of failed labor induction and its associated factors among laboring mothers at public hospitals in the Kembata Zone, Central Ethiopia. The results indicated that the prevalence of failed labor induction was 21.2%, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from 17.0 to 25.0%. The study identified several factors significantly associated with failed labor induction, including maternal age of 30 years or older, a low pre-induction bishop score, primiparity, and premature rupture of membranes.

The rate of failed labor induction in this study was consistent with findings from other studies conducted at Jimma University Specialized Hospital (21.4%), Nigeria (24.1%) [9, 14], Dessie Referral Hospital (19.7%) [29] and public health facilities in Hawassa (17.3%) [17]. This consistency could be attributed to several factors, most notably the use of similar labor induction protocols across these settings. Standardized protocols, including the methods and medications used for induction, may lead to comparable outcomes in terms of the failure rate.

However, the failure rate observed in this study was higher than that reported in studies from Dire Dawa Dilchora Referral Hospital (16.4%) and Saudi Arabia (16%) [10, 30]. This variation may be attributed to differences in sample sizes and quality of care. On the other hand, the failure rate found in this study was lower than the results from studies in Germany (37.7%) [8] and Pakistan (78.6%) [31]. The discrepancy in failed labor induction rates across studies can be attributed to several factors, including differences in sample size, quality of maternal care, and population characteristics such as health status and obstetric history. Additionally, variations in induction methods and the setting of the study (e.g., rural vs. urban healthcare facilities) likely contribute to these differences.

In this study, the odds of failed induction of labour were six times higher among mothers aged 30 years or older compared to those younger than 30 years. The result is in line with the study done in University of Gondar specialized hospital [16], Jigjiga University Sheik Hassan Yabare Referral Hospital [32], Germany [8]. This can be attributed to both physiological and obstetric factors. As women age, there is a decrease in uterine tone, cervical elasticity, and overall reproductive responsiveness, which can hinder successful induction. Additionally, older mothers are more likely to have medical conditions or unfavorable cervical conditions that further increase the risk of failed induction [33].

Women with an unfavorable pre-induction Bishop score were four times more likely to experience a failed induction of labour compared to women with a favorable pre-induction Bishop score. The finding was consistent with study done in Hawassa public facilities [17], Jimma University Specialized Hospital [14], and Amhara region referral hospitals [34]. The Bishop score assesses cervical readiness for labour, a low or unfavourable score indicates poor cervical ripening and a less favourable cervical status for induction, making it more difficult for labour to progress successfully. In contrast, a favourable Bishop score suggests that the cervix is more responsive to induction methods, increasing the likelihood of a successful labour induction [35].

The odds of failed induction of labor were nearly six times higher among primigravida women compared to multigravida women. This finding is in line with studies done in Hawassa public facilities [17], Jimma University specialized hospital [14], Amhara region referral hospitals [34], Dessie referral hospital [29], Khartoum-Sudan [11] and Saudi Arabia [10]. This might be because primigravida women have less cervical and uterine readiness for labour. Multigravida women, having experienced prior deliveries, often have more favourable cervical conditions and uterine response, making labour induction successful. In primigravida, the cervix and uterine muscles are less likely to respond effectively to induction, leading to higher rates of failure [36].

The odds of failed induction of labor were three times higher among women with premature rupture of membranes compared to women without it. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in University of Gondar specialized hospital [16] and Pakistan Karachi [31]. When the membranes rupture prematurely, it often leads to a decrease in the effectiveness of labor induction methods. The absence of the amniotic fluid cushion may result in reduced uterine contractions and an inability to maintain adequate pressure on the cervix, hindering cervical dilation and progression of labor. Additionally, it increases the risk of infection and other complications, which may interfere with the normal progression of induced labor [36].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study utilized a multicentre approach, enhancing the generalizability of its findings across public hospitals in central Ethiopia. However, the retrospective design relied on the accuracy and completeness of medical records, which could introduce potential bias. Additionally, unmeasured factors, such as provider skills and institutional differences in labor induction protocols, may have influenced the outcomes.

Conclusion

This study revealed a high rate of failed labor induction among women underwent induction in public hospitals of the Kembata Zone, highlighting it as a significant challenge in obstetric care. The key factors associated with failed induction were maternal age of 30 years or older, primigravida status, a low pre-induction Bishop score, and premature rupture of membranes.

Recommendations

Healthcare providers should closely monitor and assess high-risk groups, particularly women aged 30 years or older, primigravida women, and those with low pre-induction Bishop scores or premature rupture of membranes. Customized induction protocols, including cervical ripening methods, should be considered for these women to improve the chances of successful induction. Additionally, comprehensive patient education on the risks and expectations of labor induction should be provided to both healthcare providers and women to ensure better preparation and informed decision-making. Finally, further research is needed to explore effective interventions to optimize labor induction success and reduce caesarean delivery rates, particularly in high-risk populations.
