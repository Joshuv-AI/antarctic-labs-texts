Species-specific roles of SP1 in bovine and human embryonic genome activation and early embryonic development
Authors: Hao Jin, Lieying Xiao, Xiaolin He, Bingjie Hu, Yan Shi, Bo Xu, Xiaomei Tong, Shaohua Wang, Panpan Zhao, Lei Luo, Kun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEmbryonic genome activation (EGA) launches the embryonic program that enables the specification of lineages supporting implantation and formation of all tissues in the body, yet the molecular nature and regulation mechanisms underlying this process remain elusive. Using single-cell transcriptomics, we reveal that major EGA occurs asynchronously among blastomeres in bovine embryos. Additionally, we report that loss of transcription factor SP1 causes morula arrest in bovine and human embryos. SP1 coordinates the expression of genes specifying distinct lineages, thereby ensuring the transition from EGA to lineage segregation in bovine embryos. Alternatively, in human embryos, SP1 orchestrates the onset of EGA via direct activation of key EGA genes. These findings identify SP1 as a conserved yet species-specific regulator of early mammalian embryogenesis.

Mammalian embryonic development begins with a transcriptionally silent zygote formed by the fusion of terminally differentiated sperm and oocyte (1). As zygotic transcription initiates, a process known as embryonic genome activation (EGA) occurs, during which developmental control gradually transitions from maternal to zygotic regulation (2). The temporal occurrence of EGA is species-specific and typically manifests in waves, designated as minor and major waves. Major EGA takes place at the 2-cell stage in mice, the 4- to 8-cell stages in humans, and the 8- to 16-cell stages in cattle (3–5). It is noteworthy that, in contrast to the observations made in mice and humans, bovine embryos exhibit both morphological (6) and transcriptomic heterogeneity (7) during major EGA. However, the onset of this developmental heterogeneity and the dynamic trajectory of EGA in bovine embryos remain poorly understood, largely due to the paucity of single-cell transcriptome analysis from fully grown oocytes (FGO) to the 16-cell stage.

Following EGA, the embryo undergoes a process of compaction to form a morula. This morula subsequently cleaves and develops into a blastocyst comprising the inner cell mass (ICM), which gives rise to the fetus, and the trophectoderm (TE), which contributes to the placenta (8). Key transcription factors (TFs) involved in lineage specification have been identified, including TEAD3/4, GATA3, and CDX2 (TE) (9–11), and SOX2 and POU5F1 (ICM) (12–14). Recent studies have demonstrated the critical role of NR5A2 in the process of early lineage segregation in mouse embryos (15–17). However, the NR5A2 expression is low in human or bovine embryos, raising the intriguing question of whether alternative TFs support morula formation and mediate the transition from EGA to lineage specification in these species.

Advances in ultrasensitive chromatin analysis techniques, such as Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq), have engendered the capacity to discern dynamic and potentially functional regulatory elements that may recruit TFs to orchestrate transcriptional programs during early embryonic development (18–20). Notably, motifs of the SP family, including SP1, are enriched in accessible regulatory regions of bovine and human embryos at the 8-cell to morula stages (19, 20). SP1 is ubiquitously expressed in mammalian cells and regulates gene transcription by binding GC-rich genomic regions through its zinc finger domains, playing crucial roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and metabolism (21–23).

Notably, SP1 expression levels exhibit variation across diverse cell types and developmental stages in mice, indicating its tissue-specific regulatory functions during development (21). For example, SP1 has been shown to regulate ovarian development by activating NOTCH signaling during primordial follicle formation (24). SP1 has also been implicated in the pluripotency regulation, as it mediates PI3K-Protein Kinase B signaling and potentially facilitates the activation of core pluripotency gene networks (25). SP1 is essential for early mouse embryogenesis, and it is required for the expression of Hsp70.1, one of the first genes expressed after fertilization (26). Although SP1 knockout (KO) in mice does not impair blastocyst formation, it leads to multiple developmental defects and results in embryonic lethality around embryonic day 11 (27, 28). Recently, chemical inhibition of SP1 using Mithramycin A was reported to impair bovine blastocyst formation (29). However, Mithramycin A is known to act by binding GC-rich DNA rather than specifically targeting SP1 and may consequently affect other GC-binding zinc-finger transcription factors (30, 31). Therefore, the precise and specific function of SP1 in early bovine and human embryogenesis remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we systematically profiled single-cell transcriptomes and proteomic landscapes across the bovine oocyte-to-embryo transition (OET) to delineate the dynamics and heterogeneity of bovine EGA. Additionally, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of the functions and mechanisms of SP1 in bovine and human embryos. We demonstrate that SP1 promotes the expression of genes responsible for lineage specification by modulating nearby chromatin states and regulates genes related to cell survival and proliferation, thereby ensuring proper morula development in bovine embryos. Beyond its involvement in housekeeping pathways, we further reveal that in human embryos, SP1 directly activates key EGA genes to support the correct onset of EGA. Together, these findings uncover the asynchronous and heterogeneous activation of major EGA in bovine embryos and highlight both conserved and species-specific roles of SP1 in regulating early mammalian development.

Results

Single-Cell Analysis of Bovine Preimplantation Embryos Reveals the Heterogeneity of Major EGA at the 8-Cell Stage.

To obtain high-quality transcriptional profiles of OET in bovine early embryos, we generated 185 single-cell transcriptomes from morphologically stringently selected bovine oocytes and preimplantation embryos, including GV, MII, zygote, 2-cell (2C), 4-cell (4C), early/late 8-cell (E/L8C), and 16-cell (16C) stages (Fig. 1A and Dataset S1). Additionally, previously collected morula (MO) and blastocyst datasets were integrated for downstream analyses (32).

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that cells clustered according to their developmental stage, indicating a significant correlation between gene expression profiles and embryonic progression (Fig. 1B). Distinct transcriptional transitions were observed from 4C to E8C and from E8C to 16C, with E8C to 16C transition showing the most pronounced changes, consistent with the onset of major EGA as previously reported (33). The transition from 4C to E8C involved 582 significantly upregulated genes, which likely reflected the minor wave of EGA (Fig. 1C). Notably, 59.3% of the genes (1,395 out of 2,354) upregulated from E8C to 16C were already upregulated from E8C to L8C (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), suggesting that major EGA primarily initiates at the 8-cell stage.

Notably, prior to L8C stage, blastomeres clustered based on their embryonic identity, whereas the correlation among blastomeres within the same embryo decreased and variability increased at L8C (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B), indicating that bovine EGA begins to exhibit heterogeneity among individual blastomeres at the 8-cell stage. This finding was further supported by examining the expression of identified EGA genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C and Dataset S2) in cells at the 8-16C stage. E8C blastomeres displayed similar expression profiles, characterized by low expression levels of major EGA genes and high expression levels of maternal and minor EGA genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). In contrast, at the L8C stage, major EGA was activated in a subset of blastomeres, accompanied by the degradation of maternal transcripts (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). This asynchrony persisted into the 16C stage, at which some blastomeres still failed to activate major EGA genes, and the number of nonactivated cells varied across embryos. This variability may relate to subsequent developmental potential, which is determined by oocyte quality (34), as embryos can reach the 8-16C stage under transcription inhibition (35).

To further explore the heterogeneous gene expression during EGA, we inferred pseudotime for each cell (36), which aligned with their developmental stage (Fig. 1F), and categorized EGA genes into early-, mid-, and late-activated modules (Fig. 1G; see Materials and Methods). Major EGA genes were enriched for translation-related functions, with a shift from initiation-associated terms (mid) to elongation-associated terms (late), while RNA metabolism, such as RNA splicing, were particularly enriched in the mid-activated module. This finding suggests a gradual establishment of the embryo’s intrinsic transcriptional and translational apparatus. We also observed stepwise activation of mitochondrial programs, transition from mitochondrial inner membrane and oxidative phosphorylation (mid) to mitochondrial matrix (late), consistent with increasing bioenergetic demands at subsequent development (37). Overall, these results support a sequential activation of EGA modules, although the functional significance of this ordering remains to be determined. Notably, several TFs that are absent in oocytes become activated during minor EGA stage, including DUXA, SP1, KLF17, and MYC. Given the progressive activation of transcription during EGA, the early induction of these factors suggests their potential roles in facilitating the subsequent onset of major EGA.

Next, to assess whether lineage inclination emerges during bovine EGA, we examined the coexpression pattern of ICM and TE genes (Fig. 1H and Dataset S2), defined from prior datasets (32). Early lineage genes did not exhibit a clear correlation before blastocyst stage, whereas late lineage genes were positively correlated at 16C and morula stages, and both early and late lineage genes were negatively correlated at the blastocyst stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Consistently, bimodal expression increased from the morula stage for early lineage genes (FDR < 0.05, Dip Test of Unimodality) (Fig. 1I). These results suggest that cells activate late lineage genes in a synchronized manner during EGA. In contrast, the emergence of lineage inclination manifests at the morula stage, predominantly driven by the heterogeneous expression of early lineage genes.

Taken together, these findings indicate that bovine major EGA initiates at the 8C stage and proceeds heterogeneously across blastomeres without clear evidence of lineage inclination, highlighting a regulated yet asynchronous activation process across individual cells.

Dynamic Proteome Distinct from the Transcriptome and Translatome Around Bovine EGA.

To further characterize the molecular landscapes underlying bovine EGA, the relationship between the proteome, translatome, and transcriptome in bovine early embryos was analyzed (Fig. 1A). As previous proteomic studies lacked full-stage coverage and data integration was challenging due to methodological differences (38–40), we generated the most comprehensive proteomic profiles of bovine oocyte and early embryos (41) with high reproducibility across replicates (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). A total of 8,031 proteins that were detected in embryos and present in the translatome (based on ribosome occupied fragment, ROF) and the transcriptome (mRNA) datasets were retained for subsequent analysis (Dataset S3).

We next compared temporal dynamics across proteome, translatome, and transcriptome. PCA and Spearman correlation analyses showed concordant trajectories between transcriptome and translatome, with major transitions during EGA and the first lineage specification (Fig. 1J and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). In contrast, the proteome was relatively stable until morula and shifted dramatically between morula and blastocyst. Consistently, coefficient of variation (CV) analysis revealed that over 80% of genes exhibited dynamic regulation at the transcript and translation levels, whereas only 53.2% of genes displayed dynamic proteins during early development (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Notably, ~71.3% of strictly defined maternal proteins (127 out of 178) with low translation activity after fertilization remained detectable in blastocysts, such as RNF17, MAJIN, and DEUP1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). These results indicate the overall stability of proteome and the slow degradation of maternal proteins, which may contribute to the regulation of early development.

To understand why the proteome differs from the translatome and transcriptome during OET, a systematic comparison of their profiles across developmental stages was then conducted. A high correlation was identified between the translatome and transcriptome throughout the developmental process (Fig. 1K). However, the proteome exhibited a low correlation with both, except at blastocyst stage. Interestingly, although the translatome and transcriptome during EGA and afterward exhibited poor correlation with the proteome at equivalent stages, they demonstrated a strong correlation with the proteome of the blastocyst (Fig. 1L), consistent with delayed protein accumulation for a subset of EGA genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Indeed, when analyzing the changes between consecutive stages, the translatome and transcriptome changes during EGA and afterward often correlated with proteome at one or two subsequent stages (Fig. 1M). This delayed response in protein dynamics may be indicative of delayed protein synthesis and maturation during rapid embryonic development (42), such as EGA, as well as the limited sensitivity of current proteomic detection methods. Overall, these data provide a comprehensive proteomic resource for bovine OET and highlight substantial temporal buffering at the protein level relative to transcriptome and translatome dynamics around EGA.

SP1 Is Essential for Bovine Morula Development.

Next, to identify putative key TFs regulating bovine EGA, we reasoned that such TFs should become transcriptionally activated prior to or during EGA and exert their function through engagement with open chromatin regions. Accordingly, we reanalyzed published ATAC-seq data across developmental stages to assess the enrichment of known TF binding motifs (20). Under these criteria, SP1 emerged as a prominent candidate, as its transcripts increased during minor EGA, and its motif was particularly implicated from major EGA to morula stage (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). As a comparison, the motifs of OCT4/SOX2/NANOG (OSN) were found to be enriched in the ICM and ESC, which is consistent with their dynamic expression.

Evidence from studies of other species, including mouse, human, and zebrafish embryos, suggests that SP1 motifs show consistent enrichment in open chromatin regions during EGA (43–46). This finding provides a compelling rationale for hypothesizing that SP1 may have a potential regulatory function during EGA. To assess whether SP1 is an EGA regulator in bovine embryos, we examined its protein expression pattern during preimplantation embryos. In contrast to the fluctuations in mRNA levels, which reached a peak at the 8C stage and subsequently decreased, SP1 protein was evident from the E8C stage and maintained high levels until the BL stage (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

To ascertain the function of SP1 during early embryonic development, its expression was disrupted through the injection of targeted siRNAs into the zygote. Remarkably, in vitro culture revealed that SP1 knockdown (KD) embryos exhibited severely reduced blastocyst formation, with the majority developing normally to the 8-16C stage by Day 4 but subsequently arrested at the morula stage (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). The depletion of SP1 mRNA and protein was further verified at Day 5 (Fig. 2 D and E, respectively). To exclude the potential off-target effects of SP1 KD, two sgRNAs were designed to mutate SP1 via base editing (BE) in embryos (12), with the edited efficiency being 35.8% and 57.7%, respectively (Fig. 2 F and G). The overall success rate of SP1 editing in bovine embryos was 62.1%. The mutation of SP1 was further confirmed by genotyping, RNA-seq, and IF (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C and Fig. 2 H and I, respectively). Consistently, SP1 KO exhibited a morula arrest within mutant embryos, thus recapitulating the phenotype observed in SP1 KD embryos (Fig. 2J). Taken together, these results suggest that while SP1 may not be a decisive regulator of bovine EGA, it plays a crucial role during the morula to blastocyst transition.

SP1 Is Largely Dispensable for Bovine EGA.

Morphological inspection showed that SP1 KD embryos were indistinguishable from negative controls (NC) and had comparable cell numbers until the early morula stage (Day 5), before compaction (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). To investigate the roles of SP1 in transcriptional regulation during early development, RNA-seq was performed on 8C (Day 3), 16C (Day 4), and early morula (Day 5) stages (Dataset S4). SP1 depletion caused modest transcriptional changes at 8C, but more pronounced changes at MO stage (Fig. 3A). Consistently, only morula-specific gene activation was substantially impaired in the absence of SP1 (Fig. 3B). Moreover, SP1-dependent differentially expressed genes (DEGs) appeared to progressively accumulate, increasing from 26.4% between 8C and 16C stages to 61.0% between 16C and MO stages (Fig. 3C and Dataset S5). PCA further corroborated these findings, showing obvious deviation and developmental delay between SP1 KD and NC embryos at MO stage based on global transcription (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). In contrast, global expression profiles at 8-16C were largely similar between SP1 KD and NC embryos (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C), suggesting that bovine EGA proceeds largely normally in the absence of SP1.

Next, we directly assessed the effect of SP1 loss on EGA using the gene sets defined above. While SP1 KD embryos showed no global change in EGA gene expression at 16C stage, a slight reduction of major EGA genes was observed at 8C stage (Fig. 3E). Notably, 64.2% (43 out of 67) of these downregulated genes were still activated at 16C stage, suggesting that SP1 may contribute to the initiation of major EGA, but this effect appears to be transient and dispensable. Accordingly, only a small fraction of major EGA genes remained dysregulated at 16C (60 down- and 37 up-regulated; 7.5%) (Fig. 3F). Similar results were observed in SP1 KO embryos, which is consistent with the developmental phenotype of SP1 KD and KO embryos, as both developed normally to the 8-16C stage (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S5D).

Thus, we conclude that SP1 contributes to the regulation of a small fraction of EGA genes, but it is not an essential regulator of EGA in cattle. However, it plays a key role during the transition between 16C and morula stage.

Dynamic Binding Pattern of SP1 during Bovine Early Development.

Given the enrichment of the SP1 motif at the 8C stage, yet the subtle impact on EGA genes following SP1 loss, CUT&Tag was subsequently performed to identify the direct targets of SP1 across the genome during bovine early development. Using approximately 500 cells, the SP1 binding profiles in 8C, 16C, and MO embryos were examined. These data were highly reproducible between replicates and exhibited specific enrichment across the genome, which was absent in the SP1 KD embryos (Fig. 4A, SI Appendix, Fig. S6A, and Dataset S6). As a validation, the SP1 motif was identified as the top one enriched motif within its binding peaks at all stages (Fig. 4B).

SP1 binds to numerous genomic sites, with the majority located at promoters (~45.9%, Fig. 4C). Notably, SP1 appeared to cooperate with different factors at promoters and distal regions (Fig. 4B). At promoters, SP1 colocalized with factors that are widely expressed, including NFY and MAZ. In contrast, at distal regions, it colocalized with lineage-specific factors such as TFAP2C (a TE regulator) and OTX2 (an ICM regulator), suggesting its potential roles in stage-specific regulation via distal elements. Additionally, SP1 also colocalized with CTCF, indicating its role in chromatin remodeling (47).

We next investigated the dynamic binding of SP1 during development, which exhibited progressive changes from 8C to MO stage. SP1 predominantly occupied regions that were typically characterized by increased H3K27ac and ATAC signals, along with decreased H3K27me3 signals, suggesting its preference for binding to active regulatory elements (Fig. 4D). Nearly 36.3% of SP1 binding sites were maintained across all stages, with the majority localized at promoters (Fig. 4E). In contrast, dynamic binding regions were more frequently found at distal regions.

As has been demonstrated on numerous occasions, stage-shared SP1 bindings were found to be enriched in the vicinity of genes involved in housekeeping activities including cell division, RNA splicing, DNA repair, and chromatin remodeling (Fig. 4D, “Stable”). In contrast, genes implicated in actin cytoskeleton, in utero embryonic development and postembryonic development were identified in the proximity of SP1 bindings that were progressively acquired during development (Fig. 4D, “Up”). These findings suggest that SP1 progressively participates in the regulation of lineage differentiation. Notably, genes associated with blastocyst development were also linked to stage-shared bindings, suggesting a potential role for SP1 in lineage regulation initiated at 8C stage (Fig. 4D, “Stable”). Collectively, these data demonstrate that high-quality SP1 chromatin binding was obtained in early bovine embryos.

The Function of SP1 as an Activator or Repressor Depends on the Cellular Context.

We then investigated whether SP1 regulates the expression of its targets by primarily focusing on the 16C stage, at which point its impact on the transcriptome had already become evident. It was observed that genes containing a greater number of SP1 motif exhibited a stronger SP1 enrichment at promoter regions (Fig. 4F). Interestingly, an initial increase in gene expression with the number of SP1 motifs was observed, followed by a subsequent decline (Fig. 4G). This pattern was further substantiated by an analysis of the epigenetic states and Pol II enrichment surrounding the promoter of these genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Specifically, as SP1 enrichment increased, H3K27ac (an active marker) levels gradually rose, while H3K27me3 (a repressive marker) levels decreased, leading to open chromatin and higher Pol II enrichment. However, beyond a certain point, the opposite trend was observed, suggesting a dynamic regulatory relationship between SP1 and its targets, which may associate with its enrichment.

Additionally, genes with SP1 binding and SP1 motifs at their promoter did not exhibit obvious expression changes following SP1 depletion (Fig. 4H). This phenomenon is likely attributable to two factors: the majority of SP1 binding targets (5,117 out of 5,350) remained unaffected by the absence of SP1, and SP1 depletion led to both upregulation and downregulation of its target genes (Fig. 4I). Of note, in circumstances where SP1 binding was found to be highly enriched, there was a tendency for genes to be upregulated. Globally, SP1 exhibited similar enrichment at the promoters of both upregulated and downregulated genes, which was higher than that observed for all genes (Fig. 4J). Consistently, a substantial and comparable proportion of upregulated genes (34.2%, 91 out of 266) and downregulated genes (28.2%, 142 out of 503) showed SP1 binding at their promoters. Together, these results suggest that SP1 functions both as a transcriptional activator and a repressor in bovine embryos.

SP1 Facilitates Lineage-Specific Gene Expression by Supporting Chromatin Accessibility.

We next asked whether SP1 directly regulates lineage regulator genes at early stage. First, we examined the enrichment of SP1 near genes specific to ICM and TE. Interestingly, SP1 showed gradually increasing enrichment at the promoter regions of early ICM and early TE genes, but not late lineage genes from 8C to MO stage (Figs. 1H and 5B). Consistently, SP1 motif was more enriched in the promoter of early ICM genes compared to late ICM genes (Fig. 5C), suggesting preferential regulation of early ICM genes. These results are consistent with the function of SP1, which has been demonstrated to mediate the reprogramming transition of primed EpiSCs to naive ESCs (25). By contrast, SP1 motif showed similar enrichment in the promoter of early and late TE genes, suggesting the possibility of stage-specific regulation of TE genes by SP1 during early development. At the transcriptional level, SP1 depletion led to the repression of 18.2% (44 out of 341) early ICM and 27.7% (99 out of 358) early TE genes at the MO stage, with a global repression of early TE genes being evident as early as the 16C stage (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). This finding suggests that while SP1 regulates both ICM and TE lineages, it has a more specific and pronounced role in TE programs, potentially due to the earlier initiation of TE lineage specification (32). As examples, SP1 bound both the pluripotency genes OCT4, MGST3, and early TE genes TEAD3, TAGLN2, both of which were already downregulated in SP1 KD 16C embryos (Fig. 5 E and F). Among these, further verification was conducted to ascertain that the TEAD3 protein, which has been demonstrated to be essential for TE formation (9), was substantially decreased in expression (Fig. 5G).

We next investigated the mechanisms by which SP1 regulates target gene expression. Considering SP1’s role in chromatin remodeling, which is potentially through cooperation with chromatin remodelers (48) (e.g., p300 and HDACs), we examined chromatin state changes following SP1 depletion at the 16C stage (Dataset S6). Globally, H3K27ac and chromatin accessibility at gene-associated regions were largely unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B), suggesting that SP1 may not be essential for the extensive chromatin reprogramming during the 8-16C stage. Focusing on 8034 open chromatin regions bound by SP1 at the 16C stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C), we found that regions near genes that are activated by SP1 were more likely to lose accessibility in SP1 KD embryos, in comparison to regions in the vicinity of repressed genes (Fig. 5H). This direct regulation by SP1 was evident both globally and in genes associated with lineage specification, particularly among positive targets (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Furthermore, H3K27ac consistently decreased near positive SP1 targets in SP1 KD embryos. In contrast, regions in the proximity to repressed targets exhibited a significant increase in H3K27ac levels, which exceeded the alterations in chromatin accessibility. This finding suggests that SP1 may repress gene expression primarily by modulating nearby H3K27ac rather than the control of chromatin accessibility.

Collectively, we conclude that SP1 functions as a crucial transcription factor (TF) activated during minor EGA. It regulates lineage-specific genes by progressively modulating their chromatin accessibility. The dysregulation of these genes instigates a cascade of events that disrupt normal embryonic development, ultimately leading to developmental arrest at the morula stage.

SP1 Deficiency Leads to Defective Human Preimplantation Development.

Finally, to explore the conservation of SP1 function across species, we compared the enrichment of SP1 motif in open chromatin regions during EGA in cattle, human, and mice (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Interestingly, while SP1 motifs were highly enriched at open promoters in mouse embryos (43), they were particularly enriched in distal open chromatin regions during EGA in bovine and human embryos. This preferential enrichment suggests the potential function of SP1 in human embryonic development. According to previous reports (49), SP1 became highly translated at 8C stage in human embryo, although its mRNA was predominantly activated at 4C stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). To further examine SP1 protein expression in human embryos, we performed immunostaining. SP1 was detectable in oocytes and showed increasing expression following fertilization, with strong nuclear localization observed from 1C to the blastocyst stage, consistent with recent proteomic data (41) (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8B).

To determine whether SP1 serves as a specific regulator in human EGA and early development, we knocked down SP1 by microinjecting siRNA into human tripronuclear (3PN) zygote. The high KD efficiency was confirmed by RNA-seq (mRNA drop to 11.6 to 60.6% relative to NC embryos) and the depletion of SP1 protein was also validated by IF (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Subsequently, an evaluation of embryonic development following SP1 KD was conducted, with the assessment parameters comprising the numbers of blastomeres and the morphology of the embryos. Notably, on Day 3, SP1 depletion led to significant developmental delays, as evidenced by a reduction in the proportion of embryos with more than 10 cells and a concomitant increase in proportion of embryos with fewer than 7 cells (Fig. 6 D and E). This delay persisted on Day 4, during which no compaction was observed in SP1 KD embryos, although a subset progressed beyond the 8C stage (Fig. 6 D and E). By Day 5, SP1 KD embryos consistently failed to form blastocysts (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). These results underscore the pivotal function of SP1 in human early development.

Single embryo RNA-seq analysis further revealed that global transcriptomes at 8C stage (D3) became abnormal following SP1 deficiency and showed a clear development lagging compared with NC embryos, positioning between the 4C and 8C stage in the developmental trajectory (Fig. 6F and Dataset S7). Of note, ~59.1% (638 out of 1,080; Dataset S8) major EGA genes did not undergo activation in SP1 KD embryos (Fig. 6 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S8E), including TPRX1/2, ARGFX, DPRX, ZSCAN5B, DPPA2, DUXA, and TFAP2C. Among these, TPRX1/2, ARGFX, and DPRX have been shown to play crucial roles in the proper activation of human major EGA genes (49, 50). These data demonstrate that SP1 is indispensable for major EGA, potentially by activating downstream TFs to initiate the transcriptional burst.

SP1 Regulates Human EGA Gene Activation.

We then investigated how SP1 regulates EGA genes by identifying its binding targets at 8C stage (Fig. 6I and Dataset S9). As validation, the SP1 motif was identified as the most enriched motif within its binding peak and exhibited high reproducibility across two replicates (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 F and G). SP1 exhibited widespread binding across the genome, with ~41.3% of EGA genes (446 out of 1,080) showing SP1 binding at their promoter or potential enhancer regions, as exemplified at TPRX2, DUXA TFAP2C, and CTCF (Fig. 6I). Of note, SP1 binding was preferentially enriched at the promoter of genes that were down- rather than up-regulated upon SP1 KD (Fig. 6J), which was distinct from the observation in bovine embryos. This finding suggests that SP1 primarily functions as a transcriptional activator in human embryos. Distal SP1 peaks were consistently located in the proximity to downregulated genes (Fig. 6K). Moreover, genes exhibiting stronger SP1 enrichment at their promoter regions were more prone to be repressed in SP1 KD embryos (Fig. 6L), thereby reinforcing the function of SP1 in the direct regulation of EGA genes.

To explore the basis for the differential roles of SP1 in cattle and humans, we compared the genes regulated by SP1 during EGA in both species. This comparison identified 3,815 species-shared targets genes (Fig. 6M and SI Appendix, Fig. S8H; accounting for ~44.8% and ~54.2% of regulated genes in human and cattle, respectively). Remarkably, ~73.9% of species-shared genes overlapped with stage-shared SP1 regulated genes in bovine embryos (Fig. 6N). We then performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on both the overlapped genes and species-specific SP1 regulated genes. To focus on the primary function of these genes, we restricted our comparison to the top 100 enriched terms from each of the three groups. This analysis revealed that the overlapped genes were specifically enriched in signaling pathways essential for cell survival and proliferation (51–53), including the MAPK, AMPK, mTOR, and WNT pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S8I). Additionally, terms related to embryonic development and the Hippo signaling pathway were uniquely enriched among the overlapped genes. These findings suggest that SP1 plays an evolutionarily conserved role in regulating fundamental processes required for early embryonic development and the initiation of the first lineage specification, particularly the TE lineage.

Additionally, although the proportion of EGA genes was comparable between the human-specific and species-shared SP1 targets, we found that human-specific SP1 targets genes were more enriched for TFs known to function specifically during human EGA (Fig. 6M), such as ZSCAN4/5B, DUXA, DPPA2, DPRX, and TPRX1/2. In sum, this finding provides a partial explanation for the more critical and specific role of SP1 in human EGA.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analyzed single-cell transcriptomes during bovine OET to profile the sequential activation of genes underlying EGA. Intriguingly, our results revealed that while minor EGA is uniformly initiated at the 4 to 8 cell stage, the onset of major EGA exhibits marked heterogeneity at the L8C stage, consistent with recent single-cell profiling in bovine embryos (54). Previous studies in bovine embryos have demonstrated that successful EGA requires stepwise epigenetic remodeling (20, 55), encompassing the establishment of chromatin accessibility (4C-8C) and subsequent deposition of histone modifications (8C-MO), particularly H3K27ac. Following fertilization, H3K27ac undergoes global erasure and reestablishment and is more severely impaired upon transcriptional inhibition. These findings raise the intriguing possibility that heterogeneity in chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, particularly H3K27ac, may already exist at the 8C stage in bovine embryos and contributes to the asynchronous onset of major EGA. In the context of human 8C embryos, a certain degree of transcriptional heterogeneity has also been observed (56), although this appears to be less pronounced than in cattle.

Notably, even at 16C stage, a small subset of blastomeres still fails to complete major EGA, and the proportion of such blastomeres varies between embryos. This variability may be linked to developmental competence and could help to explain why many bovine embryos tend to arrest before the 16C stage. In addition, consistent with recent evidence in human and mouse embryos (57, 58), we detected several hundred genes upregulated from zygote to 2-cell in bovine embryos, suggesting that immediate EGA (iEGA) after fertilization is a conserved feature across mammals.

The mechanisms by which TFs regulate mammalian EGA and early embryonic development remain to be fully elucidated. In this study, we employed a screening strategy to identify TFs whose motifs are enriched during and after EGA, leading to the identification of SP1 as an essential regulator of preimplantation development in bovine and human embryos, but not in mice. We demonstrated that both SP1 KD and KO embryos arrest at the early morula stage with their transcriptomes abnormally resembling those of the 16C stage. When we were preparing the present manuscript, an independent work also reported that chemical inhibition of SP1 impairs blastocyst formation (29). However, the functional role of SP1 was not further explored in that work. In the present study, the direct binding targets of SP1 were identified and it was further demonstrated that SP1 directly activates the key lineage genes (i.e., OCT4 and TEAD3) by promoting chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac deposition at nearby regulatory regions, thereby linking EGA to the first lineage specification.

In contrast to the finding observed in bovine embryos, SP1 KD significantly compromises human EGA. Mechanistically, SP1 specifically regulates a subset of PRD-like TFs in human, but not bovine embryos, including TPRX1/2 (49) and DPRX (59), whose functions have been implicated in human EGA. Furthermore, SP1 has been observed to activate several markers of human 8C-like cells (60), including DUXA and ZSCAN5B. However, SP1 does not appear to regulate DUX4 (61), suggesting that SP1 may act in parallel with distinct TFs to orchestrate human EGA with corresponding motifs. Of note, siRNA-mediated knockdown cannot fully eliminate maternally stored SP1 protein, thus the specific contribution of maternal SP1 to human EGA remains to be determined.

Moreover, comparative analysis of SP1-regulated genes in both human and bovine embryos revealed that SP1 conservatively regulates fundamental cellular processes, embryonic development, and trophectoderm lineage specification. Recent studies have shown that, despite the absence of SP1 enrichment in all distal open chromatin regions at 2C stage in mice (50), it is highly enriched in distal regions that persist from EGA through to the late blastocyst stage. Notably, Krüppel-like factors (KLFs), a family of zinc-finger proteins closely related to SP factors (23), are also enriched in these regions, suggesting a potential role for the SP/KLF family members in early mouse development. In accordance with this observation, Sp1 KO mice embryos remain viable until Day 11 (27), whereas KO of Klf17 leads to impaired EGA (62). In consideration of the highly conserved C-terminus triple-C2H2 DNA-binding domain (DBD) of SP/KLF proteins in mammals (63), we propose a model in which the SP/KLF family factors act as a conserved regulatory axis that supports EGA (Fig. 7), operating partly in parallel with the PRD-like TFs network (49, 59, 64). Based on distinct chromatin features and motif density, SP1 binding sites exhibit distinct fast and slow binding dynamics, which are preferentially enriched at proximal and distal regulatory regions, respectively. KLF proteins are more frequently enriched at sites exhibiting slow SP1 binding dynamics, indicating that although SP and KLF factors recognize highly similar motifs, they retain distinct binding preferences and engage in complex cooperative regulatory relationships. Further research is warranted to identify the specific SP/KLF members that initiate the cascade of transcription in individual species and to elucidate their mechanisms of action during EGA. Such insights will advance our understanding of the species-specific regulatory strategies underlying EGA.

SP1 as a regulator of EGA and first lineage specification during mammalian embryogenesis. A model illustrating the conserved yet species-specific function of SP1 in early mammalian embryogenesis. SP1 is activated in minor EGA, and loss of SP1 leads to morula arrest in both bovine and human embryos. In bovine embryos, the activation of major EGA exhibits marked heterogeneity at the L8C stage. SP1 promotes the expression of TE genes such as TEAD3 and TAGLN2, as well as ICM genes such as OCT4 and DISP3, during bovine 16C to morula stages via the regulation of nearby H3K27ac. In human embryos, SP1 directly targets key EGA genes such as TPRX1/2, DUXA, TFAP2C, and CTCF at the 8C stage, orchestrating the proper onset of EGA.

Materials and Methods

All experiments involving human embryos were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (2022KY-354) and the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University (2025–0259). Human triploid embryos (3PN) were donated with informed consent from women undergoing IVF treatment. Bovine embryos were produced in vitro through standard in vitro maturation, fertilization, and culture procedures. Embryos at defined developmental stages were collected according to standard timing criteria, and morphologically normal embryos were selected for downstream analyses.

Gene perturbation experiments were performed in early embryos using siRNA-mediated knockdown or CBE-mediated gene KO. Immunofluorescence staining was conducted using standard protocols, and images were acquired using Nikon epifluorescence or Zeiss confocal microscopy systems. Transcriptomic, epigenomic, and chromatin accessibility profiling were performed using single-cell or low-input RNA-seq (32), CUT&Tag, ATAC-seq (19), and ULI-NChIP-seq (65) following established protocols with minor modifications. The preparation and analysis for LC-MS was carried out as previously published workflow with modification (41). RNA-seq data were processed using HISAT2 (66), FeatureCounts (67), and StringTie (68), with differential expression analysis performed using DESeq2 (69). CUT&Tag, ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq data were analyzed using Bowtie2 (70), MACS2 (71), deepTools (72), and BEDTools (73). Motif and peak annotation analyses were conducted using Homer (74) and ChIPseeker (75). Statistical analyses were performed using R or GraphPad Prism. Additional experimental and analytical details are provided in SI Appendix.
