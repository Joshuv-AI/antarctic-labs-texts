Phytic acid-loaded polyvinyl alcohol hydrogel promotes wound healing of injured corneal epithelium through inhibiting ferroptosis
Authors: Danni Gong, Nianxuan Wu, Huan Chen, Weijie Zhang, Chenxi Yan, Chunlei Zhang, Yao Fu, Hao Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As the important barrier of intraocular tissue, cornea is easy to suffer various kinds of injuries. Among them, acute alkali burn is a thorny ophthalmic emergency event, which can lead to corneal persistent epithelial defects, ulcers, and even perforation. Ferroptosis, a mode of regulatory cell death, has been found to play a key role in the process of corneal alkali burn, of which lipid peroxidation and intracellular iron levels are considered to be the possible therapeutic targets. To seek new effective treatments, the study herein focused on the occurrence of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in corneal alkali burn, exploring the role of phytic acid (PA), a natural small molecule with both antioxidant and iron chelating capacity, in the repair of corneal epithelial injury. The in vivo therapeutic results showed that PA eyedrops treatment promoted the recovery of corneal morphology and function, and in vitro experiments proved that PA prompted the repair of oxidative stress induced-corneal epithelial injury through ferroptosis inhibition. In addition, better drug treatment effect could be achieved through hydrogel delivery and sustained release, and our in vivo experiments showed the superior therapeutic effects of PA delivered by PVA hydrogels with larger molecular weights on corneal injury. In summary, this study demonstrated the excellent effect of natural small molecule PA with antioxidant and high efficiency chelating ferrous ions on ferroptosis inhibition, and showed the outstanding application prospect of PVA/PA hydrogels in the treatment of corneal epithelial injury.

Introduction

Nowadays, a variety of programmed cell death modes with unique biological processes and pathophysiological characteristics have been demonstrated, such as necrotic apoptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis [[1], [2], [3]]. As a new mode of regulatory cell death, ferroptosis is characterized by iron dependence and accumulation of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS), which mainly depends on iron-mediated oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation mediated cytotoxicity [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. In eye diseases, ferroptosis has been shown to be an important pathogenesis of age-related macular degeneration, Fuchs’ corneal endothelial dystrophy and other diseases [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Lipid peroxidation and intracellular iron levels have been considered to be the possible therapeutic targets for ferroptosis-related diseases. Corneal alkali burn is a tricky clinical problem worldwide, which can lead to permanent visual impairment and even blindness. As soon as alkaline chemicals penetrate into corneal tissue causing severe damages, oxidative stress occurs in the cornea, characterized by increased ROS production [[12], [13], [14]]. Excessive levels of ROS are closely related to cell death, inflammation and apoptosis, and corneal oxidative stress induced by alkali burn is usually accompanied by excessive corneal inflammation, neovascularization, and opaque scar formation [13,15]. It has been reported that ferroptosis plays a key role in the process of corneal alkali-burn injury [16,17]. As iron is a pro-oxidant, the overload of intracellular iron content results in the excessive generation of free radicals and the excessive triggering of oxidative stress. Iron overload significantly intersects with lipid metabolism, which may induce a pro-lipogenic environment and deplete essential long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids, thus contributing to the advancement and exacerbation of various diseases including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [18]. To address pathologies associated with iron overload, antioxidants emerge as a viable solution to mitigate the oxidative stress triggered by excess iron [19]. Additionally, the current studies frequently employ iron chelating agents to eliminate excess iron before it inflicts harm, such as deferoxamine, deferiprone and deferasirox. However, certain deficiencies like insufficient efficacy or toxic side effects of above drugs [20] call for searching for a multifunctional therapeutic method that can effectively remove excess iron while playing an antioxidant role, thus to inhibit the progression of ferroptosis.

Phytic acid (PA), also known as inositol hexaphosphate, is a natural antioxidant existing in leguminous foods and medicinal plants [20,21]. PA has a variety of beneficial effects on human body, including resistance to oxidation and apoptosis, enhancement of immune and anti-inflammatory activities [22,23]. In addition, PA molecule can chelate metal cations such as iron, calcium, magnesium, copper and zinc to form tightly bound soluble phytates [24]. Therefore, PA is also a natural iron chelating agent, which can inhibit the iron-catalyzed oxidation process through the chelating removal of overload iron, thus playing an important role in organ protection and the treatment of a variety of diseases such as thalassemia, Parkinson's disease and cancer [20,23,25,26]. As a natural small molecule with both superior antioxidant and iron chelating ability, PA has not been thoroughly studied as a ferroptosis inhibitor, and its application in ferroptosis-related diseases, especially in injuries or diseases of ocular surface, is still to be developed.

Hydrogels for drug delivery to ocular surface are currently being extensively investigated. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is a synthetic polymer widely used in the medical field, the physical, chemical and mechanical properties of which are determined by the amount of hydroxylation [27]. In the field of ophthalmology, PVA can not only be used as an artificial tear to improve dry eyes, but also function as a thickener to increase the viscosity of eyedrops, thereby extending the retention time of drugs on the ocular surface [[28], [29], [30]]. In addition, PVA hydrogels have been widely used in ophthalmic implants, contact lenses and corneal regenerative medicine due to their light transmission capacity, high moisture content, good biocompatibility and adequate mechanical properties [[31], [32], [33]].

This study aimed to investigate the reparative impact of PA on injured corneas, explore its potential as a ferroptosis inhibitor in the treatment of ferroptosis-related diseases, and further assess the feasibility of PVA hydrogels for the delivery and sustained release of PA, with the goal of optimizing therapeutic outcomes. This study elucidated the repair effect of natural PA on injured cornea through in vivo and in vitro experiments, and further explored the important function of PA in cornea protection through inhibiting ferroptosis. Furthermore, our research revealed the optimal molecular weight of PVA hydrogels for PA delivery and release to achieve the best therapeutic effect. While developing a practical and effective therapeutic method for clinical acute alkali burn, this study explored a new natural small molecule drug targeting the inhibition of ferroptosis to treat corneal injury.

Materials and methods

Corneal alkali-burn model

All animal experiments with mice were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ninth People's Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and were conformed to the Chinese Animal Administration and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Healthy 8-week-old female BALB/c mice were used in this study, and the establishment of corneal alkali burn model was conducted as described previously [34,35]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and received ophthalmic local anesthetic, then a round 2-mm-diameter Whatman III filter paper presoaked with 1 N NaOH (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was placed on the central cornea of the right eye for 30 s. Afterwards, the wound surface was extensively rinsed with PBS for 1 min.

Murine grouping and treatment

To investigate the therapeutic effect of PA on the alkali-burned corneas, the mice were randomly divided into Normal group, Alkali Burn group (alkali-burned corneas with PBS treatment) and +PA group (alkali-burned corneas with PA treatment). The PA solution was diluted to a concentration of 15 mM with PBS, and the pH value was adjusted to neutral. The alkali-burned mice received PBS or PA eyedrop administration three times a day for 8 consecutive days.

In order to further explore the repair effect of PA delivered by PVA hydrogels with different molecular weights (average molecular weight of 12000, 31000, and 68000 g/mol, henceforth referred to as PVA-12k, PVA-31k, and PVA-68k, respectively) on the injured corneas, the mice were divided into five subgroups. Apart from Normal group, the alkali-burned mice were randomly grouped into Alkali Burn, +PVA-12k/PA, +PVA-31k/PA and +PVA-68k/PA groups. PVA powders with different molecular weights were prepared into 10 w/v% hydrogels, which were mixed with PA adjusted to neutral (10: 1 vol ratio) to prepare the PVA/PA hydrogels for treatment in each group. The hydrogel administration was conducted once a day for 8 consecutive days.

After screening the PVA/PA hydrogel that could achieve the optimal therapeutic effect, the repair effect of hydrogel was compared to the simple PA treatment. In addition to Normal group, the mice were assigned into Alkali Burn group or treatment groups with PA, PVA or PVA/PA administration, respectively. The alkali-burned mice in +PA group received PA eyedrops treatment three times a day, while the others received PVA or PVA/PA hydrogels administration once a day.

Clinical examinations

Clinical evaluations were conducted at day 0, 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 after the alkali burn for each group and the investigators were blinded to the group division during scoring and analysis. The corneal epithelial defects shown by fluorescein staining were evaluated using 2 % fluorescence sodium (Sigma-Aldrich) under cobalt blue light at the indicated timepoints. Corneal fluorescence grading was evaluated and analyzed in according to the National Eye Institute/Industry grading scale. The corneal opacity of each group was examined and photographed by Slit-lamp microscope (SL-D7, Topcon, Togko, Japan). The evaluation of corneal edema scores was conducted in accordance with a scale from 0 to 4: 0, completely clear; 1, slightly hazy, iris, pupils easily visible; 2, slightly opaque, iris, pupils still detectable; 3, severely opaque, iris, pupils hardly visible; and 4, completely opaque, with no view of the iris and/or pupils [36]. Afterwards, the corneal thicknesses in each group were observed and recorded by anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Germany) at each examination timepoint.

Histological examinations

The mice were ethically sacrificed at selected timepoints and the enucleated eyeballs were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and then paraffin-embedded. Serial 4-μm-thick sections were cut and stained with H&E or Masson's trichrome (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Immunofluorescent staining was performed in frozen tissue sections. In brief, the sections were treated with 0.3 % Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min, followed by 5 % donkey serum (Sigma-Aldrich) blocking for 1 h at RT. Afterwards, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies of CK12 (Abcam, San Francisco, CA), E-cadherin (Abcam), ZO-1 (Abcam), Ki67 (Abcam), δNp63 (Abcam), GPX4 (Proteintech, China) and COX2 (Abclonal, China) at 4 °C overnight and then with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at RT for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Then the stained slides were viewed and photographed using the fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse 80i; Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). As for the analysis of staining results, Image J was used to analyze the corneal epithelial and stromal thickness, as well as the fluorescence intensity of target proteins.

Isolation and culture of limbal epithelial cells (LECs)

Human limbal tissues from donors were collected under the approval of the Eye Bank and Medical Ethics Committee of the Ninth People's Hospital affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and were obtained after the central corneal button removed for clinical corneal transplantation. The isolation and culture protocol of primary cells was conducted as previously described [37]. Briefly, the tissue was treated with 10 mg/mL Dispase Ⅱ (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C for 8 h, and then the epithelial cell sheets were separated by ophthalmic forceps and digested by TrypLE at 37 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, the single-cell suspensions were centrifuged and re-suspended in DMEM/F12 medium (Invitrogen) containing 10 % FBS, 1 % insulin-transferrin-selenium (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA), 1 % non-essential amino acids (Life Technologies), 0.4 μg/mL hydrocortisone (Wako, Osaka, Japan), 2 mM l-glutamine (Life Technologies), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (Life Technologies), and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic. The cells were seeded and the medium was changed every 2 days. When reaching 80 %–90 % confluence, the cells were subcultured by TrypLE, and the first to third passages were used for following research.

In this research, LECs were divided into Control, tBH, PA, tBH + PA, Fer-1 and tBH + Fer-1 groups. Cells of PA and Fer-1 groups were treated with medium containing 50 μM PA or 50 μM Ferrostation-1 (Fer-1) for 8 h, respectively. Meanwhile, medium containing 500 μM tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBH) was applied to treat cells in the tBH group for 6 h; cells of tBH + PA group and tBH + Fer-1 group were pretreated with 50 μM PA or 50 μM Fer-1 for 2 h, and then 500 μM tBH was added to the medium for further treatment for 6 h.

RNA isolation and Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from different subsets of LECs by using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The concentration and purity of the total RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer with NanoDrop 2000 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Afterwards, the reverse transcription of 2000 ng RNA was performed using PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 7500 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied to carry out PCR detection using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster, CA) and the relative expression of target genes was determined through the 2-ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences used in the research are shown in Table S1.

Live/dead staining

The cell viability of each group was detected using Live/dead Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. A number of 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded into 24-well plates and the cells were divided into six groups of Control, tBH, PA, tBH + PA, Fer-1, tBH + Fer-1 group. When the cell density reached about 80 % confluence, LECs were treated with PA, Fer-1 and tBH, respectively. After treatment, the cells were washed in PBS and then 200 μL of calcein AM (2 μM)/PI (8 μM) staining working solution was added to each well for incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with PBS twice and then observed and photographed with a fluorescent microscope.

In addition, the biocompatibilities of PVA hydrogels with different molecular weights were detected by live/dead staining. Briefly, the LECs were seeded into 24-well plates containing trans-well inserts (Millipore, Billerica, USA) and divided into PVA-12k, PVA-31k and PVA-68k groups. After culturing overnight, the medium was replaced and fresh medium with 50 μL hydrogels was added to the upper chamber. After incubation of 24 h, the cells were rinsed with PBS twice and the staining and observation procedures were employed as above mentioned.

Detection of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels in LECs of each group were measured by incubating with the redox-sensitive fluorescent dye 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). LECs of 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured. After stimulation, the cells were rinsed with PBS and then treated with 200 μL DMEM/F12 medium containing 10 μM DCFH-DA for 20 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with PBS three times, and then viewed and photographed with a fluorescent microscope.

Detection of intracellular lipid peroxides

The content of intracellular lipid peroxides was determined by Liperfluo Kit (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan), a fluorescent probe for lipid peroxides. The Liperfluo reagent was diluted with DMEM/F12 medium to 10 μM staining working solution. LECs of 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured. After the cells treated accordingly, 200 μL fluorescent probe working solution was added to each well for incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Then the cells were rinsed with PBS and observed with a fluorescent microscope.

Detection of intracellular ferrous ions

Intracellular ferrous ion content was detected by FerroOrange Kit (Dojindo Laboratories), a fluorescent probe especially for ferrous ions. The FerroOrange reagent was diluted to 1 μM for use. A number of 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded and cultured in 24-well plates, and then treated separately according to the grouping. After rinsing the cells with PBS, 200 μL fluorescent probe working solution was added to each well. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and immediately observed under a fluorescent microscope without rinsing.

Immunocytochemical staining

The LECs were seeded into 24-well plates and treated with drugs according to the grouping when they were cultured to 80 % confluency. When treatment completed, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by 0.3 % Triton X-100 permeabilizing for 15 min and 5 % donkey serum blocking for 1 h at RT. Then the cells were incubated with primary antibodies of Claudin-1 (Abcam), E-cadherin (Abcam), ZO-1 (Abcam), Ki67 (Abcam) or δNp63 (Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by 1:400 dilution of Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies incubating for 1 h at RT. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The stained cells were viewed and photographed using the fluorescence microscope, and Image J was used to analyze the expression of target proteins.

Western blot analysis

Total protein lysates were extracted from subgroups of LECs using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, China). Protein concentration in the supernatants was detected by Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis and then transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). The PVDF membranes were then blocked with 5 % nonfat milk dissolved in TBS buffer for 1 h at RT. Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies of GPX4 (Proteintech), NRF2 (Proteintech), SLC7A11 (Abclonal), HO-1 (Abclonal) or β-actin (Abclonal) at 4 °C overnight, followed by fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich) incubating for 1 h at RT. The protein bands were scanned by an Odyssey V 3.0 image scanner (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NB, USA).

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) experiment

The ultrastructure characterization of mitochondria in LECs or corneal tissues was observed using TEM (Hitachi H-7650, Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, the freshly harvested tissues or collected cells (about 1 × 107 cells) were double-fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde and 1 % osmic acid solution, then gradient dehydration and infiltration embedding were performed. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and then observed by TEM.

Observation of surface morphologies of hydrogels

The surface morphologies of PVA hydrogels with different molecular weights were observed and photographed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8100, Tokyo, Japan). The sample preparation process was divided into freezing fixation, freezing fracture, sublimation and conductive spraying. Firstly, the hydrogel samples were frozen and fixed in liquid nitrogen at −210 °C, following which the vitrified samples were broken under vacuum and low temperature by a special device to expose the fresh sections. Then the samples were sublimed for 10 min and conductive spraying was carried out. At this point, the samples could be placed on the cold table of SEM through the freezing transmission system for observation and shooting.

Mechanical properties determination of PVA hydrogels

The rheological properties of PVA hydrogels with different molecular weights were measured on a rheometer (Anton Paar MCR 302, Austria). Firstly, the viscosity of the hydrogel was measured as the temperature increased from 4 °C to 50 °C at the fixed sheer rate of 50 s−1. Then the variations in the complex viscosity, stress properties, G′ and G″ of the hydrogels were measured at a strain sweep (strain: 0.1 %–1000 %, frequency: 1 Hz), respectively. Furthermore, frequency sweep experiments were carried out in a frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s at 25 °C and 0.5 % strain.

In vitro release of PA

The release of PA from PVA hydrogels was detected by 24-well plates containing trans-well inserts. With 100 μL PVA/PA hydrogels added to the upper chamber and 500 μL PBS added to the lower chamber, the plates were placed at 37 °C. The PBS in the lower chamber was replaced with fresh PBS every 24 h, and then PA concentration was analyzed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard error of mean (S.E.M.). Depending on the grouping, the data were analyzed using Student's t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc analysis Tukey test or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test. Statistical analysis and graphical presentation were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). A p value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

PA promotes corneal wound healing after acute alkali burn

As the schematic diagram shown in Fig. 1A, an alkali burn model was constructed to examine the therapeutic efficacy of PA to heal the injured corneas. After consecutive examinations, the sodium fluorescein-staining images showed that the repair rate of corneal epithelial defects was faster in +PA group than that of Alkali Burn group (Fig. 1B and C). Bright-field corneal images revealed that PA treatment promoted the recovery speed of corneal transparency (Fig. 1D and E). In addition, the examination and measurement results of AS-OCT showed that corneas in the Alkali Burn group were significantly thicker than those in the +PA group on day 1, 2 and 4. It was demonstrated that corneas treated with PA generally returned to normal thickness more quickly (Fig. 1F and G). The above results indicated that PA eyedrop treatment could significantly accelerate the recovery of alkali-burned corneas, and we took the corneal tissues on day 4 after injury for H&E and Masson's trichrome staining. The results showed that compared with normal corneas, the corneal epithelium in Alkali Burn group was thinner, the corneal stroma was loose and the collagen fibers were irregularly arranged. PA enhances functional recovery of alkali-burned corneas

To further investigate the cellular and molecular differences in each group, immunostaining analyses were performed on the corneal sections taken on day 4 to elucidate the expression of relevant corneal epithelial functional indicators. The staining results of CK12, the corneal-specific epithelial keratin, demonstrated that PA eyedrops have an excellent effect on promoting corneal epithelial regeneration, as the significant weaker expression of CK12 fluorescence intensity in corneal epithelia of the Alkali Burn group (Fig. 2A and F). The recovery of epithelial barrier function was then detected, and results stemming from the immunostainings of E-cadherin and ZO-1 revealed that PA eyedrops treatment could substantially promote the effective restoration of injured epithelial barrier function (Fig. 2B, C, G, H). Next, the specific marker Ki67 for proliferation capacity was detected, and the greater number of Ki67-positive cells in the +PA group reflected the better proliferation characteristic of epithelial cells compared to those in the Alkali Burn group (Fig. 2D and I). Moreover, stemness characteristic of corneal epithelial cells was detected based on the staining results of δNp63, which showed that PA eyedrops treatment could improve the accumulation of δNp63-positive cells to a certain extent in the alkali-injured corneal epithelia (Fig. 2E and J). Of further interest, the morphological structures of mitochondria in the corneal epithelial cells of each group were observed by TEM. Compared to the Normal group, regular mitochondria could be rarely observed in the corneal epithelia of Alkali Burn group, mitochondrial structures were seriously damaged with mitochondrial ridges decreased or disappeared. PA effectively maintains the cellular viability and function in vitro under oxidative stress

Given that PA played a crucial role in the in vivo corneal injury repair and could improve the corneal epithelial mitochondrial damage caused by alkali burn, this study was prompted to further reveal the internal damage mechanism of corneal epithelial cells under oxidative stress and the key regulatory role of PA through in vitro experiments. The effects of PA on ROS levels reflecting the degree of oxidative stress in LECs were firstly examined. Either PA or Fer-1 could effectively inhibit the increase of ROS1 mRNA expression levels caused by tBH treatment (Fig. 3A). Moreover, intracellular ROS levels measured by the redox-sensitive fluorescent dye revealed that either PA or Fer-1 pretreatment could inhibit the generation of ROS (Fig. 3B and C). Next, stemming from the live/dead staining results, we found that tBH treatment could lead to a large number of cell death, while either PA or Fer-1 effectively inhibited the cell death caused by oxidative damage (Fig. 3D and E). Furthermore, it was shown that PA and Fer-1 were beneficial to the maintenance of intercellular barrier function damaged by oxidative stress, with superior expression of Claudin-1, E-cadherin and ZO-1 to that in tBH group (Fig. 3F–I). Subsequently, stemness and proliferation characteristics of LECs were evaluated. We found that PA could help to maintain the stemness of injured-LECs, which was made evident by the larger number of δNp63-positive cells in tBH + PA group compared to that in tBH group (Fig. 3J and K). Compared with the other two hydrogels, PVA-68k hydrogels had the largest pore sizes (Fig. 5C). Next, the rheological properties of the three hydrogels were monitored by a rheometer. As the temperature increased from 4 °C to 50 °C, the viscosities of the hydrogels decreased gradually, and the viscosity of PVA-68k hydrogels was the highest among the three hydrogels, which was 1102.9 mPa·s at 4 °C (Fig. 5D). Discussion

It is well known that the excessive formation of ROS under oxidative stress can promote the occurrence and development of a variety of diseases and damage processes. Corneal oxidative stress induced by alkali burn promotes the progression of corneal tissue injury, and ferroptosis plays an important role in the regulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory response [4,17,42,43]. As a mode of iron-dependent cell death, ferroptosis is a therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of many diseases, in which iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation signal transduction are considered to be the key factors [44]. Herein, this research explored PA, a natural antioxidant and iron chelating agent derived from natural plants, to reveal its intrinsic relationship with ferroptosis and its therapeutic potential for corneal alkali burn as a ferroptosis inhibitor. We innovatively applied PA to the repair of injured corneas, the clinical examinations and histological investigations revealed that PA treatment could achieve multi-dimensional recovery of epithelial integrity, transparency and thickness in alkali-burned corneas, and also promote the repair of corneal morphology and epithelial functions (Fig. 1; Fig. 2A–J). As excessive ROS produced under alkali-burn injury conditions can cause serious damage to the structure of mitochondria leading to the homeostasis imbalance of cells [45,46], our TEM examination results of corneal epithelial mitochondria demonstrated that PA was conducive to the maintenance of mitochondrial integrity (Fig. 2K). Based on the in vivo experimental results, as well as the natural oxidative stress protection and free iron chelating ability of PA, further in vitro research was employed to explore the internal mechanism of PA's role in damage repair. To simulate the injury environment in corneal epithelium under alkali-burn conditions, tBH was applied to induce cell death in a manner characterized by ferroptosis [47,48], and ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was used as the control drug which can inhibit the formation of lipid peroxides and reverse mitochondrial dysfunction [5,49,50]. Our results showed that PA could effectively reduce the increase of ROS, alleviate cell death and damage of cellular functions caused by oxidative stress (Fig. 3). It has been reported that the antioxidant and lipid-lowering potential of PA is due to its protective effect of inhibiting ROS production [51]. Studies related to liver injury have shown that chronic iron overload-mediated lipid peroxidation will lead to cellular damage, and PA can function as an effective lipid peroxidation inhibitor [20]. In this study, it was proved that PA can effectively inhibit the overproduction of lipid peroxides and free ferrous ions under oxidative stress conditions, while restoring the volume and morphology of intracellular mitochondria (Fig. 4A–F). In addition, investigations on GPX4, which acts as a key regulator of ferroptosis through the removal of lipid peroxides [42], confirmed the effective role of PA in ferroptosis inhibition (Fig. 4G and H). As ferroptosis is dependent on iron-mediated oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation-mediated cytotoxic effects, the relief of intracellular iron overload and the maintenance of normal lipid peroxide levels under the effect of PA demonstrated its effectiveness in inhibiting ferroptosis. Moreover, it has been reported that HO-1 serves as an inducible enzyme and a measurable indicator of oxidative stress [[52], [53], [54]], with its expression being controlled by the transcription factor NRF2 [55]. NRF2 is a key regulatory protein that senses oxidative and electrophilic stress, controlling a suite of genes that participate in the iron metabolic process [56]. However, the persistent activation of NRF2 can lead to disruptions in iron homeostasis. It has been documented that NRF2, upon its translocation to the nucleus, is capable of interacting with SLC7A11 to initiate the transcription of the target gene HO-1 in cells undergoing ferroptosis. This interaction is a critical regulatory mechanism in the response to oxidative stress associated with ferroptosis in cancer cells [57]. Additionally, HO-1-regulated ferroptosis, controlled by the NRF2/SLC7A11/HO-1 cascade, has been verified in the retinal pigment epithelium oxidative stress model, which may be an effective target for retinal degenerative diseases prevention [40]. In our study, changes in the expression level of the above pathway demonstrated the activation of ferroptosis under oxidative stress-induced conditions and the inhibitory effect of PA treatment (Fig. 4I–M).

Proving that PA eyedrops played a vital role in the repair of injured corneas by inhibiting ferroptosis, it was concerned that using eyedrops as the route of administration had certain disadvantages of high loss rate, short retention time, and repeated treatment, which resulting in the weakening of drug efficacy. In order to achieve better delivery and release of PA on the ocular surface, this study used PVA, which is widely used in the field of ocular medicine, to prepare hydrogel as the delivery carrier of PA. Since PVA's molecular weight increases with the increase of polymerization degree of vinyl alcohol monomers, the hydrogels prepared by PVA with different molecular weights (PVA-12k, PVA-31k, PVA-68k) were compared in this study. It was demonstrated that PVA hydrogels have good biocompatibility, and hydrogels of high polymerization had better rheological properties and drug encapsulation ability (Fig. 5; Fig. 6A and B). Further in vivo experiments proved that PVA-68k/PA hydrogels had the optimal efficacy in promoting the multi-dimensional recovery of injured corneas (Fig. 6, Fig. 7). What's more, the detection of ferroptosis related indicators COX2 and GPX4 in the corneal epithelium emphasized the role of PVA/PA hydrogels in promoting corneal injury repair through ferroptosis inhibition (Fig. 7J–M). While current clinical treatments for corneal alkali injury mainly aim to ameliorate excessive inflammation or promote corneal epithelial repair, our PVA/PA hydrogel system provided targeted treatment for the occurrence of ferroptosis in corneas and achieved good therapeutic effects. As a natural product, PA had good biocompatibility and low toxicity, which could avoid the toxicity and side effects that may be caused by the long-term use of some existing ferroptosis inhibitors. In addition, PA's iron chelation ability could reduce the participation of iron ions in oxidative stress and the formation of free radicals, which could fundamentally inhibit the damage resulted from ferroptosis. On this basis, the application of PVA hydrogels with drug delivery advantages was able to extend the retention time of PA on the ocular surface without affecting its function, thus to achieve better therapeutic results. We believe that PA holds promise for clinical application as an inhibitor of ferroptosis, thereby offering tissue-protective benefits. Beyond its role as an ingredient in eyedrops or the PVA hydrogel-based delivery system presented in our study, PA has the potential to be integrated with a variety of carrier systems. It is not only poised for use in the reparative treatment of corneas damaged by alkali burns, but also holds the prospect of emerging as a therapeutic agent for eye conditions and other diseases intricately linked to ferroptosis. For instance, ferroptosis is intimately related to aging and has been implicated in diseases including age-related neurodegeneration and age-related macular degeneration [58,59], and the potential use of PA to mitigate damage warrants further exploration. Moreover, the presence of ferroptosis in the aging cornea and the therapeutic role PA could play in it are worth deeper investigation in future studies. Thus, PA and its delivery systems offers a vast landscape for research and development to fully reveal the therapeutic potential of PA in various clinical applications.

In conclusion, we demonstrated the therapeutic potential of PVA/PA hydrogels in a murine model of acute corneal alkali burn. In addition, we provided further evidence on the underlying molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis responsible for corneal injury and on the potential therapeutic capacity of PA. As indicated in Fig. 8, we believe that PVA/PA hydrogels, which combined the advantages of PA and PVA, offers an ideal alternative for the treatment of corneal injury by inhibiting ferroptosis.
