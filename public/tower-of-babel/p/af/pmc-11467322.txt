Unveiling the impact of school organizational justice on students’ professional commitment through academic stress mediation
Authors: Wuwen Zhang, Wang Yan, Peng Jin, Yaoyang Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately understanding the critical elements influencing college students’ professional commitment is both an urgent practical requirement and a critical theoretical proposition in today’s intensely competitive job market. In order to provide new light on career development in the context of higher education, this study intends to investigate the complex interactions among college students between perceived organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment. Using descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, regression analysis, and structural equation modeling, we carried out a thorough investigation of H University’s full-time undergraduate students in Central China in order to thoroughly examine our hypothesis. The results show that students’ professional commitment is much lower when they view organizational justice to be lacking, and that there is a partial mediating role for academic stress in this relationship. Interestingly, organizational justice has a positive correlation with professional commitment and a negative correlation with academic stress. These findings provide educators and policymakers with insightful information, indicating that, in an increasingly competitive labor market, professional commitment is largely dependent on students’ ability to manage their academic stress and develop a sense of organizational justice. This study adds a new understanding of career development in higher education settings by merging professional commitment research with organizational justice theory. It also has significant implications for educational practices and career support measures.Keywords: Organizational justice, Academic stress, Students’ professional commitment, Influence mechanismSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

The significance of organisational justice has become apparent in determining how students’ academic and professional paths are shaped in the ever-changing context of higher education, where they must navigate an array of opportunities and obstacles1,2. The crucial importance of ensuring fair and equitable treatment for all students has been increasingly acknowledged by universities in their aim of creating settings that promote personal growth, academic performance, and job readiness3,4. Organizational justice, a complex and multifaceted construct, encompasses three distinct dimensions: distributive, procedural, and interactional justice. This concept has captured the attention of scholars, sparking extensive research aimed at unraveling its intricate influence on student attitudes, behaviors, and outcomes1,5–7. The significance of organizational justice in the realm of higher education cannot be overstated. It serves as a fundamental building block for creating an inclusive and supportive academic environment that nurtures students’ potential and enables them to thrive both personally and professionally8. When students perceive their educational institution as fair and just, they are more likely to engage actively in their studies, develop a strong sense of belonging, and cultivate lasting connections with their alma mater9,10. Conversely, perceived injustices can erode trust, hamper motivation, and lead to disengagement, ultimately hindering students’ academic progress and overall well-being11,12.

The concept of “professional commitment” originates from the studies on organizational commitment and occupational commitment. Building upon the research on organizational commitment, scholars have conducted in-depth and extensive investigations. For instance, while studying occupational commitment in 1990, Allen proposed that it encompasses a sense of belonging to the career, a sense of obligation to exert effort for the career, and a willingness to maintain occupational identity continuously13. CHEN expanded the scope of occupational commitment to professional commitment, defining it as the acceptance and confident belief in chosen majors, coupled with the active dedication of more effort by professionals. Consequently, professionals retain or even strongly desire membership in their field14. In light of the pressing need to address the phenomenon of students changing their majors or experiencing burnout, it is crucial to accurately examine the influence of a school’s organizational justice on students’ professional commitment and unveil its underlying mechanisms. This understanding will facilitate the construction of a homogeneous emotional community among students, their university, and their majors, ultimately helping more students enhance their professional recognition.In China, the study of professional commitment has a relatively short history, with the number of related studies on university students’ professional commitment showing an upward trend after 2005. Scholars have proposed detailed and systematic definitions of professional commitment from various perspectives. LIAN et al. advocated the “attitude and behavior” perspective15, emphasizing that professional commitment involves both attitudinal recognition and behavioral enthusiasm. They highlighted the importance of professional awareness and engagement in professional learning. XUE Feng, LUO Run-sheng & PENG Chun-mei, and LUO Ya-li & LIU Yun-bo supported the “attitude” perspective, defining university students’ professional commitment as the recognition and acceptance of chosen majors, the willingness to actively learn in the professional learning process, and the attitude of diligent study16–18. The relationship between students and their educational institution has been identified as a significant factor in shaping their professional commitment19,20. Jensen et al. discovered that college students may experience stress, confusion, goal disorientation, and a sense of low academic achievement if they don’t have a strong grasp of their professional identities21. Similarly, Nora et al. investigated that the institution’s commitment significantly directly affects students’ intention to persist and their actual persistence behavior, with factors such as institutional quality, actual value, and the fit between students and the institution playing a key role22. However, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding the specific influence of organizational justice on students’ professional commitment. While extensive research has been conducted on organizational justice in the workplace, its application to the higher education context has been limited23,24. This study aims to address this gap by delving into the influence mechanism of a school’s organizational justice on students’ professional commitment, taking into account the mediating effect of academic stress. This study posits that the root cause of weak professional commitment among current university students may lie in the relatively weak organizational justice within universities25. By investigating this relationship and its underlying mechanisms, the present study seeks to enrich the theoretical basis for understanding the factors that influence students’ professional commitment. In an era characterized by rapid change and evolving interpersonal dynamics, understanding the factors that contribute to professional commitment, student independence, and effective stress management is crucial for promoting occupational stability, engagement, and overall well-being. As well, the insights gained from this study also have the potential to inform professional construction, discipline construction, and the quality of talent cultivation in universities and colleges.

Literature review

School’s organizational justice and professional commitment

Organizational justice, a fundamental concept in organizational behavior research, refers to individuals’ perceptions of fairness within their organization26,27. Through the lens of Social Exchange Theory (SET), students’ perceptions of fairness can be viewed as part of a reciprocal exchange relationship with their educational institution28. In the context of educational institutions, school organizational justice encompasses students’ perceptions of fairness regarding the distribution of resources, procedures, and interactions within their school environment1,5–7. Given that schools function as organizations, the principles of organizational justice can be applied to elucidate the dynamics between students and their educational institutions29. The pursuit of fairness and justice is a fundamental human value that extends beyond mere considerations of resource distribution, as it is inextricably linked to individuals’ sense of self-worth and their perceived significance within a social system30. In organizational settings, perceptions of justice serve as powerful predictors of various outcomes, including job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and citizenship behaviors31,32. Similarly, in the context of higher education, students’ perceptions of organizational justice can significantly influence their attitudes, behaviors, and overall educational experience8,33,34. This aligns with SET’s proposition that individuals engage in social exchanges based on their perceptions of costs and benefits, and that these perceptions shape their attitudes and behaviors within the organization. Nechvoloda et al. found that college students, especially those with higher levels of education, are generally more sensitive to the allocation of resources such as scholarships, grants, awards, and opportunities for leadership roles or graduate studies35. This sensitivity can be understood as students’ evaluation of the fairness of their exchange relationship with the institution. Similarly, research by Rasooli et al., Chory-Assad and Paulsel, and Houston and Bettencourt indicates that students’ perceptions of the fairness of classroom assessments are influenced by the principles of distributive justice, procedural justice, and interactional justice. Students’ evaluations of the fairness and equity of assessment practices are largely affected by the fair distribution of grades, the transparency of the assessment process, and the quality of communication between students and teachers. These perceptions of fairness can elicit both positive and negative emotional responses from students, ultimately impacting their motivation and academic engagement36–38. Through the SET framework, these responses can be interpreted as students’ reciprocation to their perceived treatment within the educational exchange relationship.

Professional commitment, on the other hand, refers to an individual’s psychological attachment to their chosen profession, characterized by a strong belief in and acceptance of the profession’s values, a willingness to exert effort on behalf of the profession, and a desire to maintain membership within the professional community39,40. For students, professional commitment manifests as a dedication to acquiring knowledge and skills related to their chosen field of study, as well as a commitment to pursuing a career in that field upon graduation41,42. From an SET perspective, professional commitment can be viewed as a form of investment in the long-term exchange relationship between the student and their chosen profession. You-Gu’s research indicates that students with higher levels of professional commitment tend to be more engaged in their studies, exhibit greater persistence in the face of challenges, and achieve superior academic outcomes43. Similarly, Sharma & Yukhymenko-Lescroart found that students’ sense of purpose and commitment to their degrees contribute to their persistence and academic success44. Moreover, professional commitment has been linked to increased career satisfaction, enhanced job performance, and reduced turnover intentions among professionals in various fields45–47. Despite the importance of professional commitment in shaping students’ academic and career trajectories, relatively few studies have examined its relationship with organizational justice in the context of higher education. However, existing research in other organizational settings suggests that perceptions of fairness can significantly influence individuals’ commitment to their profession7,48. When employees perceive their organization as just and equitable, they are more likely to develop a stronger emotional attachment to their profession and be more willing to invest time and effort into their professional development49. Drawing upon these findings and the SET framework, it is plausible that students’ perceptions of organizational justice within their educational institution may play a significant role in shaping their professional commitment. When students feel that their school treats them fairly and equitably, they may be more likely to develop a stronger sense of identification with their chosen field of study and be more motivated to pursue a career in that field. This can be understood as students reciprocating the perceived fairness with increased commitment and engagement. Conversely, perceptions of injustice or unfairness may undermine students’ commitment to their profession, leading to disengagement, reduced motivation, and potentially even attrition from their chosen field of study. Based on this theoretical foundation and empirical evidence, we propose the following hypothesis.

H1:

The school’s organizational justice has a saliently positive correlation with students’ professional commitment.

School’s organizational justice and students’ academic stress

Academic stress, defined as the psychological strain that students experience due to the expectations and pressures they face in their academic pursuits, is a pervasive issue in higher education50–53. Research has consistently demonstrated that excessive academic stress can lead to a range of adverse outcomes, including diminished motivation, impaired cognitive functioning, and elevated risk of mental health issues such as anxiety and depression54,55. Given the detrimental effects of academic stress on students’ well-being and performance, it is essential to identify factors that may contribute to or mitigate stress levels within educational settings Organizational justice has been proposed as one such factor that may influence students’ experiences of academic stress56. When students perceive their educational environment as fair and equitable, they may experience an enhanced sense of control and predictability, potentially buffering against the deleterious effects of stress. Conversely, perceptions of injustice or unfairness can exacerbate feelings of uncertainty and lack of control, which are recognized as significant sources of stress57,58. Niknamian found significant negative correlations between the three dimensions of organizational justice (distributive, procedural, and interactional) and stress among a sample of university students59. Similarly, Judge & Colquitt demonstrated a negative relationship between organizational justice perceptions and academic stress among university faculty members56. These findings are consistent with the broader literature on organizational justice and stress in other settings As Mikula and colleagues assert, “if individuals find that they are staying in an unfair relationship, they will feel nervous. The more serious the unfair relationship is, the more serious the perceived nervousness by individuals will be”60. This suggests that perceptions of injustice can directly contribute to elevated stress levels, as individuals grapple with the psychological strain of experiencing unfair treatment In the context of higher education, students who perceive their school environment as unjust may experience heightened levels of academic stress due to the added cognitive and emotional burden of dealing with unfairness. This can manifest in various ways, such as increased anxiety about grades and evaluations, reduced motivation to engage in academic tasks, and strained relationships with faculty and peers11,12. In this study, we introduce two key concepts: school organizational justice and student academic stress, aiming to establish a theoretical foundation for the research hypothesis that organizational justice may be an important factor affecting students’ levels of academic stress. By reviewing previous literature, the review clarified the potential relationship between these two variables, laying the groundwork for subsequent research. Based on this theoretical and empirical evidence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2:

The school’s organizational justice has a saliently negative correlation with students’ academic stress.

Students’ academic stress and professional commitment

While moderate levels of stress can serve as a motivating force for learning and personal growth, excessive or prolonged stress can have detrimental effects on students’ academic and professional development61,62. According to the transactional model of stress, academic stress arises from an interaction between students and their educational environment, wherein the demands of the situation are perceived as exceeding the individual’s resources and coping abilities. Research has shown that high levels of academic stress can lead to a range of negative outcomes, including decreased academic performance, reduced motivation and engagement, and increased risk of burnout51,52,63. Moreover, excessive stress can undermine students’ sense of self-efficacy and confidence in their abilities, which may in turn weaken their commitment to their chosen profession64. Professional commitment, conceptualized as a psychological attachment to one’s chosen field of study or future profession, is a crucial factor in shaping students’ academic and career trajectories. Students with higher levels of professional commitment are more likely to persist in the face of challenges, invest time and effort into their studies, and ultimately achieve greater success in their chosen careers44. However, the relationship between academic stress and professional commitment is complex and multifaceted. On one hand, moderate levels of stress can potentially enhance students’ professional commitment by providing a sense of challenge and motivation to Excel in their chosen field65. When students perceive their academic demands as manageable and relevant to their professional goals, they are more likely to engage deeply with the subject matter and develop a stronger sense of identification with their future profession. On the other hand, excessive or chronic stress can erode students’ professional commitment by depleting their emotional and cognitive resources, leading to disengagement, burnout, and even attrition from their chosen field of study. When students feel overwhelmed by the demands of their academic environment, they may begin to question their ability to succeed in their chosen profession and lose confidence in the value of their educational pursuits52,66. This dual nature of the stress-commitment relationship underscores the critical importance of maintaining an optimal level of challenge and support for students in higher education settings. By providing students with adequate resources, coping strategies, and a sense of control over their academic experiences, educational institutions can help to foster a positive stress response that enhances professional commitment and personal growth53,67. Considering the predominantly negative effects of excessive academic stress on students’ well-being and performance, and its potential to undermine professional commitment, we propose the following hypothesis.

H3:

The students’ academic stress has a saliently negative correlation with their professional commitment

Mediating effect of students’ academic stress

The previous sections have established theoretical and empirical foundations for the relationships between school organizational justice, students’ academic stress, and professional commitment. Building on these interconnections, it is plausible to consider the potential mediating role of academic stress in the relationship between organizational justice and professional commitment. The perception of organizational justice can exert a profound influence on professional commitment via both direct and circuitous trajectories. The immediate effect materializes when equitable treatment by the educational establishment engenders a sentiment of reciprocity and allegiance to one’s elected domain of expertise. Nonetheless, the oblique impact mediated through academic stress merits meticulous scrutiny7,48,49. Empirical investigations have demonstrated that the cognizance of organizational justice can substantially modulate students’ echelons of academic stress. When learners discern their scholastic milieu as just and equitable, they are predisposed to experiencing attenuated levels of stress, concomitantly feeling more autonomous and less encumbered by ambiguities or perceived inequities57,58. Antithetically, perceptions of iniquity may exacerbate stress levels, precipitating a cascade of deleterious psychological sequelae59. Moreover, academic stress has been empirically substantiated to exert a profound influence on students’ professional commitment, engendering multifarious repercussions. Inordinate stress can insidiously erode students’ self-efficacy, intrinsic motivation, and cognitive engagement with their chosen field of study, potentially attenuating their professional commitment61,62,64. Through the synthesis of these multifaceted relationships, we can postulate that organizational justice may indirectly influence professional commitment by modulating the continuum of academic stress. When students apprehend elevated levels of organizational justice, they may experience diminished academic stress, which, in turn, could catalyze more robust professional commitment. Conversely, perceptions of injustice may engender heightened stress, potentially undermining professional commitment and precipitating a cascade of negative outcomes. This mediating mechanism aligns congruently with the broader stress literature, which posits that environmental factors such as organizational justice can modulate individual outcomes such as students’ professional commitment through their impact on stress levels. Furthermore, this conceptualization resonates harmoniously with the tenets of Social Exchange Theory, as the perceived equity of the educational exchange relationship may sculpt students’ stress experiences, which subsequently modulate their commitment to the profession. Based on this theoretical reasoning and the interconnections between the constructs, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4:

The students’ academic stress has a mediating effect on students’ professional commitment to the school’s organizational justice.

Research design

Participants

To ensure the representativeness and generalizability of our findings, this study employed a two-stage sampling method of stratified sampling and simple random sampling. In the first stage, stratified sampling was utilized to account for the diversity of the target population and to ensure that the sample adequately represented the key demographic characteristics of undergraduate students in the H University. The stratification was predicated on salient factors such as gender, grade level, major, student cadre status, and hometown. This approach allowed for the capture of a broad spectrum of student characteristics, thereby enhancing the external validity of our findings68,69. In the second stage, simple random sampling within each stratum was implemented to select specific students participating in the study. This probability sampling technique ensures that every student within each stratum has an equal chance of being selected, minimizing potential selection bias and promoting the representativeness of the sample70. H University in Huanggang City, Central China, was selected as the focal point for data collection from representative institutions in regions with relatively low economic development. The selection of H University was predicated on its typicality among universities in less developed areas of Hubei Province, ensuring that our findings would be relevant to students facing similar challenges in terms of organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment.The research team disseminated a total of 500 questionnaires to full-time undergraduate students at H University. The meticulously crafted instrument aimed to elucidate the relationship between a school’s organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment. This comprehensive tool encompassed demographic variables and psychometrically validated scales for the key constructs of interest. Research measurements

This study meticulously employs a constellation of rigorously crafted measurement instruments to elucidate pivotal constructs: School Organizational Justice, Students’ Academic Stress, and Students’ Professional Commitment. Each instrument has undergone meticulous adaptation to ensure validity and reliability within the educational milieu. To gauge School Organizational Justice, we harnessed a scale inspired by Moorman’s seminal work, specifically tailored to capture nuances pertinent to educational institutions. This multidimensional scale comprehensively captures three dimensions of distributive justice, procedural justice, and interactional justice. Distributive justice gauges students’ perceptions of fairness in educational outcomes, procedural justice examines the fairness of processes within the institution, and interactive justice evaluates the quality of student-teacher interactions71,72. This nuanced measurement comprises multiple items evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very disagree) to 5 (very agree), designed to reflect the multifaceted nature of organizational justice as perceived by students. In assessing Students’ Academic Stress, we incorporated widely acclaimed methodologies from Parker & Decotiis and Jamal & Baba, focusing on time stress and anxiety stress. Time stress is measured by assessing students’ perceptions of pressure related to academic timelines, while anxiety stress captures emotional tensions affecting academic performance73–75. This sophisticated instrument incorporates reverse-scored items, employing the same 5-point Likert scale, ensuring a robust psychometric foundation for stress assessment. To elucidate Students’ Professional Commitment, we employed a meticulously crafted scale derived from Marsden, Kalleberg, & Cook, tailored to the context of university student dynamics76. This instrument measures commitment through items that reflect students’ dedication to their academic and professional roles, again using a 5-point Likert scale to enable nuanced responses. Each scale has undergone rigorous validation to ensure their appropriateness and reliability within this study context, thereby providing comprehensive insights into each construct’s influence on educational outcomes.

Data analysis

Common method deviation inspection and control

The respondents completed the three scales used in this study through self-report within a stipulated time frame. However, the proportional imbalance in sample gender, age, and major may introduce the possibility of common method variance, which can reduce the validity of the questionnaire and influence the research results. To address this issue, the study employed several strategies to control for common method variance before and after data collection. Prior to administering the questionnaire, the scales were refined through multiple iterations, and process control measures were implemented to minimize common method variance. These measures included controlling the sample source and collecting anonymous questionnaires. After data collection, statistical control methods, such as Harman’s single factor test and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), were applied to assess the presence of common method variance. The results of the exploratory factor analysis revealed that the eigenvalues of nine factors were greater than 1, and the interpretation rate of the first factor was 20.312%, which is well below the critical value of 40%82. Additionally, a CFA was conducted to test for common method variance among all self-evaluation items. The model fit indices for the common method variance model were inferior to those of the original measurement model, with x2/df = 1.871, CFI = 0.994, GFI = 0.994, AGFI = 0.968, NFI = 0.986, and RMSEA = 0.047. These fit indices suggest a good fit for the original model and do not indicate the presence of severe common method variance. The GFI and AGFI values were both above the recommended threshold of 0.90, indicating a well-fitting model80. The NFI value of 0.986 also exceeded the suggested cut-off of 0.9583. Furthermore, the RMR value, which assesses the average residual variance, was 0.047, below the recommended upper limit of 0.0884. These findings suggest that there is no serious common method variance problem in the study. In other words, the artificial covariance between the predictor and criterion variables due to the same data source and measurement environment is unlikely to confound the research results. Consequently, the subsequent correlation and regression analyses, structural equation modeling, and mediation tests can be considered statistically significant, and the data analysis results can be regarded as true and credible.

Discussion

The students’ professional commitment is weak as a whole

The research findings, grounded in the Social Exchange Theory (SET) framework28,85, uncover that the prevailing level of professional commitment among undergraduate students registers at a moderate intensity, reflected in a mean score of 3 on the professional commitment scale. This moderate score reveals that students’ overall devotion to and identification with their chosen academic disciplines is not exceptionally robust, suggesting a potential imbalance in the social exchange between students and their educational institutions. The data analysis elucidates that the three facets of organizational justice—namely, distributive, procedural, and interactive justice—exert a significant impact on students’ professional commitment, aligning with SET’s proposition that perceptions of fairness influence reciprocal behaviors and attitudes. Within the undergraduate context, professional commitment encapsulates the degree to which students acknowledge, appreciate, and willingly expend effort into their elected majors83. Nonetheless, survey data reveals a mere 2% of students articulate a profound sense of identification with and enthusiasm for their majors, whereas 20% express satisfaction with their selected fields of study. This evidences a relatively low affective commitment, suggesting that students’ attitudes towards their majors may oscillate, potentially due to perceived inequities in their educational exchange relationships. Several determinants influence students’ major selections, encompassing personal interests, parental or peer sway, institutional prestige, and program availability84,86. The survey’s cognizance indicates that students whose choices are driven by intrinsic motivations, like personal interests, manifest markedly higher levels of commitment, contrasted with those who are allocated majors through automatic adjustment methodologies. This finding can be interpreted through the lens of SET, where intrinsically motivated choices represent a more balanced exchange, leading to higher commitment levels. Intrinsically motivated students demonstrate greater propensity to actively partake in classroom discussions, seek supplemental learning resources, and engage in extracurricular activities pertinent to their academic sphere87. Conversely, those directed by extrinsic factors, such as parental pressure or program availability, frequently approach their academic pursuits with passivity. They may focus on fulfilling minimum course requirements or securing credit points rather than engaging profoundly with the academic content88,89. Such absence of intrinsic motivation can impede the resonance of a tenacious sense of professional commitment, reflecting an imbalanced social exchange where students perceive limited personal value in their academic pursuits. The feeble levels of undergraduate major recognition can be ascribed to a composite of social, institutional, and personal elements, all of which influence the perceived equity of the educational exchange. Should students perceive unfairness within their educational milieu, such perceptions may manifest as diminished effort and participation, consistent with SET’s prediction of reciprocal behaviors in response to perceived inequities. Cultivating a fair, equitable educational environment—paying heed to distributive, procedural, and interactive justice—is pivotal for nurturing a favorable outlook towards one’s academic pursuit and fostering professional commitment. Additionally, findings indicate when students’ perceptions of organizational justice waver at moderate levels, they might encounter heightened instances of temporal and anxiety stress in their learning trajectories. These academic stressors may curtail professional evolution and precipitate burnout or alienation from their chosen majors. Consequently, students’ esteem and affinity for their majors may wane over time. Summarily, students’ professional commitment is profoundly shaped not only by their perceptions of distributive, procedural, and interactive justice within academic institutions but also by how effectively their needs for coping with temporal and anxiety stress are addressed. Addressing these factors is essential for creating a learning atmosphere conducive to professional growth, cultivating a robust sense of commitment to one’s field of study, and ensuring a balanced and beneficial social exchange within the educational context.

The School’s organizational justice can forward predict students’ professional commitment

The research findings elucidate that organizational justice within educational institutions exercises a profound and direct influence on students’ professional commitment. Rastgoo, in his research with university students as subjects, mapped out the beneficial correlation between the triadic dimensions of organizational justice and student commitment to their vocational paths90. Rubio emphasizes a distinctly favorable correlation between interactive justice and professional dedication among students91. When individuals experience unjust treatment, their ability to choose preferred majors is hindered; the more acute the perception of injustice, the more arduous the task of major selection becomes. In the cultural milieu of collectivist China, where emphasis on harmonious interpersonal connections supersedes individual material achievements, the predictive effectiveness of distributive justice is lessened compared to interactive justice. Similarly, the influence of procedural justice is also reduced, situating interactive justice as the most critical contributor to professional commitment among the facets of organizational justice. This phenomenon can be substantially attributed to the cultural context of Chinese society, which values interpersonal accord. The foundational principles of Confucian political ethics assert that humans exist within a complex web of relationships, with these connections fostering mutual respect and societal balance92,93. Within the realm of interpersonal exchanges, fairness encompasses two core respects. Emotional respect encompasses the universal desire for total respect regardless of organizational status. Intellectual respect pertains to individuals’ aspiration to have their knowledge and abilities recognized, along with a yearning for respect and validation of their insights and suggestions. University students, inherently driven by a sense of justice, are competitive in their academic endeavors, striving for recognition and excellence over acquiescence. Emotively, they show impulsivity and may demonstrate limited self-regulation, leading to extreme reactions within interactions. As a result, they may clash with peers and educators, feeling devalued and diminished. Such perceived injustices deeply erode students’ professional commitment. The study thus concludes that heightened organizational justice in educational settings significantly promotes professional commitment among students. Consequently, when students perceive a high degree of organizational justice, their professional dedication is fortified. Conversely, exposure to unfair treatment leads to a decline in students’ professional resolve.

The students’ academic stress has mediating variable

This study reveals that academic stress acts as a mediator in the relationship between organizational justice and professional commitment. Specifically, the results illuminate that elevated levels of organizational justice are associated with reduced levels of academic stress, leading to more robust professional commitment among students. The data analysis demonstrates that distributive justice exerts an influence on professional commitment through the intermediary role of time stress, while procedural justice affects professional commitment via the mediating mechanisms of anxiety stress. In contrast, interactive justice shapes students’ professional commitment through the combined mediating effects of both time stress and anxiety stress. These findings suggest that students’ encounters with academic stress and their perceptions of organizational justice interact to shape their levels of professional commitment. Students who perceive low levels of academic stress and high levels of organizational justice are more likely to exhibit strong professional commitment. Conversely, students who experience high levels of academic stress and perceive low levels of organizational justice may struggle to maintain a strong sense of commitment to their chosen majors. To cultivate professional commitment and avert burnout, it is essential to address both organizational justice and academic stress. Educators are pivotal in aiding students to navigate academic stressors through the provision of guidance, support, and resources for stress management94,95. Additionally, facilitating opportunities for students to participate in major-related extracurricular activities and providing up-to-date information on career prospects can help enhance students’ emotional connection to and recognition of their chosen fields of study96. It is important to recognize that some sources of academic stress may be beyond students’ control. In such cases, educators should focus on equipping students with effective coping strategies and fostering a learning environment that maintains academic stress at a manageable level, turning it into a motivating force rather than a hindrance to learning and personal growth. Furthermore, the research findings underscore the need for a societal shift towards a more comprehensive approach to education in China. The persistent focus on examination-centric education has led to a focus on enrollment rates at the expense of cultivating students’ intrinsic learning interests. The observed deficiency in levels of professional commitment among university students can be seen as a consequence of the damage inflicted upon their learning interests by prolonged exposure to academic stress. To address this issue, it is crucial to strengthen quality-oriented education, encourage open-mindedness and diverse learning experiences, and emphasize the nurturing of students’ authentic interests in their selected fields of study. By cultivating an educational milieu that cherishes students’ intrinsic motivations and supports their holistic development, educators can empower students to build a strong sense of professional commitment and lay the foundation for future success in their chosen careers.

Management insights

In the contemporary epoch characterized by swift digitalization and burgeoning intelligence, educational institutions assume a crucial role in nurturing students’ professional allegiance, increasingly influenced by fluid cultural transitions and the evolving interpretations of equity and justice. It is recognized that absolute equity remains elusive; however, relative equity can be attained through the appreciation of individual contributions, competencies, knowledge, and skills97–99. Within the framework of Chinese culture that values concordant interpersonal relationships, comprehending and advocating for organizational equity within educational settings is vital. As educational establishments address future developmental trajectories, they should augment distributive, procedural, and interactive equity to strengthen students’ affinity to their academic disciplines. Fostering distributive equity involves amplifying opportunities for pragmatic engagement and experiential learning. Students articulate a distinct desire for heightened exposure to industry practices, facilitating the interface between theoretical understanding and practical applications. Partnerships between academic institutions and industry entities are pivotal, offering internships and site visits to expand students’ professional horizons. Moreover, harmonizing recruitment methodologies with targeted professional orientations can alleviate the employment stress stemming from academic incongruences, particularly for students in the humanities and social sciences. Concurrently, procedural equity can be fortified by delivering comprehensive vocational and psychological guidance. The academic milieu profoundly influences students, affecting their adaptation to chosen disciplines. By promoting a positive learning atmosphere and behaviors concomitant with academic success, universities can enhance students’ professional adaptation. Faculty and mentors should engage proactively and meaningfully with students, addressing barriers potentially impeding their academic journey, hence cultivating a nurturing community endorsing healthy competition and personal growth. Moreover, fostering interactive equity involves nurturing students’ capacities to autonomously traverse academic and social challenges. Respect for students’ autonomy and potential is of paramount importance, as mutual respect and acknowledgment are critical in fostering professional dedication. This environment empowers students to excel and foster resilience, thereby addressing dissatisfaction proactively and positively. As well, fostering interactive equity involves nurturing students’ capacities to autonomously traverse academic and social challenges. Respect for students’ autonomy and potential is of paramount importance, as mutual respect and acknowledgment are critical in fostering professional dedication. This environment empowers students to excel and foster resilience, thereby addressing dissatisfaction proactively and positively. Furthermore, addressing academic stress from both exogenous and endogenous dimensions is vital for preserving psychological well-being and sustaining motivation. Schools must cultivate environments that mitigate unnecessary stress while cultivating an atmosphere of comprehensive evaluation criteria that extend beyond mere academic performance100. Educators should foster engaging, stress-mitigating learning experiences that stimulate students’ intrinsic interest in their studies, nurturing both direct and indirect engagement with their scholarly pursuits. At last, by strategically focusing on these dimensions of equity, respect, and stress management, educational institutions can profoundly enhance students’ professional allegiance, endowing them with the skills and resilience needed to thrive in their professional journeys. By cultivating such an enriching educational environment, students will emerge as confident, competent individuals poised to contribute meaningfully to society.

Theoretical contributions

This research endeavor enriches the extant corpus of knowledge concerning organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment within the realm of higher education. This study offers substantive theoretical insights that deepen our understanding of the complex interplay among these critical constructs in academic settings. Foremost, our investigation broadens the application of Social Exchange Theory (SET) to the educational milieu, particularly in elucidating the intricate interplay between organizational justice and professional commitment among undergraduate scholars. By empirically demonstrating that students’ perceptions of fairness within their academic institutions significantly shape their dedication to their chosen field of study, we furnish robust support for SET’s postulations in an academic context. This novel extension of SET to educational settings offers a fresh perspective on how reciprocal exchanges mold students’ attitudes and behaviors towards their scholarly pursuits, thereby enriching our understanding of the social dynamics within academic environments. In a significant advancement of organizational justice literature, our study delineates the differential impacts of distributive, procedural, and interactive justice on students’ professional commitment. Notably, our findings underscore the paramount significance of interactive justice in the Chinese educational landscape, a phenomenon attributable to the collectivist cultural ethos that places a premium on harmonious interpersonal relationships. This nuanced insight augments our comprehension of how cultural factors moderate the effects of various dimensions of organizational justice, thereby enriching cross-cultural perspectives in organizational behavior research and highlighting the necessity of culturally sensitive approaches in educational management. Furthermore, our research advances the understanding of the complex interplay between organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment. By identifying academic stress as a mediating variable, we unveil a more sophisticated mechanism through which organizational justice influences professional commitment. This finding bridges the chasm between organizational justice theory and stress literature in educational settings, offering a more comprehensive model of how institutional factors and individual experiences interact to shape students’ commitment to their chosen profession. This integrative approach provides a holistic framework for understanding the multifaceted influences on students’ professional development, emphasizing the interconnectedness of institutional practices, personal experiences, and professional aspirations. In a notable contribution to the literature on academic stress, our study differentiates between time stress and anxiety stress, demonstrating their distinct mediating roles in the relationship between different dimensions of organizational justice and professional commitment. This granular approach to academic stress provides a more refined understanding of how different types of stress impact students’ academic experiences and professional aspirations. By elucidating these nuanced relationships, our research offers valuable insights into the complex nature of academic stress and its varied implications for students’ professional commitment, paving the way for more targeted interventions and support strategies in higher education. Lastly, our research findings illuminate the intricate interplay between organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment in higher education. Through rigorous empirical analysis, we have uncovered significant relationships between students’ perceptions of fairness within their academic institutions and their dedication to their chosen fields of study. Our data reveal that intrinsic motivation and perceived equity play crucial roles in shaping students’ professional commitments. The study’s results delineate a complex network of factors influencing the development of strong professional identities among undergraduate students, including institutional practices, personal experiences, and psychological processes. These findings suggest that the cultivation of equitable and supportive learning environments may be instrumental in nurturing students’ professional aspirations. However, it is important to note that these relationships are nuanced and context-dependent, necessitating careful consideration of institutional and cultural factors in their interpretation and application.

These theoretical contributions not only advance our understanding of the complex dynamics at play in higher education settings but also provide a solid foundation for future research exploring the intersections of organizational behavior, stress management, and professional development in academic contexts. Moreover, our findings offer invaluable insights for educational policymakers and administrators seeking to create more equitable and supportive learning environments that foster students’ professional commitment and long-term career success. By illuminating the intricate relationships between institutional practices, our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that informs future discussions on potential enhancements in educational policies and practices. While acknowledging the multifaceted nature of educational reform, this study offers a foundation for further exploration and refinement of strategies aimed at optimizing the higher education experience and its long-term outcomes for students.

Conclusion

This study focuses on full-time undergraduate students at H University in Central China and investigates the impact of school organizational justice on students’ professional commitment, as well as the mediating role of academic stress. The results demonstrate a significant positive correlation between school organizational justice and students’ professional commitment, with academic stress partially mediating this relationship. This finding underscores the importance of enhancing perceptions of organizational justice in educational management to strengthen students’ professional commitment. Initially, the results indicate that school organizational justice significantly reduces academic stress among students, thereby enhancing their professional commitment. This aligns with existing research, emphasizing the critical role of perceived fairness in educational environments. Furthermore, the structural equation model employed in this study further reveals the partial mediating role of academic stress between school organizational justice and students’ professional commitment. This insight offers a new perspective on understanding students’ experiences and behaviors within educational settings. This study offers significant insights for educational administrators, in the process of improving educational quality and student satisfaction, it is crucial to focus on enhancing perceptions of school organizational justice and to appropriately manage academic stress among students.98 This not only aids in the personal career development of students but also promotes the healthy growth of the entire educational system.

Limitations and future research recommendations

This study also has its limitations. The sample is restricted to undergraduates at H University in Central China, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future research should be conducted across a broader geographical area and in different types of educational institutions to enhance the representativeness and applicability of the results. As well, the study utilizes a cross-sectional research design, which does not allow for the exploration of causal relationships. Therefore, we suggest, future studies could employ a longitudinal design to better understand the dynamic relationships between school organizational justice, academic stress, and professional commitment.
