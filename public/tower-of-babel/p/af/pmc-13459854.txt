Mpox Presenting as an Anorectal Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Mathew Vadukoot Lazar, Sai Harsha Yendluri, Shruthi Chamala, Krishnamurthy Subbian, George S Zacharia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mpox (monkeypox) is an emerging viral infection with a broad and evolving clinical spectrum that increasingly spans beyond its characteristic cutaneous manifestations. Anorectal manifestations are well recognized in patients with Mpox in recent outbreaks. However, the gastrointestinal (GI) or anorectal symptoms may precede the typical rash, creating significant diagnostic challenges and delaying appropriate infection control measures. We describe a quinquagenarian man with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus who presented with fever, diarrhea, tenesmus, and severe perianal pain. Cross-sectional imaging demonstrated diffuse rectosigmoid inflammation, while endoscopy revealed severe ulcerative proctitis. Initial evaluation favored acute infectious proctitis, and treatment was directed accordingly. However, the subsequent development of disseminated pustular skin lesions prompted consideration of Mpox, which was ultimately confirmed by polymerase chain reaction testing of the lesions. Extensive testing excluded concurrent human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection and other common sexually transmitted infections, and rectal biopsy demonstrated nonspecific acute colitis. The patient recovered with supportive management and had complete endoscopic resolution on follow-up colonoscopy. This case illustrates the relevance of considering Mpox in the differential diagnosis of acute proctitis and lower GI symptoms, even before characteristic skin lesions appear. It also underscores the diagnostic value of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with severe anorectal disease and points out that diabetes-associated immune dysfunction may play a role in such cases. Early recognition of atypical GI manifestations is essential for timely diagnosis, appropriate patient isolation, avoidance of unnecessary interventions, and optimal clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Mpox (monkeypox) is an emerging zoonotic viral infection characterized by classic skin lesions. It is caused by the Mpox virus of the Orthopoxvirus family [1]. Gastrointestinal (GI) involvement is particularly common in patients with Mpox, occurring in up to 30% of patients [1]. Proctitis is the single most important GI manifestation, with a pooled prevalence of 24.8% [1]. The exact pathophysiology of GI or rectal involvement is largely unclear; however, direct rectal mucosal invasion, triggering an inflammatory response, is often proposed [1]. It is estimated that around 14% of patients present with anorectal symptoms [2]. The recent and ongoing Mpox outbreak originated in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and subsequently spread far beyond the African continent. The Middle East, although in close proximity to Northern Africa, had reported far fewer Mpox cases than the rest of the world. Human-to-human transmission of the infection has been well described, especially through sexual transmission [2]. A close association with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection and other sexually transmitted diseases has been described as well [2]. We report a case of Mpox presenting with lower GI symptoms with proctitis on endoscopy. This case highlights Mpox as an important mimic of infectious and inflammatory proctitis.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old male with a history of diabetes mellitus and hypertension presented with a four-day history of fever, diarrhea, and tenesmus and a two-day history of perianal pain. The fever was low-grade and continuous, with no rigors or chills, and associated with headache and bodyache. The stools were watery, non-bloody, and contained mucus, with multiple episodes daily. He reported severe pain in the anal region, even at rest. He denied recent hard stools, excess straining during bowel movements, bleeding per rectum, anal instrumentation or intercourse, or recent travel. He denied any concomitant respiratory or urinary symptoms. He admitted to not using his insulin since the onset of his diarrheal disease. On examination, a few tender perianal lesions were noted. The anal sphincter was spastic; it was not possible to complete the digital rectal examination due to exquisite tenderness. No other skin lesions were noted over the body.

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed mild leukocytosis and hyperglycemia with ketoacidosis; otherwise, the hemogram and metabolic panel were within normal limits. The C-reactive protein (CRP) was markedly elevated at 146 mg/L. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated diffuse circumferential rectal and rectosigmoid wall thickening accompanied by mesorectal fat stranding and multiple prominent mesorectal lymph nodes. Fecal evaluation was positive for inflammatory markers (calprotectin and fecal leukocyte antigen) and guaiac-positive. He was admitted with a provisional diagnosis of acute proctitis and diabetic ketoacidosis, and was started on intravenous fluids, insulin infusion, empiric antibiotics: ciprofloxacin and metronidazole, and other symptomatic and supportive measures.

He continued to have perianal pain, except for transient relief with analgesics. On day 5 of admission, the patient developed multiple pustular skin lesions on the face, thighs, palms, and the perianal area (Figure 1). Dermatology was consulted; a concern for Mpox was raised, given the skin and GI manifestations. The patient was isolated in accordance with institutional protocols, and swabs were collected from skin lesions for Mpox nucleic acid polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing. Limited sigmoidoscopy revealed severe proctitis with scattered ulcers; a mucosal biopsy was performed (Figure 2). He tested negative for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia infection. He was continued on supportive measures; his symptoms improved by day 15. Meanwhile, the mucosal biopsy revealed non-specific acute colitis (Figure 3), and the PCR detected Mpox deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). The glycosylated hemoglobin was 13.9%.

The patient was discharged with clinical improvement and normal inflammatory markers. He remained asymptomatic during outpatient follow-up visits. Nearly two months after discharge, he underwent a full-length colonoscopy, which was normal except for a diminutive polyp in the ascending colon, which was hyperplastic on histology.

Discussion

The monkeypox virus was first described in 1958 among monkeys in Singapore, while the first human case was reported in 1970 in the Congo [2]. It is a double-stranded DNA virus with a large genome and has two main clades: Clade I, which is endemic to Central Africa, and Clade II, which is endemic to Western Africa [3]. The current outbreak, which began in May 2022 in Congo and later emerged as a global health concern, is caused by the Clade II strain of the virus [4]. In August 2024, a second Mpox-related Public Health Emergency of International Concern was declared owing to a surge in Clade I cases in the Congo [5]. In November 2022, owing to stigmatization, the World Health Organization (WHO) renamed Monkeypox to Mpox. It is caused by DNA viruses in the Orthopoxvirus family and is traditionally considered a zoonotic infection transmitted from infected animals [2]. However, the 2022 outbreak has been associated with sustained human-to-human transmission [6]. Human-to-human transmission occurs through respiratory droplets and contact, either direct or via fomites. High viral loads have been reported in semen, saliva, urine, and feces [2]. Sexual transmission is considered a significant mode of transmission among humans; men who have sex with men (MSM) are at the highest risk [2]. The incubation period typically ranges from one to two weeks, and three weeks is considered the upper limit of normal [2].

Prodromal symptoms classically include fever, lymphadenopathy, myalgias, malaise, and headache and were often mild and occurred concurrently with the rash during the 2022 outbreak. Skin lesions, the hallmark of Mpox, are typically painful and evolve through a predictable sequence over two to four weeks, beginning as macules and progressing to papules, vesicles, pustules, and finally crusted lesions. During the 2022 outbreak, these lesions were mostly anogenital and perioral, with a median of ~10 lesions with asynchronous progression. This contrasts with the classic centrifugal distribution and synchronous progression of the Clade I presentation [5,7].

Proctitis is thought to result from direct anorectal inoculation during receptive anal intercourse, but it has been reported in cases with no such history. In some patients, proctitis is severe enough to require hospitalization, and complications can include stricture formation or bowel obstruction [8]. Pharyngitis may cause odynophagia and dysphagia, while urogenital involvement can result in dysuria, paraphimosis, and urethral strictures. Ocular involvement ranges from conjunctivitis and keratitis to corneal scarring, with potential vision loss [5]. Complications are more frequent and severe in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with HIV with CD4 <350 cells/mm³ or solid organ transplants. These include necrotizing skin lesions with bacterial superinfection, superimposed bronchopulmonary pneumonia, encephalitis, myocarditis, and disseminated multiorgan disease with hepatosplenic and renal involvement [9].

The gold standard for Mpox diagnosis is real-time PCR of skin or mucosal lesions. At least two lesions from separate sites should be vigorously swabbed using sterile synthetic swabs. In the absence of cutaneous lesions, mucosal specimens from the oropharynx, anorectum, or saliva can support diagnosis [10]. Validated PCR assays display sensitivity and specificity approaching 100% for lesion swab specimens, with a very low limit of detection. Point-of-care platforms, such as Xpert® Mpox, allow real-time bedside diagnosis and are valuable for prompt diagnosis, especially in patients with atypical presentations [11]. In cases with Mpox proctitis, proctoscopy typically shows mucosal inflammation, erythema, friability, and shallow ulcerations with purulent exudate. Associated layered findings, such as anal margin lesions, anal canal ulceration with associated hypertonia, and rectal mucosal lesions, have also been noted in prior retrospective studies [12]. While rectal biopsies in Mpox proctitis revealed findings similar to those in cutaneous lesions, Mazzotta et al. described the first histological characterization of Mpox proctitis, namely, lymphofollicular lesions [13]. Coinfection with HIV was common, occurring in an estimated 38-50% of cases, and a concurrent sexually transmitted infection was reported in 15-30% of patients with Mpox, warranting the need to exclude concomitant infections [14,15].

Our patient presented with diarrhea and severe anorectal disease, and in fact, the diagnosis of Mpox was not on the list of initial differentials, likely due to the rarity of the disease in the Middle East. During the recent outbreak, the first case of Mpox in the Middle East was reported from Israel on May 20, 2022, followed by the second case in the United Arab Emirates on May 24, 2022. To date, the largest number of Mpox cases in the Middle East has been reported from the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Of late, the number of reported cases has dramatically decreased, and cases are extremely rare in the United Arab Emirates [16]. The rarity of the disease, together with the atypical presentation, made the diagnosis challenging; however, the evolution of the skin lesions raised suspicion and led to the final diagnosis. Furthermore, a confirmatory diagnosis was delayed by the unavailability of point-of-care PCR assays. The case also highlights the need to consider Mpox as a differential diagnosis in patients with acute-onset lower GI and anorectal disease. The poorly controlled diabetes mellitus may have contributed to the severity of Mpox infection.

Conclusions

Mpox can present with severe proctitis and ulcerative rectal lesions before cutaneous manifestations appear, posing a major diagnostic challenge for gastroenterologists, notably in regions where Mpox is uncommon. A high level of suspicion and early Mpox PCR testing, especially bedside real-time assays, in patients with unexplained proctitis and systemic inflammation, is essential to avoid misdiagnosis, reduce exposure risk, and guide timely management. It is possible that poorly controlled diabetes mellitus and associated immunological dysfunction could contribute to severe Mpox; however, it requires further evaluation and confirmation.
