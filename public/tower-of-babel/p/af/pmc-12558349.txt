Solitary thalamic abscess without meningitis from Coccidioides immitis: illustrative case
Authors: Adhith Palla, Nora C Kim, Sarra Belakhoua, David Zagzag, Scott Weisenberg, Peter A Rozman

Brain abscesses once carried a mortality rate of 40%, but over the last 5 decades, advances in intracranial imaging, neurosurgical technique, and microbiological management have reduced this figure to 10%.1 However, 30% of patients with brain abscesses do not make a full recovery, highlighting the need for prompt surgical intervention and postoperative microbiological management.2 While abscesses within deep-seated midline nuclei such as the thalamus are rare, they tend to be less encapsulated, which raises the risk of intraventricular rupture—a complication associated with mortality ranging from 39% to 80%.3 These deep-seated abscesses are challenging to treat with systemic therapy due to the blood-brain barrier and, furthermore, can be less pharmacologically accessible than cortical abscesses due to lower leptomeningeal vessel perfusion.4 With the mortality of fungal abscesses threefold higher than other types of abscesses, their management, particularly of deep-seated variants, has not benefited from the same progress as has the management of bacterial abscesses.5

Coccidioides immitis is a fungal pathogen endemic to the arid soil of the southwestern United States. The incidence of coccidioidomycosis in endemic states has increased—almost fivefold (4.9 times) in Arizona since the turn of the century and more than eightfold (8.9 times) in California over the same time period.6 Furthermore, the endemic zone is expanding northward due to climate change.7 Primary coccidioidomycosis is a pulmonary disease, but the CNS can be affected in up to one-half of patients with disseminated disease and represents the most common site of extrapulmonary involvement.8 This classically manifests as meningitis, yet multiple case series of CNS coccidioidomycosis have reported intraparenchymal abscesses, which can occur with or without associated meningitis.9–12

We present the second known case of a solitary thalamic lesion from coccidioidomycosis. The absence of associated meningitis in our case underscores the divergent forms of this organism’s dissemination within the CNS. Key to the diagnosis was histopathological evaluation, despite negative fungal cultures and serology. We report a well-documented strategy of stereotactic aspiration followed by medical management with systemic antifungals and steroids that successfully resolved neurological deficits and led to radiographic regression of the lesion.

Illustrative Case

A 59-year-old right-handed female with a history of Raynaud’s phenomenon presented to the emergency department with 1 week of unsteady gait, left-sided facial weakness, dysarthria, mild dysgraphia manifesting to family via text messages, and intermittent headaches. Following a bicycle accident and worsening unsteady gait, she presented to her primary care physician, who referred her to the emergency department.

In addition to her neurological symptoms, the patient reported nausea and 2 weeks of intermittent nonbloody diarrhea prior to admission. She denied fever or chills, and denied a personal or family history of multiple sclerosis, cancer, sarcoidosis, or other inflammatory or immune conditions. She reported travel to the Dominican Republic several months prior and had lived in Southern California for 2 years with exposure to the desert.

On admission, she was afebrile with normal vital signs. On neurological examination, left facial weakness was noted. Head CT without contrast (Fig. 1A) showed a hypodense expansile right thalamic lesion measuring 1.2 × 1.5 × 2.4 cm. There was no overt hydrocephalus. Chest CT revealed nonspecific subcentimeter lung nodules in the right upper lobe and trace pleural effusions. Laboratory values were notable for an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (44 mm/hr), with all other values within normal limits.

A: Preoperative axial CT scan demonstrating an ill-defined hypodense mass in the right thalamus. B–F: Preoperative MR images demonstrating a 1.2 × 1.5 × 2.4–cm lesion with rim enhancement on postcontrast MP-RAGE (B and C), a T2 FLAIR hyperintense rim with surrounding vasogenic edema (D), and central diffusion restriction (E and F).

Follow-up T1-weighted postcontrast MRI (Fig. 1B– F) showed a rim-enhancing lesion with restricted internal diffusion centered in the right thalamus, most consistent with a brain abscess but with a hypercellular neoplasm remaining in the differential. Given the proximity of the lesion to the third ventricle and lack of accessible lesions elsewhere, needle aspiration of the lesion was indicated to obtain a diagnosis, relieve the mass effect, and reduce the risk of intraventricular rupture.

Treatment

Surgical Procedure

The patient underwent a right frameless stereotactic needle aspiration and biopsy of the thalamic abscess. The VarioGuide system (Brainlab) was used to facilitate accurate targeting of this deep lesion. Antibiotics were withheld preoperatively to maximize the biopsy microbial yield. A single trajectory was used.

Eight core samples were taken. The posterior aspect of the abscess was noted to contain solid tissue, while the anterior and lateral aspects revealed liquid beige aspirate with hemorrhagic components. Immediately after obtaining the samples, the patient was started on dexamethasone for cerebral edema and empiric ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and vancomycin for presumptive coverage of the abscess microorganisms. The sampled abscess material was sent for studies, including biopsy culture and Gram stain for aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, acid-fast bacilli, fungal cultures, and tissue bacterial 16s rDNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection. Intraoperative samples were sent for fungal tissue culture and 28s rDNA PCR to assess fungal pathogens. Solid biopsy components were additionally sent to pathology for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), flow cytometry, and cytology. The patient tolerated the procedure well, without complications, and was neurologically stable postoperatively.

Microbiology and Pathology

Blood cultures returned negative. Serum antifungal and antiparasitic antibodies were analyzed for Coccidioides, Blastomyces, Schistosoma, Toxocara, and Echinococcus species and cysticercosis, which were all negative. Urine antigen analysis was performed for Histoplasma and Blastomyces species, along with ova and parasite stain. An in-house molecular diagnostic laboratory tested bacterial markers of 16s rDNA and D2-LSU-rDNA. Toxoplasma IgG and IgM were negative. Initial pathology analysis of the solid surgical abscess specimens showed ovoid and round structures on H&E staining suspicious for fungal organisms (Fig. 2). Additional Grocott methenamine silver (GMS) stains demonstrated basophilic spherules containing endospores, a finding pathognomonic for C. immitis (Fig. 3). For further testing, specimens were sent to an outside laboratory for 16s rDNA testing for fungal sequencing.

H&E stain of abscess tissue at high-power magnification (×200). Basophilic spherules of Coccidioides are distributed throughout abscess tissue in different stages of endospore formation, as indicated by arrows.

GMS stain of abscess tissue at high-power magnification (×400). A Coccidioides spherule containing numerous endospores is demonstrated in the early stages of rupture.

Following histological identification of spherules and while awaiting confirmation of the diagnosis of coccidioidomycosis, the patient was started on fluconazole 800 mg daily, and the other antibiotics were discontinued. At her 2-week postoperative follow-up, a neurological examination demonstrated improved gait and resolution of her facial weakness. Her intermittent headaches had resolved. By this point, a Blastomyces dermatitidis urine antigen test and serum antibodies returned negative. Three weeks postoperatively, C. immitis was confirmed with PCR of biopsy material positive for Coccidioides genus DNA. All cultures remained negative at the 4-week follow-up.

At the 8-week follow-up, the patient’s initial neurological deficits of dysarthria, gait instability, and left-sided facial weakness were completely resolved, and the dexamethasone taper had been completed. Brain MRI 5 weeks postoperatively (Fig. 4A– C) revealed a reduction of vasogenic edema and evolution of a small hematoma from the time of biopsy.

A–C: Five-week postoperative MR images demonstrating improvement in enhancement, mass effect, and edema, with some residual evolving blood products related to the biopsy, as seen on T1-weighted contrast-enhanced imaging (A and B) and T2 FLAIR (C). D–F:One-year postoperative MR images demonstrating no further enhancement or edema on T1 contrast-enhanced imaging (D and E) and T2 FLAIR (F).

At the 1-year follow-up, the patient indicated that she had discontinued her fluconazole after 11 months due to side effects. Repeat MRI at that time demonstrated no residual enhancing collection (Fig. 4D– F), and the patient remained stably improved from a neurological standpoint. The patient remains under observation for signs of relapse with a low threshold to resume fluconazole therapy.

Discussion

This case of C. immitis manifesting as a solitary thalamic abscess represents the second confirmed case in the literature.

Epidemiology of CNS Coccidiomycosis

Coccidioidomycosis is classically characterized by pulmonary involvement, but up to 1% of patients present with disseminated disease.13 Immunosuppression is the main risk factor for disseminated disease, although many patients with extrapulmonary involvement do not have a history of preexisting immunosuppression.14 In these cases of dissemination, up to 50% of patients experience disease involving the CNS, typically in the form of a meningitis with a mean untreated survival time of 3.6 months.8,15 To add to our understanding of the neurotropic nature of the fungus, we report the second confirmed case of an isolated C. immitis abscess in the thalamus, without associated meningitis.16 Prior large case series on CNS coccidioidomycosis have found variable but relatively low rates of co-occurrence between brain abscess and meningitis. Heidari et al. analyzed 153 cases of coccidioidomycosis meningitis and found that intraparenchymal abscesses were rare, occurring only in 3 cases (1.96%).17 A study by Drake and Adam of 71 coccidioidomycosis-related meningitis cases in a large referral center within the endemic American Southwest reported 7 patients (9.86%) with intraparenchymal brain abscesses.9 The mortality rate when brain abscesses accompany meningitis may be increased from that of solely meningitis, as Mischel and Vinters found that in 8 autopsy cases with Coccidioides meningitis, 5 had granulomas or abscesses as well.10 There is a need for further large case series from centers serving endemic areas to clarify the actual co-occurrence rate and differing onset of parenchymal abscess versus meningitis in this disease.

Mechanisms and Pathophysiology of Solitary Abscess

The previously reported case of a solitary thalamic lesion from coccidioidomycosis also describes a 4-mm area of caseating necrosis within the right “optic thalamus” without meningitis.16 A potential mechanism for direct abscess production via hematogenous spread of Coccidioides spherules from primary pulmonary sites directly to deep-seated brain regions has been described.11 Vasculitic events within CNS coccidioidomycosis may decrease blood-brain barrier integrity locally and allow for spherule deposition in nearby perivascular spaces of the parenchyma.18 Animal studies have found upregulated matrix metalloproteases surrounding vasculitic lesions from coccidioidomycosis, creating potential weak points for fungal extravasation.19 It is plausible, then, that abscess formation in coccidioidomycosis represents a hematogenous infiltration of fungal endospores into the parenchyma. In their case series, Sobel and colleagues noted that infarcts and endarteritis obliterans most often occurred in the small vessels of deep nuclei, such as the basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebral white matter.15 In the series of Bañuelos et al. on coccidiomycosis CNS abscesses, 5 of 6 (83%) involved subcortical regions.12 Thus, a predilection of hematogenous spread to affect the deep nuclei of the brain may exist. Further studies are required to understand the different forms of CNS coccidioidomycosis dissemination.

Diagnosis of CNS Coccidiomycosis

Diagnosis of coccidioidomycosis can be made with a positive serum Coccidioides antibody test or, in cases of new neurological deficits with worsening headaches, CSF anticoccidial antibodies or complement-fixation antibody test.11,20 Fungal cultures are not commonly positive, and even rarer is the identification of fungal elements in direct microscopy, which in the case of spherules can be pathognomonic.11,20 In our case, serum antibodies to Coccidioides were negative even on the identification of spherules in biopsy, which adds to existing evidence that serum antibodies to Coccidioides can be negative even with pathological evidence of the microorganism in the CNS. When available, PCR testing has a high sensitivity (98%) and specificity (100%) for the organism.11,21 In the case of drainable fungal abscesses, aspiration may supplement the serum and CSF analysis and allow for identifying pathognomonic fungal morphology. If the abscess has solid components and the patient’s history raises suspicion for organisms that may not grow on culture, there may be utility in sending surgical samples to pathology to analyze the morphology of infectious organisms. In our case, the presence of spherules prompted us to pursue further PCR testing targeting a specific species.

Observations

In our case, the management of the coccidioidomycosis abscess was informed primarily by its location in the right thalamus. Deep-seated thalamic abscesses tend to be more encapsulated on the parenchymal side than the ventricular side and risk rupture into the third ventricle, an occurrence associated with up to 80% mortality.3 Each 1 mm of proximity between an abscess and the ventricular wall has been modeled to increase intraventricular rupture risk by 10%.22 Additionally, abscesses larger than 2.5 cm are generally recommended to be aspirated.23 Therefore, reducing the mass effect surgically and then initiating postoperative glucocorticoids plus azole therapy are essential steps in preventing morbidity and mortality. Drainage of deep abscesses can be performed effectively with frameless navigation systems and no postoperative deficits, as in our case, rendering the benefits of aspiration favorable when compared to the risk of nonoperative management.

Regarding antifungal therapy, the current standard of care for coccidioidomycosis meningitis is fluconazole 800 mg daily, tapered to 400 mg or maintained at 800 mg per the clinician’s discretion after months of symptomatic improvement.11 Lifelong azole therapy is indicated for meningitis, with a risk of relapse of around 78% upon cessation, even years after the initial infection.24 Our patient remained on fluconazole for 11 months before self-discontinuing the medication due to side effects. Her most recent imaging demonstrated resolution of the abscess, and she has had no associated symptomatic recurrence. Our case supports the successful resolution of abscess and neurological symptoms with a prolonged course of systemic fluconazole. This case further demonstrates that while many patients with primary pulmonary coccidioidomycosis may not require treatment, patients with CNS disease can be successfully treated with prolonged azole therapy and monitoring for neurological improvement alongside surveillance imaging for recurrent disease.

Some discussion exists regarding corticosteroid use within CNS fungal infections.25 Dexamethasone may be used to decrease cerebral edema and inflammation from abscesses without an increase in mortality.26 In a retrospective study of the records of 18 patients with coccidioidomycosis-related cerebrovascular incidents, postoperative dexamethasone did not complicate the course of fungal treatment but significantly reduced the risk of secondary cerebrovascular incidents.27 Evidence also exists that dexamethasone augments azole antifungal therapy for fungi in the yeast form that Coccidioides is known to take in human tissue.28 These data point to the potential augmentation of azole antifungal therapy with corticosteroids in the setting of postoperative care following treatment of intracranial Coccidioides abscesses.

Increasing Incidence of Coccidioidomycosis

The number of coccidioidomycosis cases reported annually, primarily in the Southwest United States, has increased more than sixfold since 2000, with 17,612 cases reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 2022.6 However, true case volume is likely underestimated and has been projected to range up to 150,000 annually.29 The increasing incidence of coccidioidomycosis is primarily driven by urbanization of the hyperendemic Southwest United States and the increase in occupational work exposing populations to the soil.13 Migration into endemic areas has also introduced large immune-naive populations to the fungus.30 Several outbreaks mapped in Northern California and Utah indicate the extension of the endemic zone northward, and the endemic zone is projected to double in size by 2100 with the current high greenhouse gas emissions scenario.7 Climate change is further expanding the risk outside of traditional endemic zones by accelerating phenomena like wildfires that can carry infectious arthroconidia of coccidioidomycosis with their winds.31–33 Larger wildfires with plumes expanding to large metropolitan areas can increase population exposure risk and also pose a risk to first responders, such as firefighters, who are more directly exposed to wildfire fumes.32 The evolving microbial risks from wildfire exposures are superimposed on our growing understanding of the state of respiratory and immune compromise created by wildfire smoke exposure.34,35 As these previously geographically isolated conditions are becoming more widespread, recognizing the atypical manifestations of endemic mycoses, such as our patient’s solitary abscess remotely preceding a period of residency in an endemic region, will be of the utmost importance to allow for timely and accurate diagnosis, and avoidance of unnecessary morbidity and mortality.

Lessons

CNS involvement of C. immitis is associated with a significant increase in mortality and can take the form of meningitis, intraparenchymal abscess, or both. Importantly, initial fungal cultures may be negative, and histopathological inspection may be useful to identify the pathognomonic spherules. Managing solitary coccidioidomycosis abscesses can be successfully done with stereotactic needle aspiration, followed by corticosteroids and fluconazole. With the increasing incidence of coccidioidomycosis within endemic zones and the spread of the endemic zone itself, understanding the different forms of dissemination and establishing diagnosis and management strategies is paramount.
