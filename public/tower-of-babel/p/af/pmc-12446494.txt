Correspondence on “Custom-made, 3D-printed bolus cap for a case of scalp metastasis: A single-institution study”
Authors: Amnuay Kleebayoon, Viroj Wiwanitkit
Published: 2025

Dear Editor, the publication on “Custom-made, 3D-printed bolus cap for a case of scalp metastasis: A single-institution study [1]” is hereby discussed. Treatment of many scalp melanomas is difficult owing to the wide and complex irradiation region. Although this study claimed encouraging outcomes using a 3D-printed bolus that conforms to the contour of the head, the data presented is based on a single case (n = 1), making it difficult to draw statistical inferences. Target volume (PTV) coverage, such as 95 % receiving 98.85 % of the authorized dose, cannot be assessed. Despite this promising signal, the variability of patients and various treatment strategies cannot be determined. As a result, these findings should be interpreted with caution because there is no comparison to a control group or other established procedures.

Technical and methodological restrictions are also important. The use of the Halcyon machine, which has 3DoF bed movement, may limit the capacity to alter the radiation angle for appropriate dose distribution. A 3D-printed bolus fits to the shape of the head and enhances placement, but there is no comparative evidence on how it differs from regular boluses or the VMAT approach. Furthermore, the 6-month follow-up time does not account for long-term outcomes such tumor recurrence or side effects from continuing radiotherapy.

A detailed examination of the data revealed that the use of tailored boluses resulted in a conformity index (CI) of up to 0.95, indicating optimal dose distribution and the capacity to keep the PTV at the recommended dose near to the planned dose. However, the conclusion that this procedure is “successful” is based on a single example, and it may not be applicable to other individuals with different skull shapes or metastases. A multi-patient research with comparisons to standard methods would demonstrate its validity and efficacy.

To broaden the topic, other study questions should be posed, such as: How does using individualized 3D-printed boluses affect patient comfort and compliance? Are there any changes in dose distribution and side effects between typical bolus and conventional IMRT/VMAT? Furthermore, long-term follow-up (1–3 years) will provide valuable information about recurrence rates and quality of life. Cost-effectiveness study of 3D-printed bolus utilization is another intriguing issue for clinical application.
