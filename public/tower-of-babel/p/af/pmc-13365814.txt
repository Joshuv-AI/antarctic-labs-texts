Hybrid multilayer perceptron models optimized by evolutionary algorithms for urban air quality forecasting: a case study of Shiraz, Iran
Authors: Marjan Salari, Loke Kok Foong, Esmaeil Salami Shahid, Hossein Moayedi, Mehmet Akif Cifci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate air quality predictions are vital for environmental management and public health, especially in urban areas facing rising pollution levels. This study investigates the potential of hybrid machine learning models based on Multilayer Perceptron neural networks, optimized using three nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms: Optics-Inspired Optimization, Biogeography-Based Optimization, and the Multiverse Optimizer. The aim is to enhance the prediction of key air quality indicators, carbon monoxide, ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and the Air Quality Index, using long-term air quality measurements collected from Shiraz Province, Iran, from April 2019 to November 2024. These data were used to train and evaluate the models. Each metaheuristic was applied to optimize the Multilayer Perceptron training across varying population sizes, and the performance of the resulting models was evaluated using the root mean squared error and the coefficient of determination on both the training and test datasets. Among the tested models, the hybrid combining the Multiverse Optimizer with the Multilayer Perceptron achieved the best generalization performance, with a test root mean squared error of 6.71 and a test coefficient of determination of 0.9903. The Biogeography-Based Optimization with Multilayer Perceptron model also yielded accurate results, confirming the strength of nature-inspired optimization in neural network training. This study provides a novel comparative framework for air quality forecasting by integrating underexplored metaheuristic algorithms with neural models. The results suggest strong potential for application in smart environmental monitoring systems. Future research could explore hybrid deep learning methods and real-time predictive models using broader environmental datasets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52228-w.

Introduction

Air pollution arises when substantial amounts of harmful substances ranging from particles and gases to biological molecules are released into the Earth’s atmosphere1. It constitutes a mixture of suspended particulate matter and gaseous compounds whose concentrations have reached levels detrimental to human health. This phenomenon affects both indoor and outdoor environments2. Studies reveal that the health risks posed by air pollution disproportionately affect low- and lower-middle-income countries, making them much more vulnerable than high-income ones3. The Air Pollutant Index (AQI), comprising nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO₂), ozone (O₃), and sulfur dioxide (SO₂), as well as fine particulate matter (PM2.5), and similar compounds, reflects the environmental impact of industrial activity, which is known to trigger respiratory and cardiovascular complications4. With the expansion of environmental data and the need for integrated platforms for storage, retrieval, processing, analysis, and visualization, advanced scientific and technological tools have become essential. Historically, both deterministic and stochastic models have been employed to forecast air pollution. Among deterministic methods, diffusion models have been widely used to simulate pollutant dispersion. However, these models depend heavily on accurate input data on pollutant movement and atmospheric dynamics5. Recent advancements have brought data mining and machine learning (ML) techniques to the forefront as alternatives to traditional prediction frameworks6.

Given the growing global emphasis on air quality indices, there has been a shift from classical mathematical and statistical approaches toward more efficient methods. Traditional models often demand extensive computational resources and offer limited flexibility for improvement despite large training datasets7. Artificial intelligence including machine learning (ML) and deep learning is improving traditional environmental health models. This technology enables real-time tracking of pollution hotspots, identifying trends, and modeling the impact of meteorological factors, helping researchers and policymakers manage air quality and protect public health8. ML has proven to be a powerful tool in air pollution forecasting, owing to its ability to uncover complex, nonlinear relationships among multiple pollutants. Models such as neural networks, support vector machines (SVM), and ensemble learning techniques have shown enhanced performance compared to traditional deterministic models by effectively capturing dynamic interactions9–13. This study aims to estimate the spatial concentration of air pollution across Shiraz by leveraging multiple machine learning algorithms to identify the most influential gaseous pollutants. According to previous literature, two primary modeling categories for estimating air quality exist: knowledge-based and data-driven approaches14,15.

Related works

Numerous studies have focused on AQI prediction using ML and neural network models. Similarly, Zhou (2018) and Natarajan (2024) introduced neural network-based approaches that yielded strong AQI forecasting performance16,17. Gladkova and Saychenko (2022) used several machine learning models such as ARIMA, Facebook Prophet, and LSTM to solve the problem of predicting time series of particulate matter concentration values18.

Parveen et al. (2021) demonstrated that elevated concentrations of PM2.5, NO₂, and CO are associated with increased respiratory disease among industrial workers and their families. Moreover, long-termexposure to fine particles less than 2.5 μm in diameter may raise the risk of cardiovascular disease by 1%to 3%19. In a recent study, Bacanin et al. (2022) used GLSTM and Dragonfly optimization for air quality prediction and sensor localization20. Bacanin et al. (2024) tuned XGBoost using the Reptile search metaheuristic and SHAP to analyze toluene–benzene interactions21. Both studies support the use of metaheuristic‑optimized models for environmental forecasting, complementing our OIO/BBO/MVO‑MLP comparison. Also, Jovanovic et al. (2023) coupled XGBoost, optimized with a metaheuristic algorithm, with SHAP to identify key factors affecting benzo(a)pyrene concentrations. The results, self-adaptive sine‑cosine algorithm achieved the best performance (R2 = 0.9) revealing that temperature, arsenic, PM10, and NOx had the greatest impact on this pollutant. This work demonstrates the effectiveness of combining metaheuristic‑optimized machine learning with explainable AI to uncover complex environmental interactions, aligning with our approach of using nature‑inspired algorithms to optimize MLPs for air quality prediction22. Babic et al. (2025) used recurrent neural networks optimized with the elk herd algorithm to predict atmospheric benzene concentrations and reported high accuracy23.

Yin et al. (2026) proposed a hybrid deep learning model, CBLA, combining 1D-CNN, BiLSTM, an attention mechanism, and XGBoost for air quality prediction. The model achieved high accuracy on Beijing’s air pollution dataset by extracting spatial‑temporal features and weighting the influence of different conditions24.

By combining two algorithms into a hybrid framework, the limitations of each can be mitigated by the other’s advantages. Consequently, hybrid optimization approaches can determine the parameters of machine learning models with greater accuracy24. Valikhan Anaraki et al. (2023) evaluated nine machine learning models for rainfall–runoff prediction and found that the hybrid KNN–GTO model, combined with data preprocessing (EMD and PCA), achieved the highest accuracy (RMSE = 0.4741, R = 0.9607), significantly outperforming standalone models. Their work highlights the effectiveness of integrating metaheuristic optimization with machine learning for environmental forecasting25.

Chianeh et al. (2023) introduced a novel methodology for predicting the simultaneous removal efficiency of four dyes (AR33, RO7, AY3, MG) from wastewater. The approach combined a c‑vine copula function to generate synthetic data, machine learning models (ANN, ANFIS, LSSVM, LSTM) for modeling removal efficiency, and multi‑objective optimization to identify optimal conditions. The best performance was achieved at pH = 6.60, NaCl = 0.67 g/L, time = 96.93 min, and current = 0.01 A, yielding removal efficiencies of 98.88%, 37.02%, 91.19%, and 98.33% for AR33, RO7, AY3, and MG, respectively. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating data generation, machine learning, and multi‑objective optimization for complex environmental applications26.

Yang et al. (2023) focused on AQI prediction in Beijing and Taiyuan using multilevel decomposition and enhanced SVR, which improved MSE and RMSE metrics27. Studies by Keshirsagar and Shah (2022) comprehensively reviewed neural network models, regression, and mixed modelsin the field of air pollution28. Clearly, air pollution modeling has been a major focus of research, as reflected by the extensive body of literature on the topic. Given the inherent complexity of air pollution dynamics, most prior work has proposed specific strategies for prediction. However, a key research gap remains: The comparative application for training neural networks in the context of urban air quality forecasting has not been sufficiently investigated. The main contributions of this study are as follows:

First, this work presents the first comparative application of optics-inspired optimization (OIO), biogeography-based optimization (BBO), and multiverse optimizer (MVO) for training Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) networks specifically in the context of urban air quality forecasting.Second, it provides a systematic evaluation of these three under‑explored metaheuristics across various population sizes using a long‑term (2019–2024) air quality dataset collected from Shiraz, Iran.Third, a robust comparative framework is developed that offers detailed insights into the exploration–exploitation behavior of each algorithm when applied to complex, nonlinear environmental data.

Machine learning (ML)

ML focuses on algorithms that improve their performance through experience. They can find nonlinear relationships and patterns in datasets without being explicitly programmed to do so30,31. The process of analytical modeling, which turns ML algorithms into concrete ML models for use in intelligent systems, comprises four steps: data input, feature extraction, model building, and model assessment32. ML algorithms are commonly grouped into shallow and deep ML algorithms. Each ML algorithm has different strengths and weaknesses regarding its ability to process data. Shallow ML algorithms generally require feature selection to identify relevant attributes for model training. This task can be nontrivial and time-consuming if the dataset is high-dimensional or the context is unfamiliar to the model engineer. Common classes of shallow ML algorithms include linear regression, decision trees, and support vector machines (SVMs). Deep neural networks with multiple hidden layers and advanced neurons for automatic representation learning provide a computation- and data-intensive alternative30,32.

Multilayer perceptron (MLP)

A feedforward artificial neural network (ANN), called a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), has three layers: an output layer, one or more hidden layers, and at least one input layer. Except for the input nodes, every node is a neuron that uses a nonlinear activation function. MLPs are powerful tools for pattern recognition that are widely employed in various domains, including time-series prediction, picture and speech recognition, and energy consumption forecasting33,34.

Architecture

However, artificial neural networks are not without limitations. For one, there is no guarantee that they will converge to an optimal solution. Moreover, they tend to overfit the training data that is, they memorize it rather than learning the underlying patterns. To address these issues, researchers turned to deep architectures. The term “deep” in deep learning refers to the number of processing layers through which data must pass. A deep learning model consists of multiple layers stacked sequentially. The first layer, known as the input layer, contains units that send values to every neuron in the first hidden layer. The model’s predictions are then produced by the output layer, which is the final layer. The number of units in this output layer equals the number of target classes. The hidden layers, situated between the input and output layers, apply weights to the incoming data and then pass the result through an activation function. This activation function allows the network to introduce nonlinearity and learn complex relationships within the data35. In this work, we investigate the application of three well-known nature-inspired algorithms, optics-inspired optimization (OIO), biogeography-based optimization (BBO), and multiverse optimizer (MVO), to improve MLP model performance in air quality prediction. Each of these algorithms offers distinct advantages and qualities that can greatly increase the precision and generalizability of machine learning models. According to Wolpert and Macready’s No Free Lunch theorem36, no single optimizer is universally superior. Therefore, evaluating multiple algorithms with different search mechanisms here, physics‑based OIO, BBO, and cosmology‑based MVO is essential to identify the most suitable one for urban air quality forecasting.

Optics-inspired optimization (OIO)

One of the newest population-based metaheuristic algorithms that draws on physics is OIO37. The optical properties of convex and concave mirrors, which can be applied to solve a variety of difficult problems, served as the model for OIO. The light beams converge and reflect in the direction of the main axis when they hit the concave mirror. The light beams diverge and reflect away from the main axis when they strike the convex mirror. OIO’s capabilities for exploration and exploitation are managed through these mirror events. Multiverse optimizer (MVO)

The MVO, presented by Raziani & Azimbagirad (2022), is a recently developed metaheuristic algorithm inspired by cosmology, particularly multiverse theory. In the universe of MVO, the candidate solution is a universe, and its quality corresponds to its inflation rate. Higher-quality universes attract resources from lower-quality ones. MVO utilizes mechanisms such as white holes, black holes, and wormholes to balance exploration and exploitation: white holes allow high-quality universes to share their features, black holes remove poor features, and wormholes introduce random changes, thereby boosting diversity and avoiding local optima. MVO, coupled with MLP, optimizes the weights and biases by effectively exploring the weight space and overcoming local minima during training. The randomness in the wormhole provides immunity against overfitting and improves results on nonlinear datasets. Hybridizing MVO with a backpropagation algorithm enables MVO to find a near-optimal setting globally, while backpropagation fine-tunes that solution locally for better accuracy and convergence speed. Optimally performing MVOs serve admirably for a wide range of large-scale, high-dimensional tasks, such as classification and time series prediction. Inherently, MVO’s parallelizability makes it well-suited for resource-intensive application areas. This combination of exploration, exploitation, and fine-graining provides more effective training of multilayer perceptron models42,43.

According to the multiverse hypothesis, the universe wasn’t born from a single Big Bang but rather several of them. In this framework, researchers have mathematically modeled a wheel-like mechanism for transporting “universe products,” as well as wormhole tunnels (both white and black) that connect two different universes. A roulette wheel is then used to select the universe with the highest inflation rate, granting it a white hole after each repetition. Examination of statistical parameters

It’s critical to evaluate the model’s predictive performance, particularly for regression tasks such as estimating air quality levels. The accuracy of predictions is frequently assessed using several criteria, each offering a unique perspective on the model’s performance. The three most important measures are Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and R-squared (R²).

RMSE is the square root of the average squared discrepancy between the expected and actual values. Because errors are squared before averaging, this provides a clear indication of how far the forecasts are from the actual values. It is a popular tool known for its ability to highlight greater variances, making it suitable for situations where significant prediction errors must be avoided. Better model performance is indicated by lower RMSE values, indicating more accurate predictions. MLP optimization result

In the proposed hybrid models, the decision variables are the weights and biases of the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) network. For our specific architecture with 4 input features, one hidden layer containing 5 neurons, and one output neuron, the total number of decision variables is (4 × 5) + 5 + (5 × 1) + 1 = 31 variables (2–4 S). Each optimization algorithm (OIO, BBO, and MVO) iteratively adjusts these variables within the range [−1, 1] (after normalization) to minimize RMSE and maximize R².

The calibration methodology employed in this study follows a rigorous two-stage hybrid optimization framework designed to maximize predictive accuracy. Initially, the MLP architecture underwent comprehensive structural calibration, where in various network topologies encompassing hidden layer configurations, neuron quantities, and activation functions were systematically evaluated through 1000 iterations to identify the architecture yielding minimal baseline error. This foundational step ensured that subsequent metaheuristic optimization operated upon an inherently efficient neural structure. Following architectural determination, the optimized MLP was hybridized with three nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms: OIO, BBO, and MVO. These algorithms were utilized to calibrate the MLP’s synaptic weights and bias terms through sophisticated global search strategies, with each metaheuristic executing 1000 iterations across population sizes ranging from 50 to 500. This dual-phase calibration approach, combining architectural optimization with stochastic parameter refinement, enables robust convergence toward globally optimal solutions while mitigating the local minima entrapment inherent in conventional backpropagation training. To ensure statistical robustness against the stochastic nature of metaheuristics, each configuration underwent several independent runs with randomized initial populations. Performance metrics (RMSE, R², MSE) were computed as ensemble averages across these repetitions, with standard deviations reported to quantify uncertainty bounds. Convergence was determined via a dual-threshold approach: either achieving a plateau in objective function improvement (< 0.001 variation over 50 consecutive iterations) or reaching the maximum iteration limit (500).

The dataset was partitioned into 70% training and 30% testing subsets through stratified random sampling to maintain distributional consistency. This calibration methodology ensures that the reported optima represent globally robust solutions rather than local anomalies, providing readers with a reproducible protocol for hybrid neural network training in environmental forecasting contexts.

By contrasting the expected values of the hybrid design with the actual data, the results of the second stage are ascertained. Conclusions

This study aimed to develop and evaluate hybrid nature-inspired algorithm–MLP models for predicting air quality indicators in Shiraz, Fars Province, Iran, using a dataset covering CO, O₃, NO₂, SO₂, and AQI values from April 2019 to November 2024. The main objective was to optimize the training of the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model using three advanced metaheuristic algorithms: OIO, BBO, and MVO. The key findings from this research demonstrate that the MVO-MLP model outperformed the other two hybrid approaches in terms of prediction accuracy, achieving the lowest testing RMSE (6.71) and the highest testing R² (0.9903). The BBO-MLP model also showed competitive performance, while OIO-MLP delivered acceptable but relatively less accurate predictions. The novelty of this study lies in its comparative investigation of rarely used hybridizations (OIO-MLP, BBO-MLP, etc.) for air quality forecasting in a specific urban context, using a robust long-term dataset. This multi-algorithmic approach not only enhances neural network training but also reveals how different metaheuristics affect prediction efficiency. As for future work, we can build on this framework in a few ways. For example, we could bring in deep learning models like LSTMs, CNNs, or even the Flow Direction Algorithm (FDA), and combine them with metaheuristic methods. Adding weather variables and spatial data could also help improve prediction accuracy. Another direction is to use ensemble models or transfer learning to predict air quality across different regions. And finally, we could take this further by applying it in real time within smart environmental monitoring systems. All in all, this study shows that hybrid models combining optimization and machine learning have real potential as practical tools for forecasting and managing air pollution in cities.
