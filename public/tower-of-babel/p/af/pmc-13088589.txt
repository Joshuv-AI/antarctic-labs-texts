Molecular characterization of Eimeria necatrix surface antigen SAG8 and SAG36 genes and their immunoprotective efficacy in chicken
Authors: Feiyan Wang, Chi Zhang, Yongcui Feng, Danli Yan, Dandan Liu, Shijie Su, Zhaofeng Hou, Jinjun Xu, Jianping Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurface antigens (SAGs) are a class of membrane proteins anchored to the parasite surface via glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors. These antigens play critical roles in parasite adhesion and host cell invasion. However, studies on SAGs from Eimeria necatrix remain limited.ResultsIn this study, two sag genes, which exhibit differential expression in SZ and MZ-2 stages of E. necatrix, were cloned and expressed. The Ensag8 (XM_013584257.1) and Ensag36 (XM_013577436.1) genes, encoding proteins of 263 and 265 amino acids with predicted molecular weights of 27.83 kDa and 28.66 kDa, respectively. Bioinformatic analysis indicated that neither protein contains transmembrane domains, and phylogenetic classification placed both within the SAGA sub-family, with EnSAG8 showing close evolutionary proximity to EtSAG8. Recombinant proteins rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 were expressed predominantly as inclusion bodies in Escherichia coli, with apparent molecular weights of approximately 32 kDa and 36 kDa, respectively. Both rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 were recognized by a mouse anti-His tag monoclonal antibody and by convalescent serum from chickens infected with E. necatrix and E. tenella. The native EnSAG8 protein was detected in MZ-2 with an observed molecular weight of ~ 35 kDa, and EnSAG36 was detected in both SZ and MZ-2 at ~ 36 kDa. Immunolocalization analysis revealed that both native proteins are localized on the membranes of SZ and MZ-2. Transcriptional analysis indicated that Ensag8 is more highly expressed in MZ-2 than in SZ, whereas Ensag36 shows higher transcriptional levels in SZ than in MZ-2. Following the E. necatrix challenge, the anticoccidial index (ACI) of both the rEnSAG8- and rEnSAG36-immunized groups exceeded 160 at an immunization dose of 100 µg per chicken per immunization, with the rEnSAG8 group achieving a higher ACI than the rEnSAG36 group. Combined immunization with rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 conferred greater protection than immunization with either antigen alone.ConclusionsThis study provides insights into the characterization of SAGs from E. necatrix. The differential expression of Ensag8 and Ensag36 highlights their potential roles in different parasite stages. Immunization with these recombinant proteins demonstrated significant anticoccidial protection, with combined immunization offering the best results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05404-z.

Background

Chicken coccidiosis, caused by obligate intracellular parasites that belong to the genus Eimeria, was estimated to cause global economic losses exceeding £12.9 billion in 2022 [1]. Of the known seven species of Eimeria species that infect poultry, Eimeria necatrix is the most pathogenic species, causing significant morbidity and mortality globally. It particularly affects in 5–7-week-old broiler flocks or 9–18-week-old layer and breeder flocks [2]. Currently, chicken coccidiosis control relies on anticoccidial drugs, live vaccines, and environmental management. However, prolonged use of drugs can lead to resistance and residue accumulation, thereby compromising food safety. Although live vaccines are effective, they are costly and exhibit variable efficacy. Environmental management in high-density farming systems also requires significant resources [3–5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find new prevention and control strategies. Subunit vaccines, due to their safety, precision, and stable quality, can be used as alternatives to drugs and live vaccines [6]. Eimeria species possess a nuclear genome with 42 to 72 Mbp of DNA [7]. Those with a fully sequenced genome can express between 6,000 and 9,000 proteins across all developmental stages [8]. Screening effective antigens from a multitude of parasite proteins is the foundation for developing subunit vaccines.

Eimeria spp. are obligate intracellular parasites, and successful invasion of host cells by the parasite is crucial for establishing infection. Preventing the parasite from invading host cells is an important approach for controlling coccidiosis. Therefore, proteins involved in the invasion process may be potential target antigens that can be used in the development of subunit vaccines. Surface antigens (SAGs) are anchoring proteins characterized by a signal peptide at the amino terminus, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring sequence at the carboxyl terminus, and a hydrophobic domain [9]. These proteins are anchored to the parasite surface via a GPI anchor and play a critical role in the initial interactions between the parasite and host cells, mediating adhesion and invasion [10, 11]. Moreover, SAGs can effectively elicit an immune response in the host and demonstrate strong immunogenicity [12, 13]. Consequently, they are regarded as promising candidates for developing vaccines against parasitic infections.

In a prior study, our research group conducted transcriptomic analyses on unsporulated oocysts (UO), sporozoites (SZ), second-generation merozoites (MZ-2), and third-generation merozoites (MZ-3) of E. necatrix. This analysis identified a total of 109 sag genes, some of which showed differential expression [14]. Additionally, a proteomic analysis of UO, SZ, and MZ-2 stages revealed 90 SAG proteins, with several proteins exhibiting differential expression [15]. Ensag8 was found to be upregulated during the MZ-2 stage, whereas Ensag36 was upregulated during the SZ stage. It is well known that sporozoites and merozoites are the invasive stages of Eimeria parasites. Inhibiting parasite invasion during these stages is crucial for controlling coccidiosis [16]. Thus, this study selected two sag genes, Ensag8 (XM_013584257.1) and Ensag36 (XM_013577436.1), for cloning, expression, and localization analyses. Recombinant proteins rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 were produced and evaluated individually and in combination for their protective efficacy, providing a theoretical basis for the development of subunit vaccines against chicken coccidiosis. This study also lays the foundation for further research into the functional roles of SAG proteins in the host cell invasion process of Eimeria.

Methods

Parasites and animals

The Yangzhou strain of E. necatrix used in this study was isolated by Parasitology Research Laboratory, Yangzhou University, China, as confirmed by microscopic examination and sequence analysis of the internal transcribed spacer region of genomic DNA. This strain has been maintained in our laboratory [17]. One-day-old yellow-feathered chickens, purchased from Yangzhou Lihua Livestock and Poultry Co., Ltd., were housed in wire cages under coccidia-free conditions, and provided with sufficient feed and water without coccidiostats. Six-week-old specific-pathogen-free male BALB/c mice were purchased from Yangzhou University (Comparative Medicine Center) and maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions.

Preparation of SZ and MZ-2

A total of 2 × 104 E. necatrix sporulated oocysts were orally inoculated into twenty 20-day-old yellow-feathered chickens. Oocysts from the feces of 6–10 days after infection were isolated using the saturated salt solution flotation method, and incubated in a 2.5% potassium dichromate solution at 28 °C for 72 h [17]. Then, the sporulated oocysts were treated with pre-cooled 70% hypochlorite solution on ice for 5 min, and collected by centrifugation at 3,500 g for 15 min. After washed five times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the oocysts were disrupted with glass beads (150–212 μm in diameter, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to release over 90% of the sporocysts. The sporocysts were digested with 100 mL/L bile and 7.5 mg/L trypsin at 41 °C for 2 h to release over 90% of the SZ. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the SZ were washed with sterile PBS, resuspended, and purified by filtration through a G3 funnel [18]. The purified SZ were then stored at −80 °C for further use.

A total of 2 × 104 E. necatrix sporulated oocysts were orally inoculated into ten 20-day-old yellow-feathered chickens. After 138 h post-infection (hpi), the chickens were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the infected intestinal tissues were removed and homogenized, then sieved through 260- and 10-mesh sieves. The filtrate was centrifuged at 2,500 g for 5 min to collect the sediment. After washed twice with PBS, the pellet was resuspended in 30% percoll (Biosharp, Beijing, China) and performed centrifugation at 2,500 g for 15 min to purify the MZ-2. The supernatant was discarded, and the MZ-2 treated with red blood cell lysate (Solaibao, Beijing, China) at 4℃ for 30 min to remove most of the red blood cells. The sample was then centrifuged again at 2,500 × g for 5 min, and the purified MZ-2 were collected and stored at –80 °C for further use.

Cloning and sequencing of Ensag8 and Ensag36 gene

Total RNA of SZ and MZ-2 were extracted and transcribed into cDNA using a FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and Reverse Transcriptase kit™ (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) respectively, according to the manufacturer’s specific extraction procedure. The target cDNA was amplified using RNA LA PCR KitTM (TakaRa) and specific primers were designed based on the sag sequences (GenBank accession number: XM_013584257.1 and XM_013577436.1) (Table 1). The PCR amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 4 min; denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s; annealing at 55 °C for 15 s; extension at 72 °C for 1 min, repeated for 30 cycles; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The RT-PCR products were analyzed by 1.50% agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by purification and subcloning into the pMD18-T vector (TaKaRa). The signal peptide of the SAG protein was predicted using SignalP-5.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/). Transmembrane helices in protein were identified using TMHMM-2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/). The GPI anchor sequence of the SAG protein was analyzed online with PredGPI (https://busca.biocomp.unibo.it/predgpi/). The 3D protein structural models were built using AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates in IQ-TREE v1.6.12. The optimal amino acid substitution model was automatically selected using ModelFinder based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the LG + G4 model was identified as the best-fit model. Toxoplasma gondii SAG protein was used as the outgroup.

Construction and identification of the recombinant expression plasmids

Primers incorporating EcoR I and Hind III restriction sites were designed based on the Ensag8 and Ensag36 gene sequences, excluding the signal peptide regions (Table 1). The corresponding fragments were amplified by PCR using pMD18-T-Ensag8 and pMD18-T-Ensag36 plasmids as templates and subsequently ligated into the pET-28a(+) vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) at 16 °C for 10 h. The ligation products were transformed into DH5α-competent cells (TransGene, Beijing, China), and positive clones were identified by restriction enzyme digestion (Fig S2). Recombinant plasmids were sequenced and subsequently transformed into Rosetta (DE3) cells (TransGene), resulting in the constructs pET-28a(+)-EnSAG8/Rosetta and pET-28a(+)-EnSAG36/Rosetta, respectively.

Expression and identification of the recombinant protein and polyclonal antibody production

Expression of the recombinant protein

The recombinant bacteria were inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing 100 μg/mL kanamycin and cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 200 g for 4 h. Induction was then carried out with 1 mmol/L isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for an additional 4 h. The rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 protein were separately purified using a Ni–NTA affinity chromatography column and renatured with dialysates containing varying urea concentrations (6, 4, 2, 1 mol/L), as described previously [17]. Following affinity purification, residual endotoxins were removed from the recombinant protein using a commercial endotoxin removal kit (GenScript, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting proteins were separated with 12% SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 (Sigma). The concentrations of the purified recombinant proteins were determined using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Vazyme).

Convalescent sera production

The convalescent sera were prepared as previously described [17]. Briefly, four groups of 10-day-old chickens (5 chicks per group) were orally inoculated with 2,000 sporulated oocysts of E. necatrix, E. tenella, E. acervulina and E. maxima, respectively, followed by two booster inoculations of 2,500 sporulated oocysts at 10 and 20 days post-primary infection. Blood samples were collected 7 days after the second booster, and sera were separated and stored at − 80 °C.

Identification of the recombinant protein

The antigenicity of the recombinant protein was assessed by Western blot (WB). The purified rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 were subjected to 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to the nitrocellulose membrane (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), respectively. The membranes were blocked overnight at 4 °C with 5% skim milk (Biyuntian, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, they were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with the primary antibody, mouse anti-His tag monoclonal antibody (diluted, 1:10,000, BIO BASIC, Markham, Canada) and the convalescent serum (dilution, 1:200) of chicken infected individually with E. necatrix, Eimeria tenella, Eimeria acervulina or Eimeria maxima. After primary antibody incubation, the membranes were probed for 1 h at 37 °C with HRP-labeled rabbit anti-mouse IgG (diluted, 1:10,000, BIO BASIC) and HRP-labeled goat anti-chicken IgG (diluted, 1:10,000, BIO BASIC). Images were collected using the Tanon-5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon).

Polyclonal antibody production

Polyclonal antibodies (pAb) against rEnSAG8 or rEnSAG36 were raised in mice following the instructions of QuickAntibody-Mouse3W adjuvant (Biodragon, Beijing, China). The rEnSAG8 or rEnSAG36 were diluted to 2 μg/μL with PBS, respectively. Each recombinant protein was mixed with the Quick Antibody-Mouse 3 W adjuvant at a ratio of 1:1 (v/v) and administered via intramuscular injection to five 6-week-old male BALB/c mice, with a total injection volume of 100 μL per mouse. The sterile PBS mixed with QuickAntibody-Mouse3W adjuvant was used as control. Blood was collected 7 days after the second immunization, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 2,000 g for 10 min and stored at −80℃. The serum antibody titer was determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method as described in a previous study [19].

Characterization analysis of Ensag8 and Ensag36 gene

Detection of native EnSAG8 and EnSAG36

Native proteins were extracted from SZ and MZ-2. Protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Xinsaimei, Suzhou, China) were separately added to approximately 1 × 10⁸ SZ and MZ-2 resuspended in 300 μL of cell lysis buffer. The samples were subjected to ultrasonic disruption and lysed on ice for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent Western blot analysis. The primary antibodies used were mouse anti-rEnSAG8 pAb (diluted 1:200) and mouse anti-rEnSAG36 pAb (diluted 1:200).

Localization of EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 in parasites

Freshly purified SZ and MZ-2 were smeared onto slides, and fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After washed three times with PBST (PBS + 0.5% Tween-20), the slides were blocked in a solution containing 30 g/L BSA at 37 °C for 3 h. Mouse anti-rEnSAG8 pAb (diluted 1:200) and mouse anti-rEnSAG36 pAb (diluted 1:200) were used as the primary antibody and incubated in a humid chamber at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes with PBST (5 min each), FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (dilution, 1:100, KPL, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) was applied as the secondary antibody and incubated in a humid chamber at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by another three washes with PBST. After air-drying, an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (Beyotime, China) was applied to the slides, which were then sealed with coverslips. The results were observed and photographed using a laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) (Leica DM2500, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

Transcript levels of Ensag8 and Ensag36 gene in different developmental stages

The transcription levels of Ensag8 and Ensag36 in SZ and MZ-2 were detected using quantitative real-time PCR. The 5.8 S rRNA of E. necatrix was used as an internal reference gene, and primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software. (Table 1). The SYBR Green Master Mix fluorescent quantitative reagent kit (Vazyme) was used for the experiment. Three replicates were performed for each sample, and the Ct values of the gene in different samples were recorded. The relative expression levels of each group were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [20]. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

S.C. subcutaneous injection

Immunizations were administered via subcutaneous injection at one-week intervals. Complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for the first immunization, while incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich) was utilized for the second immunizations. Recombinant protein was diluted in PBS and mixed with the corresponding adjuvant at a 1:1 volume ratio. Blood samples were obtained from 3 chickens that randomly selected from each group at day 14 (one week following the first immunization) and day 21 (one week following the second immunization). All groups were challenged with 2.5 × 104 sporulated E. necatrix oocysts on day 21 except for the UU group.

Assessment of the protective effect of protein

All chickens were euthanized on 8 days post challenge (on day 29). An evaluation of protective efficacy was conducted based on lesion scores, survival rate, relative body weight gain (RWG), oocyst reduction (OR) and anti-coccidiosis index (ACI) [21]. Lesion scores were assessed on day 29 using a 0 to 4 scale, as described by Johnson and Reid [21]. Survival rate (%) was calculated based on the survival of chickens in each group. Body weight gain (BWG) was recorded between day 7 and day 21 (BWG1), and between day 21 and day 29 (BWG2). RWG (%) was calculated as follows: [BWG of the groups IC or UC/BWG of group UU] × 100. OR (%) was calculated with the formula: (OR of group UC—OR of groups IC)/OR of group UC) × 100. ACI calculated as follows: [RWG (%) + Survival rate (%)]—[Oocyst value + Lesion score]. To summarize, an ACI value below 120 was considered to indicate an inactive anticoccidial effect, 120–140 was classified as mild or slight, 140–160 as moderate, 160–180 as marked, and values above 180 were deemed excellent [22].

Serum antibody levels of chickens

ELISA was employed to detect serum antibody levels of the chickens. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4℃ with 1 μg/well of purified rEnSAG8 or rEnSAG36. For the combined immunization group, rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and coated. Plates were washed three times (15 min each) with PBST and then blocked with 1.0% BSA in PBS for 1 h at 37℃. After blocking, diluted serum samples (1:200) were added to the wells and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. Plates were then washed five times with PBST, and bound antibodies were detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-chicken IgY (H + L) antibody (1:20,000; GenScript). The optical density at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀) was measured using an ELISA reader (Sunrise-Basic, Tecan Trading AG, Männedorf, Switzerland). All samples were assayed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as means ± SEM for all treatments performed using the GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism 8.0, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used for statistical analysis. Significant differences were referred as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001.

Results

Cloning and sequencing of Ensag gene

Sequence analysis revealed that the full-length Ensag8 gene comprises 789 base pairs (bp) (Fig. S1), encoding a protein of 263 amino acids (GenBank accession number: PV889396.1). The predicted EnSAG8 protein contains a 23-amino acid N-terminal signal peptide and a 32-amino acid C-terminal GPI anchor sequence with the ω-site at Gln230. It is characterized by six conserved cysteine residues distributed between the N- and C-termini. The protein has an estimated molecular weight of 27.83 kDa, lacks a transmembrane domain, and has a calculated isoelectric point (pI) of 4.61. Similarly, the full-length Ensag36 gene is 795 bp in length (Fig. S2), encoding a 265-amino acid protein (GenBank accession number: PV877947.1). The predicted EnSAG36 protein contains a 20-amino acid N-terminal signal peptide and a 26-amino acid C-terminal GPI anchor sequence with the ω-site at Gln238. Like Ensag8, it contains six conserved cysteine residues between the termini. The estimated molecular weight is 28.66 kDa, and it lacks any transmembrane domain, with a calculated pI of 4.57.

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted based on the representative amino acid sequences of SAGA, SAGB and SAGC sub-family members in Eimeria, with T. gondii SAG protein was selected as outgroups. The results showed that EnSAG8 has 99.9% sequence similarity to the Houghton strain (XP_013432890.1). It is part of the SAGA sub-family and groups with EtSAG8 (Fig. 1). Consequently, the sag gene identified in this study was named Ensag8. Similarly, EnSAG36 shares 99.2% homology with the Houghton strain sequence (XP_013230797.1) and belongs to the SAGA sub-family. Since this protein has not been previously named, it was provisionally designated EnSAG36 in this study, based on the approximate size of its recombinant protein. This designation is putative and intended to facilitate reference until a standardized functional annotation becomes available.

Discussion

Surface antigens (SAGs) are located on the surface of the parasite and play a critical role in the invasion process [9]. The primary structure of SAG proteins comprises three main components: an N-terminal signal peptide, a C-terminal GPI anchor sequence (approximately 20 amino acids in length), and a cysteine-rich extracellular domain positioned between the signal peptide and the anchor sequence [9]. Based on the number of cysteine residues in the extracellular domain, the SAG family is divided into three sub-families: SAGA, SAGB, and SAGC. Members of the SAGA and SAGB sub-families contain six cysteine residues, whereas members of the SAGC sub-family contain four [23]. In the present study, both the EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 were found to have a GPI anchor sequence and six cysteine residues within the domain between the signal peptide and the hydrophobic region, aligning with the structural characteristics of SAG proteins. Phylogenetic analysis showed that EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 cluster within the SAGA sub-family. Previous research indicates that several SAG proteins from the SAGA sub-family can adhere to cultured cells, suggesting that adhesion may be a key functional attribute of SAGA sub-family members [18, 23, 24]. This finding suggests that EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 may play a critical role in the interaction between E. necatrix and host cells. Due to their adhesive properties, these proteins represent promising targets for developing vaccines aimed at disrupting the early stages of parasitic infection.

AlphaFold-based structural modeling reveals that both EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 share a similar architecture, comprising of seven α-helices and four β-strands, closely resembling the structural features of EtSAG19 [25]. The β-sheets are tightly packed, creating a rigid scaffold that stabilizes the overall protein conformation. The alpha helices are positioned around the periphery, contributing to both structural integrity and potential functional interfaces. Notably, the six cysteine residues in the core regions of EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 are likely to form stable cyclic motifs or intermolecular disulfide linkages, enhancing the structural stability of the proteins [26–28]. These disulfide bonds not only help maintain the protein’s folded state but may also play a crucial role in regulating protein activity or interactions with other molecules [29–31].

The complete amino acid sequence of EnSAG8 exhibited 100% identity with EtSAG8, and phylogenetic tree analysis clustered them within the same branch, indicating that they might share similar functions or have a close evolutionary relationship. EtSAG8 was first identified by analyzing 28,550 EST sequences from E. tenella SZ and MZ-2. Twenty specific EST clusters corresponding to EtSAG8 were found in MZ-2, whereas none were detected in SZ, suggesting that EtSAG8 expression is mainly restricted to MZ-2 [9]. Chicken anti-rEtSAG8 pAb successfully localized EtSAG8 in E. tenella MZ-2 [9], consistent with Western blot analysis of EnSAG8 in this study. EnSAG8 was detected in E. necatrix MZ-2 using mouse anti-rEnSAG8 pAb, but no corresponding band was observed in SZ. qPCR results indicated that Ensag8 transcription level was significantly higher in MZ-2 compared to SZ, suggesting that EnSAG8 expression is primarily regulated at the transcriptional level and may play a more significant role during MZ-2 stage. IFA analysis revealed membrane localization of EnSAG8 in both SZ and MZ-2, implying that low-level expression in SZ might still be functionally relevant. The discrepancy between IFA and WB results is likely attributable to the significantly lower EnSAG8 protein abundance in SZ, which falls below the detection limit of WB analyses. IFA analyses, as a more sensitive technique for detecting membrane-localized proteins, is capable of visualizing low-abundance surface proteins that are undetectable by WB analyses. In contrast, EnSAG36 was consistently detected in both SZ and MZ-2 using mouse anti-rEnSAG36 pAb, with matching results from both WB and IFA analyses. However, qPCR analysis showed that Ensag36 transcription level was significantly higher in SZ than in MZ-2, whereas the protein levels were comparable in both stages. This mismatch between mRNA and protein levels suggests post-transcriptional regulation. These findings indicate that EnSAG36 may be regulated through more complex mechanisms than EnSAG8 and could perform crucial functions during both early (SZ) and later (MZ-2) schizont development.

SAGs play a critical role in the invasion process of Eimeria and are among the first parasite molecules recognized by the host immune system, highlighting their potential as vaccine candidates. Previous studies have demonstrated that several SAGs can elicit protective immune responses in immunized hosts [12, 32–34]. In the present study, immunization with rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 significantly alleviated clinical symptoms and improved growth performance in chickens. Notably, co-immunization conferred enhanced protection against E. necatrix challenge compared to individual immunizations, with the highest ACI of 173.58 observed in the co-immunization group (rEnSAG8-M + rEnSAG36-M). In contrast, the co-immunization group (rEnSAG8-L + rEnSAG36-L) exhibited a reduced ACI of 159.35, which was lower than that of the single-antigen groups, possibly due to insufficient antigenic stimulation; however, this requires further investigation.

Our results indicate that both rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 can be recognized by convalescent sera from chickens infected with E. tenella, suggesting their potential for cross-protective immunity. This cross-reactivity is likely attributable to high evolutionary conservation between E. necatrix and E. tenella SAG proteins. Notably, EnSAG8 shares 99.9% amino acid sequence identity with EtSAG8, meaning they possess identical linear epitopes. EnSAG36 also exhibits 99.2% homology with its E. tenella ortholog. Furthermore, both proteins belong to the SAGA sub-family and share conserved structural features, including six cysteine residues forming three disulfide bonds and a similar architecture of seven α-helices and four β-strands, which likely preserves conformational epitopes across species. These highly conserved sequences and structural motifs may serve as shared antigenic determinants recognized by the host immune system, providing the molecular basis for cross-species immune recognition. However, epitope mapping was not performed in this study, and the specific epitopes responsible for cross-reactivity remain to be identified. Future studies should incorporate B- and T-cell epitope prediction and validation to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying cross-protection, and challenge trials with E. tenella are needed to confirm cross-protective efficacy.

In this study, we acknowledge the limitations in evaluating the immunoprotective efficacy of recombinant proteins. First, Freund’s adjuvant was used to maximize immune responses for proof-of-concept evaluation. As this adjuvant is not compatible with field poultry vaccination, the protective efficacy observed may be partly attributed to its strong immunostimulatory properties. Second, both rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 were expressed as inclusion bodies in the E. coli system, which may compromise the preservation of native conformational epitopes. Consequently, the immune responses elicited were likely biased toward linear epitopes. Third, although both rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 can be recognized by convalescent sera from chickens infected with E. tenella, suggesting their potential for cross-species immunity, the present study used only a single E. necatrix strain (Yangzhou strain) for challenge, and cross-protection against other strains remains to be evaluated. In addition, the immune mechanism underlying the observed protection was not fully elucidated, as only humoral responses and performance-based indices were assessed. Cellular and mucosal immunity, which are known to play critical roles in protection against Eimeria, were not evaluated. Future research should address these aspects, including the use of poultry-compatible adjuvants or delivery systems, eukaryotic expression systems or refolding strategies to preserve conformational epitopes, multi-strain challenge trials, evaluation of cellular and mucosal immune responses, and longitudinal immunity assessment, to further validate the vaccine potential of these antigens.

Conclusions

This study offers novel insights into the characterization of surface antigens (SAGs) from E. necatrix. The differential expression patterns of Ensag8 and Ensag36 suggest distinct roles for these antigens in the various developmental stages of the parasite. The identification of EnSAG8 and EnSAG36 as membrane-associated proteins with stage-specific expression profiles provides valuable information for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying host-parasite interactions and parasite invasion. Furthermore, immunization with recombinant rEnSAG8 and rEnSAG36 demonstrated promising anticoccidial effects, with the combined immunization offering superior protection. These findings lay the foundation for further investigations into the immune mechanisms involved in E. necatrix infections and highlight the potential of using SAGs as components of a multivalent vaccine strategy for poultry.
