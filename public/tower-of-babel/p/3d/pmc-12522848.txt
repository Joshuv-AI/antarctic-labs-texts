Global, regional, and national trends and inequality analysis of the total cancers and 29 cancer types from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Shuzhi Lin, Qian Liu, Lin Yin, Wei Liu, Xiaoying Zhu, Yifang Shen, Zimeng Li, Bianling Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCancer poses a formidable challenge to global public health, with significant disparities in care access persisting across regions and socioeconomic groups. Utilizing data from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 study, we analyzed epidemiological trends for total cancers and 29 site-specific cancer types between 1990 and 2021.MethodsTo quantify trends in cancer burden, we calculated the Estimated Annual Percentage Changes (EAPCs) in age-standardized incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 1990 to 2021. We also examined the temporal trends in socio-demographic index (SDI)-related disparities across all cancer types by using the slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index (CI). Additionally, we calculated the Quality of Care Index (QCI) and explored its correlation with SDI using Pearson correlation coefficients.ResultsIn 2021, the global incidence of total cancers reached 23.56 million cases, and we observed a growing trend in the age-standardized incidence. In contrast, the age-standardized mortality and disability-adjusted life years rates showed a downward trend. A substantial cancer burden was concentrated in high-SDI countries. Trends in global cancer inequality improved overall. We observed a significant shift in the burden of certain cancer types toward low-SDI regions in recent years, suggesting that the unequal distribution of the cancer burden has changed across regions. The correlation coefficient between the QCI and the SDI for total cancers was 0.87 in 2021. And the QCI was highly positively correlated with the SDI for 16 out of the 29 cancer groups, while non-melanoma skin cancer showed the lowest correlation.ConclusionThis study highlights that although global cancer treatment has improved and cancer deaths/DALYs inequality across countries has decreased, there are still challenges with high cancer incidence worldwide. Notably, cancer care quality remains closely correlated with the social development index. The distinctions between the 29 cancers have significant therapeutic ramifications, highlighting the significance of allocating resources and developing cancer treatment plans according to the kind of disease. Furthermore, these results provide insightful direction for the creation of future health policies. Introduction

Cancer, a complex disease involving interactions between internal and external cellular factors [1] and with limited treatment options, has become a major contributor to global mortality and is a global public health problem [2]. The 70th World Health Assembly adopted the resolution Cancer Prevention and Control in the context of an integrated approach (WHA70.12), which urges countries worldwide to adopt measures to reduce premature deaths from cancer and other non-communicable diseases. Cancer metastasizes by affecting the microenvironment of distant organs [3]. The metastatic nature of cancer is a key factor that affects patient survival [4]. Effective screening techniques facilitate early cancer detection [5, 6]. Combined with high-quality care and treatment, these techniques can reasonably prevent cancer metastasis, thus improving the survival rate of populations with cancer.

Imagine a world where your chance of surviving cancer depends not on the disease itself, but on your zip code or the color of your skin. Unfortunately, this is not just a hypothetical scenario; it’s a harsh reality for many. Social factors like income and education play a significant role in how cancer is identified and treated, leading to stark disparities in care [7].

Black patients are known to have lower rates of cancer surgery acceptance than white patients [8], and educational level has an independent effect on health outcomes among oncology patients [9]. Overall, considerable inequalities exist in the distribution of cancer-related health resources between countries with high and low human development indices [10].

Globally, we’ve made strides in understanding the social factors behind cancer disparities, but our knowledge is still piecemeal. We know that there are vast inequalities in how cancer-related health resources are distributed, especially between countries with high and low human development indices. Yet, the picture is not complete, studies have been limited to specific countries or cancers, and it’s unclear if these inequalities are improving over time.

Furthermore, because the malignancy and preventability of various cancer types vary greatly [8, 11, 12], understanding the degree to which the burden of various cancer types is concentrated in nations with varying degrees of development is crucial. This knowledge can enable countries to create targeted cancer control programs and guide the sensible distribution of scarce healthcare resources.

This study investigates the temporal trends of various cancers in important dimensions including incidence, mortality, prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and years of life lost (YLLs). The study further investigates absolute and relative disparities in the cancer burden by using the slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index (CI) to identify the characteristics of the distribution of health inequalities. These goals are achieved through a thorough analysis of the Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD2021). This study also calculates the Quality of Care Index (QCI) for cancer to propose strategic directions and potential intervention techniques to lower the number of early cancer deaths from many angles.

Methods

Data sources

In May 2024, the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation released the latest version of its global and regional quantitative health data, the GBD2021 [13]. The GBD2021 allows the assessment of the distribution of the burden of disease and injury for a total of 371 diseases and injuries in 204 countries and territories worldwide from 1990 to 2021. Data assessment is fully compliant with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) [14, 15], and the study was approved and endorsed by the University of Washington Ethics Committee.

In this study, illnesses were selected from 29 categories of malignant neoplasms or cancers with International Classification of Diseases-10 codes C00–C96; “total cancers” were also selected (see Supplementary Table 1). This analysis covers estimates from 1990 to 2021. The regions are divided into three categories: global, regional, and country-level (with 204 countries and territories). The primary analytical indicators cover the following six areas: deaths, incidence, prevalence, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs. The measures are age-standardized rate (ASR; per 100,000 population) and crude number.

The socio-demographic index (SDI) is a composite representation of three dimensions: lag distributed income, mean education for those aged 15 years and older, and total fertility rate (for those under the age of 25 years) [16]. Each country and territory was assigned an annual SDI value, which was employed in our analyses to systematically examine associations between socioeconomic development levels and health outcomes.

Estimated annual percentage change

We longitudinally analyzed the ASR data for the six primary indicators from 1990 to 2021 and calculated the Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) using the generalized linear model. The connection between ln(ASR) and year is consistent with the log-linear assumption, which is the same as the constant change assumption [17]. The following relationship exists [18]: ln(ASR) = α + β* year + σ and EAPC = 100 × (exp(β) − 1). The positives and negatives of β and EAPC vary in the same direction. EAPC and its 95% confidence interval are bigger than 0 or less than 0, indicating that the observations from 1990 to 2021 generally indicate an increasing trend or a downward trend. The more pronounced the trend change, the bigger the absolute EAPC value.

Health inequity analysis

The study introduced the WHO-recommended absolute inequality index, the slope index of inequality (SII), and the relative inequality index—the concentration index (CI) [19, 20]—to quantify the inequality of the SDI-related cancer burden across the dimensions of analysis. The SII is derived from the Pen’s Parade, which ranks all countries by their incidence, death, or DALY rates from highest to lowest. Each country’s position on the horizontal axis is determined by its share of the world’s population, represented as the width of a “step” [21]. The SII is the coefficient (slope) of a weighted linear regression model, which taps into the age-standardized differences in age-standardized health indicator values between the highest and lowest SDI groups globally and within a given region. In contrast, differences in health indicator values (i.e., CI) are the result of the area between the Lorenz curve and the equality line [22]. A positive value signifies that the burden is concentrated in high-SDI areas, and a value closer to 1 means greater inequality. There were six criteria used to classify the shifting trends in inequality between 1990 and 2021 [23].

Quality of care index

This study evaluated the variability in the quality of cancer healthcare services across countries using a multidimensional approach with the QCI developed by Farzadfar et al. [24]. First, we constructed four secondary indicators based on the age-standardized rates of six primary indicators that were extracted from the database using the following ratios of indicators: deaths/incidence, YLLs/YLDs, DALYs/prevalence, prevalence/incidence. We then performed data dimensionality reduction using principal component analysis, extracted the first principal component with the highest variance explained, and rescaled according to 0–100 to obtain the QCI value. We utilized Pearson correlation analysis to explore the correlation between the SDI and the corresponding QCI of cancers for 204 countries and territories worldwide. Yearly variations in the correlation were also considered. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) ranged from − 1 to 1. An absolute coefficient value closer to 1 signifies that the correlation between the variables is strong. An absolute coefficient value of more than 0.8 means hat the variables are strongly correlated with one another [25]. Positive and negative values were used to determine the direction of the correlation (i.e., whether the correlation was positive or negative).

Statistical analysis

In the statistical analyses, we measured the level of uncertainty using 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.41) The health inequality analysis was also conducted using STATA 16.

Results

EAPC for cancer burden

Globally, in 2021, the number of new cancer cases reached 23.56 million (more than twice as high as in 1990), and 9.83 million deaths were observed. The rise in incidence was largely attributable to non-melanoma skin cancer, which was 3.81 times more prevalent in 2021 than in 1990. Tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancers of the respiratory system were the main drivers of high cancer deaths and accounted for 20.51% of all deaths in 2021 and a substantial (86.79%) increase in the number of deaths in 2021 compared with 1990. Figure 1 shows the number of cancer incidence and mortality by sex. Breast cancer had the highest incidence among women and was the leading cause of mortality in this group. In the male population, the risk of death from gastrointestinal cancers such as stomach, colon, and rectum cancers was high and deserves considerable attention.

With EAPCs exceeding 1, thyroid, testicular, and non-melanoma skin cancers showed high increases in ASIR. Among the 21 regions (see Supplementary Table 2), the increase in ASIR for total cancers was mainly driven by the high-income North American region, which had an EAPC (1.63, CI: 1.3 to 1.97) that was five times higher than the world average, whereas Central Asia experienced the largest decrease (− 0.67, CI: −0.77 to − 0.57). In contrast to the increasing trend in ASIR, the age-standardized death rate (EAPC: −0.86, CI: −0.89 to − 0.82) and age-standardized DALYs rate (EAPC: −1.04, CI: −1.08 to − 1.01) of total cancers showed a decreasing global trend (Table 1), and the changes in DALYs were mainly in the YLLs dimension (EAPC: −1.08, CI: −1.11 to − 1.04). The results in Fig. 2 (B and C) show that countries with increasing trends in the burden of deaths and DALYs for total cancers were mainly concentrated in Africa (especially the Southern region) and Southeast Asia. Between 1990 and 2021, the ASIR of the age-standardized death rate, and the age-standardized DALYs rate were on a declining trend in only 39 countries (of a total of 204) mainly in Central Asia, Western Europe, and Eastern sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). At the global level, stomach cancer showed the most pronounced downward trend in disease burden in all of the cancer groups. The EAPC results for each cancer type across countries are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Health inequity analysis

We discovered significant absolute and relative inequities in SDI-related cancer burden as measured by age-standardized incidence, deaths, and DALYs rate (Figs. 3 and 4). As shown in Table 2, the global burden of disease for total cancers was more severe in higher-SDI regions where the SII of incidence showed an upward trend over time (Worsening inequality among higher SDI countries), signifying that the age-standardized incidence rate gap between the highest SDI and lowest SDI countries increased from 184.72 in 1990 to 194.66 in 2021. The SII for the remaining two burdens of disease dimensions showed substantial declines (deaths: from 69.33 to 25.46, DALYs: from 1489.87 to 302.90). The absolute inequitable change in the burden of cancer disease in the Caribbean, Central Latin American, Central sub-Saharan African, North African, and Middle Eastern regions was in Line with the global trend. Notably, within the high-income North American and Andean Latin American regions, the SII results demonstrated that lower-SDI areas bore a heavier cancer burden, and these gaps appeared to narrow further in 2021. Of the 19 regions examined in the study (Table 2), health disparities across all three dimensions show “Worsening inequality among higher SDI countries” in Eastern Europe, Oceania and Southeast Asia. East Asia, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia fall into the “Shift to higher burden among higher SDI countries” of health inequality in all three dimensions. In terms of changes in mortality inequality, Central Latin America, Andean Latin America, High-income North America, Central Europe and High-income Asia Pacific have all experienced shifts in areas of concentration of the burden, and the pattern types can be summarized as follows: Shift to higher burden among lower SDI countries.

Similarly, the CI results indicated that the burden of disease for total cancers was more concentrated in high-SDI countries, with inequities in the incidence dimension far exceeding those for deaths and DALYs; the CI for total cancers in 2021 was as high as 0.32 (Fig. 4). The inequities in ASIR for cancer were mainly reflected in non-melanoma skin cancer and malignant skin melanoma, both of which had a CI of more than 0.6. Inequities associated with SDI varied dramatically across cancer types, with liver cancer and esophageal cancer having the lowest levels of inequity by degree of health inequity. In terms of the direction of concentration, the burden of disease for cancers such as lip and oral cavity cancers and cervical cancer was concentrated in low-SDI areas, whereas malignant skin melanoma, mesothelioma, pancreatic cancer, and tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancers were concentrated in high-SDI areas and ranked high in inequity. Intriguingly, the CI for breast cancer decreased from 1990 to 2021 for incidence, deaths, and DALYs, and the direction of concentration of the burden of deaths and DALYs shifted from high-SDI to low-SDI areas.

Quality of care index

Globally, the QCI for total cancers steadily increased over time from 59.22 in 1990 to 73.21 in 2021. In 2021, Australasia (96.66) and Western Europe (93.60) had the highest level of care for total cancers. Eastern sub-Saharan Africa (41.47) and Central sub-Saharan Africa (38.31) were lagging. The top three QCI-ranked countries were Kuwait, Bermuda, and Finland, and the lowest-ranked country was Mongolia (Supplementary Table 3). As shown in Fig. 5, the QCI trend for most of the 29 cancer types was consistent with that of total cancers. The cancers with a QCI increase of more than 70% were nasopharynx (88.44%) and esophageal (72.35%) cancers and a total of five cancers experienced a QCI increase of more than 50%. Only non-melanoma skin cancer and mesothelioma showed a fluctuating downward trend in the QCI, with overall decreases of 0.16% and 1.56%, respectively. The QCI for breast cancer showed a downward trend from 1990 to 1994; however, since the turning point in 1994, the index slowly rose and ultimately increased by 5.79%.

The results in Fig. 6 support a highly positive correlation between QCI and SDI for total cancers; the correlation remained essentially unchanged over the 1990–2021 period. In 2021, the statistics showed a strong correlation (i.e., PCC > 0.8) between QCI and SDI for 16 of the 29 evaluated cancer groups. With a correlation coefficient of more than 0.9, Hodgkin lymphoma ranked first among the 29 cancer groups. Although non-melanoma skin cancer treatment showed the weakest correlation with SDI among all cancers (PCC = 0.06 in 2021), this malignancy demonstrated the greatest increase since 1990, with the coefficient increasing fivefold (from 0.01 in 1990). Moreover, the PCC between the QCI for liver cancer and the SDI showed a considerable increase, rising from 0.53 in 1990 to 0.63 in 2021, an 18.87% increase. In contrast, the PCC for ovarian cancer had experienced the largest decline, decreasing from 0.58 in 1990 to 0.51 in 2021, a 12.07% reduction.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed trends in disease burden, advances in equity, and levels of care for total cancers and 29 groups of cancer in various countries worldwide between 1990 and 2021. The high global incidence of cancer continues to pose a significant burden, with not only the number of cases substantially increasing but also the age-standardized incidence rate worsening. These findings are consistent with those of a previous study that predicted 35 million new cancer cases by 2050 [26]. The increase in incidence burden is largely attributed to population growth, population aging, and lifestyle changes [27–29]. Healthy lifestyles such as adequate fruit and vegetable intake, smoking abstinence, and appropriate physical activity significantly reduce the risk of cancer [30, 31]. Countries worldwide should actively promote the implementation of cancer prevention strategies, and high-SDI countries with a disproportionate share of the disease burden should pay more attention.

Encouragingly, the 21 st century has seen breakthroughs in cancer treatment in several areas [32], including targeted therapy, the development of immune checkpoint inhibitors, and the use of chimeric antigen receptor T-cell immunotherapy. Given both the upward trend in the global level of cancer care and this study that demonstrate positive outcomes in reducing the burden of age-standardized deaths and DALYs rate in cancer, steady progress is being made toward the World Health Organization’s sustainable development goal of reducing premature deaths from chronic non-communicable diseases by one-third by 2030 [33]. However, advances in cancer care are more pronounced in high-income countries, which have more health resources and technology. More than 90% of high-income countries provide comprehensive cancer care compared with only 15% of low-income countries [34]. Our study corroborates this finding by indicating a general increasing trend in the burden of cancer deaths in African countries and a high positive correlation between the quality of cancer care and a country’s SDI. The cancer burden in deaths and DALYs in low-income countries remains a serious challenge.

The study used the SII and the CI to quantify the degree of cancer inequity. Despite the global trend of reduced inequity of the cancer burden, inequity remains generally high, with the vast majority of the cancer burden still concentrated in high-SDI regions. However, between regions such as high-income North America, where the SDIs are all high, the study found that the SII indexes for the incidence, deaths, and DALYs dimensions were all negative, signifying that the cancer burden was concentrated in the countries with the lowest SDI rankings in the region. This may be because an increase in the SDI leads to an increase in life expectancy and advances in cancer surveillance methods [6], whereas the consumption of high-fat and high-sugar foods [35] and a gradual increase in unhealthy habits [36] lead to a significant increase in the cancer disease burden. When the country’s SDI rises to a certain level, a well-developed healthcare system and preventive measures significantly reduce the burden associated with cancer prognosis. This phenomenon also reinforces the fact that cancer is, to a certain extent, a “rich person’s disease.”

This study reveals differences in trends and inequities in the burden of different types of cancer, considers sex and region, and provides a scientific basis for countries to develop policies that maximize health benefits. For example, cervical cancer is a disease that can be prevented with HPV vaccination [37]; the burden of this disease is higher in countries with lower levels of economic development. These countries must prioritize the allocation of healthcare resources to increase vaccination rates and actively seek international cooperation. The incidence burden of breast cancer is currently concentrated in high-SDI regions, but the concentration of deaths and DALYs has shifted from high-SDI regions to low-SDI regions in recent years, a change that requires considerable attention in developing countries. To manage cancers such as tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancers and mesothelioma that have well-defined pathogenic factors, countries should incorporate tobacco control into their policy formulation and ban the use of known carcinogens such as asbestos.

The strength of this study is that it is the first to use the most recent version of the Global Burden of Disease data to analyze trends in the burden of disease for all types of cancer and assess inequalities. However, some study limitations remain. First, underreporting due to insufficient surveillance systems in low-income countries may have resulted in an underestimation of the cancer burden in some cases. Second, the health inequity analysis used in this study makes it difficult to quantify the influence of environmental variables on cancer burden, further prospective studies may improve the study’s reliability and validity. Finally, the study results cannot be specifically linked to any one form of cancer because we employed a wide range of analytical techniques and examined several cancer types. Although this diversity provides a wide perspective, it also increases the difficulty in conducting a thorough analysis of a particular cancer.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that the continued rise in the burden of cancer incidence should make the focus on screening and prevention a high priority for policymakers, especially in high-SDI regions where the burden of incidence is more concentrated. Despite the trend of decreasing national inequalities in cancer burden, uneven levels of QCIs across countries with different SDIs should not be ignored. In conditions such as breast cancer in which the burden of disease is gradually concentrating in low-SDI regions, low-SDI countries should develop management strategies as early as possible. Countries must allocate healthcare resources while considering the unique requirements and variations in cancer types. Major obstacles remain on the path to achieving worldwide cancer control.
