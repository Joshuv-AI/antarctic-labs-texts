(20S)-protopanaxatriol attenuates Ang II-induced renal injury via PTPN1-mediated AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway
Authors: Risheng Zhao, Zhuoqun Wang, Chang Liu, Linxin Zhang, Min Zhang, Huizhu Du, Gege Yang, Haiming Sun, Wei Liu, Shuang Yan, Mengyang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background(20S)-Protopanaxatriol (PPT), a bioactive triterpenoid from Panax ginseng, has been used in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) to tonify kidney yang and regulate blood pressure. The research aimed to elucidate PPT's protective effects against angiotensin II (Ang II)-induced renal injury.MethodsA C57BL/6 mouse model of kidney injury was established by continuous infusion of Ang II for 4 weeks using an osmotic minipump. Treatment with PPT was initiated two weeks after Ang II infusion and administered daily for 14 days. Molecular docking, drug affinity response target stability (DARTS), and cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) were employed to identify and validate potential targets of PPT in alleviating Ang II-induced renal injury. Additionally, biochemical assays, histopathological analysis, and Western blotting were performed to assess renal function, tissue damage, and signaling pathway modulation.ResultsOur findings demonstrated that PPT exerted protective effects against Ang II-induced renal dysfunction in mice by significantly attenuating renal fibrosis and inflammation. Mechanistically, PPT targeted PTPN1 and inhibited the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby effectively alleviating autophagy dysregulation in both renal tissues of mice and Ang II-stimulated NRK-52E cells in vitro. Molecular docking, DARTS, and CETSA experiments confirmed PTPN1 as a direct target of PPT, providing a molecular basis for its renoprotective effects.ConclusionThese findings validate ginseng's traditional use in kidney disorders and demonstrate PPT's potential as a therapeutic agent for hypertensive nephropathy by targeting PTPN1/AMPK/mTOR signaling. Further clinical exploration is needed to translate these results into practice.

Introduction

The rising incidence of hypertensive nephropathy associated with urban population aging has become a predominant contributor to end-stage renal disease development [1]. As a frequent complication of chronic hypertension, hypertensive nephropathy manifests through pathological features including glomerulosclerosis, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration [2]. The progression involves hypertension-induced activation and injury of glomerular mesangial cells, leading to enhanced Ang II production [3]. This principal renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) mediator exerts its critical pathophysiological effects through various mechanisms [[4], [5], [6]]. Notably, renal injury triggers intrarenal renin-angiotensin system (RAS) activation via upregulating multiple components, including angiotensinogen, ACE, renin, and Ang type 1 receptors [7]. Particularly, ACE and renin collaboratively regulate Ang II synthesis, which initiates BP-independent renal damage through complex cellular processes. Recent mechanistic studies reveal that Ang II binding to AT1 receptors induces receptor-effector complex internalization, facilitating intracellular calcium elevation and subsequently stimulating NF-κB-mediated transcriptional upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines NHE-3 exchangers, and growth factors [8]. Such molecular cascades underscore the necessity for exploring novel therapeutic agents targeting Ang II-induced oxidative stress in hypertensive nephropathy management.

Panax ginseng Meyer is a traditional Chinese medicine with a long history of use; its saponins (ginsenosides) are considered the main active ingredients [9]. (20S)-Protopanaxatriol (PPT, Fig. 1A) is one of the most important active ingredients, has antioxidant, anti-apoptosis and anticancer effects and also becomes a potential supplement to prevent or treat skeletal muscle aging. Protopanaxatriol, a dammarane-type tetracyclic triterpenoid sapogenin, has garnered significant attention due to its potent biological activities [10]. The stereoisomer, characterized by its S-configuration at the C-20 position, exhibits distinct pharmacological properties compared to its (20R)-counterpart [11]. Modern research confirms ginseng's nephroprotective effects, primarily attributed to ginsenosides, which modulate oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis [12] Studies demonstrate efficacy in diabetic nephropathy and chronic kidney disease by targeting pathways like TGF-β/Smad and Nrf2 [13]. These findings bridge historical applications with contemporary pharmacology, supporting ginseng's potential in renal therapy. PPT significantly protects renal function and alleviates renal damage in models of kidney injury, nephritis, fibrosis, and diabetic nephropathy [13]. Recent studies have demonstrated that PPT possesses remarkable anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antifibrotic effects, making it a potential therapeutic agent for kidney diseases [14]. Additionally, Han et al. found that PPT saponins inhibit uptake processes and cell proliferation in renal proximal tubular cells through the phospholipase A2 signaling pathway and c-fos/c-jun genes [15,16]. This study aims to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the renoprotective effects of PPT in hypertensive nephropathy, with a focus on its role in regulating inflammation, autophagy, and fibrosis. Materials and methods

Reagents

(20S)-Protopanaxatriol (PPT, CAS No. 30636-90-9) was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (China). Angiotensin II (Ang II) and Trodusquemine were acquired from Aladdin (USA; Cat. #4474-91-3) and MedChemExpress (USA; Cat. #186139-09-3). For in vivo administration, Ang II and PPT were formulated in a 0.5 % sodium carboxymethyl cellulose suspension. In vitro experiments utilized DMSO as the solvent for Ang II, PPT, Trodusquemine.

Antibodies against PTPN1 (Cat. #11334-1-AP), P62/SQSTM1 (Cat. # 18420-1-AP), Beclin 1 (Cat. # 11306-1-AP), LC3 (Cat. # 14600-1-AP), IκBα (Cat. # 10268-1-AP), p-P65 Ser468 (Cat. # 14600-1-AP) were procured from Proteintech (China). p-AMPKα Thr172 (Cat. #2535), AMPKα (Cat. #2532), p-mTOR Ser2448 (Cat. #5536) and mTOR (Cat. #2983) were procured from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Antibodies against COL-IV (ab6586), lamin B (ab133741), TGF-β1 (ab92486), P65 (ab32536), and p-P65 (ab31624) were procured from Abcam (UK). F4/80 (29414-1-AP) and CD86 (13395-1-AP) were obtained from Proteintech (IL, USA). GAPDH (5174; Cell Signaling Technology) served as the loading control.

Commercial assay kits included an Ang II ELISA kit (Shanghai Tongwei Biological Technology, China) and biochemical detection kits for serum creatinine (Cr; C011-2-1), urinary albumin (Alb; E038-1-1), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN; C013-2-1) from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (China). Histological staining reagents (Masson's trichrome: G1340; Picrosirius Red: S8060; H&E: G1120) were commercially sourced from Solarbio Life Sciences (China).

Ang II-induced kidney injury model

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (USA) guidelines and ARRIVE ethical reporting standards, with protocol approval from the Beihua University Animal Policy and Welfare Committee (SCXK(JI)2023-0002) [17].

Thirty male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were acquired from Changchun Yisi Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd., and maintained under standardized conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature, ad libitum diet). The dose of PPT given to mice via oral gavage was determined with reference to prior studies [[18], [19], [20]]. After a 2-week acclimatization, mice were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 6/group).1.Control (CON): 0.5 % CMC-Na vehicle.2.Ang II: Ang II infusion +0.5 % CMC-Na.3.Ang II + 10 mg/kg PPT: Ang II + 10 mg/kg/day PPT.4.Ang II + 20 mg/kg PPT: Ang II + 20 mg/kg/day PPT.5.PPT (20 mg/kg): 20 mg/kg/day PPT alone.

Hypertensive renal injury was induced via 4-week subcutaneous Ang II infusion (1000 ng/kg/min) using Alzet micro-osmotic pumps (MODEL 1004, USA) [21]. Post-surgery, mice received neomycin (1 g/L) in drinking water and wound disinfection. Systolic blood pressure (BP) was monitored weekly using a tail-cuff telemetry system (BP-2010A, Softron Biotechnology, Japan) [22]. PPT treatment commenced 2 weeks post-Ang II infusion and continued for 4 weeks.

Following the experimental period, mice were euthanized under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia. Urine, blood, and renal tissues were collected for biochemical analysis. Urinary albumin (Alb), serum creatinine (Cr), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were quantified using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Tissue Ang II concentrations were measured via ELISA (Shanghai Tongwei Biological Technology, China).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Group differences were assessed using one-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc tests. For longitudinal measurements, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons correction was applied, assuming homogeneity of variance across datasets. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0.

Detailed methods are described in the supplementary file.

Results

PPT ameliorates Ang II-induced renal dysfunction and renal fibrosis

To evaluate PPT's therapeutic potential in hypertensive renal injury, we established an Ang II-induced hypertensive nephropathy model through continuous subcutaneous Ang II infusion (1000 ng/kg/min) via osmotic pumps. PPT treatment (10 or 20 mg/kg) commenced 14 days post-Ang II administration (Fig. 1B). We found that Ang II significantly increased serum Ang II levels and systolic blood pressure, which were unaffected by PPT treatment (Fig. 1C–D). Renal functional analysis revealed PPT significantly attenuated Ang II-induced increases in serum creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (Alb/Cr) (Fig. 1E–G). Histopathological assessment via PAS staining demonstrated PPT-mediated mitigation of glomerular basement membrane thickening, mesangial expansion, and tubular epithelial damage (Fig. S1). These findings collectively confirm PPT's renoprotective efficacy against Ang II-induced structural and functional renal impairment, independent of blood pressure modulation.

Histopathological evaluation revealed PPT's protective effects against Ang II-induced renal damage. H&E staining showed PPT treatment substantially ameliorated glomerular structural disintegration in Ang II-infused mice (Fig. 1H). Fibrotic progression, a hallmark of hypertensive nephropathy pathogenesis, was quantified using Picro Sirius Red and Masson's trichrome staining, demonstrating significant fibrosis reduction in PPT-treated groups versus Ang II controls (Fig. 1I–L). Molecular analyses corroborated these findings: Ang II upregulated fibrotic markers Col4 and Tgfb mRNA (Fig. 1M–N) and their protein products COL-IV/TGF-β1 (Fig. 1O–P), effects potently reversed by PPT. These multimodal data establish PPT's capacity to counteract Ang II-driven structural degeneration and fibrotic remodeling in renal tissue.

The potential target proteins of PPT were determined through network analysis and the effect of PPT on autophagy of renal cells was confirmed

We use https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/and http://swisstargetprediction.ch/predict potential targets for PPT. As shown in Fig. 2A, the PTPN1 protein ranked among the top hits. Molecular docking simulations predicted PPT's binding site on PTPN1, proteins 24ARG, 46TYR, 182PHE, 217ALA, 219ILE form hydrophobic interaction with small molecules, and 24ARG, 262GLN form hydrogen bonding with small molecules. The binding energy of protein and small molecule is −6.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 2B). In addition, to benchmark the binding affinity of PPT, we have selected Finerenone, a clinically used medication for kidney disease, as a positive control ligand for comparison. Molecular docking experiments revealed that the binding energy of Finerenone to PTPN1 was −6.2 kcal/mol (Fig. S2). Notably, the binding affinity of PPT to PTPN1 (−6.4 kcal/mol) was slightly higher than that of Finerenone, predicting a superior binding affinity for the PTPN1 active site. Direct binding was confirmed through drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay, showing increased protease susceptibility of PTPN1 in PPT-treated cell lysates (Fig. 2C). Protective role of NF-κB in regulating autophagy

Autophagy and the NF-κB signaling pathway are closely related in inflammation [24]. Ang II functions as a critical pathological mediator by activating the NF-κB pathway-a central signaling axis for its pro-inflammatory effects [25]. PPT treatment significantly attenuated renal infiltration of pro-inflammatory CD86+ and F4/80+ macrophages in Ang II-challenged mice (Fig. 3C), indicating immunomodulatory activity. Mechanistically, Ang II triggered IκBα degradation and p65 phosphorylation (NF-κB activation markers) in renal tissues, which PPT potently inhibited. Nuclear translocation of p65-a hallmark of NF-κB pathway activation [26] -was markedly suppressed by PPT, as evidenced by reduced nuclear p65 accumulation (Fig. 3D–E). These findings collectively demonstrate PPT's capacity to disrupt Ang II/NF-κB signaling cascades through dual inhibition of cytoplasmic pathway activation and nuclear translocation.

IHC staining indicated that Ang II infusion decreased the ratio of p‐mTOR/mTOR, while increased the ratio of p‐AMPK/AMPK compared to the control group, which were all attenuated after PPT treatment (Fig. 3G). Consistent with in vivo study, PPT treatment significantly increased the ratio of p‐mTOR/mTOR and decreased the ratio of p‐AMPK/AMPK in Ang II stimulated kidney tissues (Fig. 3H–I). These results suggested that PPT treatment suppressed the AMPK/mTOR signalling pathway in vivo.

PPT reduces Ang II‐induced fibrosis in NRK-52E cells

Building on in vivo antifibrotic observations, we investigated PPT's effects on renal epithelial cells in vitro. Dose-response screening revealed concentration-dependent inhibition of NRK-52E proliferation, with 20 μM showing marked cytotoxicity (Fig. 4A). Subsequent experiments therefore utilized sub-cytotoxic concentrations (5–10 μM). Ang II stimulation significantly upregulated profibrotic markers COL-IV and TGF-β1 as well as the mRNA levels of their encoding gene (Col4 and Tgfb) in NRK-52E cells, mirroring in vivo fibrotic pathology. PPT treatment dose-dependently suppressed these Ang II-induced elevations in both profibrotic protein and their corresponding mRNA levels (Fig. 4B–D), confirming its conserved antifibrotic mechanism across experimental models. Consistent with the in vivo findings, Ang II upregulated the protein level of PTPN1 in cells, while PPT attenuated this Ang II-induced increase (Fig. 4E–F). Discussion

Our study elucidates a novel protective mechanism whereby PPT ameliorates hypertensive inflammatory kidney injury through PTPN1-mediated regulation of the AMPK/mTOR/autophagy axis. These findings significantly advance current understanding of ginsenoside metabolites in renal protection and provide a targeted therapeutic strategy for hypertension-induced nephropathy (Fig. 6).

Current clinical therapeutic options for hypertensive nephropathy, including thiazide diuretics, calcium-channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin-receptor blockers [28], are effective in normalizing BP levels. However, these treatments are associated with certain side effects and challenges in precise dosage control. These limitations highlight the need for novel drug candidates to enhance treatment efficacy.

(20S)-Protopanaxatriol (PPT), a bioactive ginsenoside metabolite, has emerged as a multifunctional modulator of autophagy and inflammatory pathways across diverse disease models. PPT inhibits D-galactose-induced brain aging in mice by promoting mitochondrial autophagic flow by increasing the expression of TFEB/LAMP2 protein [18]. Ginsenoside PPT predicts the potential binding of PPT to Akt through molecular docking and inhibits the unprotected autophagy and apoptosis of triple-negative breast cancer cells induced by the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [20]. PPT affects the AMPK family proteins, mTOR and IGF-1 transcription factors, and targets the IGF-1/mTOR and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways to prevent aging-related kidney diseases [14]. This dual regulation of upstream signaling and downstream lysosomal biogenesis distinguishes PPT from rapamycin analogs. In inflammatory bowel disease, PPT of ginseng saponins significantly inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation in multiple primary cells, suppressed systemic inflammation, restored liver function, and attenuated liver inflammation as well as fibrosis in MCD--induced mouse MAFLD [29]. Similarly, in ulcerative colitis models, PPT intervention can regulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1 in serum, as well as the expressions of MPO and NO in the colon, significantly inhibiting metabolic dysfunction in ulcerative colitis mice [30]. Notably, PPT significantly improves the inflammatory response in T2DM mice by inhibiting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in serum and the expression of genes in the liver. It also enhances the antioxidant capacity by increasing serum superoxide dismutase and reducing serum malondialdehyde levels [31]. These pleiotropic effects position PPT as a unique autophagy-inflammatory modulator with tissue-adaptive properties. Our findings further reinforce the antioxidative properties of PPT in combating hypertensive nephropathy. The reductions in urea nitrogen and serum Cr levels induced by PPT indicated its effectiveness in treating kidney injury. Moreover, H&E, Picro Sirius Red, and Masson's three-color staining experiments revealed that PPT significantly alleviates renal fibrosis in mice.

Growing evidence underscores the pivotal role of autophagy markers-p62, LC3-II/LC3-I, and Beclin1-in various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cancer, and metabolic syndromes [32]. In Alzheimer's disease (AD), p62 accumulation indicates impaired autophagic clearance of toxic protein aggregates, while reduced LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin1 levels correlate with defective autophagy, exacerbating neuronal damage [33]. Conversely, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), elevated Beclin1 and LC3-II/LC3-I ratios are linked to excessive autophagy, promoting tumor survival under metabolic stress [34]. In our study, the downregulation of p62 and elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio suggest that PPT enhances autophagy activation, while the upregulation of Beclin1 further supports autophagosome formation. These effects were closely associated with PPT-mediated AMPK/mTOR pathway modulation, wherein AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition collectively promoted autophagy, thereby alleviating inflammatory kidney injury. This aligns with prior studies demonstrating that AMPK/mTOR-driven autophagy suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine release [35]. Notably, autophagy induction has been implicated in mitigating renal fibrosis and inflammation in hypertensive nephropathy [36], reinforcing our findings that PPT's targeting of this axis could represent a therapeutic strategy for autophagy-related inflammatory diseases.

Ample evidence has demonstrated the pivotal role of the hyperactive NF-κB signaling pathway in the progression of renal fibrosis. Hyperactive NF-κB leads to the activation and recruitment of immune cells, which triggers inflammation. This inflammatory response, in turn, induces OS through the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species by activating leukocytes and resident cells. These events mediate organ injury through mechanisms such as apoptosis, necrosis, and fibrosis [37]. During NF-κB activation, IKKs phosphorylate IκBα, leading to its degradation. This releases the NF-κB p50/p65 subunits, resulting in their phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to regulate gene expression in nuclei [38]. In our study, we observed that in the kidneys of mice and NRK-52E cells exposed to Ang II, p-P65 protein levels were upregulated, IκBα protein levels were downregulated, and nuclear P65 levels were elevated. PPT treatment significantly reversed these changes, highlighting its role in kidney protection by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. Our findings align with the study of Wang et al., which demonstrated that Cofilin1 is involved in hypertension-induced renal damage by regulating NF-κB in renal tubular epithelial cells [39]. Similarly, our study demonstrates that PPT mitigates Ang II-stimulated kidney damage by suppressing the inflammatory response driven by the autophagy/NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings emphasize the potent anti-inflammatory properties of PPT, suggesting its potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for hypertensive nephropathy. It is important to note that the pharmacokinetic profile of PPT remains to be fully elucidated. The lack of comprehensive PK data is a limitation. Future studies characterizing the PK properties of PPT will be crucial for optimizing its dosing regimen and bolstering its potential as a therapeutic agent. We have now incorporated a discussion on this point in the revised manuscript.

Conclusion

In summary, our study unveils a previously unrecognized mechanism by which PPT attenuates hypertensive inflammatory kidney injury via PTPN1-dependent modulation of the AMPK/mTOR/autophagy pathway. These findings not only deepen the understanding of the renal protective effects of ginsenoside metabolites but also highlight PPT as a promising therapeutic candidate for hypertension-associated nephropathy. Targeting the PTPN1/AMPK/mTOR/autophagy axis may offer a novel strategy to mitigate renal inflammation and injury in hypertensive conditions, paving the way for future translational research.
