Hospitalisation, associated factors and out-of-pocket expenditure among patients with multimorbidity in Kerala, India: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sherief Adhila, Jeby Jose Olickal, Kavumpurathu Raman Thankappan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo estimate the rate of hospitalisation, identify associated factors and assess out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) among adults with multimorbidity in Kerala, India.DesignCommunity-based cross-sectional study.SettingTwo districts of Kerala, representing different geographic regions of the state.ParticipantsA total of 432 adults aged ≥30 years with multimorbidity were selected using multistage cluster sampling; 52.3% were women.Outcome measuresSelf-reported hospitalisation in the preceding 12 months and OOPE related to multimorbidity.ResultsHospitalisation in the past year was reported by 26.62% of participants (n=115; 95% CI 22.5% to 31.1%). Among them, the majority (n=78, 67.83%) were admitted to government hospitals, and most hospital stays lasted <7 days (n=77, 67%). Hospitalisation was significantly higher among residents of Kollam district (adjusted prevalence ratio (APR) 1.52; 95% CI 1.09 to 2.12), participants with health insurance (APR 1.38; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.89) and those with more than two chronic conditions (APR 1.76; 95% CI 1.28 to 2.41). Among participants who incurred OOPE (90.7%), the median annual expenditure was ₹30 720 (US$353) (IQR ₹17 850–59 725). Medicines constituted the largest share of OOPE (61.7%).ConclusionOne in four adults with multimorbidity in Kerala reported hospitalisation. A significant proportion of OOPE was for drugs. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted financial protection measures, equitable resource allocation and strengthening of primary care services to reduce avoidable hospitalisations and economic burden. The increase in hospitalisation among those with insurance coverage requires careful attention from policy makers. Regulation of drug prices may be required to reduce the financial burden imposed by drugs.

Introduction

Multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions within an individual, has emerged as a major public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Globally, the burden of multimorbidity is substantial, with a recent meta-analysis estimating a prevalence of 37.2% across community-based populations.1 In India, this figure is 32.1%, with wide interstate variations.2 A study from the Pathanamthitta district of Kerala, the district with the highest proportion of older adults in the state, reported a multimorbidity prevalence of 45.4% among adults aged 30–69 years, the highest in the country.3

The presence of multimorbidity has profound implications for healthcare utilisation and out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE). Individuals with multimorbidity often require polypharmacy, frequent healthcare interactions and multiple specialist consultations, increasing both outpatient and inpatient service use.4 5 Hospitalisations in this group are frequently necessitated by acute exacerbations, poor care coordination or late presentation of chronic conditions. Recent studies using nationally representative data have estimated that nearly 13.75% of individuals with multimorbidity in India experience hospitalisation within a year.6 One large-scale analysis showed that more than 63% of households with members suffering from multimorbidity incurred catastrophic health expenditure, compared with 46.7% for those with single chronic conditions.7

OOPE continues to dominate India’s health financing landscape. Although the overall share of OOPE in total health expenditure has declined nationally from 69.4% in 2004–2005 to 47.1% in 2019–2020, it still represents a considerable financial burden on households.8 The situation is worse in Kerala, which paradoxically combines high public sector health infrastructure with one of the highest OOPE rates among the Indian states, particularly for chronic conditions and inpatient care.9 National Health Accounts data confirm that Kerala’s OOPE is disproportionately driven by the management of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and hospitalisation.10 Factors contributing to this include a preference for private-sector care, longer hospital stays among older patients and inadequate outpatient coverage under existing insurance schemes.11

Several individual- and system-level determinants influence the risk of hospitalisation and financial distress among people with multimorbidity. These include advancing age, female gender, lower educational status and higher socioeconomic position.12 13 Kerala’s unique demographic profile, characterised by India’s highest proportion of older adults and a high urbanisation rate (47.7%), substantially increases demand for chronic disease management. The state has the highest hospitalisation rate of 10.2% in the general population, compared with the national average of 2.9%, and this gap widens among older adults.14 Other studies from Odisha and Punjab show that multimorbidity-related inpatient care is significantly higher in private facilities and often results in catastrophic expenditure, especially for diseases such as diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and stroke.15 16 Notably, those seeking care in private-sector facilities incur significantly higher OOPE than those seeking care in public facilities.17

The impact of multimorbidity is not limited to physical health alone; it is also closely linked to functional limitations and disability. Nearly 90% of older adults with multimorbidity in India experience some level of disability, further compounding their healthcare utilisation and financial burden.18 Even in a relatively better-performing health system like Kerala, OOPE contributes to financial distress among the older adults, particularly among lower-income and socially disadvantaged groups.19 20 There is also a growing recognition that standard definitions and measurements of multimorbidity, disability and healthcare expenditure need to be better harmonised to guide national and subnational interventions.21 Data on hospitalisation, associated factors and their OOPE are limited in Kerala. Therefore, we aimed to assess the rate of hospitalisation, identify its associated factors and estimate OOPE among individuals with multimorbidity in Kerala.

Methodology

Study design

We conducted a community-based cross-sectional analytical study.

Study setting, population and eligibility

The study included community-dwelling adults aged 30 years and above residing in the selected areas of Kollam and Thrissur districts. The lower age threshold of 30 years was chosen because the prevalence of multimorbidity is low in younger adults, whereas chronic non-communicable conditions and their clustering become substantially more common from the fourth decade of life onwards. Eligible participants were required to have lived in the study area for at least 6 months.

Study duration

The overall study duration was 1 year, and data collection was conducted between 20 December 2024 and 20 February 2025.

Sample size

The sample size was 427 participants, assuming a 13.75% prevalence of hospitalisation among patients with multimorbidity,6 with an absolute precision of 4%, a 95% confidence level and a design effect of 1.5 to account for cluster sampling. This sample size was calculated using OpenEpi V.3.0. Because the study included 24 selected clusters, each with 18 participants, the final sample size was 432.

Sampling technique

In this study, two districts in Kerala, Thrissur and Kollam, were randomly selected, representing the state’s northern and southern regions, respectively. In Thrissur, the only city corporation and two of its seven municipalities were selected to represent urban areas, while three of the district’s 86 panchayats were chosen to represent rural areas. From each of these urban and rural areas, two wards (the smallest administrative unit in Kerala) were selected, for a total of 12 wards in Thrissur district. Similarly, in Kollam district, the sole city corporation and two of its four municipalities were selected for urban representation, and three of the district’s 68 panchayats were selected for rural representation. As in Thrissur, two wards were selected from each area, resulting in 12 wards in Kollam. In total, 24 clusters (wards) were included in the study, with 18 participants recruited from each cluster (figure 1).

Study tools

Data were collected using a validated semistructured interview schedule. The schedule included sections on sociodemographic characteristics, self-reported chronic health conditions, the number of hospitalisations in the preceding 12 months and OOPE related to multimorbidity during the same period. Expenditure data were captured from the participant’s perspective and included direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs and indirect expenses. The interview schedule was developed based on a review of relevant literature and previously used population-based surveys, followed by expert review by public health and health system researchers to establish content validity. The tool was pilot-tested in a non-study area, and minor refinements were made to improve clarity and contextual relevance before field implementation. The final interview schedule is provided as an online supplemental file 1.

Procedure of data collection

Before initiating data collection, local authorities in the selected urban and rural areas were informed. A central point in each chosen ward was identified, and households were visited as per the WHO cluster sampling method until 18 eligible participants (aged ≥30 years with multimorbidity) were identified.22 Data on OOPE were collected directly from participants.

Independent and outcome variables

Independent variables were age in completed years, gender (male/female/transgender), place of residence (urban/rural), marital status (currently married, widowed, separated, deserted/not married) and education. The outcome variables were hospitalisation (yes/no) and OOPE, including medical and non-medical expenditures

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection began.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into the EpiCollect5 mobile app and analysed using Stata V.14. All categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarised as mean (SD) or median (IQR), based on the normality of the data. The prevalence of hospitalisation was calculated with 95% CIs. Sociodemographic and morbidity-related factors associated with hospitalisation were examined using the χ2 test. A log-binomial regression was performed using variables with p values <0.2 in the unadjusted analysis, and adjusted prevalence ratios (APRs) with 95% CIs were calculated. The association of sociodemographic factors with the total OOPE was analysed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal-Wallis test. As multiple comparisons were performed, p values from bivariate analyses were interpreted cautiously and alongside effect sizes and 95% CIs. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the healthcare utilisation patterns and OOPE associated with multimorbidity among adults in Kerala. The prevalence of hospitalisation among individuals with multimorbidity was 26.62%, with significant variations by district, health insurance status and number of chronic conditions. The OOPE was substantial, with a median of ₹33 675 among those incurring any costs, and medication expenses formed the largest share. Hospitalisation, increasing age, district of residence and higher number of morbidities were all associated with a significantly higher OOPE.

The observed hospitalisation rate of 26.62% among individuals with multimorbidity in this study aligns with evidence from other Indian and international settings. Pati et al reported a nearly threefold increase in hospitalisation among individuals with multimorbidity compared with those without chronic conditions in India.23 Similarly, Mini and Thankappan found higher hospitalisation rates among older adults in Kerala with multiple chronic diseases.24 A study among Portuguese adults reported a 12-month hospitalisation rate of 25.8%, with this figure being significantly higher among older adults with multimorbidity.25 These findings corroborate the growing body of literature suggesting that multimorbidity is a strong driver of inpatient care utilisation across age groups. Such high rates of hospitalisation raise concerns about the adequacy of primary and preventive care services in managing multimorbidity effectively at the community level. The concept of avoidable hospitalisation, often operationalised through ambulatory care sensitive conditions (ACSCs), provides an important framework for interpreting the high hospitalisation rates observed in this study. ACSCs refer to conditions for which timely and effective outpatient care can prevent disease progression, complications or the need for inpatient admission. Many chronic conditions contributing to multimorbidity in this population, such as hypertension, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases and cardiovascular conditions, are widely recognised as ACSCs. High hospitalisation rates among individuals with multimorbidity may therefore reflect gaps in continuity, quality or accessibility of primary and chronic care services, rather than unavoidable clinical necessity alone.

Hospitalisation was significantly higher among participants in Kollam compared with Thrissur, suggesting underlying differences in health system capacity and care-seeking patterns across districts. Kollam has a relatively higher density of both public and private hospital beds, enhancing the overall capacity for inpatient care across the district, not limited to urban areas.26 This broader infrastructure advantage may improve access to hospitalisation, particularly for individuals with chronic conditions that require frequent monitoring or acute interventions. While urban Kollam does benefit from a greater concentration of urban primary health centres within city limits, the district as a whole is also characterised by a comparatively higher availability of healthcare facilities, including private hospitals and secondary-level institutions.27 The widespread presence of private healthcare providers across Kollam may be associated with differences in hospitalisation patterns, as observed in health systems where private care constitutes a substantial share of service delivery. These findings emphasise that district-level health system characteristics, including provider density, facility accessibility and public–private mix, can significantly shape hospital utilisation patterns among individuals with multimorbidity.

The observed district-level differences in morbidity burden, hospitalisation and OOPE highlight important equity dimensions within Kerala. Although both districts are considered relatively well performing on health indicators, participants from Kollam consistently experienced higher morbidity complexity, a greater likelihood of hospitalisation and a higher financial burden than those from Thrissur. These differences persist even after adjusting for individual-level factors, suggesting that structural characteristics of local health systems, such as provider density, public–private mix and patterns of service utilisation, may differentially shape health outcomes and financial exposure. Such interdistrict variation underscores that equity gaps can exist even within comparatively developed health systems and that targeted, context-specific policy responses are required.

Participants with health insurance were more likely to report hospitalisation compared with the uninsured. This is consistent with findings by Karan et al, who showed that individuals with insurance coverage under schemes like Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana had higher inpatient service utilisation.28 The observed association between health insurance coverage and a higher likelihood of hospitalisation should be interpreted with caution. This relationship may reflect reverse causality, whereby individuals with more severe illness or greater healthcare needs are more likely to enrol in insurance schemes, rather than insurance itself causing increased hospitalisation. While insurance schemes aim to improve access and reduce financial barriers, they may also inadvertently incentivise admissions, particularly in private healthcare settings. The higher rates of hospitalisation among insured persons need attention from policy makers. In districts such as Kollam, where private healthcare facilities are more widely available, insurance coverage may be associated with increased hospitalisation by reducing financial barriers to inpatient care; however, this pattern warrants careful interpretation and further investigation to distinguish appropriate access from potentially avoidable admissions.

The presence of more than two chronic conditions emerged as the strongest predictor of hospitalisation in this study. Lee et al demonstrated that each additional NCD raised the odds of hospitalisation by nearly 60% across multiple middle-income countries, including India.29 Similar associations have been reported in Indonesia, Ethiopia and other settings worldwide, emphasising the consistent burden of multimorbidity.3032 Afshar et al noted that this relationship holds true even after adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic factors.32 These results reinforce the need for integrated chronic disease management models tailored to individuals with multiple morbidities to reduce hospitalisation and its downstream consequences.

A striking 90.7% of participants in our study reported OOPE, with a median amount of ₹33 675. Medicines accounted for the largest share of this expenditure (61.74%), consistent with previous studies in India and other LMICs. Pati et al and Lee et al highlighted that medication costs often exceed consultation or diagnostic charges, particularly in chronic care settings.23 29 McRae et al showed that older Australians with multimorbidity incurred disproportionately high OOPE primarily due to pharmaceutical needs.33 Bhojani et al found similar patterns among the urban poor in Bangalore, where repeated purchases of medicines for chronic conditions contributed significantly to household financial strain.34 These findings underscore the critical role of medicine pricing and availability in determining OOPE. However, the study did not collect information on whether medicines were procured as generic or branded formulations, nor on their sources (public vs private supply). Consequently, while improved access to affordable generic medicines is frequently proposed as a strategy to reduce OOPE, the present data do not allow assessment of the extent to which medicine-related costs in this study were driven by branded drug use, availability gaps in public supply or prescribing practices. These interpretations should therefore be viewed cautiously.

Certain subgroups incurred significantly higher OOPE in this study, including hospitalised individuals, those aged 60–79 years and residents of Kollam. Hospitalised participants had a median OOPE of ₹73 000, nearly three times higher than that of non-hospitalised individuals. Although hospitalised participants incurred significantly higher OOPE than those not hospitalised, the observed difference may appear modest relative to the expected cost differential between inpatient and outpatient care. This likely reflects how expenditure was captured in the study, in which total annual OOPE included cumulative outpatient costs, such as repeated consultations, investigations and long-term medication use, in addition to inpatient expenses. In contrast, hospitalisation was recorded as the occurrence of at least one admission during the recall period, without information on the number of admissions, duration of stays or frequency of outpatient visits. Consequently, substantial outpatient expenditure among non-hospitalised individuals with multimorbidity may attenuate the apparent cost gap between the two groups. This finding is similar to a study by Srivastava et al, who reported that hospitalisation substantially increased the risk of catastrophic health spending in Indian adults with multimorbidity.35 In Ethiopia, Habtemichael et al observed similar vulnerabilities among older adults.31 Moreover, Mishra and Shukla et al confirmed that healthcare costs rose with both age and disease count, adding to the cumulative economic burden in older multimorbid populations.19 36 These results emphasise the need for targeted financial protection strategies for older adults and those with frequent hospital needs.

OOPE was notably higher among residents of Kollam, reinforcing the influence of health system factors on financial outcomes. Ghosh and Arokiasamy have shown that OOPE varies widely across Indian states, largely due to differences in service availability and reliance on private sector care.37 In areas like Kollam, with dense private healthcare networks, service frequency and cost are typically higher, leading to greater financial exposure. Zhao et al further suggested that the clustering of chronic diseases, such as cardiometabolic conditions, can increase healthcare costs among multimorbid individuals.38 From an equity perspective, higher OOPE in Kollam suggests greater reliance on cost-intensive care pathways, potentially reflecting differential access to affordable public-sector services and greater exposure to private-sector care. Our findings suggest that both the regional health system context and the patients’ clinical profiles must be considered in OOPE mitigation strategies.

The financial impact of multimorbidity is cumulative, with OOPE increasing alongside the number of chronic conditions. Each additional morbidity contributed to greater healthcare use and higher expenditures. This trend is well-supported by findings from Afshar et al and Shukla et al, who demonstrated a progressive rise in both service utilisation and OOPE with increasing disease count.32 36 Agrawal and Agrawal also reported greater health expenditure among older Indian adults with multiple morbidities, particularly for outpatient visits and medications.39 These patterns highlight the long-term financial vulnerability associated with multimorbidity and the importance of preventive care and chronic disease control to reduce avoidable costs. For policy relevance, OOPE was examined both for the total sample and among the subset of participants who reported any expenditure during the preceding 12 months. While the present analysis highlights substantial OOPE associated with multimorbidity, particularly among hospitalised individuals and those with more chronic conditions, the study was not designed to estimate catastrophic health expenditure. Information on household income or consumption expenditure, which is required to apply standard catastrophic expenditure thresholds (eg, OOPE exceeding 10% or 25% of household resources), was not collected. Consequently, although the magnitude and distribution of OOPE are clearly demonstrated, the risk of catastrophic health expenditure could not be formally assessed in this study.

The substantial OOPE reported in this study must also be contextualised within the broader issue of inadequate public health financing in India, including in high-performing states like Kerala. Public expenditure on health remains well below the global average and among the lowest in the world, at 1.3% of GDP, compared with the proposed 2.5% in the Government of India’s 2017 National Health Policy,40 resulting in a dominant role for the private sector in service delivery. Because multimorbidity care often requires frequent, long-term interaction with the health system, individuals may increasingly seek care from multiple providers, including private facilities, which can lead to higher OOPEs. This dynamic exacerbates financial vulnerability and pushes households into poverty, a phenomenon well-documented by Tran et al and others linking chronic disease care with catastrophic health spending.41 In Kerala, despite its achievements on health indicators, public-sector infrastructure has not kept pace with the growing burden of NCDs, leaving multimorbid patients increasingly reliant on expensive private care. Kerala has relatively high health insurance coverage compared with many other Indian states, largely driven by publicly financed schemes, such as the Karunya Arogya Suraksha Padhathi and earlier Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana–linked programmes, alongside varying levels of private health insurance coverage. These schemes have substantially reduced financial barriers to inpatient care, particularly for economically vulnerable populations, but coverage is predominantly focused on hospital-based care, with limited protection for outpatient consultations, diagnostics and long-term medication needs. In the context of multimorbidity, where care is largely outpatient and continuous, this imbalance may inadvertently shift care trajectories towards hospitalisation when insurance coverage is available.

Key strengths of this study include its community-based design and the direct collection of patient-level data on hospitalisation and OOPE, enhancing internal validity and contextual relevance. Restricting inclusion to individuals aged ≥30 years with established multimorbidity enabled focused examination of a clinically high-risk population that is often under-represented in health systems research. The findings should, however, be interpreted in light of several limitations. Reliance on self-reported information for hospitalisation history and healthcare expenditure introduces the potential for recall bias, particularly for events and costs occurring earlier in the 12-month recall period. Although efforts were made to validate responses using available medical records where feasible, some degree of misclassification or under-reporting cannot be ruled out. The absence of data on the number of hospital admissions, duration of hospital stays and frequency of outpatient visits limited the ability to undertake per-episode cost comparisons between hospitalised and non-hospitalised participants. The cross-sectional design further restricts causal inference and precludes establishing temporal relationships among multimorbidity, hospitalisation and financial outcomes. Additionally, information on facility type (public vs private), provider characteristics and provider-specific expenditure patterns was not collected, limiting assessment of differential financial risk across healthcare settings and precluding empirical evaluation of provider-induced demand. Lack of data on the type of health insurance coverage (public or private) constrained the assessment of scheme-specific effects on hospitalisation and OOPE. The study also did not capture whether medicines were procured as generic or branded formulations or their sources, limiting a more granular analysis of the drivers of medicine-related expenditure. Furthermore, the absence of household income or consumption expenditure data precluded estimation of catastrophic health expenditure among participants who incurred costs. Excluding common mental health conditions from the multimorbidity definition may have led to an underestimation of the overall burden and complexity of multimorbidity. Although the two districts were purposively selected to represent different geographic regions of Kerala, the findings may not be generalisable to the entire state or to other parts of India due to contextual differences in health system organisation, service availability and socioeconomic profiles.

The findings from this study highlight the urgent need for health system reforms that address both access and financial protection for individuals with multimorbidity. Increasing the public sector allocation to health from the current level of 1.3% of GDP, strengthening public sector infrastructure, improving access to medicines through generic and essential drug schemes and expanding insurance coverage for outpatient care are key priorities. District-specific strategies that account for differences in infrastructure, provider density and patient demographics can improve targeting. Integration of care models, such as family health centres and chronic disease registries, may help deliver more coordinated and cost-effective care. As multimorbidity becomes a dominant healthcare challenge in India, systemic responses must be equally multifaceted and proactive.

Conclusion

This study highlights that over one in four adults with multimorbidity in Kerala had experienced hospitalisation in the past year, with medication costs forming the bulk of health-related expenditure. The findings underscore the need for strengthened public health financing, improved access to affordable care and targeted financial protection strategies to address the healthcare needs of this high-risk population. Strengthening financial protection mechanisms across the health system, including improved coverage for outpatient care and medicines, is essential to mitigate the risk of OOPE among individuals with multimorbidity.
