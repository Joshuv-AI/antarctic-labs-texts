Impact of coordinate system alignment on CT-based distal radius morphometry relevant to surgical planning: A bilateral comparative study
Authors: Sang Anh Nguyen, Doanh Quoc Tran, Anh Hoang Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputed tomography–based three-dimensional morphometric analysis is increasingly used for surgical planning and contralateral templating in distal radius surgery. However, the influence of coordinate system alignment on morphometric measurements has not been systematically evaluated.MethodsA cross-sectional imaging study was performed using CT-derived three-dimensional models from 83 healthy Vietnamese adults (166 distal radii). Distal radius width and thickness were measured using two coordinate systems: the default software bounding-box orientation and an anatomically axis-corrected system aligned with the distal radius longitudinal axis. Measurements were compared using paired analyses, and inter-observer reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients.ResultsWidth measurements showed no statistically significant differences between the two coordinate systems. In contrast, thickness measurements were consistently greater after anatomical axis correction, with median differences of 0.18 mm on the right and 0.15 mm on the left (both p < 0.001). Inter-observer reliability was excellent for all measurements (ICC = 1.0), reflecting the high reproducibility of the standardized coordinate alignment workflow.ConclusionAnatomical coordinate system alignment introduces small but consistent differences in CT-based distal radius morphometric measurements, particularly for thickness estimation. Although the absolute differences were small, standardizing coordinate system alignment may improve the reproducibility and comparability of CT-derived measurements used for morphometric analysis, preoperative planning, and contralateral templating. Future CT-based morphometric studies should explicitly describe the coordinate system used for measurement.

Introduction

Accurate restoration of distal radius anatomy is a fundamental objective in the surgical management of distal radius fractures. Precise reconstruction of radial width, thickness, and overall metaphyseal morphology is critical for optimal implant positioning, appropriate screw trajectory, and adequate subchondral support during volar plate fixation. Even small deviations from native anatomy may affect implant selection and increase the risk of intra-articular screw penetration or insufficient subchondral fixation. In current clinical practice, computed tomography (CT)–based three-dimensional (3D) analysis is increasingly utilized for preoperative planning, contralateral templating, and morphometric assessment of the distal radius, particularly in complex intra-articular fractures and corrective osteotomy planning [[1], [2], [3]].

Advances in 3D imaging and modeling software have enabled automated morphometric measurements using coordinate-based bounding-box techniques. These approaches enable rapid extraction of anatomical dimensions, including distal radius width and thickness, from reconstructed CT datasets. However, such measurements are inherently dependent on the orientation of the coordinate system used to define anatomical axes. Variations in coordinate alignment may lead to systematic differences in morphometric values, particularly when the longitudinal axis of the distal radius is misaligned with the software's default coordinate system. Despite the expanding role of CT-based morphometry in both clinical and research settings, the influence of coordinate system alignment on distal radius measurements has not been systematically evaluated [[4], [5], [6]].

Most previous morphometric studies of the distal radius have focused on describing anatomical parameters across populations or informing implant design, with limited attention to methodological factors that may affect measurement accuracy and reproducibility. Several investigations have highlighted the importance of contralateral templating and 3D preoperative planning for optimizing plate positioning and determining appropriate screw length during distal radius fixation [7,8]. Because subchondral screw placement requires millimeter-level precision, even small discrepancies in measured metaphyseal thickness may influence surgical decision-making and the risk of intra-articular penetration [9]. Nevertheless, the extent to which coordinate system alignment contributes to variability in CT-based measurements remains unclear.

Standardization of coordinate alignment may enhance the reproducibility of CT-based morphometric analysis and improve the reliability of measurements used for surgical planning. Determining whether an anatomically axis-corrected coordinate system produces different morphometric values compared with a default software coordinate system is therefore clinically relevant. Such information facilitates comparison across studies and improves consistency in CT-based morphometric research.

The present study aimed to evaluate the effect of coordinate system alignment on CT-based morphometric measurements of the distal radius. Specifically, distal radius width and thickness measurements obtained using a default software coordinate system were compared with those obtained using an anatomically axis-corrected coordinate system. Inter-observer reliability was assessed, and the potential clinical implications of any observed differences were explored. We hypothesized that axis-corrected measurements would demonstrate systematic differences and improved reliability, particularly for thickness measurements relevant to surgical planning. Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to determine whether coordinate system alignment introduces systematic differences in CT-based distal radius measurements and to evaluate the potential implications for reproducibility and surgical planning.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This investigation was a cross-sectional, imaging-based morphometric study aimed at establishing reference measurements of the distal radius using computed tomography–derived three-dimensional models. A total of 83 adult Vietnamese volunteers were enrolled, providing 166 distal radii for bilateral analysis. All participants were recruited prospectively from individuals undergoing CT imaging for research purposes.

The institutional ethics committee approved the study protocol, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant before inclusion. All procedures complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligibility criteria were defined to ensure inclusion of a healthy adult reference population appropriate for CT-based morphometric assessment. Individuals were considered eligible if they were between 18 and 59 years of age, provided informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of study procedures, and had no clinical or radiological evidence of previous injury, deformity, or pathological conditions affecting the forearm, wrist, or hand. Participants were also required to have CT datasets of adequate quality for three-dimensional reconstruction and morphometric analysis.

Participants were excluded if they had a history of wrist or forearm fracture, significant trauma, or prior surgical intervention involving the upper limb. Additional exclusion criteria included degenerative joint disease of the wrist, congenital skeletal abnormalities affecting the forearm or hand, or imaging findings suggestive of bone pathology such as malunion, infection, or structural deformity. Individuals unable to provide informed consent were not enrolled. Female participants who were pregnant or breastfeeding at the time of recruitment were also excluded.

All CT datasets were reviewed after acquisition to confirm the absence of structural abnormalities and to ensure suitability for morphometric analysis. Only datasets meeting predefined quality criteria for three-dimensional reconstruction were included in the final analysis.

CT acquisition and three-dimensional reconstruction

Computed tomography examinations of the wrist were performed using a multi-detector CT scanner (Somatom Definition AS+, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) according to a standardized acquisition protocol to ensure uniform image quality across all participants. Images were obtained with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm or less, providing sufficient spatial resolution for detailed visualization of cortical and subchondral bone architecture required for morphometric assessment.

The DICOM datasets were subsequently exported to a three-dimensional modeling platform (SolidWorks, Dassault Systèmes, France, version 2023, Windows platform) for reconstruction and quantitative analysis. Multiplanar reconstructions were systematically reviewed in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes to confirm anatomical orientation and to facilitate accurate segmentation of the distal radius. Based on these datasets, three-dimensional surface models of the distal radius were generated to enable precise geometric measurements.

Morphometric analysis was conducted within the modeling environment using a standardized workflow for coordinate alignment and dimensional extraction. All measurements were obtained after establishing predefined reference axes, ensuring consistency in spatial orientation across specimens. The software, operating on a Windows-based system, provides high-resolution geometric analysis tools that support reproducible three-dimensional evaluation of osseous structures. Coordinate system definition and measurement workflow

Two coordinate systems

Default coordinate system: Measurements were obtained using the native bounding-box coordinate system generated automatically by the software, without axis correction.

Axis-corrected coordinate system: The longitudinal axis of the distal radius was first defined based on anatomical alignment. A corrected coordinate system was then established with the bounding box oriented perpendicular to the anatomical long axis of the distal radius. Morphometric measurements were subsequently extracted using this corrected orientation.

This approach allowed direct comparison between measurements obtained from the default software coordinate system and those obtained after anatomical axis correction.

The measurement workflow

Step 1: Initial bounding-box measurement and definition of reference point S.

A three-dimensional bounding box was automatically generated from the reconstructed distal radius model within the native software coordinate system. Reference point S was defined as the most distal point of the distal radius along the proximal–distal direction of the global bounding-box axis. The spatial coordinates of point S were recorded for each observer and used as a fixed reference for subsequent alignment procedures (Fig. 2c, 2d).

As the bounding box is defined by the extreme planes of the model in the global coordinate system, the most distal point along the proximal–distal bounding-box axis represents a fixed geometric extremity. Therefore, reference point S remains constant across observers and measurement sessions, ensuring a stable reference for subsequent axis alignment and measurement.

Step 2: Definition of the longitudinal axis of the distal radius.

An anatomical longitudinal axis of the distal radius was established to provide an orientation-independent reference for measurement. Two cross-sectional planes perpendicular to the shaft were generated at distances of 3 cm and 5 cm proximal to point S along the radial shaft (Fig. 3). The centroids of these two planes were automatically determined from the segmented bone model. Step 3: Axis-corrected measurement.

A new coordinate system aligned with the reconstructed longitudinal axis was then established. An axial reference plane (axial_S) was defined as the plane passing through point S and oriented perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the distal radius. Distal radius width and thickness were subsequently re-measured using this axis-corrected coordinate system.

All observers followed an identical predefined alignment protocol. Once coordinate alignment was completed, dimensional parameters were automatically extracted from the three-dimensional model to minimize operator-dependent variability.

Morphometric measurements

The following parameters were measured bilaterally for each distal radius: (i) Distal radius width (mm); (ii) Distal radius thickness (mm); (iii) Width-to-thickness ratio (W/T ratio).

Measurements were generated automatically by the software once coordinate alignment was established. Three independent raters performed coordinate alignment procedures using the same standardized workflow. Because dimensional values were automatically calculated after axis alignment, inter-observer reliability reflected consistency in coordinate alignment rather than variability in manual measurements. Median values and ranges were recorded for each parameter.

Inter-observer reliability

Inter-observer reliability across the three raters was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). A two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement was used to evaluate reliability for both the default and axis-corrected coordinate systems. ICC values were calculated separately for width and thickness measurements on both the right and left sides. Because dimensional values were automatically generated once coordinate alignment was established, ICC values reflect reproducibility of alignment rather than manual measurement variability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because most variables were not normally distributed, results are presented as median (minimum–maximum) and interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Paired comparisons between measurements obtained using the default and axis-corrected coordinate systems were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Analyses were conducted separately for right and left distal radii. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Measurement standardization framework

CT-based morphometric studies often rely on software-defined global axes that do not necessarily align with anatomical axes. The present findings indicate that such misalignment introduces systematic bias in thickness measurements. We therefore propose that distal radius morphometry be performed using an anatomically aligned coordinate system defined by the distal radius's longitudinal axis. Standardizing coordinate orientation may improve reproducibility across studies and enhance the comparability of CT-based morphometric datasets.

Methodological bias

The present study demonstrates that coordinate-system alignment can introduce systematic differences in CT-based morphometric measurements of the distal radius, particularly in metaphyseal thickness. Although the absolute magnitude of the change was small, the direction of the difference was consistent on both sides, suggesting methodological bias related to spatial orientation rather than random measurement error.

Three-dimensional CT–based morphometric analysis has become increasingly common in both research and preoperative planning of distal radius fractures. However, the accuracy and reproducibility of these measurements depend heavily on consistent anatomical alignment and the definition of reference axes. Variability in image orientation and coordinate definition has been shown to influence quantitative measurements of osseous structures, particularly when automated or semi-automated bounding box–based methods are used. A systematic experimental study evaluating CT-based fracture models reported that all workflow variables significantly affected dimensional accuracy. However, mean deviations remained within sub-millimeter ranges considered clinically acceptable for preoperative planning [10]. Importantly, that study highlighted that even when absolute errors are small, systematic biases may arise depending on imaging orientation and reconstruction workflow.

In the current investigation, coordinate alignment relative to the anatomical long axis produced consistent directional differences in thickness measurements. This suggests that bounding-box–based measurements derived from default software axes may introduce systematic bias when anatomical axes are not explicitly defined. Because thickness represents the anteroposterior dimension of the metaphyseal region, it is particularly sensitive to rotational misalignment. By contrast, mediolateral width appears less affected by coordinate orientation, likely because it is geometrically stable across different axes.

From a methodological standpoint, these results emphasize the need for explicit reporting and standardization of coordinate systems in CT-based morphometric studies. Without such standardization, comparisons across studies or between institutions may be affected by hidden measurement bias. Similar concerns have been raised in prior work evaluating segmentation-related inaccuracies and workflow-dependent variation in CT-derived bone models, which underscores the importance of reproducible measurement pipelines in musculoskeletal imaging research [10].

Clinical relevance

Although the differences in thickness measurements observed in this study were modest in magnitude, they may have practical implications in surgical scenarios requiring millimeter-level precision. Accurate assessment of distal radius thickness is particularly important during volar plate fixation, where subchondral screw length and trajectory must be carefully selected to achieve stable fixation while avoiding intra-articular penetration or tendon irritation. Clinical series have reported that hardware-related complications, such as joint penetration by distal screws or irritation of surrounding tendons, may occur when screw length is inaccurately estimated or when implant positioning does not adequately account for local anatomy [11]. More recent evidence has further emphasized that hardware-related events remain a notable source of postoperative morbidity following volar locking plate fixation. Intra-articular screw placement, dorsal screw prominence within tendon compartments, and implant malposition have been identified among the factors leading to secondary procedures, including hardware removal or revision surgery. These observations underscore the importance of precise morphometric assessment and meticulous intraoperative control of screw length and trajectory during fixation [12].

Within this clinical context, systematic differences in thickness measurement introduced by coordinate alignment may influence preoperative planning and implant selection. Although the magnitude of variation identified in the present study is unlikely to independently determine clinical outcomes, consistent measurement bias may affect digital templating workflows that rely on CT-derived dimensions. When high-resolution three-dimensional imaging is used for surgical planning, even small discrepancies in thickness estimation can influence decisions on screw length, subchondral support, and implant positioning.

Contralateral templating

Contralateral templating is increasingly used in the preoperative planning of distal radius fractures and corrective osteotomies, particularly when native anatomical parameters must be restored despite comminution or articular disruption. Three-dimensional computed tomography allows the unaffected side to serve as a patient-specific anatomical reference, enabling surgeons to approximate native morphology and guide reconstruction to restore alignment and joint congruity. Because this approach relies on the assumption of bilateral geometric similarity, the reproducibility of contralateral comparisons depends on the consistent spatial orientation of the three-dimensional models.

Variations in coordinate alignment between sides or across measurement sessions may introduce systematic discrepancies that are unrelated to true anatomical asymmetry. In three-dimensional–assisted surgical planning workflows, mirrored contralateral models are commonly used to support reduction strategies and implant positioning. Such approaches inherently require standardized coordinate definitions to ensure that bilateral measurements are directly comparable and free from orientation-related bias [13].

The present findings indicate that coordinate system alignment can influence thickness measurements derived from CT-based models of the distal radius. When contralateral templating is employed, both sides should therefore be analyzed using identical coordinate frameworks to minimize artificial differences introduced by measurement orientation. Although the magnitude of the observed discrepancies was small, their consistent direction suggests that even minor systematic variation may affect templating accuracy in situations requiring precise anatomical restoration.

Standardizing coordinate alignment during CT-based morphometric analysis may enhance the reliability of bilateral comparison and improve the validity of measurements used for contralateral templating. Such methodological consistency is particularly relevant in complex fractures and deformity correction, where subtle differences in reconstructed anatomy may influence implant positioning, screw trajectory, and restoration of joint congruity.

Surgical planning

Contemporary preoperative planning for distal radius fractures increasingly relies on advanced cross-sectional imaging to delineate fracture morphology and guide anatomical reconstruction. Computed tomography allows comprehensive evaluation of articular congruity, distal radioulnar joint alignment, and fragment configuration, and may reveal intra-articular involvement that is not clearly visible on standard radiographs. Multiplanar reconstructions and axial views can also be compared with the contralateral wrist to approximate native morphology, thereby assisting restoration of anatomical parameters during fixation. Imaging-based planning is particularly valuable in complex or comminuted fractures, in which an accurate understanding of three-dimensional anatomy is critical for achieving stable fixation and satisfactory reduction [14].

Within this context, the present study demonstrates that coordinate system alignment can influence CT-based measurements of distal radius thickness, a parameter directly relevant to screw length selection during volar plating. Although the absolute differences observed were small, they were consistent and reproducible, suggesting a systematic measurement effect rather than random variation. In clinical scenarios requiring high precision, such as intra-articular fracture fixation or corrective osteotomy, even minor discrepancies in thickness estimation may influence decisions regarding subchondral support and implant positioning.

Previous experimental investigations of CT-derived three-dimensional bone models have shown that dimensional variation may arise from differences in model orientation, segmentation workflow, and coordinate definition, even when overall geometric accuracy remains within clinically acceptable limits [10]. These observations underscore the importance of standardized measurement protocols when morphometric data are used to inform surgical planning. Aligning coordinate systems with anatomical axes enhances the reproducibility of measurements and reduces variability in planning decisions based on CT-derived dimensions.

The high inter-observer reliability observed in this study further indicates that, once coordinate alignment is standardized, automated or semi-automated measurement techniques can achieve consistent results across operators. This supports the feasibility of incorporating axis-corrected measurement workflows into routine morphometric assessment and preoperative planning for distal radius surgery. Standardization of measurement orientation may improve the comparability of CT-based morphometric data across studies and clinical settings, while reinforcing the reliability of measurements used to guide implant selection and positioning.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the study population consisted of healthy adult volunteers without distal radius fractures or deformities. Although this allowed the establishment of a controlled reference dataset for evaluating measurement methodology, the results may not fully reflect measurement behavior in fractured or severely deformed distal radii, where anatomical landmarks and long-axis definition may be altered. Future investigations in fracture populations are therefore warranted to determine whether coordinate alignment introduces similar systematic differences in clinically complex cases.

Second, the measurement workflow was implemented using a specific three-dimensional modeling environment based on SolidWorks. While the underlying geometric principles of coordinate alignment are software-independent, variations in segmentation methods, model reconstruction pipelines, and coordinate definition across platforms may influence absolute measurements. The extent to which the observed differences generalize to other software environments or automated planning systems warrants further exploration.

Third, although statistically significant differences in thickness measurements were identified, the absolute magnitude of these differences was small. The clinical relevance of submillimeter variation remains context-dependent and is unlikely to determine surgical outcome in most routine cases independently. However, in high-precision planning scenarios, such as intra-articular fracture fixation, corrective osteotomy, or digital templating workflows, systematic measurement bias may still influence implant selection or screw length estimation. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted primarily in the context of methodological standardization rather than direct prediction of clinical outcomes.

Finally, the inter-observer reliability observed in this study reflects the reproducibility of a semi-automated measurement workflow after coordinate alignment had been established. Because measurements were generated from geometry defined by the software rather than from manual caliper placement, the reliability estimates primarily reflect consistency in model orientation and coordinate definition, not purely manual measurement variability. Future studies comparing different segmentation pipelines and alignment strategies may provide additional insight into sources of variability in CT-based morphometric analysis.

Conclusion

Coordinate system alignment introduces small but consistent differences in CT-based morphometric measurements of the distal radius, particularly affecting thickness parameters. Alignment with the anatomical long axis produced slightly greater thickness values, whereas width measurements remained largely unchanged, indicating that thickness is more sensitive to spatial orientation. Although these differences are unlikely to alter outcomes in routine cases, systematic bias may influence measurement-dependent decisions in CT-based surgical planning and contralateral templating workflows. Standardizing coordinate alignment with anatomically defined axes may therefore improve measurement reproducibility and comparability across studies. Explicit reporting of coordinate system definitions is recommended for future three-dimensional morphometric analyses of the distal radius.
