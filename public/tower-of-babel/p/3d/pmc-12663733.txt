The neurons in NAc core receiving ACC excitatory afferents contribute to chronic postoperative pain
Authors: Yaowei Xu, Fei Xing, Chenge Hu, Xiaoshan Shi, Xin Wei, Daoke Yao, Xiaoling Wang, Mingquan Xing, Jingjing Yuan, Zhisong Li, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryChronic postoperative pain (CPOP) remains a significant clinical challenge, with central sensitization being an important mechanism. However, the neuronal circuit-mediated mechanisms associated with this disorder are poorly understood. Here, we identified the nucleus accumbens core (AcbC) received excitatory projections from calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)-positive neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), playing an important role in the development of CPOP. We demonstrated that the AcbC neurons displayed the enhanced responses to both non-nociceptive and nociceptive stimuli following skin/muscle incision and retraction. Furthermore, fiber photometry and electrophysiological recordings confirmed that the activity of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway was also elevated after surgery. Inhibition of AcbC neurons or the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway alleviated pain hypersensitivity in CPOP mice, whereas their activation induced pain phenotypes in naive mice. These findings reveal critical roles for the AcbC neurons and ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in CPOP pathogenesis, offering potential therapeutic targets for postoperative pain management.

Introduction

Chronic postoperative pain (CPOP) is clinically characterized as persistent surgical wound pain that extends beyond the normal tissue healing period of three months.1 Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that the prevalence of CPOP varies between 10% and 50% across different surgical populations,2,3 with particularly elevated incidence rates following specific procedures such as thoracic surgery and limb amputation.4,5 This persistent pain condition not only adversely affects postoperative rehabilitation outcomes but also contributes to increased hospital readmission rates, ultimately resulting in significant socioeconomic burdens for both affected individuals and healthcare systems.6 Central sensitization involves maladaptive changes in nociceptive signal processing and pain perception, resulting in hyperalgesia and allodynia, which are frequently observed in CPOP patients.7,8 However, the plasticity changes of neural circuits involved in central sensitization are still unclear in the context of CPOP.

The nucleus accumbens (NAc), a critical neural hub located at the convergence of the basal ganglia and limbic system, serves as a key modulator in diverse neurobehavioral processes, including reward processing, emotional regulation, addiction, and fear responses.9,10 Emerging evidence has established the significant involvement of NAc in pain modulation and processing.11 The 90%–95% of total neurons in NAc are composed of medium spiny neurons (MSNs). According to the different expressions of dopamine receptors, they are divided into dopamine receptor type 1 (D1R-MSNs) and dopamine receptor type 2 (D2R-MSNs).12 Mechanistic study has revealed distinct functional roles of NAc dopamine receptor subtypes: activation of D1R-MSNs potentiates pain sensitivity, induces sleep-wake transitions, and prolongs wakefulness, whereas D2R-MSNs exert analgesic effects and promote sleep maintenance.13 Anatomically, the NAc maintains extensive reciprocal connections with multiple cortical and subcortical regions, forming integrated neural circuits that regulate adaptive behavioral responses.14 Particularly, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)-NAc pathway has been identified as a critical neural pathway that mediates social pain transmission and empathic responses.15 Despite these advances, the specific mechanisms through which the NAc contributes to the development and maintenance of CPOP remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation.

In this study, we employed a skin/muscle incision and retraction (SMIR) model to recapitulate the clinical features of CPOP. Our investigations revealed significant hyperactivation of neurons within the NAc core subregion (AcbC). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that AcbC neurons displayed the enhanced responsiveness to both non-nociceptive and nociceptive mechanical stimuli. Through comprehensive anatomical tracing and functional validation experiments, we established that AcbC neuronal activity was modulated by inputs from calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)-expressing neurons in the ACC. Utilizing chemogenetic and optogenetic approaches, we systematically explored the functional contribution of the AcbC neuronal pathway in SMIR-induced CPOP. The experimental design diagram was shown in Figure S1. Our results provide the evidence that AcbC neurons and ACCCaMKII-AcbC neural pathway play a pivotal role in CPOP pathogenesis. These findings not only advance our understanding of the neural circuitry underlying CPOP, but also identify a potential therapeutic target for the development of analgesic strategies.

Results

The significant activation of AcbC in SMIR mice

To investigate the pathological mechanisms underlying CPOP, we established a rodent model of SMIR as illustrated in Figure 1A. The 50% paw withdrawal threshold (PWT50) was measured by von Frey test for quantifying mechanical allodynia, and paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was measured by Hargreaves test for quantifying thermal hyperalgesia. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the PWT50 of ipsilateral hind paws (same as the surgical side) in SMIR mice from postoperative day (POD) 1 through POD14, while contralateral hind paws (opposite to the surgical side) remained unaffected (Figures 1B and S2A). However, no statistically significant differences were detected in PWL between ipsilateral and contralateral paws in either SMIR or sham groups (Figures 1B and S2A). These findings indicate that SMIR-induced CPOP manifests specifically as mechanical allodynia without affecting thermal sensitivity. Furthermore, to evaluate potential motor function impairment following SMIR surgery, we performed open field testing (OFT) on both SMIR and sham groups. Given the well-established role of C-FOS as a reliable biomarker for neuronal activation in the central nervous system, we performed immunofluorescence staining to quantify C-FOS expression. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant contralateral increase in C-FOS-positive cells in the AcbC of SMIR group (Figures 1C and 1D). Notably, the contralateral AcbC exhibited a significantly greater density of C-FOS-positive cells compared to the ipsilateral side in SMIR mice (Figures 1C and 1D), suggesting lateralized neuronal activation patterns following nerve injury. Furthermore, quantitative analysis demonstrated that 52.97% ± 11.03% of C-FOS-positive cells co-localized with D1R-MSNs, while 37.52% ± 5.15% were immunoreactive for D2R-MSNs (Figures 1E and 1F). To further characterize these neuronal responses, we conducted in vivo electrophysiological recordings by implanted multichannel electrodes targeting the contralateral AcbC in freely moving mice (Figure 1G). Single-unit recordings revealed a significant elevation in neuronal firing frequency in SMIR mice compared to sham controls (Figures 1H–1J). These multimodal findings provide evidence for SMIR-induced activation of AcbC neurons, characterized by increased cellular activity.

To precisely quantify neuronal activity dynamics, we employed fiber photometry to monitor neuron responses in freely moving mice. Following stereotaxic injection of AAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMP6s into the contralateral AcbC, we implanted optical fibers for calcium ion (Ca2+) activity recording (Figures 2A and 2B). Mechanical stimulation using 0.6 g (non-nociceptive) and 2 g (nociceptive) von Frey filaments revealed distinct response patterns. The selection of filaments was based on their performance in the sham group and naive group, as well as previous study.16 In sham group, 0.6 g stimulation elicited slight Ca2+ transients in AcbC neurons, whereas SMIR mice exhibited significantly enhanced responses (Figures 2C and 2D). Under 2 g stimulation, both groups showed increased Ca2+ activity, with SMIR mice demonstrating significantly greater responses compared to sham group (Figures 2F and 2G). Quantitative analysis of the area under the curve (AUC) revealed a significant increase in the SMIR group with the induction of both 0.6 g and 2 g filaments (Figures 2E and 2H). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry results showed co-localization signals between GCaMP-positive neurons and C-FOS-positive cells in the SMIR group (Figure 2I). The role of AcbC neurons in SMIR-induced CPOP

To investigate the role of AcbC neurons in SMIR-induced mechanical allodynia, we employed a chemogenetic approach using designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs). AAV2/9-hSyn-hM4D(Gi)-EGFP was microinjected into the contralateral AcbC of experimental mice. On POD7, clozapine N-oxide (CNO; 3.3 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally to activate the viral construct (Figure 3A). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in C-FOS-positive cells within the transfected region following CNO administration (Figure 3B), confirming the effective inhibition of AcbC neuronal activity through DREADDs-mediated silencing. Chemogenetic inhibition of AcbC neurons specifically attenuated the SMIR-induced reduction in ipsilateral PWT50, while contralateral PWT50 remained unaffected (Figure 3C). No significant differences in PWL were observed between the SMIR+CNO and SMIR+saline groups (Figure 3D). To further elucidate the pain-modulatory capacity of AcbC neurons, we examined the effects of neuronal activation in naive mice using an analogous DREADD strategy. AAV2/9-hSyn-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP was injected into the left AcbC, followed by CNO administration on day 21 to activate the transfected neurons (Figures 3F and 3G). Chemogenetic activation of AcbC neurons significantly reduced both PWT50 and PWL in right hind paws of naive mice (Figures 3H and 3I), without affecting motor performance (Figure 3J). These findings demonstrate that AcbC neurons are capable of bidirectional pain modulation, with neuronal activation inducing nociceptive hypersensitivity and inhibition attenuating mechanical allodynia.

AcbC neurons received positive regulation from CaMKII-positive neurons in the ACC

In a preliminary experiment, we delivered DIO-dependent anterograde non-transsynaptic virus (AAV2/9-EF1α-mCherry-DIO) into the ACC of CaMKII-Cre mice (Figure 4A). Histological analysis revealed mCherry-positive neuronal soma within the ACC (Figures 4B and 4C) and identified mCherry-labeled nerve fibers in the AcbC (Figure 4D), indicating that AcbC received projection terminals from the ACCCaMKII neurons. This finding prompted us to hypothesize the existence of a direct neural pathway between the AcbC and ACCCaMKII neurons. To systematically examine this potential connectivity, the CRE-dependent anterograde transsynaptic virus (AAV2/1-CaMKIIα-CRE) was administered into the bilateral ACC regions. Meanwhile, DIO-dependent retrograde tracing virus (AAV2/Retro-hSyn-mCherry-DIO) was simultaneously injected into the AcbC, to stably express mCherry indicator protein, with the assistance of CRE elements (Figure 4E). Subsequent immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated the presence of mCherry-positive axonal terminals and neuronal cell bodies in the AcbC (Figures 4F and 4H). Notably, mCherry-expressing neurons were observed in the ACC and predominantly localized in the Cg2 subregion (Figures 4F and 4G). To further investigate the functional properties of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC neural pathway, we employed a combined chemogenetic approach and fiber photometry. AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry was stereotaxically injected into the ACC to enable chemogenetic manipulation of ACC neurons, while AAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMP6s was delivered into the ipsilateral AcbC for calcium signal recording (Figures 5A and 5B). The results revealed that pharmacological activation of ACCCaMKII neurons significantly increased the number of calcium signal spikes in AcbC neurons (Figures 5C–5G). Complementary in vivo electrophysiological recordings in the AcbC revealed that the activation of ACCCaMKII neurons significantly elevated the firing frequency of AcbC neurons (Figures 5H–5K). Detailed analysis of interspike interval (ISI) showed a significant reduction in the CNO-treated group compared to saline controls, with most ISIs falling below 0.03 s (Figures 5L and 5M). These electrophysiological findings, combined with the calcium imaging results, provide the evidence that ACCCaMKII neurons exert excitatory modulation on AcbC neuronal activity. SMIR surgery increased the activity of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway

To further evaluate the impact of SMIR surgery on the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway, we next performed fiber photometry recording to monitor the activity of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in sham and SMIR mice. The anterograde transsynaptic AAV2/1-CaMKIIα-mCherry-CRE virus was injected into the ACC. The anterograde transsynaptic spread property of AAV2/1 could deliver CRE elements to AcbC neurons that receive projections from ACCCaMKII neurons. Assisted by CRE elements, AAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMP6s-DIO could stably express in the ipsilateral AcbC (Figure 6A). The optic fibers were also implanted into the AcbC to measure the Ca2+ activity of AcbC neurons, which receive projections from the ACC (Figure 6B). Our results showed that the application of 0.6 g von Frey filament stimulation increased the Ca2+ activity of AcbC neurons received projections from the ACC in SMIR group (Figures 6C and 6D). Intriguingly, the application of 2 g von Frey filaments increased the Ca2+ activity of these neurons in both groups, but the magnitude of this increase was significantly greater in the SMIR group than in the sham group (Figures 6F and 6G). Furthermore, the area under the time-F/F0 curves for the SMIR mice significantly increased with the induction of both 0.6 g and 2 g filaments (Figures 6E and 6H). To further characterize the functional involvement of ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in nociceptive processing, we conducted complementary experiments using channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2). The anterograde transsynaptic AAV2/1-CaMKIIα-hChR2-mCherry-CRE was injected into the left ACC, while AAV2/9-hSyn-mCherry-DIO was administered to the ipsilateral AcbC, followed by optical fiber implantation (Figure 7F). Motor function remained unaltered following ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway activation (Figure 7J). These findings collectively demonstrate that optogenetic activation of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway induces the pain hypersensitivity in naive mice, suggesting its crucial role in the bidirectional modulation of nociceptive processing.

Discussion

Chronic pain patients often exhibit neuroplasticity dysregulation within the central nervous system.17 Emerging evidence underscores the critical role of central sensitization in the induction and maintenance of CPOP.18 In this study, we observed significant activation of AcbC neurons in SMIR mice, which was associated with heightened responses to both non-nociceptive and nociceptive stimuli. Chemogenetic inhibition of these neurons attenuated mechanical allodynia in SMIR mice. Notably, we identified that AcbC neurons receive excitatory input regulation from the ACCCaMKII neurons. The ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in SMIR mice also showed markedly enhanced responses to both non-nociceptive and nociceptive stimuli. Optogenetic inhibition of these neurons alleviated mechanical allodynia, while their activation induced pain hypersensitivity phenotypes in naive mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AcbC neurons and ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway play crucial roles in CPOP (Figure 8).Figure 8The diagram showed that the activated ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway was involved in chronic postoperative pain

The diagram showed that the activated ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway was involved in chronic postoperative pain

NAc, a critical brain structure located at the junction of the basal ganglia and limbic system, plays an essential role in pain modulation through its extensive cortical connections.19,20 Nevertheless, the specific involvement of the NAc in CPOP and its responsiveness to nociceptive stimuli remain poorly characterized. In this study, we detected a significant elevation in C-FOS-positive cells within the AcbC subregion of NAc in SMIR mice. Interestingly, although SMIR surgery was performed on the ipsilateral paws, we observed a contralateral increase in C-FOS-positive cells in the AcbC. Moreover, the number of C-FOS-positive cells in AcbC on the contralateral side of SMIR mice was significantly greater than that on the ipsilateral side. This finding is consistent with the cross contralateral transmission of nociceptive signals in previous studies.21 Furthermore, the results showed that C-FOS-positive cells had cross sets with both D1R-MSNs and D2R-MSNs, and were more coupled with D1R-MSNs. Consistent with these findings, in vivo electrophysiological recordings further confirmed the enhanced excitability of AcbC neurons following SMIR surgery. Clinical studies have shown that the brain rhythm and connectivity of subcortical networks are significantly increased when processing nociceptive signals.22 Notably, we observed significant increases in Ca2+ activity of AcbC neurons in SMIR mice under both non-nociceptive and nociceptive mechanical stimuli. The colocalization of C-FOS and virus transfection was used to validate the effect of chemogenetics. The results showed that chemogenetic inhibition of these neurons attenuated mechanical allodynia in SMIR mice. However, the activation of these neurons not only induced mechanical allodynia, but also induced thermal hyperalgesia in wild-type mice.

As a central component of the limbic system, the NAc receives extensive neural projections from multiple brain regions.23 These NAc-related projections have been demonstrated to play critical roles in pain modulation.24 The ACC, as an integrative hub for both external motor actions and internal cognitive processes, is fundamentally involved in pain regulation and associated affective responses.25,26 Specifically, ACC neurons projecting to the NAc have been identified as essential mediators in reward integration and effort evaluation.27,28,29 The functional significance of the ACC-NAc pathway in pain processing has gained increasing attention. Through the combined application of retrograde and anterograde viral tracing techniques, we revealed that AcbC neurons receive substantial input from CaMKII-positive neurons in the ACC, particularly those in the Cg2 subregion. Each ACC subregion exhibits different functional characteristics. Recent evidence suggests that Cg1 and Cg2 have opposite effects on anxiety-like behavior30 and itching31 induced by chronic pain. Therefore, the current results may point out the activating effect of Cg2 on chronic pain.

Pharmacological activation of ACCCaMKII neurons confirmed the functional integrity of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway, indicating a robust excitatory interaction between ACCCaMKII and AcbC neurons. lt is worth noting that chemogenetic inhibition experiments will help to further clarify the bidirectional regulatory role of ACCCaMKII neurons to AcbC neurons. Our experimental results demonstrated significantly elevated Ca2+ activity in the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway of the SMIR group during both non-nociceptive and nociceptive paw stimulation. SMIR surgery induced significant mechanical allodynia in mice, rather than thermal hyperalgesia, which is consistent with previous study.32 This may be due to that the mechanical force induced by SMIR surgery preferentially activates mechanical pressure-sensitive channels, not the temperature-sensitive channels, in peripheral primary sensory neurons.33,34 However, optogenetic inhibition of ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway effectively alleviated mechanical allodynia in SMIR mice. However, the activation of ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway induced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in naive mice. This difference further demonstrates the integrated role of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in multidimensional pain signals and deserves further in-depth research. These findings collectively suggest that the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CPOP in SMIR mice. This finding is consistent with a previous report demonstrating that the ACC-NAc pathway is involved in the social transfer of pain and analgesia.15 Compared with previous studies, our study revealed identified that the NAc core subregion receives projections from CaMKII-positive neurons in the ACC, which plays important roles in CPOP. These findings represent a higher degree of cellular and anatomical specificity. While our current investigation focused on the positive projection between ACC and AcbC neurons, the potential connections involving inhibitory neurons within the ACC and AcbC remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, contrasting evidence shows reduced excitatory synaptic transmission in the NAc shell region in neuropathic pain models,35 potentially reflecting differential pain signal processing between the NAc core and shell regions.35,36 This regional disparity may stem from distinct connectivity patterns with various brain regions,37 highlighting the importance of investigating pain processing differences across NAc subregions. The anatomical and functional characteristics of ACC-NAc shell projections warrant further investigation.

In this study, we have elucidated the mechanistic role of activated AcbC neurons and the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in the development of CPOP. We demonstrated that targeted inhibition of either AcbC neurons or the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway effectively alleviated mechanical allodynia in SMIR mice without impairing motor function. Conversely, optogenetic activation of these neuronal populations induced pain hypersensitivity in naive mice. Neuroregulatory strategies, including deep brain stimulation,38 transcranial magnetic stimulation,39 and transcranial direct current stimulation,40 have broad application prospects in various neurological disorders. The neuroregulatory strategies targeting specific circuits will be more precise and effective. Therefore, exploring the contribution of the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in the context of CPOP can provide a perspective for the clinical application of neuroregulatory strategies to intervene in postoperative pain.

Limitations of this study

This study has two main limitations, which should be addressed in future research. First, we evaluated the pain behaviors of SMIR mice using mechanical and thermal stimuli. However, pain also contains emotional components (such as anxiety and depression-like behaviors). Therefore, investigating the multidimensional manifestations of CPOP in SMIR mice is important. Second, all experiments were conducted in male mice only. There is the well-established evidence for sex differences in pain processing and neural circuitry. Further researches should be conducted to explore potential sex-dependent mechanisms mediated by the ACCCaMKII-AcbC pathway in CPOP. Finally, we evaluated the overall regulation of AcbC by ACCCaMKII-positive neurons but lacked projection specificity. Thus, further experiments are necessary to distinguish projection-specific activity from the general AcbC population.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse

Adult male C57BL/6J mice, weighing between 25 g and 30 g and aged 7–8 weeks, were obtained from Sbeff Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Additionally, adult male CaMKII α-ires-Cre mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., NM-KI-242500) were generously provided by Dr. Songxue Su from Zhengzhou University. The mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle at room temperature (23°C–28°C) and humidity (50%–60%), with free access to water and food. All the animals were randomly assigned to various experimental groups, with 3–5 mice per cage. The sample size for each experiment was determined through previous studies and pre-experimental data, while adhering to the principle of minimizing animal usage. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Experimental Animal Platform of the School of Medical Sciences, Zhengzhou University (approval number: ZZU-LAC20231201[12]) and was conducted following the Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Skin/muscle incision retraction mouse model

On the basis of the method described by Flatters,41 we established a mouse model of chronic postoperative pain induced by skin/muscle incision retraction (SMIR). Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 2%–3% isoflurane (RWD Life Science Co., Shenzhen, China). Following anesthesia, the mice were placed in a supine position and secured. A 1 cm-wide incision was made adjacent to the saphenous vein in the right leg, and the superficial gracilis muscle was bluntly dissected from the underlying tendon fascia of the adductor muscle group. A microdistractor (RWD Life Science Co., R22030-03) was then implanted to stretch the skin and muscles to a length of 1–1.5 cm for 1 h. During the procedure, the incision was covered with sterile, saline-moistened gauze, and the mouse was placed on a thermal blanket to maintain body temperature. After 1 h, the skin and muscles were sutured, and antibiotics were administered to prevent infection. In the sham group, the tendon fascia of the adductor muscle group was exposed but not subjected to traction.

Method details

Behavioral testing

The 50% paw withdrawal threshold (PWT50) was used to assess mechanical allodynia in the mice by von Frey test. Briefly, following the methodology described by Chaplan et al.,42 the up-down method was adopted for measuring the PWT50. Prior to testing, all the mice were acclimated to a Plexiglas box (Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with a metal mesh floor for 30 min daily over three consecutive days. Once habituated to the environment, a series of von Frey hairs with logarithmically increasing stiffness values (0.04, 0.07, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 1, 1.4, and 2 g; Aesthesio, USA) were employed to stimulate the hind paws. The filaments were applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface with sufficient force to bend for 5 s. Rapid lifting or retraction of the paw was considered a positive response. Subsequent to a positive response, a finer filament was utilized; in the absence of a positive response, a thicker filament was used in the next test. Tests were conducted with 5-min intervals between each test.

Paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was used to assess thermal hypersensitivity by Hargreaves test.43 The mice were individually housed in test compartments (Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., XR1880) positioned on a temperature-controlled glass platform maintained at 30°C. The lateral plantar surface of each hind paw was stimulated via a radiant heat source directed through an aperture. The duration from stimulus initiation to paw withdrawal was recorded as the thermal paw withdrawal latency. Each hind paw underwent five trials, with a 5-min interval between each trial, and the latency values were averaged. To prevent tissue damage due to prolonged thermal exposure, a cutoff latency of 15 s was implemented.

The open field test (OFT) was used to assess the motor and emotional behaviors of the mice. In summary, all the mice were allowed to accustomed to the testing environment for 1 h over a period of two to three days. During the test, each mouse was placed in a square box (50 × 50 × 50 cm3, Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., XR-XZ301) and allowed to move freely for 5 min. The test chamber was meticulously cleaned with 70% ethanol between each session to eliminate any lingering odors. The parameters evaluated included the number of entries into the central area, the duration spent in the central area, and the total distance traveled. These parameters were analyzed via VisuTrack software (Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd.).

Stereoscopic surgery

The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and maintained under anesthesia with 1% isoflurane. Their heads were securely fixed in a stereotactic framework (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd.) mounted on a stereotactic fixation device. To prevent corneal dryness, erythromycin was applied to the eyes. All skull measurements were referenced to the bregma. A virus mixture of 200 nL was administered at a rate of 20 nL/min through a microelectrode (RWD Life Technology Co., Ltd.). Following the injection, the microelectrode was left in place for an additional 10 min to ensure adequate virus diffusion. The coordinates for the injections were as follows: for the ACC, anterior posterior (AP): +0.85 mm, medial lateral (ML): ±0.55 mm, and dorsal-ventral (DV): −2.00 mm; for the AcbC, anterior posterior (AP): +0.85 mm, medial lateral (ML): ±1.00 mm, and dorsal-ventral (DV): −4.3 mm.

For virus tracing experiments, rAAV2/9-EFIα-mCherry-DIO-WPRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and rAAV2/1-CaMKIIα-CRE-WPRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were injected into the ACC, and rAAV2/R-hSyn-mCherry-DIO-WPRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was injected into the AcbC. The data were excluded from the experiments when viral injections were inaccurate.

Fiber photometry recording

In the context of fiber photometry experiments, rAAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMp6s-WPRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was administered to AcbC. Additionally, rAAV2/1-CaMKIIα-mCherry-CRE-WPRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was injected into the ACC, whereas rAAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMp6s-DIO-WPREs (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was injected into the AcbC. An optical fiber was subsequently implanted into the AcbC, which was positioned 100–200 μm above the virus injection site (200 μm OD, 0.39 NA; Inper Life Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). The mice were separated and housed individually. Following recovery, neuronal calcium dynamics were recorded via a fiber photometry system (Inper Life Technology Co., Ltd.) during filament stimulation. Calcium-dependent signals were detected via light at 470 nm (45 mW), whereas calcium-independent signals served as a balanced control and were detected via 410 nm light (25 mW). During the recordings, von Frey stimulation was applied to the planar surface of each mouse, with a 0.6 g filament representing non-nociceptive stimuli and a 2 g filament representing nociceptive stimuli. These based on their performance in the sham group and naive group in the pre-experiment, as well as previous study.16 Each mouse underwent five repeated stimulation trials. The data were then subdivided on the basis of behavioral events within individual experiments, and the calcium signal change value (ΔF/F) was calculated as (F-F0)/F0 via InperPlot software (Inper Life Technology Co., Ltd.). Baseline measurements were determined via the ΔF/F value recorded 2 s prior to von Frey stimulation. For the sorting of spikes related to neuronal calcium activity, we selected the calcium signals of neurons in mice that were freely active for 5 min in an open field experiment for analysis. The spike threshold for calcium-dependent signals was calculated via the formula Median+3∗median absolute deviation (MAD), implemented through InperPlot software (Inper Life Technology Co., Ltd.).44 MAD was used to calculate the absolute median difference of the 5-min data range. In order to avoid false positive spikes being screened out, we chose a windowsize of 5 s. The spike was the calcium signal above the threshold within the windowsize.

In vivo electrophysiological recording

For in vivo electrophysiological recordings, an electrode array consisting of eight 25-μm diameter microwires (KedouBC Technology Co., Ltd, Suzhou, China) arranged in a 4 × 2 configuration was surgically implanted into the AcbC. Two skull-mounted screws were connected to stainless steel wires, which served as the ground electrode. Post-surgical animals were individually housed and allowed a 7-day recovery period. Prior to electrophysiological recordings, all animals were subjected to a 2–3-day habituation protocol, during which they were acclimated to the recording headstage for 10 min daily within a copper mesh-enclosed open field apparatus. Neural signals were acquired using an Apollo II 32-channel digital signal processor (Bio-Signal Technologies, Nanjing, China), with spontaneous firing activity filtered at 200–3000 Hz through the system’s acquisition software. Subsequent data analysis was performed using Neuroexplorer software (Nex Technologies, USA), where well-isolated neuronal units were classified through an unsupervised k-means clustering algorithm. Offline spike sorting was implemented based on three principal parameters: half-spike width, half-valley width, and mean firing rate.

Chemogenetic and optogenetic experiments

In the context of chemogenetic experiments, either rAAV2/9-hSyn-hM4D(Gi)-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-polyA or rAAV2/9-hSyn-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was injected into the AcbC. Additionally, rAAV2/9-CaMKIIα-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was administered into the ACC. Following stable viral expression, all the mice were acclimated to the behavioral testing room one day prior to the experiment. Subsequently, clozapine N-oxide (CNO) (MCE), which was prepared at a concentration of 3.3 mg/kg in 1% DMSO, was administered intraperitoneally to the mice, and behavioral tests were conducted 30 min later.45

For our optogenetic experiments, we injected rAAV2/1-CaMKIIα-eNpHR3.0-mCherry-CRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) or rAAV2/1-CaMKIIα-hChR2-mCherry-CRE-hGH-polyA (BrainVTA Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) into the ACC. Subsequently, rAAV2/9-hSyn-mCherry-DIO-hGH-polyA was injected into the AcbC. An optical fiber was implanted into the AcbC, which was positioned 100–200 μm above the viral injection site (200 μm OD, 0.39 NA; Inper Life Technology Co., Ltd.). The mice were housed and fed individually to ensure proper recovery. Following recovery, we employed an optogenetic system (Inper Life Technology Co., Ltd.) to regulate neurons that had successfully expressed the viral constructs. During behavioral testing, mice expressing eNpHR3.0 were continuously stimulated with 589 nm yellow light at a constant power of 10 mW,46 whereas mice expressing hChR2 were stimulated with 473 nm blue light in 15 ms pulses at 4 mW.47

Immunohistochemistry

The mice were euthanized under terminal anesthesia and perfused through the ascending aorta with normal saline, followed by a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following perfusion, the brains were extracted and postfixed in the same fixative for 12 h. The fixative was subsequently replaced with 30% sucrose in PBS for an additional 48 h. Sections of the ACC with a thickness of 25 μm were cut using a cryostat (Leica, Japan, CM1950) and prepared for immunohistochemical analysis. The sections were washed with PBS, blocked with 5% goat serum in 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at 37°C, and then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The next day, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody for 2 h at 37°C. The brain slices were then mounted with DAPI Fluoromount-G mounting medium (SouthBiotech, USA, 0100-20). The stained sections were visualized and examined via a Nikon AIRMP+ laser confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Quantitative and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software and SPSS software. The normality of data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. All statistical tests were two-tailed. For comparisons between two groups with equal variances, an unpaired Student’s t test was applied. For comparisons between two groups with unequal variances, the Welch’s t test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed if variances were equal; if not, the Welch ANOVA with Games-Howell post hoc test was used instead. Comparisons between multiple groups with two factors, were performed using the Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A probability value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.
