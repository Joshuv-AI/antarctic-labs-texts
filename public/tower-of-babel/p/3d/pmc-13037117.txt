Association of fasting plasma glucagon with osteopenia/osteoporosis in Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Lei Zhang, Pu Zhang, Ruifeng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBone mass abnormalities (osteopenia/osteoporosis) are highly prevalent in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), yet identifying the systemic metabolic indicators of skeletal deterioration remains a critical clinical challenge. This study aimed to investigate the independent association between fasting plasma glucagon levels and osteopenia/osteoporosis in Chinese T2DM patients, and to evaluate whether elevated glucagon acts as an independent risk indicator for osteopenia/osteoporosis.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted in which medical records were collected from 218 patients with T2DM, and plasma glucagon levels were obtained to assess the association with osteoporosis/osteoporosis. Bone mineral density (BMD) was evaluated by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA).ResultsT2DM patients with bone mass abnormalities (osteopenia/osteoporosis) exhibited significantly higher fasting plasma glucagon levels. Glucagon levels were inversely associated with BMD T-scores at the total hip and femoral neck. After adjusting for age, BMI, diabetes duration, and antidiabetic therapies, multivariable logistic regression confirmed that elevated fasting glucagon was independently associated with a higher risk of osteopenia/osteoporosis (OR = 1.112, 95%CI:1.032–1.198, P = 0.006). ROC curve analysis indicated that fasting glucagon possessed a moderate predictive capacity for identifying bone loss, with an area under the curve of 0.614. The optimal cutoff value was 12.205 pmol/L, yielding a sensitivity of 76.3% and a specificity of 43.6%.ConclusionsElevated fasting plasma glucagon is independently associated with reduced bone mass and serves as an independent associated factor for osteopenia/osteoporosis in Chinese patients with T2DM. Introduction

Glucagon is mostly recognized for its function in regulating blood glucose concentrations. Besides this major role, it also exerts various other metabolic actions. For example, it can promote lipolysis, thereby increasing energy expenditure [1]. In pancreatic α-cells, proglucagon is selectively cleaved to glucagon by prohormone convertase 2 (PC2). PCs in different tissues produce a range of proglucagon-derived peptides (PGDPs) with different biological functions involved in important physiological processes such as glucose regulation, energy metabolism and neural signaling [2]. Notably, glucagon levels are markedly elevated in T2DM patients relative to healthy subjects [3, 4], and it is thought that hyperglucagonemia is due to α-cell dysfunction [5].

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a multifaceted metabolic disorder with far reaching chronic complications, among which its deleterious impact on the skeletal system has emerged as a significant clinical concern [6]. Despite the complexity of diabetic bone disease, the clinical impact of reduced bone mass in T2DM remains profound, with the prevalence of osteoporosis reaching as high as 35.6% among patients in mainland China [7]. This skeletal fragility stems from a multifactorial compromise of both bone quality and quantity, driven by a consensus of pathological factors including chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [8–10]. These systemic derangements collectively result in aberrant bone metabolism and heightened bone fragility, placing T2DM patients at a significant risk for progressive bone loss [11–13]. Within this pathological framework, fasting glucagon emerges as a key metabolic indicator that integrates these deleterious signals. For instance, insulin resistance in pancreatic α-cells impairs the physiological suppression of glucagon secretion, while T2DM-associated chronic inflammation and oxidative stress directly perturb α-cell function, leading to systemic dysregulation [14, 15]. Meanwhile, subtle declines in renal function reduce the metabolic clearance of glucagon, further contributing to its elevation [16]. A recent cross-sectional study demonstrated that plasma glucagon levels are positively associated with serum bone turnover markers in T2DM patients [17]. These findings suggest that elevated glucagon reflects an accelerated bone turnover state. However, clinical evidence specifically linking glucagon levels to osteopenia/osteoporosis remains limited and inconsistent.

Consequently, the aim of this study was to investigate the independent relationship between fasting glucagon levels and the risk of bone loss in patients with T2DM. By investigating these associations, we seek to provide novel insights into the metabolic risk stratification of skeletal fragility in patients with T2DM, potentially facilitating the early identification of individuals at risk for bone mass abnormalities.

Methods

Study subjects

This retrospective study utilized de-identified clinical data extracted from the electronic medical records of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between April 2024 and January 2026. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria:1. age ≥ 18 years; 2. diagnosis of T2DM; 3. availability of complete dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) measurements. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:1. diagnosis of diabetes other than T2DM; 2. premenopausal women, defined based on menstrual history documented in the medical records as ongoing menstruation or amenorrhea for less than 12 consecutive months; 3. severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; 4. current or recent use (within 6 months) of medications known to affect bone metabolism, including glucocorticoids, estrogen, or progesterone.

Clinical data measurements

De-identified clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical records of the Department of Endocrinology. Fasting venous blood samples were collected in the morning following an overnight fast. Clinical laboratory measurements included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). In accordance with the standardized clinical protocol of our department, fasting plasma glucagon is measured as a routine component of the comprehensive pancreatic islet function assessment for all hospitalized patients with T2DM. Accordingly, all plasma glucagon levels reported in this study represent fasting states. Plasma glucagon levels were determined using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) on a fully automated system with the Glucagon Assay Kit (Jinde Biotech, Guangzhou, China). The assay has a detection range of 2–128 pmol/L, with an established clinical reference range of 2–18 pmol/L. To ensure analytical reliability, both the intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were maintained at ≤ 10.0%, and the linearity correlation coefficient was ≥ 0.9900. Serum C-peptide was measured by immunoassay, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] was quantified by electrochemiluminescence. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. According to the criteria of the Chinese Obesity Working Group Collaborative Meta-Analysis Group, BMI < 24 kg/m² was considered normal, while BMI ≥ 24 kg/m² was classified as overweight or obese.

Bone mineral density (BMD) measurement

To investigate the association between plasma glucagon levels and bone loss in T2DM patients, this study combined patients with osteopenia and osteoporosis into a single group (osteopenia/osteoporosis), aiming to comprehensively assess the relationships between glucagon and BMD. BMD was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (GE-Lunar, DAX, Madison, WI, USA) at the Bone Density Laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, performed by a specialized radiologist. BMD was assessed at three distinct sites for each patient: the whole lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck and hip. Patients were classified into two groups based on the minimal BMD T-score: normal (T ≥ -1.0) and osteopenia/osteoporosis (-2.5 < T < -1.0 for osteopenia; T ≤ -2.5 for osteoporosis).

Statistical analysis

R 4.3.1 and SPSS 27.0 were used for data analysis. The study’s demographic and clinical characteristics were described using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, normal distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard error, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For statistical purposes, a P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. The associations between fasting glucagon and BMD or T-scores were evaluated using multivariable linear regression models to adjust for potential confounders. A binary logistic regression was used to analyze the potential relationship between glucagon and the risk of osteopenia/osteoporosis. Additionally, a receiver operating characteristic curve was performed to demonstrate the predictive efficacy of glucagon.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The study included 218 patients with T2DM, of whom 76 demonstrated normal bone mineral density, 91 were diagnosed with osteopenia, and 51 with osteoporosis. Table 1 lists the characteristics of the T2DM patients (n = 218) included in this study. The clinical characteristics of T2DM patients stratified by BMD status are summarized in Table 1. Among the participants, the normal BMD group consisted of 56 males and 20 females, while the osteopenia/osteoporosis group included 93 males and 49 females. Compared with patients in the normal BMD group, those with bone mass abnormalities (osteopenia/osteoporosis) were significantly older and exhibited markedly elevated plasma glucagon levels. Conversely, TG and LDL-c levels were significantly lower in the osteopenia/osteoporosis group. To further evaluate the impact of glucagon, patients were stratified into three groups based on glucagon tertiles: T1 (Glucagon < 8.91 pmol/L; 48 males and 25 females), T2 (Glucagon 8.91–13.01 pmol/L; 44 males and 29 females), and T3 (Glucagon > 13.01 pmol/L; 57 males and 15 females). Significant differences were observed across the three tertiles regarding serum 25(OH)D, BMI, and fasting C-peptide levels (Table 2). While no significant differences were observed in absolute BMD values across the tertiles, bone health parameters presented as standardized scores exhibited significant variations. Specifically, Lumbar spine BMD T-scores (P = 0.004) and Total hip BMD T-scores (P = 0.020) were significantly different among the groups. Among the common anti-diabetic medications, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors and biguanides showed statistically significant differences across the glucagon tertiles. Figure 1 illustrates the prevalence of bone mass abnormalities (osteopenia/osteoporosis) among T2DM patients stratified by glucagon tertiles. As plasma glucagon levels increased from the lowest (T1) to the highest tertile (T3), a significant upward trend in the prevalence of osteopenia/osteoporosis was observed.

The associations of plasma glucagon levels with risk of osteopenia/osteoporosis in T2DM patients and ROC curve analysis

To further explore the independent association between fasting glucagon and bone loss, we conducted multivariable regression analyses. As shown in Table 4, after adjusting for covariates including BMI, age, sex, diabetes duration, HbA1c, fasting C-peptide, 25(OH)D, medication history, fasting glucagon remained an independent negative predictor of the minimum T-score (β =-0.203, P = 0.010). With the same covariate adjustments, multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that higher fasting glucagon was an independent risk marker for osteopenia/osteoporosis (OR = 1.112, P = 0.006). As presented in Table 5, a significant positive trend was observed across the increasing tertiles of fasting glucagon. After adjusting for the full set of confounding factors (Model 3), patients in the highest tertile exhibited increased risk of osteopenia/osteoporosis compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR = 3.597, P = 0.003). To evaluate the utility of glucagon in identifying bone mass abnormalities (osteopenia/osteoporosis), we performed ROC curve analysis. As depicted in Fig. 2 ROC curve of the glucagon prediction model for osteopenia/osteoporosis, fasting glucagon exhibited moderate predictive capacity for bone mass abnormalities, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.614. The optimal cutoff value of fasting glucagon was determined to be 12.205 pmol/L, which yielded a sensitivity of 76.3% and a specificity of 43.6%.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is a novel investigation into the correlation between glucagon and bone mass abnormalities (osteopenia/osteoporosis) in individuals with T2DM. T2DM patients with osteopenia/osteoporosis had higher glucagon levels and older age. Our results demonstrated that elevated fasting plasma glucagon levels were significantly associated with reduced BMD T-scores across multiple skeletal sites. Furthermore, an increase in glucagon levels was shown to correlate with a heightened risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis in patients with T2DM.

A “diabetic bone paradox” exists in T2DM, characterized by normal or even elevated BMD alongside a significantly higher incidence of fragility fractures compared to non-diabetic patients [18, 19]. This paradoxical phenomenon is primarily attributed to impaired bone quality, such as collagen cross-linking damage induced by AGEs, rather than isolated abnormalities in bone mass [20]. Although fracture risk is elevated in all patients with T2DM, those who concurrently meet the diagnostic criteria for osteopenia or osteoporosis face the double jeopardy of further compromised bone quality and reduced bone mass, resulting in a markedly higher absolute fracture risk [21, 22]. In mainland China, the prevalence of osteoporosis among T2DM patients is as high as 35.6%, which underscores the substantial size of this high-risk subgroup [7]. Given the diabetic bone paradox, recent consensus recommends a proactive threshold (T < -2.0) for T2DM, with adjusted T-score assessments remaining an accessible risk stratification tool [23, 24]. Exploring the potential link between fasting glucagon and bone abnormalities in T2DM is significant for both identifying individuals at risk for bone mass loss and better understanding the metabolic signals related to concurrent bone loss and underlying metabolic dysfunction.

In present study, while medication use was balanced between BMD groups, SGLT2 inhibitors and biguanides differed significantly across glucagon tertiles. This observation is noteworthy because both SGLT2 inhibitors and biguanides have been reported to elevate glucagon levels through complex and sometimes contradictory mechanisms [25, 26]. To isolate the potential effect of glucagon from these pharmacological influences, we adjusted for all agents in our multivariable models [27–29]. This adjustment ensures that the observed association between glucagon and bone status remains independent of interference from current medical therapy.

Research directly linking glucagon to bone metabolism remains limited. No direct evidence supports its effects on human bone cells, given the absence of GCGR in the mouse osteoblast precursor cell line MC3T3 and failure of pharmacological glucagon to modulate osteogenic differentiation via GLP-1R or homologous receptors [30]. While a large community-based study reported a positive correlation between glucagon and bone turnover markers in T2DM patients, but its cohort limitations prevent definitive conclusions on skeletal health impacts [17]. To address these gaps, our study provides robust clinical evidence. After adjusting for age, BMI, sex, diabetes duration, and medication use, plasma glucagon levels were inversely associated with T-scores at the femoral neck and total hip, though no significant associations with absolute BMD were observed. This discrepancy likely stems from the nature of the T-score as a standardized metric. By comparing individual BMD against a sex-matched healthy young reference population, the T-score effectively minimizes inherent age- and sex-related variance, thereby better capturing the independent association between glucagon and bone status. In the tertile analysis of glucagon levels, lumbar spine T-scores differed significantly across groups, yet no clear linear trend was observed. The lumbar spine is predominantly composed of trabecular bone, which exhibits a higher turnover rate and greater sensitivity to metabolic and endocrine signals. Therefore, this non-linear association may reflect a more complex correlation between glucagon and trabecular bone metabolism, warranting further investigation [31].

Osteoporosis is diagnosed based on the lowest T-score among multiple skeletal sites; the minimum T-score is the most clinically relevant metric. Given the heightened fracture risk in T2DM, international guidelines advocate for a more proactive approach, emphasizing that osteopenia (− 2.5 < T < − 1) should be integrated into risk assessments to avoid underestimating skeletal fragility [32]. In line with these considerations, our study demonstrated that glucagon levels were independently and negatively associated with the minimum T-score and served as a potential risk indicator for osteopenia/osteoporosis. Although ROC curve analysis revealed that glucagon has only modest utility in predicting bone loss among T2DM patients (AUC = 0.614), this finding is not unexpected. T2DM-related osteoporosis is a multifactorial metabolic disorder [33], and thus a single hormonal indicator is inherently insufficient to serve as a comprehensive diagnostic tool. However, the confirmed independent association between glucagon levels and bone loss underscores its potential role in enhancing clinical risk assessment. Future research should explore the integration of glucagon into multivariable risk stratification models, where it may contribute as a valuable metabolic parameter alongside other clinical risk factors for bone loss in this population.

In our study, elevated glucagon levels positively correlated with AST and ALT specifically among patients with bone mass abnormalities (Table S1), potentially reflecting a disrupted liver-α-cell axis. Specifically, metabolic liver dysfunction in T2DM impairs hepatic amino acid metabolism and induces glucagon resistance, leading to a compensatory increase in glucagon secretion. Recent evidence emphasizes the liver-bone axis in skeletal health, with hepatic fibrosis being identified as a robust driver of osteoporosis risk in T2DM patients [34]. Mechanistically, glucagon is known to stimulate hepatic FGF21 release [35], a mediator of the liver-bone axis that impairs bone health by inhibiting osteogenesis and enhancing osteoclastogenesis via IGFBP1 [36]. However, further prospective and mechanistic research is essential to validate these implications.

Limitations

This study is subject to certain limitations. Its retrospective design precluded prospective control over factors like specific medication regimens and limited access to variables such as physical activity. Additionally, the modest sample size constrained robust subgroup analyses. Furthermore, owing to the cross-sectional nature of this retrospective analysis, we could not establish a definitive causal relationship between elevated glucagon and bone mass reduction. To address these, we plan to collect a larger cohort to validate a glucagon-integrated predictive model for bone loss in T2DM.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings indicate that elevated fasting plasma glucagon levels are significantly and negatively associated with bone mineral density T-scores in patients with T2DM. Higher glucagon levels are independently associated with bone loss in Chinese T2DM patients, even after adjusting for multiple metabolic confounders and medication use. These results offer a novel perspective for early metabolic-based risk stratification and enhance our understanding of the complex relationship between glucagon dysregulation and bone loss.
