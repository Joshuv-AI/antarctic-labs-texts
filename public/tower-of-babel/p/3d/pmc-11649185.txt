Exploring the Relationship Among Financial Hardship, Anxiety, and Depression in Patients With Cancer: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Betina Yanez, Laura M. Perry, John Devin Peipert, Maja Kuharic, Chloe Taub, Sofia F. Garcia, Alma Diaz, Diana Buitrago, Quan Mai, Laila A. Gharzai, David Cella, Sheetal M. Kircher
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEFinancial hardship (FH) is a complex issue in cancer care, affecting material conditions, well-being, and coping behaviors. This study aimed to longitudinally examine FH, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and their associations while incorporating social determinants of health and health care cost covariates in a sample of patients diagnosed with cancer.METHODSThis prospective, longitudinal cohort study analyzed data from 2,305 participants from the Northwestern University Improving the Management of Symptoms during and following Cancer Treatment trial. Outcomes assessed at baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postbaseline included depressive symptoms, anxiety, and FH. Analysis involved random intercept cross-lagged panel models to explore between- and within-person effects, incorporating factors such as age, sex, insurance status, neighborhood area deprivation, health care charges, out-of-pocket costs, and health literacy.RESULTSThe cohort had a mean age of 60.7 (standard deviation [SD] = 12.7) years and was mostly female (64.9%) and White (86.2%). Correlations were found between FH and depressive symptoms (r = 0.310) and anxiety (r = 0.289). A predictive relationship was observed between FH and depressive symptoms, with baseline and 6-month depressive symptom levels predicting later FH (baseline β = .079, P = .070; 6-month β = .072, P = .081) and 9-month FH significantly predicting 12-month depressive symptoms (β = .083, P = .025), even after accounting for health care charges and out-of-pocket costs. Baseline and 9-month anxiety showed a predictive relationship with subsequent FH (baseline β = .097, P = .023; 9-month β = .071, P = .068).CONCLUSIONFH emerged as a prominent issue, with nearly half of participants experiencing some level of FH. Depressive symptoms and anxiety were related to FH. These findings underscore the need for a comprehensive approach in cancer care that concurrently addresses anxiety, depressive symptoms, and FH, recognizing their interconnected impact.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer is among the leading causes of morbidity in the United States, with significant implications for public health and the health care economy.1,2 The cost of cancer care substantially affects the lives of cancer survivors, prompting them to make significant lifestyle changes and causing some to incur long-term medical debt.3 In the United States, about 20% of cancer survivors reported financial hardship (FH) because of cancer treatment in 2016.4 Additionally, a 2021 American Cancer Society Cancer Action Network survey found that 61% of US patients and survivors struggled with cancer care costs, and 37% had difficulty affording prescription drugs and treatments.3 Even with health insurance, cancer survivors are at a higher risk of FH compared with individuals without a cancer history, facing out-of-pocket expenses that may exceed 20% of their annual family income.5,6

FH in cancer care is a complex issue encompassing three domains: material conditions such as out-of-pocket expenses, employment limitations, lost income, and medical debt; well-being responses like distress due to costs; and coping behaviors, including skipping medical appointments or medication due to cost.7 Key socioeconomic predictors of greater FH include lack of health insurance or underinsurance, lower income, unemployment, and younger age at cancer diagnosis.8,9 Inadequate insurance coverage exacerbates FH, often resulting in high out-of-pocket costs.8 Employment challenges further exacerbate this hardship, as reduced opportunities for steady income, difficulty returning to work, and potential job loss because of prolonged absences significantly diminish financial stability.10,11 Ultimately, FH extends beyond material struggles, intertwining with emotional and social aspects that can lead to increased anxiety and depressive symptoms and ultimately affect patients' health-related quality of life (HRQoL).8,12-15

The intricate relationship between FH, anxiety, and depressive symptoms has not been comprehensively investigated, in part because many previous studies have been limited by cross-sectional designs.14,16,17 While most research has explored how FH leads to psychological distress, the prospective and potentially reciprocal impact of distress on patients' financial situations has not yet been examined. This limits our understanding of longitudinal changes and the full spectrum of challenges patients with cancer and survivors face. Moreover, existing studies have primarily relied on patient-reported data to estimate patients' health care costs. While this approach offers valuable insights, it is prone to recall bias and does not reflect the full spectrum of costs.6,18 Recognizing the need to address this gap, this study aims to longitudinally examine patients' broad perceptions of FH, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and their associations while incorporating social determinants of health and health care cost covariates in a longitudinal sample of patients diagnosed with cancer. We hypothesize that after adjusting for relevant covariates, greater FH will predict greater depressive symptoms and anxiety.

METHODS

Design and Procedures

This prospective longitudinal cohort study involved oncology patients in active treatment and survivors from the Northwestern University, Improving the Management of Symptoms during and Following Cancer Treatment trial (Appendix Fig A1, online only).19,20 Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older, had a medical chart diagnosis of any type of cancer and stage within the Northwestern Memorial Healthcare system, had a valid email address, and had planned cancer treatment or follow-up within Northwestern Medicine Health System. Recruitment used an automated electronic health record (EHR) algorithm to identify and enroll eligible patients daily, storing their details in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database. Participants completed surveys using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Northwestern at study baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postbaseline, with their responses later linked to EHR and medical billing data.21,22

Measures

Outcome Variables

Outcomes of interest, including depressive symptoms, anxiety, and FH, were assessed at baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postbaseline. Depressive symptoms and anxiety were measured using the Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System computer adaptive tests scored on a T-score metric that norms raw scores to the mean of a reference population (50 represents the mean of the reference population, and each 10-point increment indicates one standard deviation [SD] above or below the mean).23 FH was measured using question 12 from the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST) version 2, rated from 0 [not at all] to 4 [very much] (COST-FACIT item: My illness has been a FH to my family and me).24 Item 12 from the COST-FACIT item has previously been used as screening item for FH.25

Sample Characteristics

Baseline surveys gathered demographic and clinical data such as age, sex, race, ethnicity, education, insurance status, cancer type, and health literacy, as measured by the Single Item Literacy Screener (SILS).26 Socioeconomic status was inferred using participants' zip codes to calculate their Area Deprivation Index (ADI) score, a measure based on 17 socioeconomic indicators, ranked from 1 (least disadvantaged) to 100 (most disadvantaged).27

Health Care Charges

We acquired total health care charges and out-of-pocket amounts from billing records covering all health care encounters in the 6 months before the baseline assessment date. Total healthcare charges were the cumulative sum of charges within the health care system (both professional and facility charges), covering inpatient, outpatient, emergency room services, laboratory test results, imaging, and procedures. These charges represent the amount billed to the payer and patient, incorporating any negotiated contracted rates with the payer. For self-pay patients, the total charge amount excludes any subsequent discounts offered. Out-of-pocket amounts were defined as the total sum expected to be paid by the patient after factoring in insurance payments, adjustments by the health system, and encompassing all health care encounters over the 6 months before the baseline assessment date.

Analysis

Data on the sample's baseline characteristics, health care costs, and longitudinal responses were obtained from REDCap and Northwestern University's Enterprise Data Warehouse (EDW) linked using each participant's unique study ID. The EDW is a comprehensive, integrated repository of all clinical and research data sources at Northwestern Medicine. Analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.3.1. First, data were screened and cleaned for response validity, outliers, and missing data. Next, descriptive statistics were conducted to examine sample characteristics and sample-wide changes in outcome variables over time.

Primary analysis involved two separate random intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPMs) that were specified in the lavaan package for R.28 The first model examined longitudinal associations between depression and FH, whereas the second was between anxiety and FH. These RI-CLPMs are longitudinal, multilevel structural equation models that separate the associations between two variables over multiple time points into between-person effects and within-person effects.29,30 Between-person effects capture the association between stable, trait-like individual differences in the two variables across time (eg, people who tend to score higher on depressive symptoms compared with others across time points also tend to score higher on FH compared with others). However, of particular interest to our hypotheses were the within-person cross-lagged paths, which test whether individual changes in one variable at a specific time point prospectively predict subsequent changes in the other variable at the next time point. Based on published recommendations,31 we used the following benchmarks for interpreting the size of our standardized cross-lagged path coefficients: small effect >0.03, medium effect >0.07, and large effect >0.12. Both RI-CLPMs were estimated using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation to handle missing data. Missing data because of dropout over time could have biased results by reducing the observed sample size at later time points, which could reduce the representativeness of the data and results. However, FIML is usually preferred among longitudinal structural equation modeling.32,33 Each model's adequacy was evaluated using several fit indices. The thresholds used were as follows: Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) values >0.95, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) values above 0.95, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) values below 0.06, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values below 0.08, each indicative of an acceptable model fit.34

Ethical Approval and Informed Consent

This study was approved by the Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine institutional review board. All participants provided their informed consent before study inclusion.

RESULTS

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Our analytic sample comprised 2,305 participants. At the 12-month assessment, sample sizes because of attrition ranged from 1,411 to 1,486, depending on the variables of interest (Appendix Fig A1). At baseline (Table 1), the mean age was 60.68 years (SD = 12.72), with most participants being female (64.9%), White (86.2%), and non-Hispanic/Latin (96.6%). Most of the participants completed college (71.1%) and reported high health literacy on the SILS (87.5%). The ADI percentile mean was 30.22 (SD = 19.40), indicating a relatively less disadvantaged cohort on average. Insurance status varied, with the majority having private insurance (51.9%) or Medicare Advantage (33.3%). In the 6 months before providing consent for the study, 97.8% of participants had at least one health care encounter, with a median of eight encounters per participant (range, 1-65). Regarding health care costs during this period, participants' median total health care charges were $21,834.78 US dollars (USD; range, $0 USD-$1,803,050 USD). Meanwhile, median out-of-pocket expenses were $0 USD (range: $0 USD-$16,577.64 USD). The most common cancer types were breast cancer, followed by lymphoma and leukemia.

Longitudinal Assessment of FH, Anxiety, and Depressive Symptoms

At baseline, almost half of the participants (49.3%) reported experiencing some level of FH because of their illness.35 Specifically, 23% reported a little bit, 14.7% somewhat, 5.7% quite a bit, and 6% very much FH. Over the study period, this pattern of FH persisted, with 10%-12% of participants consistently reporting high levels of FH (quite a bit or very much) at any given time point.

Longitudinal Associations Between FH, Depression, and Anxiety

Both longitudinal RI-CLPM models demonstrated excellent fit, evidenced by the following indices for depressive symptoms: TLI = 0.99, CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.02, and SRMS = 0.02 and for anxiety: CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.02, and SRMR = 0.02 (Appendix Figs A2 and A3).

Between-Person Effects

The results show positive correlations between FH and both depressive symptoms (r = 0.310, P < .001) and anxiety (r = 0.289, P < .001) when examining between-person effects.

Covariate Effects at Between-Person Level

Table 2 presents the standardized regression coefficients indicating the strength and direction of associations between various covariates and the outcome variables of depressive symptoms, anxiety, and FH. Notably, both total health care expenditures (β = .047, P = .018) and total out-of-pocket costs (β = .101, P < .001) were positively associated with FH, indicating small and medium effect sizes, respectively. A significant large effect was observed for associations with higher FH among those with younger age (β = –.316, P < .001), participants uninsured or on Medicaid (β = .111, P < .001), and those from more deprived areas (ADI; β = .127, P < .001). Regarding anxiety, younger age (β = –.177, P < .001) and lower health literacy (β = –.187, P < .001) were associated with higher anxiety levels, both showing large effects. Furthermore, being female (β = .167, P < .001) was significantly associated with higher anxiety levels, with a large effect size, and being uninsured or on Medicaid (β = .059, P = .010) showed a small effect in the same direction. Depressive symptoms followed similar trends: younger individuals (β = –.093, P < .001) and those with lower health literacy (β = –.218, P < .001) were associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms, exhibiting medium and large effects, respectively. Additionally, being female was associated with greater depressive symptoms (β = .145, P < .001), with a large effect size, whereas being uninsured or on Medicaid (β = .073, P = .002) and living in more deprived areas (β = .060, P = .014) were associated with higher depression levels, both with small effect sizes, respectively. Because a large proportion of the sample was patients with breast cancer, a sensitivity analysis was run examining both models in a subgroup of the sample (n = 1,366; 47.2% female, 52.8% male) that did not include participants with the following cancer diagnoses: breast, cervical, ovarian, prostate, uterine, and other gynecologic. In the depression model (CFI = 0.992, TLI = 0.988, RMSEA = 0.023, 0.020), female sex remained associated with higher levels of depression (β = .172, P < .001). In the anxiety model (CFI = 0.988, TLI = 0.983, RMSEA = 0.028, SRMR = 0.021), female sex also remained associated with higher levels of anxiety (β = .195, P < .001). Across both models, sex remained unrelated to FH (β = –.004, P = .938).

DISCUSSION

This longitudinal study investigates a dynamic interconnectedness between patients' broad perception of FH, depressive symptoms, and anxiety, along with their links to various sociodemographic factors, highlighting the temporal sequencing of these experiences. We observed a significant association between depressive symptoms and FH and between anxiety and FH. This finding suggests that individuals experiencing anxiety and depressive symptoms are at risk of experiencing greater FH and that patients with greater FH are at risk for elevated depressive symptoms, even when accounting for material costs such as healthcare charges and out-of-pocket costs, insurance, and area deprivation.

FH emerged as a prominent issue, with nearly half of the participants experiencing some level of FH affecting themselves or their families because of their illness. This was consistent over 12 months, highlighting FH's chronic nature. Both total health care charges and out-of-pocket costs were positively associated with FH, with large effect sizes suggesting a substantial impact of medical expenses on patients' FH. Age, insurance status, the deprivation level of the residential area, and health literacy significantly predicted FH. This aligns with the existing literature on the profound impact of health care costs on the financial stability of patients with cancer, especially those with limited resources or lower socioeconomic status, highlighting existing disparities.36 Furthermore, depressive symptoms and anxiety in our cohort were associated with being female, being uninsured or having Medicaid, and area deprivation, underscoring the intersectionality of mental health with sex, socioeconomic status, and access to health care among patients with cancer. Given our findings showing the complex interplay between FH and mental health, the association of sex with mental health may be related to known disparities in pay, career opportunities, and child-rearing burdens that were not captured by our broad assessment of FH.37-39 The inverse relationship of anxiety and depression with age suggests that younger individuals might be more vulnerable to the psychological impacts of cancer and its financial repercussions. This aligns with research supported by the literature that points to younger age adults with cancer bearing disproportionate FH and higher levels of anxiety, possibly due to less accumulated wealth, fewer resources, or more significant career and family impacts during their treatment period.40,41

Our study's longitudinal design provided an in-depth analysis of the temporal relationships between FH, depressive symptoms, and anxiety. These results are in line with previous research indicating that cancer-related FH is a crucial factor affecting patients' psychological well-being.12,42-44 FH predicts subsequent depressive symptoms. This observation is crucial for designing future interventions as it suggests that addressing FH as soon as possible may also help alleviate depressive symptoms among individuals diagnosed with cancer. Elevated depressive symptoms can lead to a poorer HRQoL and compromise patient outcomes, potentially resulting in higher rates of mortality in cancer.45,46 A meta-analysis revealed that patients displaying even a few depressive symptoms may have a 25% increased risk of mortality.47 Additionally, our findings highlight the role of early anxiety in predicting later FH, pointing to anxiety as a factor that might amplify financial concerns. Although our measure of FH was limited to broad perceptions of hardship, one possible interpretation of our findings is that FH may intensify depressive symptoms because of its potential impact on reduced spending and savings and on financial strain concerns caused by cancer.7,48 Regarding anxiety, patients might be experiencing elevated anxiety regarding future treatment costs. More research is needed to elucidate how additional aspects of FH, such as material burden, relate to depressive symptoms and anxiety.

The findings from this study have significant implications for clinical oncology practice. Our findings highlight the need for an integrated care approach that combines financial counseling and psychological support, especially for groups at risk for greater FH, such as younger patients, women, and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Incorporating financial navigators alongside mental health professionals could offer a coordinated approach to simultaneously manage the economic and emotional challenges faced by patients with cancer.6 Despite the widespread recognition of FH screening in clinical care, there is a lack of systematic integration of FH screening in oncology clinical care. However, evidence from previous pilot studies and one randomized controlled trial supports the effectiveness of financial navigation programs in reducing FH.25,49 Findings of earlier studies suggest that although patients with cancer favor discussing FH with their health care providers, they may be reluctant to initiate the conversation.50,51 The absence of established guidelines for clinicians to address FH with their patients points to a potential gap in current oncology clinical practices, and integrating routine FH screening into patient-reported monitoring programs could be a solution.25 Early screening for both FH and depressive and anxiety symptoms holds the potential for timely, effective interventions, underscoring the necessity for proactive, comprehensive care in oncology.

Although the longitudinal design of this study provides valuable insights, the limitations, including missing data and demographic homogeneity, necessitate caution in the interpretation and generalization of the findings. Future research should aim to include a more diverse demographic profile to better understand the cultural and socioeconomic variations in experiences of FH and their impact on quality of life. At the study baseline, approximately 12% of our population experienced either quite a bit or very much FH, and our model effect sizes may have been higher in a more socioeconomically diverse patient population. Additionally, the FH measure used in this study focused on a broad perception of FH through an established single screening item, potentially underestimating additional domains of FH, such as material and coping aspects of FH. We used total charges and out-of-pocket costs from billing data to estimate direct FH. While patients' out-of-pocket expenses reflect their direct medical costs, it is important to recognize that our analysis did not cover indirect FH, such as lost income, childcare, and transportation expenses, which also burden patients. Additionally, the cost variables used in this study only reflect billing records covering health care encounters in the 6 months before the baseline assessment, and additional information, such as requests for financial assistance and participant income, was not available for analyses in this study. Finally, there was not a sufficient sample size in the low health literacy group to do a multigroup analysis, limiting our knowledge of this group.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the interconnectedness of FH, anxiety, and depressive symptoms in cancer care. This study underscores the importance of addressing FH and its psychological effects to improve patient-centered care, emphasizing the relationship between FH and depressive symptoms and the role of anxiety in predicting FH. Multidimensional care strategies in oncology are essential for better patient outcomes and quality of life and advancing integrated care models that address financial and emotional health needs is critical to achieving patient-centered oncology care.
