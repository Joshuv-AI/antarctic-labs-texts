Public Oral Healthcare Utilisation and Its Associated Factors in Malaysian Adults: A Time-Series Analysis (1992-2019)
Authors: Yeung R’ong Tan, Lokman Najihah, Jamaludin Marhazlinda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsUnderstanding the trend and associated factors are essential for enhancing equitable access to care, particularly given persistent disparities in healthcare utilisation across different population groups. This study examined the trend and factors associated with public oral healthcare services utilisation among adults in Malaysia over a 28-year period.MethodsUsing trend analysis and a multiple linear regression framework applied to time-series data from 1992 to 2019, the study explored factors associated with public oral healthcare utilisation among Malaysian adults. The analysis was guided by Andersen's Behavioral Model, with secondary aggregated annual data obtained from the Health Information Management System, the Department of Statistics Malaysia, the Malaysian Dental Council, and the United Nations Food Balance Sheets. Variables were categorised into predisposing, enabling, and need-based factors.ResultsThe analysis revealed four distinct phases in utilisation: a period of decline (1992-1997), stabilisation (1997-2002), gradual growth (2002-2012), and sharp increase (2012-2019). Key associated factors of public oral healthcare utilisation among Malaysian adults included a negative association with higher household income, unemployment, relative poverty, and a positive association with internet access.ConclusionThese results highlight the influence of enabling factors such as socioeconomic and digital factors in shaping healthcare access. Targeted strategies must address structural and economic barriers while leveraging digital technologies to improve access and utilisation, essential for achieving equitable oral health outcomes in Malaysia.Key words: Public oral healthcare, Healthcare utilisation, Healthcare inequalities, Multiple linear regression, Time series analysis, Malaysia

Introduction

Oral healthcare is a fundamental component of overall public health, contributing to the quality of life, well-being, and social functioning of individuals.1,2 Despite the well-established link between oral health and general health, access to public oral healthcare services remains uneven across different populations, particularly in developing countries such as Malaysia.3,4 As oral health is shaped by various social, economic, and environmental factors, understanding the drivers of healthcare utilisation is crucial for developing effective public health policies to improve access and equity.3, 4, 5 In Malaysia, public oral healthcare facilities, under the Ministry of Health (MOH), serve as essential access points for a comprehensive range of oral health services. These facilities deliver primary care services tailored to various population groups, including toddlers, preschool children, schoolchildren, antenatal mothers, and adults, along with community and oral health promotion initiatives.6,7 Across these service types, a wide array of interventions is offered, including preventive care, routine dental examinations, treatment for oral health conditions, and referrals to specialised care as needed.6,7 Specialist services are provided both at non-hospital-based and hospital-based specialist clinics.6,7

In Malaysia, the healthcare system comprises both public and private sectors.7 The public healthcare system, overseen by the Ministry of Health (MOH), provides affordable and accessible healthcare services to the majority of the population through a network of facilities including public hospitals, clinics, and healthcare facilities distributed across urban and rural areas.8 Oral healthcare is integral within this system, with dental services offered alongside other medical specialties.

Public Primary oral healthcare services play a critical role in promoting oral health and preventing oral diseases through essential preventive measures such as dental examinations, oral health promotion and education, fluoride treatments, and basic restorative care.9 Accessibility to these services, particularly in public oral healthcare facilities, is essential for ensuring equitable access across different socioeconomic groups.3,4,10 Public facilities cater to a diverse patient population, including individuals from underserved communities who may face barriers to accessing private dental services.4 Ensuring the availability and accessibility of primary oral healthcare services in public facilities is crucial for addressing oral health disparities and promoting population-wide oral health.11 However, despite the efforts of the Malaysian healthcare system, disparities in healthcare utilisation persist across various socio-economic and demographic groups.3,4 Studies have shown that factors such as education levels, income, access to healthcare professionals, and public health interventions significantly influence the extent to which individuals utilise healthcare services, including dental care.3, 4, 5

The Health Information Management System (HIMS) in Malaysia is a comprehensive data repository, aggregating data from various healthcare facilities, clinics, and programs nationwide.12 These reports, encompassing various healthcare services including oral health, are routinely compiled at the clinic level and aggregated nationally through the HIMS, a reporting platform for healthcare programs. It provides valuable insights into healthcare utilisation patterns and trends, including detailed information on patient demographics, diagnoses, treatments, and healthcare services utilised. HIMS is the primary data source for analysing trends and projections of primary oral healthcare utilisation in the public sector in Malaysia, supporting evidence-based decision-making and policy formulation.

Over the years, Malaysia has experienced significant improvements in literacy rates, economic growth, and healthcare infrastructure.8,13 Despite these positive trends, challenges remain in ensuring equitable access to oral healthcare. Understanding the factors driving these disparities is crucial for improving public oral healthcare services and ensuring all individuals have access to necessary dental care. The Andersen Behavioral Model of Health Services Use provides a valuable framework for analysing healthcare utilisation, categorising determinants into predisposing, enabling, and need factors.14 Predisposing factors such as literacy rates, dietary habits, age, gender, race or ethnicity influence individuals’ propensity to seek oral healthcare. Enabling factors, including income, employment, education, urbanisation, health insurance membership, family and neighbourhood support, and access to healthcare infrastructure such as availability of dental chairs, either facilitate or hinder access to services.3, 4, 5,15, 16, 17 With the rise of digital literacy, internet access has emerged as an enabling factor, providing greater access to health information and resources that empower individuals to make informed decisions about their oral health.18 Need factors reflect individuals’ perceived or assessed healthcare requirements, influenced by conditions such as chronic illnesses, mental health status, dental health practices, and perceived oral problems.3,4,16,17,19,20

While existing literature offers valuable insights into oral healthcare utilisation trends, significant gaps remain, particularly regarding long-term analyses of determinants associated with oral healthcare utilisation. Most studies in Malaysia are cross-sectional, limiting the ability to observe changes over time.3,4 This lack of studies examining temporal trends restricts our understanding of how predisposing, enabling, and need factors evolve and impact utilisation patterns over extended periods. Analysing these trends over time would provide a more nuanced understanding, enabling more effective, evidence-based interventions to improve oral health outcomes. Hence, this study aims to address these gaps by exploring factors associated with public oral healthcare utilisation among the adult population in Malaysia from 1992 to 2019 by incorporating key predisposing, enabling, and need factors.

Methods

Study design and participants, data source and description

This study employed a multiple linear regression (MLR) framework applied to time-series data to examine factors associated with public oral healthcare utilisation among the adult population in Malaysia from 1992 to 2019. Adults were defined as individuals aged 18 to 59 years21 and older adults as those aged ≥60 years, in accordance with the Ministry of Health (MOH) Malaysia’s operational reporting framework.8 The MOH, through the Oral Health Programme (OHP), compiles and reports dental service data under seven standardised population categories which are infants and children (0-4 years), preschool children (5-6 years), school children (7-17 years), special needs population, antenatal mothers, adults (18-59 years), and older adults (≥60 years).8 These categories are consistently applied across all public dental clinics for national planning, monitoring, and annual reporting.

The unit of analysis was the calendar year, with each observation representing a single year, resulting in 28 annual data points. Public oral healthcare utilisation was measured as the proportion of new patients receiving public dental services within a given year.8 In accordance with the Ministry of Health Malaysia’s definition, it was calculated as:

This indicator reflects the annual proportion of the population utilising public dental services and is reported separately for adults (18-59 years) and older adults (≥60 years) in accordance with the Ministry’s national health information framework.8 These operational definitions remained consistent across all study years, ensuring comparability and reliability of trend analysis.

To assess whether internal ageing within the adult group could have affected utilisation trends, population data from the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) for individuals aged 15 to 59 years during 1992 to 2019 were analysed, as the data were reported in fixed five-year age bands.13 The inclusion of those aged 15 to 17 years was therefore necessary to align with DOSM’s reporting structure and is unlikely to materially influence the findings, given the small proportion of this subgroup relative to the total adult population. The proportion of older adults aged 45 to 59 years increased modestly from 17.3% in 1992 to 22.5% in 2019, compared to a corresponding decline among those aged 15 to 44 years, reflecting a 5.2 percentage point rise over 28 years. Detailed information on the age composition of the adult population is provided as supporting information.

Secondary, aggregated annual data were collected from four main sources: the Health Information Management System (HIMS), the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), the Malaysian Dental Council (MDC), and the United Nations Food Balance Sheets (FBS).8,13,22, 23, 24 The dependent variable was the annual prevalence of adults receiving primary oral healthcare services in public facilities. Independent variables were guided by Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use and categorised into predisposing, enabling, and need factors.

The predisposing factor included the literacy rate, sugar consumption, and water fluoridation coverage. Enabling factors comprised employment ratio, gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, population per dental unit, population per capita dentist, median household income, unemployment rate, relative poverty incidence (RPI), and population using the internet. The need factor, caries-free prevalence among 16-year-olds, was included as a proxy indicator of the historical effectiveness of national preventive dental programs, recognising its potential indirect impact on adult oral health needs.

Variable definitions and data sources were as follows: HIMS provided data on public oral healthcare utilisation, literacy rate, caries-free prevalence, water fluoridation coverage, and population per public dental unit8; the MDC contributed data on population per registered dentist22; DOSM supplied information on median household income, GDP per capita, RPI, employment ratio, unemployment rate, and internet usage13; and FBS captured sugar and sweetener consumption in kilocalories per capita per day.23

All datasets were compiled and verified by the respective agencies. No record linkage was performed, and all data were de-identified and at an aggregate-level. Missing values were imputed using linear interpolation, which preserves temporal structure and is considered appropriate when missing data are below 60%.25,26 Variables with imputed values included median household income (57.14%), RPI (53.57%), caries-free prevalence (7.14%), fluoridation coverage (21.43%), population per registered dentist (17.86%), and population per public dental unit (17.86%). Although the data were obtained from multiple national agencies with distinct mandates, all indicators share the same national population base and were reported using standardised definitions within each organisation.8,13,22,23 To address potential inconsistencies across data sources, only variables that were continuously available and consistently defined from 1992 to 2019 were included. A harmonisation protocol was applied to standardise definitions, denominators, and time frames across all data sources to ensure comparability.27 Indicators were aligned by calendar year and standardised as per-capita or percentage values using population denominators from the Department of Statistics Malaysia. Economic variables were taken in constant prices to ensure temporal comparability. Visual inspection of trends and reference to agency documentation were used to confirm logical continuity and identify any possible reporting anomalies. These steps reduced the likelihood of measurement bias when integrating datasets collected through different methodologies.27 To address skewness and stabilise variance, a logarithmic transformation was applied to all continuous variables.28 Outliers were identified through visual inspection of residual plots within EViews 12 and corrected as necessary, ensuring that identified anomalies reflected genuine data irregularities rather than measurement errors.29,30

As a time series study, formal sample size calculation was not applicable. However, with four estimated model parameters (intercept and three coefficients), a minimum of five data points was sufficient.31,32 The 28 time points available met the requirement for robust estimation.

Statistical analysis

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) was first conducted to assess the quality and distribution of the data, including identification of missing values, assessment of variable distributions, exploring correlations and distributions, and detection of outliers.30,34 Variables exhibiting non-normality or variance instability were log-transformed to improve linearity and homoscedasticity.35, 36, 37, 38, 39

Diagnostic checks were conducted on all time-series regression models to confirm that model assumptions were satisfied, given the time-ordered nature of the data. To account for the time-series structure, the stationarity of each variable was assessed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and Phillips-Perron (PP) tests.34,40,41 Non-stationary variables were differenced until stationarity was achieved (P < .05). Multicollinearity was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and variables with VIF values above 10 were excluded or combined to avoid instability in coefficient estimation.42,43

Variable selection was performed using the General-to-Specific (GETS) procedure via the VARSEL method in EViews 12, which iteratively removes statistically insignificant variables (P > .25) from an initial theory-based model.44, 45, 46 This approach ensured that statistical selection did not override theoretical relevance. The variables considered included literacy rate, sugar consumption, median household income, GDP per capita, population per dentist, population per public dental unit, employment ratio, unemployment rate, internet access, caries-free prevalence among 16-year-olds, water fluoridation coverage, and RPI.

Given that the data were time-indexed and transformed to achieve stationarity, Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) was chosen as the final estimation technique for the multivariate time series analysis due to its simplicity and adherence to the parsimony principle for model selection.47 MLR is appropriate when assumptions are met, including linearity, normality, independence, and homoscedasticity.31,48 These assumptions were validated post-estimation using residual diagnostics.49,50 When assumptions were violated, transformations and robust standard errors were considered.

This rigorous analytical approach ensured that identified determinants reliably explained the variation and trends in adult public oral healthcare utilisation in Malaysia over the 28-year period. All statistical analyses were performed using EViews 12 (IHS Markit Ltd.), including data differencing, log transformations, stationarity testing, diagnostic checks for autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity, as well as multiple linear regression (MLR) modelling with robust standard errors where appropriate.

Discussion

This study examined trends in public oral healthcare utilisation among Malaysian adults and identified the significant predisposing, enabling, and need factors influencing their use of public dental services. Spanning nearly three decades, the analysis highlights periods of both growth and stagnation in utilisation. By examining key sociodemographic and structural determinants, the study provides a comprehensive understanding of what drives adults to engage with public oral healthcare. These findings can inform more equitable, targeted policies and interventions to improve access, especially for underserved populations.

Public oral healthcare utilisation among adults in Malaysia

Between 1992 and 2019, public oral healthcare utilisation among Malaysian adults demonstrated four phases: an initial decline (1992-1997), stabilisation (1997-2002), gradual growth (2002-2012), and a sharp increase (2012-2019). These shifts reflect the interplay of evolving national oral health policies, service delivery infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and targeted interventions.

The decline from 6.90% to 5.38% between 1992 and 1997 (a 22.03% reduction) likely stemmed from limited access, low public oral health awareness, and economic constraints. According to the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 1996, 57.5% of individuals who were ill or injured did not seek care, with 62.0% perceiving care as unnecessary, suggesting a generally low level of healthcare-seeking behaviour during this period, which likely extended to dental services.51 While early surveys, such as the Dental Epidemiological Survey of Adults (1974) and the Oral Mucosal Lesion Survey (1993-1994) provided foundational data on adult oral health, they were not effectively translated into policy.52 Comprehensive strategies, such as the National Oral Health Plan for Malaysia 2011 to 2020, had yet to be introduced, and organised efforts to prioritise adult oral health were minimal.53

During the stabilisation phase from 1997 to 2002, adult public oral healthcare utilisation mainly unchanged, slightly dropping from 5.38% to 4.94% (an 8.17% decrease). This stagnation likely reflected limited resources, the lack of focused adult-oriented policy efforts, and prevailing economic challenges. While significant progress was made in children’s oral health such as the National Oral Health Survey for Schoolchildren (NOHSS 1997) and the School-Based Fissure Sealant Programme in 1999, the adult population remained underserved.52,54 The National Oral Health Survey of Adults (NOHSA) 2000 provided important baseline data, but strategic efforts to improve adult utilisation were still nascent.55 The lowest adult utilisation rate recorded in 2002 may be a marker of this strategic shortfall.

From 2002 to 2012, adult public oral healthcare utilisation in Malaysia rose from 4.94% to 7.30%, a 47.77% rise. A temporary dip during the 2007 to 2009 global financial crisis was likely due to indirect costs such as transportation, lost income due to time off work, and perceived non-urgency of dental care, but utilisation rebounded with strategic reforms.4,56,57 The NOHSA 2010 provided refreshed insights into adult oral health needs,58 while the National Oral Health Plan for Malaysia 2011 to 2020 introduced structured objectives to improve access and service quality.53 System-level improvements such as the integration of the Oral Health Clinical Information System (OHCIS) with the Human Resource Management Information System (HRMIS), the use of electronic guarantee letters (e-GL), online appointment, SMS reminders, and performance-based key performance indicators (KPIs) in 2012, enhancing service efficiency and user experience.52,53 Moreover, expanding services to Urban and Rural Transformation Centres (UTCs and RTCs) improved accessibility for underserved populations.52

Between 2012 and 2019, adult public oral healthcare in Malaysia utilisation rose sharply from 7.30% to 10.10%, representing a 38.36% rise. This reflects the sustained investment in infrastructure, digital health integration, and the intensification of health promotion efforts. Key developments included mobile dental units under the 12th Malaysia Plan, the 2014 primary care endodontic guidelines, and a series of innovative outreach programmes and promotional campaigns aimed at increasing utilisation among specific groups such as Ikon Gigi (2016), Oral Health and Religious Programme (KOA, 2017), Kembara Sihat 1Malaysia (2017), Transformation with 1Smile Together (TW1ST 2017), and Program Pergigian Mesra Rakyat (2018) sought to engage communities through culturally tailored, demographically targeted strategies.52,59 The 2019 National Oral Health Promotion Week institutionalised education efforts, while digital tools like MyGusi and MyOrtho in 2018 expanded geriatric and orthodontic care.52 Moreover, the continued operation and transformation of UTCs and RTCs into community health clinics after 2018 underscores the critical role of health and oral healthcare infrastructure in national development.52 Unlike many other initiatives from the previous administration that were discontinued following a change in government, these facilities were not abolished but instead rebranded and integrated into the public healthcare system, demonstrating their enduring relevance and alignment with long-term goals for integrated, people-centred care.

Despite progress, need-based utilisation patterns persist, reflecting ongoing challenges. NHMS 2019 found that most adults did not seeking dental care due to perceived lack of necessity, indicating low preventive care-seeking behaviour among adults.4 This underscores the importance of sustained efforts in health literacy, public engagement, and behavioural change communication.60,61 While increasing trends suggest positive infrastructural and policy impacts, the 2002 drop highlights the sensitivity of public dental service utilisation to economic downturns and systemic limitations.3,57 These findings collectively emphasise the need for ongoing monitoring, adaptive policy development, and proactive strategies to sustain and further enhance adult utilisation. Future efforts should aim to strengthen equity in access, address financial and non-financial barriers, and support a population-wide transition toward preventive dental care behaviours.

Factors associated with public oral healthcare utilisation among adults in Malaysia

This study identified four enabling and need-based factors as significant determinants of adult public oral healthcare utilisation in Malaysia that shape healthcare-seeking behaviour and highlight persistent structural inequities in access and utilisation.

Although the current study defined adults as individuals aged 18 to 59 years and older adults as those 60 years and over, consistent with national reporting categories, it is acknowledged that demographic ageing within the adult group could have influenced utilisation patterns over time.62 As older adults tend to use dental services more frequently,62 a gradual ageing shift within this group may partially account for observed upward or stabilising trends. However, the analysis of population composition indicated only a modest ageing shift, suggesting that internal ageing within the 18 to 59-year group was limited and unlikely to have materially biased the observed trends in public oral healthcare utilisation. Therefore, the observed utilisation trends likely reflect broader behavioural and systemic influences rather than compositional ageing effects. Future research incorporating finer age disaggregation or age–period–cohort modelling would provide deeper insight into these dynamics.

Median household income was inversely associated with adult public oral healthcare utilisation. As financial capacity increases, individuals shift toward private providers offering shorter waiting times, greater convenience, and services like cosmetic dentistry not typically available in the public sector.56,63, 64, 65, 66 This trend mirrors patterns seen in other middle-income countries, such as Brazil, South Africa, Ghana and Thailand, where rising incomes correlate with greater use of the private sector due to perceived superior quality, urgency of care and broader services.67, 68, 69, 70, 71 Similar associations between socioeconomic status and private dental expenditure have been reported in Iran, further confirming that affordability and perceived quality consistently shape utilisation patterns in mixed public-private systems across middle-income settings.72 In China, which practices a dual healthcare system, although private facilities make up nearly half the system, limited insurance coverage and a stronger public preference lead to underutilisation, highlighting the importance of financial protection in promoting fair access which are key priorities for middle-income countries like Malaysia.73,74 Similar trends are also evident in high income countries such as Finland, the United States, Sweden, and the United Kingdom, where wealthier individuals favour private dental service.75, 76, 77, 78 Higher income often brings better education and health literacy, further influencing health care-seeking behavior.79,80 Even in low-income settings like urban Nepal, wealthier individuals turn to private providers despite free public services, due to long wait times and inconsistent quality.81 In contrast, China’s tax-funded public system remains the primary option for lower-income groups.73,74

Public-private partnership (PPP) models offer a promising solution.82 Subsidising private dental care for low- and middle-income households could reduce strain on public facilities while promoting more equitable service delivery. International evidence suggests that systems financed through taxation or social insurance achieve more equitable utilisation than systems dominated by private provision.74,76,77 Comparable system-level challenges have been reported in other countries with constrained public health resources. In Sudan, limited public funding, uneven workforce distribution, and inadequate integration between primary and specialist services contribute to restricted oral healthcare access.83 Although Malaysia has a stronger public oral healthcare infrastructure, both contexts highlight how system organisation and governance critically determine utilisation patterns and equity in service delivery. In dual healthcare systems like Malaysia, comprehensive strategies are essential where policies should aim to reduce financial barriers, improve service quality, and introduce risk-sharing mechanisms. China’s gradual expansion of social insurance shows how such reforms can improve equity across sectors.74 To reduce income-related disparities in healthcare use, Malaysia must balance public investment, public-private partnerships, and financing reforms, thus ensuring access is based on need, not income.

Relative poverty incidence was also negatively associated with public oral healthcare utilisation among Malaysian adults which was an unexpected finding, as greater poverty typically implies higher need. In Australia, for example, lower socioeconomic individuals experience a higher dental caries burden due to individual risk factors and broader structural determinants.84 However, global evidence shows that poverty does not always translate to increased service use. In the United States, for example, low-income populations underutilise dental services despite public insurance, due to indirect costs, limited provider access, and coverage gaps.85,86 Similar patterns are observed in Mexico, where systemic issues such as low government prioritisation of dental health, dentist shortages, and uneven service distribution constrain access.86,87 Financial hardship also often deters care-seeking through costs like transportation and lost wages, while low health literacy and daily survival needs further reduce preventive dental visits.88, 89, 90

In Malaysia, access barriers are compounded by geographical and social disparities. Unequal distribution of dental clinics, particularly in rural and underserved areas, limits access for low-income individuals.3,4,56 Long wait times, overcrowding, and perceptions of low service quality in public facilities may deter those with limited flexibility or unstable employment from seeking care.91,92 To address these challenges, countries like Thailand and Japan have implemented inclusive schemes such as mobile clinics and broad insurance coverage for dental care to reduce financial and geographic barriers.66,93 Hong Kong’s health vouchers and Indonesia’s Jaminan Kesihatan Nasional (JKN) also aim to expand access, though challenges remain.94,95 These examples show that combining financial protection with outreach is key to equitable care. Expanding mobile dental services and integrating oral health with social services for Malaysia could help close utilisation gaps among marginalised groups.96 Importantly, PPP models should be tailored to extend care options to areas with high poverty where public service delivery is limited.82 This finding reinforces the idea that while poverty increases the need for oral healthcare, it often reduces the capacity to seek care, underscoring the urgency of addressing both economic and structural determinants of access.

Unexpectedly, the unemployment rate was negatively correlated with public oral healthcare utilisation. While unemployment often leads to decreased income and a presumed increased dependence on public healthcare,97,98 this study highlights more complex access barriers among the unemployed in Malaysia. Unable individuals may deprioritise oral healthcare, who face financial hardship, opportunity costs, and transportation barriers.99,100 Additionally, preventive dental care is often perceived as non-essential during times of financial instability.4,88,101 Employment status also influences access via institutional structures. Government employees benefit from paid time off and subsidised care under systems such as the electronic guarantee letter (eGL), privileges unavailable to unemployed individuals or those in informal work.102,103 This disparity reinforces structural inequities tied to employment.

International evidence supports these findings. In the United States, higher unemployment was linked to fewer preventive dental visits, even among those with insurance, highlighting that economic hardship can deter care-seeking regardless of coverage.104 In Australia, unemployed individuals were nearly three times more likely to skip needed dental treatment due to cost.105 These findings highlight that even in high-income countries with relatively robust health systems, unemployment can swiftly compromise access to oral healthcare. Even in Japan, where the unemployment rate is low and universal coverage exists,106 public assistance recipients had lower dental attendance, suggesting that non-financial barriers like stigma or low health literacy also play a role.107 Interestingly, in Bangkok, unemployed individuals used dental services more than private-sector employees under the Social Security Scheme, possibly due to better awareness of their entitlements.108 These examples suggest that while unemployment may increase the need for public healthcare, actual utilisation is heavily influenced by system design, service availability, and social support infrastructure.

In Malaysia, although outreach initiatives such as People’s Housing Project and Adopted Village aim to improve access for vulnerable groups, limitations in scheduling, resource allocation, and healthcare worker availability restrict their reach, particularly on weekends.109, 110, 111, 112, 113 Mobile dental units and subsidised transportation systems could help. However, current models, such as free public buses in Selangor, suffer from irregular service and misaligned routing, reducing their utility for dental care access.114, 115, 116 Improving route planning, frequency, and coordination with clinic hours could improve the feasibility of these solutions.117, 118, 119 The negative association between unemployment and utilisation signals a need for responsive policies beyond affordability to address temporal, spatial, and institutional barriers.

Internet usage in Malaysia is positively associated with the prevalence of adult public oral healthcare utilisation. Working-age adults often utilise the internet to access information about public dental services, locate nearby clinics, and schedule appointments. Digital campaigns such as Remember Birthday, Remember Dentist encourage annual dental visits by leveraging preventive care messages.52 Public dental services are seen as a cost-effective alternative to private clinics, particularly for basic treatments. The affordability of public services, combined with digital tools like online appointment systems and clinic locators, improves access and enhances utilisation.63

In the United Kingdom, digital platforms like the National Health Service (NHS) website and mobile applications have helped improve dental service uptake by increasing awareness and simplifying booking.120 Similarly, a study in South Korea’s Gangwon-do highlighted how digital oral health literacy, influenced by socioeconomic status, age, and education, affects access and utilisation, with older adults and lower income individuals struggling to navigate online resources.121 Malaysia’s MySejahtera app, now featuring the MyVas dental appointment system, along with clinic locators and reminders, offers similar potential to improve public oral healthcare access.122,123 Incorporating campaigns into the MySejahtera app could further encourage regular check-ups through personalised reminders.124

However, rural and low-income populations often face digital barriers due to poor internet access or digital literacy.125 Bridging this divide requires affordable smartphones, low-band width applications, community-based networks, rural connectivity expansion, and digital literacy programs.126,127 Global examples from Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, and India show that community-driven digital networks can improve healthcare access and local development.126 Implementing similar approaches in Malaysia could enhance digital engagement with public oral healthcare services and reduce disparities in utilisation.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Its large sample size and longitudinal design enhance reliability and generalisability. It applies Andersen’s Behavioral Model by incorporating predisposing, enabling, and need factors, offering a comprehensive view of public oral healthcare utilisation. The methodology is robust, using multiple linear regression with differenced and log-transformed variables to address non-stationarity, along with diagnostic tests for autocorrelation, homoscedasticity, and normality.128, 129, 130 Analysing variables like income, unemployment, and internet access provides policy-relevant insights. The study highlights the importance of enabling and need factors, such as internet access and relative poverty, in shaping service use and informing targeted interventions.

This study has several limitations. First, post-2019 data were excluded due to the unprecedented disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, which introduced anomalies and atypical fluctuations in service utilisation.131 While this preserves the integrity of trend analyses, it limits insights into pandemic and post-pandemic patterns. Future research should apply statistical methods that effectively handle structural breaks and outliers, including such periods.132 Second, this study relies on secondary data, which may be subject to measurement errors, inconsistencies, or changes in data collection methods over time.133 Although data were triangulated from reliable national sources, such limitations may affect data accuracy. Additionally, omitted variable bias may exist, as factors like regional disparities, policy changes, or awareness campaigns were not included.134 The exclusion of private healthcare data further narrows the scope, potentially overlooking usage patterns among higher-income groups. In addition, the study is ecological in nature and based on aggregated population-level data. As such, the associations observed reflect relationships at the national level and cannot be directly inferred to individuals, introducing the possibility of ecological fallacy. This limitation does not undermine the study’s purpose, which is to understand system-level and contextual determinants of public oral healthcare utilisation. The consistent use of standardised, validated indicators over a long time span enhances the reliability of temporal patterns observed, but interpretations should remain within the population-level scope of inference. Lastly, multiple linear regression identifies associations but cannot establish causality.135 Future studies could adopt causal inference methods, such as natural experiments or randomised controlled trials, to better understand the drivers of public oral healthcare utilisation.

Conclusion

This study identified key trends and associated factors of adult public oral healthcare utilisation in Malaysia, highlighting the roles of enabling factors such as median household income, relative poverty, unemployment, and internet usage. The findings emphasise the need for targeted strategies that address socioeconomic barriers and enhance digital access. Policy efforts should focus on reducing poverty and unemployment, expanding outreach programs, leveraging digital platforms for health promotion, and fostering public-private partnerships. Inclusive digital health interventions are essential to ensure equitable access, supporting national goals for improved oral health outcomes.
