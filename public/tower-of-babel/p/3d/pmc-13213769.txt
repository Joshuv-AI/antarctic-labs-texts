A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis on the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment in middle-aged and older adults from Latin America
Authors: Angel Gabriel Garrido-Dzib, Saliha Karina Hernández-Chávez, Héctor Rubio-Zapata, Gonzalo Jorquera, Ana Ligia Gutiérrez-Solis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesReduction in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function, such as sarcopenia, have been associated with cognitive impairment. In Latin America (LATAM), evidence remains scarce, a critical gap given the region rapid demographic aging. Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment among middle-aged and older adults in LATAM countries.DesignA systematic review and meta-analysis.SettingA systematic literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, LILACS, SciELO and sources of grey literature. The association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment (odds ratio) was pooled in a meta-analysis using a random-effects model. The quality of the methodology used in each study was evaluated using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tools.ParticipantsMiddle-aged and older adults from cross-sectional and longitudinal studies conducted in Latin American populations.MeasuresThe primary outcome was the pooled association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment.ResultsA total of 8 studies and 13,395 community-dwelling adults were included. The meta-analysis showed that sarcopenia was associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment (OR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.68–3.15). However, the magnitude of this association varied according to definition as probable cognitive impairment (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.85–3.53, P < 0.001) and diagnosis criteria of sarcopenia by EWGSOP2 (OR = 2.96; 95% CI: 2.15–4.08, P < 0.001).ConclusionIn LATAM, sarcopenia doubles the odds of cognitive impairment in adults. Future research must elucidate the inflammatory, oxidative, and mitochondrial pathways linking muscle and brain health.

Introduction

Latin America (LATAM) is a region comprising the countries and territories of Mexico, Central America, South America, and some Caribbean countries. In this region, the proportion of older adults has been steadily increasing due to declining fertility and mortality rates [1]. In 2022, LATAM reported 88.6 million older adults (≥60 years), and this number is projected to nearly triple to 220 million within the next 40 years, which is comparable to the older population of Europe [2].

Population aging, defined as the increase in the number and proportion of older adults, is one of the main demographic phenomena in Latin America and worldwide [1]. The chronic health conditions pose a challenge to current health systems, as these systems have not adapted to demographic changes and specific health needs of older adults [1]. Among these are neuromuscular impairments resulting from cellular senescence and physical inactivity, which can lead to a decline in the ability to perform activities of daily living [3]. Sarcopenia, a progressive and generalized reduction in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function, is one of the most common muscle diseases according to the consensus of the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) [4]. These structural and functional changes in muscle fibers associated with sarcopenia decrease the quality of life of this population due to increased probability of falls, fractures, low physical performance, and neurological disorders [5,6]. Petermann-Rocha and colleagues reported that the prevalence of sarcopenia in South America was 21%, which is comparable to regions with higher populations such as Asia (21%) and even higher than reported in North America (14%) [7,8].

Cognitive impairment represents the central clinical manifestation of neurocognitive disorders. In this continuum, mild neurocognitive disorder, or mild cognitive impairment (MCI), corresponds to an intermediate clinical state in which there is objective evidence of moderate cognitive decline (e.g., in memory, learning, or language), without substantial impairment of functional or social autonomy. A formal MCI diagnosis requires clinical concern, objective neuropsychological deficits, preserved functional independence, and informant input, according to international criteria [8,9]. Conversely, dementia (major neurocognitive disorder) entails severe cognitive impairment that compromises the individual's independence in everyday activities [8]. Age is the main risk factor for MCI, although it is important to note that this condition may also manifest at earlier stages of life (from approximately 50 years onwards) [9]. The decline in skeletal muscle quantity and quality leads to reduced physical activity and, consequently, may contribute to cognitive decline as it is associated with decreased cerebral blood flow, alterations in the secretion of neurogenic hormones (e.g., myokines), increased accumulation of neurotoxic proteins, and insulin resistance [10].

MCI may be considered an early manifestation of dementia; therefore, these individuals have been reported to be at higher risk of progression compared with cognitively healthy adults [9]. The detection of modifiable risk factors that may contribute to the early onset of neurocognitive disorders is essential for the development of intervention and preventive strategies, particularly in LATAM, a region with a rapid increase in the prevalence of MCI (14.95%) compared to high-income countries [11]. Recent studies conducted in Asia and Europe have reported a significant association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. In LATAM, this association has also been observed (Odds Ratio [OR] = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.01–2.42; P = 0.043); however, this evidence is based on a limited number of studies [5,12]. Considering the paucity of regional evidence, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment among middle-aged and older adults in LATAM countries.

Methods

Study selection, search strategy, and selection criteria

A systematic and exhaustive search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, LILACS, and SciELO by two independent reviewers (AGGD and SKHC), with no restrictions on publication date. The search covered the period from April to June 2025. Grey literature was also screened through Google Scholar and the “Red de Repositorios Latinoamericanos”. The search strategy was developed using the PEO (Population, Exposure, Outcome) framework and included both controlled vocabularies as Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. In the case of LILACS and the “Red de Repositorios Latinoamericanos”, equivalent Spanish search terms were applied, which is described in detail in Table S1 and Table S2.

The eligibility criteria for the studies were as follows: (1) cross-sectional and longitudinal studies in which the main outcomes of interest were the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. (2) middle-aged (50 years) and older adults. (3) studies including populations residing in LATAM countries. (4) studies that identification sarcopenia, probable sarcopenia or risk of sarcopenia according to EWGSOP or EWGSOP2 criteria, as exposure of interest. (5) studies that identified MCI using standardized diagnostic criteria, or that identified cognitive impairment through validated cognitive screening tests, were considered for the outcome of interest.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) Studies using the same or overlapping sample; (2) posters, commentaries or reviews; (3) studies with an unclear diagnosis of sarcopenia or cognitive impairment; (4) studies that measured sarcopenia or cognitive impairment as continuous variables; (5) studies including participants with a diagnosis of dementia.

First, the studies were identified, and duplicates were subsequently excluded. Then, two authors (AGGD and SKHC) screened the titles and abstracts; the remaining articles that were not eliminated were assessed in full text according to the eligibility criteria (AGGD, ALGS, GJ). The current systematic review and meta-analysis followed the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis 2020 statement (PRISMA) and was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under reference CRD420251024834 [13].

Data extraction and research quality assessment

Data extraction from the eligible studies were performed independently by two investigators (AGGD, SKHC). Data regarding the country, name of the first author, study design, sample size, proportion of females, study population, the criteria of sarcopenia diagnosis and cognitive function tests were integrated. Participants in studies that exclusively administered cognitive screening tests were classified as having "probable cognitive impairment" when they scored below the established cutoff threshold, given that no standardized diagnostic criteria for MCI or dementia were applied.

The quality of the methodology used in each study was evaluated by three researchers (AGGD, ALGS, and GJ) using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies and the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies, as applicable. Studies scoring above 70% were at low risk of bias in their design [14].

Statistical analysis

A primary meta-analysis was conducted including all eligible studies, regardless of the diagnostic approach used for cognitive impairment or sarcopenia. The odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined from the number of events (cognitive impairment) in exposed (sarcopenia) and unexposed (no sarcopenia) groups. A random-effects meta-analysis with the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment was conducted to pool the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment and the results are illustrated in a forest plot.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were pre-specified according to cognitive outcome definition (formally diagnosed MCI vs. probable cognitive impairment); and sarcopenia diagnostic criteria (EWGSOP vs. EWGSOP2). These analyses aimed to determine whether the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment varies according to the diagnostic rigor of each condition. As a secondary analysis, adjusted odds ratios (AOR) reported by the individual studies were incorporated when available. This analysis specifically included estimates adjusted for confounding factors reported in the original studies, such as age, sex, education, income, civil status, physical activity, smoking, alcohol drinking and comorbidities.

Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated by I2 index, tau2 (τ2) index and prediction intervals. The symmetry of the primary funnel plot was evaluated visually and combined with Egger’s test to evaluate publication bias [15]. Statistical analyses were undertaken using the software RStudio version 4.5.1 and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Literature search

In total, 116 articles were identified. Additionally, 2 articles were found in the gray literature. Altogether, 29 articles were eliminated due to duplications in the search, and 65 manuscripts were removed by title and abstract before screening. Therefore, 24 articles were reviewed in full text, and 16 of them were subsequently excluded, mainly because they did not clearly diagnose sarcopenia or MCI, assessed these conditions as continuous variables, or were posters, commentaries, or review articles. Geographical distribution and characteristics of included studies.

Five of the eight included studies selected their samples from nationally representative surveys using probabilistic sampling methods (Fig. 2) [16,[18], [19], [20],22]. The SAGE Mexican cohort was analyzed in two studies: Jacob and colleagues recruited participants from wave 1 (cross-sectional), whereas Salinas-Rodríguez and colleagues derived their sample through follow-up of participants from waves 1, 2, and 3, representing the only longitudinal study included in this meta-analysis [18,21]. In addition, we estimated the total number of events based on the proportions reported within subgroups (e.g., nutritional status or sex) of studies of O'Donovan and colleagues., and da Silva and colleagues [16,22].

Table 1 shows that four studies reported sarcopenia confirmed diagnosis by EWGSOP [16,18,20,21]. Three studies indicated probable sarcopenia and one study evaluated risk of sarcopenia, based on the updated EWGSOP2 criteria [17,19,22,23]. The presence of probable cognitive impairment was assessed with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (n = 4) [16,17,19,22], the Mini-Cog test (n = 1) [20], and the Pfeiffer questionnaire (n = 1) [23]. Meanwhile, MCI was identified according to the algorithm of the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AAA) (n = 2) [18,21].

Quality assessment

In the evaluation of methodological quality, we observed that most studies had a low risk of bias (n = 7). However, some studies did not meet all quality criteria. The aspect with the lowest compliance were related non-application of strategies to deal with confounding factors (n = 3) [16,20,23]. Vidal-Cuéllar presented a moderate risk of bias in its design due to an unclear definition of sarcopenia, the lack of strategies to deal with potential confounding factors, and the use of non-validated instruments to measure the outcome (Figure S1) [23]. The longitudinal study by Salinas-Rodriguez and colleagues did not describe whether participants were free from the outcomes of interest at baseline. Additionally, there was loss to follow-up, yet the authors did not report any adjustments made to account for this attrition in the sample (Figure S2) [21].

Association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment in middle-aged and older adults from Latin America

Pooled results from primary meta-analysis showed that adults with sarcopenia had higher odds of cognitive impairment compared with those without sarcopenia (OR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.68–3.15, P < 0.001). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 79.8%, τ² = 0.09%), and the 95% prediction interval (1.05–5.02) indicates that the mean group difference could vary substantially depending on the sample (Fig. 3A). Exploratory visual inspection of the funnel plot and Egger’s test did not reveal asymmetry among studies or significant evidence of publication bias (P = 0.547) (Figure S3).Fig. 3Forest plot on association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment in studies included (A) diagnosis of probable cognitive impairment (B) and diagnosis of sarcopenia trough EWGSOP2 (C).Fig. 3

Additionally, the pooled adjusted odds ratio (AOR) was attenuated compared with the unadjusted estimate but remained statistically significant (AOR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.58–2.37, P < 0.001).

A significant positive association was observed among studies using probable cognitive impairment as the outcome (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.85–3.53, P < 0.001), albeit with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 83.4%, τ² = 0.07) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, no significant association was found in studies with formally diagnosed MCI (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 0.30–6.67, p = 0.584; I² = 0%, τ² = 0). Salinas-Rodríguez and colleagues reported a significant longitudinal association between sarcopenia and incident mild cognitive impairment (AOR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.02–2.96, P = 0.04). No other longitudinal studies were available for pooled analysis.

A significant positive association was also observed among studies that defined the exposure as probable sarcopenia and risk of sarcopenia according to the EWGSOP2 criteria (OR = 2.96; 95% CI: 2.15–4.08, P < 0.001), with no evidence of between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0%, τ² = 0.09) (Fig. 3C). In contrast, no significant association was found in studies that diagnosed sarcopenia based on the original EWGSOP criteria (OR = 1.86; 95% CI: 0.95–3.64, P = 0.060; I² = 73.3%, τ² = 0.12).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study specifically evaluating the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment in middle-aged and older adults from LATAM. This meta-analysis indicates that adults with sarcopenia have two times higher odds of presenting cognitive impairment.

Our findings are consistent with recent studies that have quantified the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment [5,12,24]. Notably, our results indicate that adults with sarcopenia in LATAM have a higher likelihood of cognitive impairment (OR = 2.30) compared with other regions. In Asia, a meta-analysis reported an OR of 1.95 (95% CI: 1.75–2.17, P < 0.001), while in Europe, an OR of 1.70 was observed (95% CI: 1.22–2.38, P = 0.002) [5]. Previous studies have suggested an association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment in Latin American and Central American populations (OR = 1.56–1.57), however, this evidence is insufficient to draw firm conclusions, as it is based on a sub-analysis of only two studies [5,12]. Therefore, the present study provides more robust evidence from the LATAM region which has been under-investigated.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 79.8%), reflecting considerable clinical and methodological diversity. Subgroup analysis by cognitive outcome definition revealed that studies using screening-based probable cognitive impairment showed a strong positive association with sarcopenia, whereas those applying formal diagnostic criteria for MCI did not. These findings are consistent with those reported by Amini and colleagues., in a recent meta-analysis, which also evaluated the association between sarcopenia and a broad spectrum of cognitive impairment. The authors found a stronger association between sarcopenia and Alzheimer's disease (OR = 2.97; 95% CI: 2.15–4.08; P = 0.449) compared with the likelihood of presenting MCI (OR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.42–1.76; P = 0.400) [12]. Notably, they also acknowledged that these results may be partially explained by the scarcity of studies applying validated diagnostic criteria for MCI, a limitation that similarly applies to our own subgroup analysis [12]. Future research with larger samples and standardized diagnostic approaches is required to clarify whether the strength of this association truly differs across the cognitive continuum.

Meanwhile, when a subgroup analysis was conducted according to the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, studies using the EWGSOP2 criteria were found to be highly consistent with each other (I² = 0.0%) and showed a stronger association with cognitive impairment (OR = 2.96) compared to those using EGWSOP criteria (I² = 73.3%) [5,25]. Similar findings were reported by Huang and colleagues., in a recent meta-analysis, where the EGWSOP2 subgroup exhibited a stronger association with cognitive impairment (OR = 2.92; 95% CI: 1.62–5.24; P < 0.001) compared to the EGWSOP subgroup (OR = 1.39; 95% CI: 0.90–2.13; P = 0.132) [5]. The absence of a significant association and the considerable heterogeneity observed in studies using the original EWGSOP criteria may be related to the lack of standardized cut-off points and use of methods with limited accessibility to assess muscle mass for the diagnosis of sarcopenia (e.g., dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, bioelectrical impedance analysis, muscle ultrasound). In contrast, the consistency observed among studies applying the EWGSOP2 criteria could be explained by the incorporation of an updated detection and diagnostic algorithm, revised cut-off points, and the prioritization of reduced muscle strength as a key indicator of sarcopenia [4,26]. Previous evidence has shown that muscle strength is significantly associated with cognitive performance, regardless of sex and age. Moreover, grip strength has been recognized as a reliable marker for identifying sarcopenia across different racial and ethnic groups, including Hispanic populations [27,28]. In this context, current evidence supports the use of clear and updated diagnostic criteria that take race and ethnicity into account to enable the early detection of sarcopenia.

The mechanisms by which sarcopenia could trigger cognitive impairment are not precisely known; however, it has been postulated that metabolic alterations, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial and vascular dysfunction may alter signaling in the muscle-brain axis [29].

Sarcopenia often results in limited mobility and physical inactivity. In mouse models, it has been observed that skeletal muscle atrophy due to immobilization induces an increased secretion of myokines into the bloodstream. For example, hemopexin exerts detrimental effects on the hippocampus region, leading to memory loss [30]. In contrast, aerobic exercise increases peripheral levels of other myokines, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), irisin, and neurturin, which promote synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, learning, and memory [29,31]. Moreover, the reduction in myosin synthesis promotes a sustained increase in proinflammatory mediators (low-grade chronic inflammation) and alterations in glucose metabolism (insulin resistance), leading to muscle atrophy with subsequent effects on brain morphology. This has been reported in a study of 1,284 Korean participants (58 ± 6.0 years) with a 4-year follow-up, in which a significant reduction in gray matter volume (particularly in the parietal lobe) was observed among subjects with low muscle strength and mass, potentially explaining cognitive decline [32].

Muscle atrophy is also characterized by impaired mitochondrial function, leading to an imbalance in the clearance of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn exacerbates damage to myocytes and potentially to cells of the central nervous system [33]. In this context, a cohort study indirectly assessed skeletal muscle mitochondrial function using the phosphocreatine recovery rate constant (kPCr) and biomarkers of neurodegeneration. The study found that greater mitochondrial function (↑kPCr) was significantly correlated with lower concentrations of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and phosphorylated tau181 (pTau181) (r = −0.351; r = −0.310; r = −0.215, respectively) in specific brain regions. Overall, higher kPCr was associated with a lower risk of cognitive impairment and dementia (HR = 0.483, 95% CI: 0.240–0.972, P = 0.042) [34].

Meanwhile, Jo and colleagues., suggest that insulin resistance and low-grade chronic inflammation associated with sarcopenia may contribute to atherosclerotic plaque formation and vascular endothelial dysfunction. These alterations reduce the supply of oxygen and essential nutrients to brain tissue due to limited cerebral blood flow. It has also been suggested that sarcopenia may be linked with vascular cognitive impairment, although the evidence remains inconclusive [35,36].

Clinical and research implications

In LATAM, the prevalence of non-communicable diseases and unhealthy lifestyles has increased in recent years, showing patterns like those observed in high-income countries. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the region is characterized by a high prevalence of elevated fasting blood glucose (10%), overweight (43.2%), smoking (41.3%), and insufficient physical activity (27.3%) [37]. This health profile has contributed to a growing prevalence of both sarcopenia and cognitive impairment and, consequently, to a substantial economic burden on the healthcare system driven by years lived with functional limitations and high service utilization across all levels of care [38]. Evidence indicates that older adults with sarcopenia experience more years lived with disability compared to those without sarcopenia (5.8 years vs. 2.0 years, respectively) [38]. Likewise, cognitive impairment has been significantly associated with a progressive increase in disability over a 12-year follow-up period, regardless of age, sex, and other factors [39]. In Mexico, the annual cost of caring for older adults with disability and functional dependence has been estimated at $2.43 billion dollars. These costs are driven by loss of income among working-age caregivers, caregiver expenses, hospitalizations, nursing home care, lost educational opportunities, and retired individuals assuming caregiving roles [40]. Latin American population with disabilities (functional, visual, hearing, psychosocial, or cognitive limitations) are known to use primary care, outpatient care, or hospitalization services more frequently than those without disabilities. This generates an unsustainable financial burden for both healthcare systems and households, particularly in a regional context where family incomes are already limited [41].

The promotion of healthy lifestyles is included in WHO guidelines as a key therapeutic and preventive strategy to address geriatric syndromes such as cognitive impairment and sarcopenia [42]. Emerging evidence suggests that lifestyle factors modulate the muscle–brain axis at multiple levels, primarily by targeting chronic low‑grade inflammation and oxidative stress [29]. Regular physical activity, particularly moderate aerobic and resistance exercise, reduces pro‑inflammatory cytokines, enhances antioxidant enzyme activity (e.g., superoxide dismutase), and improves mitochondrial efficiency [29,31]. Concurrently, healthy dietary patterns rich in fresh fruits, vegetables, and nuts provide abundant bioactive compounds such as carotenoids, polyphenols, and antioxidant vitamins. These compounds directly neutralize ROS and modulate key inflammatory pathways, including the inhibition of NF‑κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [43,44]. In addition, moderate consumption of red wine may contribute to anti‑inflammatory and antioxidant defenses through the capacity of its polyphenols to scavenge free radicals and modulate cellular stress responses [45].

Strengths and limitations

Our study provides original evidence not previously reported, based on studies with good methodological quality. Moreover, our meta-analysis indicates a significant association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment, even after adjusting to a more conservative and robust statistical method such as the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment [15].

However, certain limitations are acknowledged. First, this topic remains underexplored in the LATAM region compared with Europe and Asia, leading to a small number of predominantly cross-sectional studies with limited representativeness of the regional population. The limited number of studies restricts the reliability of both Egger’s test and the funnel plot in assessing potential publication bias. The wide prediction interval (1.05–5.02) indicates that the true effect in future research could range from null to substantially higher. Additionally, two studies originated from the same Mexican SAGE cohort, introducing potential participant overlap. Nevertheless, given the scarcity of evidence in LATAM, we included all available studies. Therefore, the results of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution and further high-quality studies in the region are warranted to strengthen the current evidence base [46].

Second, given the cross-sectional design of the studies included in this meta-analysis, we cannot establish the causal or temporal direction of the observed association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. Reverse causality remains a plausible alternative explanation [12,17]. Mechanisms supporting this direction include that cognitive impairment may reduce physical activity and impair nutritional intake due to apraxia or forgetfulness [10]. However, a longitudinal study with a large sample of Asian older adults (n = 2689) found that although sarcopenia and cognitive function were reciprocally linked, the findings suggest a predominantly unidirectional relationship, in which sarcopenia is more likely a cause than a consequence of cognitive impairment [47]. Therefore, cohort studies with long follow-up times are needed to provide insights into whether to better understand the cause‐and‐effect nature of this association.

On other hand, the included studies employed different instruments and cut-off points to assess global cognition (e.g., MMSE, Pfeiffer questionnaire, Mini-Cog), and only two of them applied clinical diagnostic criteria for cognitive impairment (e.g., NIA-AA). This lack of standardization in cognitive assessment likely introduced misclassification bias, which may have led to either an underestimation or overestimation of the true association, thereby limiting the external validity of our findings [9]. Nevertheless, the inclusion of both formally diagnosed MCI and probable cognitive impairment was necessary to allow analysis of the available evidence in a region with limited diagnostic resources [11].

Conclusion

In the LATAM region, middle-aged and older adults with sarcopenia have two times higher odds of presenting cognitive impairment. In the LATAM region, middle-aged and older adults with sarcopenia have significantly higher odds of cognitive impairment. This association was particularly pronounced in studies that defined cognitive status through screening tools as probable cognitive impairment and in those that used the EWGSOP2 criteria for sarcopenia diagnosis. Given the current health landscape in LATAM, the promotion of healthy lifestyles is fundamental as both a therapeutic and preventive strategy to address these conditions. Future efforts should focus on generating robust evidence to elucidate how chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction collectively link muscle and cognitive health.
