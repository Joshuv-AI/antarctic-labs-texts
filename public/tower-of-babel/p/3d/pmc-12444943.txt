Source Attribution and Health Burden of PM2.5 in Mainland Thailand
Authors: Worapop Thongsame, Daven K. Henze, Mary Barth, Gabriele Pfister, Rajesh Kumar, Ronald Macatangay, Sherin Hassan Bran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPM2.5 is a critical air pollutant that significantly impacts human health and the environment. To develop effective air quality management and mitigation strategies, understanding PM2.5 source attribution and associated health risks is essential. This study investigates the source attribution and health burden of PM2.5 focusing on Mainland Thailand (MT), North Thailand (NT), and the Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR), using the WRF‐Chem model and a brute‐force method for source attribution. PM2.5 contributions from biomass burning including both crop and non‐crop burning are quantified, along with contributions from transportation, industry, energy, residential, and other anthropogenic sectors. This study focuses on the haze season (February–April) in 2019. Our research shows that in‐domain foreign country's biomass burning is a major contributor to PM2.5, accounting for 23%–38% of PM2.5 concentrations in MT. In NT, non‐crop burning within MT contributes the most (21%–36%) to PM2.5 levels, while crop burning within MT has a minimal impact (less than 6%). In the BMR, PM2.5 is strongly impacted by sources outside the model domain. Overall, industrial and transportation emissions are the most impactful anthropogenic sources. We further estimate the total health burden, associated with long‐term PM2.5 exposure during the haze season contributes to 46% of this PM2.5 health burden in MT in 2019, 66% in NT, and 37% in the BMR. These findings suggest that reducing biomass burning within MT and from in‐domain foreign countries during February–April could reduce the annual health burden in MT by up to 20%.

Introduction

Ambient fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter smaller than 2.5 microns (PM2.5) is a significant environmental and public health concern in Thailand (Bran et al., 2022; Reddington et al., 2019; Supasri et al., 2023; Yabueng et al., 2020). An increase in population along with industrial and agricultural activities has driven the escalation of PM2.5 levels in recent years (Fold et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2013; Moran et al., 2019), with PM2.5 having gone up by 0.1 μg/m3 per year from 2002 to 2021 (Petchprayoon et al., 2022). Long‐term exposure to PM2.5 poses severe threats to human health, contributing to health issues such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and lung cancer (LC) (Cohen et al., 2017; Murray et al., 2020; West et al., 2016).

The Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR)–the most populous city in Thailand with approximately 11 million residents (Losiri et al., 2016)– is subject to severe air pollution. During the dry season of 2018, the seasonal average of PM2.5 concentration reached 29 μg/m3, with the main contribution being biomass burning (34%), transportation (23%), and secondary inorganic aerosols (17%) (Narita et al., 2019). PM2.5 pollution in Bangkok greatly contributes to non‐accidental, cardiopulmonary, and LC mortalities, and has been estimated that Bangkok could prevent 1,393 premature deaths annually by reducing PM2.5 levels to meet Thailand's national air quality standard (Fold et al., 2020). North Thailand (NT) also routinely experiences high PM2.5 levels, with an annual average of 33 μg/m3 in 2019, peaking at the seasonal average of 70 μg/m3 during the summer (Amnuaylojaroen et al., 2020; Bran et al., 2024; Yabueng et al., 2020). Exposure to PM2.5 in NT is associated with an increase in the number of people who have experienced circulatory and respiratory diseases (Mueller et al., 2021; Thongtip et al., 2022). The main source of PM2.5 in this area, characterized by extensive forests and agricultural fields, is biomass burning during the dry season (Moran et al., 2019; Thongsame et al., 2024). To help address this, the local government aims to reduce crop burning through a zero‐open burning policy that regulates anthropogenic burning in nine provinces in Upper NT for around 60 days each year (Yabueng et al., 2020). However, PM2.5 levels have continued to exceed World Health Organization standards after the policy enactment (http://air4thai.pcd.go.th/webV3/) because of the PM2.5 originating from other biomass burning such as non‐crop fires, areas outside Northern Thailand, and burning activities in neighboring countries (Thongsame et al., 2024).

Source attribution studies provide useful information about PM2.5 that can aid in formulation of air pollution mitigation efforts (e.g., McDuffie et al., 2021). Previous studies in Thailand have used chemical observations to identify sources of PM2.5, but have been limited to a few locations (Chansuebsri et al., 2022; Fold et al., 2020; Nuchdang et al., 2023). For example, Chansuebsri et al. (2022) found high potassium nitrate (KNO3) and potassium chloride (KCl) in PM2.5 from two rural and two urban sites in Northern Thailand, indicating open burning as the main source of PM2.5 during the haze season. Moreover, a study by Nuchdang et al. (2023) on element concentrations in PM2.5 at a suburban site in Pathum Thani found that the primary sources of PM2.5 in the BMR are a combination of soil dust and biomass burning (32%), and road dust and industrial emission (25%). In comparison, at larger spatial scales, assessments of PM2.5 sources have predominantly focused on Southeast Asia or global scales with insights into broad categories of emissions, such as biomass burning and anthropogenic sources (McDuffie et al., 2021; Reddington et al., 2019). At these scales, chemical transport models are powerful tools for source attribution, especially in areas with limited observations (McDuffie et al., 2021).

Beyond source attribution, health burden assessments provide crucial information on how PM2.5 exposure relates to mortality, aiding in the formulation of effective mitigation strategies. However, in Thailand, health burden assessments typically address PM2.5 exposure from all sources, making it difficult to pinpoint which sources pose more health risks. For example, a health burden analysis in Bangkok using the Environmental Benefits Mapping and Analysis Program (BenMAP) indicated that PM2.5 exposure resulted in 4,240 non‐accidental mortality in 2016 (Fold et al., 2020). Additionally, a study by Supasri et al. (2023) employing a life cycle assessment methodology estimated that PM2.5 contributed to LC and cardiopulmonary disease‐related deaths in approximately 0.04% and 0.06% of the population in NT. Furthermore, Jarernwong et al. (2023) found significant correlations between PM2.5 and the incidence of dermatitis (R = 0.84), conjunctivitis (R = 0.81), stroke (R = 0.74), and LC (R = 0.73) in Chiang Mai, a major city in Northern Thailand.

Despite these findings, there is a lack of comprehensive source attribution and health impact assessments throughout Thailand, especially in areas such as Bangkok and Northern Thailand. This gap has posed challenges for the formulation of local PM2.5 mitigation strategies. To help address this, here we aim to examine the source attribution and health burden of PM2.5 in Thailand including the BMR and NT. We identify the contribution of PM2.5 from crop burning and non‐crop burning for biomass burning emissions. For anthropogenic emissions, contributions from transportation, industry, energy, residential and other sectors are identified. We use the Weather Research and Forecasting Model coupled with Chemistry (WRF‐Chem) model, a widely used tool for simulating PM2.5 at local and regional levels (Qiao et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2015) to investigate PM2.5 concentration in Thailand. We then quantify the premature deaths attributable to PM2.5 exposure from each source following the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework (Murray et al., 2020) in order to underscore the health burden associated with specific PM2.5 sources.

Methodology

WRF‐Chem

In this study, the WRF‐Chem model version 4.2.2 (Fast et al., 2006; Grell et al., 2005) is used to simulate PM2.5 concentrations and attribute their sources in Thailand. The model domain (Figure 1) and configuration largely follows Thongsame et al. (2024). It employs 42 vertical levels, with an upper boundary of approximately 63 hPa, and a horizontal resolution of 9 × 9 km2. The chemical and aerosol scheme is the MOZART‐MOSAIC scheme (Emmons et al., 2010; Knote et al., 2014; Zaveri et al., 2008), with a 45‐s time step for both meteorological and chemical simulations. The CAMS‐GLOBAL‐ANT version 5.3 inventory (Granier et al., 2019) is used for anthropogenic emissions and MEGAN version 2.04 (Guenther et al., 2006) is used for biogenic emissions. Although Thongsame et al. (2024) recommended using FINN version 1.5 (FINN1.5) for operational PM2.5 simulations in Thailand, it is not well suited for source attribution and other studies due to its outdated land cover data from 2002 (Wiedinmyer et al., 2011). Therefore, we use FINN version 2.5 using Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer only fire detections (FINN2.5), which incorporates land cover data from MODIS (MCD12Q1 version 6) for the year 2018 (Wiedinmyer et al., 2023) to estimate the type and amount of biomass burned. To account for the overestimation of PM2.5 by FINN2.5 in Thailand (Thongsame et al., 2024), we apply a 30% reduction to these emissions, based on the ratio between the monthly average PM2.5 concentration from the ground‐based air quality stations and WRF‐Chem simulations for March 2019. To evaluate the WRF‐Chem simulations, PM2.5 concentrations from the Pollution Control Department (PCD) of Thailand air quality stations (http://air4thai.pcd.go.th/webV3/) are used (Pollution Control Department, 2019). Our sensitivity analysis of the scaling factor in March 2019 shows that the 30% reduction for all species provides acceptable performance in simulating PM2.5 concentrations in Mainland Thailand (MT) (Text S1 in Supporting Information S1).

WRF‐Chem model domain (Green) and Thailand national border (Black), which are grouped into North Thailand (NT, Blue) and Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR, Red). Mainland Thailand represents Thailand within our WRF‐Chem domain, which excludes South Thailand.

We acknowledge that applying a uniform scaling factor does not account for uncertainties in the PM2.5 and VOC speciation profiles in FINN2.5. To assess the implications of this, we compare our simulated PM2.5 species with those reported in previous studies based on chemical composition analysis (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). Our results show that the simulated ratio of organic carbon (OC) to black carbon (BC) is generally consistent with previous studies in Chiang Mai, NT, and absolute carbonaceous aerosol concentrations were well simulated in 2019. In contrast, secondary species appear to be underestimated. These findings indicate that the OC and BC emissions factors in FINN2.5 are likely reasonable for simulating PM2.5. Nevertheless, the development and application of species‐specific scaling factors are necessary to enhance the accuracy of PM2.5 simulations in the future.

Atmospheric aerosols affect meteorology via several mechanisms in our WRF‐Chem simulations (Zhu et al., 2021). The direct aerosol effect is the aerosol scattering of solar radiation that results in a cooling of the atmosphere and surface (Ramanathan et al., 2001). The semi‐direct effect is the aerosol absorption of solar radiation by BC and other absorbing aerosol compounds (Koch & Del Genio, 2010). This absorption heats the atmosphere and eventually reduces cloud formation (Johnson et al., 2004). The aerosol absorption and scattering can reduce the surface heating that contributes to the development of the boundary layer, thereby decreasing the Planetary Boundary Layer Height (PBLH) (Forkel et al., 2012). The indirect effect is the impact of aerosol on cloud condensation nuclei, influencing the optical properties of clouds and impacting cloud cover, lifetime of clouds, and precipitation (Haywood & Boucher, 2000). In WRF‐Chem, the direct and semi‐direct effects of aerosols are controlled by a namelist variable, while the indirect effects are embedded in the chemistry and aerosol scheme (Chapman et al., 2009; Forkel et al., 2012).

Previous studies have shown that aerosol radiation feedback could amplify the benefits of emission control strategies (Zhu et al., 2021). Consequently, in this study, we use two WRF‐Chem settings: WRF‐Chem with aerosol feedback (FB) and WRF‐Chem without aerosol feedback (NFB). The FB refers to Chem_opt = 202 with aer_ra_feedback = on in WRF‐Chem's namelist and aerosol‐cloud interactions active. This configuration provides the most rigorous representation of aerosol radiation interactions. The aerosol feedback option is used for the baseline PM2.5 simulation and health impact calculation. The NFB configuration is deactivating both direct and indirect aerosol radiation feedback, which is Chem_opt = 201 with aer_ra_feedback = off. This configuration is used for our source attribution calculations using the brute‐force method. This is to isolate the influences of emissions perturbations separate from their impact on the PBLH, which can respond nonlinearly in the absence of aerosol owing to reduced absorption, leading to negative source contributions estimated using the brute force method.

In this study, we focus on three areas in our domain: NT, the BMR, and MT (which includes both NT and the BMR). Here the term “MT” specifically refers to the portion of Thailand within our simulation domain (Figure 1). We recognize that this excludes the southernmost part of the country; this is a reasonable tradeoff for computational expediency given that, for primary PM, emissions in MT constitute 89.16% of Thailand's anthropogenic emissions and 99.82% of its biomass burning emissions, based on the 2019 CAMS‐GLOB‐ANT and FINN2.5 inventories, respectively. Furthermore, MT's population‐weighted annual mean PM2.5 in 2019 was 92.21% of that of all of Thailand (Shen et al., 2024), and MT comprises approximately 86% of Thailand's population, based on 2019 demographic data (WorldPopSchool of Geography and Environmental Science, 2020). North Thailand constitutes the nine provinces in Upper Northern Thailand including Chiang Mai, Lamphun, Lampang, Tak, Phrae, Nan, Phayao, Chiang Rai, and Mae Hong Son (Yabueng et al., 2020). This area is characterized by significant seasonal air quality variations and the implementation of a zero‐open burning policy. The BMR comprises Bangkok and its surrounding areas, including Nonthaburi, Samut Prakan, Pathum Thani, Samut Sakhon, and Nakhon Pathom, areas characterized by high urban density and industrial activity which are crucial to understanding regional pollution dynamics.

Source Attribution

The brute‐force method is a widely used technique for source apportionment in atmospheric modeling (Blanchard, 1999; Yarwood et al., 2006). This method involves running the model multiple times, each time perturbing the emissions from a specific source while keeping others constant (Kelly et al., 2015). To assess the impact of each source on PM2.5 concentrations, the perturbed and baseline simulations are compared (Clappier et al., 2017). However, for species such as PM2.5 whose formation is nonlinear, the results of brute‐force source attribution may depend on the magnitude of the emission perturbation (Clappier et al., 2017; McDuffie et al., 2021). In our study, we perform perturbed simulations by completely removing (100% emission reduction) the emissions from targeted sources between February and April 2019 acknowledging that our estimates might be impacted by non‐linear effects. To assess the impact of non‐linear effects, we compared results using 20% versus 100% perturbations for short simulations from 1–9 March 2019 in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1 and found that the impact is limited to a few percent, given the large fraction of PM2.5 from primary (i.e., linear) sources in our study period and domain.

The contribution percentage of each source is calculated using Equations 1 and 2. In Equation 1, the mass contribution of source j (PM2.5NFB,j) is determined by comparing the perturbed simulations (PM2.5NFB,PT,j) with the baseline simulation (PM2.5NFB,BL), while Equation 2 provides the percentage contribution (%PM2.5NFB,j), calculated from the sum of PM2.5 from all j sources. The contribution of each source is obtained from WRF‐Chem without aerosol feedback as a percentage and then applied to the concentration from WRF‐Chem with aerosol feedback enabled ([PM2.5]FB) in Equation 3. (1) PM2.5NFB,j=PM2.5NFB,BL−PM2.5NFB,PT,j (2) %PM2.5NFB,j=PM2.5NFB,j∑i=1nPM2.5NFB,i×100 (3) PM2.5FB,j=PM2.5FB×%PM2.5NFB,j

This study focuses on anthropogenic and biomass burning sources in MT. The biomass burning emissions (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1) are classified into five categories based on burning type and region: crop and non‐crop burning in NT, crop and non‐crop burning in the rest of MT, and burning from in‐domain foreign countries. Table 1 presents the percentages of PM2.5 emissions from FINN2.5 during February–April 2019 relative to MT's total biomass burning emissions, along with the absolute emissions in MT and the domain total for each burning category. The monthly biomass emissions are estimated by aggregating daily emissions in FINN2.5. Crop and non‐crop are classified by the land cover in FINN2.5. The non‐crop burning represents fire spots located in grasslands and savanna, woody savanna/shrublands, tropical forests, temperate forests, boreal forests, and temperate evergreen forests, while crop burning is in croplands. As the zero‐open burning policy has been implemented in NT, we distinguish between North and the rest of MT. We assume the zero‐open burning policy applies to crop fields only.

Anthropogenic emissions (Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1) are separated based on sectors in CAMS‐GLOB‐ANT: transportation, industry, energy generation, residential burning and other sectors within MT, and all anthropogenic emissions from in‐domain foreign countries (Table 2). The monthly temporal profiles in CAMS‐GLOB‐ANT are derived from the CAMS‐GLOB‐TEMPO data set (Guevara et al., 2021; Soulie et al., 2023), which provides country‐specific profiles developed using data from global organizations. However, in developing countries such as Thailand, some data are unavailable, for example, in the energy and transportation sectors. CAMS‐GLOB‐TEMPO fills these gaps using proxy data from European sources, specifically the Netherlands Organization for Applied Scientific Research (TNO). As a result, the temporal profiles for these sectors may not fully reflect emission patterns in Thailand. This categorization aims to identify strategies for reducing anthropogenic PM2.5 emissions in MT with minimal economic and societal impacts. To account for sources outside our model domain, we use concentrations from CAM‐Chem global simulations (Buchholz et al., 2019) as boundary conditions, which represent the inflow outside the model domain. We perturbed the boundary conditions to assess the regional impact of air pollution coming from outside our modeling domain. The contribution from biogenic emissions is determined by turning biogenic emissions from MEGAN off in our domain.

Health Impact

The GBD framework (Murray et al., 2020) is a widely used method to estimate the health impact of PM2.5 (e.g., Nawaz & Henze, 2020) by estimating the attributable fraction of specific diseases to ambient exposure. This involves determining the total impact of diseases on population and applying relative risks derived from epidemiological studies to quantify number of premature deaths attributable to specific risk factors, such as long‐term exposure to ambient PM2.5 (Bu et al., 2021).

In this study, we define health burden as the number of premature deaths caused by long‐term PM2.5 exposure across all selected diseases and age groups. In this study, we focus on six PM2.5‐related diseases, consistent with McDuffie et al. (2021): chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, type II diabetes (DM), ischemic heart disease, LC, lower respiratory infections, and stroke.

We use Equation 4 to calculate the age‐specific attributable fraction (AF) for PM2.5 exposure, which determines the proportion of the health burden attributable to long‐term PM2.5 exposure. The calculation incorporates PM2.5 exposure levels (z) and the associated relative risks (RR) of health outcomes: (4) AFi,O(z)=RRi,O(z)−1RRi,O(z) where z is the annual PM2.5 concentration, and RR is the relative risk for relevant age groups (i) and disease (O) based on the Meta Regression‐Bayesian, Regularized, Trimmed (MR‐BRT) spline from the 2019 GBD study (Murray et al., 2020). RR estimates the risk of health burden between an exposed population compared to an unexposed one. The total premature deaths for each disease and age group are calculated using Equation 5: (5) Di,O=Pi×BMRi,O×AFi,O(z) where D is the age‐specific premature death for each i and O. It is determined by the population in MT (P), the national baseline mortality rate (BMR) for that disease, and AF. Population data for 2019 in MT is from WorldPopSchool of Geography and Environmental Science, (2020) and the BMR and AF are from Murray et al. (2020). We utilize BMR, P, and RR, aggregating across all age groups over 25 years in 5‐year age brackets similar to Murray et al. (2020). The number of premature deaths from all diseases and age groups are then summed to obtain the health burden. The population data, originally at a spatial resolution of 1 × 1 km2, is regridded to match the WRF‐Chem resolution of 9 × 9 km2. The BMR values are specific to Thailand and vary by disease type and age group. The AF is calculated based on PM2.5 concentrations in each grid cell, resulting in the age‐specific premature mortality at a 9 × 9 km2 resolution.

To calculate the health burden, WRF‐Chem with aerosol feedback (PM2.5FB) is used for z in Equations 4 and 5. The annual health burden refers to the total premature deaths caused by PM2.5 exposure in 2019. We assess the health burden attributable to specific source j using source attribution results from Equation 3. The PM2.5 concentration in the absence of specific sectors (zj) is calculated using Equation 6: (6) zj=z−PM2.5FB,j

The zj is applied to Equations 4 and 5 to calculate premature deaths in the absence of source j. The health burden of source j is determined by subtracting the premature deaths in the absence of source j from the total premature deaths due to PM2.5 exposure in 2019.

Results

Model Evaluation

Thongsame et al. (2024) provide a thorough evaluation of WRF‐Chem across the MT domain during March and October in 2019. The model displays a low mean bias, with values of −4 μg/m3 in October and 16 μg/m3 in March, but it struggles to accurately represent the diurnal variability of PM2.5 concentrations during both periods (Thongsame et al., 2024). Figure 2 presents a comparison of our PM2.5 surface concentrations from our baseline WRF‐Chem simulation (FINN2.5 emissions scaled down by 30%) to PCD station data. We compare the annual average of 2019 and the monthly averages for February, March, and April 2019 (Table S3 in Supporting Information S1). We find a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.69 and the root mean square error (RMSE) is 10 μg/m3 for the annual average. This indicates that our model has acceptable performance in PM2.5 simulation in 2019. Moreover, WRF‐Chem shows higher correlation to PCD station data during the haze season with an R of 0.76 in February, 0.89 in March, and 0.92 in April. The RMSE is 12 μg/m3 for February and April, and 30 μg/m3 for March. In general, our model slightly underestimates PM2.5 in 2019 except in March when the mean bias is (MB) of 3 μg/m3. This indicates the model has reasonable fidelity in capturing PM2.5 at these timescales.

Source Attribution

We next present the results of our source attribution of PM2.5 concentrations in Figure 3. During the haze season (February to April), external contributions (in‐domain foreign countries burning, inflow from outside the model domain (boundary conditions), and in‐domain foreign countries anthropogenic emissions) dominate PM2.5 in MT, particularly in March and April when they account for 57% and 76% of PM2.5 levels respectively (Figure 3a). The main external sources are biomass burning from in‐domain foreign countries, which intensify during March and April, and boundary conditions. Domestically, non‐crop burning in NT and the rest of MT are significant contributors, contributing 25% in February, 28% in March, and 15% in April. However, the contribution of crop burning in the rest of MT peaks at about 13% in February and decreases markedly by March (5%) and April (2%). Crop burning in NT is a small source of PM2.5 in MT, remaining at about 1% throughout the period. Overall, during the biomass burning season anthropogenic emissions account for approximately 10% of PM2.5 concentrations across MT and the contribution of biogenic emissions in our domain is about 1%. However, biogenic emissions outside the domain are included in the boundary conditions, which contribute to secondary organic aerosols transporting to and/or forming in our domain.

The source attribution of PM2.5 from February to April in each area: (a) Mainland Thailand, (b) North Thailand, and (c) the Bangkok Metropolitan Region. The monthly PM2.5 concentrations averaged from each area are presented over the pie chart.

In NT, we attribute over 60% of PM2.5 to biomass burning during the haze season (Figure 3b). The contribution from crop burning in NT, targeted by zero‐open burning policies, is minimal (∼1%). In contrast, we find that non‐crop burning in NT is more substantial, contributing about 20% throughout the season. The PM2.5 from in‐domain foreign country's biomass burning and boundary conditions are the major external contributors, particularly in March and April. The PM2.5 in NT originating from these external sources surpasses those from biomass burning within NT. Internal burning from the rest of MT contributes less than 15% to NT's PM2.5 levels. Anthropogenic emissions contribute similarly less than 10%. Biogenic emissions also have a minimal impact (∼1%), despite the substantial forest cover in the region.

In the BMR, biomass burning in NT has negligible effects on PM2.5 levels both crop and non‐crop (Figure 3c). Instead, boundary conditions are the primary source, increasing from about 30% in February to around 50% in March and April due to the high burning outside the domain. In‐domain foreign countries biomass burning also significantly contribute about 30%. This indicates that external PM2.5 sources are more important than internal PM2.5 sources for the BMR during this season, showing approximately 60% contribution of PM2.5. Local crop and non‐crop burning in the rest of MT have small impacts on the BMR's PM2.5, contributing less than 15%. Unlike in NT, anthropogenic emissions significantly influence the BMR air quality, ranging from 20% to 10% contribution from February to April, with industrial emissions dominating. The contribution of biogenic emissions in this area is again about 1%.

In summary, PM2.5 pollution in MT is largely attributed to transported pollution during February to April, with biomass burning from in‐domain foreign countries and air pollution from outside our model domain playing dominant roles. The contribution from domestic sources varies between NT and the BMR. In NT, non‐crop burning is a significant source, while in the BMR, anthropogenic emissions are the primary contributors to PM2.5 levels. Biogenic emissions have a small impact on PM2.5 in all regions.

Health Impact

Source attribution of the annual PM2.5 health burden attributable by month is presented in Figure 4. Annual average PM2.5 concentrations for 2019 are calculated with WRF‐Chem to be 21 μg/m3 for MT, 27 μg/m3 for NT, and 17 μg/m3 for the BMR. The annual health burden in 2019 is 20,406 [95% Confidence Interval: 12,057–29,613] in MT, 3,024 [95% CI: 1,994–4,135] in NT, and 4,925 [95% CI: 2,715–7,481] in the BMR. Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1 shows the spatial distribution of the annual health burden in 2019. Notably, the months from February to April contribute significantly to annual PM2.5 loads, accounting for 54% in MT, 66% in NT, and 31% in the BMR. Reducing PM2.5 during these critical months could potentially reduce premature deaths by 9,488 [95% CI: 6,926–11,421] (46%) in MT, 1,993 [95% CI: 1,502–2,342] (66%) in NT, and 1,801 [95% CI: 2,163–2,239] (37%) in the BMR (Table 3). These numbers represent the total health burden from all emissions from February to April.

Blue highlighting varies to show different levels of health benefits: dark blue indicates regions with more than 30% health burden, medium blue for 20%–30%, light blue for 10%–20%, and white for lower than 10%.

We next consider the impact of the health burden owing to sector‐specific emissions during the February to April haze season (Table 3). We find a strong correlation between the source attribution of PM2.5 and health burden. Specific sectors with large contributions to concentration similarly have high health burdens. PM2.5 originating from boundary conditions accounts for approximately 27% of the total health burden in MT, 22% in NT, and 41% in the BMR. Furthermore, biomass burning from in‐domain foreign countries contributes about 25% of the total health burden in MT, 28% in NT, and 26% in the BMR. In contrast, anthropogenic emissions from in‐domain foreign countries have a minimal impact on health burdens, contributing only 1%–4% across all regions in MT. Focusing on domestic sources, biomass burning—both crop and non‐crop—within MT is the leading contributor to health burdens, especially in NT. Biomass burning within MT accounts for 20% of the total health burden in MT and 24% in NT, but only 7% in the BMR. Conversely, the impact of PM2.5 from anthropogenic emissions is more significant in the BMR, where 13% of the total health burden is attributed to anthropogenic emissions within MT. In Northern Thailand, anthropogenic emissions within MT contribute to only 4% of the total health burden, while they account for 9% for MT.

Our research indicates that domestic PM2.5 sources, including biomass burning and anthropogenic emissions, resulted in 2,686 [95% CI: 2,153–2,970] health burdens in MT, 549 [95% CI: 463–582] in NT, and 340 [95% CI: 251–400] in the BMR between February and April 2019. Notably, external sources of PM2.5, including boundary conditions, biomass burning from in‐domain foreign countries, and anthropogenic emissions from in‐domain foreign countries, are a key factor. They contribute to 5,119 [95% CI: 3,941–5,878] health burdens in MT, 1,025 [95% CI: 852–1,102] in NT, and 1,264 [95% CI: 916–1,526] in the BMR. The potential impact of current and future policies aimed at mitigating PM2.5 levels and associated health burdens are discussed in the following section.

Discussion

Comparison of Our Source Attribution Estimates With Other Studies

In this section, we compare our source attribution results with previous studies. Notably, differences in experimental design, such as variations in time periods, atmospheric models, and source attribution methodologies, can lead to significant discrepancies in source attribution estimations (Butt et al., 2020; Giani et al., 2020; Kiely et al., 2020). In our study, biomass burning emerges as the dominant source of PM2.5 in MT, contributing approximately 56%–63% to average surface concentrations from February to April, while anthropogenic emissions account for about 5%–15%. Our analysis focuses on the haze season, during which biomass burning plays a more critical role than other sources. Extending the analysis to cover the entire year of 2019 would likely increase the relative contribution of anthropogenic emissions to PM2.5.

Using the WRF‐Chem model and a brute‐force method, Bran et al. (2024) similarly found that biomass burning (both in MT and outside MT) was the primary source of PM2.5 in 2019 in all year, contributing 47%. However, their study reported a higher contribution from anthropogenic emissions (24%) compared to our results. This difference likely stems from the increased impact of anthropogenic emissions during the off‐haze season. Previous studies by Reddington et al. (2019) and McDuffie et al. (2021) reported even higher contributions from anthropogenic sources. Reddington et al. (2019) found that anthropogenic emissions are responsible for 62% of PM2.5 in 2014, while McDuffie et al. (2021) reports a 50% contribution in 2017. Biomass burning, in contrast, accounts for only 16% and 10% of the annual PM2.5 in these respective years. This large variation in source attribution is likely due to differences in time periods and biomass burning inventory. For instance, Bran et al. (2024) showed that the contribution of biomass burning to PM2.5 dropped from 47% in an El Niño year (2019) to 34% in a La Niña year (2017), while the contribution of anthropogenic emissions increase from 24% to 32%. They also found a significant difference in observed PM2.5 concentrations between El Niño and La Niña years, with values of 26 ± 12 μg/m3 in 2019 and 2022 ± 10 μg/m3 in 2017, indicating that these climatic phenomena have a substantial impact on PM2.5 from biomass burning in MT. Additionally, these studies use different biomass burning inventories, FINN1.5 (Reddington et al., 2019), GFEDv4.1 (McDuffie et al., 2021), and FINN2.5 (this study and Bran et al. (2024)). FINN1.5 and GFEDv4.1 report lower emissions of PM2.5 than the FINN2.5 inventory for MT (Thongsame et al., 2024; Wiedinmyer et al., 2023). These differences in inventories likely contribute to variations in biomass burning attribution estimates.

In Northern Thailand, Inlaung et al. (2024) use WRF‐Chem and the brute‐force method to show that biomass burning from outside NT contributes approximately 27% in February, 46% in March, and 30% in April to PM2.5 in 2019. These findings correlate with our study, which reported contributions from outside North burning (including crop and non‐crop in the rest of MT and in‐domain foreign countries biomass burning) of 37% in February, 43% in March, and 40% in April. Hassan Bran et al. (2024) also found that during the summer (mid‐February to mid‐May) in NT, biomass burning accounts for 78% of PM2.5 in 2019, with anthropogenic emissions contributing 10%. Our results align with those of Inlaung et al. (2024) and Bran et al. (2024), confirming that biomass burning is the main source of PM2.5 in NT in 2019 with 62%–66% contribution. Additionally, our study shows that crop and non‐crop burning in NT contributed around 22%–27% of PM2.5, with emissions from external sources playing a similarly important role during the February to April period.

In the BMR, Narita et al. (2019) used data from PCD stations and Chemical Mass Balance analysis to identify the main sources of PM2.5. They found that during the 2018 dry season, biomass burning contributes 34% to PM2.5, a finding that closely aligns with our results, which show biomass burning contributing 32%–44%. Regarding anthropogenic emissions, our study identifies industry emission as the primary contributor, accounting for 4%–9% of PM2.5. However, Narita et al. (2019) reported that transportation contributes 23% to PM2.5, surpassing industry's 17%. This discrepancy is due to the proximity of many monitoring stations in the BMR to roadside locations. Additionally, our research reveals that over 50% of PM2.5 in the BMR originates from outside the area. This finding aligns with Tessum et al. (2022), who showed that approximately 35% of PM2.5 in this region originates locally, with the remainder being transported from external sources.

Comparison of Our Health Burden Estimates With Other Studies

This section provides a comparison of the health burden from PM2.5 exposure between our findings and previous studies. Prior research has focused on the health burden from total biomass burning emissions in regional or global areas (Linh Thao et al., 2022; Mueller et al., 2021; Reddington et al., 2021). However, our research focus on health burden assessments specific to MT, with emphasis on biomass burning subsectors. Total biomass burning in this section refers to emissions from crop and non‐crop burning in NT and the rest of MT, and in‐domain foreign countries biomass burning.

We first compare our health burden results to previous studies. Mueller et al. (2021) estimate the health burden from PM2.5 exposure in Thailand in 2016 based on data from PCD monitoring stations, finding a total health burden of 50,019. In contrast, our result based on WRF‐Chem simulation estimates the health burden in 2019 to be 20,406. Meanwhile, McDuffie et al. (2021), using the GEOS‐Chem model, estimate the health burden in Thailand in 2019 to be 30,000. Additionally, Reddington et al. (2019) show that the health burden in Thailand in 2014 is 33,400 based on WRF‐Chem simulation. Our results are closer to those of McDuffie et al. (2021) and Reddington et al. (2019), as we also rely on an air quality model and employ the same GBD framework for the health impact analysis. However, the variations among these estimates arise from differences in experimental design, atmospheric modeling, and the data used for health impact estimation. Moreover, it's important to note that our study's “MT” domain excludes Southern Thailand, which may also contribute to the differences in health burden estimates.

Biomass burning emissions are a key contributor to PM2.5‐related health burdens. Reddington et al. (2021) found that biomass burning accounted for 8,500 premature deaths in 2014, representing 15% of all premature deaths that year. Similarly, Linh Thao et al. (2022) reported that in March 2012, biomass burning emissions led to 3,629 premature deaths. Our results align with these findings, showing that biomass burning during the February–April period in 2019 contributed to 4,210 premature deaths or 21% of total premature deaths in that year.

Potential Impacts of PM2.5 Mitigation Policies

To address PM2.5 pollution, the Thai government has already implemented a variety of policies targeting emissions from both anthropogenic sources and biomass burning (Chavanaves et al., 2021; Narita et al., 2019). The National Master Plan for Open Burning Control (adopted in 2003) and the National Fire and Haze Control Plan of Action (adopted in 2013) aim to control biomass burning (Narita et al., 2019). These plans are part of a broader regional effort, the ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (2002), which seeks to curb haze and forest fires across Southeast Asia (ASEAN Secretariat, 2021). Thailand aims to reduce forest burning to 48,000 ha per year and to achieve zero crop burning across Thailand by 2030. Notably, the zero‐open burning policy in Northern Thailand is a key measure under this action plan (Narita et al., 2019). In this section, we assess the potential impacts of PM2.5 mitigation policies in MT, with a focus on the February–April 2019 period, when these policies are assumed to be in effect. We note that even if all PM2.5 emissions are eliminated during these months alone, the maximum possible impact on the annual health burden would be 9,488 premature deaths in MT (46% of the MT annual total), 1,993 premature deaths in NT (66% of the NT annual total), and 1,801 premature deaths in the BMR (37% of the Bangkok annual total).

Our study shows that crop burning in NT during the February–April period in 2019 contributes to 12 premature deaths, accounting for less than 1% of the NT annual total premature deaths. This suggests that the zero‐open burning policy could potentially reduce premature deaths by less than 1%. However, since 2019 marks the implementation year of the policy, the biomass burning emissions from crop burning in NT are already reduced by regulatory efforts. Without the policy, emissions and the health burden attributed to crop burning in NT would likely be higher. Therefore, the actual health benefits of the zero‐open burning policy could be more significant if we account for the additional impact of reduced emissions that the policy has already achieved. However, the zero‐open burning policy limits the crop burning in NT for 60 days during February to April only. This potentially shifts crop burning activities to periods outside the policy's enforcement window.

We also find that including reductions to crop burning throughout the rest of MT, in addition to burning in NT, would lead to 573 fewer premature deaths in all MT. If Thailand successfully implements its zero crop burning goal by 2030, the potential reduction in premature deaths could reach 3% annually. Achieving this goal would require utilizing crop residues in alternative ways, such as for animal feed, fertilizer, or electricity generation. These alternatives not only reduce crop burning but could also provide significant economic benefits, estimated at 431, 427, 422, and 399 billion THB, respectively, exceeding the economic value of current burning practices (Deuja et al., 2024).

Regarding forest burning, which we classify as non‐crop burning in this study, the health burden from February–April 2019 was 1,296 premature deaths. This suggests that policies such as forest management targeting non‐crop burning could reduce premature deaths by up to 6%.

In terms of regional cooperation, the ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution is expected to play a role in reducing in‐domain foreign countries biomass burning. Our study estimates that the health burden from in‐domain foreign countries biomass burning in February to April for MT could be reduced by 2,341 premature deaths, representing an 11% reduction in the annual health burden.

In addition to biomass burning reduction efforts, Thailand has implemented the National Roadmap to Solve Particulate Matter Pollution, which aims to further reduce emissions from the transportation sector (Chavanaves et al., 2021). Thailand recently has set emission standards for vehicles, adopting EURO 3 standards for heavy‐duty diesel vehicles and EURO 4 standards for passenger cars (Narita et al., 2019). According to our research, transportation emissions with the current standard contribute to 48 premature deaths in BMR (1% of the BMR annual total) during the February–April in 2019. When extrapolated to an annual estimate for 2019, transportation emissions are linked to 197 premature deaths (4% of the BMR annual total). The new roadmap could potentially reduce PM2.5 emissions in the BMR by up to 82% (Chavanaves et al., 2021). Our results suggest that the health burden reduction from this policy could reduce the annual total health burden by about 5%. However, anthropogenic emissions, including those from the transportation sector, play a significant role during non‐haze periods, which are not fully captured in the current analysis.

In summary, addressing air pollution in MT requires a multifaceted approach, including enhanced regional cooperation to tackle air pollution coming from outside MT, regulations for crop and non‐crop biomass burning reduction, and strengthened anthropogenic emission control policies. Moreover, investments in renewable energy and advanced modeling tools for informed policymaking are essential steps toward achieving long‐term air quality improvements and safeguarding public health.

Limitations and Uncertainties in Source Attribution Analysis

This study investigates the source attribution of PM2.5 from different emission sectors during the haze season in Thailand using the WRF‐Chem model and a brute‐force perturbation method. Despite our efforts, several limitations and uncertainties remain due to constraints in the current data sets and methodology.

First, there is limited information available to develop scaling factors for updating the FINN2.5 biomass burning emissions. In our study, we apply a uniform scaling factor based on ground‐based measurements of PM2.5 from the PCD. However, this approach does not account for uncertainties in emission speciation. Our comparisons with previous studies on the contributions of OC and BC to PM2.5 suggest that the speciation profiles in FINN2.5 may not accurately reflect precursor species emissions responsible for secondary aerosol formation in Thailand (Text S2 in Supporing Information S1). Although species‐specific scaling factors would improve the accuracy of WRF‐Chem, the lack of available PM2.5 chemical composition data for 2019 prevents their development in this study. We recommend that future work leverage data from field campaigns such as ASIA‐AQ, along with expanded chemical composition measurements, to refine species‐specific scaling factors for more accurate PM2.5 simulations.

Second, there is uncertainty associated with the monthly temporal profiles of anthropogenic emissions. While the CAMS‐GLOB‐ANT inventory applies country‐specific monthly temporal profiles from the CAMS‐GLOB‐TEMPO data set, data gaps—particularly in sectors such as energy and transportation—are filled using proxy data from European sources. This introduces potential inaccuracies in the representation of anthropogenic emissions, especially in NT, where energy and transportation are key emission sources during the haze season. As a result, the contribution from anthropogenic sectors may not fully reflect monthly temporal patterns of activity in Thailand.

Third, the nonlinearity of PM2.5 formation poses an inherent limitation to the brute‐force method. Although our sensitivity analysis using two perturbation levels (100% and 20%) shows only a modest difference in results (see Supplement S1), the choice of perturbation magnitude still affects our estimated source attribution. This limitation arises because the brute‐force method assumes a linear response, which does not account for the nonlinear chemical processes involved in PM2.5 formation. Consequently, the sectoral contributions derived from our method could vary depending on the perturbation level used or differ from results obtained through other attribution approaches. We recommend that future studies explore a wider range of perturbation levels and incorporate more advanced sensitivity or source apportionment techniques to better capture nonlinearity in secondary aerosol formation.

Conclusion

This study investigates the source attribution of PM2.5 in MT, NT, and the BMR during the haze season (February to April) in 2019, using the WRF‐Chem model and brute‐force method. We found that biomass burning, both within MT and from in‐domain foreign countries, is the primary source of PM2.5 during this period across the country. In NT, non‐crop burning in NT and in‐domain foreign countries biomass burning are the main contributors. Furthermore, transported pollution is the dominant source of PM2.5 in the BMR.

Additionally, we estimated the health burden associated with PM2.5 exposure from various sources using the GBD framework. Our results indicate that PM2.5 exposure during the haze season accounts for 46% of the total annual health burden in 2019 in MT, 66% in NT, and 37% in the BMR. Thailand has implemented policies to reduce biomass burning emissions domestically, alongside international agreements to address cross‐border pollution from neighboring countries. Our findings suggest that eliminating biomass burning in MT and in‐domain foreign countries from February to April could reduce the annual health burden by up to 20%.

While anthropogenic emissions from all sectors contribute only 4% to the health burden during the haze season, their impact becomes more significant when considering the entire year, including off‐haze periods. This underscores the importance of policies targeting anthropogenic emissions, as their reduction is crucial for improving air quality and reducing the overall health burden in MT throughout the year.
