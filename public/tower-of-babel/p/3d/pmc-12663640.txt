Breast milk-acquired cytomegalovirus (aCMV) infection with sepsis-like syndrome in extremely preterm twins
Authors: Wen Sun, Lili Ma, Hongmin Xi, Miao Qin, Liangliang Li, Lulu Zhang, Ping Yang, Xiangyun Yin, Xianghong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is a common perinatal viral infection. Most CMV infections are asymptomatic; only a part of them present with symptoms, particularly in infants fed fresh breast milk. This article reports a case of two extremely preterm twins who simultaneously developed severe breast milk-acquired CMV infection. By reviewing the medical records of two infants, observing their clinical symptoms, collecting laboratory test results (including blood routine, biochemistry, inflammatory indicators, etc.), and conducting continuous monitoring of CMV-DNA in blood, urine, and breast milk, the research results were obtained. Both infants presented with sepsis-like syndrome: fever, apnea, pneumonia with respiratory failure, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, but antibiotic treatment was ineffective. Finally, the diagnosis of breast milk-acquired CMV infection was confirmed by testing of CMV DNA. Severe breast milk-acquired CMV infection occurring simultaneously in preterm twins was extremely rare. In clinical practice, early detection, timely diagnosis, and positive intervention are key points for breast milk-acquired CMV infection.Keywords: Cytomegalovirus, Breast milk, Cytomegalovirus-related sepsis-like syndrome, Twins, Premature infant

Introduction

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) belongs to the Herpesviridae family and is a common pathogen causing perinatal infections. Perinatal CMV infection primarily occurs through three main routes: first, vertical transmission through the placenta; second, transmission during delivery via genital secretions; and third, postnatally acquired CMV infection, which is mostly transmitted through breast milk. Breast milk-acquired CMV(aCMV) infection in term infants is mostly asymptomatic, but preterm infants may suffer from thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, cholestasis, sepsis-like syndrome, respiratory failure, necrotizing enterocolitis, and even death [1]. The early symptoms in preterm infants, especially extremely low/very low birth weight infants, are often nonspecific, making timely clinical identification and intervention challenging. In this case report, we discuss early identification and prevention strategies for aCMV infection in extremely preterm twins.

Case report

The firstborn of twins was a male born at 26 weeks+ 4 days gestation, weighing 980 g. Premature rupture of membranes for 11 days, intrauterine distress, breech delivery with assistance, weak spontaneous breathing after birth, cyanosis of the limbs and poor responsiveness. He was intubated and ventilated with positive pressure. Apgar score was 3 at 1 min, 6 at 5 min, and 6 at 10 min. Twenty minutes after birth, the infant was transferred to our NICU. After admission, enteral and parenteral nutrition support was provided. Fresh breast milk from his own mother was initiated at 3 days postnatally, and full enteral feeding was achieved by 14 days postnatally.

‘Postnatal monitoring of blood routine and inflammatory markers revealed low leukocyte counts, low platelet counts, and elevated CRP levels. Combined with premature rupture of membranes for 11 days, the diagnosis was early-onset sepsis. We provided antimicrobial therapy, concurrently conducting cerebrospinal fluid routine and biochemical testing, blood and cerebrospinal fluid cultures, sputum culture, and urine CMV-DNA testing, all of which were negative. On day 5 postnatal, cranial ultrasound showed grade Ⅱ periventricular-intraventricular hemorrhage. Cardiovascular ultrasound revealed patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) 0.32 cm, left atrial and left ventricular enlargement, diagnosed as haemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus (hsPDA), and oral ibuprofen therapy was administered. Follow-up cardiovascular ultrasound showed PDA 0.23 cm. On day 12 postnatal, follow-up cranial ultrasound showed no progression of intracranial hemorrhage. The infant remained on invasive ventilation and could not be discontinued. On day 13 postnatally, the infant was administered dexamethasone a randomized trial (DART) protocol, and on day 16 postnatally, the ventilator was discontinued and replaced with a non-invasive ventilator. However, at 26 days postnatal (corrected gestational age 30 weeks), the infant suddenly demonstrated a deterioration in condition, presenting with poor responsiveness, frequent apnea, pallor, and a partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO₂) of 81 mmHg on arterial blood gas analysis. Chest X-ray suggested pneumonia, and empirical antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated. We conducted several blood tests, CRP and PCT tests, which showed no significant elevation. Blood culture was negative, and sputum culture indicated Escherichia coli (sensitive to piperacillin). Antibiotic treatment showed no improvement, so we changed to meropenem + linezolid and oral fluconazole. But the pneumonia got worse. At 38 days postnatally, the patient exhibited fever, with a platelet count of 59 × 10⁹/L, a peak CRP of 7.6 mg/L, and normal PCT. On day 44 postnatal (day 6 of fever), we performed CMV testing: urine CMV-DNA:1.8 × 10 ³ copy counts/mL; blood CMV-DNA:5.6 × 10⁴ copy counts/mL; breast milk CMV-DNA:5.4 × 10⁴ copy counts/mL. Next-generation sequencing (blood, deep sputum lavage fluid) detected CMV. It was a pity that we did not perform CMV genotyping, which could determine whether the genotype of CMV in breast milk and the patient belonged to the same strain. But we conducted metagenomic sequencing, identifying CMV as the sole pathogen.

We reviewed the medical records of their mother. The mother’s IgM of CMV was negative, but IgG of CMV was positive, indicating she had a previous CMV infection. But she showed no clinical symptoms during either the prenatal period or lactation, such as fever, pharyngitis, fatigue, or swollen lymph nodes. The patients' IgG of CMV was positive, which was transferred through the maternal placenta. But the tests of CMV PCR in urine and CMV IgM were both negative in the patients at birth, which indicated that the infection was not congenital. The primary route of acquired CMV infection is through breast milk, followed by blood transfusion. In our case, the patient had not received any blood transfusions before the worsening of symptoms. Therefore, the CMV infection was considered to have been acquired from breast milk. Before the aCMV infection diagnosis, we chose the patients’ own mother’s fresh breast milk without freezing or pasteurization. After the urine and blood CMV-DNA test was positive, we stopped breastfeeding and replaced it with premature infant formula milk due to the lack of a pasteurization facility. Administered intravenous ganciclovir antiviral treatment for 6 weeks. During the treatment, we evaluated the quantitative CMV-DNA in the blood and adverse drug reactions, including blood routine tests, liver and kidney function, and coagulation function, at intervals of 1–2 weeks. Blood CMV-DNA turned negative on the 19th day after the antiviral treatment, and urine CMV-DNA turned negative on the 64th day after the antiviral treatment, but breast milk CMV-DNA remained positive (Table 1). The patient’s condition improved after 1.5 months of invasive respiratory support and 1.5 months of non-invasive respiratory support. He ceased respiratory support at corrected gestational age of 38 weeks. Based on the 2018 NICHD diagnostic criteria for bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), he was diagnosed with BPD Grade III [2]. At discharge, the corrected gestational age was 46 weeks+ 6 days, weight was 5.62 kg (P65), length was 58 cm (P65), and head circumference was 38 cm (P50). Left ear auditory evoked potentials failed. Stage III retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) was diagnosed and treated with ranibizumab. PDA closed spontaneously. At 3 months of corrected gestational age, brainstem auditory evoked potentials passed bilaterally. Post-discharge follow-up: Satisfactory weight and length gain, small head circumference, and delayed motor development.

The second-born of twins was a female born at 26 + 4 weeks of gestation, weighing 750 g. After birth, she had weak spontaneous breathing and was intubated and T-piece assisted ventilation. The Apgar score was 5 at 1 min and 7 at 5 min. She was transferred to the NICU at 14 min postnatal. After admission, she received invasive mechanical ventilation, pulmonary surfactant replacement therapy. The DATA regimen was initiated at 21 days postnatal, and non-invasive respiratory support was initiated at 22 days postnatal.

Four days after the firstborn's condition worsened (at 30 days postnatal), this infant also showed changes in her condition, manifested as frequent decreased oxygenation and carbon dioxide retention. She was intubated and given invasive ventilator-assisted ventilation. Her body temperature was 37.5°C, and she was given piperacillin sodium and tazobactam sodium for anti-infective treatment. At 37 days postnatal, blood routine tests showed thrombocytopenia, with no improvement despite antibiotic therapy. At 44 days postnatal, urine CMV-DNA levels were 6.7 × 10 ³ copy counts/mL, blood CMV-DNA levels were 7.9 × 103 copy counts/mL, and breast milk CMV-DNA levels were 5.4 × 10⁴ copy counts/mL, diagnosing CMV infection. The infant's condition gradually improved, with platelet counts recovering to 125 × 10⁹/L. At 61 days postnatal, the infant was removed from the invasive mechanical ventilator and switched to a non-invasive ventilator. Simultaneously, blood CMV-DNA turned negative. Oxygen therapy was discontinued at 112 days postnatal. And she was also diagnosed with BPD.

Finally, the infant was hospitalized for 114 days. At discharge, the infant's corrected gestational age was 42 weeks + 6 days, weight was 3.69 kg (P 25), head circumference was 35 cm (P 25), and length was 50 cm (P 10). Brainstem auditory evoked potentials were normal in both ears, and stage III retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) was diagnosed and treated with ranibizumab. Post-discharge follow-up: Satisfactory weight(P60), length gain(P85), and head circumference(P10), normal motor and speech development.

Discussion

Due to its important immunological and nutritional value, breast milk is the first choice for enteral feeding in preterm infants. However, breast milk may also transmit viruses, including CMV. A meta-analysis from Korea indicated that aCMV infection rate among preterm infants fed CMV-positive breast milk was 20.7 % [3]. The clinical symptoms of aCMV infection mainly include thrombocytopenia, sepsis-like syndrome, neutropenia, hepatosplenomegaly, abnormal liver function, and neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis. Sepsis-like syndrome mainly includes dyspnea, apnea, bradycardia, hepatosplenomegaly, gray or pale complexion, abdominal distension, pneumonia, severe hepatitis, hemorrhagic diarrhea, etc [4]. The results of a meta-analysis in Austria showed that the aCMV infection rate of preterm infants (<34 weeks, <1500 g) after breastfeeding was 5.7 %-58.6 %, and 0–13.8 % of them developed severe sepsis-like syndrome [5], [6]. Katrin Mehler et al. reported the aCMV infection rate of 65 % in preterm infants(22–24weeks), with the incidence of sepsis-like syndrome reaching 55 %, but none of them died [7]. Josephson and Cassandra et al. indicated that preterm infants(average gestational age of 27 weeks, average weight of 1000 g) had the aCMV infection rate of 5.4 %(29/539), an incidence of sepsis-like syndrome of 17.2 % (5/29), and 60 % (3/5) of them died from sepsis-like syndrome [8]. At present, the sepsis-like syndrome of aCMV infection in preterm infants has been reported, but severe aCMV infection in preterm twins at the same time is rarely reported, which was difficult to distinguish from sepsis caused by bacterial infection, and antibiotic treatment was ineffective.

In fact, CMV may reactivate during lactation with rates reported as high as 96 % and be limited in the breast tissue rather than spreading throughout the body [9]. And the prolonged viral shedding in maternal breast milk should be highlighted. At the beginning after birth, colostrum is very small, and the patient's enteral intake was minimal. So, the clinical manifestations may not occur. Later, CMV secretion into breast milk starts at the first week postpartum and reaches a peak at 4–8 weeks, then gradually declines by 9–12 weeks [10], [11]. In another study, CMV viral loads in breast milk peak at 3–5 weeks [1]. And there is a latent phase from CMV infection to the detection of a positive CMV-DNA, with an average of 42 days as reported in the literature [12]. This may explain why, in our case, the twins appeared to have a worsening of their condition and sepsis-like syndrome at 26 days and 30 days postnatally rather than presenting with clinical manifestations immediately after birth. In our case, CMV-DNA in breast milk was still detectable at 16 weeks postpartum.

Very/extremely low birth weight infants with severe postnatal CMV infection should be treated actively. Both of our twins have experienced deterioration in the clinical condition, accompanied by fever, thrombocytopenia, and other presentations of sepsis-like syndrome, which are compliant with the treatment indications. However, there is little experience in antiviral treatment for extremely preterm infants. Usually, ganciclovir (GCV) and oral valganciclovir were used. The standard dose was 12 mg/ (kg·time) twice daily for at least 2 weeks. Some studies reported that ganciclovir may develop toxicities such as neutropenia, renal or hepatic toxicity [13]. So, we performed monitoring of adverse effects, and fortunately, no adverse drug reactions were reported. But there were no recommendations about the antiviral treatment duration of CMV infection in preterm infants [14]. In this aspect, more prospective studies are still needed.

There is no consistent conclusion on the prognosis of aCMV infection in preterm infants. Some studies have found that infants with aCMV infection have an increased risk of BPD [15], [16], neurodevelopmental impairment, including global developmental delays, hypotonia, psychomotor, language, and visuospatial delays [17]. There is no sufficient evidence to support the association between CMV infection and visual/hearing damage [18], [19]. Case reports indicate that aCMV infection may result in worsening ROP in preterm infants [20]. In our case, both twin infants developed BPD, ROP, and one of them had transient hearing damage, which requires more follow-up studies on the relationship between them.

Prevention strategies: The risk factors for aCMV infection in preterm infants primarily included: high CMV viral load in breast milk, long duration of breastfeeding, and premature rupture of membranes [8]. High-risk factors for severe infection include small gestational age or extremely low birth weight [5]. Therefore, for the very low birth weight (<1500 g) and extremely preterm infants(< 32 weeks), who are at high risk for developing severe clinical manifestation, several methods have been developed to inactivate CMV in breast milk, including long-term pasteurization(heat treatment at 63°C for 30 min), short-term pasteurization (72°C for 5 s), and freeze-thaw (freezing breast milk at −20°C for 24–72 h). Freezing can reduce, but not fully inactivate, the infectivity of CMV. The advantage of freezing is to preserve nutritional and protective factors in breast milk [11]. In China, the breast milk was suggested to be frozen for at least 24 h if the virus copies are less than 1000 copies /mL and frozen for at least 72 h or short-term pasteurization if the virus copies are more than 1000 copies /mL. Long-term pasteurization or short-term pasteurization can completely inactivate the infectivity of CMV, but nutritional and immunologic components of breast milk may be partly destroyed, including inactivated alkaline phosphatase and lipase activity, and lower sIgA and lysozyme levels [21]. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that, short-term pasteurization of breast milk should be considered for CMV-IgG seropositive mothers’ preterm infants [22]. And in France, newborns with a gestational age of less than 28 weeks or weighing less than 1000 g are also suggested to be fed with pasteurized breast milk [23]. However, the ESPGHAN Committee on Nutrition statement did not recommend pasteurization of breast milk due to loss of benefits [24]. Additionally, a center in South Korea reports weekly breast milk testing for very preterm infants and formula feeding if CMV is detected in milk [25]. In China, the IgG positivity rate for CMV among pregnant women exceeds 90 %. CMV infection in term infants and most preterm infants is often asymptomatic or presents with mild symptoms. Considering cost-effectiveness, our NICU did not conduct routine monitoring for CMV in breast milk for preterm infants. Given the severe CMV infection observed in this case, we recommend that early dynamic monitoring for CMV in breast milk before the high-risk period for infection (the peak period of maternal milk shedding) may offer a preventive benefit for extremely high-risk infants, such as extremely low birth weight (<1000 g) or extremely preterm infants (< 28weeks). As a result, there is currently no consensus about prevention strategies, which requires further research to explore how to properly balance the risks and benefits for preterm infants.

In conclusion, CMV infection can lead to severe sepsis-like syndrome. Although the incidence is extremely low, clinicians should be highly vigilant when inflammatory indicators are normal and blood culture is negative. And CMV testing in blood and/or urine should be considered in preterm infants with sepsis-like symptoms and negative bacterial cultures. Follow-up studies are needed for the long-term prognosis, especially the neurodevelopmental impairment. Due to the lack of sufficient evidence for antiviral treatment, antiviral therapy should be carefully evaluated. Given the irreplaceable advantages of breast milk, whether to pasteurize or freeze the breast milk requires a thorough consideration of the risks and benefits for preterm infants.
