Electronic frailty index based on comprehensive geriatric assessment in primary care: feasibility, validity, and reliability
Authors: Xiaowei Song, Grace Park, Barry Clarke, Samuel D Searle, Belinda Robinson, Hilary Low, Jodie Penwarden, Kaitlin Harding, Sherri Fay, Margaret CM Lin, Melissa K Andrew, Anson Li, Valerie Lewis, Paul Fluit, Olga Theou, Kenneth Rockwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo aid frailty management in primary care we developed an electronic Frialty Index based on a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (eFI-CGA). Here we report the feasibility, validity, and reliability of the eFI-CGA when conducted in primary care.MethodsTo assay feasibility, we measured assessment completion rate and time required. We tested validity by analyzing the content and characteristics of the eFI-CGA, its associations with age and the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), and prediction of three-year adverse outcomes. Reliability was evaluated by having primary care providers and geriatricians independently assess patients at baseline and at six months, yielding inter- and intra-rater estimates, as well as validity compared with a reference standard.ResultsCommunity-dwelling older adults from 27 clinics were enrolled (n = 281; 65% females). The eFI-CGA showed a four-factor structure, including function/mobility, psychological/social, cognition, and physical/medical problems. The eFI-CGA and CFS scores were moderately correlated: r = 0.65 (95% confidence intervals (CI): 0.56 − 0.72). The age − eFI-CGA correlation (r) was modest (r = 0.22; CI: 0.10 − 0.34). Each 0.01 eFI-CGA increment was associated with a Hazard Ratio (HR) of 1.04 for three-year mortality (CI: 1.02 − 1.06). The eFI-CGA discriminated individuals with an adverse outcome with 75% accuracy (CI: 68%−82%). The eFI-CGA conducted by geriatricians and primary care providers showed comparable characteristics and predictive capacities. Inter-rater intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were high: 0.91 (CI: 0.84 − 0.94) baseline) and 0.87 (CI: 0.80 − 0.92) follow-up. Intra-rater ICCs were excellent 0.95 (CI: 0.92 − 0.97) for geriatricians and 0.93 (CI: 0.89 − 0.96) for primary care providers.ConclusionsFrailty assessment using the eFI-CGA is feasible, valid, and reliable in primary care settings. Background

As people age, they are more likely to develop frailty, a complex, age-associated clinical condition characterized by a reduced ability to withstand or recover from acute stressors [1, 2]. Frailty is associated with many adverse outcomes, including increased healthcare utilization, institutionalization, and mortality [1, 2]. Frailty often interacts with social vulnerability, further complicating healthcare for older adults [3, 4]. Traditionally, frailty management has been the domain of geriatricians, who are trained to address complex geriatric syndromes like frailty and associated care needs. Rapid population ageing requires frailty management be extended, especially to generalist primary care providers.

Frailty can be more effectively managed or even prevented when addressed early [5–7], especially by targeting modifiable factors for deterioration or adverse health events. Primary care providers, including family physicians and nurse practitioners, though positioned at the frontline of care, can find their potential in identifying, managing, and preventing frailty yet to be fully realized, despite the availability of clinical guidelines and innovative models of care [8, 9]. Meaningful progress is most likely when interventions are publicly reimbursed, practical to implement and maintain, and clinically relevant: for example, by integrating them into electronic health records and enabling support from the broader healthcare team [8–10]. This contrasts with interventions that are viewed primarily as administrative tasks, such as audits or seemingly box-ticking exercises.

As a multidimensional diagnostic and therapeutic process, Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) long undergirds specialized geriatric care [11]. It incorporates demographic information and medical data, including the presenting chief complaint, an inventory of current illnesses, past and present medical conditions, family history, and social factors such as education, occupation, and marital status. It also assesses self-rated health, mental health, cognitive decline, communication, nutrition, and evaluates basic, instrumental, and advanced activities of daily living, as well as adequacy of income, housing, and current home care. Additional social questions address power of attorney, code status, caregiver attributes, and assessment of caregiver stress [11, 12]. The CGA is the reference standard for frailty management, supporting evidence-based care planning and decision-making [12]. Recent research has advanced the CGA in primary care [13, 14].

Our group has a long history with both CGA and frailty assessment. In 1994, we described a standardized approach to CGA [15]. An early version of that standardized CGA was used in a randomized controlled trial of CGA in rural, community-dwelling older adults [16]. Over subsequent years, we refined that further, developing a one-page form designed to better recognize common disease presentations in frail older adults with acute illness [17]. In that work, we elaborated its summary as a Frailty Index [18, 19] based on the CGA (FI-CGA). We then began developing an electronic FI-CGA [20].

Items of standard CGA evaluation have been integrated into a FI-CGA, derived from both secondary analyses of research databases and purpose-built tools for CGA settings [21, 22]. The FI-CGA score can quantify the level of frailty and associated risks and care needs. Previous studies have validated the FI-CGA, compared its performance with other frailty scores, or used it as benchmark to test other tools [23].

The electronic Frailty Index (eFI), derived from routinely collected electronic health records data has notably advanced frailty screening in primary care. The original eFI automatically generated frailty scores from 36 deficits mapped to more than 2,000 diagnostic codes and has been validated in millions of community-dwelling older adults [24]. Multiple later studies from countries including the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, and the United States have confirmed that eFI can reliably identify frailty and predict adverse outcomes [25–27]. However, eFI performance can be limited by the availability and quality of electronic health records data, which primarily include demographics, laboratory tests, and diagnoses [24–27]. Ambiguity persists about its usefulness as a latent measure of health [28]. The FI-CGA shows promise for improving clinical acceptance by leveraging the eFI with reference standardized inputs [23, 25].

Digital translation of the CGA and FI-CGA addresses the challenges of limited evidence-based frailty tools validated in primary care. We have integrated the electronic CGA and the electronic Frailty Index based on CGA (eFI-CGA) software tools into several electronic health records and other platforms [23, 29, 30]. For community settings, the paper CGA form, designed by a co-author (Dr. Kenneth Rockwood) and commonly used in hospital settings, was adapted with minor modifications, including the addition of vaccination status, exercise habits, alcohol use, and smoking. The CGA items were grouped into categories (cognition, emotion, motivation, communication, sleep, pain, exercise, strength, balance, mobility, nutrition, daily living function, events, and support) to facilitate easy navigation. Using a standard procedure, an eFI-CGA score is automatically calculated for each session of the electronic CGA [20, 30].

Still, the gap to be addressed is how well primary care providers can use the electronic CGA and eFI-CGA. Here, our primary aim was to evaluate the feasibility, validity, and reliability of the electronic CGA and eFI-CGA when administered by primary care providers in community-dwelling older adults. We compared the CGA performed by primary care providers and geriatricians and assessed the ability of the eFI-CGA in predicting adverse outcomes. A secondary aim was to examine the characteristics of the eFI-CGA in relation to the Clinical Frailty Scale, a multidimensional, judgment-based tool used to summarize CGA results or as a screening instrument preceding full CGA [31, 32].

Methods

Patient participants

Community-dwelling Canadian adults aged 65 and over who were under the care of the Fraser Health Authority (Fraser Health) or Nova Scotia Health (NS Health) and screened by their treating family physician as mildly to moderate frailty using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), classified as Managing Well; Living with Very Mild Frailty; Living with Mild Frailty; Moderately Frail [31, 32], were recruited following invitations from their family physicians who participated in the study.

Physician assessors

Physician assessors were recruited through the collaborative Community Action and Resources Empowering Seniors (CARES) Initiative between Fraser Health and NS Health. Interested primary care providers participated in educational sessions focusing on frailty and the CGA. All physician assessors were provided with opportunities for hands-on review and practice of the CGA and eFI-CGA methods, with their questions addressed prior to data collection.

CGA evaluation and eFI-CGA automation

The eFI-CGA software package with electronic CGA interface was embedded in multiple electronic health records (EHRs; e.g., Intrahealth Profile, Telus Wolf, Telus, Med Access). Patient participants were assessed by primary care providers through in-person or virtual interviews. Independent assessments by geriatricians were conducted within four weeks of the initial assessment for the first 100 patients who consented. Six months post-baseline, both primary care providers and geriatricians independently reassessed these patients.

CGA data, including both raw physician input and coded values (Supplementary Table S1) were captured, and used to calculate automated eFI-CGA scores [20, 30].

Having completed the CGA, primary care providers and geriatricians independently assigned CFS scores, grading frailty on a scale from 1 (very fit) to 7 (living with severe frailty; 8 and 9 are for terminally ill) [32], blinded to each other’s evaluations.

Participants’ medication histories and medical conditions were obtained from EHRs. Documented medication and care notes were incorporated into subsequent evaluations.

The extent to which research personnel were required to assist in completing the CGA was recorded.

Data quality assurance

Quality checking occurred concurrently with data collection to retrieve all missing values (if any) from the original assessment sources. All data were entered into a standalone eFI-CGA tool [20], ensuring accuracy and consistency across assessment platforms.

Outcome measures

Participants were followed for three years. Incidental adverse events, e.g., death, institutionalization, and initiation of palliative care were documented along with the date of each event, linked to routine hospital care and service records. Outcomes were analyzed in terms of mortality and a combined measure of adverse events, encompassing death, institutionalization, and receipt of palliative care.

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and related characteristics, using means and standard deviations (sd) or frequencies and percentages for continuous or categorical variables. Group comparisons used independent Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test and chi-square test as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) strength was graded as moderate (0.30–0.50), strong (0.51–0.70), and very strong (0.71–1.0) [33], with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained via percentile bootstrap resampling with 5000 iterations [34]. EFA was conducted on 30 electronic CGA items. Factor retention was guided by multiple criteria, including eigenvalues > 1.0, scree plot inspection, and clinical interpretability of the resulting factors, consistent with recommended best EFA practices [35, 36]. Cohen’s effect size (ES) tested scores between baseline and follow-up, as small (≤ 0.2), medium (0.5), and large (≥ 0.8) [37]. Reliability was considered as poor (ICC < 0.50), moderate (0.51–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), and excellent (> 0.90) [38]. Logistic regression odds ratios were used to assess the risk associated with individual CGA items. Time to adverse events analysis used Cox models, reporting proportional hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. Kaplan–Meier curves (KM) illustrated differences in event-free home living, striating levels of eFI-CGA (< 0.2, 0.2–0.35, > 0.35) and CFS (≤ 4, 5, ≥ 6). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess the discriminative performance of the scores for individuals with different outcomes [39].

Fraser Health participants who withdrew prior to study completion were excluded; otherwise, all participants were included. Analyses were performed for the entire sample, geriatrician subset, and by sex and study site. Additional sensitivity analyses examined score variations with assessment intervals and virtual vs. in-person settings. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26 and programs developed with Matlab version 2021a. Significance level was p < 0.05. See Appendix A for additional methods and Appendix B for the list of acronyms used.

Results

Feasibility

Of 281 patients enrolled from April 2019, 271 (96.4%) completed the initial CGA by their primary care providers (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S1). Participants were most often females, in their seventh to ninth decade, and well educated (Table 1). Electronic CGA assessments were made by 63 primary care providers, including 34 physicians and 16 nurse practitioners from 19 Fraser Health clinics, and 10 physicians and 3 nurse practitioners from 3 NS Health clinics (Fig. 1).

Additionally, 10 geriatricians (5 from each site at 5 clinics) independently assessed 98 participants at baseline and 81 at follow-up reassessments (Fig. 1). Participants assessed by both primary care providers and geriatricians tended to be slightly older, better educated, more likely to experience cognitive decline (Table 1).

Research staff coordinated assessment scheduling and setting, conducted EHR record retrieval and data organization and quality control, and assisted with software tool training. In certain Fraser Health primary care clinics, due to the COVID-19–related physical distancing policies, research nurses assisted with virtual completion of the CGA form for primary care provider review and validation. In the remaining Fraser Health primary care clinics, as well as in all Fraser Health geriatric medicine clinics and all NS Health clinics, patient assessments were completed by physicians in collaboration with their clinical teams, without research staff involvement.

Most primary care providers completed most of the electronic CGA items; the median completion rate was 98.2% and 37.2% completed 100% before audit. The lowest-scoring physician completed 85% of required items before audit; items most often showed missing values required measurements (Supplementary Tables S2). Clock data on timing proved difficult to complete as most physician respondents rounded time to 5- and 10-minute intervals. Electronic record times ranged from 30 to 100 minutes (with intermediate pulses: median = 45; Inter Quartile Range 39 − 51). Physician estimated the duration of a baseline assessment session to range from 25 to 60 minutes, with an average of 45 minutes. Assessors also reported that follow-up evaluations were “shorter” to “much shorter”, with a time saving of up to 20 minutes.

Validity

The eFI-CGA scores ranged from 0.07 to 0.67 (mean = 0.28 ± 0.12). The eFI-CGA and CFS scores were closely correlated (r = 0.65, 95% CIs = 0.56 − 0.72); the age − eFI-CGA correlation (r) was 0.22 (95% CIs = 0.10 − 0.34; Fig. 2). Similar relationships were observed for the geriatrician assessments (Supplementary Fig. S2). A higher mean eFI-CGA was seen in participants with MCI or dementia than in those without cognitive impairment (Table 1).

The eFI-CGA exhibited a four-factor structure, with 75% of the input variables loading substantially. Although additional factors had eigenvalues > 1.0, these did not yield clinically interpretable or stable constructs and therefore were not retained. The rotation sum of squared loadings for each factor were as follows: function/strength/mobility (0.46 − 0.87), emotion/social/sleep (0.51 − 0.62), cognition (0.65 − 0.67), and physical/medical problems (0.45 − 0.65). Still, the remaining CGA items contributed to the total variance (Supplementary Fig. S3). Notable cross-loadings were observed for variables such as independent walking (function and physical/medical problems) and bathing (emotion and cognition).

At three years, 48 participants had died, another 17 were institutionalized or with palliative care, and 33 had dropped out, leaving 173 (63.8%) participants remained alive at one’s own home (Fig. 1). Compared to event-free participants, those with adverse outcomes had higher mean eFI-CGA (0.26 ± 0.12 vs. 0.35 ± 0.11; p < 0.001) and CFS (3.6 ± 1.2 vs. 4.4 ± 1.2; p < 0.001). Deficits in individual electronic CGA items were positively associated with the outcomes, with statistically significant odds ratios (p < 0.05) observed for 48% of items in relation to death and 45% in relation to combined adverse events (Supplementary Table S2).

The eFI-CGA and CFS showed dose-response relationships with the risk of death and the combined adverse outcome (Fig. 3): Hazard ratio (HR) = 1.04 (95% CI = 1.02 − 1.06) for each 0.01 eFI-CGA increase; OR = 1.42 (95% CI = 1.16 − 1.72) for each category of CFS increase, adjusted for age, sex, education, and study site (Table 2; Supplementary Table S3). The scores discriminated individuals who experienced an adverse outcome (AUC = 0.75 with eFI-CGA; AUC = 0.72 with CFS; p’s < 0.001), higher than with age (Fig. 3). Discrimination was consistent for scores by geriatrician and primary care providers (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Reliability at baseline and at six months

Inter-rater: The inter-rater ICC for eFI-CGA scores between primary care providers and geriatricians was 0.91 at baseline (95% Cl = 0.86 − 0.94) and 0.87 at follow-up (0.80 − 0.92). Inter-rater agreement for CFS was also significant, with ICC = 0.78 (0.67 − 0.85) at baseline and 0.76 (0.60 − 0.84) at follow-up (Table 3). The eFI-CGA scores between geriatrician and primary care providers were closely related (Supplementary Fig. S5); mean difference was 0.01 ± 0.07 at baseline (range: -0.16 to 0.20) and 0.00 ± 0.08 (range: -0.24 to 0.18) at follow-up. Most electronic CGA items (saving some sensory and intimate personal care items) individually showed good to excellent ICC between geriatrician and PCP assessments, with inter-rater ICC > 0.50, p < 0.002; Supplementary Table S4). For the CFS, the mean difference scores between geriatricians and primary care providers were 0.13 ± 0.97 at baseline and 0.15 ± 0.96 at follow-up (range = -2 to 2 at each time point).

Discussion

We conducted a prospective cohort study with three-year follow-up of vital and residential status to determine whether computer-aided CGA evaluation with automated frailty index calculator might be feasible, valid, and reliable for use in primary care. We examined the characteristics, construct, predictive values, and reliabilities of the eFI-CGA and CFS.

The primary care provider-conducted eFI-CGA reflected well-established features of the frailty index, including its characteristic distribution pattern, an appropriate upper limit, and a modest correlation with age [1, 2]. In addition, the four-factor eFI-CGA structure confirmed recent reports of multidimensional frailty characterization of clinical [35] and biological data [40, 41]. The eFI-CGA and CFS by primary care providers and geriatricians were closely correlated. Critically, both scores effectively graded risks and discriminated between individuals with and without adverse outcomes.

Notably, across two geographically distinct study sites, over two-dozen clinics and seventy healthcare providers, we observed excellent agreement between primary care providers and geriatricians for the eFI-CGA, underscoring its reliability for CGA based frailty assessment in primary care. The CFS also exhibited strong primary care provider-geriatrician agreement. As a judgment-based instrument, the CFS complements the algorithmic eFI-CGA by allowing clinicians to interpret frailty status holistically. Although some benefit of holistic judgment has been observed in acute care, here the CFS did not translate to better discrimination (Fig. 3) [42]. While the CFS provides rapid categorical risk assessment, the eFI-CGA offers more precise quantification of frailty and identifies the contributing deficits, supporting patient-centered care planning. Clinicians may use the CFS for frialty screening, but the eFI-CGA is recommended for comprehensive assessment and risk stratification.

Most individual CGA items showed good agreement between primary care providers and geriatricians, as well as between assessments by the same physician type. A 2014 report noted a type of “wisdom of the crowd” in this regard: even though several important items covered in the CGA were not associated with mortality, aggregated in the frailty index, they proved predictive [43].

The generalizability of a bicoastal convenience sample of Canadian clinics might well be limited. For example, here participants aged < 70 years were less healthy and likely selected due to frailty (Fig. 2). Further work, including large-scale clinical trials is warranted. Although feasibility was an objective, being assessed by completion rate and time, the former proved more durable. This reflects too that physicians often fit the extra work in around scheduled visits and sometimes were interrupted. With most having moved/moving to digital data collection, accuracy of such estimates could improve.

The COVID-19 pandemic with physical distancing policies inevitably affected our work. Virtual assessments were conducted in some Fraser Health clinics, which had enrolled patient participants pre-pandemic; NS Health assessments, being post-pandemic, were conducted in-person. Although analyses with site stratification and adjustment showed no significant differences, we recommend using identical assessment methods where feasible. Further research to elucidate the merits and shortcomings of virtual verses in-person CGAs in frailty management is warranted. Studies investigating primary care provider and geriatrician eFI-CGA score trajectories are also needed.

Our reliability tests were based predominantly on NS Health data, where physicians (rather than research nurses as in some Fraser Health primary care clinics) performed all assessments. Whatever the bias from this, the inclusion of multiple study sites enriched our findings, possibly enhancing generalizability. The observed site-wise variations provide valuable insights to scaling the CGA application.

This is early effort to validate a primary care tool for frailty assessment and management. As older adults, including those experiencing frailty and multiple health conditions, commonly prefer aging in place, primary care plays a key role in their medical care. Referral to geriatric specialty services remains standard practice for frailty management in Canada, where a CGA is conducted and results communicated back to primary care. By demonstrating good feasibility, validity, and reliability of the electronic CGA and eFI-CGA in primary care, this study represents a critical step forward toward early identification and management.

Primary care providers are advantaged in managing frailty because they are familiar with the full spectrum of their patients’ health factors, facilitating the development of individualized care plans [44]. Frailty management could thus begin without waiting for specialty referrals, through interventions such as exercise coaching, nutritional support, social prescribing, and other evidence-based programs [5–9]. This work supports integrated frailty care and prevention within multidisciplinary teams, which can also optimize the use of geriatric specialty by prioritizing referrals and tailored interventions, and inform health system innovation in policy, resource allocation, and training [3, 8, 45].

It is important to note that engaging primary care providers in promoting early management of frailty does not override the role of geriatricians. Instead, primary care providers may prioritize needs-based referrals for geriatric consultation, thereby allowing geriatricians to focus on more complex cases. In our data, geriatricians identified a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia (Table 1; Supplementary Table S2) and assessed patients across a wider range of eFI-CGA scores (Supplementary Fig. S5-a) compared with primary care providers, although these differences did not always reach statistical significance. We emphasize that geriatricians continue to play an irreplaceable role in the care of older adults, particularly in managing complex, multimorbid, and advanced frailty cases.

This study builds on work that uses deficit-accumulation eFI from EHR data of various quantity and quality levels. Even though it inherited basic characteristics of any deficit accumulation frailty index, the eFI-CGA is not merely another in the host of eFIs that are appearing especially since the United States Centers for Medicare and Medicaid release of their Age Friendly Hospital initiative. Rather, building specifically from a reference-standard CGA, as noted in the Introduction, this eFI-CGA is long in the making, and rests on far more than diagnostic codes. Physicians can be confident in this type of frailty assessment and its clinical relevance [25, 28, 46–48]. In short, the eFI-CGA aims to be a digital equivalent of the CGA, i.e., to have more in common with CGA than eFI for digital translation of the frailty index. As discussed, the eFI-CGA preserves the clinical meaning of CGA domains, but benefits from an automated summary score. Furthermore, multimodality FI-CGA has demonstrated superior predictive validity compared with generalized measures [23, 49, 50]. In our study, the ≥ 0.75 AUC of the eFI-CGA for discriminating individuals with adverse outcomes aligns with prior findings using the FI-CGA [23, 49, 50].

The eFI-CGA leverages the digital frailty method for reference-standard CGAs for clinically evident deficits [13, 16]. This undertaking contributes to sustainable, time-efficient, and cost-effective care in family medicine. As electronic CGA and eFI-CGA tools become increasingly accessible across multiple EHRs, as well as online/mobile, and other platforms [51] to support primary care providers (e.g., https://efi-cga.ca), they further reduce technological barriers in real-world deployment. Further research is needed to evaluate implementation across diverse healthcare settings and populations, and to assess the impact on clinical workflow, patient outcomes, and cost-effectiveness.

Conclusions

An electronic CGA and eFI-CGA in primary care were feasible and demonstrated excellent comparability with a geriatrician reference standard. The eFI-CGA and the CFS effectively discriminated adverse outcomes. Results highlight the clinical translational potential of a valid and reliable tool to facilitate early management of frailty in “geriatrizing” primary care. While geriatricians continue to play an irreplaceable role in frailty care, their expertise may be best leveraged by focusing on complex cases and need-based consultations. Future research is warranted to scale up electronic CGA and eFI-CGA applications and better integrate multidimensional frailty care as a standard practice at the first point of contact for older adults.
