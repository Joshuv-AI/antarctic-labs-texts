Exploring AI Driven Autonomous Vehicles as Mobile Health Units to Expand Access to Healthcare in Underserved Communities
Authors: Adewunmi Akingbola, Adegbesan Abiodun, Olajide Ojo, Otumara Urowoli Jessica, Uthman Hassan Alao, Ademola Aiyenuro, Petra Mariaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccess to quality healthcare remains a persistent challenge in many low- and middle-income countries, especially for rural and underserved populations. This paper explores the transformative potential of autonomous vehicles (AVs) as mobile health units, proposing a new model for healthcare delivery that leverages emerging transportation technology. We examine how AVs can be equipped with diagnostic tools, telemedicine capabilities, and essential medical supplies to bridge existing gaps in health access. Drawing from current use cases in mobile clinics and early AV deployments, we analyze the opportunities and limitations of this approach through a public health lens. Key barriers such as infrastructure deficits, digital inequity, policy uncertainty, and ethical concerns are critically discussed. The paper also provides targeted recommendations to policymakers, health institutions, and private sector stakeholders on how to advance AV-based healthcare solutions in an inclusive and sustainable manner. By integrating technology with health equity goals, AVs offer a novel and scalable path toward universal health coverage, particularly in contexts where traditional service delivery falls short. This work contributes to the growing conversation on how innovation can reshape public health systems in the 21st century.Keywords: Autonomous vehicles, Mobile health units, Health equity, Digital infrastructure, Public health innovation, Access to care.

Introduction

Access to healthcare includes the availability, accessibility, affordability, and acceptability of health services [1]. Achieving access to healthcare for all is an international global goal, which is further reinforced by the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG-3) -To ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages [2]. However, global disparities in health care access, particularly in rural or underserved areas lead to marginalized areas having the worst health outcome [2]. Global disparities in healthcare access refers to significant differences in health status, access to health services, and health outcomes in different countries and social groups [3]. These inequalities are influenced by social determinants of health, such as poverty, education, education, employment, living environment and gender or ethnic based discrimination [3].

Transportation plays an important role in shaping public health outcomes especially in rural areas and transportation related factors contribute enormously to avoidable mortalities and a substantial burden of disease [4, 5]. According to the World Health organization (WHO) in 2016, 1.4 million deaths were due to motor vehicle crashes globally [6, 7]. In 2019, 6.6 million deaths were attributed to ambient air pollution and traffic related air pollution accounted for one-fifth of these fatalities in countries like the United Kingdom, the United States and Germany [6, 7]. Traffic contaminants, traffic related stress, lack of active travel, physical inactivity and greenhouse gases are a few detrimental effects related to the transportation which could lead to worsened health outcomes and premature deaths [7].

Autonomous vehicles therefore present an opportunity to transform transportation networks and consequently, impact public health positively [7, 8]. Autonomous vehicles utilize sensorial technologies such as computer vision, lasers, GPS, odometry to navigate and these technologies are therefore used to determine environment and location [9]. AVs can avert deadly crashes, provide important mobility to the elderly and disabled, increase road capacity, save fuel as well as lower emissions [8, 9]. However, despite the numerous benefits, identifying the impacts and extent is required to govern autonomous vehicles and prevent the unintended negative consequences of this technology which are related to safety, mobility, legal regulatory and social issues [8, 9].

Although autonomous vehicles as mobile health units may reduce traffic-related air pollution, their environmental impact potentially extends beyond operation. The production, use, and disposal of AV batteries and electronic hardware could generate significant waste, including toxic metals and non-recyclable components [10, 11]. If not properly managed, this could introduce new environmental health hazards, particularly in underserved communities where waste management infrastructure is limited. Therefore, to ensure sustainability, life cycle assessments of AV components, adoption of recyclable or low-toxicity materials, and responsible battery recycling programs are essential for use in these autonomous vehicles [12].

Artificial intelligence is an important tool for enormous AV functionality [13]. AI algorithms play an important role to improve the performance of AV and these roles range from perception, decision making, localization and mapping so as to improve the performance of AV [13]. AI algorithms influence various stages of autonomous vehicles development ranging from the initial coding to the post-deployment maintenance and some of the benefits of AI in this process include safety, efficient traffic flow, easy accessibility, energy savings, productivity and convenience [14, 15]. Ethical dilemmas, user privacy, continuous monitoring and adaptation and public trust and acceptance pose some of the challenges to autonomous vehicles acceptance [14, 15]. Therefore, the success of autonomous vehicles therefore requires balancing their potential benefits with addressing the challenges through collaborative efforts in technological development and regulation.

This commentary aims to explore the public health potential of AI-powered autonomous vehicles as mobile health units, highlighting gaps in existing healthcare infrastructure, especially regarding transportation and reach. Additionally, this commentary addresses challenges such as technical and infrastructural barriers, privacy and algorithm bias and equity risks as well as proposed opportunities for pilot programs and recommend regulatory frameworks for safety, liability, and clinical quality assurance.

The Public Health Case for AV-Based Mobile Health Units

One of the targets outlined, in 2015, in Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG3) was universal healthcare coverage [16]. Unfortunately, more than a decade later, inequitable access to healthcare remains a global problem. The gaps which exist in access are not limited only to low-and-middle income countries, nor rural and poorer communities within those same countries, but are also seen to exist in high-income countries, where there are significant underserved populations who face significantly worse health outcomes when compared to the wider population [17–19]. A 2021 study by the National Center for Health Statistics reported that in 2019, rural areas of the United States had a 20% higher death rate than urban areas [20]. In both LMIC, and high-income countries alike, the disparities which exist can often be linked to a few key contributing factors. Geographic inaccessibility is one key barrier that exists in both contexts. In sub-Saharan countries, 29% of people, and 28.2% of women and children were found to be located more than 2 h away from the nearest hospital [21]. In Brazil’s rural northeast, over 60% of people are farther than 10 km from the nearest Hospital or Emergency care unit [22]. This distance in combination with often poor road infrastructure, and sparse public transport facilities further exacerbates the issues. In high-income countries, for example, the United States, rural residents face the same barriers [23]. These barriers correlate strongly with lower healthcare utilization, worse chronic disease management outcomes, and delays in receiving ordinary preventative, and emergency medical care [18]. Indeed, the American Hospital Association reports that over 130 rural hospitals were closed between 2010 and 2020 further widening this divide [24].

One more major challenge contributing to such disparities is the lack of health care workers. WHO projects that by 2030, there will be a global shortfall of 10 million healthcare workers, with the greatest deficiencies experienced by LMICs [25]. However, even in well-resourced settings, health care workers, and specialists tend to concentrate in urban environments, creating inequities to access for populations who live in more remote or underserved regions [19, 20]. These access issues compounded by systemic inequities also disproportionately affect racial and ethnic minorities. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, although Black and non-Hispanic Black individuals accounted for only 13% of the U.S. population, they represented 17% of COVID-19-related deaths highlighting how structural and workforce-related inequities can contribute to worse health outcomes [26]. These challenges when considered together demonstrate the failure of our current healthcare delivery models to equitably serve all populations. It highlights the need for innovative, adaptable, health delivery platforms - autonomous vehicle based mobile health units could offer a real, scalable solution to the above issues and help advance strides towards our goal of universal healthcare coverage. AV-MHUs can provide point-of-care testing for infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, early treatment initiation, and adherence monitoring, reducing prevalence and transmission. Similarly, maternal mortality, frequently over 400 deaths per 100,000 live births, is driven by limited antenatal care and delayed recognition of complications [27, 28]; these mobile units can deliver risk screening, prenatal check-ups, and referral services, improving outcomes. Furthermore, AV-MHUs can support chronic disease management and childhood immunizations, mitigating morbidity and bridging access gaps in underserved populations.

One of the most immediate contributions autonomous vehicles could bring to the landscape of healthcare inequity is in bridging the physical gap that exists between remotely located patients, and care providers. Travel time, poor road infrastructure, and poor or non-existent public transport links are often cited as significant barriers to receiving care in LMICs and High-income countries alike [15, 21, 23]. In these environments, autonomous mobile health units may operate either on demand, or via pre-scheduled routes bringing diagnostics, or consultations directly to these hard-to-reach populations [29]. AV based mobile clinics may also mitigate against some of the effects faced by a dwindling healthcare workforce - equipping the vehicles with embedded diagnostic AI tools and telehealth capabilities will allow basic services to be delivered without the constant presence of a doctor or nurse. For instance, autonomous vans equipped with AI-powered triage systems or portable labs could allow remote screening for conditions such as tuberculosis, diabetes, or cervical cancer. In this way, only more complex cases would be referred to, for in-person care. This decentralized model also reduces clinician burnout and optimizes resource use by concentrating on human healthcare workers where they are most needed, while allowing routine services to be delivered autonomously or semi-autonomously.

Marginalized populations, including migrants, people experiencing homelessness, and those with disabilities, also often face systemic exclusion from mainstream health systems. AV-based platforms could reach these groups more effectively by removing the need for fixed addresses, documentation, or extensive travel [30, 31]. Mobile AV health units can operate flexibly in urban or peri-urban environments, delivering medications, monitoring chronic conditions, or providing mental health outreach through remote consultations. In addition, AVs designed with universal accessibility in mind can dramatically improve mobility for the elderly and disabled groups that often face transportation challenges due to physical or cognitive limitations. By offering door-to-door transport or on-site healthcare delivery, AVs can enhance autonomy, reduce social isolation, and improve access to preventive and routine care for these populations. Despite progress in healthcare technology, access disparities persist globally. AV based mobile health units present a flexible, innovative platform for closing these gaps. By integrating AI, telemedicine, and mobility, they offer a potentially promising solution for underserved populations, particularly in remote, resource-constrained, or marginalized settings. Pilot projects involving AVs in the logistics of both hospital and last-mile delivery have emerged in countries like the United States, United Kingdom, and Thailand. However, there remains a notable scarcity of pilot programs exploring the utilization of AVs and autonomous, community-facing units. This gap in implementation may be linked to public perceptions of AVs in some communities, AV adoption rates, or other logistical and regulatory concerns. Nevertheless, it represents both a challenge and an opportunity to be explored - where, if addressed strategically, meaningful strides could be made towards advancing universal healthcare coverage.

Challenges and Ethical Considerations

Overcoming Technical and Infrastructural Constraints in AV-Enabled Healthcare Delivery.

AI is changing the healthcare industry by improving the effectiveness, efficiency, and patient-centeredness of decision-support systems. In addition to improving the way medical professionals work with data, these AI-powered tools are also helping to monitor and support patients, which will ultimately raise the standard of healthcare delivery. The use of autonomous vehicles (AVs) as mobile health units has enormous potential to close the gaps in healthcare access in underprivileged communities [5]. But there are some fundamental obstacles to this innovation, chief among them being the infrastructure and technical limitations that may prevent successful deployment. First, internet access is still a major obstacle, particularly in low-income and rural areas. The adoption of next-generation technologies is hampered by the digital divide, which is made worse by limited broadband access and digital unaffordability, as noted by [37]. To operate effectively, AVs with AI-driven diagnostic tools and telehealth interfaces require dependable, fast internet; otherwise, service delivery is dispersed or completely unavailable [38].

Second, there are additional restrictions related to digital interoperability and road infrastructure. According to Iyanna et al. [39], One of the biggest obstacles to technology-enabled care is a lack of infrastructure, which includes issues like uneven digital tool deployment across organizations and poor integration between health IT systems. When used in regions with poor roads or without interoperable medical databases, AVs may experience interruptions, data entry mistakes, or system malfunctions that jeopardize the promptness and quality of care [39]. Technical issues are further exacerbated by system security and reliability threats. Healthcare workers identified common issues as limited cross-platform compatibility, old hardware, and frequent system outages [39]. If these problems are repeated in AV-based units, patient confidence may be damaged, and care continuity may be strained. In essence, even if AVs present great potential to increase healthcare access, their success depends on addressing fundamental infrastructure issues. Not only is consistent connectivity, strong IT systems, and physical infrastructure a logistical concern, but they also define equity in digital health delivery.

Ethical Considerations in AI-Driven Health Services: Privacy, Consent, and Algorithmic Fairness

As digital technology and data-driven approaches expand in the healthcare sector, the protection of data privacy has emerged as a paramount issue. The broad implementation of electronic health records (EHRs), telemedicine, and artificial intelligence (AI) has resulted in a tremendous amount of sensitive patient data collection, storage, and transmission [40]. This rapid increase in data applications presents substantial privacy, security, and ethical challenges [41–43]. Data breaches may have extensive effects on healthcare organizations and individuals. Protected Health Information exposure may result in identity theft, illicit activities, and loss of trust built between patients and healthcare professionals. As there is more digitization of health information, these platforms become more vulnerable to cyberattacks, and therefore, the necessity of having strong cybersecurity measures to protect patient information from harmful vulnerabilities is more critical than ever [44, 45]. The application of artificial intelligence in healthcare also has significant implications for data privacy, as these technologies rely on sensitive patient information. Informed consent and transparency regarding data use are essential to maintain patient trust and adhere to privacy regulations. Informed consent can be facilitated through intuitive digital interfaces that clearly communicate the purpose, scope, and potential risks of data collection and clinical assessments [46]. For example, multimedia tools, such as videos, interactive prompts, and simplified language can guide patients through consent forms, while confirmation mechanisms (e.g., electronic signatures or biometric verification) ensure understanding and actual consent [47, 48]. Effective communication and ethical data stewardship are crucial in mitigating these risks [49, 50].

Patient-generated health data (PGHD) from mobile apps and wearables empowers patients to take charge of their health, but it also raises significant privacy and security concerns. Fears of identity theft may discourage patients from sharing information, highlighting the need for enhanced protective measures and public education. Current legislation, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), provides essential protections, but it must be updated to keep pace with advancements in digital health technology. Ongoing collaboration among policymakers, healthcare professionals, and tech entrepreneurs is necessary to safeguard privacy while fostering innovation in artificial intelligence [50, 51].

Furthermore, regarding algorithm bias and fairness, AI diagnostic systems often rely on datasets that overrepresent high-income or majority populations, which can reduce the accuracy for underrepresented groups and risk misdiagnosis or delayed care, especially for racial and ethnic minorities [52]. Factors like skin tone, genetics, and social determinants can further affect AI performance [53]. Without addressing these biases, mobile health units may reinforce health disparities rather than alleviate them. Therefore, public health implementation requires using diverse training data, validating models across varied populations, transparent reporting of subgroup performance, and community involvement in the AI design [53–55]. These steps are essential to ensure AI-enabled health tools serve marginalized populations equitably.

Equity Challenges: Ensuring AV-Enabled Health Services Reach the Digitally and Economically Marginalized

An equal opportunity for people to gain from the knowledge and practices associated with the creation and application of digital technologies to enhance health is known as digital health equity.

One of the tenets of the World Health Organization’s global strategy on digital health 2020–2025 is promoting appropriate use of digital technologies without leaving anyone behind, thereby guaranteeing digital health equity [56]. The digital determinants of health are defined by Crawford and Serhal’s Digital Health Equity Framework (DHEF) [56] as follows: [1] people’s access to digital resources; [2] their use of these resources for health seeking; [3] their digital health literacy; [4] their beliefs about the potential benefits or drawbacks of digital health care; [5] their cultural preferences and values regarding the use of digital resources; and [6] the integration of digital resources into community and health infrastructure. Finding and addressing possible gaps in digital determinants is necessary to increase the likelihood of attaining digital health equity, especially from the viewpoint of individuals who run the risk of being shut out of digital services.

To lessen digital inequality and boost the use of technology and digital services, mitigation techniques like expanding physical access, digital skills, and social support, as well as enhancing digital remote support infrastructures, have been suggested in the past [57, 58]. Furthermore, the significance of hybrid strategies, which combine online and offline tactics and incorporate both high- and low-tech viewpoints, has been emphasized [59]. Several development areas have been proposed in order to enhance the equitable accessibility of digital health services, considering the difficulties that vulnerable groups have been found to face. Better usability of digital services, as well as people’s potential to gain from them, should be the main goals of development.

To envision and achieve a seamless and healthier future through digital innovation, it is imperative to increase clinical and field research, invest more in evidence-based research, and secure the support of multiple stakeholders [60]. Governments and their partners should give digital health top priority in Universal Health Coverage (UHC) initiatives because of its revolutionary potential to achieve health goals. In addition to other significant UHC investment areas, this entails prioritizing greater investments in digitally enabled health systems. Governments should develop and implement digital health programs while involving partners and communities to guarantee alignment with UHC goals [61]. These strategies also need to address issues of connectivity, particularly in underserved and rural areas where a lack of broadband access exacerbates health disparities [62].

Future Directions and Policy Implications

As part of transformative technology to strengthen healthcare access, Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) are reshaping healthcare service delivery and the supply chain for health commodities [5]. In support of these transformative changes, Universal Health Coverage (UHC) in underserved communities need not be left behind to benefit from the opportunities AVs pose. As such, leveraging multiple drivers for healthcare access should be prioritized. However, these drivers are linked to service value and quality healthcare [63]. In underserved communities where healthcare access is tied to different drivers, among which are poverty and geographical location, the use of AVs will forge multiple opportunities that will not only make healthcare accessible but will also proffer opportunities for health innovations and provide avenues for Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) to strengthen health outcomes [64, 65].

Hence, to harmonize PPP approaches, there is a need for supportive policies to strengthen collaboration in the health sector and generate political will to leverage the service value of healthcare innovations, which in this case is the use of AVs, especially in the underserved communities [66]. While ensuring the enactment of workable policies and strong political will, PPP will strengthen the actualization of this approach, in leading implementation, resource pooling, collaboration and infrastructural development [67]. This will help to support the booming opportunities in the use of AI in healthcare. AVs will serve as a pipeline to ensure healthcare service delivery reaches everyone in underserved communities without compromise or discrimination.

Political commitment is essential to translate the promise of autonomous vehicle–enabled healthcare into practical implementation. Governments play a central role in establishing regulatory frameworks for autonomous mobility, integrating AV-mobile health units into national health systems, and aligning them with universal health coverage (UHC) strategies. In many low-resource settings, supportive policies can incentivize investment through tax relief, innovation grants, and infrastructure development that facilitate deployment in remote regions. Political leadership is also crucial for coordinating cross-sector collaboration among health ministries, transport authorities, technology firms, and local governments. By embedding AV initiatives within national digital health and primary care strategies, policymakers can ensure sustainability, accountability, and equitable distribution of services.

Regulatory Frameworks for Safety, Liability, and Clinical Quality Assurance

AVs success cannot be achieved without carefully developing workable frameworks for implementing AVs in underserved communities. Among these is the Autonomous Driving risk Prediction Model based on Artificial Intelligence Technology [68]. At Waymo (an SAE Level 4 Automated Driving System (ADS)), has been proven to be efficient in ensuring efficiency and safety and reducing fatality [69, 70]. The frame further developed a Case Credibility Assessment structure that leverages the credibility of safety and evidence to monitor occurrences on which the risk prediction is being improved upon. This opportunity will be a game changer in healthcare access, as it will not only envision a quality service delivery, but it will also provide an avenue to improve UHC without leaving anyone behind, including people living in underserved communities [71]. For instance, when a misdiagnosis or clinical error is detected, the system flags the event for review by a qualified clinician, triggering corrective actions and documentation. Ethically, this ensures patient safety and transparency, while legally it aligns with medical liability standards, establishing accountability for AI-assisted decisions. This approach allows autonomous mobile health units to continuously refine risk predictions while safeguarding clinical quality and regulatory compliance.

By leveraging existing frameworks to improve health access using AVs, this approach has been reshaping the transportation sector, and merging this opportunity in the health sector will expand the potential in ensuring health access [5]. Before then, there is a need to effectively set aside clinical quality assurance because of the sensitivity of the health dataset [72]. However, this opportunity does not stop at data quality checks but also creates evidence that describes people’s acceptance and social and behavioral changes that will drive quality data to inform AVs’ improvement in catering for health disparities, especially in underserved communities. These opportunities cannot come to fruition without understanding contextual policies that resonate with individual health needs that promote efficiency [8].

Need for Inclusive Design, Community Engagement, and Continuous Evaluation to Ensure AVs Support, not Widen Health Equity

Having engaged in the efficient way of driving innovation by leveraging PPP, policy recommendations and data quality assurance, using AVs in underserved communities cannot be achieved without inclusivity that drives health equity. Contextually, this is embedded in community engagement and a continuous drive that will support evaluation and ownership without discrimination and prejudices [73]. In underserved communities, health equity has been compromised. Adding AVs will pose a threat to achieving UHC and, as such, a drawback to the 2030 agenda [74]. In this article, we propose an inclusive design, stakeholder engagement and continuous evaluation that will not just support the enactment of AVs policies rather that will also incorporate their implementations for healthcare access.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Recommendations

To unlock the full potential of autonomous vehicles (AVs) as mobile health units, we recommend a multi-layered approach centred on equity, infrastructure, and innovation. First, governments and development partners must prioritize investment in digital infrastructure, particularly in underserved and rural communities where broadband access and road conditions remain major barriers. Without reliable connectivity, the effectiveness of AVs equipped with AI diagnostics or telehealth platforms will be severely compromised. Targeted funding for road upgrades, localized 5G deployment, and mobile health support systems is critical to ensure AVs can reach and operate efficiently in high-need areas. However, it should be noted that the implementation of 5G towers in low-density rural regions presents significant cost implications [75]. Building and maintaining 5G infrastructure may be expensive relative to the small populations served, potentially limiting scalability and sustainability as against traditional mobile clinics that require less infrastructure investment and can flexibly reach dispersed communities, though they lack the AI-driven diagnostic capabilities of AV. Secondly, policies enabling the deployment of AV-based health units must integrate inclusive design principles and be co-created with the communities they aim to serve. This includes engaging local stakeholders in the design, implementation, and evaluation of AV services to ensure cultural relevance, trust, and long-term adoption. Additionally, regulatory bodies should develop clear guidelines for safety, data privacy, liability, and clinical quality, informed by emerging best practices from pilot projects globally. Public-private partnerships, academic institutions, and health ministries should work collaboratively to establish adaptable, evidence-based models that can be replicated across diverse health systems.

Conclusion

Autonomous vehicles represent a promising frontier in the delivery of healthcare services to marginalized and hard-to-reach populations. While the technology holds significant potential, its success depends on addressing key challenges related to infrastructure, digital equity, ethics, and policy. By approaching AV integration with a focus on inclusivity, safety, and sustained community engagement, we can reshape how health systems respond to access gaps, moving closer to a more equitable, connected future of care.
