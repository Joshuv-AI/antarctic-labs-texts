Unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Getie Mihret Aragaw, Nuhamin Tesfa Tsega, Tazeb Alemu Anteneh, Alemneh Tadesse Kassie, Kindu Yinges Wondie, Tadesse Getu, Shambel Dessale Asmamaw, Berihun Agegn Mengistie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnmet need for family planning remains a major challenge worldwide, particularly in Africa, contributing to unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and increased maternal morbidity and mortality. Reducing the unmet need is paramount in improving maternal and newborn health, reducing unintended pregnancies, and preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV. In Africa, many women living with HIV continue to experience unmet need for family planning, and existing studies in the region report highly inconsistent findings. Thus, this study aimed to estimate the combined prevalence of unmet need for family planning and to determine the associated factors among women living with HIV in Africa.MethodsPrimary studies in this systematic review and meta-analysis were retrieved through comprehensive searches in PubMed, ScienceDirect, Research4Life, and Google Scholar. The Joanna Briggs Institute quality assessment tool was used to check the quality of each study. STATA version 17 statistical software was applied to analyze the pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning. The I-squared (I2) statistic was employed to check statistical heterogeneity among the included studies, and publication bias was evaluated using a funnel plot and Egger’s test. A random-effects model was used to estimate the overall prevalence and associated factors of unmet family planning needs.ResultsA total of 40 primary studies with 22,914 study participants were included in this review. The pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning among women living with HIV in Africa was 29.26% (95% CI: 25.54–32.99). The unmet need was associated with younger age, rural residence, having a living child, and unknown partner HIV status. Additionally, women with no formal education, those who did not receive contraceptive counseling, inadequate knowledge of maternal and child health, unfavorable attitudes toward family planning, and shorter duration on antiretroviral therapy had higher odds of unmet need for family planning.ConclusionThis meta-analysis reveals that nearly one in three women living with HIV experiences unmet need for family planning. Strengthening the integration of reproductive health programs and HIV services and addressing the identified barriers are essential to reduce the unmet need and improve maternal health.RegistrationThe protocol for this systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD420251125860).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04435-w.Keywords: Unmet need, Family planning, Women living with HIV, Reproductive-age women, Africa, Systematic review and meta-analysis

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) continues to be a significant public health challenge that has contributed to the morbidity and mortality of infected individuals [1, 2]. Globally, around 40.8 million people were living with HIV in 2024, of which 53% were women and girls. From these, 1.3 million people were newly infected, and 45% of the population are reproductive-age women [1]. Africa remains the continent most severely affected by the pandemic, and a large proportion of reproductive-age women continue to live with HIV in the region [1–4]. Mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) of HIV remains the predominant source of new HIV infection. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), an estimated 80% of children acquire the infection through MTCT during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding [5–7].

Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) empowers individuals and couples to make informed decisions regarding their reproductive lives. It ensures that people can decide on the size of their family, if and when to have children, and experience a safe pregnancy and childbirth. Additionally, it contributes to the health and well-being of their newborns and promotes the right to have safe and satisfying sexual experiences [8, 9]. Family planning is a key element of SRH services, and plays a fundamental role in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [10–12]. Accessing family planning methods and meeting contraceptive needs is beneficial to prevent unintended pregnancies and unsafe abortions, reduce under-five mortality, and ultimately decrease maternal mortality [13, 14]. Effective utilization of modern contraceptive methods can reduce unintended pregnancy, unsafe abortion, and maternal death by approximately 68%, 72%, and 62%, respectively, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [8]. Despite their desire to delay and limit childbearing, approximately 41% of women in LMICs who wish to avoid pregnancy are not using contraceptive methods [15].

Unmet need for family planning refers to reproductive-age women who are fecund, sexually active, and want to limit or space childbirth, but are not using any contraceptive methods [9, 16, 17]. Globally, around 164 million reproductive-age women have an unmet need for family planning, with the highest proportions observed in LMICs, particularly in SSA [8, 18, 19]. Approximately 90% of unintended pregnancies in Africa are from not using effective contraceptives [20]. An unmet need for family planning is associated with adverse maternal and child health outcomes. It has been shown to trigger unwanted pregnancy and its complications [21]. Unintended pregnancy is high among HIV-positive women in Africa, ranging from 41 to 60% [20, 22, 23]. These pregnancies increase the risk of MTCT of HIV, worsen maternal health outcomes, and impose psychological and economic burdens. Concurrent sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in this population exacerbate the transmission and acquisition of HIV and further compromise their immune function [24, 25].

Effective family planning has a major public health importance, particularly for people living with HIV, as it helps in preventing MTCT, reducing unintended pregnancies, and limiting and spacing childbirth. Moreover, the use of dual protection methods simultaneously prevents STIs and unintended pregnancies, promoting sexual and reproductive health, and improving quality of life [26, 27]. Ensuring equitable and broad access to various contraceptive methods and informed choice of contraceptive use are essential measures for preventing unintended pregnancies and eliminating new infant HIV infections globally [7, 28].

However, despite the many benefits of contraceptives, many HIV-positive women in developing countries are encountering unacceptably high unmet need for family planning and experience disproportionately high rates of unintended pregnancy and abortion [7, 29, 30]. Evidence indicates that comprehensive SRH care for women living with HIV is limited, especially in developing countries [25, 31]. In many African settings, a significant proportion of HIV positive women continue to have low levels of contraceptive use. Despite their increased risk of unintended pregnancy and transmission of the virus to their children, many are not accessing or consistently using modern contraceptive methods [32, 33].

To achieve universal access to reproductive healthcare, it is important to secure family planning services and expand access to contraceptives, particularly for people living with HIV. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) plan, the target is to eliminate MTCT of HIV and the unmet need for family planning by 2030 through a rights-based approach that ensures all reproductive-age individuals have access to safe and effective contraceptive methods [34, 35]. Achieving these goals is linked to human rights, gender equality, education, and social and economic empowerment of women [19]. Integrating family planning with HIV care is essential to ensure the delivery of comprehensive reproductive health services and to reduce the unmet need for contraceptives in this population [36, 37]. This approach enhances consistent use of modern contraceptive methods and ultimately decreases the risk of MTCT of HIV [38].

Recognizing and addressing the unmet need for contraception among HIV-positive women is a critical concept for designing family planning programs and improving maternal and child health. Understanding the extent and identifying the contributing factors of unmet need for family planning is important for developing effective SRH interventions aimed at reducing the risk of HIV transmission and preventing unintended pregnancies among women living with HIV. Findings from multiple studies across African settings have demonstrated regional variation in the prevalence of unmet need, reflecting differences in access, awareness, and service delivery. Therefore, the present study aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning and identify associated factors among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa.

Methods

Data source and search strategy

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis of both published and unpublished studies that examined the prevalence of unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa. The result of the study was reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-2020) guidelines [39] (see Additional file 1). The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration No.: CRD420251125860). Studies for this review were systematically searched through electronic databases, including PubMed, Research4Life, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar. This meta-analysis used the condition, context, and population (CoCoPop) framework to formulate the research question and retrieve relevant articles. Systematic and comprehensive search strategies were formulated using Boolean operators (‘‘AND’’ and ‘‘OR’’) to construct the search string for each database. We used the combination of the following search terms to retrieve articles: “unmet needs for family planning”, “unmet need for contraception”, “unmet need for birth limiting”, “unmet need for birth spacing”, “use of family planning”, “contraceptive use”, “fertility desire”, “reproductive age women”, “women living with HIV”, “HIV-positive women”, “HIV-infected women”, “antiretroviral therapy”, “determinants”, “associated factors”, “predictors”, “barriers”, “Africa”, and “Sub-Saharan Africa”. Two authors (GM and NT) independently conducted the database searches, with the last search carried out on September 14, 2025. The full search terms used to retrieve articles in each database are found in the Additional file 2.

Eligibility criteria

All observational studies, such as cross-sectional, case-control, or cohort designs reporting the prevalence and/or associated factors of unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV were included. Primary articles with complete data, reported in the English language, and studies conducted in Africa since 2014 were included in this review. Both published and unpublished studies were also considered in this study.

Systematic reviews, studies with incomplete or inadequate data, inaccessible full texts, qualitative studies, or studies that did not specifically address findings on the unmet need for family planning were excluded from the review.

Outcome measurements

The first outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis was the prevalence of unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa. The second objective of the study was to identify factors associated with the unmet need for family planning. Women were considered to have an unmet need for family planning if they were fecund and sexually active and wanted to delay their next birth for at least 2 years or were undecided about the timing of their next pregnancy (unmet need for spacing) or no longer wanted any more children (unmet need for limiting) but were not using any contraceptive methods. In addition, women with a mistimed or unwanted current pregnancy or who are postpartum amenorrhea and whose last birth in the previous two years was mistimed or unwanted were also classified as having an unmet need for family planning [9, 16, 17, 40].

Study selection process

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote X9 reference manager software to identify and remove duplicate records and facilitate the screening process. After removing duplicates, three authors (GM, BA, and NT) autonomously screened the articles based on the titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full text of potentially suitable articles was obtained and evaluated against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and further evaluated for quality.

Quality assessment of the included studies

The scientific strength and quality of the included studies in this review were assessed by using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist [41]. The quality of each included article was evaluated by two authors (GM and NT). Any disparities during the quality assessment process were resolved through discussion and consensus based on established criteria, and when required, consulting with a third author (TA). In the assessment process, studies scored five or higher on the JBI checklist were considered low risk and included in the analysis (Table 1).

Data extraction and management

Following the application of prespecified inclusion criteria to retrieve the primary studies, two authors (GM and BA) independently extracted data using a standardized data extraction format developed in Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The data extraction format contains information on the primary authors, study year, study setting, study design, publication status, publication year, country name, sample size, the prevalence of unmet need for family planning, and the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) at 95% confidence interval of factors associated with the outcome. To capture reported determinants, variables that were identified as significant factors for unmet family planning needs in at least two primary studies were included (see Additional file 3).

Uncertainties during the extraction process were resolved by logical consensus between the two authors, and the final consensus was approved by consultation with authors TA and KY.

Statistical analysis and synthesis

After data extraction using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, the data were exported to STATA 17 statistical software, where all statistical analyses were carried out. The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis were summarized using texts, tables, and figures. The combined prevalence of the studies was checked for heterogeneity using the I-square (I2) test and heterogeneity across the included studies was categorized as low (< 25%), moderate (50%-75%), or high (> 75%) [82].

A random-effects meta-analysis model with DerSimonian and Laird’s method was employed to estimate the pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning. Subgroup analyses were carried out by region, country, study setting, sampling method, and study period to identify potential sources of heterogeneity among the included studies. In addition, we have executed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to determine the effect of a single study on the pooled estimate. Furthermore, publication bias was assessed graphically using funnel plot symmetry and statistically using Egger’s and Begg’s tests, with a p-value less than 0.05 considered to declare the presence of publication bias. Finally, the meta-analysis results were presented using tables and forest plots, and the AOR at 95% confidence intervals was reported.

Results

Study selection process

The literature search identified a total of 1,306 records. From these, 464 records were removed before screening due to duplication (457) or non-primary research articles (7), including book chapters, guidelines, and poster presentations. The remaining 842 records were eligible for screening, and 799 studies were excluded based on the title and abstract screening. The full texts of the remaining 43 articles were assessed for eligibility, and 3 studies were excluded for different reasons, such as insufficient data on the outcome variable, irrelevance to the outcome of interest, and study context. Finally, 40 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart of the study selection process on unmet need for family planning in Africa

Characteristics of the included studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 40 full-text articles that have investigated the level of unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa. A total of 22,914 study participants were represented across these studies. In the included studies of this systematic review and meta-analysis, twenty-four studies were conducted in Eastern Africa, eleven in Western Africa, two in Central Africa, and the remaining three studies were from Southern Africa. The quality of the included studies was evaluated based on the Joanna Briggs Institute Meta-Analysis of Statistics Assessment and Review Instrument (JBI-MAStARI). The JBI quality score of each primary study ranged from 6 to 9 (Table 1).

Unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa

The pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa was 29.26% (95% CI: 25.54–32.99, I2 = 97.81%, P = 0.00) (Fig. 2). The prevalence varies across countries from 9.00% (95% CI: 6.34–11.66) in Togo to 42.70% (95% CI: 38.90–46.50) in Tanzania. According to studies included in this review, the unmet need for spacing and limiting childbirth was reported across 16 African countries. The pooled prevalence of unmet need for spacing and limiting was 12.05% (95% CI: 9.16–14.93) and 13.39% (95% CI: 10.88–15.89), respectively.

Method-specific contraceptive use among women living with HIV

Information on contraceptive methods used by WLHIV was inconsistently reported throughout the included studies of this meta-analysis. Injectable contraceptives, condoms, and implants were the most frequently reported methods, with pooled prevalence estimates of 32.25% (22.30, 42.20), 29.06% (20.33, 37.79), and 13.64% (11.16, 16.13), respectively (Table 2).

Heterogeneity and sub-group analysis

Heterogeneity among the reported prevalence of the review was assessed by computing p-values for the I2 test. In this review, significant heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 97.81%, P = 0.00). Thus, a random-effects model was computed to estimate the pooled prevalence. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to identify the possible sources of heterogeneity across studies. The subgroup analysis was carried out by country, geographic region, study setting, sampling method, and study period of the included studies. When stratified by geographical region, the unmet need for family planning was highest in Southern Africa (38.82%; (95% CI: 36.26–41.37) and lowest in the Central Africa region (19.11%; (95% CI: 17.59–20.62). At the country level, the lowest prevalence was observed in Togo (9.00%; (95% CI: 6.34–11.66)), while the highest prevalence was from Tanzania (42.70%; (95% CI: 38.90–46.50)). Based on the study settings, the pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning was 29.39% (95% CI: 25.25–33.54) in institution-based settings, and studies from community-based settings showed a prevalence of 28.38% (95% CI: 20.43–36.33). Additionally, to further explore the potential sources of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was also performed based on the study period, categorizing the studies into the pre-SDG era (before 2016) and the SDG era (2016 and after). The pooled prevalence of unmet family planning need was 30.88% (95% CI: 24.91–36.86) in studies conducted before the years 2016, and 27.87% (95% CI: 22.99–32.76) in researches done in 2016 and later. However, despite performing a subgroup analysis based on the above-mentioned factors, substantial heterogeneity persists across most of these subgroups (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

The leave-one-out approach of sensitivity analysis was used to assess the effect of each study on the overall combined prevalence of unmet need for family planning by systematically excluding one study at a time. The result indicates that no single study had a significant effect on the pooled estimate, as the point estimates from the omitted analysis remained within the confidence interval of the overall pooled estimate (Fig. 3). The potential risk of publication bias was assessed using funnel plot analysis and statistical tests. On visual inspection, the funnel plot showed a relatively symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled effect size, with some studies scattered on the right side of the plot (Fig. 4). However, both Egger’s (P = 0.0003) and Begg’s (P = 0.0004) regression tests showed statistically significant publication bias across the included studies. To further explore, a nonparametric trim-and-fill analysis was conducted to determine the number of potentially missing studies and to adjust for publication bias in the included studies. The method did not impute any missing studies, and the pooled prevalence estimate remains unchanged at 29.26% (95% CI: 25.54–32.99) (Table 4). These findings suggest that although the statistical tests indicate potential publication bias, the trim-and-fill analysis did not demonstrate an impact on the pooled prevalence estimate.

Factors associated with the unmet need for family planning

This meta-analysis identified several factors that were significantly associated with the unmet need for family planning among women living with HIV. In the random-effects model, the following factors were found to be significantly associated with the unmet need for family planning. Participants aged 15–24 were 6.58 times more likely to have an unmet need for family planning than the older age group (OR = 6.58, 95% CI: 4.21, 10.28). The odds of unmet need for family planning was 2.30 times more likely among women from rural areas in their residence than those from urban areas (OR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.41, 3.75).

According to the analysis of three studies, women who have a living child were 3.32 times more likely to have unmet need for family planning than those who have no living child (OR = 3.32, 95% CI: 2.04, 5.40). Women whose partner’s HIV status is unknown have a higher chance of unmet need for family planning. Those with unknown partner HIV status have 2.00 times higher odds of unmet need than their counterparts (OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.32, 3.04). Similarly, women who had been on Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) for less than 24 months were 2.03 times more likely to have unmet need for family planning than those who were on ART for 24 months or longer (OR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.42, 2.90). In addition, the pooled odds ratio of the four included studies showed that women who did not get contraceptive counseling were 2.71 times more likely to have unmet need for family planning than their counterparts (OR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.99, 3.68) (Table 5).

Discussion

Preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV is a crucial strategy for achieving an HIV free generation. This systematic review and meta-analysis examined the pooled prevalence and contributing factors of unmet need for family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa. The review revealed that a significant proportion of women living with HIV have an unmet need for family planning. Despite the availability of prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) programs and the increasing integration of family planning services into HIV care [83, 84], the findings of the review highlight a gap in meeting the needs of family planning in this vulnerable population.

The present study estimated an overall prevalence of unmet family planning needs of 29.26% (95% CI: 25.54–32.99). The finding is consistent with studies conducted in sub-Saharan African countries, reported prevalences of 30.10% [85] and 25.72% [86]. The possible reasons might be due to similarities in sociodemographic and cultural contexts, economic conditions, and the healthcare system. Many African countries share the same gender roles, fertility norms, and cultural expectations [87, 88]. Moreover, poverty, low educational achievements, stigma toward people living with HIV, and partner opposition are common in African settings. These shared determinants will influence fertility desire, decision-making, and use of family planning [89–92]. Furthermore, access to contraceptives may not differ much between regions. In many African settings, people living with HIV receive ART for free of charge from public health facilities, and HIV-infected women of reproductive age are forced to obtain their antiretroviral medications through these health facilities for financial reasons, where integration of ART and family planning services remains limited [36, 93]. Therefore, implementation of multiple strategies, including integration of the family planning service into HIV care, is necessary to meet the sexual and reproductive health needs among these women.

The pooled prevalence of unmet need found in this study is higher than the global estimate of 8.3% and the regional prevalence of 18% for SSA, reported by the Global Burden of Disease study 2019 [19]. The finding of the present study is also higher than studies from India, 17% [27], Nepal, 20.8% [94], SSA, 15.57% [95], 22.90% [96], and Ethiopia: 25.13% [30], 16.2% [97]. The observed variation may be explained by differences in the study population, geographic and regional contexts, health service factors, and socioeconomic and educational conditions. Most of the aforementioned studies were conducted among the general population of reproductive-age women, whereas our study specifically examined women living with HIV. Evidence shows that marginalized populations, including WLWH, face greater barriers to access for family planning [30, 36]. Similarly, people living with HIV may want to limit the number of children by fearing the vertical transmission of the virus to their child, and may need more contraceptives than women who are free from the virus. These could contribute to a higher level of unmet need [98]. Moreover, HIV positive women may face stigma or fear of side effects of contraceptives interacting with the ART drugs, which can limit their use of the methods than the general population. Furthermore, socio-cultural norms, fertility desire, and service availability might vary across Africa and Asia. Some of those countries may have better access to family planning, leading to lower unmet need [99]. In some regions of Africa, coverage of the health system and linkage of ART and family planning services may influence the unmet need. Our study is among WLHIV who may have a lower chance of educational and socioeconomic status than the general population. These underscore the need to increase service accessibility, availability, and integration of ART and family planning services to meet the needs of family planning among HIV-positive women across African regions.

However, the finding of the review is lower than studies from Pakistan, 34% [100], Nigeria, 41.7% [101], and Angola, 51.7% [102]. The difference might be due to the study population, setting, and health service utilization. The unmet need for family planning was assessed among the general reproductive-age women in the above-mentioned studies, unlike the present study, where HIV positive women were used. WLWH are more likely to engage regularly with the healthcare system for their routine HIV care follow-up. These women may receive counseling on family size, the risk of vertical transmission of HIV, and the need for family planning from their healthcare providers, or may have the chance of peer education during their ART follow-up, which could reduce unmet need compared to the general population. HIV positive women will have the chance of frequent contact with health facilities, which will increase the opportunities for family planning counseling and contraceptives provision. Whereas women in the general population may only access health facilities occasionally, resulting in a higher chance of unmet need. Many HIV programs promote the integration of ART services with family planning, and the integration may increase the availability of contraceptives, specifically for WLWH [36, 37, 84]. Furthermore, the study from Pakistan is among the most populous countries in the world [100], where in large populous countries, accessibility of contraceptive services may be scarce, and some sociocultural barriers may increase the unmet need. The studies from Nigeria and Angola were community-based, whereas facility-based studies were also included in our review. Many women in the community-based setting may lack access to contraceptive services, counseling, or awareness. In contrast, those who have health facility visits may have better awareness and access to contraceptives. Thus, strengthening integration of family planning with the HIV care and ensuring wider community accessibility are essential to reduce the unmet need of family planning among reproductive-age women.

Significant heterogeneity was observed among the included studies in this meta-analysis (I2 = 97.81%, P = 0.00), and subgroup analysis was carried out for the following factors. The highest pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning was observed in the Southern Africa region, with 38.82% (95% CI: 36.26–41.37), and the lowest was in the Central Africa region at 19.11% (95% CI: 17.59–20.62). A subgroup analysis by country level indicates that the pooled prevalence of unmet need for family planning was highest in Tanzania (42.70%; 95% CI: 38.90–46.50) and lowest in Togo (9.00%; 95% CI: 6.34–11.66). The observed differences may reflect variations in sociocultural and demographic contexts, health system policies, and service integrations of family planning and HIV across regions and countries. The subgroup analysis by study period showed that the prevalence of unmet need for family planning is higher in studies conducted before 2016, 30.88% (95% CI: 24.91–36.86), compared to studies conducted in 2016 and later, 27.87% (95% CI: 22.99–32.76). This disparity may reflect the growing impact of global and national initiatives, including policy shifts from Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) to SDGs, which emphasize expanding access to modern contraceptives that brought funding, advocacy, and program scaling up, particularly in low and middle-income countries where unmet need is unnecessarily high [103, 104]. Emerging evidence shows a measurable progress in family planning indicators [19, 105–108]. Countries’ efforts to achieve SDG 3, which emphasizes universal access to sexual and reproductive health, may have likely facilitated improved availability and accessibility of contraceptives [9–11, 109]. Moreover, service integration of family planning with HIV care has increased over time, making family planning services more accessible in routine care. Strengthening of the health system, focusing on primary healthcare and expanding community health in recent decades, will have a direct impact on decreasing the unmet needs [110–112].

The review found that the unmet need for family planning was more likely among young study participants, rural residents, participants who had a living child, unknown partner HIV status, had been on ART for less than 24 months, had not received contraceptive counseling, had no formal education, had poor knowledge of MTCT, and had unfavorable attitudes toward family planning. Young study participants in this study were more likely to have an unmet need for family planning. The finding is in agreement with previous studies [86, 113, 114]. This may be because young women may have limited awareness and knowledge about contraceptive methods and fertility [86]. Fear of side effects and misconceptions, such as fear of infertility, is common among adolescents, which can hinder the utilization of contraceptives among young women [115]. In many African settings, social and cultural norms may limit the use of contraceptives among adolescents, and the young women may have less decision-making autonomy in the utilization of contraceptives than older women [113]. Therefore, awareness-raising interventions and strengthening youth-friendly family planning services are necessary to address the unmet need for family planning among young people.

Women from rural areas were 2.30 times more likely to have an unmet need for family planning than those from urban areas. The finding is consistent with previous studies [115, 116]. The disparity may be explained by the fact that family planning services are readily accessible in urban settings. Moreover, women in the urban areas may have better awareness and knowledge of contraceptives because they may have better educational attainment and often have greater access to health information by utilizing media, digital platforms, and counselling from healthcare providers, which may enhance their awareness and use of contraceptives [117]. Thus, addressing the rural-urban disparities in access to family planning is necessary to lower the unmet need. Expanding outreach programs and improving service availability in rural communities are key strategies to reduce the unmet need for family planning among women in rural settings.

The odds of unmet need for family planning among women who have a living child were 3.32 times more likely than those who have no living child. This is supported by studies from Pakistan, Indonesia, and sub-Saharan Africa [21, 114, 115]. This could probably be because women who have children may wish to limit or delay their fertility. HIV positive women with children may also be more aware of the risk of MTCT transmission of the virus and, therefore, want to avoid another pregnancy. Inadequate contraceptive counseling, poor service integration, and method unavailability may increase the risk of unmet need among these women. In addition, in many African settings, male partners take the decision-making of family planning use for their women [118], and they may oppose the use of contraceptives among this group of women, which can further increase the chance of having unmet need. So, spatial focus, such as strengthening contraceptive counseling and service integration of family planning with HIV care, plays a critical role in addressing the high unmet need among this group of women.

The findings of the review also identified that study participants who did not receive contraceptive counselling and had been on ART for less than 24 months were significantly associated with the unmet need for family planning. Similar findings were identified from a previous study [119]. This suggests that comprehensive family planning counselling and the duration of HIV care are critical components in shaping contraceptive uptake. New ART program participants may have limited contact with healthcare providers for contraceptive counselling. At the same time, women who are new to HIV care may not have the opportunities for peer education regarding their adherence to the care in general and contraceptives in particular. These findings highlight the need for stronger service integration of family planning to HIV care and adherence counselling on contraceptive use.

Consistent with previous studies [86, 100, 113, 114], this study revealed that women’s educational attainment was significantly associated with unmet need for family planning. Women who had no formal education had higher odds of unmet need than those who attained higher education. A plausible explanation could be that education serves as a crucial means of raising awareness and provides better access to information on family planning [120]. Non-educated women may have limited awareness and knowledge of the options and benefits of contraceptive methods, poor health literacy, and low decision-making autonomy [121]. Additionally, women with no formal education may be limited in grasping and adhering to the counselling provided by health care providers. These all may cause limited capacity in utilizing the available methods and thereby increase their unmet need for family planning. Consequently, special attention shall be provided for those HIV positive women with no schooling. A prioritized family planning education is crucial to addressing the unmet needs in this group.

In addition, women who had poor knowledge of family planning methods and MTCT were more likely to have unmet need than those with better knowledge. This is in agreement with previous studies [21, 114, 122]. This finding could be explained by the fact that knowledge empowers women to recognize the risk of unintended pregnancy in the context of HIV and plays a critical role in shaping the utilization of reproductive health services [123]. Women with limited awareness of contraceptives and poor knowledge of MTCT of HIV may not understand the health risk of an unplanned pregnancy, including the increased risk of vertical transmission of HIV. Moreover, women with poor knowledge may not be aware of the wide range of contraceptive options and may not be able to select a method that fits their needs. These poorly informed women may have a fear of side effects or concerns about the safety of the contraceptives with HV, and reduced engagement with health services, all of which contribute to unmet need. Therefore, efforts to strengthen knowledge about family planning and MTCT among women living with HIV should be prioritized to decrease the unmet need.

Finally, the study affirmed that women with unfavorable attitudes toward family planning were more likely to have unmet needs than their counterparts. The finding of the review is consistent with a study from Ethiopia [124]. A favorable attitude towards family planning is crucial as it strongly predicts an individual’s behavior. Women who have unfavorable attitudes may not perceive the benefits of family planning or may have misconceptions about safety and health harms. Such negative beliefs discourage women’s utilization of contraceptives despite their expressed fertility intentions [124]. Moreover, women’s attitudes toward family planning are often influenced by cultural and religious norms in many African contexts [124–126]. HIV-positive women may also fear stigma, especially when family planning services are provided separately from the HIV care. Women with negative attitudes toward family planning may be less likely to have contraceptive counseling, attend follow-up visits, and adhere to methods. Integrated service approach of family planning with HIV care, addressing cultural and religious beliefs, and reducing stigma, are essential to enhancing attitudes and reducing the unmet need of family planning among HIV positive women.

Implications of the study

This review highlights a high level of unmet need for family planning among women living with HIV. This finding underscores the urgent need to strengthen national and regional efforts to meet the contraceptive needs of HIV positive women. Thus, the findings of this review provide important baseline evidence for health professionals, researchers, policymakers, and global partners to design and implement evidence-based interventions to achieve the elimination of MTCT of HIV within the WHO triple elimination initiative [34]. Strengthening policies that promote the integration of family planning services within HIV/ART programs is essential for reducing unmet need. Policymakers should ensure the provision of reproductive health, including routine family planning counseling and method provision as part of standard HIV service delivery.

Furthermore, healthcare providers should provide individualized, client-centered contraceptive counseling and ensure method availability during routine HIV care visits. Strengthening facility and community-level educational interventions may empower women living with HIV to make informed reproductive choices and improve the uptake of family planning services.

While the unmet need for family planning among WLHIV is a critical component of comprehensive SRH care, it represents only one domain of the broader SRH construct. SRH is multidimensional and encompasses sexual function, intimate partner communication, and quality of life, which may be particularly affected among WLHIV [127–129]. The sexual function of these women and their parents is affected by chronic disease and their long-term treatment effects, stigma, partner involvement, relational dynamics, and psychological factors [130–133]. These factors may also shape contraceptive decision-making and utilization of the services among HIV positive women. Providing an integrative SRH service, including couple-level considerations, among this marginalized population is essential to promote safe, healthy, and satisfying sexual and reproductive health, and improve overall quality of life and wellbeing [129, 134]. Future research aimed at this group should incorporate more comprehensive SRH outcomes, including sexual function, intimacy, and couple-level quality of life.

This systematic review and meta-analysis have certain strengths and limitations. The study employs rigorous systematic review and meta-analysis methods to synthesize available evidence on the unmet need of family planning among reproductive-age women living with HIV in Africa, addressing a key gap in the critical domain of the SRH. The findings provide baseline evidence and offer valuable insights for clinicians, policymakers, researchers, and other stakeholders in strengthening family planning service integration and addressing unmet needs among women living with HIV across the region.

However, despite these strengths, the study has some limitations. The first limitation of this study is the limited number of prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses available for comparison. The review predominantly involved cross-sectional studies, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the unmet need for family planning and the associated factors. In addition, the available evidence did not cover all African countries, which might reduce its representativeness for the continent. Furthermore, the included primary studies did not assess sexual function, sexual satisfaction, or couple-level quality-of-life measures, limiting the ability of the current review to address the broader dimensions of sexual health and wellbeing among women living with HIV. Finally, a high level of heterogeneity was observed, which may affect the robustness of the results.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, a high proportion of women living with HIV experience unmet need for family planning. The meta-analysis revealed that the age of women, residence, number of living children, HIV status of partners, duration on ART, educational attainment, level of knowledge, and attitude status toward family planning were the determining factors that affect the unmet need of women for family planning.

Targeted interventions are required to achieve the goal of eliminating the unmet need for family planning. To promote healthy lives and well-being for all, countries should strengthen and scale up their efforts to ensure universal access to sexual and reproductive health. Comprehensive and client-centered family planning counselling, strengthening youth-friendly services, and integrating family planning services with HIV care are recommended to increase the uptake of contraceptives among HIV-positive women. Enhancing women’s awareness about family planning and advancing PMTCT of HIV should be prioritized to decrease the unmet need for contraception in this population. Family planning programs should focus on empowering women to help them make informed choices regarding family planning services.
