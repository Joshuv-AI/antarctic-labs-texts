Adverse childhood experiences and psychiatric outcomes among women with schizophrenia and depression in Singapore
Authors: Mythily Subramaniam, Edimansyah Abdin, Anitha Jeyagurunathan, Muhammad Iskandar Shah Mohmad Khalid, Rajeswari Sambasivam, Yunjue Zhang, Jianlin Liu, Paul Shing Fong Chan, Xinyi Kang, Lilian Fong Eng Mark, Sutapa Basu, Gillian Lim, Nisha Chandwani, Steve Yin Lam Lee, Siow Ann Chong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are important determinants of adult mental health, yet data on their prevalence and consequences among women with severe mental disorders in Asian settings remain limited.Objective: To examine the epidemiology of ACEs and their associations with symptom severity, quality of life (QoL), substance use, and intimate partner violence (IPV) among women with schizophrenia and depression.Methods: A total of 255 women aged 21–65 years with a clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia or depression were recruited from a tertiary psychiatric hospital in Singapore. ACEs were assessed using the ACE-10 questionnaire. Outcomes included symptom severity, quality of life, substance use, and intimate partner violence. Linear and logistic regression models assessed associations, adjusting for confounders.Results: Over three-quarters of participants (77.7%) reported at least one ACE, with emotional abuse being the most common (51.0%). A clear dose–response relationship was observed: women reporting ≥3 ACEs had higher depression severity (B = 5.44, p < .001) and overall psychiatric symptom burden (B = 6.27, p < .001), poorer QoL across all domains (e.g. physical health B = –16.32, p < .001), and increased odds of psychological IPV (OR = 6.03, p < .001) and physical IPV (OR = 3.79, p = .007)Conclusions:ACEs were highly prevalent among women with schizophrenia and depression in this Singaporean clinical sample. These findings highlight the importance of routine trauma assessment and the integration of trauma-informed approaches within mental-health services.KEYWORDS: Adverse childhood experiences, depression, intimate partner violence, quality of life, schizophrenia, trauma-informed care, women’s mental health

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are universally recognised as major social determinants of mental health, contributing substantially to the global burden of psychiatric morbidity (Felitti et al., 1998; Hughes et al., 2017). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC, 2024) categorised ACEs to include direct experiences such as physical, emotional, or sexual abuse, and various forms of neglect. Additionally, this definition encompasses aspects of household dysfunction that erode the safety of a child’s environment, such as growing up in a household with substance use, mental illness, or instability due to parental separation or incarceration. While broader conceptualizations of early adversity exist, the present study utilises the original framework, which remains widely used in epidemiological research, to investigate these adverse exposures that shape mental health outcomes.

ACEs represent cumulative, population-level exposures that shape mental health trajectories across the life course, and are prevalent, as indicated by national surveys in the United States of America (USA), with nearly two-thirds (63.9%) of adults reporting at least one ACE and 17.3% reporting four or more (Swedo et al., 2023). Among the various types of ACEs, emotional abuse (34.0%), parental separation or divorce (28.4%), and living in a household with substance abuse (26.5%) are among the most frequently reported by US adults (Swedo et al., 2023). A national survey from Singapore has similarly reported a lifetime prevalence of 63.9% in the adult population (Subramaniam et al., 2020), with the most common type of ACE experienced being emotional neglect (46.5%), followed by parental separation, divorce, or death (21.8%), and battered mother/ female guardian (8.2%).

While ACEs affect all genders, evidence suggests patterns of exposure and associated mental health outcomes may differ between women and men. The original CDC-Kaiser Permanente ACE Study found that 15.2% of women reported four or more ACEs, compared to 9.2% of men (Felitti et al., 1998). A notable study by Haahr-Pedersen and colleagues utilised latent class analysis to demonstrate that females tend to exhibit more complex and varied patterns of childhood adversity than their male counterparts (Haahr-Pedersen et al., 2020). Similar findings have been reported in population studies in which individuals in multiple-adversity classes were more likely to be female (Liu et al., 2022). These patterns may be shaped by gendered sociocultural dynamics, including gender-based violence, power inequalities, and caregiving roles, all of which may intersect to influence women’s vulnerability to adverse outcomes (Almuneef et al., 2017; Whitaker et al., 2021). In terms of outcomes, studies have also shown that women are more likely to develop internalising disorders, such as depression and anxiety, following exposure to ACEs (Jiang et al., 2022; Whitaker et al., 2021).

Building on these gendered vulnerabilities, research has increasingly focused on how ACEs manifest in women with severe mental disorders. Women with severe mental disorders report a high number of ACEs (Jangam et al., 2015; Köhler-Forsberg et al., 2024; Prokopez et al., 2018), and ACEs have been associated with earlier onset of psychiatric illness, higher symptom severity, and poorer treatment outcomes (Cancino et al., 2018; Quidé et al., 2020; Teicher et al., 2022; Teicher & Samson, 2013). ACEs have also been shown to impair therapeutic engagement and increase risk of relapse, highlighting the enduring role of early trauma in shaping the course of mental illness (Teicher & Samson, 2013).

The World Health Organisation (2012) defines Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) as a ‘behaviour within an intimate relationship that causes physical, sexual or psychological harm, including acts of physical aggression, sexual coercion, psychological abuse and controlling behaviours.’ This definition applies to current or former relationships, regardless of gender, age, or sexual orientation. A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of 37.3% lifetime IPV among women (White et al., 2024). Emerging evidence indicates that childhood adversity exerts long-term effects on emotional regulation, interpersonal functioning, and vulnerability to later victimisation. A recent integrative model, proposed by Fereidooni et al. (2023), conceptualises the link between childhood maltreatment and adulthood victimisation as a multilevel process spanning cognitive, emotional, and behavioural domains. Their model identifies maladaptive attachment patterns, emotion dysregulation, loneliness, dissociation, and post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms as key mediators connecting early maltreatment to later revictimization. These findings underscore the cumulative and interactive nature of adversity, suggesting that early experiences of abuse and neglect not only increase psychiatric risk but also shape vulnerability to interpersonal harm.

Early studies have also suggested that the consequences of childhood abuse may diverge along gendered lines, reflecting differences in the expression of psychopathology. Maltreated boys are more likely to externalise distress, manifesting in aggression and a heightened risk of becoming perpetrators of violence, whereas maltreated girls are more likely to internalise their pain, which may foster attachments to abusive partners and consequently elevate their risk of IPV victimisation (Downey et al., 1994; Widom, 1999). Widom et al. (2014) found that abused/neglected females were more likely to report being injured by their partner and that child maltreatment increases risk for the most serious form of IPV involving physical injury. A recent meta-analysis reiterated that ACEs are associated with both IPV victimisation and perpetration across diverse populations (Zhu et al., 2024).

Given the high prevalence and significant adverse implications of ACEs in women with mental illness, understanding their patterns is of considerable clinical and public health importance. This understanding could inform the development of screening and intervention protocols, guide the tailoring of trauma-informed treatment models, and contribute to breaking the intergenerational transmission of adversity. However, there is a dearth of studies investigating the prevalence and impact of ACEs specifically among women with schizophrenia and depression in Asian populations, particularly in a Singaporean context. Schizophrenia and depression represent two clinically distinct psychiatric disorders in which early trauma has been implicated in illness onset, symptom severity, and treatment outcomes (Bränn et al., 2023; Rocca et al., 2025). Examining these diagnostic groups together allows comparison across disorders characterised by differing psychopathological profiles but shared vulnerability to early adversity. Furthermore, most of the established ACE literature remains Western-centric. In Asian societies, cultural paradigms such as filial piety, collectivism, and a strong stigma against disclosing family shame may influence the reporting of childhood adversity (Guo et al., 2025).

To address this knowledge gap the main aims of the current study were (i) to examine the prevalence of ACEs among women diagnosed with schizophrenia and depression receiving treatment in a tertiary psychiatric treatment setting in Singapore, (ii) to investigate the associations between ACE exposure and clinical outcomes, including symptom severity, quality of life, substance use, and intimate partner violence. By situating women’s mental health within the broader framework of early life adversity, this study aims to highlight the importance of integrating trauma awareness into psychiatric care and to provide evidence for policy and practice interventions that support recovery and well-being.

Methods

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling among women seeking treatment at the outpatient clinics of the Institute of Mental Health (IMH), a tertiary care hospital in Singapore, and its affiliated clinics. Eligible participants were Singapore Citizens or Permanent Residents aged 21–65 years, and were literate in English, Chinese, Malay, or Tamil. We decided to recruit those aged 21 or older to align with local ethical and legal guidelines, which state that 21 is the age of majority for providing independent informed consent for clinical research in Singapore without parental involvement. Attending clinicians or other mental health professionals (e.g. case managers or research staff) referred interested patients with a current primary clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia or depression, established according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) criteria. Participants were classified according to their primary clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia or depressive disorder. Individuals with intellectual disability or significant cognitive impairment that precluded the ability to provide informed consent were excluded.

The survey was conducted in a quiet and private setting. The research staff emphasised the importance of confidentiality, and participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires independently, without others present. Recruitment procedures were designed to ensure that women were approached only when they were alone and not accompanied by a partner.

Following consent, the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale-Expanded (BPRS-E; Lukoff et al., 1986) was administered by a trained clinician or researcher. All other instruments were self-administered to safeguard confidentiality and were made available in the four official languages of Singapore (English, Chinese, Malay, and Tamil). Identifying information was not linked to the data. To support participant well-being, all women received brochures containing helpline information for trauma-related concerns. Participants were also informed that if immediate assistance was required, the researcher could facilitate a referral to Medical Social Workers, who were co-investigators in the study.

Questionnaires

The Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-10) was used to evaluate adverse experiences of abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction during the first 18 years of life (Anda et al., 2010). The participants were asked to indicate (Yes/ No) if they had experienced any of the ten adverse experiences assessed in this study which included, physical, emotional, and sexual abuse; physical and emotional neglect, parental divorce or death, witnessing domestic violence, household substance abuse, household mental illness or having a family member attempt or die by suicide, and having an incarcerated household member.

The Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale-Expanded (BPRS-E) is a 24-item scale that is used to evaluate the severity of positive psychotic symptoms, negative psychotic symptoms, and depressive/anxiety symptoms (Lukoff et al., 1986). Each item in the BPRS is rated on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘1’ (not present) to ‘7’ (extremely severe). Thus, the sum of the items ranges between 18 and 126, with higher scores indicating more severe symptomatology. The inter-rater agreement of BPRS-E was 0.82 in the current study.

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is a 9-item self-administered instrument used for screening, diagnosing, monitoring, and measuring the severity of depression (Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002). Items are scored on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) in the last 2 weeks. The sum of the items can range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe symptomatology.

The Drug Abuse Screen Test (DAST-10) has been designed as a brief instrument for clinical and non-clinical screening to detect potential drug abuse, which excludes alcohol or tobacco use, during the past 12 months (Skinner, 1982). Each ‘yes’ response receives 1 point, except on question 3, for which a ‘no’ response receives 1 point. Scores of 0–2 indicate low-level or no substance use problems; 3–5 indicate moderate problems; 6–8 indicate substantial, and 9–10 indicate severe problems (Yudko et al., 2007).

The World Health Organisation Quality of Life (BREF) (WHOQOL-BREF) assesses an individual’s subjective well-being over the past 2 weeks. The 26-item generic questionnaire examines the multidimensional concept of quality of life across four domains: physical health (seven items), psychological health (six items), social relationship (three items), and environmental (eight items). The remaining two items assess individuals’ overall quality of life (QOL) and subjective satisfaction. Higher scores indicate a higher quality of life, and the mean score of items within each domain is used to calculate the domain score. Items are scored on a Likert scale of 1–5, enquiring on ‘how much’, ‘how completely’, ‘how often’, ‘how good’, or ‘how satisfied’ the individual felt with reference to each item (WHOQOL Group, 1994).

The World Health Organisation Violence Against Women (WHOVAW) instrument was initially developed for the WHO Multi-Country Study on Women’s Health and Domestic Violence Against Women (Garcia-Moreno et al., 2005). The instrument has been validated cross-culturally and widely applied in epidemiological research (Vyas et al., 2023). IPV domains include psychological abuse (e.g. humiliation, intimidation), physical (e.g. being slapped, pushed, hit, or threatened with a weapon), and sexual (e.g. coerced sexual acts, forced intercourse). Respondents are asked behaviourally specific questions, with dichotomous (Yes/No) responses.

A structured questionnaire was used to capture important socio-demographic and socio-economic data, such as age, ethnicity, religion, marital status, length of relationship, number of children, ages of children, educational level, occupational history, monthly income, smoking history, alcohol history, clinical diagnosis (as stated by the referring clinician), duration of illness (since diagnosis) and the age of onset of illness.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using Stata version 19.5. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were calculated for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. The number of ACEs that the respondents reported experiencing was summed into a continuous count and subsequently categorised into four groups (0, 1, 2, and ≥3 ACEs). This categorisation was chosen to ensure adequate and relatively balanced cell sizes for robust regression analysis while effectively capturing the established dose–response threshold. Missing data were less than 5% and handled using listwise deletion. Multinomial logistic regression was used to determine socio-demographic factors associated with ACE categories. Subsequently, multiple logistic and linear regressions were used to determine the association between ACE categories as the main predictor and symptom severity, quality of life, and IPV as outcome variables after adjusting for age, ethnicity, marital status, education, employment status, and income. Prior to analysis, standard linear regression assumptions, including normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and the absence of multicollinearity, were checked for all models. For the WHOQOL domains (Physical, Psychological, Social, and Environmental), the models demonstrated appropriate specification, homoscedasticity (Breusch – Pagan test, p > .05), and normally distributed residuals (Shapiro–Wilk test, p value > .05). For other outcomes, specifically PHQ-9, BPRS, and DAST, where diagnostics indicated departures from these assumptions, such as heteroskedasticity or non-normal residual distributions, we utilised Huber-White robust standard errors to ensure the validity of our statistical inferences (Yang et al., 2019). Across all models, multicollinearity was absent, with all Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) falling well below the threshold of 10 (Mean VIF = 1.57).

As this study was exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, we did not apply formal adjustments for multiplicity. We prioritised identifying potential associations across multiple outcomes to inform future research (Hooper, 2025). All statistically significant differences were evaluated at the 0.05 level using two-sided tests.

Prevalence of ACEs in the sample

The prevalence of ACEs was high in this clinical sample. Over three-quarters of the participants (77.7%) reported experiencing at least one ACE. Among the sample, 19.2% reported experiencing exactly one ACE, 14.1% reported two ACEs, and a substantial portion (44.3%) reported experiencing three or more ACEs.

As shown in Figure 1, the most frequently reported ACE was emotional abuse (51.0%), followed by emotional neglect (45.1%). Other common experiences included physical abuse (32.2%) and household mental illness (30.7%). The least prevalent ACE was having an incarcerated household member (12.2%).

Similarly, a higher number of ACEs was associated with a lower quality of life. Experiencing three or more ACEs was significantly associated with lower scores across all four domains of the WHOQOL-BREF: physical health (B = −16.32, p < .001), psychological health (B = −16.61, p < .001), social relationships (B = −20.44, p < .001), and environment (B = −13.29, p < .001). Women with three or more ACEs also had significantly higher odds of being an alcohol drinker (OR = 3.73, p = .003).

A history of ACEs was strongly associated with experiences of intimate partner violence (IPV). Experiencing even a single ACE was associated with significantly higher odds of psychological IPV (OR = 5.36, p = .001). This pattern remained significant for those with two ACEs (OR = 4.32, p = .008) and three or more ACEs (OR = 6.03, p < .001). For physical IPV, a cumulative effect was observed, with the association becoming significant for women who had experienced three or more ACEs (OR = 3.79, p = .007).

Discussion

This study sought to investigate the prevalence and impact of ACEs among women with schizophrenia and depression in Singapore. Our findings show a disproportionately high burden of childhood adversity in this clinical population, characterised by a significant dose–response relationship. As ACE exposure increased, symptom severity rose, and quality of life across physical, social, and psychological domains markedly declined.

Prevalence of ACEs among women with schizophrenia and depression

The prevalence of ACEs was high, with over 77% of women reporting at least one ACE. This is considerably higher than the 63.9% prevalence found in Singapore’s general adult population and 65.1% among women (Subramaniam et al., 2020), underscoring the disproportionate burden of early life adversity among individuals with schizophrenia and depression. It should be noted that because our sample was recruited entirely from a tertiary psychiatric institution, the cohort represents individuals with severe, complex, or treatment-resistant presentations, rather than mild community cases.

Comparisons across studies are complicated by differences in patient populations (e.g. diagnostic groups, age ranges) and in how ACEs are assessed or reported (e.g. the type of ACE and the assessment tool). Nevertheless, most investigations consistently show a high prevalence of ACEs among women with mental illness. For instance, in a clinical psychotherapy sample, Fontanil et al. (2021) found that 86.5% of women had experienced at least one ACE. In a population-based cohort of women with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, Köhler-Forsberg et al. (2024) reported that 48% had experienced ≥5 ACEs, compared with 19% among women without these conditions. Similarly, among female patients with depression, Shah et al. (2021) found that 68% reported at least one category of childhood adversity.

Clinical and socio-demographic correlates of ACEs

A key finding was the significantly higher prevalence of ACEs among women with depression compared to those with schizophrenia. This supports a large body of literature linking early life trauma to the development of mood disorders (Barrera et al., 2025; Gloger et al., 2021). While not directly measured in the current cross-sectional study, the established literature proposes several putative neurobiological mechanisms, including dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, alterations in stress reactivity, and trauma-induced epigenetic changes, that might contribute to this increased vulnerability (Tafet & Nemeroff, 2016; Weder et al., 2014). Psychologically, it is theorised that early trauma fosters maladaptive cognitive schemas and impaired emotion regulation, both of which are central features observed in clinical depression.

Interestingly, younger participants in our sample were more likely to report ACEs than older women. This contrasts with the findings of Subramaniam et al. (2020), who reported higher ACE prevalence in older cohorts within the Singaporean general population. This discrepancy likely reflects differences between the general population and a severe clinical sample. In our tertiary setting, younger women presenting with schizophrenia and depression may represent a clinical phenotype characterised by high trauma exposure associated with earlier and more severe disease onset. This may also reflect sociocultural factors like generational differences in willingness to disclose trauma, reduced stigma, or greater awareness of ACEs among younger cohorts (Hughes & Thomas, 2024). Alternatively, it could signal true increases in exposure to ACEs among women with schizophrenia and depression, though this requires further investigation.

The lack of significant associations between ACE categories and socio-economic factors in our study contrasts with general population trends. One possible explanation is the relative homogeneity of socio-economic characteristics within this clinical sample recruited from a tertiary psychiatric institution. Additionally, the requirement that participants be literate in one of Singapore’s official languages may have inadvertently excluded the most socioeconomically disadvantaged women, limiting our ability to detect these traditional associations.

The dose–response relationship of ACEs with poorer outcomes

Women who experienced three or more ACEs were also significantly more likely to experience severe depressive and general psychiatric symptoms, greater severity of drug use, and markedly poorer quality of life across all domains – physical, psychological, social, and environmental. Our findings reaffirm the well-documented cumulative and dose – response relationship between ACEs and adverse mental health outcomes as well as the increased risk of substance misuse and drug-related problems (Giampetruzzi et al., 2023; Köhler-Forsberg et al., 2024; Prokopez et al., 2018; Yousef et al., 2022). Additionally, women with three or more ACEs showed significantly higher odds of alcohol use, underscoring ACEs’ role in maladaptive coping mechanisms that may exacerbate mental health symptoms and increase relapse risk in this population (Hughes et al., 2017; Mersky et al., 2013). This highlights the need for integrated substance use screening in trauma-informed care.

Importantly, the observed associations extended beyond psychiatric symptoms to encompass physical health, social functioning, and environmental well-being, suggesting that ACEs exert a pervasive influence across multiple dimensions of life. Notably, even lower levels of ACE exposure (1 or 2) were associated with significant impairments in specific quality of life domains, such as social relationships (for 1 ACE) and physical health (for 1 and 2 ACEs), indicating that cumulative effects may begin at milder thresholds.

Beyond symptoms, the cumulative impact of ACEs extended to a significantly increased likelihood of experiencing IPV in adulthood. Even a single ACE significantly increased the odds of experiencing psychological IPV, while cumulative exposure (three or more ACEs) elevated the risk of both psychological and physical IPV. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that early trauma predisposes women to revictimization through negative cognitions of being worthless, bad, and guilty, which can lead them to feel that abuse is justified; traumatic helplessness and powerlessness leading to low self-efficacy; maladaptive attachment patterns; and lack of boundaries (Motley, 2016; Wöller, 2005). It is also in line with the pathways described in the Fereidooni et al. (2023) model, which links childhood maltreatment to adulthood victimisation through emotional and relational vulnerabilities. Although our study did not directly test these mechanisms, the parallels suggest that women exposed to multiple ACEs may exhibit patterns of heightened emotional reactivity and social isolation, contributing to psychiatric morbidity and increasing their susceptibility to external harms within intimate and interpersonal relationships.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the first to provide detailed data on ACEs among women with severe mental illness in a Singaporean context. The use of standardised, validated instruments and the inclusion of a clinical sample with confirmed diagnoses are key strengths. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; longitudinal research is required to establish temporal pathways. Second, the reliance on retrospective self-report for ACEs is subject to recall bias. Third, as participants were recruited via convenience sampling from a single tertiary institution, the findings may not be generalisable to all women with these conditions in Singapore. Fourth, to ensure confidentiality and avoid desirability bias, the study relied on self-administered questionnaires, which may have resulted in the exclusion of some of the most disadvantaged women. Fifth, to ensure participant safety and to adhere to strict safety protocols for IPV-sensitive research, women were approached only when unaccompanied. While necessary, this procedure may have inadvertently resulted in the exclusion of some of the most vulnerable subgroups, such as women experiencing severe coercive control whose partners would not leave them home unaccompanied, or those more severely ill requiring the constant presence of a caregiver. Finally, the study did not explore other potential mediating or moderating factors, such as social support or coping mechanisms, which could influence the relationship between ACEs and mental health outcomes.

Clinical and public health implications

These findings have important implications for psychiatric service delivery and public health planning, particularly the need to integrate trauma screening and trauma-informed approaches within routine mental healthcare. The high prevalence of ACEs among women with schizophrenia and depression in a tertiary psychiatric setting highlights the critical need for routine screening for childhood adversity in mental health settings. Our findings also inform the need for training of clinicians working with women with schizophrenia and depression in mental health settings. Adopting trauma-informed, attachment-focused interventions in psychiatric settings for women with extensive childhood adversity histories is essential to help build trust, improve engagement, and prevent re-traumatization (Goldstein et al., 2024). Furthermore, the strong dose–response relationship between ACEs and illness severity, as evidenced by more severe symptom scores, suggests that interventions targeting the sequelae of trauma, such as emotion regulation skills, trauma-focused psychotherapy, and building secure attachments, could lead to substantial improvements in clinical outcomes and quality of life (Sweeney et al., 2018). From a public health perspective, prevention strategies targeting childhood maltreatment remain critical to reducing the long-term burden of mental illness (Pierce et al., 2023). This includes strengthening child protection systems, enhancing parenting support, and implementing school- and community-based interventions that promote safe and nurturing environments (Matjasko et al., 2022).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a high prevalence of ACEs among women with schizophrenia and depression in Singapore. The accumulation of these experiences is strongly associated with greater symptom severity and poorer quality of life. These findings emphasise the enduring impact of early life adversity and call for a greater integration of trauma-informed principles into mental healthcare to address the needs of this vulnerable population. Future longitudinal research is needed to elucidate the causal pathways between ACEs and women with schizophrenia and depression and to develop and test targeted interventions.
