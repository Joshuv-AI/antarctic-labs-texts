Comfort distance between patients and pharmacists during medication instruction: A prospective observational study at a cancer chemotherapy center
Authors: Saori Gocho, Yukina Miyagi, Chika Nakayama, Yuka Miyachi, Shoshiro Okada, Kenta Maruyama, Taeyuki Oshima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe distance from the patient is a crucial factor in the communication with patients. The distance between patients and pharmacists varies depending on several factors. In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between comfort distance and patients' physical condition and mood by measuring this distance at a chemotherapy center.MethodsA total of 114 patients were surveyed regarding their physical condition and mood. The distance at which the patients were best able to talk to the pharmacists was measured. For comfort distance measurement, the pharmacists were instructed to approach or move away from the patients.ResultsThe correlation between physical condition, mood, and comfort distance was examined in both male and female patients, and no significant correlation was found; however, there was a strong correlation between physical condition and mood in female patients. We looked at correlations by further dividing patients into those over and under 65 years of age and found a slight correlation with comfort distance in women under 65. They tended to shorten the distance when they felt well and lengthen the distance when they felt not well.ConclusionsNo correlation was found between physical condition or mood and comfort distance in male or female. A slight correlation was observed when age was included; however, the results were not satisfactory. By directly measuring the distance in actual patients, we obtained an actual measurement of the comfort distance that synthesized the patient's condition and various backgrounds during chemotherapy, providing a foothold for future studies.Keywords: interpersonal distance, mood, non‐verbal communication, personal space, pharmacist

INTRODUCTION

World Health Organization recognizes that effective communication is crucial for pharmacists. 1 An important element for pharmacists is ensuring patient understanding of their treatment regimen to encourage proper medication adherence. Through medication administration guidance services, pharmacists provide appropriate information based on pharmacological evidence and continuously monitor the status of patients' understanding and adherence, therapeutic efficacy, and occurrence of side effects. 2 All these require pharmacists to have sufficient communication skills 3 and sensitivity to build better relationships with patients and maintain a comfortable distance from them during medication instruction. 4

Communication can be categorized into two types: verbal and nonverbal. Verbal communication involves the transmission of information through spoken language, whereas nonverbal communication involves the use of expressions, eyes, posture, physical movements, and personal space (PS). 5 Individuals convey detailed information to others during face‐to‐face conversations through both verbal and nonverbal communication. 6 , 7 For nonverbal communication, we focused on PS, which includes interpersonal distancing. PS, defined as the immediate area surrounding individuals in which the majority of interactions with others occur, 8 varies among individuals and facilitates interpersonal relationships. 9 Aono 10 review the history of research on PS and present the major findings and controversies, focusing on gender differences. Among them, they acknowledge a consistent trend that gender differences in interpersonal distance are smaller in women than in men. According to Dr. Kashiwagi, “the distance desired by patients changes daily; hence, considering patient comfort distance is important.” 11

Concerning nonverbal communication between patients and pharmacists during medication instruction, Arita 12 showed that sitting on the patient's side and leveling the posture to the eye level of the patient prevented unnecessary pressure on patients lying in bed. Several textbooks have described responses that consider PS in medical personnel. However, only a few studies have been conducted involving an actual measurement of PS that patients consider comfortable or evaluating changes in PS while considering the patients' physical and psychological conditions. Previously, we examined these measures in simulated patients to determine the relationship between their physical condition, mood, and the distance at which they were comfortable. 13 Since pharmacists and other healthcare professionals (physicians, nurses, etc.) have different opportunities for direct contact with patients, different images of the profession, and different conversations, the comfort distance sought by patients is likely to differ. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between comfort distance and patients' physical condition and mood by measuring this distance in the actual clinical setting of a bedside pharmacist interview at a chemotherapy center.

METHODS

Participants

This study included a total of 114 patients (61 men with a mean age of 71.6 ± 13.9 years and 53 women with a mean age of 67.5 ± 12.7 years) who received bedside chemotherapy in the supine position at the Nagoya Ekisaikai Hospital Chemotherapy Center. Patients who were deaf or unable to understand the purpose of the study were excluded. After obtaining informed consent from the patient, the patients' physical condition and mood were surveyed, and the distance at which the patients were best able to talk to the pharmacists was measured. The corresponding pharmacists included two male pharmacists with 12–14 years of experience and one female pharmacist with 24 years of experience. The study period was from February 2020 to December 2021, with an interruption during the study period owing to the COVID‐19 pandemic.

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Nagoya Ekisaikai Hospital (approval no.: 2019‐055).

Survey of patients' physical condition and mood

The patients' physical condition and mood were assessed using a questionnaire before the start of the distance measurement (Figure 1). The patients' mood was quantified using the Japanese version of the Brief Mood Questionnaire Checklist (BMC‐J), which was adapted from Thomas and Diener's (1990) Brief Momentary Mood Checklist (BMMC). The BMMC was utilized to measure relatively short‐term emotions (temporary mood) at the time of checklist entry using four positive (namely, “happy,” “joyful,” “pleased,” and “enjoyment/fun”) and five negative words (namely, “depressed/blue,” “unhappy,” “frustrated,” “angry/hostile,” and “worried/anxious”). The questionnaire was designed to evaluate the emotional state on a Likert‐type scale (0 = not applicable at all; 6 = very applicable). 14 , 15 The physical condition scale was set similarly to that of the BMC‐J, with a score of 0 indicating good physical condition and a score of 6 representing poor physical condition. The following abbreviations are used to denote the nine sentiments in the BMC‐J: HAP (happy), JOY (joyful), PLZ (pleased), ENJ (enjoyment/fun), DEP (depressed/blue), UHA (unhappy), FRU (frustrated), ANG (angry/hostile), and WOR (worried/anxious). At the same time, the patient's background was investigated in terms of age, gender, height, weight, body surface area, number of times the pharmacist had interviewed the patient, and ECOG Performance Status Scale.

Physical condition and mood questionnaire form and position of patient and pharmacist during measurement.

Comfort distance measurement

Comfort distance is defined as the distance at which the patient subjectively feels comfortable talking to the pharmacist. The pharmacist approached the patient half a step at a time from a position far from the patient and signaled STOP when the patient felt “just right” to receive a pharmacist interview, at which point the pharmacist measured the distance from the patient. The distance between the pharmacists' and the patients' chins was measured using a laser rangefinder (Zamo II; Robert Bosch GmbH, Robert‐Bosch‐Platz 1, 70839 Gerlingen, Baden‐Württemberg, Germany). Similarly, from the point where the pharmacist approached the patient, the pharmacist took a half step away and measured the distance at the point where the patient signaled “just right” Because we believed that approaching and moving away felt differently, we used a two‐directional approach. The measured comfort distances when approaching from far and when moving away from near tended to be shorter when moving away from near, but the difference between the two was generally in the range of 10–20 cm, and the comfort distance was defined as the average of both distances. To exclude measurement bias, the patient's position on the bed was standardized to supine, and the comfort angle between the patient and pharmacist was set at 45°, with the pharmacist approaching or moving away from the patient at that extension (Figure 1). A preliminary study indicated a comfort angle of 45°, 13 and Futami et al. reported a similar method 16 ; hence, the angle was set to 45° in the present study. Additionally, the pharmacists' eye level was aligned with that of the patients to avoid bias, such as intimidation due to eye level.

Statistical analyses

Because the size of personal space differs between males and females, analyses were conducted separately for male and female patients. 10 All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 27 (IBM Corp., minato, Tokyo, Japan). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for analysis of variance and correlation was evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

Results of physical condition and mood questionnaire

The mean scores for each mood were calculated using the physical conditions and mood questionnaires. For physical condition, the higher the score, the sicker the patient was; male patients scored an average of 2.0 ± 1.3 points, while female patients scored an average of 2.3 ± 1.3 points.

Regarding positive emotions, the average scores for male patients were HAP (4.0 ± 1.3 points), JOY (3.8 ± 1.5 points), PLZ (3.6 ± 1.2 points), and ENJ (3.6 ± 1.1 points); for female patients, HAP (4.4 ± 1.1 points), JOY (3.9 ± 1.3 points), PLZ (4.1 ± 1.2 points), ENJ (3.9 ± 1.4 points). Conversely, for negative emotions, the mean scores for male patients were DEP (2.2 ± 1.8 points), UHA (1.3 ± 1.7 points), FRU (1.0 ± 1.6 points), ANG (0.7 ± 1.2 points), and WOR (2.3 ± 1.8 points); for female patients, DEP (2.2 ± 1.6 points), UHA (1.2 ± 1.3 points), FRU (1.1 ± 1.3 points), ANG (0.8 ± 1.2 points), and WOR (3.1 ± 1.7 points). There were some change in the scores for DEP and WOR; however, for UHA, FRU, and ANG, most patients had low scores between 0 and 1. The WOR was also slightly higher in women.

Correlations between comfort distance and patients' physical condition and mood

Correlations between physical condition, mood, and comfort distance between patients and pharmacists were determined (Table 2). Although a weak positive correlation (r = 0.295) was found for “HAP” in male patients, no significant correlations were found for other moods, and the relationship between comfort distance and patients' physical condition and mood could not be demonstrated.

The reason why the results in three were not obtained may be due to factors other than gender, we tested the difference in comfort distance between the two groups in the high (above average) and low (below average) patient background factors showed significant differences in age, height, and BSA (age: p = 0.048, height: p = 0.001, BSA: p = 0.004) (Table 3). As height and BSA are correlated with gender, we again examined the correlation with comfort distance, taking into account age. By the definition of “elder,” the analysis was divided into two groups: those aged 65 years or older and those aged less than 65 years. The mean age and comfort distance for both groups were 57.9 ± 4.4 years and 106.2 ± 12.0 cm for male under 65, 74.6 ± 5.3 years, and 99.7 ± 23.1 cm for male over 65, 51.6 ± 4.6 years and 89.5 ± 21.2 cm for female under 65, and 74.8 ± 5.8 years and 94.0 ± 19.3 cm for female over 65 (Table 4). Multiple comparisons of the comfort distance for each group showed no significant differences (p = 0.068).

The correlation between physical condition and mood and comfort distance in the four groups with the addition of the age factor is shown in Table 5, and a scatter plot of the correlated items among these is shown in Figure 2. In male, a negative correlation (r = −0.255) was found only for PLZ among those under 65 years of age, but no correlation was found overall, but a weak positive correlation was observed between HAP and JOY in those over 65 years (HAP: r = 0.354, JOY: r = 0.202). Among females, a weak positive correlation was found with physical condition (r = 0.314) and a weak negative correlation with HAP, JOY, and PLZ among those under 65 years of age (HAP: r = −0.441, JOY: r = −0.308, PLZ: r = −0.313). Among females over 65, a weak positive correlation was found with FRU (r = 0.318), but no overall correlation was observed. As shown in the scatterplot in Figure 2, the coefficient of determination r 2 value of the approximate equation was low and varied. More female patients under the age of 65 than male patients showed a slope depending on their physical condition and mood. These results suggest that when age and gender were considered, there was a slight tendency for older males to take longer distances when they felt well, and for females under 65 to take shorter distances when they felt well and longer distances when they felt unwell.

Correlation between patients' physical condition and mood

To ascertain the extent to which physical condition affects the patient's mood. The correlation between patients' physical condition and mood was determined (Table 6). Male patients showed a weak negative correlation between mood and physical condition in the PLZ (r = −0.320) and a weak positive correlation between UHA and FRU (UHA: r = 0.326; FRU: r = 0.275), but the overall correlation was low. Conversely, female patients showed strong negative correlations, especially in positive emotions (HAP: r = −0.746, JOY: r = −0.570, PLZ: r = −0.466, ENJ: r = −0.597). A positive correlation (r = 0.522) was found for DEP among negative emotions and a weak positive correlation (r = 0.308) with UHA. These results show a relationship between mood and physical condition in female patients, who either felt worse as their physical condition deteriorated or worsened as their physical condition declined. In the WOR, which is the most likely variable in patients with cancer, no correlation with physical condition was found in either males or females.

DISCUSSION

The purpose of this study was to investigate how patients' comfort distance during interviews change depending on their physical condition and mood and to consider what pharmacists should care for during interviews. Therefore, we conducted a questionnaire survey on the physical condition and mood of patients undergoing outpatient chemotherapy and measured the comfort distance between the patient and the pharmacist during the interview. The outpatient chemotherapy center has certain conditions where many patients come and go every day and are undergoing anticancer drug treatment. In this context, changes in physical condition and mood are likely to occur depending on the disease status of the cancer, side effects from the treatment, and the nature of the consultation from the doctor, and was selected as a suitable measurement site for this study. The patients' conditions differ depending on the type of cancer, medical condition, and treatment regimen, but even if they have the same cancer, the same medical condition, and the same treatment regimen, the patients' subjective evaluation of their physical condition and mood will be different. Therefore, in this study, “physical condition” and “mood” were evaluated by a questionnaire directly from patients.

Despite the prediction that physical condition and mood are likely to change during anticancer drug treatment, the patient background showed that positive affect was overwhelmingly higher than negative affect and that change was scarce. In addition, since it was found that male and female patients had different physiques and comfort distances to begin with, the correlation between physical condition, mood, and comfort distance was investigated separately for male and female patients. As a result, a positive correlation was found only for “happy” in males, and no correlation was found between physical condition or mood and comfort distance overall.

To examine the factors that influence comfort distance more than gender, we divided patients into two groups, one with higher than average values for each of the patient background items and the other with lower than average values, and tested for differences in comfort distance. There have been various reports on factors that influence interpersonal distance, such as height 17 and face width‐to‐height ratio 18 ; however, in this study, “age” was taken into consideration, and the correlation between physical condition, mood, and comfort distance during the interview was determined again. Comfort distances for each group tended to be longer for under 65 years old male patients than for the other groups, but the difference was not significant. The correlations showed that male patients over 65 years old tended to maintain a longer distance from the pharmacist when they felt well, while female patients under 65 years old tended to maintain a shorter distance when they felt well and a longer distance when they felt unwell. However, these are weak correlations, and further verification is required.

The relationship between the physical condition and mood was also examined. Because we thought that the relationship between physical condition and mood itself may differ by gender. The results showed no correlation between the physical condition and mood in male patients, whereas a strong correlation was observed in female patients. Thus, there exists a relationship between poor mood and poor physical condition in women. Understanding the “mind‐brain–body” interaction is important for elucidating the mechanism of emotion, 19 and there exists gender differences in these interactions. Therefore, female patients are more likely to express their physical and mental states, and it is easier to judge them during interviews, whereas male patients are less likely to express their physical and mental states, making it more difficult to grasp their physical and mental states.

In addition, as the participants were cancer patients, we expected to find individual differences in “worry” values, and therefore expected to find a relationship between physical condition and comfort distance; nonetheless, we did not find any correlation between worry and physical condition, or between worry and comfort distance. It was thought that “worry” is always present in cancer patients and is unlikely to be a factor that influences their physical condition or comfort distance “at that moment.” As for “worry,” it is thought that in situations such as being in the dark or a hospital waiting room, the PS would be smaller in the sense of “huddling together.” Conversely, when worry or sadness is too strong, people want to be alone, and PS is considered larger. The relationship between worry and PS is complex and many aspects remain unresolved. 20

As the study was conducted at a single site in an actual medical facility, the number of pharmacists involved was limited. In the future, we will add conditions on the pharmacists' side to the study design. In addition, chemotherapy centers have limited space for measurements. Because of this spatial bias, only weak correlations between physical condition/mood and comfort distance could have been obtained.

This study confirms that the method of simply confirming the comfort distance to the patient is the comfort distance based on a synthesis of various factors perceived by the patient at that time, and that confirming this at the beginning of each interview may be the best and simplest method.

CONCLUSION

The research question was, “If the distance between the pharmacist and the patient undergoing anticancer drug treatment is adjusted in consideration of the patient's physical condition and mood during the interview, will communication with the pharmacist be smoother?” Based on this research question, we asked patients to indicate their physical condition and mood and then we measured the comfortable distance between them and the pharmacist. The correlation between physical condition, mood, and comfort distance was not found only by sex, but a trend was observed when age was added. Patients have a wide variety of requirements for determining the comfort distance during interviews, and their physical condition and mood may have an influence. Although this study was not able to find a specific trend, we believe that the data obtained by measuring distance will be valuable for gaining new knowledge by improving data accuracy in the future. In addition, pharmacists need to pay attention to their distance from the patient to ensure patients' feelings of security and trust.
