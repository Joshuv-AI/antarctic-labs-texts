Age-related regional homogeneity changes in first-episode major depressive disorder: a REST-meta-MDD project study
Authors: Zhipeng Liu, Hailin Wu, Yingxiang Xu, Lin Zhang, Kaili Tian, Longfei Li, Zhen Zhang, Ping Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) is associated with altered spontaneous brain activity, commonly assessed by regional homogeneity (ReHo), a measure of local synchronization of neuronal synchronization. While previous research has highlighted ReHo changes in MDD, their age-related variations remain underexplored. To address this, this study investigated first-episode MDD patients from the REST-meta-MDD project, dividing them into three age subgroups: young (16–24 years), adults (25–39 years), and middle-aged participants (40–54 years), and examined the age-related ReHo changes and interactions with disease. The results revealed distinctive age-specific patterns of ReHo alternations. Specifically, young patients showed decreased ReHo in the right middle frontal gyrus, right superior parietal lobule, and left inferior temporal gyrus; adult patients exhibited reductions in the right superior frontal gyrus, left middle frontal gyrus, and right inferior frontal gyrus; and middle-aged patients displayed reductions in the right paracentral lobule, right inferior temporal gyrus, and left middle occipital gyrus. A progressive age-related ReHo decline was observed in the left postcentral gyrus, left superior parietal lobule, and left superior temporal gyrus. Significant disease effects was noted in the right superior frontal gyrus, with an age-by-disease interaction in the right superior occipital gyrus. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis indicated robust diagnostic potential for MDD-related ReHo patterns in adult and middle-aged groups. These findings suggest ReHo as a promising biomarker for first-episode MDD, with dynamic, age-dependent characteristics across the lifespan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07406-x.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a pervasive psychiatric condition, with lifetime prevalence rates estimated at approximately 16.2% in the general population [1]. It is characterized by core symptoms such as persistent low mood, loss of interest, lack of energy, and cognitive deficits [2]. These symptoms severely impair individuals’ quality of life and increase the risk of suicide [3]. MDD impose profound burden, encompassing personal suffering, social dysfunction [4], substantial healthcare costs [5, 6] due to its chronic and recurrent nature [7]. Despite considerable advancements in research, the precise etiology and pathogenesis of MDD remain incompletely understood. Several models, including the well-established “monoamine,” “cytokine,” and “stress-induced” hypotheses, have been proposed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the disorder [8].

Early identification of MDD through neuroimaging biomarkers is crucial for preventive intervention and effective treatment [9]. Neuroimaging techniques provide essential insights into the complex neuropathological mechanisms of this disorder, with resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) being a powerful approach for analyzing intrinsic brain activity by spontaneous fluctuations in blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal fluctuations [10]. Common rs-fMRI metrics, such as amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) and functional connectivity (FC), have been widely used in MDD research. These metrics have revealed abnormalities in prefrontal, limbic, and subcortical regions, contributing to our understanding of the pathophysiology of MDD [11, 12]. Among these, regional Homogeneity (ReHo), a robust and data-driven metric, quantifies the synchronization of BOLD signals within a given region and its immediate neighbors, providing a reliable measure of local neural coherence [13]. Compared to other rs-fMRI metrics, such as ALFF or network-based FC, ReHo is prioritized in this study for its sensitivity to local neural synchronization, making it particularly suited for detecting region-specific abnormalities in MDD [14].

Prior studies on MDD have demonstrated altered ReHo in brain regions involved in autonomic regulation, emotional processing, and cognitive control. For instance, decreased ReHo in the right angular gyrus and right precuneus has been associated with deficits in working memory and visual learning, both of which are commonly observed in individuals with MDD [15, 16]. Similarly, reduced ReHo in the hippocampus and cingulate cortex [17, 18], regions critical for emotional memory [19, 20], has been linked to the negative affect characteristic of MDD [21, 22]. Conversely, increased ReHo in the medial prefrontal cortex [23], a region implicated in self-referential thought, may contribute to the maladaptive rumination, a hallmark of the disorder [24]. However, findings from prior studies remain inconclusive, with inconsistencies potentially attributable to differences in medication status, disease duration, and sample sizes across studies [25–27]. Investigating ReHo patterns in first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients with large samples are therefore essential for advancing our understanding of the disorder’s neurobiological basis.

Furthermore, age significantly modulates ReHo abnormalities in MDD, most likely driven by neurodevelopmental shifts. In the first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients, ReHo abnormalities are typically more localized, primarily affecting regions critical for cognitive and emotional maturation, such as the prefrontal cortex and precuneus, compared to recurrent cases where more widespread disruptions, including limbic and subcortical areas, are observed due to chronicity and recurrent effects [25, 28]. These shits manifest as disrupted neural synchronization in first-episode patients, reflecting impairments in process such as synaptic pruning and cortical maturation [29, 30]. In adolescent-onset MDD, for example, decreased ReHo values have been observed in the right cuneus and left precuneus [31]. These regions are involved in executive functions, visual processing, and working memory [32, 33]. These alterations may stem from disrupted neurodevelopmental trajectories, contributing to the heightened vulnerability of young MDD patients to cognitive impairments [34]. In adults and middle-aged MDD patients, ReHo abnormalities are primarily localized to the right frontoparietal regions, right insula, and left hippocampus [35], which are critical for emotional regulation [36, 37]. These changes likely represent downstream effects of earlier neurodevelopmental disruptions, exacerbating core MDD symptoms such as emotional numbing, irritability, and social withdrawal in these age groups. The varying patterns of ReHo abnormalities across age groups underscore the necessity of incorporating lifespan-specific neural mechanisms into understanding the pathophysiology of MDD.

Prior studies have identified ReHo abnormalities in MDD but often focused on narrow age ranges or heterogeneous cohorts, including chronic or medicated patients, which obscures age-specific and early disease effects [38, 39]. Given these gaps, this study aimed to investigate ReHo abnormalities in patients with first-episode MDD across different age groups, focusing exclusively on treatment-naive patients to provide novel insights into age-specific neurodevelopmental disruptions. We hypothesized that ReHo patterns in MDD would differ across age groups. Additionally, we predicted that MDD patients across all age groups would exhibit distinct ReHo alterations compared to healthy controls. Finally, we anticipated that these ReHo changes would closely correlate with clinical measures, specifically Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores and illness duration.

Methods

Sample selection

This study analyzed data from the REST-meta-MDD project (http://rfmri.org/REST-meta-MDD), which aggregated 23 independent datasets encompassing 1,300 MDD patients and 1,128 healthy controls. Demographic and clinical data were collected at each participating site. Although the inclusion criteria exhibited minor variations across sites, all MDD diagnoses were established using either the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV or the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10). Patients were excluded if they presented with comorbid Axis I disorders, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders, substance abuse or dependence, acute physical illnesses, or a history of brain injury resulting in loss of consciousness. Similarly, healthy controls were excluded if they or their first-degree relatives had any history of psychiatric disorders.

Additional exclusion criteria included incomplete data, poor spatial normalization quality, insufficient brain coverage, or excessive head motion (average frame-wise displacement [FD] > 0.2 mm). Following these exclusions, the final analytical sample comprised 495 first-episode MDD patients and 912 healthy controls. For subgroup analyses, MDD patients were stratified by age into three categories: young (16–24 years), adult (25–39 years), and middle-aged (40–54 years), based on their age distribution. All contributing studies received approval from their respective local ethics committees, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Clinical assessments

Clinical assessments were conducted to evaluate depression severity and illness duration for all participants in the REST-meta-MDD project. The 17-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-17) was used to measure the severity of depressive symptoms, encompassing mood, cognitive, and somatic domains [40], in accordance with standardized criteria for psychiatric outpatients with MDD [41]. Assessments were administered by trained clinicians at each participating site during baseline evaluations, ensuring consistency across the multisite dataset. All clinical data were thoroughly verified for completeness and consistency across sites, facilitating robust integration into the analyses.

Data preprocessing

Preprocessing details are described in the prior study [42]. Specifically, the rs-fMRI data from the REST-meta-MDD dataset underwent standardized preprocessing using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF) protocol (http://rfmri.org/DPABI) and SPM (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) within MATLAB platform. Preprocessing steps included discarding the first 10 volumes for signal equilibration, slice-timing correction, and realignment to correct for head motion. Functional data were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template, and bandpass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz) to isolate low-frequency fluctuations. Motion artifacts were controlled in the primary analysis using the Friston 24-parameter model to regress out head motion effects [43], with mean FD included as a covariate in group analyses. Signals from white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, head motion parameters, and the global brain signal were regressed out to minimize respiratory, cardiac, and drift effects. Quality control, including visual inspection and motion correction outputs, ensured data integrity.

ReHo calculation

The ReHo was calculated by using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF) toolkit (https://rfmri.org/DPARSF). ReHo maps for each participant were generated by computing the Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (KCC) of the time series of a given voxel with those of its 26 nearest neighbors. This voxel-wise analysis assumes that the temporal activity of a voxel is functionally correlated with its neighboring voxels [44]. To minimize inter-individual variability, the ReHo values for each voxel were normalized by dividing the KCC of the voxel by the mean KCC of the whole brain for that individual. The resultant ReHo maps were then spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 4 mm to improve data consistency.

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics, including age and education, were compared were compared using independent two-sample t-tests following confirmation of normality via Shapiro-Wilk tests, with Mann-Whitney U tests applied when normality was not satisfied, while gender differences were assessed with chi-square tests. A significant threshold of p < 0.05 was applied. ReHo differences between MDD patients and healthy controls were initially examined within each age subgroup (e.g., young patients vs. young controls) using independent-sample t-tests, with sex, age, education, site, and head motion included as covariates. Significance was determined at a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.05 with corrected for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) [45]. Only clusters larger than 30 voxels were reported.

To further investigate combined effects, data from all participants were analyzed to evaluate the roles of age group, MDD diagnosis, and their interaction on ReHo. A two-by-three covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted using a full factorial design in SPM toolbox, with factors including MDD diagnosis (MDD vs. control), age group (young, adult, middle-aged), and the interaction between diagnosis and age group. Covariates included sex, education, head motion, and site. Notably, the site effect was specifically accounted for to control variability across multi-site REST-meta-MDD dataset, which utilized standardized preprocessing pipelines and harmonized ReHo calculations [42], thereby ensuring robust and reliable ReHo findings. Significant effects were defined as p < 0.05 with cluster sizes > 30 voxels after FDR correction. For significant main effects of age group, Bonferroni post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to determine differences between specific age groups, with significance set at p < 0.05.

ROC analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was utilized to assess the ability of specific brain regions to differentiate MDD patients from healthy controls. Regions of interest (ROI) were selected based on significant ReHo differences between-groups, as determined via two-sample t-tests. In this exploratory analysis, the ROI selection was driven entirely by statistical significance from the group comparisons, and no anatomical priors or predefined ROIs were used. This approach aligns with previous studies aiming to seek potential biomarker without prior assumptions, allowing the data to identify that may have diagnostic significance [26]. ReHo values were extracted as cluster averages from each ROI, providing a robust measure of regional neural synchronization. The ROC analysis used ReHo values without covariate adjustment for factors such as sex, education, head motion, or site, to directly evaluate the discriminatory potential of ReHo. The analysis was performed on the same sample used for group comparisons, consistent with its exploratory objective and without a validation cohort, as recommended for preliminary biomarker investigation [46]. For each selected ROI, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as an indicator of discriminatory power, along with measures of accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. ROC curves visually represented the diagnostic performance of ReHo patterns within each age subgroup.

Associations with clinical variables

Post-hoc analyses were conducted to examine potential relationships between ReHo alterations and clinical features in MDD patients. Partial correlation analyses were performed between ReHo values in significant regions and HAMD-17 total scores or illness duration, controlling for sex, age, education, head motion, and site. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic data

The demographic and clinical characteristics of participants across the three age groups are presented in Table 1. All data were confirmed to conform to a normal distribution, supporting the use of parametric statistical tests. In the young group, MDD patients were significantly younger than healthy controls (t = 5.460, p < 0.001), a trend also observed in the middle-aged group (t = 3.539, p < 0.001). However, no significant age difference was found in the adult group (t = 1.834, p = 0.067). Regarding sex distribution, significant differences were noted only in the adult group, with a higher proportion of females in the MDD group compared to controls (χ² = 14.297, p < 0.001). Adults with MDD also had significantly fewer years of education compared to their healthy counterparts (t = 5.220, p < 0.001), while no differences in educational attainment were found in the young (t = 0.210, p = 0.834) or middle-aged groups (t = 1.483, p = 0.139).

Head motion was significantly higher in healthy controls relative to patients in the middle-aged group (t = 2.431, p = 0.016), but no significant differences were observed in the young (t = 0.384, p = 0.701) or adult groups (t = 1.820, p = 0.069). Moreover, ANCOVA revealed no significant differences in HAMD scores (F = 2.107, p = 0.123) or illness duration (F = 1.963, p = 0.142) across the three MDD subgroups.

ROC analysis

The ROC analysis highlighted the ability of ReHo in specific brain regions to distinguish MDD patients from healthy controls (Fig. 4). A threshold of AUC ≥ 0.60 was used to determine minimal diagnostic utility in this exploratory analysis, with significant p-values (p < 0.05) indicating discriminatory power [46]. No FDR correction was applied to the ROC analyses, as these were exploratory assessments aimed at identifying candidate biomarkers, and additional correction could reduce sensitivity for detecting preliminary findings [45]. In the adult subgroup, prefrontal regions showed moderate discriminatory power, with the right superior frontal gyrus (AUC = 0.653, p = 0.023), left middle frontal gyrus (AUC = 0.623, p = 0.024), and right inferior frontal gyrus (AUC = 0.628, p = 0.024), suggesting potential diagnostic utility. Similarly, the middle-aged subgroup exhibited variable performance, with the right paracentral lobule (AUC = 0.664, p = 0.029) and left middle occipital gyrus (AUC = 0.610, p = 0.029) meeting the threshold, while right inferior temporal gyrus (AUC = 0.580, p = 0.030) approached the threshold, indicating limited but notable potential. In contrast, the young subgroup exhibited poor discriminatory performance, with the right middle frontal gyrus (AUC = 0.558, p = 0.058), right superior parietal lobule (AUC = 0.509, p = 0.753), and left inferior temporal gyrus (AUC = 0.543, p = 0.162) all below the threshold and mostly non-significant, reflecting age-related variability and negligible clinical utility.

Associations with clinical measures

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore associations between ReHo alterations and clinical symptom (HAMD) scores and illness duration in MDD patients. Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed the normality of ReHo values and clinical variables prior to analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed for each brain region showing significant ReHo differences across all MDD subgroups and the entire MDD group. However, no significant correlations were observed between reHo alterations and the HAMD score or illness duration across all MDD subgroups or within the entire MDD group (FDR-corrected p > 0.05 for all comparisons). These correlation coefficients and their p-values are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

This study identified significant age-related variations in ReHo across three age-stratified MDD subgroups, revealing a progressive decline in ReHo with age. Notably, these alternations primarily involved neural networks associated with sensory, cognitive, and emotional processing, exhibiting a pronounced desynchronization with age. These results suggest that the neurobiological impact of MDD is modulated by dynamic neurodevelopmental processes across the lifespan, providing insights into stage-specific vulnerabilities that could guide targeted interventions to mitigate disease progression.

ReHo reductions in younger MDD patients were notable in regions such as the right middle frontal gyrus, right superior parietal lobule, and left inferior temporal gyrus. These areas, critical for spatial attention, working memory, and visual recognition [47–49], exhibit reduced local neural synchronization in early-stage MDD, potentially disrupting frontoparietal and temporal networks essential for cognitive processing. This suggests that early MDD may impair the integration of sensory and cognitive information, contributing to deficits in attention and memory often observed in younger patients [50]. In adult MDD patients, decreased ReHo was observed in prefrontal regions including the right superior frontal gyrus, left middle frontal gyrus, and right inferior frontal gyrus. These findings aligns with research linking MDD to dysfunctions in the prefrontal cortex [51, 52], a key hub for emotion regulation and decision-making [53, 54]. Reduced ReHo in these regions likely reflects impaired excitatory-inhibitory balance within the prefrontal cortex, exacerbating emotional dysregulation and cognitive control deficits characteristic of adult MDD [55]. In middle-aged MDD patients, ReHo alterations were identified in the right paracentral lobule and left middle occipital gyrus. Decreased ReHo in the paracentral lobule, involved in sensorimotor control [56], may reflect disrupted sensorimotor network synchronization, potentially driven by altered GABAergic inhibition [57], contributing to psychomotor retardation commonly observed in MDD [58]. Similarly, disruptions in the middle occipital gyrus, essential for visual processing [59], indicate impaired sensory integration, which may disrupt higher-order cognitive functions by limiting the quality of sensory input to frontoparietal networks [60]. These distinct age-specific ReHo patterns highlight how MDD disrupts neural coherence across sensory, cognitive, and emotional domains, reflecting progressive network degeneration. Alternatively, these lower ReHo values may reflect reduced synaptic density due to disrupted synaptic pruning and excitotoxicity, particularly in younger patients, which could drive early network dysfunction and contribute to the progressive desynchronization observed across age groups [61]. These alterations may not be explained by a single mechanism; rather, their nature appears to vary by brain region and disease stage, encompassing stable trait-level markers, state-dependent changes, and, in certain cases, compensatory adaptations. Persistent prefrontal reductions observed across all age groups are most consistent with trait-level markers of vulnerability to MDD [62], whereas age-specific changes in sensory and parietal regions may more likely reflect state-dependent effects reflecting current symptom burden [63]. In some cases, increased or redistributed activity in non-core regions could indicate compensatory adaptations that serve to maintain network efficiency despite primary deficits [64]. Definitively distinguishing among these mechanisms will require longitudinal studies and investigations of treatment-related changes.

ANCOVA results corroborated these findings, revealing significant age effects on ReHo in regions such as the postcentral gyrus, left superior parietal lobule, and superior temporal gyrus. Post-hoc analysis indicated a progressive ReHo decline, with the most pronounced reductions in middle-aged patients. For the left postcentral gyrus, responsible for somatosensory processing [65], this decline likely reflects worsening sensory network desynchronization, potentially linked to altered glutamatergic signaling in aging MDD patients [66]. Similarly, ReHo reductions in the left superior parietal lobule, critical for spatial attention and sensory integration [67, 68], suggest age-dependent disruptions in frontoparietal network connectivity, contributing to attention and spatial awareness deficits. Alterations in the superior temporal gyrus, associated with emotional processing [69], indicate progressive desynchronization in limbic-temporal circuits, exacerbating emotional dysregulation in older MDD patients [70]. These findings emphasize that the impact MDD on cognitive, sensory, and emotional processing intensifies with age, compounding over the lifespan and contributing to symptoms of late-life depression [71, 72]. In this context, the progressive decline in sensory and parietal regions may largely reflect state-dependent network deterioration, while the recurrence of certain deficits across multiple age groups supports the presence of a trait-level component. Given this age-related progression, ReHo patterns may serve as biomarkers for early MDD diagnosis, enabling targeted screening in younger patients to prevent cognitive decline and guiding interventions like neuromodulation in middle-aged patients to mitigate worsening symptoms.

In addition to age-related changes, ANCOVA demonstrated a significant disease effect in the right superior frontal gyrus, where ReHo was consistently lower in MDD patients compared to healthy controls across all age groups. This persistent reduction, in a region crucial for cognitive control and emotion regulation [73, 74], is most consistent with a trait-level neural marker, given its stability across age groups and its concordance with prior evidence of enduring prefrontal dysfunction in MDD [75]. These results illustrate the widespread, age-specific, and enduring effects of MDD on regional brain connectivity [76, 77], offering insights into how its progressive neural impact.

The interaction effect of age and disease revealed significant ReHo differences in the right superior occipital gyrus, highlighting its critical role in the neurobiological underpinnings of MDD. This region, integral to visual processing and sensory integration [78, 79], is selectively affected due to age-related declines in neuroplasticity, which disrupted occipitoparietal connectivity essential for coordinating sensory input with cognitive and emotional processing [80]. In older MDD patients, prolonged disease course amplifies glutamatergic dysregulation, exacerbating sensory network desynchronization compared to younger patients or healthy controls, potentially linked to deficits in cognitive control and emotional regulation [81]. The occipital alterations are more likely to reflect state-dependent dysfunction driven by cumulative disease burden and age-related network deterioration. Nonetheless, a compensatory component cannot be entirely excluded, as occipitoparietal reorganization may partly represent adaptive responses to upstream network inefficiency [82].

ROC analysis demonstrated the capability of ReHo metrics to discern MDD patients from healthy controls across different age subgroups. In the adult subgroup, prefrontal regions exhibited moderate discriminatory power, including the right superior frontal gyrus, left middle frontal gyrus, and right inferior frontal gyrus, reaffirming the critical role of the prefrontal cortex in MDD pathophysiology. Dysfunction in these regions is closely associated with deficits in executive function, emotional regulation, and decision-making frequently observed in depression [83–85]. For the middle-aged subgroup, significant discriminatory performance was observed in the right paracentral lobule, right inferior temporal gyrus, and left middle occipital gyrus, suggesting that sensory and cognitive impairments are especially salient in this group [86, 87]. However, a limited discriminatory performance in the young subgroup for regions such as the right middle frontal gyrus, right superior parietal lobule, and left inferior temporal gyrus, which may reflect the neurobiological heterogeneity of MDD in younger patients and the influence of ongoing brain maturation on ReHo patterns [88, 89]. This suggests that ReHo alone may have limited diagnostic utility in early-stage MDD, underscoring the need for multimodal biomarkers to improve accuracy [90].

Despite the significant ReHo changes observed in various brain regions, no significant correlations were found between ReHo alterations and the clinical symptom scores or illness duration in MDD patients. This lack of significant associations may be attributed to several factors. First, the heterogeneity of MDD symptoms across patients, even within subgroups, may obscure direct correlations, as ReHo alterations could reflect diverse neurobiological profiles rather than uniform clinical severity [90]. Second, ReHo, as a measure of local neural coherence, may not fully capture the complex, distributed network dysfunctions underlying symptom severity or chronicity in MDD [91]. Third, unmeasured variables, such as neurocognitive impairments or deficits in emotional regulation, may mediate the relationship between ReHo changes and clinical outcomes, potentially explaining the absence of direct associations [92, 93]. The absence of significant correlations further supports the notion that ReHo may serve as a state-independent biomarker, reflecting stable neural alterations rather than transient fluctuations in symptom intensity. This finding potentially implicates ReHo as an indicator of trait-level vulnerability to MDD rooted in early neurodevelopmental perturbations [94]. This is compounded by the inherent limitations of HAMD-17, which primarily measures state-dependent symptoms and may not capture underlying trait-related markers. Additionally, reliance on illness duration as a clinical metric overlooks the considerable heterogeneity in disease onset and neurobiological progression, thereby necessitating more nuanced clinical correlations such as connectivity-based measures. Considering that unadjusted ReHo values and the absence of a validation cohort, these findings are explicitly framed as exploratory. While the results suggest the potential diagnostic value of ReHo metrics, they should be regarded as preliminary and interpreted with caution. The implementation of covariate adjustments (e.g., for age, sex, and clinical factors) in conjunction with cross-validation is warranted to address potential confounding and ensure generalizability, thereby enhancing the overall reliability and validity of the findings.

The present study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the progressive ReHo decline observed across age groups, and does not permit confirmation of the temporal trajectory of network desynchronization. Additionally, despite covariate adjustments for site effects, inter-site variability within the multisite REST-meta-MDD dataset may introduce heterogeneity in both data acquisition and clinical assessments. Furthermore, reliance solely on resting-state fMRI restricts understanding of task-related neural dynamics, which may uncover additional MDD-specific circuit alterations. Another limitation is the absence of scrubbing in our preprocessing pipeline, which may have impacted on results, particularly considering known age-related differences in head motion. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and task-based fMRI to validate the observed ReHo decline across age groups and explore dynamic neural responses, while integrating multimodal neuroimaging provide a more comprehensive understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying MDD, ultimately improving early detection, monitoring of disease progression, and personalized intervention strategies.

Conclusion

This study investigated age-related differences in ReHo between patients with first-episode MDD and healthy controls, marked by progressive disruption in neural synchronization with increasing age. These results underscore the dynamic interplay of developmental processes in shaping the neural landscape of depression and highlight the need for age-tailored diagnostic biomarkers and interventions to address stage-specific circuit vulnerabilities.
