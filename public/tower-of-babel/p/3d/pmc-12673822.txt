Shielding Interventional Echocardiographers From Radiation Hazards
Authors: Soo-young Lee, Si-Hyuck Kang, Tae-Jin Youn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

The last decade has observed a substantial growth in the areas of structural heart disease intervention driven largely by the adoption of transcatheter aortic valve replacement, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, and transcatheter left atrial appendage closure. This trend has impacted the field of echocardiography as well as interventional cardiology. Interventional echocardiography is now widely recognized as a subspecialty of cardiology. Interventional echocardiographers play a crucial role in every aspect of percutaneous structural interventions from patient selection to final device assessment.1

Because the transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is the key tool for interventional echocardiographers, their location in the catheterization lab is necessarily close to the patient. Studies have indicated that radiation exposure is higher for interventional echocardiographers than for interventional cardiologists.2, 3, 4 Whereas radiation protection has long been a priority in interventional and electrophysiology fields, the standard protective equipment, such as ceiling-suspended shields, is designed for and positioned to protect the primary operator.5

In this issue of the JACC: Asia, Kataoka et al6 presents their effort to build a novel radiation shield for interventional echocardiographers. Notably, the investigators performed a meticulous study measuring the distribution of radiation exposure on the body surface of interventional echocardiographers during structural heart disease procedures several years previously.7 It was found that the right waist and lower body of interventional echocardiographers were exposed to high radiation doses especially when the posterior-anterior or the right anterior oblique angle fluoroscopic directions were used.

In the current study, Kataoka et al6 developed a radiation shield dedicated to interventional echocardiographers based on their previous observations while preserving the operator’s ability to manipulate the TEE probe. A free-standing lead board was chosen instead of a ceiling-mounted screen based on simulation that the latter design would provide protection only near the head. Two lateral windows were made on the board to enable TEE manipulation. The width and height of the windows were carefully chosen by assessing the workability of interventional echocardiographers using a phantom patient model and measuring radiation exposure using the Monte Carlo simulation. The different designs of the 2 windows on the board reflect their delicate and careful work: one window on the left side for interventional echocardiographers, and the other window on the right side for anesthesiologists. Finally, a significant reduction in real-world radiation exposure was demonstrated in a prospective, nonrandomized, single-center study. The reduction was most remarkable in the waist area.

The beauty of the study by Kataoka et al6 lies in its pragmatic, multistep approach to solving a real-world problem. The design process thoughtfully balanced human factors—ensuring TEE probe maneuverability without undue physical strain—with sophisticated modeling to minimize radiation exposure, followed by rigorous clinical testing. The work by Kataoka et al6 is a call to action to consider radiation protection of every participant in structural intervention. Purpose-built equipment, like the shield described here, is an excellent starting point. We anticipate it will inspire future research and more brilliant ideas for enhanced protection for the entire Heart Team.
