Are ERBB2 mutations a ‘ready for routine use’ target in patients with metastatic breast cancer?
Authors: T Grinda, B Pistilli
Published: 2023

In oncology, the term ‘precision medicine’ usually refers to the identification of the treatments that are likely to provide the greatest benefit to patients with tumors harboring mutations in genes with a critical role in tumor development and progression.1 Undoubtedly, the successful targeting of the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (ERBB2/HER2) signaling pathway has been one of the major achievements of precision medicine over the past three decades. Although activation of HER2 signaling is in general via gene amplification or HER2 overexpression, ERBB2 activating mutations can also occur and lead to HER2 signaling dependence via a constitutively active conformation of HER2 or increased dimerization (HER2–HER2, HER2–HER3).2, 3, 4 While treatment of tumors with HER2 overexpression or amplification made major strides over the past few years, targeting HER2 mutations posed hurdles because their incidence is very low (around 2%-3% of breast cancers) and ERBB2-mutant breast cancers were not sensitive to HER2-directed antibodies and reversible tyrosine kinase inhibitors.2,5 Additionally, in hormone receptor-positive (HR+) metastatic breast cancer (mBC), HER2 mutations have been identified as a mechanism of acquired resistance to estrogen receptor (ER)-directed therapies, by virtue of an extensive cross-talk between the two signaling pathways.3,4,6 Indeed, the prevalence of HER2 hotspot mutations is more than doubled in patients with HR+ mBC previously exposed to endocrine therapy, including selective ER modulators or down-regulators (SERMs, SERDs) and aromatase inhibitors.6,7 Lately, preclinical and initial clinical data also revealed that the RAS/MAPK signaling remained activated in HER2-mutant breast cancers exposed to cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)4/6 inhibitors, suggesting that HER2 mutations might serve as a resistance mechanism to this class of drugs.3,8,9

Despite these challenges, several trials recently suggested that ERBB2-mutant breast cancers could represent a genomic segment sensitive to targeted therapies, and that ERBB2 mutations should be ranked European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) 1, i.e. the highest level of evidence of actionability. Neratinib is an oral irreversible pan-HER tyrosine kinase inhibitor that, unlike other HER2-targeting therapies, showed substantial activity across a wide range of HER2 mutations, particularly in breast, biliary and cervical cancers.10 Hyman et al. showed that neratinib single agent was associated with 17% objective response and 3.6 months median progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with ERBB2 mutations who were heavily pretreated. Interestingly, patients with hotspot kinase or extracellular domain mutations presented a higher sensitivity.10 This evidence, along with a wealth of preclinical observations, made clear that neratinib is critical for targeting HER2 mutations but not enough to target both HER2 and ER signaling, as the inhibition of one pathway up-regulated the other one.4,10 Since ERBB2 mutations could be enriched in patients with HR+ mBC and could mediate resistance to endocrine therapy, a phase II trial has tested the combination of neratinib with fulvestrant. This combination showed better outcomes, with a 29.8% objective response rate (ORR) and a median PFS of 5.4 months.11 However, biomarker analysis from this cohort suggested that the activity of neratinib in this setting could be further improved. Indeed, the co-existence upfront of HER3 mutations or multiple HER2 mutations or the acquisition of additional HER2 mutations or amplification at progression on neratinib + fulvestrant was associated with worse treatment outcomes, underlining the need for a more complete HER2 signaling inhibition. These findings, along with preclinical evidence showing that the addition of trastuzumab to neratinib prevents HER2–HER3 heterodimerization and obtains a greater inhibition of HER2 signaling,12 were the rational to test the addition of trastuzumab to neratinib + fulvestrant. In a recent paper, Jhaveri et al. presented the efficacy and safety of the triplet combination, showing an ORR of 39% and a median PFS of 8.3 months.13 These results are of particular clinical relevance given that all patients were heavily pretreated (median of three prior lines of therapy, the majority pretreated with fulvestrant), had prior exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors and the presence of additional HER2 or HER3 mutations did not affect treatment efficacy. However, on the flip side, the addition of trastuzumab and fulvestrant to neratinib seems to increase the risk of grade 3 diarrhea that was almost twice that of prior doublet therapy with neratinib, despite the mandatory loperamide prophylaxis. Since grade 3 diarrhea can take its toll on patients over the first weeks of treatment, further efforts should be made to prevent and manage this side-effect, if this combination is used in clinical practice.

Therefore, adding both trastuzumab and fulvestrant maximizes the efficacy of neratinib in patients with HR+/HER2-mutant mBC and may become a novel therapeutic approach in this setting; yet some critical questions remain. Firstly, while the results of the triplet in the SUMMIT trial are encouraging, it would not be possible to assign a ranking on the ESMO Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (MCBS) for single-arm studies, which is applicable so far to ‘orphan’ or ‘with high-unmet needs’ diseases and typically used for treatments with a single agent.14 Secondly, these results call into question whether the current ranking in the ESCAT of HER2mut should be updated from tier IIb to tier Ic and if testing should be routinely considered in patients with HR+/HER2 mBC previously exposed to estrogen deprivation.15, 16, 17 The third question is about positioning of the combination of neratinib + fulvestrant + trastuzumab. Combination between targeted therapies and endocrine therapies are traditionally introduced after resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors. However, it remains unclear whether emergence of HER2 mutations occurs upon exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors or mediates resistance upfront and therefore whether testing should be carried out before or after first-line therapy. Fourthly, how do the results of neratinib-containing triplet compare with other treatment options after progression on CDK4/6 inhibitors? Recently, the addition of capivasertib to fulvestrant exhibited a meaningful improved PFS (7.2 versus 3.6 months, hazard ratio 0.60, 95% confidence interval 0.51-0.71) among patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors. Considering that ERBB2 mutations hyperactivate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, there is a reasonable likelihood that this combination might also offer some efficacy in HER2-mutated patients, although no specific analysis by HER2mut were conducted in the CAPITELLO 291 trial.4,18 Moreover, given that HER2-mutant tumors are mostly HER2-low and trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) has demonstrated significant efficacy in HER2-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer, it becomes important to assess T-DXd outcomes in both the DESTINY-BREAST 04 and the upcoming DESTINY-BREAST 06 trials across patients harboring HER2 mutations19,20 Finally, while a large genomic analysis of breast cancer samples highlighted that ERBB2 and ESR1 mutations are mutually exclusive, studies on circulating tumor DNA showed that these alterations can co-exist in different subclones, thus raising the question whether further combinations of neratinib and trastuzumab with new SERDs could be even more active in patients with HER2-mutant endocrine-resistant breast cancers.6

Overall, targeting of ERBB2 mutations by neratinib in patients with mBC perfectly depicts the challenges of modern oncology. Indeed, HER2 mutations are very rare, interplay with other molecular pathways and could be potentially targeted by other drugs (such as capivasertib and trastuzumab deruxtecan), underlining the need for randomized trials in specific lines of therapies. However, the rarity of these mutations poses major hurdles to conducting randomized trials, emphasizing the necessity to revise existing frameworks for ranking new combinations with targeted therapies, which can be tested only in single-arm trials. Finally, it underlines the necessity to establish patient access to medications in situations where the likelihood of benefit is high, even though formal comparisons might not be feasible.
