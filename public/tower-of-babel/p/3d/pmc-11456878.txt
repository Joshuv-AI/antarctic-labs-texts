Introduction

Goldenhar syndrome (GS) was first documented by Maurice Goldenhar, an ophthalmologist and general practitioner in 1952. It is also known as oculoauriculovertebral dysplasia or hemifacial microsomia which manifests as a triad of ocular, auricular, and vertebral anomalies, encompassing a spectrum of clinical presentations marked by craniofacial, vertebral, cardiac, renal, and central nervous system anomalies [1,2]. Its etiology is rooted in developmental defects affecting the first and second brachial arches [3]. Common systemic features of GS include microtia, mandibular hypoplasia, and vertebral anomalies [4,5]. The common ocular manifestations in GS include microphthalmia, epibulbar dermoid/limbal dermoid, lipodermoid, coloboma, strabismus, and Duane retraction syndrome (DRS). Epicanthal folds, anophthalmia, lacrimal drainage system anomalies, retinal and optic nerve anomalies, and glaucoma have also been reported [[6], [7], [8]]. MED may be congenitally or acquired, presents as a unilateral limitation of elevation, stemming from paretic, restrictive, or combined etiologies and is associated with hypotropia, often coinciding with ptosis/pseudoptosis [9]. Remarkably, the co-occurrence of MED with Goldenhar syndrome has not been previously documented. Hence, we present a case study detailing MED in association with Goldenhar syndrome secondary to radiologically proven vascular insult and provide a comprehensive review of the relevant literature.

Presentation of case

A 9-year-old male presented with drooping of upper eyelid OD, absence of the right ear, and a persistent swelling near the ear since birth as noticed by his mother. The antenatal and birth history were unremarkable and non-consanguineous with a negative family history. Ophthalmic examination revealed right mild simple congenital ptosis (2 mm) with absent upper eyelid crease (Fig. 1), fair Bell's phenomenon, poor levator action (4 mm) and absence of any abnormal head posture. The best corrected visual acuity with Snellen's chart was 6/24 in OD with a refractive correction of +5.50 DS/−1.50 × 140 and 6/9 in the left eye (OS) with a refractive correction of +1.00 DS/−0.50 × 20. Anterior segment examination was unremarkable in both eyes. The patient was orthophoric in primary gaze. Extraocular movements showed a marked limitation of elevation (−3) in OD in all three upgazes (Fig. 2). Posterior segment examination with indirect ophthalmoscope of OD showed grade IV extorsion and OS showed grade I intorsion (Fig. 3). Axial length was 20.56 mm and 21.91 mm in the right and left eye respectively on an optical biometer suggesting right microphthalmos. A forced duction test was conducted under sedation for elevation deficiency in OD, yielding negative results and ruling out restrictive etiology. On local examination, the patient was found to have right-sided hemifacial hypoplasia, shortened malformed right upper eyelid with lagophthalmos and loss of nasolabial fold with facial deviation to the left side, and weakness of the right buccinator muscle (Fig. 4A). The patient was evaluated by an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) consultant, who revealed a grade III microtia and a mass measuring approximately 3 × 6 cm near the external auditory meatus. The mass was vertically oval with no skin changes. It was soft, non-tender, non-compressible, with ill-defined boundaries on palpation (Fig. 4B). Pure tone audiometry revealed moderately severe conductive hearing loss on the right side. Ultrasonography (USG) of the face and neck revealed features suggestive of a first branchial cyst, likely infective, with stenosed common carotid artery (grade I). High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) temporal bone revealed non-visualization of the right facial nerve canal in its entire course after the canalicular part along with hypoplastic right petrous bone. It also revealed stenosed right external auditory meatus and middle ear cavity. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CEMRI) of the face and neck confirmed the findings of HRCT and revealed a well-defined cystic lesion in the posteroinferior aspect of the right pinna, likely a first branchial cleft cyst (Fig. 5A, B). Mild right cerebral hemi atrophy likely due to vascular insufficiency was noted (Fig. 5C). The right common carotid, internal, and external carotid arteries were attenuated. The right internal carotid was severely attenuated and tapered down to complete occlusion (Fig. 5D). Hypoplastic right petrous bone and non-visualization of the right facial nerve were also confirmed on CEMRI. The patient was evaluated by the Pediatric team to rule out other systemic associations and was advised USG of the abdomen which was unremarkable. X-ray of the spine was within normal limits. The cardiac workup of the patient was also unremarkable. Based on the above clinical findings, a diagnosis of right -sided MED (due to Superior rectus palsy) with mild ptosis, moderate anisometropic amblyopia, microphthalmos, and right-sided lower motor neuron facial nerve palsy was made. Additionally, the presence of a right infected first branchial cyst with the stenosed common carotid artery, radiological findings confirmed Goldenhar syndrome. The patient was managed conservatively with a spectacle prescription and amblyopia management by part-time occlusion therapy as he was orthophoric in the primary position. The ptosis correction of deferred because of right-sided hemifacial hypoplasia, right shortened malformed upper eyelid, and lagophthalmos, which were contraindications for ptosis surgery. The patient was referred to the plastic surgery department for further management of the right infected branchial cyst, which was excised under broad-spectrum antibiotic cover. The patient has been planned for staged otoplasty followed by external ear reconstruction by the plastic surgery team. Later on, the ENT team will take care of the conductive deafness on right side. Discussion

Goldenhar syndrome, also referred to as oculoauriculovertebral (OAV) dysplasia, is a complex disorder encompassing a spectrum of craniofacial abnormalities. This condition is characterized by a trio of primary features: hemifacial microsomia, ear and ocular involvement, and vertebral anomalies probably caused by a local ischemic insult or abnormal neural crest cell migration during the fourth week of gestation [11]. Research indicates that its prevalence varies, occurring in approximately 1 out of every 3500 to 7000 live births [12], with a majority of cases being sporadic in nature.

Several theories have been offered to explain the origins of the OAV spectrum, but none have been validated until now. OAV abnormalities affect various systems, including the heart, lungs, neurological system, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal. The most common malformations are facial asymmetry (65 %), epibulbar dermoids (35 %), and external ear anomalies (ranging from anotia to mildly dysmorphic) with pre-auricular tags (skin tags located between the mouth angle and the tragus). Microtia and/or auricular tags have a 100 % occurrence [10,11].

Ocular manifestations are common in Goldenhar syndrome, affecting approximately 30–60 % of individuals with the condition. These manifestations can include a range of abnormalities such as anophthalmia (absence of one or both eyes), microphthalmia (abnormally small eyes), blepharophimosis, epi-bulbar tumors, strabismus, lid coloboma, epicanthal folds, lacrimal drainage abnormalities, glaucoma, DRS, and epibulbar dermoid [13,14]. Craniofacial abnormalities are highly prevalent in individuals with Goldenhar syndrome, affecting approximately 80–99 % of cases. These abnormalities may include hemifacial microsomia, cleft lip or palate, cranium bifidum, microcephaly, dolichocephaly, and plagiocephaly. Additionally, spinal abnormalities are observed in 40–60 % of cases, and individuals may also present with mental retardation and cardiac defects such as tetralogy of Fallot [15].

Several factors have been identified as potential contributors to the development of Goldenhar syndrome, including exposure to certain medications, vascular insults during early gestation, maternal diabetes, bleeding during the second trimester of pregnancy, malnutrition, and maternal tobacco use. Consanguineous marriages may also pose an increased risk [13]. Congenital malformations of the carotid vessels, such as agenesis, aplasia, and hypoplasia, are uncommon occurrences. These abnormalities impact the internal carotid artery in less than 0.01 % of the population [16]. However, there have been around 8 reported cases of unilateral absence of the external carotid artery and less than 25 cases of absence of the common carotid artery so far [17].

Furthermore, there is limited literature linking Goldenhar syndrome with abnormalities of the external and/or common carotid artery on the same side as hemifacial microsomia. Only one reported case has shown an association between OAV syndrome and the absence of the internal carotid artery on the same side, and one on the opposite side [18,19].

In addition to being a rare occurrence, the correlation between Goldenhar syndrome and the previously mentioned carotideal anomalies may support the vascular theory, which holds that the OAV spectrum's appearance may be influenced by an early reduction in blood flow in the cephalic region at the I and II branchial arches.

In our case, the patient had moderate ptosis, microphthalmos, and MED as ophthalmic associations of Goldenhar syndrome, which were not reported in the literature to date. Furthermore, the report presents a unique and rare combination of findings, including the absence of the seventh cranial nerve on the same side, an infected brachial cyst, conductive hearing loss, and the absence of the intracranial component of the internal carotid artery on the same side supporting the vascular etiology of the condition.

Due to the complex nature of Goldenhar syndrome and its associated anomalies, a multidisciplinary approach to patient care is essential. This typically involves collaboration among pediatric specialists, ear-nose-throat specialists, orthopedic surgeons, neurosurgeons, orthodontists, maxillofacial surgeons, and ophthalmologists. Together, these experts can develop a tailored treatment plan that addresses the unique needs of each patient, taking into account factors such as age and systemic associations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while Goldenhar syndrome presenting with MED is relatively rare, a coordinated and comprehensive approach to care is crucial for optimizing patient outcomes and well-being.
