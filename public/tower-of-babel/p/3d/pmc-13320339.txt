Predictive Value of the Intermountain Mortality Risk Score for Saphenous Vein Graft Degeneration: A Retrospective Cohort Analysis
Authors: Suha ASAL, Sahhan KILIC, Mert BABAOGLU, Mohaddeseh Davoudi BILEHSAVAR, Yusuf Turan GUL, Hatice ALTINISIK, Samet YAVUZ, Akin TORUN, Didar MIRZAMIDINOV, Tufan CINAR, Ahmet L. ORHAN
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe Intermountain Risk Score (IMRS), derived from routine laboratory parameters, predicts cardiovascular outcomes. Its association with saphenous vein graft degeneration (SVGD) post-coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains underexplored.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 261 CABG patients who underwent coronary angiography. SVGD was defined as ≥50% graft stenosis. IMRS was calculated using laboratory data, age, and sex. Multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were used to assess IMRS’s predictive value for SVGD.ResultsOf the 261 patients, 138 (52.9%) had SVGD. The SVGD group had higher median IMRS scores (12.5 vs. 10, p<0.001). After adjusting for confounders, IMRS was an independent predictor of SVGD (OR 1.640, 95% CI 1.310-2.054, p<0.001), as were chronic kidney disease and smoking. ROC analysis showed an area under the curve of 0.676, with a cutoff of IMRS >12 yielding a specificity of 83% and a sensitivity of 50%.ConclusionsElevated IMRS independently predicts SVGD in post-CABG patients. As a cost-effective tool, IMRS may guide preoperative risk stratification and postoperative prevention strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains a cornerstone of treatment for patients with complex or multivessel coronary artery disease, offering durable revascularization and superior long-term outcomes compared to percutaneous interventions in select populations1. However, the long-term success of this landmark procedure is frequently compromised by the progressive failure of its most commonly utilized conduit, the saphenous vein graft (SVG)2. Despite the established superiority of arterial conduits, SVGs are still employed in over 90% of CABG procedures, often to supplement a left internal mammary artery graft to the left anterior descending artery3. This continued reliance is overshadowed by the SVG’s inherent vulnerability to degeneration; failure rates are estimated at 10-20% within the first postoperative year, escalating dramatically to 40-50% by the 10-year mark4. This high rate of attrition is not a benign phenomenon, as it frequently precipitates recurrent angina, myocardial infarction, the need for high-risk repeat revascularization procedures, and a significant reduction in both survival and quality of life5.

The pathophysiology of saphenous vein graft degeneration (SVGD) is a multifactorial process that serves as a compelling model of accelerated, inflammation-driven vascular disease6. Unlike the decades-long progression of native coronary atherosclerosis, SVGD unfolds on a compressed timeline, driven by a sequence of overlapping pathological phases: early thrombosis, intermediate intimal hyperplasia, and late-stage accelerated atherosclerosis7. The entire cascade is initiated by the acute and profound endothelial injury sustained during graft harvesting, surgical preparation via manual distension, and subsequent exposure to the unaccustomed high- pressure, high-shear- stress arterial environment. This initial trauma acts as a potent trigger, disrupting the endothelium’s critical homeostatic functions and shifting its phenotype towards a pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory state6. This injury incites a maladaptive healing response, manifesting as intimal hyperplasia, which is characterized by the rampant proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix, leading to progressive luminal narrowing7. A persistent state of both systemic and local inflammation is the critical driver of this process. Inflammatory cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages, infiltrate the graft wall, releasing a milieu of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that perpetuate VSMC proliferation and create a fertile ground for lipid deposition and the eventual development of friable, rupture-prone atherosclerotic plaques8.

The challenge of predicting and preventing SVGD is compounded by the limitations of conventional risk assessment models. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus, while central to the development of native vessel atherosclerosis, have demonstrated limited and inconsistent utility in predicting graft-specific outcomes2. This “detection gap” suggests that the patient’s systemic biological milieu may play a more dominant role than individual risk factors in determining graft fate8. The failure of these traditional metrics has spurred the evolution of multi-parameter risk scores, which aggregate multiple clinical and biomarker data points to provide a more holistic and accurate assessment of individual risk9. While scores like the Framingham Risk Score and the more recent PREVENT equations have refined primary prevention, a similarly robust and accessible tool for secondary prevention in the post-CABG population is urgently needed10, 11.

The Intermountain Risk Score (IMRS) has emerged as a novel, laboratory-based prognostic tool with the potential to fill this void. Originally developed to predict all-cause mortality, the IMRS is calculated from 14 widely available and inexpensive parameters derived from a routine complete blood count and basic metabolic panel, and is integrated with patient age and sex. Its prognostic utility has been extensively validated in diverse clinical cohorts, where it has been shown to predict not only mortality but also a spectrum of major cardiovascular morbidities, including incident heart failure and myocardial infarction12. The strength of the IMRS lies in its capacity to capture the subtle systemic derangements that underpin cardiovascular pathology. Its components inherently reflect systemic inflammation (e.g., white blood cell (WBC) count, red cell distribution width), metabolic stress (e.g., glucose, creatinine), and hematologic status, all of which are recognized as key drivers of the SVGD process12.

Given that SVGD is fundamentally an inflammatory and metabolic disease process, it is plausible that the IMRS, which encapsulates these systemic disturbances, could serve as an effective predictor of graft failure8. While various individual inflammatory markers have been linked to SVGD, the predictive value of a comprehensive, integrated score like the IMRS remains underexplored13. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the hypothesis that an elevated IMRS is independently associated with the presence of SVGD in a cohort of patients who underwent coronary angiography following CABG.

MATERIALS and METHODS

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, single-center, observational study was conducted at a tertiary care center specializing in cardiovascular medicine. The study cohort was derived from a hospital database of patients who underwent coronary angiography between January 2019 and December 2023. The study protocol was designed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received full approval from the institutional review board of Hamidiye Faculty of Medicine (decision number: 24/185, date: 08.05.2025). Due to the de-identified and retrospective nature of the analysis, the requirement for individual patient informed consent was waived.

Participant Selection

Patients were identified from the hospital’s electronic health records. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a documented history of CABG surgery performed prior to 2019; (2) availability of coronary angiography results from the specified study period; (3) a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) >50%, a criterion established to mitigate the potential confounding effects of advanced heart failure on systemic inflammatory and metabolic markers; and (4) complete laboratory data from blood samples drawn within 24 hours prior to the index angiography, to allow accurate and timely IMRS calculation. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) no prior history of CABG; (2) LVEF ≤50%; (3) a history of concomitant major cardiac surgery, such as valve replacement, at the time of the initial CABG; (4) presentation with an acute coronary syndrome at the time of the follow-up angiography; or (5) incomplete or missing medical records for key demographic, clinical, or laboratory variables essential for the analysis.

Data Collection and Definitions

Data were systematically extracted from structured fields within the hospital’s electronic health records. Collected demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, and established cardiovascular risk factors: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, chronic kidney disease (CKD), peripheral artery disease, and history of cerebrovascular accidents.

The primary angiographic endpoint was the presence of SVGD. This was defined as a luminal stenosis of ≥50% in at least one SVG upon visual assessment by two independent, experienced interventional cardiologists who were blinded to the patients’ laboratory data and IMRS values to minimize assessment bias. The control group consisted of post-CABG patients with patent SVGs, defined as <50% stenosis in all vein grafts. Additional angiographic data, including the age of the SVG (calculated as the time from CABG surgery to the index angiography) and the coronary territory supplied by the graft [left anterior descending, circumflex (CX), or right coronary artery], were also recorded.

Blood samples drawn within 24 hours before angiography were analyzed for a complete blood count and a basic metabolic panel. The IMRS was calculated for each patient based on the sex-specific, weighted scores for 14 parameters: hematocrit, WBC count, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, calcium, glucose, and creatinine, along with age and sex. The calculation was performed according to the 30-day mortality algorithm published in the original IMRS study12. Following calculation of the numeric score, patients were stratified into three risk categories: low (score ≤10), moderate (score 11-12), and high (score >12).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics; continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the distribution of the data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %).

Group comparisons between the SVGD and control groups were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify potential predictors of SVGD. Variables demonstrating a potential association with SVGD at a p-value threshold of <0.10 in the univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. A backward stepwise selection method was employed to identify independent predictors of SVGD while adjusting for potential confounders. Results from the regression analyses were reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

The discriminatory ability of the continuous IMRS score and the categorical IMRS variable to predict SVGD was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify predictive performance. The optimal cutoff points were determined using the Youden index, a method that maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity.

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 261 patients who underwent post-CABG coronary angiography met the study’s inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The cohort was stratified into two groups based on angiographic findings: 138 patients (52.9%) with confirmed SVGD (SVGD group) and 123 patients (47.1%) with patent SVGs (control group).

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are detailed in Table 1. Patients in the SVGD group had significantly higher prevalences of diabetes mellitus (72.5% vs. 56.1%, p=0.006) and smoking (30.9% vs. 14.4%, p=0.003) compared with the control group. A critical differentiating factor was the age of the grafts; the median time from CABG to angiography was significantly greater in the SVGD group (7 years) than in the control group (4 years) (p=0.008). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, sex, prevalence of hypertension or hyperlipidemia, or in the distribution of baseline medical therapies.

Laboratory Findings and IMRS

A comprehensive comparison of baseline laboratory parameters is presented in Table 2. Patients in the SVGD group exhibited a profile suggestive of heightened inflammatory and metabolic stress. Specifically, the SVGD group had significantly higher median WBC count (8.1 vs. 7.5 ×103/µL, p=0.005), serum creatinine level (1.15 vs. 1.02 mg/dL, p=0.042), and blood glucose level (138 vs. 118 mg/dL, p=0.022).

The primary variable of interest, the IMRS, was a powerful discriminator between the two groups. The median IMRS score in patients with SVGD was significantly higher, at 12.5 (IQR 9-14), compared to 10 (IQR 8-12) in the control group (p<0.001). This difference was also evident when the score was analyzed by risk category: a significantly greater proportion of patients in the SVGD group fell into the moderate- and high-risk IMRS categories than in the control group, where patients were predominantly in the low-risk category (p<0.001).

Angiographic Characteristics

The distribution of SVGs to the major coronary territories (LAD, CX, RCA) was similar between the SVGD and control groups. The right coronary artery (RCA) was the most frequently grafted vessel in the overall cohort. Among patients with confirmed SVGD, degeneration was most prevalent in grafts supplying the RCA territory, which accounted for 47.1% of all degenerated grafts (Table 3).

Predictors of SVG Degeneration

Univariate logistic regression identified several clinical and laboratory factors associated with SVGD, including SVG age, diabetes mellitus, CKD, smoking, serum creatinine, IMRS score, and IMRS category (Table 4).

To isolate the most significant independent predictors, these variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. After adjusting for potential confounders, the IMRS score remained a strong and independent predictor of SVGD (OR 1.640, 95% CI 1.310-2.054, p<0.001). The IMRS category also retained its status as an independent predictor (OR 1.236, 95% CI 1.021-1.455, p=0.026). Other factors independently associated with SVGD in the final model were CKD (OR 2.348, 95% CI 1.144-4.819, p=0.020) and history of smoking (OR 3.133, 95% CI 1.397-7.026, p=0.005).

ROC Curve Analysis for IMRS

The overall predictive performance of the IMRS was assessed using ROC curve analysis, as shown in Figure 1, Table 5. For the continuous IMRS score, the area under the curve (AUC) for predicting SVGD was 0.676, indicating moderate discriminatory ability. Using an optimal cutoff value of >12 determined by the Youden index, the IMRS score demonstrated high specificity (83%) and sensitivity (50%). The AUC for the categorical IMRS variable was 0.605. A cut-off >1(denoting moderate or high-risk) yielded a specificity of 50% and a sensitivity of 69%.

DISCUSSION

This study provides novel and compelling evidence that the IMRS, a composite metric derived from routine laboratory tests, is a significant and independent predictor of SVG degeneration in patients following CABG. The central finding—that patients with SVGD exhibit markedly higher IMRS scores—persists even after rigorous statistical adjustment for major confounders, including CKD, smoking status, and graft age. This robust association underscores the score’s potential utility in identifying patients with a systemic biological profile that renders them vulnerable to late graft failure12.

The predictive power of the IMRS is not arbitrary; its constituent parts directly reflect the core pathophysiological mechanisms known to drive SVGD. A previous study has demonstrated IMRS as a predictor of coronary ectasia14. While relations of IMRS with coronary anatomy and coronary artery disease remain to be explored further, the score effectively serves as a holistic barometer of a patient’s cumulative systemic stress, integrating signals from the immune system, metabolic control, and renal function. The elevated WBC count, creatinine, and glucose levels observed in our SVGD cohort are not merely incidental findings but are quantitative indicators of the underlying disease processes12. An elevated WBC count is a direct marker of the systemic inflammation that fuels endothelial activation, monocyte infiltration, and VSMC proliferation within the graft wall15. Concurrently, elevated glucose levels signify poor glycemic control, a key contributor to endothelial dysfunction through the formation of advanced glycation end-products and increased oxidative stress, both of which are profoundly detrimental to graft patency16. Finally, elevated creatinine, reflecting the renal impairment of CKD, points to a uremic state that promotes vascular calcification, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation, creating a highly permissive milieu for accelerated graft atherosclerosis17. A high IMRS score, therefore, suggests that a patient’s homeostatic mechanisms are failing to cope with the cumulative burden of these risk factors, making them highly susceptible to the “second hit” of surgical trauma and hemodynamic change that precipitates SVGD. The score predicts failure because it measures the host’s inability to mount a successful adaptive response to these stressors12.

In the evolving landscape of inflammatory biomarkers for predicting SVGD, the IMRS offers a unique profile. The discriminatory ability observed in our study (AUC=0.676) indicates moderate predictive power, a finding consistent with its performance in other high-risk cardiovascular cohorts, such as patients with heart failure or ST-elevation myocardial infarction, where reported AUCs typically range from 0.65 to 0.7512. While other, more focused inflammatory indices have shown promise, the clinical value of IMRS lies in its breadth. For instance, studies on the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) have reported high sensitivity and specificity for SVGD, effectively capturing the immune-thrombotic axis of the disease18, 19. Similarly, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) has demonstrated excellent performance, confirming the central role of inflammation. However, these markers primarily assess a single domain of pathophysiology18, 19, 20. The IMRS, by contrast, is not just an inflammatory score; it is a global health score. A patient could have a high hs-CRP but be metabolically healthy, or have renal dysfunction without overt inflammation20. The IMRS captures all these domains simultaneously. Its strength lies in its ability to identify patients with a composite risk profile—for example, an individual with moderate inflammation, mild renal dysfunction, and borderline hyperglycemia—who might be missed by single-domain markers. It provides a more robust, real-world, comprehensive assessment of a patient’s vulnerability to graft failure. Recently, the Naples Prognostic Score, which also comprises parameters from various biological systems as part of a holistic approach to prognostic evaluation, has been found to be a predictor of SVGD21. This also suggests a direction for prognostic algorithms.

The clinical implications of these findings are profound and position the IMRS as a pragmatic, cost-effective, and readily implementable tool for preoperative risk stratification. By identifying high-risk individuals before surgery, clinicians can make more informed decisions about revascularization strategy. For a patient with a high preoperative IMRS, a surgeon might be guided to prioritize multiple arterial grafts, such as bilateral internal thoracic arteries or a radial artery, which have substantially superior long-term patency rates compared to SVGs and are more resilient to the patient’s adverse systemic environment. This represents a tangible, evidence-based strategy to mitigate the high risk of graft failure identified by the score22. Furthermore, a high IMRS can identify patients who are likely to benefit from more aggressive, personalized postoperative secondary prevention12. This could involve intensified lipid-lowering therapy, stricter glycemic control, consideration of novel anti-inflammatory agents, and a more rigorous schedule of clinical and imaging surveillance following discharge. In the future, risk stratification could be enhanced by developing hybrid models that combine a systemic biomarker score like IMRS with anatomical scores (e.g., SYNTAX score) or advanced secondary prevention scores like the SMART2 score, which has been validated in post-CABG cohorts, to create a highly personalized risk profile to guide all aspects of patient care23.

It is important to contextualize the IMRS within the broader landscape of inflammatory and vasoactive biomarkers that have been investigated in the pathogenesis of SVGD. Beyond the hematological indices constituting the IMRS, specific molecular mediators play central mechanistic roles in graft degeneration that merit discussion. hs-CRP is a canonical downstream effector of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling axis and a well-validated marker of vascular inflammation; preoperative hs-CRP elevation has been associated with early graft occlusion and adverse cardiovascular events following CABG. The C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio, a composite inflammatory index of particular relevance in the post-CABG literature, has been independently associated with SVGD severity in large-scale clinical cohorts, underscoring the additive value of combining acute-phase reactants with nutritional markers. Endothelin-1, a potent vasoconstrictor released by activated endothelial cells following graft trauma and exposure to arterial pressure, is upregulated in diseased vein grafts and promotes smooth muscle cell proliferation through paracrine signaling. Concurrently, the reduction in bioavailable nitric oxide (NO), resulting from endothelial denudation during surgical preparation and abrogation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression, removes a critical anti-thrombotic and anti-proliferative safeguard, rendering the conduit susceptible to the full cascade of SVGD. Adhesion molecules, including vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and selectins, mediate the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the injured endothelium and their expression in the graft wall is upregulated within hours of surgical handling. While the IMRS does not directly quantify any of these molecules, its constituent hematological and metabolic parameters collectively reflect the systemic milieu that governs the upregulation of these mediators. Future studies specifically designed to examine the co-association of IMRS with hs-CRP, IL-6, soluble endothelin-1, flow-mediated dilation, and endothelial adhesion molecules would substantially enrich the mechanistic understanding of the present association and may support the development of a more granular, multi-domain prediction model for SVGD.

Study Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, observational design establishes a strong association, but precludes any definitive conclusions regarding causality; it cannot be demonstrate that a high IMRS directly causes SVGD. The observed relationship between IMRS and SVGD represents a statistical association derived from observational data; as with all retrospective designs, this study is inherently susceptible to residual and unmeasured confounding. A high IMRS score reflects a systemic biological state that may predispose to graft degeneration, but the mechanistic pathway linking an elevated composite laboratory score to structural graft failure cannot be delineated from the present data alone and therefore requires dedicated translational and prospective investigation to establish. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other patient populations with different demographic characteristics, comorbidity profiles, or local clinical practices. Third, there is potential for selection bias because the study population was limited to patients who underwent a second coronary angiogram, often because of clinical symptoms. This may enrich the cohort with higher-risk individuals, potentially overestimating the true prevalence of SVGD and the strength of the observed associations. Fourth, the laboratory data used to calculate the IMRS were collected at the time of the follow-up angiography, not preoperatively. While these values likely reflect a chronic systemic state, they may not perfectly represent the patient’s baseline perioperative condition. This is a critical methodological consideration: for IMRS to function as a true preoperative predictive instrument, its values must be obtained before surgery and correlated prospectively with subsequent graft outcomes. The current retrospective design, in which laboratory data were drawn at the time of follow-up angiography, cannot unambiguously confirm that the observed high IMRS scores preceded, rather than resulted from, the chronic inflammatory state engendered by graft pathology itself. Prospective studies are therefore indispensable to validate the preoperative predictive utility of IMRS. Fifth, although the multivariate model adjusted for several key confounders, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as genetic predispositions or patient adherence to medical therapy, cannot be entirely excluded. Sixth, and importantly, the IMRS does not incorporate intraoperative and technical surgical variables that are well-established determinants of graft fate, including the surgeon’s anastomotic technique, the degree of endothelial trauma sustained during vein harvesting, the quality and diameter of the conduit selected, the presence of competitive flow, and the hemodynamic characteristics of the target coronary vessel. These anatomical and technical factors are direct, proximate determinants of graft patency, and any composite risk score, however broadly validated, must be positioned as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, the detailed intraoperative and anatomical assessment provided by the operating surgeon and the interventional cardiologist.

CONCLUSION

In this retrospective analysis, an elevated IMRS emerged as a significant and independent predictor of SVG degeneration. This association is plausibly driven by the IMRS’s unique ability to holistically capture the systemic inflammatory, metabolic, and hematologic derangements that constitute the pathophysiological foundation of graft failure. Calculated from routine, low-cost, and universally available laboratory tests, the IMRS holds considerable promise as a pragmatic method for preoperative risk stratification. The integration of IMRS into clinical workflows could help identify high-risk CABG candidates who may benefit most from the preferential use of more durable arterial conduits and from more intensive, personalized postoperative secondary prevention. Such a strategy has the potential to mitigate the long-term burden of graft failure and improve cardiovascular outcomes. Prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore the clinical utility of IMRS-guided therapeutic strategies.
