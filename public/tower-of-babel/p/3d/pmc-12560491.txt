Physical human-robot interaction mediates the association of motor impairment and kinematic performance for poststroke arm rehabilitation
Authors: Ze-Jian Chen, Yong Chen, Jiang Xu, Xiao-Lin Huang, Chang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobot-assisted therapy (RAT) has demonstrated potential in revolutionizing poststroke rehabilitation settings. Beyond facilitating motor recovery, robotic systems can offer kinematic assessments to objectively measure arm performance. Physical human-robot interaction (pHRI), a core component of RAT, enables the system to adapt kinematic performance tailoring arm motor impairments. While previous studies have explored correlations between onboard kinematics and clinical outcomes, the specific role of pHRI in mediating motor impairment and kinematic performance remains under-investigated. This study aims to examine whether pHRI mediates the relationship between arm motor impairment and onboard kinematic performance, and whether higher-quality pHRI contributes to improved motor outcomes in individuals with stroke.MethodsA mediation analysis study was conducted using data from the Exoskeleton-assisted Anthropomorphic Movement Training (EAMT) trials between December 2018 and August 2021. Data were analyzed September to December 2024. Physical human-robot interaction that provides real-time adjustments to maintain a more physiological arm posture was applied during functional tasks in RAT. Participants underwent RAT using an exoskeleton that measured kinematic metrics (movement time, smoothness, and postural error) and pHRI. Motor impairment was assessed using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity (FMA-UE). Mediation analysis was employed to examine whether pHRI mediated the association between motor impairment and kinematic performance.ResultsAfter screening 312 patients for eligibility, data of 50 participants (mean [SD] age, 49.5 [10.2] years; mean [SD] time since stroke onset, 55.2 [44.0] days) with subacute stroke were included in the study. Higher FMA-UE scores were significantly associated with improved kinematic performance, including reduced movement time (standardized r = -0.459, P < 0.001), increased smoothness (standardized r = -0.369, P = 0.005), and reduced postural error (standardized r = -0.488, P < 0.001). Moreover, pHRI significantly mediated the association between motor impairment and postural error, accounting for 43.7% of the total effect (95% CI: 12.3 to 75.1, P = 0.006). No mediation was observed for movement time or smoothness. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings.ConclusionspHRI accounted for 43.7% of the relationship between motor impairment and postural error during robotic training for stroke rehabilitation, indicating a substantial mediating effect. These findings highlight the potential of optimizing pHRI to improve the effectiveness of rehabilitation robots for broader motor outcomes, particularly in enhancing motor control and investigating the underlying pHRI mechanisms regarding robotic design and control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01362-5.Keywords: Physical human-robot interaction, Robot-assisted therapy, Motor impairment, Kinematics, Mediation analysis, Causal effect

Introduction

With neuroengineering advancements, robot-assisted therapy (RAT) has been increasingly applied in the field of physical medicine and rehabilitation after stroke [1–3]. As a nonpharmacological treatment approach, RAT can potentially revolutionize motor rehabilitation settings by providing more accessible and automized exercise for postacute care facilities worldwide [4]. In addition, robotic systems can facilitate upper limb rehabilitation by offering objective kinematic measurements that have demonstrated significant associations with clinical scales [5, 6]. Rehabilitation robots, which are interdisciplinary systems, are empowered with diverse technological components, including machinery, computer science, and control theory, etc [7]. However, whether individuals with arm hemiplegia can benefit from RAT remains controversial, and undoubtedly clinical evidence entails constant optimizations of robotic devices in the long run [8–10].

Apart from delivering training programs, the key elements of RAT are environmental enrichment and closed-loop biomechanical feedback derived from physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) [11, 12]. In the past decades, it has been well-established that increased sensory or cognitive stimulation can significantly facilitate post-stroke neural and functional recovery [13]. This principle has been effectively incorporated into the audiovisual interaction process within the training paradigm, leveraging the engaging nature of serious games in task-specific context to enhance clinical efficacy [14, 15]. For example, Crignis et al. found that robotic task-oriented training could be optimized by augmented reality in the neurorehabilitation settings [16]. Despite being a fundamental RAT configuration for tailoring individual arm disabilities, evidence on the causal effects of pHRI in addressing the relationship between motor impairment and improving kinematic performance remains scarce.

Since pHRI focuses on how to better integrate robots with the human body in its design, it has attracted significant attention in recent years for increasing the efficiency and clinical efficacy of RAT [17]. With the human-centered properties, mechanical joints of robotic exoskeletons are generally aligned to human articulations for delivering repetitive, assist-as-needed exercise tailoring to motor impairment of the patients. Hereby, these robots can actively offer participants with intensive physical interaction on the inherent mechanical configuration basis. By estimating real-time human motor intention or performance, robots provide feedback and collaborate with patients to achieve a functional task in design. From the engineering perspective, this process requires advanced sensors in the sophisticated control loop to detect individual response for physical interaction [18]. Placing human into the loop, robotic devices receive recurrent biomechanical parameters from the participants to optimize the bio-cooperative goals, such as joint profiles and muscle activity [7, 19]. In short, pHRI as well as its optimization procedure is conventionally navigated by online, continuous measurements of human kinematic performance, which has been also supported by prior correlation analyses [5, 20, 21].

Unfortunately, it is not easy to assess how pHRI could influence the relationship between motor impairment and onboard kinematics in clinical contexts for several reasons. First, while feasible solutions have been demonstrated in RAT paradigm, the man–machine systems are inevitably coupled when discussing intervention outcomes, making it difficult to disentangle the causal effects of pHRI. In previous literature, these effects were usually examined by pre-post comparison or offline analyzing the active actuating systems [22]. However, the unactuated components of robotic devices, such as weight support, may have contributed to the changes in arm kinematic performance [23]. Second, conventional pHRI procedures imply that real-time robotic performance of the patients could directly represent their arm motor impairment after stroke. The two aspects of indicators are often adopted for outcome measures in trial settings. From the clinical perspective, these two ingredients may be associated but their statistical relationship is not fully examined, which could be confounded by user-initiated pHRI during the training contexts. Moreover, bi-directional communications between human and robot further can lead to recursive or indirect perturbation on the bio cooperative system [24]. As a result, the real-time onboard human-robot performance and motor impairment, as well as their relationships with pHRI, should be assessed for better optimizing the technical advantage of RAT in clinical practice.

Causal models, as the theoretical framework, have been used to describe the relationships between variables through cause-and-effect pathways [25]. In the context of investigating pHRI during RAT, mediation model may specifically elucidate the mechanism by which the independent variable (motor impairment) influences the dependent variable (kinematic performance) through an intermediary variable (physical human-robot interaction). To quantify the interaction between the exoskeleton and participant throughout the anthropomorphic reaching movement, the metric of mechanical error (ME) is constructed. This mediation model provides insight into how the quality of pHRI serves as a mediator, explaining the underlying process through which man-machine interfaces impacts arm motor recovery.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the complex relationship among arm motor impairment, pHRI, and kinematic performance in individuals with stroke. We hypothesize that (1) the quality of pHRI mediates the effect of motor impairment on kinematic performance during robot-assisted training, and (2) higher-quality pHRI—characterized by more naturalistic mechanical output—contributes to better motor performance, regardless of initial impairment severity.

Materials and methods

Study setting and participants

We conducted a mediation analysis using data of experimental group from the Exoskeleton-assisted Anthropomorphic Movement Training (EAMT) trials in patients with subacute stroke between December 2018 and August 2021 (Fig. 1). The pilot and randomized EAMT trials were analogous in design that were registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900026656 registered on October 17, 2019 and ChiCTR2100044078 registered on March 9, 2021) [26, 27]. All the included participants were allocated to exoskeleton or control intervention through computer-generated random numbers. Approvals for the studies were obtained from the ethics committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, as well as from all participating centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all inpatients or their legal representatives at the rehabilitation centers. The study follows the recommendations outlined in the A Guideline for Reporting Mediation Analyses (AGReMA) Statement (Supplement 1) [28].

Flow Chart of Study Participants

The patient eligibility criteria have been previously described [26, 27]. In brief, the study enrolled adult patients who experienced a first-ever, unilateral stroke, confirmed by neuroimaging (CT or MRI) within 6 months prior to enrollment. Eligible patients exhibited moderate-to-severe upper extremity motor impairment, as measured by a Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) score of 8–38, and were able to sit unsupported for at least 45 min to participate in robot-assisted training. Exclusion criteria included orthopedic conditions affecting the paretic arm (e.g., fixed contractures, shoulder subluxation, severe arthritis), concurrent diagnoses that could impede rehabilitation or outcome assessments (such as significant cognitive deficits or aphasia), and participation in another clinical trial.

Robotic setup

The robotic setup utilized in experimental group of the studies is designed to facilitate EAMT therapy by replicating human-like movement patterns (Armule, Intelbot Intelligent Machine Ltd, Wuhan, China). This advanced system enables the delivery of both passive and active-assistive exercises, featuring adjustable linkages that can be customized to accommodate varying arm lengths and circumferences (Fig. 2A). Participants underwent robotic sessions lasting 45 min, aiming for 600 to 800 repetitions per training session. The training protocol, which was introduced to participants prior to the commencement of therapy, was conducted and supervised by the same physical therapist to ensure consistency and safety.

The exoskeleton operates unilaterally but is designed with the flexibility to support either the left or right paretic limb, depending on the patient’s specific needs. It incorporates seven degrees of freedom (DOFs) to enable comprehensive simulation of upper extremity movements. These include five DOFs for active shoulder and elbow movements, encompassing shoulder flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation, elbow flexion/extension, and forearm pronation/supination. Additionally, two passive wrist DOF, flexion/extension and radial/ulnar deviation, are actuated automatically based on the positions of the proximal joints.

During training sessions, the exoskeleton employs six-dimensional force/torque sensors (Mini45; ATI Industrial Automation Inc., USA) integrated into the mechanical arm to detect human-robot interaction forces and positions. These sensors simultaneously identify arm displacement and the participants’ motion intentions. The pHRI is further enhanced through the incorporation of a virtual reality environment. Participants engage with interactive tasks on the screen such as shooting targets and cleaning windows, which are transformed from UE movements related with daily lives (Fig. 2B). The optimal trajectory for reaching tasks is determined by the exoskeleton, represented by the shortest distance from the start to the target points. This setup enables real-time responsiveness of the exoskeleton to the participants’ movements, promoting motor recovery and functional improvement in a safe and controlled manner. Mediating variable: physical human-robot interaction

As shown in the simplified schematic representations of the causal pathways (Fig. 2C), pHRI is hypothesized to mediate the relationship between arm motor impairment and kinematic performance during EAMT therapy. To achieve smooth and coordinated movements, admittance control, a compliant control strategy, is implemented. This strategy mitigates the impact of robotic inertia and translates the participants’ movement intentions into corresponding postural adjustments within the force field. By utilizing the Jacobian matrix, this method enables real-time adjustments to minimize discrepancies between target and actual UE positions. When the shoulder and elbow joint profiles closely approximate natural, coordinated reference values, the exoskeleton operates smoothly. However, any deviations from these reference values trigger audiovisual cues and prompt corrective adjustments to UE movements within 0.1 s. The ME metric was designed to evaluate pHRI forces between the exoskeleton and the participant.

ME refers to the covariance distance between the actual mechanical control of stroke patients and the principal components of normal human mechanical output throughout the task trajectory, which is normalized for time. Both excessive and insufficient force outputs can lead to an increase in deviation from normal control, where denotes the principal components of normal human mechanical output, denotes the principal components of the patient’s joint mechanics at any time , and represents the mathematical integral over the entire movement.

Dependent variable: kinematic metrics

Kinematic metrics were used to assess inter-joint coordination and motor control through the inherent sensors within the exoskeleton. During EAMT therapy, these metrics included movement time, smoothness, and postural error, all derived from sensor data integrated into the exoskeleton [26, 27]. Two six-dimensional torque transducers were enclosed in linkage cuffs for real-time torque measurement and feedback-based control. Additionally, joint angle encoders were employed to capture shoulder, elbow, and wrist angles across each DOF. As participants interacted with the sensors while moving their affected upper limbs, joint angle data were simultaneously transmitted to a computer for analysis.

Participants were instructed to perform an independent virtual reaching assessment simulating frying eggs, repeating the task five times. The goal was to move a virtual egg to a pan displayed on-screen, following a prescribed posture and trajectory. This was adjusted to 60% of the arm length, measured from the medial axillary border to the index fingertip, and positioned at the height of the xiphoid process along the body’s midline. Participants were verbally encouraged to complete the tasks at their own pace. Data were recorded at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz and stored in text format. A semi-automated custom program written in MATLAB (Version 2018a) was used to extract the data, defining movement onset and end based on a velocity threshold of 0.025 m/s for further computing the kinematic metrics.

Movement time

a key objective and quantitative variable commonly used in clinical assessments to evaluate movement performance. It is assumed to be prolonged in reaching movements for individuals with stroke, reflecting the effectiveness of their motor control.

Smoothness

a measure of how individuals complete a task with fewer, larger, and more fluid sub-movements. It evaluates the degree to which movement deviates from a typical, smooth trajectory. Movement smoothness during reaching is characterized by path jerk, with a lower jerk indicating a smoother trajectory.

Postural error

the deviation of the actual posture from the ideal anthropomorphic posture throughout the reaching movement. Both excessive and insufficient representation of the upper limb’s spatial trajectory contribute to deviations from natural movement patterns.

Independent variable: measure of upper limb motor impairment

Upper limb motor impairment was measured before the robotic test. The FMA-UE is considered a “gold-standard” assessment for the evaluation of upper limb motor impairment after stroke due to its excellent reliability. It consists of 33 items that evaluate typical movements and reflex function, each rated on a scale from 0 (cannot perform), 1 (partially perform), to 2 (fully perform). Higher scores indicate greater recovery with a maximum score of 66 [29].

Covariates

The potential covariates included stroke type (ischemic or hemorrhagic), side (left or right), gender (male or female), age (≥ 50 or < 50 years), and duration (days since stroke onset). These data were collected as part of the demographic information for subsequent sensitivity analyses [30].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted from September to December 2024. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize continuous and categorical variables. The normality of distributions was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and histograms. For categorical variables, counts and percentages were reported, while continuous variables were summarized using either the mean (SD) or median (interquartile range). Linear regression was utilized to explore the association between FMA-UE scores, physical human-robot interaction, and kinematic metrics.

Mediation analysis was conducted to test whether alterations in physical human-robot interaction are on the potential causal pathway of the association between FMA-UE scores and kinematics, as it is biologically plausible that the kinematic performance is affected by upper limb motor impairment. Additionally, the proportion of the mediation effect was calculated by dividing the mediation effect estimate by the total effect estimate. Power analysis with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the mediation analysis was performed using Monte Carlo simulations, which indicated a power of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.81 to 0.92) for the sample size of 50 participants, and 0.77 (95% CI: 0.64 to 0.86) for the sample size of 40 participants (eFigure 1 in Supplement 2) [31]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by adjusting for demographic covariates in model 2 (stroke type, side, and gender) and model 3 (stroke type, side, gender, age, and duration) to test the robustness of the study associations. All statistical analyses were considered significant at a 2-sided α level of ≤ 0.05. The R statistical package (version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the lavaan package 0.6-5 was used for the analysis.

Association analysis: upper limb motor impairment and kinematic metrics

As shown in Fig. 3, the kinematic and mechanical metrics, as well as their associations with FMA-UE, were stable across different cohorts. Higher FMA-UE scores were significantly associated with improved kinematic performance. Specifically, higher FMA-UE scores correlated with lower movement time (standardized r = -0.459, 95% CI: -0.706 to -0.212, P < 0.001), increased smoothness (standardized r = -0.369, 95% CI: -0.626 to -0.112, P = 0.005), and reduced postural error (standardized r = -0.488, 95% CI: -0.729 to -0.247, P < 0.001).

Correlation Analysis. MT, movement time; SM, smoothness; PE, postural error, ME, mechanical error

Association analysis: upper limb motor impairment and pHRI

Higher FMA-UE scores were also associated with lower levels of pHRI, as indicated by a significant negative correlation (standardized r = -0.439, 95% CI: -0.688 to -0.190, P = 0.001).

Association analysis: pHRI and kinematic metrics

Moreover, pHRI was found to be positively associated with postural error (standardized r = 0.486, 95% CI: 0.253 to 0.719, P < 0.001). However, there was no significant correlation between pHRI and movement time (standardized r = 0.024, 95% CI: -0.250 to 0.158, P = 0.862), or smoothness (standardized r = 0.121, 95% CI: -0.163 to 0.405, P = 0.403).

Causal mediation analysis

Figure 4 illustrates the results of the mediation analysis. After including pHRI in the correlation model, the relationships between FMA-UE scores and kinematic metrics were changed. Specifically, the correlation with movement time was reduced (standardized r = -0.448, 95% CI: -0.722 to -0.174, P = 0.001), the correlation with smoothness was also reduced (standardized r = -0.316, 95% CI: -0.600 to -0.032, P = 0.030), and the correlation with postural error was further attenuated (standardized r = -0.275, 95% CI: -0.508 to -0.042, P = 0.021).

Mediation Analysis. MT, movement time; SM, smoothness; PE, postural error; FMA-UE, Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity

The mediation analysis indicated that pHRI mediated 43.7% (95% CI: 12.3 to 75.1, P = 0.006) of the relationship between upper limb motor impairment and postural error, with an indirect effect of standardized r = -0.214 (95% CI: -0.373 to -0.055, P = 0.008). However, no mediation was found for the relationship between motor impairment and either movement time or smoothness (eTable 1–3 in Supplement 2).

Sensitivity analysis

To obtain much power of the mediation effect, cohort 2 was included in the study despite its small sample size. Sensitivity analyses (Fig. 5; eFigs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 in Supplement 2) demonstrated the robustness of the results, with consistent findings across model 2 (adjusted for stroke type, side, and gender) and model 3 (adjusted for stroke type, side, gender, age, and duration).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effect of pHRI in mediating the relationship between upper limb motor impairment and onboard kinematic performance in individuals with stroke. The findings highlight a complex interaction between these variables, offering new insights into how robotic interventions can influence rehabilitation outcomes tailoring individual severity of arm impairment. The mediation analysis revealed that pHRI accounted for approximately 43.7% of the total effect between motor impairment and postural error. However, it is important to note that no significant mediation was found for movement time or smoothness. These findings can inform the structural design or control strategies of future robotic systems for stroke neurorehabilitation.

The results demonstrate a significant association between motor impairment, as measured by FMA-UE, and onboard upper limb performance in terms of movement time, smoothness, and postural error. These findings align with previous studies that have shown that more severe motor impairment corresponds to poorer movement efficiency and greater deviation from optimal movement trajectories [32]. It is difficult to accurately capture and interpret the complex dynamics of the interaction between human and rehabilitation. Here, we quantify the pHRI metrics by detecting force mismatch between the real-time sensor data and the given naturalistic task.

Importantly, our results indicate that pHRI plays a role in mediating this relationship, particularly for postural trajectory [33]. This suggests that the quality of the physical interaction between the patient and the rehabilitation robot, under the EAMT paradigm for adaptive compliance control, may influence the ability to maintain a more natural movement pattern. This finding underscores the potential of pHRI to serve as a key mechanism through which powered exoskeletons may improve motor function in stroke rehabilitation [34]. It is possible that under the current robotic control approach, pHRI might be particularly effective in improving the dynamic alignment of the upper limb during movements. But its impact on movement efficiency and fluidity may be more limited or influenced by other factors, such as the patient’s intrinsic motor capabilities or the specific nature of the robotic assistance provided [35].

The association between pHRI and postural error is particularly noteworthy, as it indicates that the feedback provided by the robot through force/torque sensors and real-time biomechanical adjustments could help individuals with stroke maintain a more physiological arm posture during functional tasks. This is consistent with the concept that EAMT, through timely impedance or assistance, can guide patients toward more effective and functional movement patterns by aligning their movements with optimal trajectories [36]. The real-time adjustment of arm postures and joint angles based on the exoskeletal pHRI may thus help counteract compensatory patterns that often emerge in individuals with stroke, contributing to better functional outcomes [37]. As the current pHRI algorithm was settled, adding impedance learning control approach could help adapt to the physical dynamics and individualized intentions based on patient’s experience during therapy for more natural online collaboration [24]. Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying these effects and investigate how pHRI can be further tailored to address different aspects of motor impairments, ultimately improving the clinical efficacy of rehabilitation robots for individuals after stroke.

Our results demonstrated the lack of mediation effect for movement time and smoothness. A possible reason was that the type of pHRI assessed in this study was originally intended for postural control processes and these kinematic metrics may involve other motor functionalities [38]. The ME metric was time-normalized, ensuring that it captured robotic dynamics independent of task duration or their intrinsic interaction [39]. Although MT itself may be relevant to motor performance, our study specifically aimed to isolate the interaction quality independent of speed. Consequently, ME and MT, as well as SM, are mathematically distinct in its mediation pathway. The lack of a significant mediation effect therefore reflects limited influence of pHRI on task duration once motor impairment is accounted for, considering this normalization intentionally minimized the overlap between the two metrics. While physical interaction certainly plays a role in correcting postural deviations, its influence on the broader aspects of motor performance, such as reducing movement time or improving smoothness, may require further adjustment of the exoskeletal configurations.

Other variables, such as the patients’ sensory or perceptual functions, may also interact with pHRI to affect these kinematic measures [40]. Furthermore, this study did not monitor participant feedback about comfort during arm training. But then again, it has been demonstrated valuable to offer automatic, sensitive, and robust tracking and prognosis of sensorimotor function during RAT [41, 42]. Although robotic platforms may introduce mechanical constraints or unnecessary assistance compared to sensor-based assessments [43], certain metrics, such as smoothness and time in the EAMT paradigm, are less influenced by pHRI and show promise for objective arm evaluation [44].

Strengths and limitations

Previous studies have demonstrated correlations between robotic kinematic metrics and clinical scales [45, 46]. However, the potential influence of human-robot interaction on these correlations has not been investigated. This issue is important to elucidate the mechanisms through which human-robot interaction affects therapeutic outcomes, thereby informing the development of more adaptive and effective rehabilitation protocols.

To our best knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the causal role of pHRI in its design for clinical rehabilitation after stroke [21]. This study presents several strengths, including a robust design using data from the EAMT trials, which involved well-defined cohorts of subacute stroke patients from multiple clinical centers, enhancing the generalizability of the results. The use of objective kinematic metrics obtained from exoskeleton sensors allowed for precise and quantifiable assessments of motor performance, minimizing bias from subjective scales. Most importantly, the employment of advanced mediation analysis provided valuable insights into the mechanisms by which pHRI mediates the relationship between motor impairment and kinematic performance during RAT. The inclusion of sensitivity analyses, adjusting for potential confounders such as stroke type, side, gender, age, and duration, supported the robustness of the findings, providing further confidence in the reliability of the observed relationships. The post-hoc power analysis also indicates a sufficient statistical power of 0.87 to detect meaningful effects by including data from both cohort 1 and cohort 2.

The present study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design substantially limits the ability to draw causal inferences, as the temporal sequence between variables could not be established. A larger prospective study with follow-up of the long-term robotic effects and control group would enhance the generalizability of the results. The findings may not apply to individuals with milder impairments as the study focused on those with moderate-to-severe motor impairment post-stroke. Additionally, the use of a single robotic device may restrict the applicability of the findings to other neuroengineering systems, which may have different interaction dynamics, such as cortical activation and functional connectome [47, 48]. While several key confounders were controlled for, other unmeasured variables, such as proprioception, nonverbal cognition, and visuospatial deficits, may have influenced the outcomes, introducing potential bias or residual confounding [49, 50]. Finally, this study did not include standardized assessments of upper limb functional use, such as the Motor Activity Log (MAL) or the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), which limits the ability to interpret how changes in pHRI and kinematic performance translate into functional improvements in daily activities.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence into the causal role of physical human-robot interaction for individuals with upper limb motor impairment after stroke. By investigating its effects in mediating the relationship between motor impairment and kinematic parameters, the findings suggest that pHRI of EAMT setting may be critical in reducing postural errors, but has no significant effect on movement smoothness or speed. These results highlight the importance of further optimizing robotic systems to promote comprehensive post-stroke motor recovery.
