Application of Mendelian randomization in the discovery of risk factors for cancer from 2014 to 2024: a bibliometric review
Authors: Junchi Yang, Dongyan Wang, Yixiao Han, Xinzhe Tan, Haibin Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCancer represents a significant threat to human health, resulting in considerable suffering and placing strain on the medical system. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a cutting-edge technique widely utilized in epidemiological studies, demonstrating distinct advantages in identifying risk factors associated with various diseases. In recent times, there has been considerable interest in MR studies within cancer research. Researchers are increasingly utilizing MR to explore potential risk factors and mechanisms related to cancer, which enables researchers to evaluate the causal relationship between genetic variation and cancer risk more accurately, serving as a crucial foundation for cancer prevention and treatment.MethodsThis study conducted a bibliometric analysis of MR research in cancer, documented in the Science Network (WOS) Index from 2014 to 2024. By systematically retrieving and summarizing relevant literature, the analysis covered data points such as countries, institutions, authors, journals, publications, keywords, exposures and outcomes.ResultsThe study included 1211 articles written by 5810 scholars affiliated with 1254 organizations in 66 nations, published in 302 journals. China, UK, USA and Australia emerged as the leading countries involved. The key affiliations included the University of Bristol, Karolinska Institute, Sichuan University and Zhejiang University. Prominent journals include ‘Frontiers in Oncology’, ‘Frontiers in Genetics’, ‘International Journal of Cancer’ and ‘Scientific Reports’. The keyword analysis indicated that obesity was the most frequently cited exposure factor in cancer-related MR literature, followed by body mass index, inflammation and smoking. Breast cancer was the most common outcome factor, followed by colorectal cancer, prostate cancer and lung cancer.ConclusionMore and more attention has been paid to the research of MR in tumors, revealing a variety of potential risk factors. However, some risk factors found by MR analysis (such as obesity on lung cancer subtypes) also have contradictory effects, highlighting the complexity of the specific causal relationship between exposure factors and cancer. Future research needs to focus on three directions: integrating cross-omics data (such as epigenetics, single cell analysis) to analyze the mechanism of tumor microenvironment; we use longitudinal MR and Bayesian model to optimize the robustness of causal inference. Strengthen cross-ethnic verification and GWAS data construction of rare cancers, and promote the transformation of MR findings into precise prevention strategies.Keywords: Bibliometric analysis, Mendelian randomization, Tumor, Cancer, Visualization analysis

Introduction

Cancer has become a primary contributor to mortality worldwide [1], particularly in countries with a low Human Development Index [2], where the burden of cancer is on the rise. Research indicates that these nations experience a high incidence of cancer but have relatively low cure rates owing to inadequate resources and limited medical infrastructure [3, 4]. This situation exerts a significant pressure on the global health system. The frequent occurrence and low cure rates of cancer present severe challenges for these countries in managing and addressing the disease while also imposing a persistent burden on global public health resources and medical systems [5]. In 2019, data released by the WHO indicated that cancer has become the primary or secondary cause of mortality for those under 70 years of age in 112 out of 183 countries globally, and is positioned as the third or fourth leading cause in 23 more nations [6]. In response to this urgent global health challenge, a growing number of researchers are dedicated to uncovering the pathogenesis of cancer and exploring innovative treatment options [6–8]. To date, a substantial body of research has been conducted in this field; however, it remains a challenging and complex endeavor to accurately assessing the risk of early disease [9]. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are considered the ‘gold standard’ for examining causal relationships between diseases, but their high cost and extended timeframes often limit their effectiveness in identifying cancer risk factors [10]. Mendelian randomization (MR) has become a significant epidemiological research tool for identifying disease risk factors [11]. Compared with traditional methods, MR analysis can effectively mitigate the influence of confounding factors; therefore, MR analysis can partially address the limitations associated with randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [12, 13]. Currently, MR is extensively used to investigate cancer-related risk factors, offering novel insights and data. However, despite the considerable potential of MR in this domain, a comprehensive systematic evaluation of its application effects and the current state of research remains necessary [14]. Bibliometric analysis (BA) is a method for assessing and interpreting literature data, helping researchers grasp the current landscape of cancer Mendelian randomization (MR) research in a swift and intuitive manner [15]. This study employs the BA method to systematically outline trends in cancer MR studies, partnerships between institutions, and the predominant research patterns and areas of focus. Through this analysis, we aim to elucidate the primary directions and developmental trends of the research, thereby providing valuable references for future investigations.

Methods

Since the research presented in this article does not involve animal experiments, human studies, recognizable human images, or relevant data, ethical review approval was not required.

Citation indexes and searching keywords

We utilized WoSCC to screen cancer studies involving Mendelian Randomization (MR) from January 1, 2014, to July 1, 2024. To identify relevant research during this decade, we developed the following search strategy: TS = (Cancer OR Neoplasia OR Neoplasias OR Neoplasm OR Tumor OR Cancer OR Cancers OR Malignant Neoplasm OR Malignancy OR Malignancies OR Malignant Neoplasms OR Neoplasm, Malignant OR Neoplasms, Malignant OR Benign Neoplasms OR Neoplasms, Benign OR Neoplasm, Benign OR Benign Neoplasm) AND TS = (Mendelian randomization) [16]. Our search scope was confined to the literature published in English and only incorporated papers and reviews into the research. All documents that met the search criteria were downloaded from the WoSCC database and in-depth data analysis was conducted using scientific measurement tools.

Bibliometric analysis software

We used five tools for bibliometric analysis: VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), CiteSpace (version 6.3.R1), R (version 4.4.1), Scimago Graphica (version 1.0.43), and GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3) software. Among these, VOSviewer (version 1.6.20) was employed to identify high-yield countries, regions, journals, and authors, as well as to conduct citation analyses of institutions and authors. In addition, it facilitates keyword co-occurrence analysis to identify primary co-cited institutions, journals, authors, and references, thereby constructing a relevant visual network. Furthermore, it generates a density visualization map of published journals, co-cited journals, cited authors, and co-cited authors [17, 18]. The connections between nodes illustrate the co-occurrence relationships, where the thickness of the links indicates how often the co-occurrences happen.CiteSpace (6.3.R1) was employed to investigate the research trends and dynamics of Mendelian randomization within the field of cancer as well as to visualize and analyze the associated knowledge domains and emerging trends [19]. This encompasses the network diagrams of authors, countries, institutions, and co-cited journals, along with the clustering of co-cited references and the timeline view of keywords. Furthermore, CiteSpace generates citation burst diagrams for keywords and references, utilizes the time axis view to cluster keywords, and organizes and names clusters based on the extracted keywords. Additionally, we used CiteSpace (6.3. R1) to identify keywords that exhibit strong citation bursts.We utilized the R package ‘bibliometrix’ in conjunction with RStudio software (version 2024.04.2 + 764) for our bibliometric analysis. These tools facilitate the analysis of keyword word clouds, the creation of a three-domain map, the generation of a world map that depicts the scientific output of each country, and the examination of research output from active authors over various time periods. Our analysis also incorporates identification based on Bradford’s law, which delineates the distribution of citations within a topic or field, and aids in the identification of the most frequently cited journals and core sources in the discipline. This approach enabled us to identify the key journals and essential research resources in the field [20]. In addition, we utilized GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3) to generate a line chart illustrating the trend in publication counts in relation to the cumulative number of publications. Concurrently, we employed Scimago Graphica (version 1.0.43) to create a world map that effectively displayed the distribution of publications across various countries. These visualization tools enhance our understanding of the geographical distribution and developmental trends of research outcomes in a more intuitive manner.

Countries analysis

Mendelian randomization-related cancer publications spanned 66 nations or regions (Fig. 2C). In the global map, countries or regions shaded in blue indicate the presence of the corresponding authors, with the intensity of the blue hue reflecting the publication volume. Deeper shades of blue indicate a higher number of publications. China, the UK, and the USA contributed the largest proportion of these publications, representing 65.2%, 10.1%, and 8.3% of the total publications, respectively. The countries leading to the publication of research papers included China, the United Kingdom, the United States, Australia, Sweden, France, Germany, Denmark, South Korea, and Spain (see Table 1). Furthermore, the ten most cited countries are the United Kingdom, China, the United States, France, Australia, Sweden, Denmark, Germany, Greece, and Spain (see Table 1).The phenomenon that Chinese publications account for up to 65.2% is worthy of further discussion. This is the result of multiple factors. First of all, thanks to the Chinese government ‘s continuous and substantial increase in investment in biomedical research, especially in genomics, precision medicine and cancer research. Major national strategic projects such as the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC) and the National Key Research and Development Program clearly support large-scale genetic research, biomarker discovery, and the application of new epidemiological methods such as Mendelian Randomization (MR) to analyze complex diseases such as cancer. This targeted investment provides key resources for data generation and analysis. Secondly, the huge population size gives it a unique advantage. China has established national and regional large-scale biological sample libraries (such as the China Chronic Disease Prospective Study Project CKB), and collected genome, phenotype and health outcome data from hundreds of thousands to millions of individuals, providing a massive data pool for the development of powerful genome-wide association studies (GWAS) -the basis for identifying genetic instrumental variables in MR analysis. Data of this size makes it possible to conduct robust MR studies that may not be statistically effective in smaller populations.

The Mendelian randomization study on telomere length and cancer risk published by Haycock et al.in ‘JAMA Oncology ‘in 2017 [21], as the most cited fundamental literature in this field (360 times), has far-reaching and multidimensional influence. It pioneered the use of MR methods at the genetic level to confirm that longer telomere length is a causal risk factor for a variety of cancers (especially glioma, low-grade malignant ovarian cancer, and lung adenocarcinoma), rather than just a related marker, which provides strong genetic evidence for understanding the central role of telomere biology in carcinogenesis and suggests that telomere maintenance mechanisms are potential intervention targets.This study revealed the significant heterogeneity of the effect of telomere length on the risk of different cancer types (especially in rare cancers and tissues with low stem cell division rate), which prompted follow-up studies to pay more attention to the unique etiology and biological mechanism of specific cancer subtypes, and promoted more refined etiological exploration. The potential protective effect of telomere lengthening on certain non-neoplastic diseases (such as coronary heart disease) (risk reduction of 22%) highlighted the'double-edged sword'effect of telomere length in health and disease, prompting follow-up studies to evaluate the potential clinical value of telomere-targeted interventions. Multi-disease joint risk assessments must be performed to weigh their possible carcinogenic risks and other health benefits, avoiding the one-sidedness of a single interpretation of telomere test results.In addition, the huge impact of this study is also reflected in the fact that it significantly improves the credibility and application breadth of the MR method in exploring the causal relationship between complex biological phenotypes (such as telomere length) and diseases, and encourages more researchers to use similar methods to explore the links between other intrinsic biomarkers and cancer risk.

Figure 10C illustrates the co-occurrence of these 1946 keywords and analyzes the themes of cancer research in Mendelian randomization. By classifying clusters based on terms extracted from relevant publications, we identified 11 major clusters: ‘#0 breast cancer,’ ‘#1 inflammatory bowel disease,’ ‘#2 cutaneous melanoma,’ ‘#3 hepatocellular carcinoma,’ ‘#4 follicular lymphoma,’ ‘#5 gut microbiota,’ ‘#6 esophageal cancer,’ ‘#7 myeloproliferative neoplasm,’ ‘#8 lung cancer,’ ‘#9 C-reactive protein,’ and ‘#10 atopic dermatitis’ (Fig. 10D).It is worth noting that behind these seemingly scattered disease clusters, keyword analysis reveals the core exposure factors and potential common mechanisms that run through multiple cancer types. The upstream exposure factors showed the commonality of cross-cancer species. The imbalance of # 5 (gut microbiota) and the systemic inflammation marked by # 9 (C-reactive protein) constituted a unified driving axis, which induced the risk of colorectal cancer in # 1 (inflammatory bowel disease) in mucosal tissue and promoted the occurrence of # 3 (hepatocellular carcinoma) through enterohepatic circulation. The same inflammatory pathway exhibits tissue-specific- # 9 (C-reactive protein) -associated obesity-related inflammation in solid organs activates estrogen signaling in # 0 (breast cancer), while in # 8 (lung cancer), it cooperates with smoking to reshape the immune microenvironment, leading to contradictory effects of cancer subtype differentiation (such as promoting small cell carcinoma but inhibiting adenocarcinoma).This differentiation is particularly significant in immune disease-related clusters: intestinal chronic antigen stimulation in # 1 (inflammatory bowel disease) and skin barrier destruction in # 10 (atopic dermatitis), respectively, through Th1/Th17 and Th2 immune shifts, leading to distinct malignant endpoints: the former promotes metaplastic lesions in # 6 (esophageal cancer), while the latter is susceptible to lymph node proliferation disorders in # 2 (cutaneous melanoma) and # 4 (follicular lymphoma).As a typical representative of the hematological system, # 7 (myeloproliferative neoplasm) exposes another transformation pathway of metabolic inflammation-JAK-STAT signal mutation-driven clonal evolution, and together with the BCL2 translocation of # 4 (follicular lymphoma), it explains the universal law of 'inflammation-clonal selection-canceration' under chronic antigen stimulation. This framework highlights that the current research is shifting from the analysis of isolated cancers to the cross-scale integration of 'exposure-microenvironment-cloning evolution'.

Exposures and outcomes analysis

Table 7 displays the ten most frequent exposure and outcome factors. The most common exposure factor identified was obesity, followed by body mass index, inflammation, smoking, and cardiovascular disease. Regarding outcome factors, breast cancer was the most frequently reported, followed by colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and ovarian cancer. Exposure factor analysis showed that obesity was the most frequently identified exposure factor in cancer-related Mendelian randomization studies (88 times). Highly cited literature [23, 29] and [30] shows that the carcinogenic mechanism of obesity is mainly reflected in the interaction between metabolic disorders and tissue microenvironment: excessive proliferation of adipose tissue leads to insulin resistance, induces compensatory hyperinsulinemia, and directly promotes cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis by activating insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling pathway (especially in small cell lung cancer and pancreatic cancer); at the same time, dysregulation of adipokine secretion-elevated leptin levels drive chronic inflammatory response and JAK/STAT cancer-promoting pathway, while adiponectin reduction weakens its anti-cancer effect and jointly shapes the tumor-promoting microenvironment. In addition, obesity indirectly promotes tobacco-related lung cancer types (such as squamous cell carcinoma) by increasing the frequency and intensity of smoking by affecting the hypothalamus-reward pathway (genetic evidence shows that each standard deviation of BMI increases by 1.27 cigarettes per day); the imbalance of sex hormone metabolism (such as changes in the ratio of estrogen to androgen) further amplifies tissue-specific risks, and eventually forms a multi-level carcinogenic network with metabolic disorders as the core, behavioral factors as the mediation, and differences in tissue microenvironment responses as the basis. However, in the highly cited literature [29], obesity (elevated BMI) showed a histologically specific two-way effect on the risk of lung cancer : on the one hand, it increased the risk of small cell lung cancer through direct biological mechanisms (multivariate MR adjusted smoking OR = 1.28, P = 0.011), on the other hand, it significantly reduced the risk of lung adenocarcinoma (OR = 0.86, P = 0.008), while its weak cancer-promoting effect on lung squamous cell carcinoma (univariate MR OR = 1.19) completely disappeared after adjusting smoking (OR = 1.02), indicating that the increased risk of lung squamous cell carcinoma was completely mediated by smoking behavior. This contradictory effect stems from multiple mechanisms : the pro-inflammatory pathway of adipokines (such as leptin) may specifically drive the development of small cell lung cancer, while the potential protective effects of tumor suppressor factors such as adiponectin or differences in metabolic microenvironment may inhibit lung adenocarcinoma; at the same time, the strong association between obesity and smoking behavior (BMI increased smoking probability OR = 1.24, early age smoking risk OR = 0.91, smoking amount OR = 1.39) became a key confounding factor, especially amplifying the false risk of lung squamous cell carcinoma. The study further revealed that the neuroendocrine characteristics of small cell lung cancer may enhance its sensitivity to obesity-related metabolic disorders, and the sample size difference (small cell lung cancer n = 2,664 cases) and the dynamic change of BMI (genetic tools cannot capture precancerous weight loss) may partially amplify the contradictory effect, highlighting the necessity of independent research on lung cancer subtypes. Breast cancer was the most frequent outcome factor observed in this study (64 times), and the number of Mendelian randomization studies was significantly higher than that of other cancer types. This gap can be largely attributed to the richness and maturity of high-quality genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data available for different cancer types. Breast cancer has a large and publicly available GWAS data set (such as breast cancer data in the BCAC Alliance, UK Biobank). These studies usually contain hundreds of thousands of sample sizes, identify a large number of robust genetic variations, and provide a solid foundation for building effective instrumental variables. In contrast, GWAS studies on many other cancers (such as some rare cancers, specific subtypes or some geographically high-risk cancer species) started late, the sample size is relatively limited, and the statistical power is low, resulting in a small number of available genetic instrumental variables, weak efficacy or insufficient specificity, which limits the feasibility and reliability of MR-based causal inference research. In addition, breast cancer research can often integrate multi-omics data (such as hormone levels, tissue-specific expression of quantitative trait loci [eQTL]), which further deepens the exploration of mechanisms, while other cancer types often have gaps in the availability and integration depth of such supporting data. The difference in the richness of GWAS data directly leads to the imbalance of research opportunities. In the future, it is necessary to strengthen the large-scale GWAS investment and data sharing of other cancers, especially the lack of data resources, in order to narrow the research gap and reveal the etiology of various cancers more comprehensively.

Discussion

This study represents the first bibliometric examination of Mendelian randomization in cancer research on a global scale. The objective of this study is to utilize the WoSCC database, along with tools inculding VOSviewer, the R package ‘bibliometrix’, Scimago Graphica, GraphPad Prism, and Citespace, to analyze research trends and hotspots related to Mendelian randomization and cancer. Through these tools and platforms, this study shows the research progress in this field, identifies the key research topics, and points out the direction for future research in this field.We identified 1211 articles and reviews published between 2014 and 2024. The results indicated a general increase in the number of published articles over the decade, suggesting that researchers’ interest in Mendelian randomization in cancer research has continued to grow.

Cancer-related Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have been published worldwide. The geographical distribution of these publications is shown in Fig. 2C. China had the highest number of publications (n = 789), followed by the UK (n = 122) and the United States (n = 100). This indicates that China significantly outperformed other countries in terms of publication volume. Within the top ten countries/regions, China not only published the highest number of papers but also ranked second in terms of citations. Table 3 lists the ten organizations that have achieved the most significant research results in this area, with five located in China, underscoring China’s substantial influence in this field over the last ten years. The University of Bristol released 110 publications, which have been cited 2,743 times, establishing it as a leading institution in cancer-related MR research. Martin RM from the University of Bristol has authored the most papers in this field, with a total of 71 papers cited 516 times, highlighting his significant contributions. In a study authored by Haycock PC et al. [21]. in ‘JAMA Oncology’ during 2017, the research results from the University of Bristol garnered the maximum citation count in this particular area. Studies have demonstrated that extended telomeres are significantly associated with an increased risk for multiple cancers. Among these, the correlation with glioma was the strongest, with an odds ratio of 5.27 (3.15–8.81);the second strongest association was found with serous ovarian cancer of low malignant potential, at 4.35 (2.39–7.94); followed by lung adenocarcinoma at 3.19 (2.40–4.22); neuroblastoma at 2.98 (1.92–4.62); bladder cancer at 2.19 (1.32–3.66); melanoma at 1.87 (1.55–2.26); testicular cancer at 1.76 (1.02–3.04); renal cell carcinoma at 1.55 (1.08–2.23); and endometrial carcinoma at 1.31 (1.07–1.61). Additionally, the study found that for rare cancers and tissues with low stem cell division rates, the correlation between telomere length and cancer risk was more pronounced.

The dual-map overlay of the journal illustrates the spread of Mendel randomized cancer research across various academic fields and the citation relationships among these journals. This study revealed that articles in this domain span multiple disciplines, including neurology, sports, ophthalmology; psychology, education, health; physics, materials, chemistry and veterinary, animal, science. Among the top 11 core journals, ‘Frontiers in Oncology’ and ‘Frontiers in Genetics’ emerged as the most prolific publishers, underscoring their central role in this field. In accordance with Bradford’s law, these journals are acknowledged as significant publications, as they disseminate cutting-edge findings and major breakthroughs in cancer-related Mendelian randomization research. The two most frequently cited journals, ‘Nature Genetics’ and ‘International Journal of Epidemiology’, further demonstrate the substantial influence of these publications in related research.

The most frequently cited publication was an article by Haycock PC published in ‘JAMA Oncology’ in 2017. This study investigated the association between telomere length and cancer risk using Mendelian randomization, establishing itself as a foundational piece of literature in this domain [21]. The causal association between telomere length and cancer risk in this study provides a new path for the optimization of clinical screening strategies. It was revealed that hereditary telomere elongation significantly increased the risk of specific cancers, especially in tissues with low stem cell division rate (such as 427% increased risk of glioma, 219% of lung adenocarcinoma, and 335% of low-grade malignant potential ovarian cancer). This finding supports the deep screening of rare and high-mortality cancer species (such as brain MRI or low-dose CT) for high-risk groups (such as familial tumor history or specific gene carriers). However, it is necessary to be alert to telomere prolongation and reduce the risk of non-tumor diseases such as coronary heart disease (−22%). Therefore, a multi-disease joint risk assessment model should be established in clinical practice to avoid single interpretation of telomere detection results. Following this is a study by Papadimitriou N [22] published in 2020 systematically employed Mendelian randomization to systematically investigate the relationship between physical activity and the likelihood of developing breast and colorectal cancer. Co-citation analysis of references provides insight into the knowledge structure of related research fields, and the analysis of the co-citation network diagram of the references revealed that the most frequently cited work is ‘Mendelian randomization with invalid instruments: effect estimation and bias detection through Egger regression,’ authored by Bowden J and published in the International Journal of Epidemiology in 2015 [31]. This study addressed the issues of effect estimation and bias assessment linked to Egger’s regression in Mendelian randomization analysis. It is concluded that the enhancement of Egger’s regression (MR-Egger) is capable of detecting breaches of conventional instrumental variable assumptions and offers robust effect estimations against these violations. This advancement facilitates sensitivity analyses to evaluate the strength of the findings in MR research.The trend in the application of MR-Egger and other advanced methods in cancer research reflects a significant evolution in the instrumental variable (IV) bias control of the Mendelian randomization (MR) method: from relying on strict IV validity assumptions (e.g., no pleiotropicity) to developing more flexible statistical tools to deal with widespread invalid tools (especially directed pleiotropicity). The core driver of this evolution is the massive genetic variation provided by large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS), prompting researchers to integrate multiple potentially ineffective IVs for analysis.Traditional methods (such as two-stage least squares TSLS or inverse variance weighted IVW) will produce bias in the presence of directional pleiotropy, while MR-Egger transforms the asymmetry of funnel plot (reflecting small research bias) into a tool for detecting directional pleiotropy through Egger regression in analogy meta-analysis, and provides unbiased causal estimation under the weaker InSIDE hypothesis (tool strength and direct effect independence), even if all IVs are invalid. In addition, other methods such as weighted median method (requiring half of IVs to be valid) and LASSO-based invalid IV screening method further expand the scenario of dealing with invalid tools, but the unique advantage of MR-Egger is that it does not require most IVs to be valid.In cancer research, these methods are widely used for causal inference of complex exposures (such as obesity, inflammatory markers) and cancer risk. The funnel plot visualization and MR-Egger intercept test are used to evaluate the pleiotropic bias, and the heterogeneity test (such as Cochran ‘s Q) is used to improve the robustness of the results. However, its limitations (e.g., the InSIDE hypothesis may be violated due to confounding paths, and the statistical performance is low) also promote subsequent development (e.g., two-sample MR for correcting weak instrumental bias, models for processing related IV). The overall trend is reflected in the shift from idealized assumptions to pragmatic processing of the complexity of real-world genetic data, strengthening the reliability of causal conclusions.

Cluster analysis elucidates the primary research topics, emerging directions, and advancements in this field by conducting an in-depth examination of various clusters. In bibliometrics, hot topics are characterized as scientifically related problems or themes that have received attention in the literature over a specific period. The co-occurrence network diagram of keywords is a crucial instrument for identifying the hot topics.In this study, we used the CiteSpace software to cluster keywords, identify 1,946 keywords, and categorize them into 11 primary clusters. These clusters included: “#0 breast cancer”, “#1 inflammatory bowel disease”, “#2 cutaneous melanoma”, “#3 hepatocellular carcinoma”, “#4 follicular lymphoma”, “#5 gut microbiota”, “#6 esophageal cancer”, “#7 myeloproliferative neoplasm”, “#8 lung cancer”, “#9 C-reactive protein”, and “#10 atopic dermatitis.“Keyword analysis revealed the ten most common exposure and outcome factors in cancer-related Mendelian randomization studies. The predominant exposure factors include obesity, body mass index, inflammation, smoking, and cardiovascular disease. The most frequently observed outcome factor was breast cancer, followed by colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and ovarian cancer. These findings suggest that cluster analysis serves as a rapid and effective method to comprehensively understand the primary trends and focal areas in cancer research utilizing Mendelian randomization.This study highlights the central role of obesity in cancer etiology and its importance as a key modifiable risk factor. In view of the potential causal association suggested by Mendelian randomization method, the public health significance of obesity is particularly important. This means that prioritizing effective interventions for overweight and obesity at the population level (such as promoting healthy eating, increasing physical activity, and improving the built environment to support healthy lifestyles) may be an important strategy to reduce the risk of multiple cancers. Therefore, focusing resources on obesity prevention and management not only helps to improve the health of the overall population, but also is likely to be one of the most cost-effective public health priorities for reducing the future cancer burden.

However, the results of studies examining the relationship between the aforementioned exposure factors and cancer risk are inconsistent. The variability in these conclusions may arise from variations in the number of SNPs incorporated in the analyses and the sample size used in the genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Variations in the research methodology may also play a significant role in producing inconsistent results. Different studies may employ diverse analytical strategies, data sets, and methodologies, all of which can influence the final research conclusions. At the same time, both 'selection bias of genetic instrumental variables' (such as weak instrumental variable problem) and 'pleiotropy of one cause' will interfere with MR results. Future Mendelian randomization (MR) studies should thoroughly evaluate all GWAS data pertaining to both exposures and outcomes in order to reach more dependable conclusions. Furthermore, the mediational MR approach should be utilized to assess the influence of various SNP-related intermediate variables on the results, thereby offering a robust scientific foundation for obtaining more reliable conclusions.

Limitations

Although WoSCC is widely recognized as a significant source for bibliometric analysis in the scientific field, our study focused exclusively on cancer research related to Mendelian randomization, published within the WoSCC database. This limitation may not fully capture the current landscape of all cancer-related Mendelian randomization studies. Additionally, as we only included studies published in English, this may have introduced selection bias, potentially rendering our findings inapplicable to related studies published in other languages. Despite the rigorous measures implemented during the search and screening process, it is possible that some unrelated literature was inadvertently included, which may have obscured other pertinent studies. Finally, our analysis of cancer-related Mendelian randomization studies was preliminary and has not yet undergone in-depth verification. There may be limitations in the applicability of our recommendations in this field.

Conclusion

This research offers an extensive review and examination of the literature on cancer-related Mendelian randomization (MR). It effectively summarizes published MR articles pertaining to cancer and meticulously examines various characteristics, including country, institution, author, journal, highly cited articles, title, publication year, abstract, keywords, and both exposure and outcome factors. This study outlines the exposure and outcome factors associated with cancer, discusses their stability, and suggests directions for future research. Mendelian randomization research is gradually expanding from traditional risk factor exploration to the field of tumor treatment response prediction, especially in the identification of immunotherapy biomarkers. Single-cell Mendelian randomization (scMR) and multi-omics Mendelian randomization (multi-omics MR), as the integration direction of emerging technologies, have shown significant potential in the study of cancer mechanisms, especially in the analysis of tumor microenvironment. This study provides important perspectives and directions for subsequent cancer MR research. While acknowledging its limitations, this study established a solid foundation for further exploration in this field. Cross-omics integration (such as the synergistic analysis of Mendelian randomization and epigenetic data) can deepen the mechanism exploration, and analyze the molecular pathways of obesity, inflammation and other exposure factors driving cancer by integrating epigenetic markers such as DNA methylation. Clinical transformation should focus on controversial risk factors (such as the relationship between cholesterol level and specific cancer species), design targeted intervention trials, verify the causal hypothesis found by MR and promote the implementation of precise prevention strategies. In the future, it is urgent to develop advanced models such as Bayesian Mendelian randomization, and to deal with complex confounding such as weak correlation and pleiotropy of instrumental variables through the probability framework, so as to improve the robustness of causal inference. The longitudinal Mendelian randomization (Longitudinal MR) design will be able to more effectively capture the impact of exposure factors (such as obesity, inflammatory markers) on cancer risk over time, revealing a time-dependent causal path; mendelian randomization meta-analysis (Meta-MR) can significantly improve the statistical power and robustness of results by integrating genetic association data from multiple independent studies, especially for the exposure-outcome relationship with small effect size or scattered data, and help to identify the source of heterogeneity. Cross-ethnic MR Validation is essential for assessing the universality of causal associations that have been found. Repeated analysis in populations with different genetic backgrounds can test the portability of genetic instrumental variables (such as whether they are affected by differences in population structure or linkage disequilibrium patterns) and the consistency of causal effects in different populations, which is of great significance for the development of global cancer prevention strategies. Breakthroughs in these directions will promote MR research from association discovery to mechanism analysis and clinical practice. These deepenings will significantly improve the reliability and generalization of MR research results and their practical value for cancer etiology understanding and precise prevention.
