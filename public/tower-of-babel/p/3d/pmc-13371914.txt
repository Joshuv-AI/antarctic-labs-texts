Opposite and reversible optical switching in soft-lattice halide perovskites using light and heat
Authors: Mansha Dubey, Tristan J. Deppe, Andrii Kanak, Abigail R. Hering, Peifen Lyu, Declan Kopper, Paulina V. Escobar, Maksym V. Kovalenko, Jeremy N. Munday, Marina S. Leite
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Halide perovskites have soft lattices with strong interactions between electronic and structural degrees of freedom, making them promising for next-generation optoelectronics. Recent studies have uncovered their unique photo- and thermally induced optical behavior, yet the potential for dynamic, optical switching remains underexplored. Here, we demonstrate reversible and diametrically opposite optical switching in single-crystal cesium lead bromide (CsPbBr3), driven independently by either above-bandgap illumination or thermal excitation. We measure tunable changes in mid-infrared transmission from 3 to 20 micrometers, exceeding 10% at 20 micrometers, with opposite polarity under light and heat, and modulation times <1 millisecond. This antithetical behavior arises from a polaron-like lattice distortion under illumination (reducing transmission), and from lattice expansion from heating partnered with the material’s negative thermo-optic coefficient (increasing transparency). Our findings establish inorganic halide perovskites as a viable material platform for reconfigurable photonics where transmission can be controlled independently through distinct mechanisms intrinsic to their soft lattices.

INTRODUCTION

Optical switching devices are sought after for their fast and dynamic control of light while eliminating the need for lossy, delay-inducing conventional electronic circuits (1–5). The quest for typical switching devices operating in the mid-infrared (MIR) region of the electromagnetic spectrum has relied on phase-change materials (PCMs) that function on the principle of solid-state phase transitions (6, 7). However, these devices often require >130-mW powered laser pulses to enable the switching in as small as 50-nm-thick films, suffer from nonnegligible losses in the insulating state, and only exhibit two distinct states (8, 9). Alternative approaches for optical switching have been shown with inducing changes in material composition through metal hydrogenation (10–12), metal-to-insulator temperature-dependent transitions such as in VO2 (13, 14), bias-induced switching in vacancy-enriched MoS2 (15), polymer-dispersed liquid crystals (16, 17), and mechanically strained metamaterials (18). Optical devices based on Mg have also presented transient response when exposed to water (19, 20), suitable in situations where a single use is required. Yet, these strategies are limited to the visible and near-infrared (NIR) spectra and PCMs often degrade over several thousand cycles.

Until now, bulk and low-dimensional halide perovskites (HPs) have been researched predominantly for photovoltaic (21), lasing (22), and light-emitting diode applications (23), in the visible and NIR ranges. More recently, photo-induced lattice distortions (24), strain (25, 26), and optical polarization (27) in HPs have been investigated with strong promise for switching, photo- and electrostriction, and memory devices. Properties such as long carrier diffusion lengths (28) and high photoluminescence (PL) quantum yields (29) have brought HPs to the forefront of next-generation semiconductor research. However, modulation of the optical properties of HPs has been primarily interrogated in the visible range of the spectrum (30, 31), while the MIR range remains an open avenue for further investigation. Strong electron-phonon coupling leading to the formation of polarons in HPs has been reported to elicit unique optical behaviors (32–37) such as long carrier lifetimes and high color-purity emission. While these studies have provided important insights into the temporal, structural, and electronic dynamics of polaron formation and dissipation, effects on the dynamic optical properties, especially in the MIR spectral bandwidth, are yet to be established. Similarly, the thermal properties of three-dimensional HPs also reveal unusual phenomena such as a large thermal expansion coefficient (38), a negative thermo-optic coefficient (39), and an increasing bandgap energy with temperature (40), which are atypical for conventional semiconducting materials.

Here, we demonstrate large, contrasting changes in the broadband, optical response of millimeter-thick, inorganic HP single crystals, cesium lead bromide (CsPbBr3), under heating versus above-gap illumination using visible-pump-MIR-probe optical measurements. Upon heating, we detect a reversible increase in the MIR transmission, and conversely, photoexcitation is found to reversibly decrease its MIR transmission. We quantify the modulation of the MIR transmission as ∆T = Tfinal − Tinitial, where Tfinal and Tinitial represent the transmission either under stimulus (light and heat) and no stimulus (dark and room temperature), respectively. In the case of heating, we attribute the increased transparency of CsPbBr3 to its negative thermo-optic coefficient and substantial thermal expansion of the lattice at 80°C. However, under illumination, ∣∆T∣ monotonically increases with above-gap laser excitation power, and we report a maximum∣∆T∣ of ∼15% at 40 mW when the sample is illuminated from both sides. Our findings clearly establish that in the case of laser excitation, the ∆T stems directly from charge carrier generation enabling multistate optical switching. We track the cyclic modulation of MIR transmission at speeds as fast as <1 ms, highlighting the suitability of HPs for applications in optical switching, limiting, and cloaking. Our results lay the foundation for future reconfigurable photonics based on all-inorganic HPs that can achieve large changes in transmission on the order of milliseconds.

RESULTS

We quantify the changes in the optical transmission of bulk CsPbBr3 single crystals under two different stimuli of equivalent energy: heat and light (Fig. 1A). We use high-quality centimeter-scale single crystals grown using the Bridgman method (see Materials and Methods for details), with strong absorption and low loss (41–47) (see note S1 and figs. S1 to S4). First, we measure an increase in the MIR transmission, ∆T, of ∼10% at 20 μm when the sample is heated to 80°C (Fig. 1A, top panel). We then perform optical visible-pump-MIR-probe measurements to determine ∆T when the crystal is illuminated with above-bandgap excitation (532 nm, 40 mW) at room temperature (Fig. 1A, bottom panel, and fig. S5). The MIR transmission in this case decreases by ∼10%, revealing an opposite trend to when the HP material is heated. The pump laser beam diameter is set to 3 mm to ensure complete overlap with the 3-mm MIR probe beam transmitting through the crystal. The reflectivity of the crystal remains unchanged under the same illumination conditions (fig. S5). Hence, the change in transmission is unrelated to the reflectivity of the sample. The two stimuli (light and heat) represent equivalent energies under the assumption that all incident photon energy (532 nm, 40 mW) is converted to thermal energy in the HP. In such a case, the sample is expected to heat up to 80°C (see Materials and Methods, fig. S6, and table S1 for heat transfer calculations). It is important to note, however, that the two stimuli, light and heat, elicit two distinct optical responses from the material, indicating that different mechanisms dominate in each regime. To verify whether the two effects have competing or complementary contributions, we perform the same optical pump-probe measurements at 80°C and find that |∆T| under illumination at 80°C is the same as that at room temperature (fig. S7, A and B). As such, the two mechanisms appear independent of each other; however, the optical responses from each stimulus could have an effectively cancelling effect if acting together (fig. S7C). Therefore, we dismiss any competing contributions to ∆T from laser heating and focus on the light- and heat-induced changes independently.

To investigate whether the changes in optical properties are related to structural modifications in the crystal, we perform x-ray diffraction (XRD) under the same heating and illumination conditions (see Fig. 1B). The complete measured single-crystal XRD scan covering a 2θ range of 10° to 80° and the simulated powder XRD scan for comparison are shown in fig. S8. The crystal has an orthorhombic structure at room temperature. For the XRD measurements, the crystal is oriented such that the {010} planes are normal to the axis of measurement. A picture of the laser-illuminated crystal in the XRD chamber is shown in fig. S9. On heating the crystal to 80°C, we measure a notable decrease in the 2θ positions of both the (040) and (202) peaks and an increase in the intensity of the (202) plane peak as shown in Fig. 1B. The (202) plane is a rotational structural twin of the (040) plane (45). The increase in intensity of the (202) peak is likely due to a change in strain in the lattice favoring the (202) orientation over the (040). We further investigate this finding by heating the crystal up to 100°C where it exists in the tetragonal phase (40) (fig. S10) and observe a similar shift in 2θ to the scan at 80°C, and the (040) peak retains a higher intensity compared to the (202) peak. Further heating the crystal to 150°C causes a transition to the cubic phase where the (040) and (202) peaks consolidate verifying the absence of structural twins. However, upon cooling the crystal back to 25°C, the (202) peak shows a higher intensity than the (040) peak, confirming that the formation of either plane can be favored as the strain state of the crystal is modulated. In the case of illumination, there is a small but discernible decrease in 2θ along the (040) peak shown in Fig. 1C, which indicates an increase in the d-spacing along the b (long) axis of the orthorhombic crystal. The lack of change in the position of the (202) peak implies that the crystal lattice does not expand along the a and c axes, suggesting that the expansion is anisotropic. The laser (532 nm) penetration depth is on the order ∼300 nm, which corresponds to a small fraction of the 1-mm-thick crystal. The effects produced by the laser are likely confined to the illumination volume and, hence, might appear diminished when averaged over the bulk volume. Nonetheless, in all cases, once the stimulus (light or heat) is removed, the structure consistently reverts to its equilibrium state. This reversible, structural response is schematically represented in Fig. 1D, where heating the crystal to 80°C leads to a larger unit cell volume (b′ > b) as the crystal favors a more tetragonal structure. Meanwhile, illuminating the HP leads to a smaller, noticeable change to a more ordered state (b′ > b″ > b), highlighting the different structural changes resulting from the distinct stimuli.

We hypothesize that the opposite trends in ∆T arise from distinct electronic contributions from different phenomena. In the case of laser illumination, we attribute the optical and structural changes to the formation of a polaron-like state resulting in localized photo-induced phase transition (48, 49). Unlike traditional semiconductors such as Si and GaAs, HPs exhibit strong lattice anharmonicity and electron-phonon coupling (50). This coupling leads to polaron formation, under photoexcitation, leading to the coulombic screening of electrons and holes (33, 34, 51). This phenomenon has been widely postulated as the leading cause for long carrier lifetimes and slow hot carrier cooling in HPs (37, 52–55). Similarly, here we hypothesize that above-bandgap illumination results in the generation of charge carriers that couple with phonon modes causing localized deformation of the material’s crystalline lattice. The valence band in lead HPs is mainly composed of the Br 4p orbital and Pb 6s and 6p orbitals, while the conduction band is drawn from the Pb 6p orbital (56). Hence, any distortions of the PbBr64− octahedra directly influence the material band structure, as we verify later with a light-induced change in the absorption edge. Increased Pb-Br bond length reduces the overlap between the Pb 6p and Br 4p orbitals. As shown through the XRD, the Pb-Br-Pb bond length (b″) increases in the out-of-equilibrium state, suggesting that photogenerated carriers drive the lattice reorganization to a more ordered state (57) with direct implications for the polarizability of the lattice. We hypothesize that this change in crystal polarizability manifests as the observed decrease in optical transmission.

The strong lattice anharmonicity also acts as the impetus for large thermal expansion and negative thermo-optic coefficients in HPs (58, 59). Given that the refractive index of HPs decreases with temperature, transmission is expected to increase at higher temperatures as shown by our results. Conventional semiconductors like Si that have positive thermo-optic coefficients show a decrease in MIR transmission with temperature due to the well-known phenomenon of increased phonon scattering (60). We observe a decrease in the transmission upon heating a Si crystal (fig. S11A), clearly contrasting with the optical response of the HP. Under both heating and illumination, |∆T| increases with wavelength. The wavelength dependence of the change in transmission arises from the dispersive optical response of CsPbBr3 and the distinct mechanisms causing the light- and heat-induced effects. Under illumination, like most polaronic effects, the dominant features are in the MIR, where the relative change in transmission becomes larger than in the visible spectrum. During heating, the material’s large thermal expansion and negative thermo-optic coefficient likewise produce a stronger response at longer wavelengths, where phonon contributions to the dielectric function are most substantial. Moreover, Si exhibits free carrier absorption in the MIR range due to scattering from free carriers, phonons, and impurities, resulting in a decrease in the optical transmission. However, this is only observed at sufficiently high carrier concentrations (>1018 cm−3) (60). Under illumination at 40 mW, we calculate the maximum steady-state photocarrier density as 1014 cm−3 based on the carrier generation and recombination rates (61–63) (see note S2 and table S2 for calculation), which is insufficiently high to facilitate free carrier absorption. Therefore, we do not attribute the observed decrease in transmission to free carrier absorption. We verify this hypothesis by subjecting a single-crystal n-type Si wafer of similar carrier concentration to the same stimuli (fig. S9B). No response to photoexcitation in doped-Si indicates that the carrier concentration is lacking for free carrier absorption, and the lattice rigidity and harmonicity limits electron-phonon coupling, rendering the formation of a polaron-like state unlikely. It is noteworthy that the higher optoelectronic quality of commercially sourced single-crystal Si (see Materials and Methods) with a negligible defect density (<1.0 cm−2) combined with a lower absorption coefficient (6460 cm−1 for Si versus 28,000 cm−1 for HPs, at 532 nm) will lead to distinct optoelectronic behavior. This contrasting spectral behavior of HPs and Si in response to both heating and illumination further showcases the unique properties of the former, positing their application in optical switching.

Our experimental setup is depicted in Fig. 2A. The wavelengths of the probe beams range from 480 nm to 20 μm. An external pump is set up such that its beam is incident over the same side and area of the HP as the probe beam. The transmission through the sample is measured using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. We measure the absorption, A, as A = 1 − R − T (%), where R is the reflection, over the visible light spectrum as shown in Fig. 2B. Change in absorption, ∆A, is shown in Fig. 2C for clarity. The reversible red shift in the absorption spectrum under illumination corroborates the lattice expansion noted in the XRD scans (24). To gain a deeper understanding of how the pump-laser characteristics drive the optical switching phenomenon, we vary the pump intensity, polarization, and wavelength while measuring MIR transmission as shown in Fig. 2 (D to F, respectively). We adjust the pump power from 1 to 40 mW (Fig. 2D), with the pump wavelength set to 532 nm, and note a monotonic increase in |∆T|. A larger density of generated charge carriers would directly imply a higher polaron density within the material and, hence, a larger |∆T|. There is a nearly quadratic relationship with increasing pump power, reminiscent of other nonlinear optical phenomena like the optical Kerr effect (64), in which a material’s refractive index scales quadratically with the intensity of an applied electric field. We then vary the polarization of the incident laser from 0° to 90° at a power of 1 mW, in Fig. 2E, and detect no significant change in |∆T|. Thus, we conclude that |∆T| is independent of the laser polarization. Last, Fig. 2E shows that |∆T| remains consistent for pump wavelengths shorter than the bandgap (λ < 535 nm), before a distinct drop off at 535 nm and beyond. Here, too, the pump power is set to 1 mW for all wavelengths tested. In addition, there is a negligible ∣∆T∣ when illuminating the CsPbBr3 with a laser wavelength below bandgap (685 nm) and laser power as high as 40 mW (fig. S12). Our findings clearly establish that this phenomenon is a direct result of charge carrier generation and mainly driven by their density within the HP single crystal. In addition, this effect is largely surface dominated. As mentioned earlier, the penetration depth of the pump is limited to ∼300 nm from the sample surface at a pump wavelength of 532 nm. Although HPs show long carrier diffusion lengths up to 1 μm, the generated carriers likely only propagate through 0.1% of the 1-mm-thick crystal (1 μm from the top surface). When the laser beam is split and the CsPbBr3 crystal is illuminated from both sides, there is an increase in |∆T| of up to 15% (fig. S11), suggesting that |∆T| can also be controlled by the illuminated area of the sample further adding to the tunability of |∆T|.

Enhanced switching speeds with minimal hysteresis are crucial for the operation of nonlinear optical devices. Therefore, we quantify the time-dependent response of our system using a custom-built pump-probe setup that allows tracking of the MIR signal on millisecond timescales. Figure 3 (A and B) shows the recorded intensity of the transmission signal (top panels, black and gray) in response to the 532- and 685-nm laser pulses (bottom panels), respectively. The modulation in |∆T| under above-gap illumination from the 532-nm laser is detected while no change in transmission is measured under below-gap illumination from the 685-nm laser. At the detection capabilities of our set up, the switching between on/off states is hysteresis-free and occurs within 1 ms, as shown in Fig. 3C. Similarly in this case, with below-gap excitation, there is no change detected in the signal (Fig. 3D). The heights of the highlighted bars are the average signal detected for the “on” and “off” states and mainly serve as guides to the eye. Note that the CsPbBr3 single-crystal characteristics are preserved during the multiple cycles, establishing HPs as an alternative to PCM for photonics. It is essential to note that we anticipate observing changes at faster timescales than those measured here. A thinner crystal with a larger illuminated volume akin to the highly optimized PCM devices (8, 14, 65) would be required to maintain the high on/off ratio at microsecond to picosecond switching speeds. Given the primitive nature of the bulk HP sample used in this work, it is critical to keep in mind that development and optimization of a complete device is required to exceed the switching magnitudes and speeds of the state-of-the-art PCM devices. These outcomes reveal the suitability of HPs in a new realm of photonics, such as those in optical sensing, limiting and cloaking.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we present a material and pathway for broadband, low-loss, and multistate optical switching based on HPs. Changes in the MIR optical properties of CsPbBr3 single crystals under distinct stimuli, laser illumination and heating, were quantified using visible-pump-MIR-probe optical methodology and XRD measurements. |∆T| ≅ 10% was measured under both illumination and heating, but with opposite polarities. An increase in the crystal lattice parameter and a reduction in the material bandgap provided clear indications of the photo-induced structural distortions of the lattice. Meanwhile, properties such as the large thermal expansion coefficient and negative thermo-optic coefficient lead to increased transparency at elevated temperatures. The measured ∆T at 1-ms on/off cycles showcased the reversibility and robustness of the single crystal against degradation and suitability of HPs in optical switching devices. These findings have opened HPs to a new avenue of applications with the potential to yield tunable optics with simple fabrication routes, substantially lower costs, and boosted efficiencies through low-loss operation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Material synthesis

Synthesis of polycrystalline CsPbBr3

CsPbBr3 was synthesized from commercially available pure CsBr (6 N) and PbBr2 (5 N). For the synthesis of 20 g of the perovskite compound, the stoichiometric amounts of the raw materials were thoroughly mixed in a mortar, following a molar ratio of 1:1. This mixture was then loaded into a quartz ampoule with an inner diameter of 10 mm. All preparation steps were conducted inside a glovebox filled with argon gas. The prepared ampoule was connected to a vacuum pump and evacuated to approximately 10−3 mbar before being flame sealed. The synthesis of CsPbBr3 was then carried out using a solid-state method (melting the materials together) in a muffle furnace. The sealed ampoule was heated to 650°C and maintained at this temperature for 10 hours to ensure the material homogenization and the completion of the chemical reaction. Afterward, the furnace was allowed to cool to room temperature over the course of 10 hours. As a result, a bright orange polycrystalline ingot was obtained.

Bridgman growth of CsPbBr3 single crystal

After the synthesis, the ampoule with material was placed in a vertical three-zone Bridgman furnace and heated to a temperature of 650°C. The furnace was maintained at this temperature for 10 to 15 hours to ensure complete melting and homogenization of the material. After that, the ampoule was translated from the hot to the cold zone of the furnace at a speed of 0.5 to 1 mm/hour to perform directional crystallization. Once the growth process was complete, the furnace was gradually cooled to room temperature for 20 hours. The resulting CsPbBr3 single crystal was free of cracks and transparent.

Sample preparation

The obtained CsPbBr3 single crystal was cut into wafers with a thickness of 1 mm, perpendicular to the growth axis of the ingot, using a diamond wire saw. The samples were polished with sandpaper of varying grit sizes, specifically 4000, 8000, and 12,000. For the final polishing step alumina powder with a particle size of 1 μm was used. The resulting substrates were <20 nm in roughness. During the cutting and polishing process, WD-40 was used as a coolant and lubricant. Afterward, the samples were washed in toluene to eliminate any remnants of the lubricant, polishing powder, and microparticles of the material. The Si wafer was purchased from MTI Corporation (Materials & Technology Inc.), with defect density < 1.0 cm−2 and an estimated volumetric defect density < 0.1 cm−3 (assuming constant concentration of defects along the thickness of the crystal).

Material characterizations

X-ray diffraction

XRD measurements were performed using a Rigaku Smartlab Diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation. A Cu Kβ filter was used. The scans were collected over a range of 2θ = 10° to 80°, with a step size of 0.0008° (maximum system resolution) and a scan rate of 0.96°/min. The sample tilt (χ) and rotation (ϕ) were optimized to set (010) as the out-of-plane direction. Measured data (scatter plots) are fit using a pseudo-Voigt model (solid lines). A 532-nm continuous-wave laser diode (Vortran Laser Technologies) was used for the laser-illuminated scans. The laser power was set to 40 mW, and the beam diameter was 1 mm. The schematics were generated in VESTA with data for the equilibrium state from (66).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Bruker Invenio X spectrometer. A total of 200 scans were averaged over a range of 4500 to 450 cm−1 and 23,000 to 8333 cm−1 for the MIR and visible ranges, respectively. Transmission measurements were taken at normal incidence. Reflection measurements were performed at an incident angle of 12° and a gold mirror was used as the reference. A 532-nm continuous-wave laser (Vortran Laser Technologies) was used for the laser-illuminated scans. For the pump wavelength-dependent measurement, a supercontinuum laser (Fianium) was used and the power for each wavelength was set to 1 mW at a repetition rate of 80 MHz. The polarization of the laser was modulated using a half–wave plate.

Photoluminescence

PL measurements were taken with a Princeton Instruments spectrometer. The excitation laser wavelength was 457 nm and power was set to 1 mW.

Ellipsometry

Ellipsometry measurements were performed with a J.A. Woollam VIS-NIR ellipsometer. Fittings of the real and imaginary refractive indices were performed using the WVASE software. Data for the spatial maps were linearly interpolated between all measured points.

Fast timescale measurements

The fast time dynamics measurements were recorded using a custom-built setup. The sample was illuminated with 532- and 685-nm continuous-wave lasers (Vortran Laser Technologies). The laser was chopped at 100 Hz and 1 kHz using a Thorlabs MC1F30 chopper blade and MC2000B controller. The laser signal was split using a glass slide to direct a small portion of the signal to a photodiode. The chopped laser signal was recorded using a Tektonix Oscilloscope. A blackbody source (HGH Infrared Systems) set to 300°C was used as the IR light source. The transmitted IR signal was collected using a liquid nitrogen–cooled mercury-cadmium-telluride (MCT) detector (IR Associates). The MCT detector signal was simultaneously recorded on the same oscilloscope. Because of the noise in the signal at such high frequencies, we extract four data points recorded in each state (on and off) and show the mean and SD at each condition to illustrate the modulation in the transmission signal.

Heat transfer calculations

Heat transfer simulations were performed using the heat transfer module in COMSOL Multiphysics. The computational domain was simplified to a two-dimensional axisymmetric slice representing the disk in radial (r) and vertical (z) dimensions. The boundary conditions included a prescribed heat flux in the laser-irradiated region (40 mW), a constant temperature at the base to represent the aluminum sample holder, and convective heat loss elsewhere, assuming ambient conditions of 25.4°C with a heat transfer coefficient of 0.46 W/m2 K (58).
