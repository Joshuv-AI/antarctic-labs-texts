Prospective comparative study of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair with or without acellular dermal matrix augmentation: Clinical and radiological outcomes
Authors: Sergi Sastre, Maria Luisa Pérez, Yuri Postnikov, Salvador Madariaga, Albert Arman, Lluis Peidro, Guillem Claret, Victoria Pomenta, Raquel García-Tarriño
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:To prospectively evaluate the safety and effectiveness of arthroscopic acellular dermal matrix (ADM) augmentation of large rotator cuff tear repairs (RCR) in a comparative study.Methods:A prospective single-center series of 20 patients undergoing arthroscopic RCR. The patients were randomized to arthroscopic transosseous-equivalent rotator cuff patch augmentation repair with a decellularized dermis prepared in the tissue bank or a control group without augmentation. Preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes were assessed by The University of California-Los Angeles (UCLA) score. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evaluation was obtained 1 year after surgery. All adverse events were recorded. Statistical analysis was performed in R 4.2 (Boston, USA), using non-parametric group tests for paired data to evaluate functional results. The mean age was 59 years in the ADM group and 56.7 years in the control group. The gender distribution was 60% male and 40% female in both groups.Results:Each group consisted of 10 patients, with no statistically significant differences observed in age, body mass index, or preoperative UCLA assessment. In the ADM augmentation group, 2 patients required reoperation due to re-tear before the end of the follow-up period. No statistically significant differences were found in functional outcome measures at any of the evaluation points. Final MRI identified 5 reruptures in the matrix group and 6 in the control group, showing no statistical difference between groups. No adverse events attributable to the presence of the grafts were detected.Conclusion:These findings suggest that ADM augmentation may yield outcomes comparable to standard care, in terms of UCLA scores and re-tear rates. No adverse events related to ADM were observed.Level of Evidence: Level IV, Therapeutic study.

Introduction

Rotator cuff injuries are the leading cause of shoulder pain, especially among people older than 50. If a complete tear occurs, the gold standard today is arthroscopic reconstruction. Although this type of surgery is associated with better clinical results, new surgical and rehabilitation strategies are being developed to improve its outcomes. However, published studies have shown a great variability in failure rates.1 Failures have been associated with multiple factors, such as tear size, thickness, AC joint, chronicity, and intraoperative tendon quality.2 Other predictors of worse prognosis include older age, low bone density, female gender, muscle infiltration by fat, diabetes, reduced acromio-humeral space, previous surgeries, or smoking history.3 The degenerative condition of the tendon tissue is also a possible factor. Finally, the tension needed to suture the repaired tendon to the footprint (whether in double-row or single-row) may also be a reason for incomplete healing.4

In recent years, augmentation of rotator cuff repairs with biological materials has been developed with the aim of improving the healing environment at the tendon–bone interface.8 Allograft augmentation has been shown to increase initial strength and provide an environment to stimulate tissue regeneration and remodeling.9 Additionally, augmentation can provide protection during healing, potentially decreasing failure rates.5

Nowadays, synthetic meshes or biological matrices of different origins are available in clinical practice.6 Bioprosthetic materials, such as acellular dermal matrices (ADMs), have gained importance due to their biocompatibility and lower risk of infection.7 In fact, dermal regeneration matrices have proven useful in various surgeries for wound healing and tissue reinforcement, such as in breast reconstruction, abdominal wall repair, and tendon repair.8

Not all dermal matrices are suitable for rotator cuff augmentation. Thin matrices are more likely to fail during suturing, while recent research supports the use of thicker grafts for irreparable massive rotator cuff tears. These studies have shown good pain relief and functional improvement after surgery.9

We hypothesized that arthroscopic rotator cuff repair augmented with ADM would result in lower re-tear rates and improved clinical outcomes compared to standard repair.

Material and methods

Study design

A prospective, randomized, single-center clinical study was performed in a consecutive series of patients undergoing Davidson Geometric Classification10 grade 3 arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ruptures > 2 cm medio-lateral length + > 2 cm anteroposterior length). Type 4 with cuff atrophy were excluded to avoid confusion.

To determine the appropriate sample size, a power analysis was performed. The study was designed to detect significant differences in re-tear rates between groups, assuming a failure rate of 85% for the control group based on published data showing re-tear rates ranging from 60% to 90% in large tears without augmentation.14

Patients were randomized into 2 groups by sealed envelope technique opened intraoperatively. Group 1 underwent arthroscopic transosseous-equivalent (TOE) repair with ADM augmentation, while group 2 received TOE repair alone.

Patients were excluded if they had irreparable tears, significant subscapularis tears, significant biceps tendon pathology requiring tenotomy or tenodesis, or significant acromial spurs, as these conditions could confound results. Other exclusion criteria included revision surgery, inflammatory or systemic disease, infection, or smoking.

No tension-reducing procedures, such as margin convergence, were performed in either group beyond the TOE technique.

The dermal graft consisted of a human acellular dermal allograft processed in the institutional tissue bank (Banc de Sang I Teixits de Catalunya, BST®), decellularized, glycerol-preserved, and sterilized by gamma irradiation. The same type of ADM was used in all cases, and no commercial brand was used.7

Preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes were assessed with the UCLA score. Postoperative evaluations were performed at 6 and 12 months.

Magnetic resonance imaging evaluation was performed at 12 months by independent radiologists blinded to the treatment group. The MRI results were classified into 3 categories, based on Sugaya’s classification (see Table 1).11

Total healed tendon: Sugaya I

Partial healing with slimming of the remaining tendon: Sugaya II + III

Re-tear: Sugaya IV + V

This study was conducted in accordance with the approval of Ethics Committee of the University of Barcelona (Approval HCB2020/0034 Date: January, 2020) and good clinical practice guidelines, and applicable local regulatory requirements and laws. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients who agreed to take part in the study.

Statistical analysis

The gathered data were classified into 3 categories: numeric (age, height, weight, body mass index [BMI], and duration of symptoms), categorical (medical history, use of analgesics), and time related (date of surgery). Due to the limited sample size and the non-normal distribution of most of the data, as confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, the authors opted for non-parametric tests for the analysis, specifically the Wilcoxon and Fisher tests. Statistical analysis was performed in R 4.2 (Boston, USA), applying Wilcoxon and Fisher’s exact tests for non-parametric comparisons, with a significance threshold of P < .05.

Surgical procedure

Anesthesia was administered with an interscalene block followed by sedation with a laryngeal mask. The patient was then placed in a lateral decubitus position with skin traction. Tear margins were debrided until viable tissue was ready for tear repair. It is important to highlight the use of a burr to partially decorticate the footprint at the greater tuberosity where the tendon is attached; this is necessary to ensure a good tendon-bone union. The native tendon was reattached to the area adjacent to the articular cartilage or a slightly more lateral area as tendon tension and excursion permitted.

Tendon lesion was measured using a calibrated probe (Figures 1A and B), and the preparation of the graft was based on the size of the tear (usually 4 cm2) and 2 mm of thickness. The graft was intentionally oversized by 4 mm on each side to allow for the placement of sutures. In addition, some marks were drawn on the medial corners of the graft (medial anterior and medial posterior) to orient it once placed into the subacromial space.

In order to perform a TOE, 2 anchors (Healicoil Peek 5.5 mm, Smith & Nephew Andover, MA) were implanted at the medial aspect of the footprint, 1 anterior and 1 posterior, close to the cartilage line. Two high-strength threads were passed through the anterior edge of the tear and 2 more through its posterior aspect. These sutures were placed as medially as possible. Outside the arthroscopic space of the shoulder, the same threads were passed through the medial borders of the ADM as shown in Figure 1C. The sheath of the lateral working portal was subsequently retrieved, and the portal was slightly enlarged. The ADM was then carefully inserted by tensioning the slipping knots and accompanying them with the knot pusher. At this point, subacromial visualization and placement of the dermis were crucial. Time and attention were given to ensure there was no thread tangling or graft malposition. The ADM must be placed with anteromedial and posteromedial landmarks on top of the medial aspect of the rotator cuff tear accordingly.

The TOE technique ended as follows: 1 anterior and 1 posterior thread were crossed through a knotless anchor (Footprint 5.5 mm, Smith & Nephew), and the anchor was implanted at the antero-lateral aspect of the footprint. Subsequently, 1 anterior and 1 posterior thread were passed through another footprint anchor and implanted at the postero-lateral aspect of the footprint, ensuring rotator cuff repair to the footprint as well as augmentation with ADM. Finally, a lateral arthroscopic exploration ensured the correct placement of the graft and the repair of the tear (Figure 1D, Video 1).

For the control group, the same TOE technique was used without the graft above the repaired tendon.

Postoperatively, patients’ extremities were placed in a sling for 4 to 6 weeks, allowing daily pendulum motion exercises. Supervised physical therapy was started at 6 weeks, with strengthening allowed at 12 weeks postoperatively.

Adverse events occurring both in the first 30 days after the procedure and in the long term were reported and analyzed. An adverse event form was created as part of this study. The primary endpoint was to evaluate the presence of residual tendon defects on MRI 1 year post-surgery. Healing was defined as complete excursion of the repaired tendon to the greater tuberosity with attachment to bone. The secondary endpoints were to measure the graft’s effectiveness by clinical outcomes and to determine the incidence of acute or late adverse events for both study groups.

Results

Patient demographics

This study included a total of 20 patients, with 10 patients in each group. The average age in the ADM group was 59 years (range 46 to 64). The average BMI was 26.64, with a range of 20-33. The average duration of symptoms was 14.5 months, ranging from 6 to 36 months. The average age of the patients in the control group was 56.7 years (range 52 to 65), and the average BMI was 25.8, with individual BMIs ranging from 19 to 33. The average duration of symptoms was 9.5 months, with a range from 7 to 20 months. No statistical differences were identified between groups (Table 2).

Clinical scores

The clinical scores (UCLA scale) were evaluated at different points of observation, including pre-surgery, 2, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The difference from the initial score was compared between the 2 groups (Table 3). The results at the initial assessment and at the final follow-up are presented in Tables 4 and 5.

Table 4 presents the UCLA scores for the control group and the dermal matrix group at the point of initial assessment. For the control group, the mean total score was 17.9 with a SD of 3.18. The scores for pain, function, flexion, and strength were 3.8, 6.0, 3.7, and 4.2, respectively.

For the ADM group, the mean total score was 15.6 with a SD of 3.69. The scores for pain, function, flexion, and strength were 3.7, 4.4, 3.2, and 4.2, respectively. The satisfaction score was consistently 0 for both groups before surgery.

Table 5 presents the change in UCLA score from the initial assessment to the final follow-up for both groups. Positive numbers represent a higher score in comparison with the initial assessment, while negative numbers indicate a lower score in comparison with the initial assessment.

For the control group, the mean change in total score was 10.9 with a SD of 7.23. The changes in scores for pain, function, flexion, and strength were 4.0, 2.0, 0.8, and 0.3, respectively. The mean change in satisfaction score was 4.0 for this group.

For the dermal matrix group, the change in mean total score was 12.25 with a SD of 6.18. The changes in mean scores for pain, function, flexion, and strength were 3.75, 3.5, 1.625, and 0.25 points, respectively. The satisfaction score increased to 3.125 for this group.

No statistically significant differences were observed at any follow-up point in any variable. However, there was a slight trend towards better functional outcome in terms of mobility in the dermal matrix group (P = .08) at the last follow-up.

In the ADM group, 2 patients required revision surgery due to symptomatic re-tear. In the control group, although 6 patients presented re-tear on MRI, none underwent revision surgery because they reported minimal or no symptoms.

No adverse events attributable to the ADM were observed during the follow-up.

While the statistical analysis did not reveal a significant difference between the 2 groups, it is important to highlight that re-tear was the most frequently observed outcome in both groups. This observation might imply that the likelihood of re-tear is comparable between the 2 treatments, but this hypothesis would need to be validated with additional research involving more patients, because the differences that the authors expected to find between the 2 groups based on previous literature analysis were much smaller than expected.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that ADM augmentation did not significantly improve functional outcomes or reduce re-tear rates compared to standard TOE repair. This observation is clinically relevant because ADM augmentation increased surgical time and cost without demonstrating a significant additional benefit, arguing against its routine use.

One key advantage of dermal patch augmentation is its compatibility with arthroscopic procedures, which can result in reduced postoperative pain and faster recovery times. The patch provides immediate stability and strength to the repaired tendon, potentially minimizing the likelihood of re-injury.

In this series, the re-tear rate was relatively high in both groups. One possible explanation is that no additional biological enhancement methods, such as stem cells, were applied. Furthermore, no additional tension-reducing techniques beyond the TOE repair were performed, which might have contributed to increased mechanical stress on the repair site. However, it is important to note that these biological strategies have not consistently demonstrated superior healing rates in the literature, and TOE is itself considered a tension-reducing method.

These results are consistent with other studies reporting persistently high re-tear rates despite dermal matrix augmentation, highlighting the limitations of this technique. Avanzi et al12 reported that patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair using porcine dermal patches demonstrated significant improvements in strength and range of motion compared to those who had standard repairs. Augmented repairs utilizing a medialized single-row technique were associated with higher healing rates and near-normal MRI findings.

Similarly, Barber et al13 conducted a randomized, prospective, multicenter study involving 42 patients treated for larger rotator cuff tears (over 3 cm). After 2 years, the augmented group showed a significantly lower re-tear rate (13%) compared to the control group (45%). Functional outcomes, measured by American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Score (ASES) and constant scores, were superior in the augmented group, with MRI scans showing more intact repairs and no complications linked to the dermal matrix.

Another trial, led by Lee et al,14 compared rotator cuff repairs with and without allograft patch augmentation in 43 patients over a 5.7-year follow-up. Patients receiving allograft patches exhibited better ASES scores and notably lower re-tear rates (9.1% versus 38.1%). The study highlighted that when the rotator cuff footprint remains exposed after repair, patch augmentation can lower the chances of re-tears.

Gilot et al15 conducted a blinded comparative study of 35 patients, finding a 10% re-tear rate in the augmented group versus 26% in the control group, along with better outcomes in the former. However, the study’s lack of randomization may have influenced group homogeneity. In another review, Bond et al16 analyzed outcomes for 16 patients with massive rotator cuff tears treated with GraftJacket® allografts, reporting a 19% failure rate—lower than the 26.6% noted in McElvany’s meta-analysis17 and the 94% found by Galatz1 for non-augmented repairs.

Compared to these studies, the current results showed no significant difference in re-tear rate or functional outcome between ADM augmentation and standard TOE repair, which suggests that augmentation may not deliver consistent benefits across different patient populations.

It is important to recognize that dermal patch augmentation is not suitable for all rotator cuff tears. This method appears particularly beneficial for large tears or cases involving poor tissue quality. For instance, Hong et al18 reviewed 24 elderly patients with moderate-to-large tears and found significant clinical improvements following onlay patch augmentation. At the final assessment, patients reported improvements in ASES, Constant–Murley, and pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, with re-tear rates of 25% when Sugaya type 3 lesions were classified as re-tears, dropping to 16.7% when considered intact.

For smaller or medium tears, where healing rates are already high, the cost of patch augmentation may not be justified. Chalmers et al,19 in a meta-analysis, found that surgical repair significantly reduces tear size compared to nonoperative treatment, altering the progression of rotator cuff disease. Thus, patch augmentation is most beneficial for large or massive tears and irreparable cases, where it can outperform partial repairs.20

Snyder et al16 have frequently used a cellular human dermal matrix allograft (AHDMA) for revision rotator cuff repairs, achieving over 80% healing rates in a series of 13 patients. While they advocate for further development of synthetic materials, they consider AHDMA a leading option currently.

Thangarajah et al21 reviewed scaffolds for rotator cuff tears, finding potential in scaffolds and biologically enhanced materials but noting that none yet replicate the mechanical properties of native tendon. Although some studies showed improved outcomes with augmentation for large, degenerative tears, the results did not confirm such benefits, since functional scores and re-tear rates were similar between groups.

Augmentation adds complexity, cost, and risks such as longer surgical times and higher infection rates. Villatte et al22 compared autografts and allografts for large tears, reporting re-tear rates of 27% and 21%, respectively, with similar functional improvements.

This study has some limitations, including a small patient cohort and the use of only 12-month clinical outcomes, although most re-tears occur within the first year postoperatively. No immunogenic complications or adverse effects were reported in the study.

In summary, this discussion confirms that ADM augmentation did not offer significant advantages over standard TOE repair in the series, despite prior studies reporting variable benefits. Given the increased surgical time and cost, and in the absence of consistent functional improvement, ADM augmentation should not be considered standard practice for large rotator cuff tears based on the current data. Further large-scale studies are needed to confirm these findings.
