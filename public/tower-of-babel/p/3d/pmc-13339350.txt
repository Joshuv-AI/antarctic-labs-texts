7 T MRI Connectivity‐Guided Reprogramming Improves Deep Brain Stimulation Motor Outcome in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Yarit Wiggerts, Annabel van der Weide, Rick Schuurman, Pepijn van den Munckhof, Rob MA de Bie, Martijn Beudel, Maarten Bot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is an established treatment for Parkinson's disease (PD). Using 7‐Tesla (7 T) MRI connectivity analysis to visualize the patient‐specific STN motor subdivision, we hypothesize that reprogramming patients who are stimulated outside of this subdivision can improve their motor outcome substantially, without occurrence of adverse effects.ObjectivesTo determine whether connectivity‐guided reprogramming improves motor outcome in PD patients treated with STN‐DBS.MethodsPatients who underwent STN‐DBS with 7 T MRI probabilistic tractography (2022–2024) were evaluated after standardized follow‐up. Their volume of tissue activated (VTA) was visualized with clinical guided programming. Patients were reprogrammed if their VTA did not overlap with the connectivity‐defined STN motor subdivision. Follow‐up outcome was assessed using the Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale motor part (MDS‐UPDRS III; OFF medication, ON stimulation state) and hours OFF per day (MDS‐UPDRS IV).ResultsTwelve patients were included, of whom nine were reprogrammed. Mean MDS‐UPDRS III score after classic programming improved by 16 ± 27% compared to baseline (48 ± 14 to 40 ± 13), and improved significantly further to 42 ± 26% after connectivity‐guided reprogramming (28 ± 8; p = 0.005). Furthermore, OFF time after classic programming decreased from 5 ± 3 h at baseline to 3 ± 2 h, and decreased further to 2 ± 2 h after reprogramming. No stimulation‐induced adverse effects were reported after parameter adjustments.Conclusions7 T MRI connectivity‐guided (re)programming of STN‐DBS can provide substantial improvement in motor outcome in PD patients and reduce OFF time, without occurrence of side‐effects. Furthermore, response rate to DBS increases, enhancing overall DBS effectiveness in PD.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an effective treatment for Parkinson's disease (PD). 1 Overall, after the application of DBS, improvement is seen in the motor symptoms of PD including bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity. 2 Furthermore, medication can often be reduced, resulting in a reduction of dyskinesias, and patients experience less fluctuations between the OFF‐ and ON‐state. 3 On average, patients improve 50% on the Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale motor (MDS‐UPDRS III) score in the OFF‐state, measured at 6 months to 2 years after DBS of the subthalamic nucleus (STN). 4 , 5 , 6 However, up to one third of patients treated with STN‐DBS have an insufficient therapy response, and adjusting stimulation parameter settings to increase the therapeutic effect can lead to the occurrence of unwanted side‐effects. 7

In our clinic, follow‐up measurements six to eight months after DBS surgery show variable improvement scores, ranging from patients improving more than 70%, to patients with less than 30% improvement on their MDS‐UPDRS III OFF score. In general, the same protocol is followed for all patients regarding the selection criteria, pre‐operative planning, surgery and postoperative programming of the DBS system. While most individual patients improve well, optimizing outcome for the poor responders remains a challenge. A recent study from our clinic demonstrated that the use of 7‐Tesla magnetic resonance imaging (7 T MRI) with connectivity analysis for the planning of DBS surgery, led to a significant increase in the average improvement on the MDS‐UDPRS III from 50% to 56%. 8 This was largely due to a reduction of the number of non‐responders and an increased proportion of optimal responders to the therapy. In connectivity analysis using probabilistic tractography with diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) on 7 T, a region of the STN that is linked to the motor cortex can be visualized. In all PD patients that undergo DBS in our clinic a preoperative 7 T MRI is obtained, and the small STN motor region resulting from the connectivity analysis is used for surgical planning, which led to the described improvement in motor outcome of DBS after the normal process of iterative programming of stimulation parameters with a monopolar review by the neurologists or specialized nurses. However, we had not yet studied how the anatomical information from the connectivity analysis can be used directly for DBS (re)programming.

When examining the stimulation parameters of the patients that were targeted using 7 T MRI connectivity analysis but programmed by standard monopolar review postoperatively, some patients were programmed in such a way that the connectivity‐derived STN motor spot was poorly, or not at all stimulated. These patients were offered “connectivity‐guided reprogramming” to improve their clinical outcome from DBS and test whether our connectivity‐based method can facilitate in DBS programming in addition to DBS targeting. We hypothesized that by changing the stimulation parameters of the DBS (ie, active electrode contact level, voltage and/or pulse width) in such a way that this STN motor spot would be stimulated, more improvement of motor symptoms as measured by the MDS‐UPDRS III scale could be achieved, without occurrence of adverse effects while reprogramming.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

All consecutive patients who underwent bilateral STN‐DBS at Amsterdam UMC with the use of 7 T connectivity analysis for target determination since the introduction of this technique in April 2022 until November 2024, were eligible for this study. They received standardized baseline (preoperative) clinical assessment, were programmed by means of standardized monopolar review 2–4 weeks after surgery, regular outpatient clinic follow‐up visits for further programming, and subsequently received a follow‐up clinical assessment approximately 6–8 months after surgery, which is part of our standard clinical care. Determination of the STN motor subdivision by connectivity analysis using 7 T probabilistic tractography has been described in a previous study. 8 For all these patients the location of the leads was visualized in combination with the active parameter settings (ie, active electrode contact level, voltage and pulse width) at the time of follow‐up, illustrating the volume of tissue activated (VTA). This was done using the Brainlab navigation system (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany). Electrodes were localized in Brainlab Elements Lead Localization. Elements Guide™ XT (version 3.0) was used to visualize the VTA per patient individually in reference to their patient‐specific 7 T STN motor subdivision.

Based on VTA location in combination with the 7 T STN motor subdivision, patients were categorized into one of three groups: (1) complete bilateral coverage of the motor subdivision by the VTA, (2) partial coverage of the motor subdivision, either uni‐ or bilaterally, by the VTA, (3) no overlap between the VTA and the motor subdivision with current parameter settings. Patients were selected for this study if they were categorized into group 3, and thus programmed by standard monopolar review in such a way that the VTA did not stimulate the STN motor subdivision according to the connectivity analysis. The VTA was adjusted by changing the active contact level, and if desired the amount of voltage applied. Patients that participated in other studies for which parameter settings could not be adjusted were excluded from this analysis. For the selected group of patients, in Brainlab the VTA was adjusted in such a way that the motor subdivision was fully stimulated with as little overflow into adjacent structures as possible (Fig. 1).

Defining the Motor Subdivision

Defining the STN motor subdivision has been described in detail previously. 8 In short, 7 T images were processed in FSL v6.0 (FMRIB, Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain, Software Library, Oxford, UK). The two STN's and three major cortical areas, that is primary and supplementary motor cortex (motor region, Brodmann Area (BA) 4), prefrontal cortex (associative region, BA 9, 10 and 46) and frontobasal cortex (limbic region, BA 11 and 47) were manually masked per individual 7 T scan. Within the software, connectivity regions were visualized in the STN, based on connectivity to each of the three cortical regions. This created three subdivisions in the STN, one of which was the area within the nucleus predominantly connected to the motor cortex, which was always a small segment in the dorsolateral part of the STN (Fig. 1). The coordinates of the center of each individual STN motor subdivision were collected for each hemisphere, to study the spread between the anatomical location of each patient‐specific STN motor subdivision in comparison to a previously defined hotspot for STN‐DBS targeting in PD. 9

Clinical Effects

All clinical outcome scores were collected from our electronic online DBS database (Castor EDC, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Percentage improvement was calculated using the postoperative OFF medication‐ON stimulation condition compared to preoperative OFF medication condition. Last medication intake was at least 12 h prior to the clinical assessments. For patients that were categorized into group 3, new programming settings were suggested and, in consultation with the patients, implemented (Fig. 1). The nurse practitioners followed the patients in the following weeks to months and conducted a new MDS‐UPDRS III (in at least the OFF medication‐ON stimulation condition) after patients were stable with their new programming settings (usually 3–6 months later). The MDS‐UPDRS IV (motor complications), specifically hours OFF per day (MDS‐UPDRS IV sub‐item 3) was collected as well as a notable reduction in hours OFF per day was previously reported. 8 If patients were unwilling to undergo a new follow‐up, we tried to conduct rating scales via phone if possible (the MDS‐UPDRS IV). If patients were unwilling or unable to undergo assessment via phone in that manner as well, clinical status and improvement was collected based on input from nurse practitioners and notes from the electronic patient file. For all patients, patient experience and occurrence of side‐effects were also collected from the electronic patient file.

Main Study Endpoints

The main outcome of this study was the change in motor symptoms measured by the MDS‐UPDRS III from the baseline assessment to the first and the second follow‐up moment. Secondary outcomes included changes in MDS‐UPDRS IV sub‐item 3 (hours OFF per day), subjective patient experience of clinical improvement and appearance or change in adverse‐effects after parameter adjustments.

Statistical Analysis

Difference in percentages and absolute scores were compared using a repeated measures ANOVA. If available, improvement scores were compared between the first and second follow‐up for other rating scales as well, using a repeated measures ANOVA. Difference in voltage applied was tested using a paired samples t‐test. IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 was used for statistical analysis. A p‐value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical Compliance Statement

Data was collected as part of quality registration, patients were informed by means of a letter and had the possibility to opt‐out. Opt‐out informed consent protocol was used for use or collection of participant data for research purposes. This consent procedure was reviewed and approved by the ethical review board (ERB) of the Amsterdam University Medical Centers location AMC by means of a data protection impact assessment (DPIA), 26‐05‐2021. Results

All patients that were operated and for whom a baseline and follow‐up measurement was conducted between April 2022 and November 2024 were screened for potential participation in this study. See Figure 2 for a flowchart on patient selection for this study. A total of 136 patients had a preoperative 7 T T2 and DTI imaging, and complete pre‐ and postoperative UPDRS‐III scores.

One patient did not want to undergo an extra‐follow up and for one patient only the UPDRS‐IV was collected postoperatively. One patient was too fragile to undergo an extra follow‐up and had developed Parkinson‐related dementia, making it too difficult to conduct rating scales via phone. Clinical status and improvement was collected from the nurse practitioners and notes in the electronic patient file. Thus, for nine patients full follow‐up data was collected after reprogramming the stimulators. In these patients, the active contact level was changed, and amount of current applied was significantly lowered, from 2.0 ± 0.6 mA during the first follow‐up measurement to 1.7 ± 0.5 mA during the second follow‐up measurement (p = 0.034). The mean total MDS‐UPDRS III motor score decreased from 48 ± 14 at baseline to 40 ± 13 at the first follow‐up after standard programming (17 ± 27% improvement), and decreased further significantly to 28 ± 8 (42 ± 26% improvement) at the second follow‐up measurement after reprogramming based on VTA‐matching to the STN motor spot (F(1.571, 12.570) = 11.449, p < 0.001, Figure 4A). Post hoc analysis with a Bonferroni adjustment revealed that motor score did not improve significantly from the pre‐operative to the first follow‐up measurement (7.8 points improvement on UPDRS‐III (95% CI, −4.3 to 20.1), p = 0.262), but improved significantly from the first follow‐up measurement to the second follow‐up measurement (12.6 UPDRS‐III points improvement (95% CI, 2.4 to 22.8), p = 0.018) and from the pre‐operative to the second follow‐up measurement (20.4 UPDRS‐III points improvement (95% CI, 4.5 to 36.4), (p = 0.014). When looking at MDS‐UPDRS III hemibody scores, these also significantly improved further for both the left and right hemibody after reprogramming, from 19 ± 28% improvement to 46% ± 22% improvement (absolute scores: baseline: 17 ± 6, after classic programming 14 ± 6, after reprogramming 9 ± 4) (F(1.933, 15.464) = 16.876, p < 0.001) for the left bodyside. Post hoc analysis with a Bonferroni adjustment revealed that hemibody motor score did not improve significantly from the pre‐operative to the first follow‐up measurement (3.3 (95% CI, −1.0 to 7.6, p = 0.142), but improved significantly from the first follow‐up measurement to the second follow‐up measurement (4.4 (95% CI, 0.8 to 8.2), p = 0.019) and from the pre‐operative to the second follow‐up measurement (7.8 (95% CI, 3.6 to 12.0), p = 0.001). And from 24 ± 31% improvement to 53 ± 25% improvement (absolute scores: baseline: 16 ± 6, after classic programming 12 ± 5, after reprogramming 8 ± 4) (F(1.327, 10.618) = 12.421, p = 0.003) for the right bodyside. Post hoc analysis with a Bonferroni adjustment revealed that motor score did not improve significantly from the pre‐operative to the first follow‐up measurement (3.9 (95% CI, −0.1 to 7.9), p = 0.055), but improved significantly from the first follow‐up measurement to the second follow‐up measurement (4.6 (95% CI, 0.3 to 8.8), p = 0.036) and from the pre‐operative to the second follow‐up measurement (8.4 (95% CI, 1.8 to 7.9), p = 0.036).

Off‐time was reduced by 35 ± 44% (range + 2 to −7 hrs, median −1.3 hr) in the first follow‐up after classic programming compared to baseline, and this reduction was larger (73 ± 32%, range −1 to −8 h, median 3 h) in the second follow‐up after reprograming (F(1.016, 5.082) = 2.202, p = 0.197) (Fig. 4B).

For all patients, clinical status and changes were also collected from the nurse practitioners and notes in the electronic patient file. In terms of main PD symptoms, eight patients reported a decrease or complete disappearance of tremor symptoms, and four patients reported less bradykinesia and a decrease in freezing of gate after reprogramming. Furthermore, five patients reported no more dyskinesias during the second follow‐up measurement and six patients reported OFF periods had reduced to zero or almost zero. Seven patients reported feeling better after parameter adjustments. For one patient walking improved, and for two other patients this was noticed by their relatives. Other changes included better sleeping, more energy for sports, and lower dose of Levodopa medication. Levodopa equivalent dose (LED) scores were further reduced in seven patients during the second follow‐up measurement, whose dyskinesias had diminished completely in one (average of 31 ± 26% reduction). 10 No side‐effects were reported as a result of parameter adjustments and for none of the patients the adjustments seemed to affect (or worsen already existing problems in) cognition, balance, apathy, hallucinations or impulse control disorder. Two patients had developed dementia, making it difficult to consult with the patients about any changes since parameter settings were adapted. Relatives noticed less stiffness and more alertness in one patient, and a reduction in tremor and improvement in speech in the other patient. Despite tremor reduction in two patients, they reported that they still experienced tremor from inside, which was not seen by the nurse practitioners or relatives.

Discussion

In this study, the parameter settings of PD patients with STN‐DBS were reprogrammed, in a group of patients in whom traditional programming after a monopolar review had delivered a setting in which the VTA did not overlap with the motor subdivision for DBS in the STN bilaterally, based on patient‐specific probabilistic tractography. This study investigated whether changing the parameter settings of patients that were not correctly programmed based on this method could benefit from these adjustments, and hence if this method can facilitate in DBS programming in addition to DBS targeting. In total 12 patients were included and reprogrammed in this study. Patients showed significantly more motor improvement after adjusting the parameter settings, and also significant more reduction in hours OFF per day. The reduction of main Parkinson's symptoms, including tremor and bradykinesia symptoms, is also reflected by the significant improvement in motor function when testing left and right hemibody separately on the MDS‐UPDRS III rating scale. For all hemibodies, the minimal clinically important difference was reached after parameter adjustments during the second follow‐up measurement. 11 Furthermore, patients reported almost entirely or complete disappearance of dyskinesias and a reduction of the main Parkinson's motor symptoms. LED scores reduced further in seven patients, and increased during the second follow‐up measurement in one. This patient switched from a Duodopa pump during the first follow‐up measurement to only Levodopa pills during the second follow‐up measurement. No new and unwanted side‐effects were reported after the parameter adjustments. For none of the patients the adjustments seemed to worsen already existing problems in cognition, balance, apathy hallucinations or impulse control disorder. The absence of new psychiatric or cognitive side‐effects may support the hypothesis that restricting stimulation to the motor subdivision of the STN avoids spreading of current into limbic areas, which are often implicated in such complications. Two patients had developed dementia and mentioned worsening of their cognition affected their apathy‐like feelings, but the deterioration in apathy seemed more likely caused by overall worsening of activities of daily living due to PD and dementia, rather than as a consequence of the DBS.

Previous research has indicated alternative ways for monopolar review to program the DBS system, which might be as feasible and more efficient, including imaging‐ and local field potential‐guided programming. 12 In light of imaging, studies suggest that it takes significantly less time to program a DBS system, providing similar symptom control, especially compared to monopolar review. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Previous research has indicated that only using imaging might not suffice in optimizing DBS programming, but that beneficial long‐term outcome is highly dependent upon the method chosen to calculate and visualize the patient‐specific anatomy. 16 We investigated the spread of the anatomical location of the STN motor subdivision for each patient in this study. We found more than 2 mm spread in lateral‐medial, anterior–posterior, and superior–inferior direction. This spread highlights the variation in anatomical location of the STN motor subdivision between patients, and supports the added value of DBS programming using patient‐specific tractography. This study illustrates a patient‐specific method that can assist in the effectiveness of image‐based programming. Previously published methods to determine individual stimulation location are mostly based on aggregated data rather than individual analysis of the area within the STN with most condensed connections to the cortical motor areas. Our method is based on higher‐resolution MRI scans, uses a source‐available model, and provides a method to calculate and visualize patient‐specific tractography. Furthermore, DBS systems are increasingly becoming more advanced, including more and steering electrodes per lead. This complicates the process of monopolar review, as it increases programming time and parameter options. Programming DBS parameters by means of imaging in combination with connectivity analysis provides a quick method for determining the most ideal contact level or (combination of) steering electrodes in comparison to the standardized monopolar review.

No patients with <30% improvement in motor outcome were seen in the group with complete coverage of the hypothesized motor area by the VTA. This finding supports the idea that coverage of this area by the VTA is linked to a good motor response to DBS and that response rates to DBS can be increased when patients are programmed by means of connectivity analysis. We did not test reprogramming patients in this study who had partial overlap between their VTA and STN motor area. Reprogramming this group might also result in an improvement in motor outcome and even improve the number of optimal responders with only small parameter adjustments necessary. Furthermore, amount of current applied decreased significantly in the group of reprogrammed patients. This suggests that more motor improvement was reached with lower amount of current applied, lowering the chances of occurrence of unwanted side‐effects.

There are several limitations to this study. One limitation is the small number of patients included. As this study was a first pilot study in reprogramming patients based on patient‐specific connectivity analysis, and due to a large number of patients that are, based on this imaging‐method, not stimulated with ideal settings, we decided to first focus on patients for whom the VTA fell completely out of the hypothesized target area. Although the sample size of this study is small, the aim of the study was to underwrite the potential benefit of programming DBS based on patient‐specific connectivity analysis and investigate its effect. A follow‐up study could replicate the study with larger sample sizes, with inclusion of all patients with motor areas not completely covered by the VTAs. This should be done in a randomized and patient‐ and observer‐blinded fashion, comparing reprogrammed and non‐reprogrammed periods within patients. For two electrodes, the VTA was completely outside of the STN before reprogramming. This could have affected the outcome scores, as the change in outcome scores might be simply due to changing from programming outside to inside the STN. A few patients suffered from dementia, making it difficult to consult with the patients about any observed changes in (motor) outcome, or possible side‐effects induced by the adapted parameter settings. However, these patients were carefully monitored by their relatives and health‐care professionals on a daily basis and their observations were taken into account in this study. Furthermore, a limitation of this study is the use of 7 T MRI. The limited availability of 7 T MRI connectivity analysis for DBS worldwide makes it difficult at present to implement this method in most DBS centers. In order to perform our connectivity analysis, a voxel‐size of at least a 7 T MRI is required, making it not (yet) possible to perform this connectivity analysis on lower‐resolution MRI scans. Additionally, calculating 7 T MRI connectivity per patient individually is a time‐consuming process. This study is a first insight into the potential added value of 7 T MRI connectivity analysis for DBS programming and the next step would be to translate this advanced technique back to a model that can be implemented for DBS surgery worldwide, including the use of lower field‐strength MRI. The individual derived connectivity‐analysis has not yet been compared to easily available, normative‐sample‐based connectivity atlases. In previous work we showed that patient‐specific connectivity‐analysis led to variations between patients in STN motor‐area delineation relative to anatomical landmarks, and that using this method improved the outcome in our own sample compared to our historical cohort in which target‐determination was based on aggregated data. 8 The atlas‐based segmentations have great value for our understanding of the effects of DBS but were not designed for individual target determination and can lead to discrepancies with individual derived connectivity analysis. 17 However, it is unlikely that a head‐on comparison will be performed.

In conclusion, 7 T‐connectivity‐based programming of STN‐DBS seems to be a technique that can provide significant improvement in the motor outcome in PD patients. In addition to higher average symptomatic improvement, the responder rate to DBS can also increase and the patients experience less time spent in OFF moments, bringing the effect of DBS to a higher level.
