Association of serum total bilirubin with recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with in-stent restenosis: a stratified analysis by inflammatory status
Authors: Miao Yu, Yu Jiang, Yu Sun, Han Zhang, Tian-Jie Wang, Tao Tian, Jian-Song Yuan, Hai-Yan Qian, Wei-Xian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn-stent restenosis (ISR) remains a major clinical challenge despite advances in stent technology and pharmacotherapy. While inflammation plays a pivotal role in ISR progression, the prognostic value of stable biomarkers reflecting chronic inflammation has not been fully explored. This study examined the association of serum total bilirubin (TBIL), alone and in combination with high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), with long-term cardiovascular outcomes in ISR patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsA total of 2055 ISR patients who underwent repeat PCI at Fuwai Hospital from January 2017 to December 2018 were consecutively enrolled. Patients were stratified based on TBIL and hsCRP levels. The primary endpoint was the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), including all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, repeat revascularization and stent thrombosis. Multivariable Cox regression and subgroup analyses were conducted.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 36 months, 457 patients suffered from MACEs. Lower TBIL levels were independently associated with a higher risk of long-term MACEs, regardless of high hsCRP (hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/L, hazard ratio [HR] = 1.865, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.423–2.443, P < 0.001) or low hsCRP (hsCRP < 2 mg/L HR = 1.338, 95% CI: 1.039–1.721, P = 0.024). Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients with both low TBIL and elevated hsCRP had the highest incidence of MACEs (P < 0.001). The combination of TBIL and hsCRP provided additional risk stratification information, especially in patients with diabetes mellitus (P for interaction = 0.001).ConclusionSerum TBIL is a novel, independent predictor of adverse outcomes in ISR patients and provides complementary value to hsCRP for risk stratification. Its clinical utility may be particularly valuable in diabetic populations, suggesting that TBIL may help identify higher-risk subgroups within ISR populations.Clinical trial registrationThis study is not a registered clinical trial. Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with stent implantation has been extensively utilized in the treatment of symptomatic myocardial ischemia and acute coronary syndrome (ACS). However, even with contemporary drug-eluting stents (DES) platform, in-stent restenosis (ISR) still affects 5–10% of patients’ post-revascularization [1–3]. Furthermore, 10%-20% of patients who experience a first ISR event develop recurrent ISR, posing a significant clinical challenge in managing these cases [4]. While the individual patient, angiographic and procedural factors play roles, the importance of inflammation is well recognized [5]. New insights into anti-inflammatory treatments have suggested that agents like colchicine and prednisone can significantly reduce the incidence of ISR and decrease MACEs occurrence after stent implantation [6–8].

As a vital biomarker for reflecting inflammatory status, high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) serves as an effective predictor of cardiovascular events in both primary and secondary prevention [9]. Research by Niccoli et al. [5] also highlights that elevated hsCRP levels are associated with the development and progression of ISR, with residual inflammatory risk defined by hsCRP levels ≥ 2 mg/L. However, hsCRP primarily reflects acute inflammation and is susceptible to short-term influences such as infection, surgery, intense exercise, and diet. For ISR patients, research increasingly emphasizes the need to focus on chronic inflammation. Therefore, the introduction of new and more stable long-term biomarkers is preferred for use in combination to predict long-term outcomes in ISR patients.

The serum TBIL, traditionally viewed as a toxic bile substance associated with liver dysfunction and skin yellowing in jaundice, has more recently been recognized as a biomarker closely associated with metabolic and cardiovascular diseases when present at moderately elevated levels [10, 11]. Functionally, TBIL acts as a lipophilic antioxidant that activates the nuclear receptor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα), thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects and improving vascular function, which protects against hypertension and atherosclerosis [12, 13]. Recent cohort studies also demonstrate that higher TBIL levels seem to predispose apparently protection against coronary artery disease (CAD), whereas lower levels indicate an increased risk of cardiovascular events [14, 15]. The inverse and independent association between TBIL levels and CAD risks may make it an alternative prognostic biomarker in patients with ISR undergoing PCI. Compared with hsCRP, TBIL as a direct product of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), has been shown in multiple studies to be closely associated with chronic inflammation, with less fluctuation and greater long-term stability [16]. However, the prognostic relevance of TBIL in ISR, particularly regarding long-term risks of recurrent revascularization, has not been characterized. Moreover, whether TBIL provides incremental risk information beyond hsCRP across different inflammatory states remains unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between serum TBIL levels and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in ISR patients after PCI, and to examine whether this relationship differs across inflammatory strata.

Methods

Study design

This study was a single-center, observational cohort study. Between January 2017 and December 2018, a total of 2106 consecutive patients who underwent successful PCI for at least one ISR lesion were consecutively enrolled at the Fuwai Hospital. ISR was defined as a diametral restenosis of ≥ 50% within the stent or its adjacent 5 mm segments [17, 18]. Patients who were under 18 years old, had severe hepatic or kidney dysfunction, had inadequate data and lost to follow-up were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 2,055 patients who underwent PCI revascularization driven by at least one ISR lesion were included in the analysis.

This study complied with The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai hospital.

Laboratory tests and population group

Basic demographic data from all patients were collected from medical records. All peripheral venous blood samples measured at admission after at least 12-h fasting and prior to intervention for biochemical measurements. The concentrations of serum TBIL levels were measured by an automatic biochemistry analyzer. The hsCRP levels were determined using immunoturbidimetry at the core laboratory of Fuwai Hospital.

The study patients were further divided into four groups according to low or high TBIL levels and hsCRP levels. The stratification of TBIL was based on the optimal cut-off value of serum TBIL was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with Youden’s J statistic, yielding a threshold of 12.39µmol/L (Supplementary Fig. 1). To verify the appropriateness of this cut-off, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was performed, which showed no significant non-linear relationship between TBIL and MACE (p for non-linearity = 0.1289), indicating an approximately linear trend (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The inflection point of the RCS curve was 12.48 µmol/L, very close to the ROC-derived threshold, supporting its validity (Supplementary Table 1). Elevated hsCRP was defined as ≥ 2 mg/L. Based on these criteria, patients were stratified into four groups: TBIL-H/hsCRP-L (TBIL ≥ 12.39 µmol/L and hsCRP < 2 mg/L, the reference group); TBIL-L/hsCRP-L (TBIL < 12.39 µmol/L and hsCRP < 2 mg/L);. TBIL-H/hsCRP-H (TBIL ≥ 12.39 µmol/L and hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/L); TBIL-L/hsCRP-H (TBIL < 12.39 µmol/L and hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/L).

Study endpoints and follow-up

Study patients were routinely followed-up via through outpatient clinic visits or structured telephone contact until January 31, 2021, with high follow-up completeness. Telephone-reported events were verified using medical record review or physician confirmation.

The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), including all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, repeat revascularization and stent thrombosis. All endpoints were defined according to standardized criteria, adjudicated by an independent Clinical Events Committee (CEC) blinded to TBIL and hsCRP levels. The CEC members were not involved in data collection or analysis, and all events were reviewed based on predefined standardized definitions consistent with those used in contemporary PCI and ISR studies.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using the R programming language (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables with normal distribution were summarized as means ± standard deviations (SDs), whereas variables with skewed distribution were described as median and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Student’s t-test, One-way ANOVA, or Kruskal–Wallis H test (for skewed distribution) was used to compare continuous variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage and were compared using chi-squared test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Correlation analysis between TBIL and other laboratory index was conducted by Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

The cumulative incidence of outcomes in different groups was depicted by Kaplan-Meier Survival Curve, compared by log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional regression models were performed to assess the association between potential risk factors and outcomes, by calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Covariates included in the multivariable model were determined according to the predefined directed acyclic graph (DAG)-based adjustment set (Supplementary Fig. 3). Accordingly, the final adjustment set included age, sex, peripheral artery disease, hypertension, diabetes, low-density lipoprotein, high-density lipoprotein, lipoprotein(a), albumin, no. of ISR and repeated ISR. These variables were also consistent with findings from the univariable analyses and established clinical knowledge.(Supplementary Table 2). Schoenfeld residual tests and Schoenfeld residual plots were used to evaluate the proportional hazards assumption.

Sensitivity analyses

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE, m = 10), and results from imputed datasets were compared with complete-case analyses for sensitivity. To account for the competing risk of death, a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was applied as an additional sensitivity analysis. We further conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative TBIL specifications (modeling TBIL as a continuous variable, quartiles, and per-SD change). Subgroups analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of bilirubin and hsCRP in different clinical subgroups (age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, diagnosis) for outcomes. P for interaction < 0.05 indicates the presence of subgroup effects.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and results were considered statistically significant at an alpha level of 0.05.

Data source

This retrospective study analyzed de-identified electronic medical records from 2017 to 2018. Data were accessed on June 2024 through the Fuwai Hospital information system. Patient identifiers were removed prior to analysis, and researchers could not trace individual participants at any stage of the study.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Fuwai Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No.: 2023–2076).

Data availability

The data underlying this study cannot be shared publicly due to patient privacy and confidentiality agreements. Researchers interested in accessing the data may submit a request to the Fuwai Hospital Ethics Committee (contact via fawu@fuwai.com) and must comply with the criteria for confidential data access as stipulated by the committee.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 2106 consecutively recruited patients, we excluded 15 patients with severe hepatic or kidney dysfunction and 3 patients with hematologic disease. Besides, 21 patients with inadequate data and 12 patients lost to follow-up were also excluded. Finally, a total of 2055 patients who successfully underwent PCI for at least one ISR were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

Baseline characteristics of patients with and without MACEs during the follow-up period were presented in Table 1. Patients who experienced any component of MACEs were more likely to be women and had lower levels of HDL, TBIL, CBIL, CBIL and albumin (Alb), along with higher levels of Lipoprotein a (Lpa) and hsCRP compared to those in the non-MACEs group (all P < 0.05). Additionally, patients with an unfavorable prognosis were more inclined to receive aspirin plus Ticagrelor as their dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) regimen (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, patients who experienced MACEs events had a higher number of ISR lesions and a higher proportion of previous ISR occurrences than those who did not (all P < 0.05). The correlation analysis indicated a statistically significant correlation between TBIL and hsCRP, but this negative correlation is very weak (r = -0.083, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3).

Primary outcome

During a median follow-up of 36 months, a total of 457 (22.3%) patients experienced MACEs. Individual components of the primary outcome, including 60 (2.7%) all-cause mortality, 63 (3.1%) myocardial infarction, 20 (1.0%) stroke, 384 (18.7%) repeat revascularization and 16 (0.8%) stent thrombosis, were also documented (Table 3).

The prevalence of MACEs in four groups were 16.4% in TBIL-H/hsCRP-L, 21.7% in TBIL-L/hsCRP-L, 25.4% in TBIL-H/hsCRP-H, and 31.4% in TBIL-L/hsCRP-H respectively. The Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with TBIL-H/hsCRP-L had the lowest cumulative risk of MACEs, whereas those with TBIL-L/hsCRP-H had the highest (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Consistent patterns were also observed for the individual components of MACEs, including all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, and repeat revascularization (all P < 0.05; Fig. 2). At fixed time points, the cumulative incidence of MACEs was highest in the TBIL-L/hsCRP-H group (25.1% at 1 year and 31.5% at 3 years), intermediate in TBIL-H/hsCRP-H and TBIL-L/hsCRP-L, and lowest in TBIL-H/hsCRP-L (Supplementary Table 4).

Kaplan‑Meier curves for MACEs, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, and repeat revascularization. Numbers at risk and log-rank P values are shown below the plot; absolute event rates at 1 and 3 years are summarized in Supplementary Table 5

Univariable Cox regression analysis showed that patients in TBIL-L/hsCRP-L, TBIL-H/hsCRP-H, and TBIL-L/hsCRP-H groups respectively encountered a 45.6%, 59.2%, and 120.8% increase in MACEs risk, compared to the reference group. This association remained consistent in the multivariable-adjusted model, with the adjusted HRs for MACEs were 1.338 (95% CI 1.039–1.721 P = 0.024), 1.517 (95% CI 1.143–2.013, P = 0.004), and 1.865 (95% CI 1.423–2.443, P < 0.001). The proportional hazards assumption was verified and found not to be violated (Supplementary Tables 5 and Supplementary Fig. 4). A nomogram for estimating the 3-year MACE-free probability in ISR patients is presented in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Besides, patients in the TBIL-L/hsCRP-H group were at the highest risk of all cause death (HR = 2.427, 95% CI 1.151–5.116, P = 0.02), MI (HR = 3.131, 95% CI 1.371-7,148, P = 0.007), repeat revascularization (HR = 1535 95% CI 1.142–2.062, P = 0.005), TLR (HR = 1.706, 95% CI 1.193–2.438, P = 0.003), TVR (HR = 1.639, 95% CI 1.160–2.318, P = 0.005) (Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses

Results from multiple imputation were highly consistent with the complete-case analyses, indicating that missing data did not materially affect the observed associations (Supplementary Table 6).

When accounting for the competing risk of death, the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was performed. The results remained consistent with the primary Cox model, showing that lower TBIL and higher hsCRP levels were associated with a higher risk of MACEs (Supplementary Table 7).

Analyses using alternative specifications of TBIL (as a continuous variable, quartiles, and per-SD change) yielded comparable results to the primary analysis, further supporting the stability of the association between TBIL and MACE (Supplementary Table 8).

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine whether the association between TBIL/hsCRP groups and MACE was consistent across clinically relevant categories, including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and clinical presentation (ACS or CCS). As shown in Fig. 3, the association between the four TBIL/hsCRP groups and MACE was generally consistent across subgroups, with the TBIL-L/hsCRP-H group showing the highest risk of MACEs in all strata. A significant interaction was observed with diabetes status (P for interaction = 0.001), indicating that the combined effect of TBIL and hsCRP provided additional prognostic information among diabetic patients (β = 0.93, HR = 2.53, 95% CI 1.47–4.36, p < 0.001; see Supplementary Table 9). No significant interaction was observed with respect to age, sex, hypertension, or clinical presentation.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a large-scale ISR cohort to evaluate the association of serum TBIL, individually and in combination with hsCRP, with long-term MACEs post ISR-PCI.

Results demonstrated that lower baseline levels of TBIL were independently associated with a higher incidence of long-term MACEs in patients PCI for ISR. This association remained robust across different levels of systemic inflammation, as stratified by hsCRP, indicating that TBIL offers additional risk-stratification information beyond traditional inflammatory markers. Patients with both low TBIL and elevated hsCRP showed the highest MACEs incidence, whereas those with high TBIL and low hsCRP exhibited the most favorable outcomes. Multivariable Cox regression analysis further confirmed that the TBIL/hsCRP classification was independently associated with long-term adverse outcomes including all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, repeat revascularization, and stent thrombosis. Sensitivity analyses, including multiple imputation, competing risk models, and alternative TBIL specifications, yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the observed associations. Subgroup analyses revealed that the association appeared stronger among patients with diabetes mellitus (P for interaction = 0.001), whereas no significant interactions were observed for age, sex, hypertension, or clinical presentation.

ISR remains a clinically challenging complication of PCI, especially in the era of widespread stent deployment and an aging cardiovascular population. Despite advances in stent technology and pharmacotherapy, a substantial proportion of patients with ISR still experienced recurrent restenosis or adverse events [19]. Unfortunately, traditional strategies, such as re-stenting, intensified lipid-lowering, or glycemic control often fall short in this high-risk population. Anti-inflammatory therapies like colchicine and corticosteroids have shown promise, but their application is not yet routine due to the lack of robust stratification tools to identify certain high-risk individuals, leaving clinicians uncertain whether to opt for an anti-inflammatory agent or other new approaches for preemptive intervention post-TLR. As such, there is an unmet clinical need for stable, accessible biomarkers that can guide individualized risk assessment and inform therapeutic decisions in ISR management.

Systemic inflammation is widely recognized as a fundamental contributor to neointimal hyperplasia and fibroblast proliferation, representing an exaggerated homeostatic response to arterial wall injury [20–23]. C-reactive protein (CRP)/hsCRP, has long been used as a surrogate marker of inflammatory risk [24], supported by both mechanistic and interventional evidence such as the CANTOS trial. Studies in both bare-metal and drug-eluting stent settings have consistently reported that higher CRP levels are associated with an increased likelihood of restenosis after PCI [25, 26]. However, hsCRP is highly dynamic and sensitive to short-term physiological fluctuations, such as infection, procedural stress, or lifestyle factors, potentially limiting the reliability of a single peri-procedural measurement as a stable reflection of chronic inflammatory status.

In contrast, TBIL, an effective endogenous antioxidant, has been considered as a scavenger of reactive oxygen species, and mitigates the amplification of oxidized LDL at the pathophysiological level [12, 27]. Beyond its antioxidant properties, TBIL also exerted potential anti-inflammatory effects, as evidenced by inverse correlations with inflammatory markers such as CRP, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and red cell distribution width (RDW) in stable CAD patients [28–31]. Higher bilirubin concentrations have also been shown to inhibit platelet aggregation, improve vessel wall elasticity, and exhibit anticomplement activity [32]. Accumulating evidence consistently showed that lower TBIL levels are associated with an increased prevalence of cardiovascular disease. The TBIL levels were inversely correlated with the severity, vulnerability, and noncalcified morphology of atherosclerotic plaques [33–35]. What’s more, patients with chronic total occlusion (CTO) can benefit from higher TBIL level, as it promoted coronary collateral growth [36]. A U.S. study assessed a 50% decrease in TBIL was associated with a 47% increase in the odds of being classified in a more severe CAD category [37]. And an increase of 1 micromol/L in TBIL concentration was associated with a 14% decrease in the odds of having a coronary artery calcification (CAC) score ≥ 400 [38].

With regard to ISR, both experimental and clinical research suggest an inverse association between bilirubin levels and restenosis risk. Animal studies have reported that bilirubin exposure may limit injury-induced vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation [39]. A Japanese study conducted on 1076 patients who underwent stent implantation revealed a significant negative correlation between TBIL levels and ISR (95% CI, 0.39–0.89, p = 0.015) [40]. Besides, research on patients with psoriasis and coronary artery disease also found that decreased TBIL levels may slightly increase the prevalence of ISR [41]. With regard to ISR, both experimental and clinical research suggest an inverse association between bilirubin levels and restenosis risk. Animal studies have reported that bilirubin exposure may limit injury-induced vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation. Given the ISR recurrence occurs in approximately 20% of cases, accurately predicting and reducing the likelihood of adverse events following revascularization in these ISR patients is of great importance [42].

Here, we firstly conducted a statistical analysis of baseline data from 2,055 ISR patients identifying key clinical differences associated with long-term prognosis. Female patients were at higher risk for MACEs post-revascularization (p = 0.002), consistent with previous evidence of sex-based prognostic disparities following PCI (HR: 1.14; P = 0.04) and with prior studies on predictors of carotid restenosis ([43–44]). In addition, elevated levels of residual cholesterol, hsCRP, and bilirubin were more frequently observed among patients who developed MACEs. Interestingly, patients receiving ticagrelor had a higher incidence of adverse events, which may reflect a treatment selection bias favoring ticagrelor in more complex or high-risk cases, rather than drug inferiority. Importantly, lower TBIL levels were consistently associated with increased MACEs risk across hsCRP strata, suggesting that TBIL may capture a chronic inflammatory or oxidative-stress related pathway distinct from the acute-phase signal represented by hsCRP. The weak inverse correlation between TBIL and hsCRP and mediation analysis in our data further supported the possibility that these biomarkers reflect complementary components of the inflammatory milieu [45].

The clinical relevance of TBIL was especially notable in diabetic patients. This may be attributed to the amplified oxidative stress and inflammation associated with persistent hyperglycemia, which heightens the relevance of TBIL’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Supporting this, previous studies have shown that diabetic patients typically exhibit lower TBIL levels, and higher bilirubin concentrations are linked to reduced risks of diabetic microvascular complications, including retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy [46]. Additionally, research on patients with Gilbert’s syndrome has indicated a lower incidence of cardiovascular events among diabetic individuals with chronically elevated bilirubin levels [47]. Bilirubin has also been inversely correlated with glycated hemoglobin, C-peptide, and type 2 diabetes incidence, reinforcing its potential role in metabolic and vascular protection [48].

In summary, this study provided novel evidence that serum TBIL is independently associated with long-term cardiovascular outcomes in patients with ISR and may provide complementary risk information alongside hsCRP. The incorporation of TBIL into routine risk stratification protocols may help identify high-risk patients who could benefit from closer surveillance, aggressive risk factor modification, or emerging anti-inflammatory therapies. Given its low cost, widespread availability, and biological relevance, TBIL holds promise as a clinically valuable biomarker to guide personalized care in ISR. However, further prospective and mechanistic studies are warranted.

Limitations

Our study provided preliminary evidence regarding the potential prognostic value of bilirubin independent of residual inflammation in patients with ISR, though it was not without its limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective analysis, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Bilirubin distributions and clinical thresholds can vary across populations, laboratory systems, and ethnic groups; therefore, external validation in multi-center and ethnically diverse cohorts will be needed to confirm the applicability of our results. Second, our measurements of TBIL, hsCRP and all serum results were based on a single laboratory assessment. Given that these biomarkers can be influenced by short-term factors such as acute ACS status, infection, hepatic function, or medication use, thus may not fully capture chronic inflammatory status. Third, part of the endpoint data were obtained by telephone follow-up, which may have introduced recall bias and underreporting of events such as myocardial infarction or stent thrombosis. Finally, although we adjusted for potential confounders in the multivariable regression models, we must acknowledge that some key uncollected confounders were not included in the analysis, such as stent type, ISR morphological complexity, prior PCI timing, medication adherence.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted the independent predictive value of serum TBIL levels for identifying ISR patients at risk for MACEs, regardless of hsCRP levels. Therefore, the combination of serum TBIL and hsCRP could serve as a valuable biomarker for guiding the management and treatment strategies of ISR patients, especially in diabetics. However, the prognostic power of this joint indicator requires further validation through randomized controlled trials.
