Instability and Flame-Pressure Coupling in Downward Propagating Premixed H2/CO/CO2/Air Flames
Authors: Zhanglin Yang, Qiaosheng Zhang, Zhidong Guo, Yu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the instability and flame–pressure coupling characteristics of downward propagating premixed H2/CO/CO2/air flames under varying hydrogen volume fractions (HVF). Particular attention was paid to flame morphology evolution, overpressure development, thermoacoustic characteristics, and reaction sensitivity. The results show that increasing HVF markedly modifies flame dynamics. Smooth and laminar-dominated flame fronts are observed at low HVF (5%–10%), whereas pronounced wrinkling, finger-like structures, and cellular instabilities emerge when HVF ≥ 15%, indicating a transition to instability-controlled propagation. Correspondingly, overpressure responses intensify with hydrogen enrichment, evolving from weak low-frequency oscillations to strong nonlinear pressure fluctuations with distinct acoustic mode locking. The dominant frequency shifts from a low-frequency regime to a tube-governed longitudinal acoustic mode when HVF ≥ 10%, demonstrating enhanced flame–acoustic coupling. Sensitivity analysis reveals that CO oxidation (CO + OH ≤ > CO2 + H) remains the primary heat-release pathway across all conditions, while chain-branching reactions become increasingly dominant at higher HVF. The reaction network gradually shifts toward an OH-centered, strongly coupled kinetic structure, promoting flame acceleration and pressure amplification. A critical transition region is identified around HVF ≈ 10%–15%, beyond which the system evolves into a highly reactive and instability-dominated combustion regime. These findings provide mechanistic insight into hydrogen-enriched syngas combustion and its associated explosion risks in confined configurations.

Introduction

The growing integration of hydrogen into conventional fuel systems has intensified interest in the combustion dynamics of hydrogen-containing syngas mixtures. Fuel-blend combustion has become an important route for decarbonization and flexible fuel utilization in practical energy systems. In particular, blending hydrogen with carbon-based fuels can improve combustion performance while offering a realistic pathway for low-carbon fuel transition. Recent discussion has further highlighted the scientific and engineering importance of blended-fuel combustion, including H2/CO-based systems. Therefore, investigating the instability and flame-pressure coupling characteristics of hydrogen-enriched syngas mixtures is not only of fundamental interest, but also of practical relevance for advanced combustion applications. Syngas, typically composed of H2 and CO with varying fractions of CO2 and inert species, is widely encountered in coal gasification, biomass reforming, and industrial byproduct gas recovery. In practical scenarios such as ducts, vertical pipelines, and confined processing units, accidental ignition of premixed syngas-air mixtures may lead to downward flame propagation accompanied by significant pressure rise. Compared with hydrocarbon flames, hydrogen-enriched mixtures exhibit higher laminar burning velocities, stronger preferential diffusion effects, and enhanced sensitivity to perturbations, which may intensify flame instability and flame-pressure interaction in confined geometries.

Previous studies have shown that premixed flame instability is mainly governed by hydrodynamic and diffusive-thermal effects. − For hydrogen-air and CO-air flames. Hydrogen-air flames are known to exhibit early cellular transition and rapid nonlinear growth due to strong diffusive-thermal effects, , while CO-air flames generally show comparatively weaker intrinsic instability because of slower reaction kinetics and different transport properties. For blended H2/CO systems, previous studies have primarily focused on laminar burning velocity correlations, ignition delay characteristics, and explosion overpressure in closed or opened vessels. − These works have clarified that increasing hydrogen fraction enhances reactivity and pressure rise; however, detailed characterization of instability evolution, wrinkle growth dynamics, and their direct coupling with transient pressure development remains limited, particularly under confined one-dimensional propagation configurations.

The presence of CO2 introduces additional complexity. In practical syngas systems derived from coal gasification, biomass reforming, and industrial byproduct gas utilization, CO2 is commonly present as an inherent dilution component rather than an incidental additive. Therefore, understanding the role of CO2 dilution is important not only for fundamental flame studies, but also for evaluating the combustion safety and dynamic behavior of realistic hydrogen-enriched syngas mixtures. CO2 dilution reduces adiabatic flame temperature and suppresses global burning velocity through thermal and chemical effects, while simultaneously modifying mixture transport coefficients and effective Lewis numbers. , Most existing studies, however, treat dilution effects primarily in terms of global explosion parameters or steady laminar flame properties. Systematic investigations isolating hydrogen fraction under fixed CO2 dilution, with simultaneous measurement of flame structure and pressure evolution, are scarce. Consequently, the interplay between intrinsic instability growth and pressure buildup in multicomponent syngas mixtures remains insufficiently resolved.

In confined geometries, flame propagation is strongly influenced by pressure-wave interaction and boundary condition. While flame acceleration and pressure development have been extensively studied in fully closed vessels and obstructed channels, downward propagation in an open-closed vertical tube represents a distinct physical configuration. − In this geometry, buoyancy acts opposite to flame motion, altering stretch distribution, while pressure waves generated during propagation reflect from the closed end and reimpinge on an already unstable flame surface. The combined effects of hydrodynamic expansion, preferential diffusion, adverse buoyancy, and transient pressure coupling have not been systematically quantified for H2/CO/CO2/air mixtures. The novelty of the present study lies in its focus on downward-propagating premixed H2/CO/CO2/air flames in an open-closed vertical tube, where hydrogen enrichment, CO2 dilution, adverse buoyancy, flame-front instability, and reflected pressure waves interact simultaneously. By combining high-speed visualization, synchronized pressure measurement, frequency-spectrum analysis, and reaction sensitivity analysis under fixed CO2 dilution conditions, this work provides a more integrated understanding of the transition from weak coupling to instability-dominated flame-pressure interaction. Therefore, the present study is of both fundamental importance for understanding multicomponent flame instability and practical significance for the safety assessment of hydrogen-enriched industrial fuel systems.

To address these gaps, the present study investigates instability development and flame-pressure coupling of downward propagating premixed H2/CO/CO2/air flames in a vertical open-closed tube. In the present study, the CO2 volume fraction is fixed at 10% to represent realistic diluted syngas conditions. This choice is motivated by both practical syngas compositions and previous experimental benchmarks. Typical biomass-derived and coal-gasification syngas contain CO2 in the range of 5%–20%, and a dilution level of 10% has been commonly adopted in studies of syngas flame dynamics and explosion behavior. − The hydrogen volume fraction is systematically varied from 5% to 30% in 5% increments to investigate its effect on flame propagation and combustion characteristics. High-speed visualization and synchronized pressure measurements are employed to characterize flame front morphology, cellular growth dynamics, propagation velocity evolution, and transient overpressure behavior. Particular emphasis is placed on identifying instability transition regimes and elucidating how hydrogen fraction modulates the coupling between flame deformation and pressure development under adverse buoyancy conditions. Through this systematic parametric framework, the study aims to clarify the physical mechanisms governing instability amplification and pressure coupling in multicomponent syngas flames. The findings provide insight into explosion risk assessment of hydrogen-enriched industrial gases and supply experimental benchmarks for validating nonlinear instability theory and coupled flame-flow simulations in confined geometries.

Experimental Procedure

The experimental system was designed to investigate the flame dynamic behavior of gas mixtures under controlled conditions, as illustrated in Figure . High-purity gases, including carbon dioxide (CO2), hydrogen (H2), carbon monoxide (CO), and air, were supplied from pressurized cylinders. Each gas line was equipped with a dedicated mass flow controller (MFC, accuracy ± 1% of full scale) to precisely regulate the flow rate and composition of the gas mixture prior to entry into the main test section. The gases were first directed into a static mixer to ensure homogeneous mixing. The test section was fabricated from transparent poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) plates with an inner cross-sectional dimension of 60 mm × 60 mm and a total length of 600 mm, allowing for full optical access to the internal flame dynamics. The open end of the test section was sealed with a thin polyvinyl chloride (PVC) film to contain the gas mixture while permitting controlled rupture upon ignition. The ignition source was located at the top end of the test section, enabling the study of downward flame propagation on flame behavior. To capture transient phenomena such as flame front evolution, a high-speed camera (operating at up to 3000 frames per second) was positioned perpendicular to the test section, with the field of view covering the entire 600 mm length. The flame propagation process was recorded at a spatial resolution of 1080 × 800 pixels and an exposure time of 490 μs. The flame front position was determined from each high-speed image by identifying the outer luminous boundary of the leading flame edge along the tube axis. Because the flame front was often curved and nonhorizontal, the flame-front position used here was defined as the axial location of the leading edge of the flame. The downward direction was defined as positive, and the instantaneous flame front velocity was calculated from the temporal derivative of the extracted flame front position from consecutive images.

Experimental System Diagram.

In the present study, the flame propagation behavior is characterized by the evolution of flame front morphology, flame front position, propagation time, and instantaneous flame front velocity obtained from consecutive high-speed images. These parameters are used together to describe the movement, deformation, and acceleration of the flame during downward propagation. A piezoelectric pressure transducer was mounted flush with the inner wall at the end of the test section to monitor dynamic pressure changes during explosion events. The dynamic pressure signal was measured with a measuring range of −0.1 to 0.25 MPa and a resonant frequency of 200 kHz. Pressure data were recorded using a single pressure sensor connected to a multichannel data acquisition system (DAQ), and the signal was acquired through LabVIEW on the same personal computer (PC). Flame images were captured by the high-speed camera through the camera control software (PCC), also on the same PC.

To ensure reliable synchronization between pressure and flame image data under strongly transient conditions, the data acquisition and ignition procedures were coordinated as follows. The pressure data acquisition system with sampling rate 200 kHz and duration 3 s and the high-speed camera at 3000 fps were triggered simultaneously via their respective software interfaces on the same personal computer. Immediately thereafter, ignition was initiated using a press-type igniter. The ignition event itself provides a natural time reference: a distinct pressure pulse is recorded in the pressure channel, and the spark light is visible in the high-speed image sequence. Using the ignition signal as the common time-zero reference, the two data streams were aligned in postprocessing. The remaining synchronization uncertainty is governed by the camera frame interval, approximately 333 μs at 3000 fps, which is substantially smaller than the characteristic flame propagation time and the dominant acoustic period. This level of accuracy is therefore sufficient to resolve the flame-pressure coupling dynamics discussed in this study.

All data were ultimately displayed and synchronized on the computer for comprehensive evaluation. Each experimental condition was repeated at least three times to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results. The initial conditions for all experiments were atmospheric pressure and room temperature, with an initial pressure of 1 atm and an initial temperature of 298 K. The premixed H2/CO/CO2/air mixtures were prepared at a constant equivalence ratio of φ = 1.0. In all cases, the CO2 volume fraction was fixed at 10%, while the hydrogen volume fraction was varied by adjusting the H2/CO ratio in the fuel component. Before ignition, the premixed gas was allowed to remain in the tube for a sufficient period to ensure compositional uniformity.

Results and Discussion

Flame Structure Evolution

Figure shows high-speed photographic sequences of premixed flames propagating downward in a vertical tube under different HVF conditions. At HVF = 5%, the flame exhibits a relatively regular, axisymmetric “finger-like” structure with a smooth front and minimal curvature variations. During propagation from the ignition end, the flame front remains a single continuous peak, without significant transverse disturbances or cell splitting. The propagation spans a long time scale (∼10 ms to ∼1300 ms), indicating a relatively low overall reaction rate and negligible flame acceleration. In this stage, laminar combustion dominates, and flow stretching driven by thermal expansion is insufficient to trigger significant hydrodynamic or thermo-diffusive instabilities.

Flame propagation images under different HVF.

When HVF increases to 10%, the propagation time decreases markedly (∼550 ms), accompanied by a significant rise in flame speed. The front transitions from an initially smooth shape to one with slight undulations and localized protrusions, indicating the onset of interface disturbances. As the flame advances, fluctuations in the flame skirt intensify and the front curvature increases, suggesting that hydrogen addition enhances laminar flame speed and heat release rate, thereby accelerating the growth of density-gradient-driven instabilities. Initial cellularization appears, although the overall flame front remains continuous. Although the channel walls are nominally symmetric, the flame front may still become asymmetric during downward propagation due to spontaneous symmetry breaking under unstable conditions. Small perturbations, such as ignition nonuniformity, slight mixture nonuniformity, membrane rupture disturbance, and buoyancy-induced flow distortion, can be amplified by hydrodynamic and thermo-diffusive instabilities. Therefore, the observed asymmetric flame morphology is mainly attributed to instability amplification rather than to different wall effects on the two sides.

At HVF = 15% and 20%, the flame dynamics change substantially. Propagation time further decreases (∼300 ms), with more pronounced front acceleration. The flame front evolves from a single-peak “finger-like” structure to multiscale undulations, as local protrusions enlarge and split, forming distinct cellular or wrinkled patterns. Flame surface roughness and interface area increase, reflecting stronger coupling between thermal-diffusive and hydrodynamic effects. For HVF = 25% and 30%, flame instability intensifies, exhibiting pronounced nonlinear evolution. Propagation time shortens further (∼240 ms), and the flame reaches its maximum speed. The front becomes highly irregular, with coexisting large-scale folds and small-scale cellular structures, and some regions display clear finger-like branching and merging. The flame surface transforms from a smooth continuous interface to a strongly perturbed multipeak morphology, with frequent curvature variations and localized flame sheet elongation. At high HVF, hydrogen’s high diffusivity and reactivity destabilize the mixture, enhancing the coupled Darrieus-Landau and thermo-diffusive instabilities, promoting cellularization and wrinkling. Overall, across all six conditions, when HVF ≥ 15%, the flame clearly enters an instability-dominated regime, with increased interface wrinkling and intensified structural deformation, indicating a critical hydrogen fraction for the onset of instability. Higher hydrogen content amplifies heat release, strengthens flame-induced flow and pressure wave interactions, and enhances flame-pressure wave coupling, thereby accelerating the flame. Under high HVF, the flame surface rapidly expands, generating more wrinkles and increasing the reactive area, further elevating the combustion rate and creating a positive feedback that reinforces flame acceleration.

In addition, buoyancy may also contribute to the present flame evolution because the flame propagates downward while the burned gas tends to rise upward. This adverse buoyancy effect can modify the local flow field and flame stretch distribution, and may promote flame deformation and instability growth, especially under higher HVF conditions. Although hydrogen enrichment generally promotes faster flame propagation, the total propagation duration in the present downward-propagating configuration does not decrease monotonically with increasing HVF. In particular, for the 30% HVF case, the flame front exhibits stronger wrinkling, more complex local deformation, and enhanced unsteady flame-pressure interaction than in the 25% HVF case. This additional unstable evolution stage can slightly prolong the overall propagation process, leading to a marginally longer duration despite the higher laminar burning velocity. Because the difference between the two cases is relatively small, experimental uncertainty may also contribute to this nonmonotonic behavior.

Overpressure Analysis

Figure presents overpressure-time histories under different hydrogen volume fraction (HVF) conditions. At HVF = 5%, the pressure signal exhibits small-amplitude, nearly periodic oscillations, with overpressure fluctuations remaining within ± 2 kPa, and no significant pressure spikes or envelope growth observed over the ∼1500 ms time scale. These oscillations primarily arise from weak acoustic reflections driven by thermal expansion during flame propagation and the linear response of the tube’s acoustic modes. Due to the low combustion rate and smooth flame front (corresponding to the stable finger-like structure in Figure ), the system remains weakly coupled, and pressure perturbations do not amplify significantly, suggesting that combustion-acoustic coupling has not entered the nonlinear growth stage.

Temporal evolution of overpressure under different HVF.

When HVF increases to 10%, the early stage pressure oscillations remain low-amplitude and periodic, but noticeable envelope growth occurs between ∼450–550 ms, with peak overpressure reaching approximately ± 10 kPa. The oscillation frequency remains roughly constant, but amplitude rapidly increases, forming a clearly modulated structure, reflecting the onset of energy coupling between flame acceleration and pressure wave reflections. Corresponding flame images show subtle front wrinkling, suggesting that enhanced interface disturbances induce local fluctuations in heat release rate, thereby injecting additional energy into the acoustic field and triggering a quasi-resonant amplification process.

At HVF = 15%, the pressure evolution displays more pronounced nonlinear growth. Initial oscillations are around ± 3 kPa, but within ∼300–360 ms, they rapidly develop into large-amplitude pulsations with peaks approaching ± 20 kPa. The oscillation envelope exhibits a clear bell-shaped concentration, with sharp main peaks and steep rise rates, reflecting strong transient energy release. At this stage, the flame front is clearly cellularized, and the increased interface area significantly enhances volumetric heat release rate. Flame acceleration amplifies the compression wave magnitude, and as the local flame speed approaches the acoustic velocity component, strong coupling forms between the pressure wave and flame front, leading to rapid oscillation growth.

Under HVF = 20% and 25%, the pressure signals show even stronger coupling and amplification. For HVF = 20%, the main peak occurs around 240–260 ms, with maximum overpressure near ± 22 kPa, and peak timing advanced, indicating more intense flame acceleration. At HVF = 25%, the peak overpressure further increases, with negative pressure valleys exceeding −25 kPa, and strong high-frequency modulation occurs. These large, nearly symmetric oscillations indicate the system has entered a pronounced thermo-acoustic coupling stage, with pressure waves reflecting and superimposing within the tube to form localized quasi-standing wave structures. The highly wrinkled, multipeak flame front produces pulsating heat release, continuously supplying periodic energy to the acoustic field, forming a typical positive feedback loop.

At HVF = 30%, the peak overpressure is slightly lower than that for 25%, but oscillations remain high-amplitude, exceeding ± 15 kPa. Compared to HVF = 25%, the main peak persists longer and the envelope is relatively smoother, suggesting that at this higher hydrogen fraction, although the combustion rate further increases, the system may enter a different dynamic equilibrium. On one hand, the high reactivity enhances flame propagation speed; on the other hand, the flame structure may adopt a more continuous, high-speed propagation mode, slightly reducing the intensity of transient energy concentration. Consequently, the peak overpressure does not increase linearly but exhibits a degree of saturation. This nonmonotonic behavior suggests that the overpressure response at very high HVF is not governed solely by the increase in intrinsic reactivity. For the 30% HVF case, the pressure evolution becomes more prolonged and smoother, indicating that the flame–pressure coupling develops in a more complex oscillatory manner than in the 25% HVF case. As a result, part of the released energy is distributed over an extended oscillation process rather than being concentrated into a sharper transient pressure peak, which contributes to the slight saturation of the peak overpressure. Further investigation is still needed to fully clarify this behavior. Three repeated experiments at HVF = 30% show good reproducibility, indicating that the slightly distinct flame propagation is a robust observation rather than a random fluctuation. This behavior may be attributed to the increased laminar flame speed reported in previous studies, , which can modify the pressure oscillation modes during propagation. Such changes likely contribute to the differences observed at 30% HVF compared with other operating conditions. It should be noted that hydrogen volume fractions beyond the investigated range (5%–30%) were not explored in this study, representing a limitation of the current work.

Combining with flame images, it can be inferred that hydrogen addition enhances laminar flame speed and heat release intensity, and increases flame front instability. Interface wrinkling enlarges the reaction area, accelerating volumetric expansion and strengthening compression wave generation. When pressure waves match the flame propagation speed, a thermo-acoustic amplification mechanism arises, causing rapid growth of pressure oscillation amplitudes. At high HVF, the system gradually approaches a strongly coupled regime, even nearing the critical state for potential deflagration-to-detonation transition.

Figure shows the FFT spectrum distribution characteristics of the pressure signal under different HVF conditions. The spectrum is presented in normalized amplitude, clearly reflecting the dominant frequency structure and its evolution with varying HVF. Although the pressure signal during flame propagation is nonstationary, the FFT is used here to identify the dominant global frequency component and the main acoustic mode over the entire propagation process. Therefore, the FFT result is interpreted as a global spectral characterization rather than a stage-resolved description of the transient evolution. At HVF = 5%, the spectrum is dominated by a single low-frequency peak around 50 Hz, indicating weak coupling and near-linear response in the pressure oscillations. Combined with the steady flame propagation and minimal heat release fluctuations in Figure , this suggests that the system has not formed significant multimodal excitation. When HVF increases to 10%, the main frequency shifts significantly to approximately 261 Hz, and there is noticeable frequency clustering near the main peak. This indicates that flame acceleration enhances the excitation of higher-order acoustic modes, and the combustion–acoustic coupling transitions from low-order to higher-order longitudinal modes. Additionally, secondary energy peaks appear around 500–600 Hz, reflecting the onset of nonlinear interactions or harmonic components.

Frequency spectrum of overpressure.

For HVF = 15%, 20%, and 25%, the main frequencies are 272, 276, and 274 Hz, respectively. This shows that the system has entered the modal locking stage, where the flame propagation frequency matches a specific inherent acoustic mode of the pipe, forming a stable thermoacoustic coupling structure. The secondary peaks in the 500–650 Hz range gradually intensify, with a wider frequency band distribution, implying that strong flame wrinkling leads to high-frequency modulation of the heat release rate. This multifrequency coexistence is a typical nonlinear coupling feature, marking the transition from linear acoustic response to strong coupling oscillations. At HVF = 30%, the main frequency slightly shifts upward to around 280 Hz, and energy clusters appear in the high-frequency range (600–800 Hz), suggesting that the scale of unstable structures in the combustion process has further decreased, exciting higher-frequency disturbances. However, the main frequency remains in the 270–280 Hz range, showing that the pipe’s acoustic characteristics still dominate the modal selection mechanism. The acoustic coupling observed here originates from the interaction among flame wrinkling, unsteady heat release, compression-wave generation, and acoustic-wave reflection in the open-closed tube. At low HVF, the flame remains relatively smooth and the heat-release fluctuation is weak, so the acoustic field is only weakly excited. As HVF increases, the stronger reactivity and more pronounced flame-front instability enhance unsteady volumetric expansion and pressure-wave generation, allowing the flame-induced heat-release oscillation to couple more effectively with the longitudinal acoustic mode of the tube. Once this feedback becomes sufficiently strong, modal locking occurs and the pressure oscillation energy becomes concentrated in the time-frequency domain, indicating the development of a pronounced thermoacoustic amplification process.

While the standard Fourier transform provides a global representation of the frequency content of a signal, it inherently lacks the capability to reveal the temporal evolution of these frequency components. To overcome this limitation and capture the essential time-varying spectral characteristics inherent in nonstationary signals, the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) was employed. To account for the time-varying nature of the pressure oscillation, the STFT was further employed as a complementary method to reveal the temporal evolution of frequency content and energy concentration. Therefore, the combined use of FFT and STFT in this work allows both global modal identification and time-resolved analysis of the transient flame–pressure coupling process. In Figure , The time-frequency distribution from the STFT provides a clearer view of the temporal evolution of pressure oscillation energy under different HVF conditions. As HVF increases from 5% to 30%, the energy distribution transitions from a stable low-frequency linear acoustic response to a stronger coupled structure dominated by midfrequencies, with transient intensification and clear modal concentration and nonlinear amplification. At HVF = 5%, the energy primarily concentrates below 50 Hz and remains consistent throughout the propagation, with uniform amplitude distribution and no significant spikes. This matches the earlier spectral results, indicating weak flame-acoustic field coupling and no notable transient energy concentration. When HVF increases to 10%, noticeable energy clustering appears in the 450 ∼ 550 ms window, with the main frequency between 250–300 Hz, and an upward extension in the high-frequency direction. This “cone-shaped” time-frequency structure suggests that during the flame acceleration phase, rapid heat release intensifies midorder acoustic modes, which then transfer energy to higher frequencies through nonlinear interactions.

Short-Time Fourier Transform of overpressure oscillations.

In the range of HVF = 15%–25%, energy concentration shifts earlier in time, appearing at approximately 300, 250, and 220 ms, coinciding with the main pressure peaks. The main frequency stabilizes around 270 Hz, showing modal locking. The energy peaks become more concentrated and stronger, indicating the thermal–acoustic coupling has entered a strong amplification stage. High-frequency branches (500–700 Hz) appear, indicating flame interface wrinkling and small-scale instabilities that lead to higher-frequency heat release fluctuations. At HVF = 30%, the main frequency remains around 280 Hz, but the energy distribution spreads, with more pronounced high-frequency clustering. The main peak duration extends slightly, indicating that at higher hydrogen fractions, although combustion reactivity increases, the energy is distributed across multiple scales, reflecting more complex nonlinear dynamics.

Figure shows the dominant frequency characteristics of pressure fluctuations during premixed flame propagation in the pipe under different HVF conditions, comparing theoretical calculations and experimental measurements. Theoretical frequencies are calculated using the acoustic formula f = c1/(2L), where c1 is the speed of sound in the unburned gas and L is the tube length. Experimental frequencies are obtained by performing Fast Fourier Transform on the pressure signal. Theoretical results show that the dominant frequency remains around 350 Hz across all conditions, with only a slight increase as HVF increases. This indicates that the frequency is primarily determined by the pipe length and sound speed, consistent with the first longitudinal acoustic mode. Therefore, the dominant frequency is controlled by geometric acoustic boundaries rather than by the chemical reaction rate. Experimental results, however, show clear HVF dependence. At HVF = 5%, the dominant frequency is around 50 Hz, significantly lower than the theoretical value, indicating that the first acoustic mode is not yet excited and the system is undergoing low-frequency oscillations. As HVF increases to 10%, the experimental dominant frequency rises sharply to about 260 Hz, which is 70%–80% of the theoretical value. For HVF = 15%–30%, the experimental frequency stabilizes between 270 and 280 Hz, slightly increasing with HVF, but still about 70–80 Hz below the theoretical value. The differences between theoretical and experimental results can be attributed to several factors. These include nonuniform temperature distribution during combustion, which lowers the effective sound speed compared to the ideal high-temperature assumption, flame-acoustic coupling introducing additional phase lag and energy dissipation that shifts the actual frequency, and imperfect boundary conditions with damping and reflection losses.

Comparison of theoretical and experimental dominant frequency values.

The theoretical frequency calculated from f = c1/(2L) represents the ideal first longitudinal acoustic mode of the tube and is used here as a reference value. The lower experimental dominant frequency can be attributed to the combined effects of nonuniform temperature distribution, flame-motion-induced unsteadiness, phase lag between heat release and pressure response, and nonideal boundary damping and reflection. Therefore, the discrepancy of about 70–80 Hz indicates that the actual thermoacoustic response is influenced by coupled transient effects beyond the simplified acoustic model.

Coupling and Mechanistic Analysis

Using HVF = 10% and HVF = 20% as examples, Figure clearly contrasts the coupling evolution of overpressure, flame front position, and flame front velocity under different hydrogen fractions. It should be noted that a positive flame front velocity in Figure indicates downward propagation of the extracted flame-front position, whereas a negative value indicates a temporary local retreat. At HVF = 10%, the flame front position increases almost linearly with time, showing that the flame is in a relatively stable propagation phase. The corresponding flame front velocity shows only slight oscillations between 0–400 ms, with a low average value, indicating weak coupling. The overpressure curve also exhibits low-amplitude periodic oscillations in the early stage, with gradual amplitude changes. Around 480–520 ms, the flame front position slope increases sharply, and the flame speed rises rapidly with strong oscillations. Overpressure simultaneously spikes, forming a clear pressure peak. The synchronization of these three parameters indicates that flame acceleration leads to a rapid increase in heat release, triggering acoustic mode amplification and a typical thermoacoustic coupling enhancement process. However, the energy concentration is relatively late, and the peak amplitude is limited, suggesting that the system remains at a critical coupling edge under 10% hydrogen fraction. In contrast, at HVF = 20%, the coupling process occurs earlier and more intensely. The flame front position slope increases sharply between 200–250 ms, with a rapid rise in flame front velocity and large oscillations, reaching a much higher peak than at 10%. Simultaneously, the overpressure curve amplifies rapidly within the same time period, and the oscillation amplitude significantly increases, resulting in high-amplitude pressure pulses. Notably, the oscillations of flame front velocity and pressure are almost in phase, indicating a strong lock-in relationship between flame propagation and the acoustic field. The coupling trigger time is significantly earlier, and the peak amplitude is higher, suggesting that the increase in hydrogen fraction significantly enhances the reaction rate and flame acceleration, causing the system to enter a strong nonlinear amplification phase earlier. As HVF increases from 10% to 20%, the flame acceleration time is advanced, shortening the instability trigger time. Both the peak flame front velocity and overpressure amplitudes increase significantly, reflecting enhanced heat release fluctuations. Furthermore, the three parameters (overpressure, position, and velocity) synchronize closely at the peak, indicating a transition from weak to strong thermoacoustic coupling. This shows that the 20% HVF condition is closer to the instability or detonation-enhanced threshold compared to the 10% condition, marking a critical point where system risk increases significantly. These findings align with the modal enhancement and energy concentration patterns revealed by the earlier spectral and STFT analyses. The critical transition around HVF ≈ 10%–15% can be interpreted as the onset of a positive-feedback regime involving flame instability, unsteady heat release, and acoustic response. At low HVF, the flame remains relatively smooth and the pressure disturbance is too weak to form strong coupling with the tube acoustics. As HVF increases, the higher reactivity and stronger flame wrinkling enhance heat release fluctuation and pressure-wave excitation, so that flame deformation and pressure oscillation begin to reinforce each other. Meanwhile, the increasing contribution of chain-branching reactions further accelerates this transition, causing the system to evolve from a weakly coupled propagation regime to an instability-dominated regime.

In the present vertical configuration, buoyancy acts opposite to the flame propagation direction because the burned gas tends to rise while the flame propagates downward. This adverse buoyancy effect modifies the local flow field and flame stretch distribution, and therefore contributes to flame deformation during propagation. As HVF increases, the stronger heat release and gas expansion enhance this effect, making buoyancy an additional factor promoting instability growth together with hydrodynamic, thermo-diffusive, and flame–pressure coupling effects. Compared with previous studies conducted in fully closed vessels or tubes with different geometric configurations, ,, the present open–closed vertical tube exhibits a more distinctive coupling process because reflected pressure waves act together with adverse buoyancy and flame-front instability during downward propagation. Therefore, although hydrogen enrichment generally promotes flame acceleration and pressure amplification, the specific boundary condition and geometric configuration used here can make the flame evolution and pressure response more complex than those reported for fully closed or differently proportioned systems. Further experimental comparison under fully closed conditions and with different aspect ratios would be valuable for clarifying the role of boundary condition and geometry in flame instability and flame–pressure coupling.

The sensitivity results shown in Figure reveal that the dominant reaction pathways remain consistent across different HVF conditions, but the contribution of each reaction to the system’s dynamics increases significantly as the hydrogen fraction rises. First, the elementary reaction CO + OH ≤ >CO2 + H consistently exhibits the highest positive sensitivity coefficient across all conditions, dominating the overall behavior. This indicates that the CO oxidation process is the core reaction path controlling the total heat release and propagation rate. On one hand, this reaction consumes OH radicals to produce CO2, and on the other hand, generates H atoms, providing active free radicals for subsequent chain branching reactions, thus maintaining the continuity of the reaction chain. Second, the reactions O + H2≤ >H + OH and H + O2≤ >O + OH both show significant positive sensitivity, and their sensitivity coefficients increase with HVF. This indicates that as the hydrogen fraction increases, the cycle of H and O free radicals accelerates, enhancing chain branching efficiency and increasing system reactivity, which promotes flame acceleration and pressure amplification. This is consistent with the earlier observed trends in flame speed and overpressure enhancement. The reaction HO2 + H ≤ >2OH also exhibits strong positive sensitivity, with its influence becoming more pronounced under medium-to-high HVF conditions. This reaction rapidly converts HO2 into OH radicals, significantly increasing the concentration of active free radicals, and plays a key role in promoting detonation under high hydrogen fractions. In contrast, three-body reactions such as H + OH + M ≤ >H2O + M and H + O2(+M)≤ >HO2(+M) show negative sensitivity, with higher absolute values at low HVF conditions. These reactions mainly consume H and OH radicals to form more stable molecules or intermediate products, suppressing chain branching and weakening the reaction rate. As HVF increases, the negative sensitivity decreases, indicating that the chain-termination effect weakens at higher hydrogen fractions, making the system more prone to maintaining a highly reactive state. This result provides a chemical kinetic explanation for the earlier observed physical mechanisms of flame acceleration, early pressure peaks, and enhanced thermoacoustic coupling. As HVF increases, the increasing sensitivity of chain-branching reactions indicates faster radical generation and stronger heat release, which promotes flame acceleration, front wrinkling, and earlier pressure amplification. Conclusions

This study systematically examined the downward propagation characteristics of H2/CO/CO2/air premixed flames in a vertical cylindrical tube, with particular emphasis on the influence of hydrogen volume fraction (HVF) on flame dynamics, pressure evolution, thermoacoustic behavior, and chemical kinetic responses. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:1.Hydrogen enrichment markedly modifies flame morphology and stability characteristics. Under low HVF conditions (5–10%), the flame front remains relatively smooth and axisymmetric, and propagation is predominantly laminar with limited interface distortion. When HVF reaches 15% and above, the flame structure transitions to an instability-dominated regime characterized by pronounced wrinkling, finger-like protrusions, and multiscale cellular features. This transition indicates the existence of a critical hydrogen blending range that promotes the onset of hydrodynamic and thermo-diffusive instabilities, leading to accelerated and nonuniform flame propagation.2.The overpressure response strengthens significantly with increasing HVF. At low hydrogen fractions, pressure oscillations remain low in amplitude and are governed by weak acoustic excitation. As HVF increases to 10% and above, the dominant frequency rapidly shifts from low-frequency oscillations to a higher-frequency mode associated with the tube’s longitudinal acoustic resonance, indicating mode locking between flame dynamics and the acoustic field. In the 15–30% range, pressure amplitudes increase markedly and the main pressure peaks occur earlier, reflecting intensified flame acceleration and stronger thermoacoustic coupling. The most pronounced pressure amplification is observed in the intermediate-to-high HVF range, where flame acceleration and acoustic resonance establish a positive feedback loop.3.Chemical kinetic analysis reveals that hydrogen enrichment enhances chain-branching pathways and radical pool intensification. The reaction CO + OH ≤ >CO2 + H remains the dominant heat-release pathway across all conditions, while classical chain-branching reactions such as O + H2≤ >H + OH and H + O2≤ >O + OH become increasingly influential with rising HVF. Meanwhile, the relative inhibiting effect of termination reactions weakens at higher hydrogen fractions. As a result, the reaction network shifts from a relatively weak, termination-influenced regime at low HVF to a strongly branched, OH-dominated kinetic structure at high HVF, thereby promoting faster heat release, stronger flame–pressure coupling, and enhanced system reactivity.
