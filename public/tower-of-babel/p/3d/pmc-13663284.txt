In search of common ground: Louisiana’s resistance to an extreme risk protection order law for domestic violence prevention
Authors: Maeve E. Wallace, Dovile Vilda, Veronica A. Pear, Jocelyn Pinkerton, Jennifer A. Hunt, Jaquelyn L. Jahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtreme Risk Protection Order (ERPO) laws allow civil courts to authorize orders to temporarily prevent a person from purchasing or possessing firearms upon finding that they pose a significant risk of harm to themselves or others. In Louisiana, a state with one of the highest rates of firearm homicide of women in the nation, multiple attempts to pass ERPO legislation have failed despite evidence of their effectiveness. We sought to identify places of common ground on the matter of domestic violence against women in Louisiana and to understand how perspectives on politics and policy solutions – including ERPOs – diverged from there.MethodsWe interviewed 16 key informants purposefully recruited to ensure diversity in political ideology, professional roles, and regions of Louisiana. All held active roles in gun rights or gun regulation advocacy and lobbying; law enforcement and judicial systems; public health and domestic violence prevention; and state policymaking. Verbatim transcripts were analyzed via thematic analysis with deductive coding following the Multiple Streams Framework.ResultsAmong key informants, there was nearly universal agreement on the matter of domestic violence as a problem. However, there was ambiguity around the role of firearms in domestic violence and the utility of targeting their removal for the prevention of fatal violence against women. Concerns specific to ERPO law as one such approach included anticipated misuse of the law, uncertainty about its relationship to existing firearm transfer laws, undue burdens on gun owners, and due process. Aspects of the political and cultural context were identified as barriers to the passage of ERPO, including concentrated political polarity, entrenched patriarchy, and lack of connectedness with growing distrust and dehumanization of politicians and of each other. Suggested strategies for overcoming barriers to an ERPO law in Louisiana included broader education about the law and provisions, long-term relationship building outside of legislative sessions, and aisle-bridging collaboration on the drafting of legislation.ConclusionsUnderstanding the factors that have constrained ERPO legislation in Louisiana can inform research and advocacy efforts to promote evidence-based policy decisions that promote the health and safety of all people.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28597-2.

Background

Women in the US are up to 28 times more likely to be killed with a firearm than women in other high-income countries [1, 2]. Firearm violence against women is especially prevalent in Louisiana where female homicide rates are nearly double the national average, three of four cases involve firearms, and the majority are perpetrated by an intimate partner [3].

As of 2026, 22 states, the District of Columbia, and the US Virgin Islands have enacted Extreme Risk Protection Orders (EPROs) in an attempt to reduce firearm violence [4]. ERPOs allow civil courts to authorize orders to temporarily prevent a person from purchasing or possessing firearms upon finding that they pose a significant risk of harm to themselves or others. A growing body of research finds that ERPOs may effectively reduce firearm suicide [5–7], and are commonly filed to prevent events of mass violence (the killing of at least 3 people), including shooting threats at school or college campuses and businesses [8]. Given their lethality, the removal of firearms from domestic violence situations is a well-established means of promoting safety [9], and ERPOs have more recently been proposed as an additional tool for the prevention of intimate partner homicide [10–13]. ERPOs provide a mechanism to quickly remove firearms from volatile situations and do not require the victimized person (a survivor of intimate partner violence, for example) to act as a petitioner.

Louisiana does not have an ERPO law, despite multiple attempts brought before the state legislature in recent years. Louisiana has a robust gun rights lobby and is consistently considered to be one of the most “gun friendly” states in the nation [14]. Nevertheless, Louisiana prohibits the possession (and requires transfer) of firearms by persons under a domestic violence restraining order (DVRO) or convicted of domestic violence misdemeanor. The surprising passage of these laws in Louisiana in 2014 and 2018, respectively, suggest that there may be unique pathways through which firearm legislation—including ERPO laws—may be enacted, even in states that historically resist gun safety regulation [15].

Since the DVRO law was passed, however, growing political polarization both in Louisiana and nationally, concurrent with other broad societal shocks and shifts (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic, declining trust in government and institutions [16], and social movements both for and against progressive agendas), has changed the context in which state policymaking occurs. Our objective was to use Louisiana’s resistance to an ERPO law as a case study to understand how the factors enable or constrain the processes involved in policymaking for domestic gun violence prevention – from identification of the problem to the formation and passage of a policy solution.

To do this, we apply Kingdon’s Multiple Streams Framework (MSF) [17], which is increasingly used by public health researchers to understand politicization and policy-making around issues including vaccine mandates [18], anti-pregnancy workplace discrimination [19], anti-tobacco laws [20], school-based sexual abuse prevention mandates [21], telehealth policy in the context of COVID-19 [22], and even the passage of the DVRO law in Louisiana [15]. The MSF conceptualizes the policy-making process as occurring across three distinct streams that must converge to open a window of opportunity for legislative change. First, the problem stream refers to how an issue or situation is viewed (or socially constructed) as a problem requiring policy intervention. Second, the policy solutions stream involves members of a policy community (e.g., advocates, subject-area experts) identifying evidence-informed policy alternatives to address the problem. Third, the politics stream involves the broader political environment in which policymaking occurs, including public mood and opinion, elections and governmental composition (national, state, local), cultural norms and values, and economic and other features that shape priorities and decision-making [17].

For this analysis, we used the MSF to guide our qualitative inquiry with stakeholders in gun policy and violence prevention across Louisiana. We examined the extent of consensus around gun violence against women as a policy-relevant problem in communities across Louisiana and explored how perspectives on political feasibility and policy solutions diverged from there. Understanding these dynamics is key to finding a path toward bipartisan policy solutions that promote the health and safety of all people while respecting individual rights.

Methods

This report of qualitative research follows the consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) guidelines [23]. The research team were investigators with backgrounds in public health, social work, social science, and epidemiology, with expertise in firearm injury prevention, domestic violence, health policy, and qualitative research. All identified as female and all but one resided in Louisiana. Several team members have longstanding professional relationships with organizations involved in violence prevention and firearm policy in Louisiana, which facilitated recruitment and provided contextual understanding of the state’s policy landscape. We purposively invited participants from our professional networks who reflected diverse professional roles and political perspectives relevant to firearm policymaking, including gun rights or gun safety regulation lobbying and advocacy, law enforcement and judicial systems, public health and domestic violence prevention, and state policymaking. Subsequently, snowball sampling was used to identify key stakeholders with relevant expertise across different regions of the state with a question at the end of the interview asking participants to suggest others. Our sampling strategy aimed to capture a broad range of perspectives rather than achieve numerical representativeness. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, with the final interviews reinforcing existing themes rather than generating substantively new ones. We completed a total of 16 semi-structured interviews with between June 2024 and February 2025.

Interviews were conducted by co-authors MW, JH, DV, and JP via Zoom, phone, or in person, lasted approximately 60 min, and were conducted either by a single interviewer or jointly by two members of the research team. The interview guide included open-ended questions about domestic violence and firearm risk, perceptions of ERPO policy feasibility, and views on ERPOs within the Louisiana context. It, can be found in the supplementary material. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified prior to analysis. Field notes were not systematically collected; instead, analytic observations were documented through coding memos and discussed during regular team meetings throughout the analytic process. The study was approved by the Tulane Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided verbal informed consent prior to interview.

Transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis guided by the MSF. An initial codebook was developed iteratively through close reading of the transcripts and refined through team discussion. The MSF provided the overarching analytic framework, with data first organized into the problem, policy, and politics streams before themes within each stream were identified and refined inductively [24]. Transcripts were managed and coded using a combination of NVivo qualitative analysis software and manual coding. Double coding was conducted by two members of the research team and refined through iterative team discussions to enhance consistency and analytic rigor. Analytic memos documented emerging interpretations and relationships between themes throughout the coding process, while cross-stakeholder comparisons identified convergence and divergence across professional and ideological groups and to identify perceived barriers to the alignment of streams necessary for ERPO legislation in the state. Participants did not provide feedback on the findings, nor were transcripts returned to participants for comment or corrections.

The research team recognizes that our disciplinary training as well as our personal and professional experiences shape the nature of our data collection and interpretation. To enhance analytic rigor, we engaged as researchers with diverse disciplinary perspectives throughout the analytic process, used iterative team discussions to challenge emerging interpretations, and grounded themes in participants’ accounts through constant comparison across stakeholder groups representing a wide range of professional roles and political perspectives.

Problem stream: broad recognition of harm, fragmented definition of the policy problem

Across interviews, there was near universal agreement that domestic violence was a serious and persistent problem in Louisiana. Participants described it as a “huge” or “big” issue. They drew on their professional experiences, local contexts, media coverage, and, in some cases, personal connections and lived experience as survivors to underscore its salience in communities across the state.

Where perspectives diverged was in whether domestic violence and intimate partner homicide should be understood specifically as a problem relating to gun violence. While public health professionals and domestic violence advocates emphasized firearms as a factor that substantially elevates lethality risk in domestic violence contexts, several gun rights advocates resisted framing the issue in firearm-specific terms. As one participant revealed “I take issue with the question of ‘gun violence’. I don’t think guns are inherently violent” (Participant 7, gun rights advocate).

Participants who viewed firearms as central to the problem pointed to their increasing accessibility and growing ubiquity in Louisiana. As one district attorney reflected on her decades-long career, “Before you didn’t assume everybody has a gun. Now you assume everybody has a gun” (Participant 2). For these stakeholders, the combination of social stressors and widespread firearm access created dangerous and potentially lethal conditions in volatile, high stress circumstances faced by many in the state:“There’s a lot of adversity and people are under a lot of stress. <…> And I think that guns and hair trigger responses to certain stressors are not a good mix…it’s just this easy tool that escalates things so quickly.” – Participant 1, public health professional.

“There’s a lot of adversity and people are under a lot of stress. <…> And I think that guns and hair trigger responses to certain stressors are not a good mix…it’s just this easy tool that escalates things so quickly.” – Participant 1, public health professional.

In contrast, gun rights advocates consistently emphasized intent and underlying social conditions – mentioning chronic poverty, family instability, intergenerational trauma (“hurt people hurt people”), historical oppression and disinvestment, educational inequities, and loss of religiosity – rather than firearm availability as a factor in contributing to violence. As one participant explained, “If somebody is willing to be violent toward another human being, the tool is less important than the intent” (Participant 7). When reflecting on the connection between firearms and domestic violence in their community, another responded added:“It just doesn’t feel like it’s, like these two things are interrelated. I mean, certainly domestic violence could happen with a gun. But it could happen with a car, too.” – Participant 6, gun rights advocate.

“It just doesn’t feel like it’s, like these two things are interrelated. I mean, certainly domestic violence could happen with a gun. But it could happen with a car, too.” – Participant 6, gun rights advocate.

Across ideological positions on firearm laws, participants frequently connected interpersonal violence to broader structural conditions, though the relative weight assigned to these factors varied. For many gun rights advocates, including those actively involved in lobbying on behalf of the gun policy community, the deep embedding of guns in everyday life – as tools for protection, sport, identity, status and empowerment – obscured their role as a public health problem for women and survivors of domestic violence. Some participants described increased firearm ownership among women as protective rather than harmful, and a potential solution to the problem of domestic violence rather than a contributor to it:

“Now I know women that, like, fight back so… Women firearm ownership has gone up since 2020.” – Participant 9, gun rights advocate.

“Now I know women that, like, fight back so… Women firearm ownership has gone up since 2020.” – Participant 9, gun rights advocate.

“If you’re that person in that situation, what are you gonna do? Maybe the gun is the answer for you as the victim!” – Participant 6, gun rights advocate.

“If you’re that person in that situation, what are you gonna do? Maybe the gun is the answer for you as the victim!” – Participant 6, gun rights advocate.

“… I certainly would consider not attacking someone if they had something to even the odds, right? And that can be a firearm. It doesn’t have to be a firearm, but it can be. So that’s a positive thing. – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

“… I certainly would consider not attacking someone if they had something to even the odds, right? And that can be a firearm. It doesn’t have to be a firearm, but it can be. So that’s a positive thing. – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

Taken together, these narratives suggest that while domestic violence itself is widely recognized as a pressing concern, there is no shared consensus that firearm access constitutes a core policy-relevant element of the problem. Participants differed in how they understood the role of guns in shaping domestic violence risk and lethality. This divergence in framing makes it more difficult to build consensus around firearm-specific policy responses.

Policy stream: application of ERPO for domestic violence survivors, implementation concerns, and fragmented policy community

Following the ambiguity in problem framing, participants described multiple obstacles within the policy stream that prevent convergence around an ERPO law as an appropriate or feasible solution to domestic violence. For some gun rights advocates, firearm regulation was viewed as addressing a symptom rather than a root cause:“And so it’s very easy to think, ‘people are being killed by guns, let’s do something about the guns.’ That’s the easy button. The real problem is poverty, helplessness, lack of education, drugs, those are really hard things to fix, right?” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

“And so it’s very easy to think, ‘people are being killed by guns, let’s do something about the guns.’ That’s the easy button. The real problem is poverty, helplessness, lack of education, drugs, those are really hard things to fix, right?” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

Domestic violence advocates and legal-system professionals acknowledged larger root causes of domestic violence as well but viewed ERPOs as “one tool in the toolbox” for reducing firearm-related harm and preventing both fatal and non-fatal intimate partner violence in Louisiana. Asked whether they would be effective in this way they acknowledged “Absolutely”, (Participant 12, law enforcement), and “Those laws work. There have been other states where those laws work” (Participant 1, public health professional).

However, even among those generally supportive of ERPOs, uncertainty emerged regarding how such a law would interact with existing DVRO firearm transfer provisions. Questions arose around court procedures, implementation, and whether ERPOs would supplement or duplicate current mechanisms (e.g., the DVRO law was seen as “so much broader [than ERPOs] in terms of the type of relief she [hypothetical domestic violence victim] can get” - Participant 3, domestic violence advocate).

Beyond debates about policy design, participants who were generally supportive of ERPO raised concerns about persistent weaknesses in the implementation of existing DVRO firearm transfer laws. They identified shortcomings in current firearm transfer systems: limited resources, unclear enforcement roles, inconsistent judicial follow-through, and challenges verifying firearm possession. One law enforcement participant detailed the difficulty of reconciling victim reports with judicial standards of proof:“I have victims and survivors that can describe these guns to a T. You know, they know what this firearm looks like. They’re like, I don’t know if he owns it or not, but this is the firearm he has. Then these guys get arrested. They go in front of the judge and it’s like, ‘Do you own or possess or whatever on that declaration?’ And they claim no, they don’t. And so I know that’s our issue right now, or it has been for a while. So then it comes to the point, like, what can we do? Can we go get a search warrant? Is a judge even going to think we’ve got enough probable cause because the victim’s saying there is a gun. He says no.” – Participant 12, law enforcement.

Then these guys get arrested. They go in front of the judge and it’s like, ‘Do you own or possess or whatever on that declaration?’ And they claim no, they don’t. These concerns were frequently projected onto hypothetical ERPO implementation. Participants anticipated similar challenges in the absence of state-specific legislative language, clear agency protocols, funding, training, and data integration. In this sense, skepticism about ERPOs was not limited to ideological opposition; it reflected broader doubts about administrative feasibility.

Concerns about misuse and procedural fairness further complicated policy alignment. Gun rights advocates expressed fears that false allegations could result in unjust firearm removal; these concerns were situated within a broader narrative of government overreach, and, for some, racialized enforcement of laws. One Black gun rights advocate placed his concern about ERPOs in the broader context of racialized policing and mass incarceration:“We don’t want the government or law enforcement involved. So we’re trying to make it [firearm transfer] a voluntary thing…It’s got to be done right. I always feared it’s why people wind up in jail unjustly.” – Participant 10, gun rights advocate.

“We don’t want the government or law enforcement involved. So we’re trying to make it [firearm transfer] a voluntary thing…It’s got to be done right. I always feared it’s why people wind up in jail unjustly.” – Participant 10, gun rights advocate.

Stakeholders were also apprehensive about due process for ERPO respondents, resulting in participants across ideological perspectives emphasizing the importance of clear evidentiary standards and opportunities for defense (“I would love to see Louisiana have it [ERPO], but I want to make sure it’s not an abuse of an individual’s rights. I just want to make sure that the person has the opportunity to defend himself”, Participant 8, attorney). Notably, several legal professionals suggested that procedural safeguards are or could be built into ERPO legislation to address these concerns:

“There’s a standard that has to be met. There’s a process. There’s due process. I think a lot of people who are against it one day think about the Second Amendment rights, but maybe if they understand that there’s actually a process and there’s due process on the civil side, that they could support a law like that. I think that’s going to be very important as, if something like that is introduced, to explain that due process is not left out.”– Participant 13, judge.

“There’s a standard that has to be met. There’s a process. There’s due process. I think a lot of people who are against it one day think about the Second Amendment rights, but maybe if they understand that there’s actually a process and there’s due process on the civil side, that they could support a law like that. I think that’s going to be very important as, if something like that is introduced, to explain that due process is not left out.”– Participant 13, judge.

“In Louisiana we may have to tackle on some affidavit by someone who could attest that they’ve seen him with a firearm or that they’re aware that he has threatened her with a firearm…there may be some things like that that could be built into the red flag order [ERPO].” – Participant 4, attorney.

Policy alignment was further hampered by political biases and (mis)perceptions. Many participants supportive of ERPOs described the policy as politically toxic (particularly among organized gun rights constituencies) leading to a perception that engagement across ideological perspectives would be futile. Both firearm regulation lobbyists and others involved in firearm regulation advocacy expressed reluctance to pursue dialogue with gun rights activists, assuming firm opposition in advance (“I think that it [passing ERPO] elicits this gut response from gun rights folks, because it’s just seen as this incredibly egregious assault on their rights, you know?” – Participant 3, domestic violence advocate). Yet willingness to engage was expressed by at least one leader of a gun rights advocacy organization:“As long as there’s actual due process I think that that’s something that [pro-gun organization] could support that. And if we support it, it will pass. Okay? So not to be, you know, not to be over-egotistical, but, if the right people came to us and said, “help us write a law”, we could help them write a law.” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

“As long as there’s actual due process I think that that’s something that [pro-gun organization] could support that. And if we support it, it will pass. Okay? So not to be, you know, not to be over-egotistical, but, if the right people came to us and said, “help us write a law”, we could help them write a law.” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

Taken together, these accounts point to divisions within the policy stream not only around the ERPO design and implementation but also within the policy community itself – stakeholder groups across the ideological spectrum as well as those who would participate in policy implementation. Divergent expectations about feasibility, concerns about procedural fairness, and limited cross-ideological engagement appeared to prevent consolidation around a shared proposal. Although policy ideas existed, uncertainty about how they would function in practice and whether they would be accepted across constituencies appeared to limit their momentum.

Politics stream: shifting norms and values, concentrated political polarity, and a narrowing policy window

Many participants reflected on why ERPO legislation has repeatedly failed to advance in Louisiana, often situating the issue within broader transformation of the political climate. Several female participants described what they perceived as resurgence of patriarchal norms, “rampant sexism,” and the declining status of women in the US and Louisiana in particular. As a result, framing the need for ERPO as a means to prevent violence against women was viewed a less effective approach than it may have been previously. One domestic violence advocate contrasted past legislative debates with the present:“Ten years ago, you would never overtly say – you might believe in your heart – that that women lie, and that they don’t deserve these protection orders, but it would be very rude and politically unwise to say that out loud. And it’s not anymore.” – Participant 3, domestic violence advocate.

“Ten years ago, you would never overtly say – you might believe in your heart – that that women lie, and that they don’t deserve these protection orders, but it would be very rude and politically unwise to say that out loud. And it’s not anymore.” – Participant 3, domestic violence advocate.

Another participant framed the shift as both cultural and institutional:“I just think we set Louisiana women up for failure in a lot of ways or the system fails them in multiple ways. …Our humanness is not fully valued. And we see that reflected in the law in Louisiana…I think we’ve just rejected a lot of the values that we grew up with in especially Cajun and Creole communities and kind of traded that for like ‘all American values’, which also means patriarchy and silencing of women.” – Participant 14, gun regulation lobbyist.

“I just think we set Louisiana women up for failure in a lot of ways or the system fails them in multiple ways. …Our humanness is not fully valued. And we see that reflected in the law in Louisiana…I think we’ve just rejected a lot of the values that we grew up with in especially Cajun and Creole communities and kind of traded that for like ‘all American values’, which also means patriarchy and silencing of women.” – Participant 14, gun regulation lobbyist.

Two participants who had been actively involved in the successful passage of Louisiana’s DVRO firearm transfer law in 2014 described a markedly different political environment at that time. One recalled bipartisan empathy and receptivity to evidence on the role and magnitude of firearms in domestic violence contexts presented in committee hearings. When both were asked whether similar legislation could pass today, one respondent was doubtful, another responded bluntly: “It would be totally impossible” (Participant 15). The contrast between earlier legislative efforts and successes and the current climate suggests a perceived erosion of the relational and deliberative norms that previously enabled cross-aisle collaboration. As perceived by these participants, the former strategy – which centered the policy debate around domestic violence prevention rather than firearm access – would not be successful today.

Participants across political ideologies and from both sides of the gun policy debate recognized a growing polarization that was further entrenching the divide between sides. They cited skepticism about politicians’ motivations and the power of special interest lobbying groups as central barriers to firearm policy development. Politicians were perceived as prioritizing partisan alignment and donor interests over compromise:“When they [politicians] win their election, they don’t go in that back room and say, ‘Great, now how can we help the people.’ They say ‘great, how can we stay in power?’ That’s all they’re interested in. So, you know, the left is going to continue to cater to anti-gun, the right is going to continue to cater to pro-gun. And they’re not going to do anything to cause people to want to meet in the middle, think about it, discuss it.” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

“When they [politicians] win their election, they don’t go in that back room and say, ‘Great, now how can we help the people.’ They say ‘great, how can we stay in power?’ That’s all they’re interested in. So, you know, the left is going to continue to cater to anti-gun, the right is going to continue to cater to pro-gun. And they’re not going to do anything to cause people to want to meet in the middle, think about it, discuss it.” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

Several participants expressed nostalgia for a past when professional relationships and personal respect allowed more substantive engagement across ideological lines. One gun regulation lobbyist noted, “it was never this weird,” reflecting not simply interpersonal tensions but a broader shift toward symbolic politics in which firearm policy is seen as a litmus test of ideological loyalty.

Indeed, participants described a shift specifically toward concentrated polarity on the political right, with a deepening homogeny of conservative and pro-gun political views, rather than an equally bimodal distribution on both ends of the political spectrum. In this context, firearm legislation such as an ERPO law was widely viewed as politically unrealistic, regardless of how it was designed. Stakeholder accounts also suggested that this broader political context shapes how both the problem of violence and potential policy solutions are understood, limiting the ability to frame firearm risk policies as shared public safety measures.

Louisiana’s cultural identity further shaped the politics stream. Many participants spoke about the state’s dominant “culture of guns,” hunting and shooting sports traditions—describing scenes of rural Louisiana where “you have 8, 10 year olds that walk down the highway with loaded shotguns to go dove and quail hunting”—and resistance to external imposition of values. Evidence from other states was often viewed as inapplicable, and out-of-state advocacy could be interpreted as cultural condescension. As one participant noted, “Louisianans know when somebody feels like they’re treating them like a bunch of rednecks.”

Overcoming barriers: conditions for opening a policy window

Despite describing substantial constraints across the problem, policy, and politics streams, participants also identified strategies to transcend the seemingly intractable divide on firearm legislation, including an ERPO law, in a state as deeply and increasingly conservative as Louisiana. These suggestions fell into two broad categories: strategic institutional reforms and broader cultural or relational shifts.

At the institutional level, several participants with roles in public health or firearm safety regulation advocacy emphasized the need to reframe ERPOs not as “gun control” but as victim protection or suicide prevention tools, particularly when paired with clear procedural safeguards. On this level, proposed strategies included drafting narrowly tailored provisions that appease all sides (e.g., with explicit procedures); clarifying interactions with the existing DVRO firearm transfer law; investing in enforcement infrastructure (e.g., storage protocols, compliance verification, data integration); and identifying diverse allies as trusted messengers (such as sheriffs, local police, and gun rights activists) to help shape and communicate messaging about ERPOs. Trusted messengers were seen as important but not sufficient for the ERPO legislation to succeed without long-term engagement:“There was a sheriff of the other party down in one of the parishes that’s not Orleans or East Baton Rouge who supports it [ERPO]. And was like, ‘this is a good thing.’ And I was like, okay, well, I need your support. And I can’t recall if he came and testified or not but there’s just always that sense of, you know, as a one-off. <….> I do recall having some conversations with unlikely allies who supported the bill. But it wasn’t enough to get it moving.” – Participant 11, policymaker.

“There was a sheriff of the other party down in one of the parishes that’s not Orleans or East Baton Rouge who supports it [ERPO]. And was like, ‘this is a good thing.’ And I was like, okay, well, I need your support. Participants also stressed the importance of sustained education (both for lawmakers and the public) about what ERPOs entail and how they function in other states. Misinformation and fears of misuse were viewed as significant obstacles, and several legal professionals noted that clearer explanation of evidentiary standards and procedural protections could shift perceptions:

“It’s my understanding right now that the legislature is not largely comprised of lawyers. So that a lot of people do not have a legal background and even those that do have a legal background, most do not practice criminal law. So I think it’s important for them to be educated about what’s required.” – Participant 13, judge.

“It’s my understanding right now that the legislature is not largely comprised of lawyers. So that a lot of people do not have a legal background and even those that do have a legal background, most do not practice criminal law. So I think it’s important for them to be educated about what’s required.” – Participant 13, judge.

Some also suggested that policy development discussions might be more productive outside formal legislative sessions, through pre-legislative collaboration among historically adversarial groups to co-develop language that could withstand ideological scrutiny. One gun rights lobbyist shared his experience of how context shapes interpersonal interactions and of the legislature itself as a place that stands in the way of common ground:“Interestingly we get along extremely well at the state level. I mean we might fight it out in the committee, but then after the committee we’re in the parking lot talking about hunting and having legislators who were just anti-gun showing us their gun in their car. I mean, it’s crazy how well we get along, outside of the conference room. But as far as when we’re in the room, we’re on our sides. We’re bitter.” – Participant 5, gun rights advocate.

Beyond institutional reforms, participants described more fundamental challenges related to distrust, dehumanization, the erosion of empathy in political life, and lack of willingness to find common ground on firearm-related policies in the current environment. Many reflected on changes in the political and social climate that, in their view, have made constructive dialogue more difficult. At the same time, some identified sustained, face-to-face dialogue as a necessary—if difficult—precondition for progress. Across ideologies, participants expressed a sense of loss and a longing for connectedness, for recognizing that “humans are beings,” for understanding the varied lived experience of others, for empathy and morality, and for coming together as people: they desire “to sit down to have these conversations with each other, no matter how uncomfortable,” to find “some meeting of the minds” and to speak “to the head and the heart.”

Together these accounts suggest that participants viewed potential policy openings as contingent on both institutional recalibration (e.g., clarifying procedures, strengthening implementation capacity, and building bipartisan trust) and a shift in how firearm policy is framed and, more broadly, how political debates are conducted. Although many described these steps as extremely difficult in the current climate, they highlighted them as conditions that would need to be in place for legislation such as an ERPO law to move forward.

Discussion

With this study, we sought to understand resistance to ERPO legislation as an approach to reducing firearm-involved domestic violence against women in Louisiana, a state with one of the highest femicide rates in the nation. Using Kingdon’s Multiple Streams Framework, we organized stakeholder perspectives across the problem, policy, and politics streams to examine why ERPO proposals have been described as “dead on arrival” in the state legislature. Our findings suggest that resistance cannot be attributed solely to ideological opposition to firearm regulation but instead reflects fragmentation across all three streams, which constrains the conditions necessary for legislative change.

In the problem stream, domestic violence was widely acknowledged as a serious and persistent concern; yet stakeholders diverged on whether firearm access constitutes a defining element of that problem. For some, guns were understood as escalating lethality risk; for others, they were incidental to broader social conditions such as poverty, trauma, or moral decline. This lack of convergence around firearm-specific risk complicates efforts to frame ERPOs as a targeted response to domestic violence. Deeper understanding of the breadth and heterogeneity in perspectives on firearms and firearm ownership is needed to guide policy efforts to address firearm safety [25].

In the policy stream, ERPOs were viewed by some stakeholders as a potentially useful tool, yet concerns about procedural safeguards, due process, and interaction with existing DVRO firearm transfer laws limited consensus. Ongoing issues with implementation of Louisiana’s DRVO law since the time of its passage, including resource constraints, inconsistent enforcement, and verification difficulties, shaped skepticism and complicated discussions about the role and effectiveness of an ERPO law. In this context, perceived administrative capacity and weak infrastructure emerged as important factors influencing policy feasibility. Previous research has identified similar challenges with the effectiveness and implementation of DVRO laws in other states; [26, 27] however, in these contexts, ERPOs are perceived as a potentially more effective and timely approach to firearm removal than DVROs [28].

In the politics stream, participants’ perspectives aligned with research on increasing concentration of polarized ideology, and strong electoral dynamics that make bipartisan collaboration difficult [29, 30]. Several participants contrasted the current climate with the political conditions surrounding the 2014 passage of Louisiana’s DVRO firearm transfer law. Prior work applying the MSF to understand the surprising passage of this law in Louisiana highlighted the prominent role of domestic violence advocates as “policy entrepreneurs” (persons who raise attention to the problem and develop policy solutions) [17], the strategic absence of well-established gun control lobbying groups, and a tragic focusing event that crystalized the link between firearms and domestic violence across partisan lines [15]. Whether similar framing of ERPO laws – one that approaches firearm transfer as a means of protecting domestic violence survivors and others from fatal violence – could succeed under current conditions remains uncertain.

A case study of California’s successful passage an ERPO law in 2014 further underscores the importance of stream alignment [31]. There, a decades-long process of relationship and advocacy collation building, policy entrepreneurship, strategic framing, and iterative policy development preceded the opening of a policy window following a mass shooting. In contrast, participants in Louisiana described limited cross-ideological engagement and a sense of futility around dialogue, especially among gun regulation advocates who viewed opposition as immovable. The absence of sustained relationship-building across key stakeholder groups appears to severely constrain opportunities for similar legislative change in Louisiana.

The broader political environments in California and Louisiana stand on opposite ends of the political spectrum, with growing polarization in recent years. Gun regulation advocates we spoke to in Louisiana conveyed a sense of pointlessness around engaging with gun rights advocates and lobbying groups on ERPO legislation, a sentiment may have been informed by political activities in the state that were unfolding at the time of our interviews in 2024 and 2025. With a newly elected Republican governor and a legislative supermajority at the time, Louisiana enacted legislation significantly reducing firearm safety regulations, including a bill that allows citizens 18 and older to carry concealed handguns without a state permit and without the previously state-mandated training for concealed carry, as well as laws that protect firearm and ammunition businesses from discrimination by financial institutions and eliminate gun-free zones, allowing for firearms at parades and within a 1,000 foot perimeter around K-12 schools. In this context, firearm deregulation rather than restriction had recently dominated the state’s legislative agenda, reinforcing perceptions that ERPO legislation would be politically infeasible.

Our findings suggest substantial fracturing across streams that make an ERPO law unlikely to succeed in Louisiana’s current political climate. At the same time, we identified the conditions needed for such a policy to succeed, including long-term relationship building outside of legislative sessions, broader education about ERPO laws, and aisle-bridging collaboration on the drafting of legislation. In many states, ERPO laws are passed after a tragic focusing event opens a policy window, often a mass shooting [31]. The vast and disproportionate burden of domestic violence homicide in Louisiana could certainly constitute a focusing event itself, requiring urgent policy action. Unfortunately, people become inured to such chronic violence, and violence disproportionately impacting women and people of color [32] often goes unnoticed by the general public and, by extension, policymakers. An ERPO law would be an appropriate response to the domestic homicide situation in Louisiana, though the current policy environment makes it unlikely. It will be up to advocates, policymakers, and the public to take steps necessary to ripen the policy environment for an ERPO law; with luck, this will happen before another tragedy compels it.

This study has limitations. The findings reflect perspectives from a purposive sample of stakeholders within one state and may not generalize to other jurisdictions. Future research should assess whether similar fragmentation occurs in other states – including in those with more diverse political ideology – and how institutional and political contexts shape the feasibility of firearm risk legislation. We focused discussion of ERPOs primarily in the context of domestic violence against women and intimate partner homicide rather than exploring other problem streams, such as suicide or mass shootings, where ERPOs are commonly used [4, 8]. Although some participants referenced these domains, they were not the primary focus of inquiry. Additionally, while our study is strengthened by the diversity of perspectives across the political spectrum, conservative legislators who have consistently opposed ERPO legislation did not respond to invitations for participation, thus limiting direct insights into their perspectives. However, the ideological and professional diversity of the sample provides a nuanced view of how firearm policy debates unfold across stakeholder groups in a politically polarized state.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that in Louisiana’s resistance to ERPO legislation reflects not only political opposition but also contested problem framing on the role of guns in domestic violence against women, uncertainty about administrative feasibility, and stymied bipartisan relationships. Examining these dynamics through the Multiple Streams Framework highlights the critical importance of alignment across streams, as well as the institutional and relational conditions necessary for such alignment, to create the conditions needed for successful passage of gun safety regulations including ERPO.
