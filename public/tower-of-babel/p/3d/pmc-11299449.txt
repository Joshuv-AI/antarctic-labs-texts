M2 macrophage infusion ameliorates diabetic glomerulopathy via the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in db/db mice
Authors: Yulin Gu, Songyan Yu, Weijun Gu, Bing Li, Jing Xue, Jiejie Liu, Qi Zhang, Yaqi Yin, Haixia Zhang, Qinghua Guo, Mingxia Yuan, Zhaohui Lyu, Yiming Mu, Yu Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: To explore the therapeutic effects of M2 macrophages in diabetic nephropathy (DN) and their mechanism.Methods: We infused M2 macrophages stimulated with IL-4 into 10-week-old db/db mice once a week for 4 weeks through the tail vein as M2 group. Then we investigated the role of M2 macrophages in alleviating the infammation of DN and explored the mechanism.Results: M2 macrophages hindered the progression of DN, reduced the levels of IL-1β (DN group was 34%, M2 group was 13%, p < 0.01) and MCP-1 (DN group was 49%, M2 group was 16%, p < 0.01) in the glomeruli. It was also proven that M2 macrophages alleviate mesangial cell injury caused by a high glucose environment. M2 macrophage tracking showed that the infused M2 macrophages migrated to the kidney, and the number of M2 macrophages in the kidney reached a maximum on day 3. Moreover, the ratio of M2 to M1 macrophages was 2.3 in the M2 infusion group, while 0.4 in the DN group (p < 0.01). Mechanistically, M2 macrophages downregulated Janus kinase (JAK) 2 and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) 3 in mesangial cells.Conclusions: Multiple infusions of M2 macrophages significantly alleviated inflammation in the kidney and hindered the progression of DN at least partially by abrogating the M1/M2 homeostasis disturbances and suppressing the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in glomerular mesangial cells. M2 macrophage infusion may be a new therapeutic strategy for DN treatment.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) causes substantial global morbidity and mortality. In 2017, there were 697.5 million CKD patients worldwide, with a global prevalence of 9.1%, and 1.2 million people died of CKD [1]. Approximately 20–40% of diabetic patients suffer from diabetic nephropathy (DN), which is a major cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [2,3]. Notably, glomerular cells produce many inflammatory mediators in the diabetic milieu, especially as glomerular injury progresses, and these mediators can augment inflammatory damage and even lead to systemic effects [4]. Current treatments, including glycemic and blood pressure control and proteinuria control, can slow the development of DN, but a considerable number of patients still progress to ESRD. Therefore, identifying new therapeutic strategies and additional therapeutic targets for treating DN is crucial [5].

Macrophages are essential for nonspecific and specific immunity in different tissues and organs [6]. Macrophage infiltration has been found in the glomerulus and tubulointerstitium in rodent models of diabetes and in DN patients and plays a central role in the development of DN [7,8]. Macrophages can mediate injury, such as glomerular hyperfiltration and inflammation, during the progression of DN [9].

Phenotypically, macrophages that can secrete proinflammatory cytokines are defined as classically activated (M1) macrophages, and those found at sites of diabetic tissue injury in DN patients and rodents exhibit an M1 phenotype [10,11]. Macrophages that are capable of modulating immune responses through anti-inflammatory or resolution mechanisms are termed alternatively activated (M2) macrophages [12,13]. Based on their gene expression, M2 macrophages can be divided into several subtypes: M2a, M2b, M2c, and M2d. Each macrophage subtype is generated by exposure to different chemokines and drives a specific response. M2a cells are induced by IL-4 and play essential roles in immunoregulation, tissue repair, and inflammation inhibition by producing arginase-1 (Arg-1) and IL-4 [14]. Therefore, remodeling macrophage homeostasis may be a new therapeutic strategy for DN treatment.

M2 macrophage transfusion has been applied in many kidney diseases, such as antibody-mediated glomerular injury, adriamycin-induced nephropathy, and acute kidney injury, and has shown suitable therapeutic effects [15–18]. Although the efficacy of M2 macrophages in ameliorating the progression of kidney disease is well established, the therapeutic effects of M2 macrophages on DN are still unclear, and there are limited details on the anti-inflammatory mechanism of M2 macrophages. Moreover, it is unknown whether M2 macrophage infusion can mobilize endogenous M2 macrophages and restore the balance between M1 and M2 macrophages.

We hypothesize that M2 macrophage infusion can decrease chronic inflammation and delay the progression of DN by downregulating Janus kinase (JAK) 2 and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) 3 in the glomeruli. We infused M2 macrophages into db/db mice to verify this hypothesis. We found that multiple infusions of M2 macrophages could significantly alleviate kidney inflammation and hinder DN progression at least partially by downregulating JAK2 and STAT3 in glomerular mesangial cells.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the tibias and femurs of C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old). The BMDMs were then cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 100 ng/ml M-CSF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cell culture medium was replaced every 3 days during the first 3 days. Six days later, the macrophages were stimulated with 20 ng/ml IL-4 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for 24 h to obtain M2 macrophages. The macrophages were removed from the culture dish with a scraper and centrifuged for 5 min at 1500 rpm to prepare for the infusions.

SV40-MES-13 cells, mouse glomerular mesangial cells, were cultured in a mixture of DMEM-LG (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and F-12 Nutrient Mixture (Ham) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator (5% CO2 in air at 37 °C). SV40-MES-13 cells were cultured in a medium containing 5.5 mmol/L glucose as the control (Con) group, and those cultured in 30 mmol/L glucose were used as the high-glucose (HG) group. After being exposed to HG medium for 24 h, SV40-MES-13 cells (3 × 105) were cocultured with M2 macrophages in Transwell plates with 0.4 μm diameter inserts for an additional 48 h before analysis (M2 group).

Animal experiments

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital approved all the animal studies.

Eight-week-old male db/db mice or db/m mice were fed a normal chow diet for 2 weeks. Blood glucose and body weight were measured to evaluate the model each week, and urine was collected from db/db mice and db/m mice to analyze the urinary albumin to creatinine ratio (ACR).

At 10 weeks of age, the db/db mice were randomly assigned to the M2 group were fixed in a special device, and were treated with a 1 × 106 M2 macrophage once a week for 4 weeks through the tail vein after being disinfected with alcohol. The DN group was treated with PBS alone. One week after the treatment, intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (IPGTTs) and insulin tolerance tests (IPITTs) were conducted to assess the effects of M2 macrophages. For the IPGTTs, after the mice were fasted overnight, glucose (1 g/kg) was intraperitoneally injected, and for the IPITTs, insulin (1 U/kg) was intraperitoneally injected. Blood glucose levels were measured every 30 min until 120 min. Anesthesia was induced before animal sacrifice. Blood was collected and the plasma was obtained after centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min. Fasting blood insulin (FBI) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) were measured. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and the urinary ACR were measured by Servicebio Corporation.

The insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and homeostatic model assessment index of β cells (HOMA-β) were calculated as follows: HOMA-IR index = (FBG, mmol/L) × (FBI, mU/L)/22.5 and HOMA-β index = 20 × (FBI, mU/L)/[(FBG, mmol/L) − 3.5)].

Tracking M2 macrophages

M2 macrophages were labeled with CM-Dil (Life Technologies, Eugene, OR, USA). A random selection of 10-week-old DN mice and db/m mice were treated with CM-Dil-labeled M2 macrophages (1 × 106 CM-Dil-labeled M2 macrophages suspended in 0.2 mL of PBS) and sacrificed at 24 h, 2, 3, and 7 days after infusion. The heart, liver, pancreas, lung, kidney, and adipose tissue were removed to prepare tissue sections.

Histopathology and immunofluorescence staining

Kidneys were harvested, fixed in neutral formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Tissues were stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), Masson’s trichrome, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The morphological structure of the kidney was observed, and photomicrographs were taken under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). Glomerulosclerosis was graded using PAS-stained samples as follows: 0, normal; 1, <25% of the glomerular area; 2, 25–<50% of the glomerular area; 3, 50–<75% of the glomerular area; and 4, ≥75% of the glomerular area.

For immunofluorescence staining, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were dehydrated with 50% sucrose and embedded in an optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Six-micron-thick frozen sections and macrophages were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD11c (1:100, mouse, Abcam), Arg-1 (1:200, rabbit, Abcam), iNOS (1:300, rabbit, Abcam), F480 (1:100, mouse, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), α8 integrin (1:20, goat, R&D), IL-1β (1:100, rabbit, Abcam), MCP-1 (1:100, rabbit, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Collagen IV (1:200, rabbit, Abcam), fibronectin (1:250, rabbit, Abcam), and α-SMA (1:100, mouse, Sigma) and then incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488/594-conjugated, 1:500, Invitrogen) at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, Hoechst 33342 (1:1000, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added. A laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Germany) was used to observe the slides. At least 8 fields from each mouse were evaluated for each marker in at least three sections.

Flow cytometry

For macrophage analysis, BMDMs were washed twice and stained with antibodies against F480-FITC, Arg-1-PE-CY7 (for M2), and iNOS-APC (for M1) (BD Biosciences Pharmingen, San Diego, USA) at 4 °C for 15 min in the dark and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Plasma was obtained after centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min to analyze the levels of serum inflammatory factors. The IL-1β, MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α levels were quantified by using AimPlex™ assay kits (QuantoBio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and via flow cytometry.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The fasting blood insulin serum concentration was assessed using ELISA kits from Mercodia (Uppsala, Sweden). The urine albumin concentration was assessed using a mouse albumin ELISA kit from Abcam (MA, USA). The urine creatinine level was assessed using Parameter™ Creatinine Assay ELISA kits from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and MCP-1 levels in the supernatants of cultured SV40-MES-13 cells were measured using ELISA kits from Multi Sciences (Hangzhou, China). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from BMDMs, SV40-MES-13 cells, and kidney tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then reverse-transcribed to cDNA with a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Scientific, CA, USA). qRT–PCR was performed on an ABI Prism StepOnePlus thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) using SYBR Green PT–PCR reagent (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The reaction was performed at 95 °C for 30 min, followed by 40 cycles of 62 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s. The melting curve of the target genes confirmed primer specificity. β-Actin was used as the internal control. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from kidney tissue and SV40-MES-13 cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. The following antibodies were used: total or phosphorylated JAK2 (p-JAK2) (3230S and 3776S, 1:1000; rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA); total or phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) (9139S, 1:1000; mouse, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA; 9145S, 1:2000; rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA); β-tubulin (TA-10, 1:2000; mouse, ZSGB-Bio, China); and β-actin (TA-09, 1:2000; mouse, ZSGB-Bio, China). Secondary antibodies, including horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-rat IgG, were purchased from ZSGB-Bio. The blots were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Lentiviral transduction

Recombinant lentiviral vectors encoding a mouse JAK2 mimic and negative control were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). An MOI of 100 was selected with an infection efficiency of 80%. SV40-MES-13 cells were seeded in a six-well plate, transduced with the lentiviruses in serum-free DMEM-LG supplemented with HitransG A for 4 h and then supplemented with medium supplemented with 20% FBS. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with low-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS. Subsequent experiments were carried out when the viral transduction efficiency exceeded 80%.

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the means ± SDs from at least three independent samples. Six mice were selected per group in vivo. Three mice were selected to perform M2 macrophage tracking per group. Two-tailed Student’s t-tests or rank sum tests were used to assess differences between the mean values of the two groups. One-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA were used to compare the data among more than two groups. The chi-square test was used to analyze the ratios. Group differences at the level of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0.0.0 software. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and Adobe Illustrator CC 23.0.1 (Adobe Illustrator CC, San Jose, CA, USA).

Results

M2 macrophage infusion ameliorated renal dysfunction and histological injury in db/db mice

Macrophages were isolated from bone marrow, stimulated with IL-4 to obtain M2 macrophages, and identified by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence using F4/80, Arg-1, and iNOS (Supplementary Figure 1). The flow cytometry analysis of M0 cells revealed that the percentages of F4/80-, Arg-1-, and iNOS-positive cells were 99.01, 2.05, and 2.26%, respectively (Supplementary Figure 1(A)). In comparison, those of M2 cells were 99.42, 98.41, and 2.3%, respectively (Supplementary Figure 1(B)). The immunofluorescence results were similar (Supplementary Figures 1(C–E)). H&E and PAS staining of 10-week-old db/db mouse tissue revealed glomerular hypertrophy, slight mesangial cell hyperplasia, mesangial matrix expansion, and glycogen deposition (Supplementary Figures 2(A,B)). The urinary ACR was slightly increased (Supplementary Figure 2(C)). Ten-week-old db/db mice were used to construct the DN model and were randomly separated into two groups: the M2 group was treated with an infusion of M2 macrophages (1 × 106) suspended in 0.2 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) once per week for 4 weeks, and the DN group was treated with an infusion of 0.2 mL of PBS alone (Figures 1(A,B)). Furthermore, db/m mice were used as healthy controls.

To assess whether M2 macrophages ameliorate kidney injury, we measured biochemical indicators of renal function, analyzed renal histology, and performed transmission electron microscopy. As expected, the DN model mice presented higher serum creatinine (43.90 μmol/l) and BUN (41.92 μmol/l) levels and higher urinary ACRs (23.18 ng/mg) than the control mice. Treatment with M2 macrophages resulted in significant reductions in the serum creatinine (23.33 μmol/l) and BUN (35.43 μmol/l) levels and the urinary ACR (9.59 ng/mg) (Figures 1(C–E)). Histologically and quantitatively, glomerular size and mesangial expansion were significantly greater in DN model mice than in control mice, and these changes were markedly reduced by M2 macrophage treatment (Figures 1(F–H)). In addition, DN model mice exhibited a significant increase in podocyte foot process effacement, but these effects were attenuated by M2 macrophages (Figures 1(I,J)). Although the effect was nonsignificant, M2 macrophages tended to ameliorate the thickening of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) (Figure 1(K)). Insulin resistance is determined by IPITTs and the HOMA-IR index, while islet function is measured by IPGTTs and the HOMA-β index (Supplementary Figures 3(A–D)). Surprisingly, we found that insulin resistance and sensitivity did not significantly improve in the M2 group, and there was no change in weight or FBG levels (Supplementary Figures 3(E,F)). These findings suggest that M2 macrophage infusion can ameliorate kidney injury in db/db mice independently of blood glucose.

M2 macrophage infusion suppressed inflammation in glomerular mesangial cells

Enhanced inflammatory processes appear to promote the occurrence and development of DN. Mesangial cell inflammation is a histological feature that is usually observed in patients with type 2 diabetes-associated kidney damage and precedes glomerulosclerosis development in DN [19–21]. To further explore whether M2 macrophage infusion could ameliorate renal function and histological injury, we next analyzed the levels of inflammatory factors in mesangial cells. The results showed that the percentage of the total area affected by IL-1β-positive cells in the DN group was 34%, while that in the M2 group was only 13% (Figures 2(A,B)). The percentage of cells expressing MCP-1 in DN model mice was 49%, which was more than 3-fold higher than the percentage of MCP-1+ cells in the M2 group mice (Figures 2(C,D)). The gene expression of proinflammatory cytokines was quantified, and the results were consistent with the immunostaining results (Figure 2(E)). Then, we measured the serum levels of certain proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Compared with control mice, DN model mice presented markedly higher levels of IL-1β, MCP-1, and IL-6 (Figure 2(F)). The levels of all the measured proinflammatory cytokines decreased after M2 macrophage infusion.

To further explore whether M2 macrophages can alleviate inflammation in mesangial cells, we performed in vitro experiments in SV40-MES-13 cells. ELISA was used to measure the levels of proinflammatory cytokines in the supernatants. The levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and MCP-1 were increased in the HG group, and these effects were abrogated by coculturing the cells with M2 macrophages (Figures 2(G–I)). The gene expression results showed similar trends (Figure 2(J)).

M2 macrophage infusion restored the M1/M2 macrophage balance

To observe whether the infused M2 macrophages migrated to target organs to ameliorate inflammation, M2 macrophage tracking was performed. Twenty-four hours after infusion, a small number of M2 macrophages were detected in the lung (Figure 3(C)), spleen (Figure 3(A)), and liver (Figure 3(B)) of DN model mice. Little M2 macrophage engraftment was observed in the adipose tissue, pancreas, kidney (Figure 3(D)), or myocardium (data not shown). During the following days, the numbers of M2 macrophages in the spleen, liver, and kidney gradually increased, reached a maximum on day 3, and then decreased. The number of M2 macrophages in the lung reached a maximum on day 2 and then decreased. Although the number of M2 macrophages in the kidney was increased on day 3 in DN model mice compared with db/m mice (Figure 3(E)), the absolute count of M2 macrophages was still too low to account for the significant therapeutic effect on kidney disease. These trends were similar in db/m mice, except that the time at which the number of cells reached a maximum was the second day (Figure 3(E)).

Based on these results, we further investigated the role of macrophage homeostasis in ameliorating inflammation in DN. CD11c was used to identify M1 macrophages, and CD163 was used to identify M2 macrophages. The results showed that the number of CD11c-positive cells was increased in DN model mice but was decreased in M2 macrophage-treated mice (Figures 3(F,G)). CD163+ cells were increased in M2 macrophage-treated mice but decreased in DN model mice (Figures 3(H,I)). Accordingly, the ratio of M2 to M1 macrophages increased significantly in the M2 infusion group (Figure 3(J)). In the M2 infusion group, the expression of the gene encoding CD11c was downregulated, while the expression of the gene encoding Arg-1 was upregulated (Figures 3(K,L)). Similar conclusions were drawn on day 3 of M2 macrophage infusion (data are not shown). These results showed that the effect of M2 macrophages on DN does not totally depend on the migration of M2 macrophages to the kidney. Moreover, M2 macrophage infusion restored the balance between M1/M2 macrophages in the kidney.

The anti-inflammatory effect of M2 macrophages was mediated by the downregulation of JAK2/STAT3

Recent studies have shown that the JAK/STAT pathway is activated in numerous acute and chronic renal diseases. Moreover, hyperglycemia induces the activation of JAK2 and STAT3 in glomerular mesangial cells, promoting the onset of DN [22,23]. We subsequently investigated whether M2 macrophages alleviate kidney inflammation by downregulating JAK2/STAT3. The p-JAK2/JAK2 ratio was more than 4-fold greater in DN model mice than in M2 group mice (Figures 4(A,B)). The p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio was also elevated in the DN group and decreased in the M2 group (Figures 4(A,C)). Consistently, the p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3 ratios decreased in high glucose-treated SV40-MES-13 cells after M2 macrophage coculture (Figures 4(D–F)). To further determine whether M2 macrophages function through the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, SV40-MES-13 cells were transduced with a JAK2-overexpressing virus. JAK2-mimicking virus increased the protein level of p-STAT3 (Supplementary Figures 4(A–C)). After transduction with a JAK2-overexpressing virus, the therapeutic effects of M2 macrophages on fibrosis and inflammation were completely inhibited. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the percentage of Col-4+ cells or Fibronectin+ cells increased after stimulation with high glucose, but the therapeutic effect on M2 macrophages did not change after transduction of the JAK2 NC. However, these effects could be blocked after transduction of the JAK2 mimic (Figures 5(A–D)). The PCR results were similar to the immunofluorescence staining results (Figure 5(E)). The ELISA results showed that the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1, and IL-6 in the supernatants of cultured SV40-MES-13 cells were elevated after transduction with the JAK2 mimic (Figures 5(F–I)). These results revealed that M2 macrophages alleviated kidney fibrosis and inflammation by downregulating JAK2/STAT3 expression.

M2 macrophages prevented the accumulation of glomerular extracellular matrix (ECM) components

It is well known that hyperglycemia induces inflammation and the overproduction of ECM components, ultimately leading to glomerulosclerosis [24]. In DN, mesangial cells are important target and effector cells and are the primary cell type responsible for the generation of the ECM [25]. The massive deposition of collagen IV (Col-4) and fibronectin are essential components of the ECM and are critical indicators of diabetic renal fibrosis [26]. To observe whether M2 macrophage infusion can ameliorate fibrosis caused by a long-term inflammatory response, we examined the protein level of Col-4 in all groups. α8-Integrin was used to identify mesangial cells. As shown in Figure 6, glomerular Col-4 deposition was evident in DN model mice and was markedly attenuated by M2 macrophages (Figures 6(A,B)). Immunostaining for fibronectin also revealed upregulated expression in DN model mice, whereas the percentage of mesangial cells coexpressing fibronectin in the M2 group decreased by ∼38.3% (Figures 6(C,D)). Consistent with the immunostaining data, kidney Col-4, and fibronectin mRNA levels were significantly higher in DN model mice than in control mice and were significantly lower in the M2 group (Figure 6(E)). To further explore whether M2 macrophages can alleviate ECM deposition by mesangial cells, we performed in vitro experiments. Immunofluorescence revealed that the expression levels of Col-4, fibronectin, and α-SMA increased after high glucose stimulation and decreased after the cells were cocultured with M2 macrophages (Figures 6(F–I), Supplementary Figures 5(A,B)). Real-time PCR analysis revealed upregulated expression of Col-4, fibronectin, and α-SMA in the high glucose group and downregulated expression of these genes in the M2 group (Supplementary Figure 5(C)).

Discussion

In current time, the management in DN still focuses on the standard therapy, like glucose and blood pressure control, weight loss, lipid lowering, and smoking cessation. However, only a few drugs help to reduce the inflammatory response and decrease urinary ACR, resulting in better renal and cardiovascular disease outcomes [27,28]. Therefore, new drugs targeting DN pathomechanisms have become a major focus for the development of new therapies. Our study found that infusion of M2 macrophages played an important role in reducing inflammation and albuminuria in diabetic nephropathy.

M1 macrophage accumulation is closely associated with chronic renal injury, and increasing evidence has shown that inhibiting macrophages and/or their secretory products may be a potential therapeutic strategy for DN [29,30]. However, whether the infusion of M2 macrophages can directly ameliorate DN has not been clearly illustrated. In this study, we provided experimental support for our hypothesis that M2 macrophage infusion can reduce inflammatory infiltration partially by suppressing the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and abrogating M1/M2 homeostasis disturbances in the kidney, thereby preventing glomerulosclerosis, alleviating proteinuria and maintaining renal function in DN model mice.

The functions of M2 macrophages are complex and have not been fully elucidated. In recent years, M2 macrophages have been reported to play a role in the amelioration of pathological conditions, such as autoimmune diseases, inflammatory diseases, and cancer [31–33]. Du et al. reported that CD206+ M2 macrophages may have therapeutic potential against antibody-mediated glomerular injury and may be useful for the treatment of crescentic nephritis in humans [15]. However, the anti-inflammatory effect of M2 macrophage infusion derived from the bone marrow in DN has rarely been reported. Our study showed that M2 macrophage treatment reduced renal inflammation in DN patients.

Macrophage homeostasis is a critical factor in maintaining kidney function. Type 2 diabetes mellitus is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, which is accompanied by enhanced M1-like macrophage polarization and increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines [34]. Our study showed that when macrophages were administered by venous injection first to the lung and then to the liver and spleen, the infiltration of infused M2 macrophages into the kidney significantly increased on day 3, while the total number of M2 macrophages in the glomeruli was low and inconsistent. Therefore, the anti-inflammatory effect of M2 macrophage infusion could not be explained by the migration of the cells to the injured kidney. Surprisingly, we found that most of the macrophages that infiltrated the renal tubules in DN model mice and the M1/M2 ratio in the kidney were significantly increased after M2 macrophage infusion no matter on day 3 of M2 macrophage infusion or at the end of the study. Previous studies demonstrated that co-cultured M1 macrophage with M2 macrophage reduced the M1 macrophage expression of TNF-α and iNOS, and elevated the expression of Arg and CD206, and the M2 phenotype can be stable [15,17]. The similar conclusions also have been reached in vivo, which include both resident and infiltrating macrophages [17]. Though the infiltration of infused M2 macrophages reached a maximum on day 3, the M1/M2 ratio didn’t increase significantly than that at the end of the study, this may be explained by the accumulation effect of M2 macrophages. Moreover, we found that the serum levels of certain proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1, and IL-6, decreased after macrophage infusion. These findings revealed that M2 macrophages may be crucial effectors for reducing systemic inflammation to ameliorate DN and affect the polarization of tissue-resident macrophages. Therefore, chronic inflammation may be reduced by establishing macrophage homeostasis in the kidney. All of these effects appeared to occur independently of insulin sensitivity.

In DN, hyperglycemia activates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling cascade in glomerular mesangial cells, which is involved in the inflammatory response and leads to kidney injury, increasing the secretion of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [23,35]. Both in vivo and in vitro, high JAK2/STAT3 levels in DN are associated with disease progression [25,36], and suppressing the JAK/STAT pathway could protect against inflammation and injury in diabetic kidney diseases [36–38]. Although STAT3 plays a vital role in cell survival and proliferation, persistent activation of STAT3 is associated with various pathological conditions, including inflammation and fibrosis [39,40], and STAT3 induces collagen production [39,41,42]. Our results supported this notion, and JAK2/STAT3 expression was upregulated in DN and associated with increased inflammation, Col-4 expression, and fibronectin expression. After M2 macrophage infusion, the expression of JAK2, STAT3, and p-STAT3 was downregulated, and the levels of cytokines, chemokines, Col-4, and fibronectin were decreased. In cultured mesangial cells, the JAK2 mimic upregulated STAT3 and contributed significantly to inflammation, collagen production, and fibrosis. While it is currently unknown how the M2 macrophages work, possibly by secreting certain substances, like extracellular vesicles, or due to the restoration of macrophages balance which leads to the reduction of inflammation cytokines, or maybe achieved by downregulating other pathways, such as oxidative stress, nuclear factor kappa B and so on.

One limitation of our study is that the dose-concentration effect of macrophage numbers and outcomes needs to be clarified. Moreover, how many transplanted cells that survive to exert their therapeutic effects after infusion is unknown. Additional studies are needed to clarify these issues.

Conclusions

In summary, treatment with M2 macrophages effectively mitigated renal injury in db/db mice, possibly by modulating renal inflammation and ECM deposition caused by diabetes mellitus and abrogating M1/M2 macrophage homeostasis disturbances. Moreover, these effects were attributed to the downregulation of the JAK2/STAT3 by M2 macrophages. We highlighted that M2 macrophages can potentially be used as a cell therapy for DN.
