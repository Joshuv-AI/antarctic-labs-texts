Revisiting the role of local treatments in the era of targeted therapy and immunotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jia-jia Du, Fan Yang, Wei Dong, Zun-yi Zhang, Qi Cheng, Er-lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advent of targeted therapy and immunotherapy has revolutionised the management of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients with all stages, dramatically improving their survival outcomes. Currently, radical resection is still the preferred first-line treatment for early-stage HCC, nevertheless, the surgical outcomes remain unsatisfactory due to high recurrence rate of 70% within 5 years after surgery. Moreover, up to two thirds of diagnosed HCC patients are in the advanced stages of the disease, exhibiting intrahepatic or extrahepatic metastases and vascular invasion. In recent years, the combination of surgical and other local treatments with targeted therapy and immunotherapy has dramatically improved the overall survival for HCC patients and also increased the complexity of HCC management, demanding a dynamic adaptation of the available staging-based strategies and flexible therapeutic allocation. In this review, we mainly elaborate the fundamental principles and recent advancements in the surgical management of locally advanced HCC, such as neoadjuvant, adjuvant and conversion therapy, as well as the regulatory effects of local treatments on targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Finally, the value of splenectomy for unresectable HCC patients with hypersplenism is also discussed.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Surgical treatments, Targeted therapy, Immunotherapy, Local therapy

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality on a global scale and the primary cause of death among patients with cirrhosis [1]. Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection represents the most prevalent risk factor for HCC, accounting for about 54% of cases globally [2]. The selection of treatment options for HCC is based on the assessment of liver function, tumor burden and performance status. These tumor- and patient-centric features define the staging of the cancer, with each stage having a distinct prognosis [3]. Therefore, clinical practice guidelines based on tumor presentation and liver function integrate the available evidence according to well-defined clinical trials and recommend treatment strategies for each stage [4, 5] (Fig. 1). Several treatment approaches have been adopted as the standards, including liver transplantation, resection and local ablation for early-stage tumors; transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) for intermediate-stage tumors; and systemic agents for advanced-stage tumors (Sorafenib, Regorafenib, Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab) [6, 7]. The implementation of these guidelines has resulted in a significant increase in life expectancy. Patients with early-stage HCC now have a median overall survival (OS) of over 5 years, while those with intermediate-stage disease have 20–30 months and patients with advanced HCC have 10–19 months [8].

In recent years, the emergence of targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy has brought great progress in the management of HCC, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) has been demonstrated to enhance immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment, increasing the function of regulatory T cells and inhibiting effector T cells [9]. In comparison to Sorafenib, the IMbrave150 trial demonstrated that the combination of Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab resulted in a prolonged OS (19.2 mo vs. 13.4 mo, p = 0.0009) and progression-free survival (PFS) (6.9 mo vs. 4.3 mo, p = 0.0001) [10]. More recently, the phase III trial demonstrated that the combination of Durvalumab and Tremelimumab offers a survival benefit, and it is anticipated to be integrated as an alternative to the combination of Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab in the first line setting [11] (Fig. 1).

With the advancement of surgical techniques and the development of targeted therapy and immunotherapy, the individualized and comprehensive management of HCC is gradually taking shape. Local therapy has been demonstrated to reduce tumor burden and induce the release of proinflammatory cytokines and tumor antigens. Conversely, VEGF-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (VEGF-TKIs) enhance anti-tumor immunity of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (Fig. 2). Each combination therapy targets a different stage of HCC, yet there is currently a paucity of evidence to support the use of this combination therapy. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a large number of clinical and basic research to improve this situation. The objective of this review is to describe the current status of the combination of surgical and targeted therapy and immunotherapy in HCC, as well as future perspectives on combined approaches.

Perioperative targeted therapy and immunotherapy

Surgical resection of the tumor is a potential treatment modality, but the 5-year recurrence rate following surgical intervention is in excess of 70% [12]. The risk of relapse following resection is increased in patients with large tumors or microvascular invasion [13]. In general, the recurrence of the primary tumor within 2 years may be attributed to its residual and aggressive nature, whereas recurrence beyond this period is mainly due to the occurrence of new liver cancer associated with hepatitis or cirrhosis over the years [14]. In addition, poor liver function reserve and high frequency of diagnosis at advanced stages result in a significant number of patients being ineligible for surgical resection, and only 20% of HCC patients are suitable for radical resection [15]. Accordingly, patients with HCC are treated with perioperative targeted therapy and immunotherapy to improve surgical resectability, reduce postoperative recurrence and ultimately improve clinical prognosis.

Neoadjuvant therapy

Neoadjuvant therapy is indicated for patients with technically resectable liver cancer (complete resection with sufficient liver remnant) who have a high risk of recurrence. Preoperative systemic treatment is performed to shrink the tumor, eliminate invisible small lesions as soon as possible, and increase the surgical margin, so as to reduce the postoperative recurrence rate and thus prolong recurrence-free survival (RFS). In addition, neoadjuvant systemic therapy regimens may provide useful information about tumor sensitivity to anticancer agents [16] (Table 1).

18 predicted resectable HCC patients were enrolled in an open-label, single-arm phase II study. The neoadjuvant treatment regimen consisted of Camrelizumab plus Apatinib was given for 21 days, followed by 8 additional adjuvant cycles after surgery. After neoadjuvant therapy, 17 patients underwent surgery, of whom 3 had > 90% tumor necrosis and 1 had a pathological complete response. 13 patients received adjuvant therapy and the 1-year RFS was 53.9% (95%CI: 24.8–76.0). Patients with > 90% tumor necrosis had a higher RFS rate [17].

In the study by Marron et al., 21 resectable HCC patients received 2 cycles of Cemiplimab prior to liver resection (LR) and 8 additional cycles after surgery. Of the 20 patients who underwent successful resection, 4 achieved > 70% tumor necrosis and 7 were classified as responders, defined as > 50% tumor necrosis. All responders had significant immune infiltration compared with non-responders. Increased immune infiltration was also detected in responders before treatment initiation, suggesting that immune infiltration can be used as a potential predictive biomarker for response to ICIs therapy [18].

In a cohort of 27 resectable HCC patients taking Nivolumab with or without Ipilimumab, Kaseb et al. assessed the safety and tolerability of this treatment approach. 20 patients underwent resection and adverse events did not delay LR. In the resected tissue, 6 patients had > 70% necrosis: 3 of 9 patients who received Nivolumab monotherapy contrast with 3 of 11 patients who received Nivolumab and Ipilimumab. The estimated median PFS was 9.4 months and 19.53 months respectively. During 2 years of follow-up, patients with > 70% tumor necrosis did not relapse, while about half of those with less than 70% relapsed [19].

However, for some HCC patients who are not sensitive to targeted therapy and immunotherapy, neoadjuvant therapy will lead to tumor progression or even loss of the opportunity for surgical treatment. Therefore, it is of particular importance to identify biomarkers that can anticipate the efficacy of targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

In a single-arm phase Ib study of 15 locally advanced HCC patients who received Cabozantinib and Nivolumab, 12 (80%) patients received significant surgery after neoadjuvant therapy. All procedures resulted in margin-negative resections. Five resected tumors showed more than 90% necrosis [22].

In an observational study from China, 63 patients with initially unresectable HCC received a similar combination of TKIs and ICIs in neoadjuvant therapy, with 10 patients undergoing resection. Of these 10 patients, 6 had a partial response preoperatively and 6 had a complete pathological response [23].

Wu et al. hypothesised that Lenvatinib may inhibit antitumor immunity and angiogenesis in HCC, increase the proliferative influence of antidrug PD-1 antibody, and counteract the proangiogenic effect of TACE, which may itself enhance the efficacy of PD-1 antibody by releasing tumor-specific antigens. The combination of Lenvatinib, anti-PD-1 antibody and TACE was shown to be beneficial for downstaging unresectable HCC, resulting in a high tumor response rate and significantly improved OS [30].

However, we are faced with another problem. When conversion therapy has achieved good results, whether HCC patients can be exempted from surgical treatment. The TALENTop study (NCT04649489) was designed to assess the safety and effectiveness of hepatectomy in HCC patients with macrovascular invasion (MVI) after initial treatment with Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab. Patients with successful conversion were randomly assigned to the surgery group (receiving Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab for 1 year after hepatectomy) or the non-surgery group (continuing Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab) in a 1:1 ratio. So far, of 201 patients, 73 patients (36.3%) have been successfully converted [31]. We look forward to a good guiding result on whether hepatectomy with Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab provides more benefit for HCC patients with MVI than with Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab alone.

TKIs-based adjuvant therapy

Bruix et al. valuated adjuvant therapy with Sorafenib or placebo in 1114 HCC patients following LR (n = 900) or RFA (n = 214) in the STORM trial. In contrast with placebo, Sorafenib did not meaningfully prolong RFS of HCC (33.3 mo vs. 33.7 mo, p = 0.26) and adverse events were more frequent (28% vs. <1%) [33].

In a recent study, Lin et al. assessed the efficacy of combination therapy with TACE and TKIs, including Sorafenib, Lenvatinib, and Apatinib in HCC patients at high risk of early recurrence following LR. Compared with postoperative TACE alone (26.8% and 18.3%, respectively), the combination of adjuvant TACE + TKIs after LR significantly improved the 1-year and 2-year RFS rates (45.5% and 34.9%, respectively) [34].

TKIs reduce the risk of early recurrence by enhancing the clearance of occult residual disease at the systemic level. However, targeted drugs are no longer recommended for patients with early-stage HCC without lesions after radical resection.

ICIs-based adjuvant therapy

Adjuvant therapy with TKIs targeting angiogenesis alone may not be effective. In addition to high tumor burden and microvascular invasion, numerous immune mechanisms have also been demonstrated to contribute to the recurrence of HCC. ICIs can immune function, activate the immune surveillance process, effectively prevent the occurrence of metastases or new tumors and reduce the late recurrence rate [41]. Patients with a high risk of recurrence may derive the greatest advantage from ICIs [42].

IMbrave 050 is a multicentre, randomised phase III trial that assesses the efficacy of combining the Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab with the goal of reducing the risk of recurrence in HCC patients who are at high risk of recurrence following therapeutic resection or ablation. The primary endpoint of RFS was significantly improved (HR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.98, p = 0.012), and OS is not yet available [43]. However, with the extension of follow-up time, although the Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab prolonged the RFS, it did not reduce the recurrence rate. The current data do not support the use of Atezolizumab and Bevacizumab as postoperative adjuvant therapy for HCC.

Adjuvant therapy with adoptive immunotherapy (AIT) such as autologous formalin-fixed tumor vaccine [35], autologous lymphocytes [36], autologous CIK cells [37] and DC vaccine [38] has been shown to prevent recurrence and improve RFS and OS. AIT can offset latent intrahepatic or extrahepatic tumor spread associated with early recurrence, yet does not exert an influence on new tumorigenesis and late recurrence caused by cirrhosis [44].

Local therapy combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

Targeted therapy and immunotherapy combined with local therapy is a good option for inoperable HCC patients. HCC is a local manifestation of a systemic disease. Studies have found that the combined application of systemic therapy and local therapy is a more scientific and effective individualized treatment strategy. However, targeted therapy and immunotherapy combined with local therapy needs to consider the applicable population, application time and possible additive toxicity, and standard guidelines need to be proposed urgently (Table 4).

Ablation combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

For patients with unresectable HCC and high risk factors for recurrence (large tumor, multinodular tumor, macroscopic or microscopic vascular invasion), adjuvant targeted therapy and immunotherapy is required to improve the efficacy after ablation. Ablative therapy has a synergistic effect with ICIs-based immunotherapy [59]. Ablation is the procedure of discharging tumor-associated antigens, which enhances the immune reaction against the tumor cells [60], and in turn ICIs may prove more efficacious in the treatment of micrometastatic remaining tumor cells, such as in HCC patients with small tumors following ablation.

A prospective study of 32 advanced HCC patients demonstrated a notable increase in the number of CD8 + T cells within the tumor following RFA in conjunction with the anti-cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA4) antibody Tremelimumab [45]. The median OS was 12.3 months, and 6 months-, 12 months-PFS was 57.1% and 33.1%, respectively.

Nevertheless, new evidence suggests that inadequate RFA may improve the accumulation of cytokines and immunosuppressive cells in remaining tumors, leading to tumor progression and anti-PD-L1 resistance [61]. It is therefore evident that further studies are required in order to ascertain the safety and effectiveness of the combined treatment strategy of ablation and ICIs, and that high-quality evidence is needed in order to inform this research.

TACE combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

Despite TACE being the established standard of care for intermediate-stage HCC, only about 50% of patients achieve an objective response rate (ORR). Furthermore, the majority of these patients ultimately experience local and distant progression. Although tumor necrosis can be promoted by embolization of tumor-feeding arteries, treatment-caused hypoxia may act as a mediator of angiogenesis, thereby significantly reducing the efficacy of TACE [62]. Antiangiogenic agents, including TKIs or VEGF antibodies, may delay tumor revascularisation and recurrence after TACE. In addition, tumor necrosis caused by TACE may result in the liberation of tumor- related antigens, which in turn motivate the tumor microenvironment and activate particular immune responses [63], which may enhance the immune system programmed by immunotherapy drugs against cancer cells.

In Japan, the TACTIS trial randomised 156 patients with intermediate-stage HCC to receive TACE alone or in combination with Sorafenib. The results demonstrated that Sorafenib improved PFS (25.2 mo vs. 13.5 mo, p = 0.006) but had no significant impact on OS (36.2 mo vs. 30.8 mo, p = 0.400) [46]. Nevertheless, antiangiogenic agents, including Sorafenib [47], Brivanib [48] and Bevacizumab [64], have not demonstrated a survival benefit when combined with TACE in controlled clinical trials.

In a retrospective multicentre research of 323 advanced HCC patients, median OS was longer in patients treated with TACE plus Nivolumab than in those treated with Nivolumab alone (35.1 mo vs. 16.1 mo), although the results were not statistically different [65].

Yang et al. found that TACE with Regorafenib and ICIs resulted in longer PFS and OS compared with Regorafenib and ICIs [49].

SBRT combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is a form of external beam radiation therapy (EBRT). Cell death caused by ionizing radiation is immunogenic. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential of Sorafenib as a radiosensitizer [66]. In principle, it has been demonstrated in preclinical models of EBRT that an increase in the radiation dose to the tumor also correlates with an enhanced probability of eliciting a favourable immune response, including the induction of abstract effects [67].

In a recent prospective study, Chiang et al. demonstrated the potential efficacy of a combined treatment approach involving EBRT, TACE and Avelumab (anti-PD-L1) for the management of locally advanced HCC. In a total of 33 patients, 64% exhibited macroscopic venous invasion, 12% were amenable to conversion to curative intent therapy, and 42% demonstrated a complete radiological response [50].

However, local radiotherapy may intervene the immune response in the tumor microenvironment, thereby resulting in immunogenic cell death [66]. Therefore, the synergistic therapeutic effect of radiotherapy and targeted therapy and immunotherapy needs to be further investigated.

SIRT combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

Yttrium-90sirt selective internal radiation therapy (Y-90 SIRT) is also referred to as transarterial radioembolization (TARE). Similar to TACE, the hypoxia induced by TARE increases the production of VEGF, an effect that can be antagonised by VEGF inhibitors by preventing tumor revascularisation. In addition, VEGF can protect endothelial cells from radiation-induced damage, thereby reducing their radiosensitivity [68]. Blocking VEGF may therefore improve the response to radiation. TARE also appears to modulate the immune microenvironment in HCC, and the liberation of tumor-associated antigens during TARE may result in the activation of an immune reaction [63], which in turn may enhance the efficacy of immunotherapeutic drugs against cancer cells.

A retrospective report showed that the combination of Sorafenib and TARE conferred a survival advantage in advanced HCC patients when compared to Sorafenib monotherapy [69]. Nevertheless, a phase II trial comparing Sorafenib plus TARE with Sorafenib did not yield a survival advantage [51].

In a prospective trial, 40 HCC patients received TARE followed by Nivolumab every 21 days [52]. 2 patients experienced grade 3/4 treatment-related adverse events, and 5 patients experienced serious adverse events. The ORR for these patients was 30.6%.

HAIC combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

The combination of Sorafenib and hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) may prove beneficial for advanced HCC patients, with Sorafenib stabilising the disease and HAIC shrinking tumors. A randomised controlled phase III trial in a total of 205 advanced HCC patients compared Sorafenib with HAIC of Sorafenib plus cisplatin plus fluorouracil. Median OS was similar in the Sorafenib plus HAIC (n = 102) group and Sorafenib alone (n = 103) group (11.8 mo vs. 11.5 mo, p = 0.955). Grade 3/4 adverse events that were more common in the Sorafenib plus HAIC group than in the Sorafenib monotherapy group included anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia and anorexia [53].

Splenectomy combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy

In China, most HCC is caused by chronic liver disease, predominantly HBV infection. This is frequently along with clinical portal hypertension (PH). PH can induce splenomegaly and hypersplenism, which is defined as an enlarged spleen with impaired function. Indeed, advanced HCC patients may present with two distinct forms of liver status: malignancy and cirrhosis-related hypersplenism. At the same time, clinicians in these cases are faced with pancytopenia due to splenic hyperemia and therapeutic system-related haematotoxicity. Therefore, in the current era of combined systemic and local therapy, management of pancytopenia caused by hypersplenism and myelosuppression is essential to treat advanced HCC patients with hypersplenism, particularly those with a history of bleeding esophagogastric varices. In addition, HCC patients may be unable to undergo radical resection or early local treatment due to severe hyperplasia and hypersplenism, which can result in pancytopenia. This is an important consideration when evaluating the systemic treatment of advanced HCC [70].

Hypersplenism not only prevents a proportion of patients with advanced HCC from receiving targeted therapy and immunotherapy, but also reduces treatment response and survival. A splenectomy has been demonstrated to improve hypersplenism and liver dysfunction, while also reducing the tendency for esophagogastric variceal bleeding, and providing more options for subsequent treatment. Additionally, splenectomy represents a definitive solution for those afflicted with leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, which may eliminate the interruption or dose reduction of targeted therapy and immunotherapy in advanced HCC patients [71]. Platelets have been reported to play an important role in liver regeneration following partial hepatectomy in animal models [72]. Platelet counts increase rapidly after splenectomy, which may promote liver regeneration and facilitate recovery of liver function. Platelets may also delay the toxic effects of chronic fibrosis in animal models [73].

In addition, splenectomy helps to improve liver fibrosis and restore immune function. The immune status of patients with cirrhosis is usually compromised as a consequence of concomitant hypersplenism, which may affect the anti-tumor efficacy of ICIs. Studies have shown that a lower ratio of CD4+/CD8 + T cells within the tumor at the time of diagnosis is connected with a superior OS rate in patients with advanced HCC receiving targeted therapy and immunotherapy [74]. Splenectomy reduces the CD4+/CD8 + T cell ratio, resulting in a decrease in the number of CD4 + T cells and an increase in the number of CD8 + T cells. This may, in turn, enhance the anti-tumor immune response. Treg cells are an immunosuppressive factor. A cohort study showed that a lower percentage of Treg cells and a higher number of CD4+/CD8 + T cells were associated with anti-PD-L1 responses [75]. The spleen has also been reported to be an important source of Treg cells in hypersplenism [76]. Splenectomy appears to improve the immune response to ICIs by reducing Treg cells in the spleen and peripheral blood.

According to the above evidence, splenectomy maybe a selective treatment for advanced HCC patients with severe hypersplenism. It may improve the immune environment of HCC and permanently solve the hypersplenism which could enhance the effect of systemic therapy for unresectable HCC patients. The advancement of surgical techniques and equipment, particularly in the field of laparoscopic surgery, is facilitating the performance of splenectomy with greater ease and safety in liver surgery centres. Splenectomy may represent a promising clinical approach for advanced HCC patients with hypersplenism, particularly those with impaired liver function and esophagogastric varices. Therefore, we speculate that splenectomy may improve the efficacy of targeted therapy and immunotherapy and prolong the survival of patients with unresectable HCC with portal hypertension, and we registered a multicenter prospective clinical trial (NCT06280313).

Conclusions and prospect

The considerable heterogeneity of tumours represents a significant challenge in formulating a standardised treatment strategy for HCC. With the continuous breakthrough in biotechnology, NGS technology is expected to provide a solid basis for the diagnosis and treatment of HCC at the gene level. Therefore, future HCC treatment will be individualised management based on tumor burden and liver function. Increasing evidence supports the complementary role of surgical treatment combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy in different stages of HCC. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy can improve surgical resectability and reduce postoperative recurrence. Local therapy can reduce tumor burden, lead to the liberation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and tumor antigens, and improve the efficacy of targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Therefore, we suggest that early stage HCC patients with high risk factors for recurrence should receive adjuvant targeted therapy and immunotherapy after surgery, and splenectomy is feasible in unresectable HCC patients with hypersplenism. In addition, there is still a lack of standards for triple therapy of surgical treatment combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy. It is imperative that a substantial number of clinical and fundamental studies be conducted with the utmost urgency in order to enhance this situation.
