Extracellular Vesicles from Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cells Modulate Proliferation, Migration, and Chemosensitivity in Ovarian Cancer Cells
Authors: Yu-Hsun Chang, Kun-Chi Wu, Dah-Ching Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer is the most lethal gynecologic malignancy, with chemoresistance and recurrence driven by cancer stem cells (CSCs). Mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) mediate tumor–stroma communication, but their role in ovarian cancer progression and therapy remains unclear. Here, we investigated bone marrow (BM)-MSC-EVs, their effects on ovarian cancer cells, and the underlying molecular mechanisms. BM-MSCs were isolated, confirmed using flow cytometry and trilineage differentiation, and their EVs characterized using nanoparticle tracking analysis, transmission electron microscopy, and Western blotting. Kuramochi cells were treated with BM-MSC-EVs and assessed for proliferation, colony formation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and chemosensitivity. Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH+) Kuramochi cells, with or without EV exposure, were transplanted into non-obese diabetic severe combined immunodeficiency mice for xenograft studies, followed by histology, immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, and EV miRNA profiling. BM-MSC-EVs increased cancer cell proliferation but reduced colony formation, migration, and invasion in vitro. They sensitized ALDH+ CSC-like cells to carboplatin, while paclitaxel response remained unchanged. In vivo, EVs accelerated tumor growth and activated prosurvival (p-AKT, BCL-2), angiogenic (VEGFA, CD31), and epithelial–mesenchymal transition-associated (vimentin) pathways. EVs were found to be enriched in hsa-miR-100-5p, hsa-miR-122-5p, and hsa-let-7i-5p based on miRNA array analysis, and these findings were further validated by qRT-PCR. These findings reveal the dual roles of BM-MSC-EVs: enhancing carboplatin sensitivity while promoting tumor progression and angiogenesis.

1. Introduction

Ovarian cancer remains the most lethal gynecologic malignancy, primarily owing to its asymptomatic onset, late-stage diagnosis, and high rates of recurrence after standard treatment [1,2]. Despite initial responsiveness to platinum-based chemotherapy, approximately 70–80% of patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSC) relapse and ultimately develop chemoresistant disease [1,3]. The Kuramochi cell line, which closely recapitulates the genomic and transcriptional landscape of HGSC, offers a clinically relevant model for studying these mechanisms [4,5]. Improving our understanding of the biological factors that contribute to recurrence and drug resistance is critical for developing more effective therapeutic strategies.

A growing body of evidence implicates cancer stem cells (CSCs) in the persistence and recurrence of ovarian cancer [3]. CSCs, including aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH)+ ovarian cancer cells, possess enhanced self-renewal capacity, increased DNA repair activity, and elevated resistance to oxidative stress [6]. These cells survive cytotoxic chemotherapy and repopulate tumors, leading to refractory disease [7]. Targeting CSCs and their interactions with the tumor microenvironment is therefore a promising direction for overcoming platinum resistance [3]. However, the signals from the surrounding stroma that modulate CSC behavior remain incompletely understood.

The tumor microenvironment, composed of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells, and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), plays an essential role in regulating tumor progression [8]. MSCs, particularly bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs), migrate to sites of inflammation and tumor growth [9]. Once within the tumor microenvironment, MSCs can influence cancer cell proliferation, migration, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and drug response through direct contact or paracrine signaling [10,11]. MSCs have been reported to both suppress and promote tumor progression, reflecting the complexity of their context-dependent interactions with cancer cells [8].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as key mediators of MSC-driven signaling. EVs, including exosomes and microvesicles, carry a rich cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that can be transferred to recipient cells, altering their phenotype [12,13]. MSC-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) have demonstrated therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine and immunomodulation, but their effects in cancer biology are nuanced [14]. In some cancers, MSC-EVs inhibit tumor growth or enhance chemosensitivity [12,15]. In others, they promote tumor proliferation, metastasis, or angiogenesis [12]. These divergent findings underscore the importance of examining MSC-EV effects within specific tumor contexts.

In ovarian cancer, limited and often contradictory data exist regarding MSC-EVs. Some studies suggest that MSC-EVs promote cancer proliferation and metastasis through phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (AKT) or extracellular signal-regulated kinase signaling pathways [16]. In contrast, others describe anti-tumorigenic effects mediated by EV-associated miRNAs that suppress oncogenic pathways [17]. Few studies have investigated these interactions using clinically representative HGSC models, such as Kuramochi cells, and even fewer have examined the impact on CSC-like subpopulations, which are central to chemoresistance and disease recurrence.

However, the specific consequences of BM-MSC-EV transfer on HGSC behavior remain unclear. Moreover, it is unknown whether BM-MSC-EVs differentially influence cancer stem-like cells compared with bulk tumor cells, or whether these EVs alter sensitivity to first-line chemotherapies, such as carboplatin and paclitaxel. Given these knowledge gaps, we aimed to elucidate the functional and molecular consequences of BM-MSC-EV uptake by ovarian cancer cells. Understanding how EV cargo alters signaling pathways may offer new insights into tumor–stroma interactions and identify novel targets for therapeutic intervention.

2. Results

2.1. Phenotypic Characterization of Bone Marrow-Derived MSCs

BM-MSCs displayed typical fibroblast-like, spindle-shaped morphology under phase-contrast microscopy at both low and high magnification (Figure 1A,B). Flow cytometry confirmed the immunophenotypic profile of BM-MSCs, showing positivity for CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, and HLA-ABC and negativity for hematopoietic and immune-related markers CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (Figure 1C). To assess their multipotent differentiation potential, BM-MSCs were cultured in media specific to each lineage. Under adipogenic conditions, intracellular lipid droplets were visualized by Oil Red O staining, accompanied by the robust upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) (Figure 2A,B). Osteogenic differentiation was confirmed using calcium deposition detected with Alizarin Red S staining, along with significantly increased expression of ALPL and RUNX2 (Figure 2C,D). In chondrogenic medium, pellet cultures formed dense cartilage-like structures, with increased mRNA expression of COL2A1 and Aggrecan (Figure 2E,F). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated strong expression of type II collagen and aggrecan proteins within the pellets, whereas immunoglobulin G control staining was negative, confirming specificity (Figure 2G–I). Collectively, these results validate that BM-MSCs retain the characteristic morphology, immunophenotype, and trilineage differentiation potential required for mesenchymal stem cell identity.

2.2. Molecular and Morphological Characterization of BM-MSC-Derived Extracellular Vesicles

BM-MSC-derived EVs were successfully isolated and characterized. Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed a mean particle diameter of 107.8 nm with a concentration of approximately 1 × 109 particles/mL (Figure 3A). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the presence of small vesicles with the typical cup-shaped morphology (Figure 3B). EVs isolated from BM-MSCs and fallopian tube epithelial cells (FTECs) were analyzed by Western blot for classical exosomal markers CD63, CD9, and CD81. EVs from both BM-MSCs and FTECs expressed CD63. CD9 showed strong expression in BM-MSC-derived EVs and was also detected in FTEC-derived EVs. CD81 was clearly detected in FTEC-derived EVs and present at lower levels in BM-MSC-derived EVs. The presence of these tetraspanin markers confirms successful isolation of EVs from both cell types (Figure 3C).

2.3. BM-MSC-Derived EVs Promoted Proliferation but Inhibited Migration, Invasion, and Large Colony Formation in Kuramochi Cells

Treatment of Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells with BM-MSC-derived EVs significantly affected their proliferative and tumorigenic properties. Cell proliferation assays showed that EV treatment at 500× (3.2 × 105 particles) and 1000× (6.4 × 105 particles) concentrations enhanced cell growth (640 cells per well) over 5 days compared with untreated controls (Figure 4A). In contrast, colony-forming assays showed a marked reduction in the number of large colonies (>500 μm) in EV-treated groups. In comparison, the number of smaller colonies (<500 μm) remained unchanged, indicating a selective suppression of clonogenic potential (Figure 4B,C). Functional assays further revealed that EV treatment suppressed both cell migration and invasion. Wound healing assays showed reduced closure of scratch areas in EV-treated cells compared with controls (Figure 5A,B), and transwell invasion assays confirmed significantly fewer invading cells after 24 h of EV exposure (Figure 5C,D). Taken together, these findings suggest that BM-MSC-derived EVs exert dual effects on ovarian cancer cells, enhancing proliferation but attenuating their colony-forming capacity, migratory ability, and invasive potential.

2.4. BM-MSC-Derived EVs Enhanced Carboplatin Sensitivity and Apoptosis in ALDH+ Kuramochi Cells

BM-MSC-derived EVs selectively enhanced the chemosensitivity of ALDH+ Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells to carboplatin but not to paclitaxel. Dose–response analyses showed no significant difference in paclitaxel sensitivity following EV treatment, with half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values remaining similar between groups (9.73 nM vs. 9.89 nM; Figure 6A,B). In contrast, EV treatment markedly reduced the IC50 of carboplatin from 186.6 to 40.92 μM, indicating a strong sensitizing effect (Figure 6C,D). These results demonstrate that BM-MSC-derived EVs selectively enhanced carboplatin-induced cytotoxicity in ovarian cancer stem-like cells.

2.5. BM-MSC-Derived EVs Promoted Tumor Growth and Activated Survival and Angiogenic Pathways In Vivo

In vivo xenograft experiments demonstrated that BM-MSC-derived EVs promoted tumor growth in Kuramochi ALDH+ ovarian cancer cells. Mice receiving 500× EV-treated cells developed larger tumors than those receiving 1000× EV-treated cells, but no difference was observed compared with the control over 50 days (Figure 7A,B). Western blot analysis of tumor tissues revealed that EV treatment increased AKT phosphorylation, while total AKT levels decreased (Figure 7C). EV exposure also elevated the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) (Figure 7D) and markedly increased angiogenesis-associated markers, including CD31 and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), particularly in the 1000× EV group (Figure 7E,F). These findings indicate that BM-MSC-derived EVs not only enhance tumorigenicity in vivo but also activate survival and angiogenic signaling pathways that may contribute to the progression of ovarian cancer.

2.6. BM-MSC-Derived EVs Induced Vimentin Expression in ALDH+ Kuramochi Tumors

BM-MSC-derived EVs enhanced vimentin expression in ALDH+ Kuramochi ovarian cancer tumors, suggesting induction of EMT-related changes. Western blot analysis revealed an increase in vimentin protein in the EV 1000× group (2.2-fold increase), compared with the control (Figure 8A). Immunohistochemical staining confirmed higher vimentin expression in both EV-treated groups relative to control tumors (Figure 8B), and quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the number of vimentin-positive cells per field in EV-treated groups (**** p < 0.0001; Figure 8C). These findings suggest that BM-MSC-derived EVs stimulate the expression of EMT-associated markers in ovarian cancer cells in vivo, potentially contributing to increased tumor aggressiveness.

2.7. Profiling of Highly Expressed microRNAs in BM-MSC-Derived Extracellular Vesicles

The microRNA profiling of BM-MSC-derived EVs revealed the distinct enrichment of specific miRNAs. Among the top 10 most abundantly expressed species, hsa-miR-100-5p exhibited the highest expression, followed by hsa-miR-122-5p and hsa-let-7i-5p (Figure 9A). Bar graph analysis further demonstrated that a small subset of highly expressed miRNAs accounted for the majority of the EV-associated miRNA abundance (Figure 9B). BM-MSC–EV exhibited significantly higher expression of hsa-miR-100-5p, hsa-miR-122-5p, hsa-let-7i-5p, hsa-miR-21-5p, and hsa-miR-148a-3p compared with EmMSC–EVs (used as control, Figure 9C). It suggests that these selectively packaged miRNAs may play key regulatory roles in modulating signaling pathways of recipient ovarian cancer cells.

3. Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively characterized BM-MSCs and their derived EVs and demonstrated their functional impact on ovarian cancer progression. BM-MSCs exhibited typical spindle-shaped morphology, expressed canonical MSC surface markers, and retained trilineage differentiation capacity. Their secreted EVs displayed expected nanoscale size, morphology, and marker expression, confirming successful isolation. Functionally, BM-MSC-derived EVs enhanced ovarian cancer cell proliferation while selectively reducing large colony formation and significantly suppressing migration and invasion, indicating complex regulatory effects on tumor behavior. EVs sensitized ALDH+ ovarian cancer stem-like cells to carboplatin, without altering their response to paclitaxel. In vivo, EV treatment promoted tumor growth and activated prosurvival and angiogenic pathways, as evidenced by increased AKT phosphorylation, BCL-2, VEGFA, and CD31 expression. Moreover, EV exposure induced vimentin upregulation, consistent with the promotion of EMT-like phenotypes. Finally, miRNA profiling revealed that a small subset of highly enriched microRNAs, including miR-100-5p, miR-122-5p, and let-7i-5p, dominated the EV cargo, suggesting their potential involvement in mediating these functional effects. Collectively, these findings suggest that BM-MSC-derived EVs play a dual role in ovarian cancer, enhancing chemosensitivity while simultaneously promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis, and EMT, likely through the modulation of selective miRNA cargo and pathways.

MSC-EVs exert both antitumor and pro-tumor effects on ovarian cancer, depending on their molecular cargo and the tumor microenvironment [18,19,20,21,22]. MSC-EVs deliver microRNAs into ovarian cancer cells, modulating gene expression and signaling pathways involved in cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and chemoresistance [18]. Specifically, microRNAs within MSC-EVs can downregulate oncogenic pathways and upregulate tumor suppressor mechanisms, leading to reduced proliferation, migration, and increased cell death in ovarian cancer models [18,19,20,21,22]. Extracellular signaling molecules from adipose-derived stem cells and ovarian cancer cells induce a hybrid epithelial–mesenchymal phenotype in a bidirectional interaction [23]. Small extracellular vesicles derived from adipose-derived stem cells exert stronger antitumor effects on ovarian cancer cells than medium/large EVs, partly through the let-7b-5p and let-7e-5p miRNAs that suppress SKOV3/OV90 proliferation and correlate with prognostic gene targets [24]. Our study also found that BM-MSC-derived EVs exerted dual roles in ovarian cancer. These findings highlight the complex interactions between MSCs and ovarian cancer cells, underscoring the tumor microenvironment’s role in modulating MSC behavior and its potential impact on cancer progression. Several of the miRNAs we found, including miR-21-5p, miR-27a/b-3p, and miR-122-5p, have been associated with the activation of pro-survival pathways, metabolic adaptation, and resistance to chemotherapy, suggesting potential contributions to tumor-promoting effects [25,26]. In contrast, miR-100-5p, miR-99a-5p, miR-143-3p, miR-148a-3p, and miR-26a-5p have been reported to target components of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis and apoptosis-related pathways, and may therefore influence chemosensitivity or growth suppression in a context-dependent manner [27]. Members of the let-7 family, including let-7i-5p, are known regulators of differentiation and stemness, potentially modulating the balance between proliferative capacity and long-term clonogenic fitness [28]. Overall, EV-associated miRNAs appear to coordinately and dose-dependently regulate PI3K/AKT signaling and downstream survival pathways, contributing to the observed phenotypic effects.

Recent EV-based and nanomedicine studies provide a useful framework to interpret our selective carboplatin chemosensitization. A “hypoxia-potentiating, hypoxia-activated, chemo-sensitization” strategy has been described in which an anti-vascular component increases tumor hypoxia to activate a hypoxia-responsive prodrug, while a co-delivered antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) suppresses hypoxia-induced HIF1α, thereby enhancing chemotherapy response [29]. Conceptually, this illustrates how dual or opposing signals can be intentionally combined to reshape the tumor stress state and overcome resistance. In contrast to engineered systems that deliberately amplify hypoxia and then neutralize HIF1α, our EV preparation likely exerts its effects through endogenous cargo (miRNAs) that modulate survival pathways (PI3K/AKT) and anti-apoptotic signaling (BCL2), thereby lowering the apoptotic threshold specifically under DNA-damage stress induced by carboplatin. This comparison highlights that chemosensitization can arise either from microenvironmental stress–rewiring (hypoxia/HIF1α) or from intracellular survival signaling reprogramming (AKT/BCL2), and supports the idea that EV-mediated cargo may selectively influence platinum responsiveness more than taxane sensitivity.

In cancer therapy, MSCs and their EVs can be engineered to target tumors and deliver anticancer agents more effectively [30]. One approach involves genetically engineering streptavidin onto MSC-EV surfaces, enabling versatile targeting modifications with biotinylated molecules [31]. These engineered EVs demonstrate low immunogenicity and high safety in vivo. Additionally, targeting the EMT pathway, which plays a crucial role in cancer metastasis and therapy resistance, presents opportunities for pharmacological intervention in cancer treatment [32]. We observed that BM-MSC-EVs specifically influenced pathways associated with EMT. These advancements in MSC-EV engineering and EMT targeting offer promising avenues for developing more effective cancer therapies.

MSC-derived EVs activate VEGF receptors and downstream angiogenic pathways, enhancing endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and tube formation [33]. In vivo, MSC-EVs increase blood reperfusion and new blood vessel formation in ischemic limbs, with the addition of Matrigel further enhancing this effect [34]. EVs derived from renal CSCs induce pro-tumorigenic changes in mesenchymal stromal cells, including increased angiogenesis [35]. We found that BM-MSC-EVs increased the expression of CD31 and VEGFA, suggesting increased angiogenic activity in tumor tissues. These findings highlight the significant role of MSCs and their EVs in promoting tumor angiogenesis and growth.

Although our results appear to show divergent effects on cell proliferation and colony formation, these findings likely reflect the evaluation of distinct biological processes occurring over different temporal and functional scales. The proliferation assay primarily captures short-term cell cycle activity and metabolic growth [36], whereas the colony formation assay assesses long-term clonogenic capacity, which depends on sustained survival, self-renewal potential, and resistance to cumulative stress [37]. In this study, the observed increase in early proliferative activity was accompanied by a reduction in large colony formation, suggesting that treatment may promote transient cell cycle entry while impairing long-term clonogenic fitness. This pattern is consistent with a shift toward a more proliferative yet less stem-like or clonogenically competent state, potentially due to altered differentiation status, replicative stress, or the exhaustion of self-renewing subpopulations. Such discordant effects between short-term proliferation and long-term colony growth have been reported in other contexts, underscoring the importance of integrating multiple functional assays when interpreting cellular responses.

The in vivo tumor growth data demonstrated a non-linear, dose-dependent response to EV treatment, with the EV1000× group exhibiting the smallest tumor volume. Although this appears inconsistent with the tumor-promoting effects observed at lower EV doses, such biphasic responses have been reported in EV and growth factor-mediated signaling and may reflect biological saturation or stress responses at excessive concentrations. High-dose EV exposure could alter tumor–stromal interactions, impair EV uptake efficiency, or induce cellular stress, thereby limiting sustained tumor expansion in vivo [38]. In addition, EV biodistribution and microenvironmental modulation in animal models may differ substantially from those in vitro, contributing to divergent dose–response effects [39,40]. These findings suggest that EV-mediated tumor modulation occurs within a restricted concentration window and underscore the complexity of EV–tumor interactions in vivo, highlighting the importance of careful dose optimization when interpreting therapeutic or tumor-promoting effects.

EV treatment induced the non-linear regulation of AKT signaling and downstream survival pathways. In our study, EV500× markedly increased AKT phosphorylation (5.29-fold) despite reduced total AKT (0.38-fold), indicating enhanced activation of the remaining AKT pool. EV1000× did not alter total AKT (0.92-fold) but moderately increased p-AKT (2.67-fold) and selectively upregulated BCL2, suggesting a shift toward survival and stress-adaptive signaling at higher EV doses [38]. These findings highlight dose-dependent, pathway-specific EV effects that are regulated primarily at the level of signaling activation rather than protein abundance [41].

Recent nanomedicine studies contextualize our findings. Targeted nanoconjugates that enable controlled, sequential drug release have demonstrated enhanced antitumor efficacy in triple-negative breast cancer [42], while EV engineering approaches demonstrate that surface functionalization and cargo loading can convert EVs into therapeutic delivery platforms [42]. In contrast, the EVs used here rely on endogenous miRNA cargo to modulate PI3K/AKT and apoptotic signaling, suggesting that biologically driven chemosensitization may complement engineered targeting strategies in future EV-based combination chemotherapy.

A limitation of this study is the use of a single pre-established BM-MSC line and one ovarian cancer cell line, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. A limitation of this study is the small number of animals per experimental group (n = 3–4), which may reduce statistical power. A potential limitation of this study is the use of β-actin as a reference protein. Because extracellular vesicle cargo, particularly miRNAs, may influence cytoskeletal regulation. Future studies should consider alternative reference proteins with greater stability, such as ribosomal proteins.

4. Methods

4.1. Isolation of Bone Marrow Stem Cells

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB Number: IRB112-221-C). We used one previously derived BM-MSC line (IRB 103-49-A). The derivation protocol is described below. Bone marrow was aspirated from patients who had undergone orthopedic surgery owing to trauma. Five milliliters of bone marrow aspirate were mixed with 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and centrifuged at 1200× g for 6 min. The supernatant was removed. The pellet was plated on a 10 cm culture dish and supplemented with a culture medium composed of alpha-minimal essential medium Eagle (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma). BM-MSCs were maintained in the culture medium until they reached 70–90% confluence. Cells were then harvested by adding Trypsin (Sigma) and replated at a 1:3 ratio into new culture dishes.

4.2. Flow Cytometry

BM-MSCs were stained with fluorescent antibody conjugates for 30 min on ice, then washed twice with PBS. After blocking with 2% FBS-containing PBS, the cells were analyzed using flow cytometry with a FACSCalibur instrument (BD Biosciences, Franklin, NJ, USA) to assess the expression levels of the designated markers. Data acquisition and analysis were performed under standardized instrument settings, and appropriate gating strategies were applied to exclude debris and doublets. Unstained cells were included in each run as negative controls to determine baseline autofluorescence and ensure accurate discrimination of specific marker expression. The following antibodies were used: CD34, CD44, CD45, CD73, CD90, CD105, HLA-ABC, and HLA-DR (all purchased from BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

4.3. Trilineage Differentiation Capabilities

4.3.1. Adipogenesis

For adipogenic differentiation, BM-MSCs were seeded at 5 × 104 cells per well in 12-well plates and cultured until reaching 60–80% confluence. At this point, the growth medium was replaced with adipogenic induction medium consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 5 μg/mL insulin, 1 μmol/L dexamethasone, 60 μmol/L indomethacin, and 0.5 mmol/L isobutylmethylxanthine (all from Sigma). Cells were maintained in this medium for 14 days, with medium changes every 2–3 days. Throughout the induction period, cells progressively differentiated into adipocytes, as evidenced by the accumulation of lipid droplets. After 14 days, adipogenesis was confirmed by Oil Red O staining (Sigma), which visualizes lipid accumulation.

4.3.2. Osteogenesis

For osteogenic differentiation, BM-MSCs were seeded at 1 × 104 cells per well in 12-well plates and cultured until reaching 60–80% confluence. At this stage, the growth medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 μmol/L ascorbate, 0.1 μmol/L dexamethasone, and 10 mmol/L β-glycerol phosphate (all from Sigma). Cells were maintained in this medium for 14 days, with medium changes every 2–3 days. During the induction period, stem cells differentiated into osteoblast-like cells, forming a mineralized extracellular matrix. After 14 days, osteogenesis was confirmed by Alizarin Red S staining (Sigma), which detects calcium deposits indicative of mineralization.

4.3.3. Chondrogenesis

For chondrogenic differentiation, the pellet culture method was employed. BM-MSCs were harvested at a density of 2.5 × 107 cells/mL and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to form a compact cell pellet. The pellet was resuspended in chondrogenic induction medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 6.25 μg/mL insulin (Sigma), 10 ng/mL transforming growth factor-β1 (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid-2-phosphate (Sigma). Approximately 2.5 × 107 cells were cultured in 15 mL conical tubes and maintained in CM for 21 days, with medium changes every 2–3 days. During this induction period, cells aggregated to form spheroidal pellets and progressively differentiated into chondrocytes, producing extracellular matrix components rich in proteoglycans and collagen. After 21 days, chondrogenesis was confirmed by immunostaining for type II collagen and aggrecan, which served as markers of cartilage-specific glycosaminoglycan and proteoglycan deposition.

4.4. Immunohistochemical Staining (Type II Collagen and Aggrecan)

For immunohistochemical analysis of chondrogenic pellets, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C to preserve tissue morphology. Fixed pellets were embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound, cryosectioned into 10–20 µm sections, and air-dried for 30 min at room temperature to ensure proper adhesion to glass slides. Sections were then incubated in blocking solution (5% normal serum and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies against type II collagen and aggrecan (1:200; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBS, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Antibody binding was visualized using light microscopy following diaminobenzidine substrate application (all reagents from Sigma). Finally, nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and slides were mounted with a mounting medium for analysis. Positive staining for type II collagen and aggrecan confirmed the successful chondrogenic differentiation of stem cell pellets. Immunoglobulin G isotype controls were included to account for nonspecific antibody binding.

4.5. Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Total RNA was treated with DNase I prior to reverse transcription to eliminate potential contamination with genomic DNA. One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). qPCR was performed using Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase served as the internal reference gene. All reactions were run in triplicate. Target genes included lineage-specific differentiation markers: PPARγ and FABP4 for adipogenesis, ALPL and RUNX2 for osteogenesis, and Aggrecan and COL2A1 for chondrogenesis. Negative controls included water and non-reverse-transcribed RNA to exclude contamination or genomic DNA amplification. PCR product specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. Threshold cycle (Ct) values were determined using 7500 Software v2.3 (Applied Biosystems), and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [43]. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Primers for quantitative real-time PCR were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST to ensure specificity against the target sequences.

4.6. Collection of BM-MSC-Derived EVs

EVs were isolated from the conditioned medium (CM) of BM-MSCs, including lenti-control and miR-7704-transfected cells, after 48 h of culture. When cells reached 50–60% confluence, cells were washed with PBS and cultured in serum-free defined medium (MesenGRo hMSC medium, StemRD, Burlingame, CA, USA) for an additional 48 h. The CM was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 300× g for 15 min and 2500× g for 15 min to remove dead cells and debris. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Merck–Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to eliminate residual contaminants. EVs were isolated by adding ExoQuick reagent (63 µL; EXOTC50A-1, System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA) to 250 µL of CM, followed by incubation at 4 °C for at least 1 h. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 2000× g for 30 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting EV pellet was further centrifuged at 1500× g for 5 min to remove residual ExoQuick solution. All isolation steps were performed under sterile conditions. The purified EVs were resuspended in EV-Guard storage buffer (EXSBA-1, System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and stored at −80 °C until use.

4.7. EV Identification

EVs were characterized by Western blotting for canonical EV surface markers (CD63, CD9, and CD81), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight, Malvern, Worcestershire, UK), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HITACHI H-7500, Tokyo, Japan). Fallopian tube epithelial cell-derived EVs were used as a positive control.

4.8. Western Blot Analysis

Western blotting was performed to evaluate the expression of EV surface markers (CD9, CD63, and CD81) and functional proteins. Isolated EVs were lysed with protein lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) to extract total protein, which was then separated using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Sigma-Aldrich) and transferred onto membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD9, CD63, and CD81 (1:2000; Sigma-Aldrich). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Sigma) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence detection kit (Promega, Fitchburg, WI, USA).

Proteins for the following experiments from in vitro and in vivo studies were subsequently extracted, and the expression of caspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, beta-actin, Akt, phosphorylated Akt, BCL-2, CD31, VEGFA, and vimentin (all from Sigma-Aldrich) was analyzed using Western blotting.

4.9. Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis

The size distribution and concentration of EVs were assessed using a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). EV samples were diluted in 0.2 μm–filtered 1× normal saline to achieve an optimal concentration of 20–150 particles per frame, typically at a 100×–1000× dilution. Three 60 s videos were recorded for each sample, and data were analyzed using NanoSight NTA Software version 3.2 (Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). Post-acquisition settings were standardized across all samples. Data were reported as particle concentration (particles/mL), mean particle size (nm), and size distribution profile.

4.10. TEM

The morphology of EVs was examined using TEM. EVs were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4; Sigma-Aldrich), and 3 µL of the suspension was applied to carbon-coated TEM grids (697745, Sigma-Aldrich). The grids were washed and negatively stained with 1% uranyl acetate (21447-25, Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA). After air-drying, grids were imaged using a HITACHI H-7500 TEM (Tokyo, Japan). EVs were visualized as round, cup-shaped vesicles with diameters of 30–150 nm, consistent with typical exosomal morphology.

4.11. Culture of Ovarian Cancer Cells

Kuramochi cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and used for co-culture experiments. These cell lines were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Sigma) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. ALDH+ Kuramochi cells were sorted using flow cytometry, identified as cancer stem cell-like populations, and subsequently used for downstream assays.

4.12. Co-Culture of Ovarian Cancer Cells with BM-MSC-Derived EVs

Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells were co-cultured with BM-MSC-derived EVs to assess their biological effects. Briefly, BM-MSC-derived EVs (5 × 105 (500×) or 1 × 106 (1000×) particles) were resuspended in 0.5 mL of culture medium and added to each well of a 6-well plate. Subsequently, 1 × 103 Kuramochi cells were seeded in 1.5 mL of fresh culture medium and combined with the EV suspension, resulting in a final culture volume of 2 mL per well. Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions for downstream assays.

4.13. Cell Proliferation

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on cell proliferation, an XTT assay (Biological Industries Ltd., Beit Haemek, Israel) was employed. Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells/cm2 and cultured either with or without BM-MSC-EVs (at a concentration of 500× or 1000×). Cell proliferation was evaluated at days 0, 2, and 5. For the XTT assay, solutions of XTT and N-methyl dibenzopyrazine methyl sulfate (PMS; Biological Industries Ltd., New Taipei City, Taiwan, R.O.C.) were thawed immediately before use at 37 °C. PMS was mixed with the XTT reagent immediately before application, and 50 μL of the XTT/PMS mixture was added to each 100 μL of culture medium. After incubation for 2–5 h at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (DYNEX MRX II; Dynex Technologies, Chantilly, VA, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

4.14. Wound Healing Assay

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on cell migration, a wound healing (scratch) assay was employed. Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and cultured until a confluent monolayer formed. A uniform scratch was created across the monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip to generate a wound gap. Detached cells were gently removed by washing with PBS, and the cultures were then incubated in fresh medium, either without EVs (control) or with BM-MSC-derived EVs at 500× or 1000×. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 h (immediately after scratching) and at 6 h using a phase-contrast microscope. The wound area was quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.54g, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and the percentage of wound closure was calculated using the following formula: [wound closure (%) = (area-0 h − area-6 h)/area-0 h × 100]. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

4.15. Transwell Invasion Assay

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on cell invasion, a transwell invasion assay was employed. Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells (5 × 104 cells) were seeded onto Matrigel-coated transwell inserts in 24-well plates (BD BioCoat Matrigel Invasion Chamber; BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA). The lower chamber was filled with culture medium supplemented with BM-MSC-derived EVs at either 500× or 1000× concentration, while control wells contained medium without EVs. After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells and residual Matrigel on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton-tipped applicator. The process was repeated with a fresh applicator to ensure complete removal. The invaded cells adhering to the underside of the membrane were fixed with methanol for 15 min and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. Excess dye was rinsed away with distilled water, and the membranes were air-dried before being mounted onto slides. Invaded cells were visualized under a light microscope, and the number of stained cells was counted in three randomly selected fields per insert. The mean number of invaded cells per field was calculated, and all experiments were performed in triplicate.

4.16. Colony Formation Assay

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on the ability of cell colony formation, a colony formation assay was employed. Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured with or without BM-MSC-derived EVs at concentrations of 500× or 1000× of cell numbers. Cells were maintained for 9 days under standard culture conditions, with medium (supplemented with EVs where appropriate) replaced every three days. At the end of the culture period, colonies were fixed with methanol for 15 min and stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed by washing with distilled water, and plates were air-dried. Colonies were imaged using a phase-contrast microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ. Colony numbers were stratified by diameter into >500 μm and <500 μm categories. EV treatment groups were compared with untreated controls to assess the effect on clonogenic growth. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

4.17. Chemosensitivity

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on cancer cell chemosensitivity, an IC50 value from a chemosensitivity assay was used. Paclitaxel (Formoxol, Yung Shin Pharm. Ind., Co., Ltd., Taichung, Taiwan) and carboplatin (SINPHAR Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Yilan, Taiwan), two common chemotherapeutic agents used to treat ovarian cancer, were investigated in ovarian cancer cells treated with or without BM-MSC-derived EVs. We seeded 3 × 103 cells/well in a 96-well plate and treated them with increasing concentrations of the chemotherapeutic drugs for 48 h to determine their IC50 values. We measured cell concentration after chemotherapy by adding XTT solution and recording the optical density. IC50 values were calculated using a non-linear regression model in GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

4.18. Apoptosis Evaluation

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on the apoptosis of cancer cells after treatment with chemotherapeutic drugs, the expression of cleaved caspase 3 was evaluated. Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells were treated with BM-MSC-derived EVs (500 or 1000×) in combination with either carboplatin or paclitaxel for 48 h. Following treatment, total cellular protein was extracted, and caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-3 (Sigma) were analyzed. β-actin (1:5000; Sigma-Aldrich) served as a loading control. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Promega, Fitchburg, WI, USA) and quantified using densitometry with ImageJ.

4.19. Animal Xenograft Experiment

To investigate the effect of BMSC-EV on xenograft formation, an in vivo tumor-formation assay was performed. Kuramochi aldh+ CSCs were used (aldh+ is a cancer stem cell marker). The animal experiment was approved by the Animal Research and Care Committee of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital. Non-obese, diabetic, severe combined immune deficiency (strain name: NOD.CB17-Prkdcscid/JTcu, 5-week-old) mice were used and provided by Tzu Chi University. A total of 10 mice were used for in vivo experiments. Kuramochi ovarian aldh+ cancer cells (1 × 105 cells) were injected either alone or in combination with BM-MSC-derived EVs (500 [5 × 107 particles] or 1000× [1 × 108 particles]) (n = 3 per group except n = 4 in the 500× group) into the subcutaneous tissue of the dorsal region. Before transplantation, Kuramochi aldh+ cells were cultured for 7 days to obtain the required cell numbers. For injection, cells were suspended in 100 μL of culture medium and mixed with 100 μL of growth factor–reduced Matrigel (BD Biosciences, BD Matrigel™ Matrix). Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, delivered via 2 L/min oxygen, during the procedure. The mice were housed in the Animal Center of Tzu Chi University. The relative humidity was maintained at 45–65%, and cages were maintained at 20–24 °C. The mice were kept in pathogen-free rooms.

Mice were euthanized by CO2 exposure once tumor size reached approximately 500 mm3. Tumors were excised, photographed, measured, and weighed. Tumor growth was monitored weekly for 1–2 months, and volumes were calculated using the formula: (width2 × length)/2.

4.20. Histological Examination

Xenograft tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed according to standard histological protocols. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope at 200× magnification. For morphometric analysis, five representative, non-overlapping fields per section were randomly selected and analyzed using ImageJ. Nuclear morphology, cell density, and mitotic figures were quantified semi-quantitatively by two independent observers blinded to the treatment groups. The mean values from the five fields were calculated and used for statistical analysis.

4.21. Western Blotting of Xenograft Tumor Tissue

Tumor tissues were harvested, rinsed with cold PBS, and divided for downstream applications. For Western blotting, 20–50 mg of tissue was homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, then centrifuged to obtain clarified protein lysates. Protein concentrations were measured using the bicinchoninic acid assay, and aliquots were stored at −80 °C until use.

Tumor lysates were analyzed for phosphorylated AKT (Ser473; p-AKT) and total AKT, the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2, the angiogenesis marker CD31, vimentin, and VEGFA. β-Actin served as the loading control (all from Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA). EV treatment groups included the indicated doses (500× and 1000×). Band intensities were quantified using densitometry (ImageJ): background was subtracted, each target band was normalized to its corresponding β-actin band (p-AKT was additionally ratioed to total AKT), and values were expressed relative to the control lane (set to 1.0). Densitometry values normalized to the control are shown beneath each band on the blot. Replicates from independent tumors were summarized as mean ± SEM, and normalized ratios were used for statistical comparisons.

4.22. Immunohistochemistry of Vimentin in Tumor Tissue

For immunohistochemistry, tumor tissues from the control and BM-MSC EV-treated groups were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked and incubated with a primary antibody against vimentin (Cell Signaling), followed by detection with a suitable HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and visualization with diaminobenzidine. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining. Stained slides were imaged under a light microscope, and vimentin-positive cells were quantified in representative fields of view. The proportion of vimentin-positive cells was quantified from five representative fields per section and compared among the control, EV 500×, and EV 1000× treatment groups.

4.23. miRNA Array Analysis

BM-MSC-derived EVs were isolated and subjected to small RNA sequencing to profile their microRNA content. Total RNA, including small RNAs, was extracted from BM-MSC-derived extracellular vesicles using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Small RNA libraries were generated using the NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, including adaptor ligation, reverse transcription, PCR amplification, and size selection of 18–30 nt fragments using the Pippin Prep system (Sage Science, Beverly, MA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to produce single-end reads. Raw reads were processed for quality control and adaptor trimming using FastQC (Babraham Bioinformatics, Cambridge, UK) and Cutadapt (Martin, 2011). They were then aligned to the human genome and annotated against miRBase (v22, Wellcome Sanger Institute, Cambridge, UK) for microRNA identification and quantification. Normalized expression values were obtained for downstream analysis of highly expressed miRNAs.

Sequencing data were processed and normalized, and the expression of each miRNA was quantified using the least square mean values. The top 10 most abundantly expressed miRNAs were identified and ranked. Relative expression levels of these top-ranked miRNAs were further visualized in a bar graph to highlight the predominance of a limited subset of miRNAs within the EV population.

4.24. Exosome RNA Isolation and microRNA qPCR Protocol

For microRNA quantification, reverse transcription and quantitative PCR were performed using the TOOLS miRNA RT-qPCR Assay (TOOLS, TTH-mi250, New Taipei City, Taiwan, R.O.C.). Briefly, 20 ng of EV RNA was used in each 20 µL reverse transcription (RT) reaction, and 2 µL of miRNA-specific stem-loop RT primer was added to each tube. The mixture was gently mixed and briefly centrifuged before RT was performed at 42 °C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 3 min, and holding at 4 °C. The resulting miRNA-specific cDNA was subjected to TaqMan real-time PCR using a miRNA-specific forward primer, a universal reverse primer, and a fluorescent probe, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Threshold cycle (Ct) values were obtained using a real-time PCR detection system. Relative miRNA expression levels were normalized to RNU6B as the internal control using the ΔCt method (ΔCt = Ct_miRNA − Ct_RNU6B). Comparative analyses between groups were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, and fold changes were expressed as 2−ΔΔCt.

4.25. Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using nonparametric tests because the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were not met. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons among more than two groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc Dunn’s multiple comparison test when appropriate. Data are presented as mean with standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24, IBM, New York, NY, USA). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

5. Conclusions

We demonstrated that BM-MSC-derived EVs exert multifaceted effects on ovarian cancer. While EVs enhanced the proliferation of ovarian cancer cells, they simultaneously suppressed their clonogenic growth, migration, and invasion in vitro. Importantly, EVs selectively sensitized ALDH+ ovarian cancer stem-like cells to carboplatin by inducing apoptosis, highlighting their potential as adjuvants to chemotherapy. However, in vivo experiments revealed that EVs also promoted tumor growth, survival, angiogenesis, and EMT, indicating a dual role in cancer progression. MicroRNA profiling identified a subset of highly enriched miRNAs, such as miR-100-5p, miR-122-5p, and let-7i-5p, which may mediate these effects. Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity of BM-MSC-EV interactions with ovarian cancer, suggesting both therapeutic promise and tumor-promoting risks. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to delineate context-dependent effects and to harness MSC-EVs as safe and effective therapeutic tools.
