Depth-adapted portal design for unilateral biportal endoscopic discectomy in a morbidly obese patient (BMI 41): technical considerations
Authors: Shinya Arataki, Akiyoshi Miyamoto, Tadashi Komatsubara, Masato Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Morbid obesity increases operative depth and soft-tissue thickness in lumbar spine surgery, potentially complicating portal placement and working-corridor stability in unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) procedures. Although previous studies have reported acceptable clinical outcomes of UBE in obese patients, technical considerations for depth-adapted portal design and working corridor management at extreme operative depths have not been sufficiently described. A male patient in his late 20s, with a height of 183 cm and body weight of 137 kg (body mass index 41 kg/m2), presented with a large left L5/S1 lumbar disc herniation. Preoperative imaging suggested a deep operative corridor, with the relevant working depth ranging approximately from 80 to 100 mm depending on the laminar working area and the oblique trajectory toward the L5/S1 disc space. Because the skin contours were incompletely visualized on preoperative computed tomography (CT) due to the patient’s body habitus, intraoperative O-arm navigation was used as an adjunctive tool to confirm and refine the planned portal trajectories. Two 10-mm longitudinal skin incisions were created approximately 23 mm lateral to the midline, with an interportal distance of 35 mm. Adequate fascial release wider than the skin incisions was performed to reduce soft-tissue constraint at the fascial level. UBE discectomy was completed without dural injury or technical restriction, using standard UBE instruments. Postoperatively, radicular symptoms improved promptly without neurological deficits. This case illustrates depth-dependent amplification of soft-tissue constraints in UBE. As operative depth increases, small restrictions at the fascial level may proportionally narrow the functional working corridor. Adequate fascial release and thoughtful portal spacing may help preserve triangulation and facilitate irrigation outflow in deep operative fields. Rather than proposing a fixed portal template or requiring navigation, this report emphasizes individualized, depth-aware portal planning. Navigation may be useful in selected extreme cases when surface anatomy is difficult to evaluate reliably, but the same planning principles may also be applied using fluoroscopy and careful preoperative CT/MRI assessment. In morbidly obese patients with substantial operative depth, depth-aware portal planning combined with tailored fascial management may improve the reproducibility and technical stability of UBE discectomy. These findings should be interpreted as technical observations from a single case.

Introduction

Morbid obesity presents distinct anatomical and technical challenges in lumbar spine surgery, primarily because of the increased subcutaneous tissue thickness and prolonged operative depth. Although minimally invasive approaches have been adopted to mitigate soft-tissue disruption in obese patients, technical difficulties may persist when the working depth is substantial.

Unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) lumbar discectomy has gained popularity because of its enhanced visualization, continuous irrigation, and preservation of paraspinal musculature. Several cohort studies have reported that obesity does not compromise short-term clinical outcomes or increase perioperative complications following biportal endoscopic lumbar procedures. Park et al. demonstrated comparable clinical and radiologic outcomes between obese and non-obese patients undergoing biportal endoscopic lumbar discectomy (1). Similarly, Olson et al. found that obesity was not an independent risk factor for increased perioperative complications in biportal spinal endoscopy (2).

However, recent evidence suggests that the severity of obesity may influence postoperative recovery trajectories following UBE, indicating that extreme body habitus may warrant additional technical consideration (3). Furthermore, UBE has been directly compared with percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) in obese patients, demonstrating its overall effectiveness as a minimally invasive option (4).

Despite the expanding body of outcome-oriented evidence, detailed technical guidance regarding portal design and working corridor management in patients with morbid obesity and extreme operative depth remains limited. In particular, the geometric implications of increased working depth on triangulation stability and irrigation dynamics have not been systematically described.

In the present technical note, we describe a depth-adapted portal planning strategy for UBE discectomy in a morbidly obese patient, emphasizing trajectory-based portal placement, appropriate interportal spacing, adequate fascial management, and adjunctive intraoperative trajectory confirmation in a deep operative corridor. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0126/rc).

Preoperative preparations and requirements

Patient and preoperative assessment

A male patient in his late 20s, with a height of 183 cm and body weight of 137 kg (body mass index 41 kg/m2), presented with left-sided radiculopathy secondary to a large L5/S1 lumbar disc herniation. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed substantial left-sided disc extrusion compressing the S1 nerve root (Figure 1). Computed tomography (CT) was also performed to assess osseous anatomy and estimate the operative depth. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Okayama Rosai Hospital (IRB No. 602). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this article and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

(B) Preoperative axial T2-weighted image (L5/S1) showing compression and dorsal displacement of the left S1 nerve root. (C) Postoperative sagittal T2-weighted image demonstrating the removal of the extruded fragment. (D) Postoperative axial T2-weighted image (L5/S1) confirming decompression of the left S1 nerve root.

Owing to the patient’s body habitus, the external skin contour was only partially visualized on preoperative CT, limiting precise preoperative skin-based portal simulation. Retrospective reconstruction based on the final portal position demonstrated that the skin-to-spinous process distance was approximately 61 mm, the skin-to-lamina distance was approximately 81 mm, and the oblique trajectory to the L5/S1 disc target was approximately 102 mm (Figure 2). Therefore, the relevant working depth in this case was considered to range approximately from 80 to 100 mm, depending on whether the laminar working area or the oblique trajectory toward the disc space was used as the reference point.

Preoperative computed tomography-based depth assessment. (A) Sagittal reconstruction showing representative depth measurements, including an approximate skin-to-spinous process distance of 61.22 mm and an oblique depth of 101.63 mm toward the L5/S1 disc space. (B) Axial image showing an approximate skin-to-lamina distance of 81.18 mm.

These findings indicated a deep operative corridor requiring depth-aware, trajectory-based portal planning rather than a fixed skin-based template. The initial concept followed standard UBE principles, including target pathology, pedicle orientation, interlaminar window anatomy, and anatomical safety margins. A modest lateral offset was considered acceptable within anatomical limits to preserve triangulation in the elongated operative corridor.

Step-by-step description

Portal design and navigation strategy

Final portal positioning was performed using intraoperative O-arm-based navigation as an adjunctive tool for trajectory confirmation and refinement (Figure 3). Portal placement was determined sequentially rather than by applying a predetermined interportal distance. First, the working portal was planned according to the target pathology, the trajectory toward the L5/S1 disc space through the interlaminar window, and the need for safe manipulation around the left S1 nerve root. The viewing portal was then positioned to preserve triangulation with the working portal, minimize instrument interference, and maintain working freedom in the deep operative corridor.

Navigation-guided portal design in a deep operative field. (A) Intraoperative O-arm navigation demonstrating trajectory planning toward the L5/S1 disc space. (B) Skin surface markings under navigation guidance to optimize portal placement and triangulation. (C) Final portal configuration showing two longitudinal incisions approximately 23 mm lateral to the midline.

Navigation was used to confirm the target level, the relationship between the planned portal trajectories and the L5/S1 interlaminar window, and the orientation toward the disc space. Two longitudinal skin incisions, each approximately 10 mm in length, were created approximately 23 mm lateral to the midline. The final interportal distance was approximately 35 mm. This distance was the result of trajectory-based planning in this specific anatomical situation and should not be interpreted as a universal recommendation.

Fascial management and working corridor preparation

After skin incision, the thoracolumbar fascia was released wider than the skin margins to reduce soft-tissue constriction at the fascial level. The extent of fascial release was tailored to the operative depth and working requirements, rather than performed indiscriminately.

This maneuver was intended to serve two practical purposes. First, adequate fascial release may help preserve triangulation geometry. In deep operative corridors, even minor restriction at the fascial layer may narrow the effective working angle; therefore, a sufficiently wide fascial opening can allow more reproducible angular divergence of the endoscope and working instruments. Second, adequate fascial release may facilitate irrigation outflow. Because UBE relies on continuous saline irrigation for visualization, reducing fascial-level resistance may help maintain a stable visual field without excessive irrigation pressure.

Portal triangulation and irrigation outflow were confirmed before decompression.

Operative course and outcome

After portal design and fascial preparation were completed, UBE discectomy was performed in the standard manner. The endoscopic portal was established first, followed by insertion of the working portal under direct visualization. A standard 30° endoscope and standard UBE working instruments were used. No special long instruments were required despite the substantial operative depth. Irrigation was performed using a gravity-based system without a pressure-controlled pump, and radiofrequency settings were not modified from our routine UBE practice. Intraoperative O-arm imaging was performed once during portal planning and was not repeated during the procedure.

Despite the increased operative depth, no marked restriction of triangulation was perceived. The tailored fascial release may have contributed to smoother repeated instrument insertion and withdrawal by reducing fascial-level soft-tissue resistance. Visualization remained stable without the need for excessive irrigation pressure. Partial laminotomy and flavectomy were performed to expose the left S1 nerve root. A large extruded disc fragment was identified and removed (Figure 4). Adequate decompression was confirmed endoscopically. The operative time was 105 minutes, and the estimated blood loss was less than 5 mL. There was no dural injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, or depth-related technical restriction.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

After surgery, the patient’s radicular leg pain improved promptly. The visual analog scale (VAS) score for leg pain improved from 9.5 preoperatively to 1.0 at discharge. Low back pain also improved from 4.5 to 1.0 at discharge. No motor weakness or sensory deficit was observed. Clinical assessments were performed at discharge, 1 month, and 3 months after surgery, and symptom improvement was maintained during follow-up. At the most recent 6-month follow-up, the patient remained neurologically intact without recurrent symptoms.

Postoperatively, CT imaging was used to reconstruct the portal trajectory and estimate the working depth based on the finalized intraoperative portal position. This retrospective analysis demonstrated a skin-to-lamina distance of approximately 81 mm and an oblique trajectory of approximately 102 mm to the L5/S1 disc target, confirming a substantial operative depth of approximately 80–100 mm.

Tips and pearls

Several technical points may be useful when performing UBE discectomy in patients with morbid obesity and substantial operative depth. First, operative depth should be estimated preoperatively whenever possible, because increased working depth may amplify small restrictions at the skin or fascial level and narrow the functional working corridor at the target anatomy. Second, portal placement should be planned according to the target pathology, interlaminar window, disc trajectory, and anatomical safety margins, rather than by applying a fixed skin-based template. The working portal may be planned first according to the intended disc trajectory and nerve-root manipulation, followed by positioning of the viewing portal to preserve triangulation and minimize instrument interference.

Third, fascial release should be sufficiently wider than the skin incision to reduce fascial-level constriction, but it should be tailored to the operative depth and working requirements rather than performed excessively. This balance is important for preserving the minimally invasive nature of UBE while maintaining working freedom and irrigation outflow in deep operative fields. Finally, intraoperative navigation may be useful as an adjunctive tool when surface anatomy is unreliable or preoperative imaging does not fully depict the external skin contour. However, the same depth-aware planning principles may also be applied using careful CT/MRI-based depth estimation and conventional anteroposterior/lateral fluoroscopic marking of the midline, pedicle, interlaminar window, and target disc space.

Discussion

Morbid obesity presents distinct anatomical and technical challenges in lumbar spine surgery. Increased subcutaneous thickness, prolonged working distance, and altered soft-tissue mechanics may contribute to operative complexity, particularly in deep corridors. Endoscopic spine surgery has been increasingly discussed as a strategy to mitigate obesity-related surgical challenges, and a recent systematic review and meta-analysis summarized encouraging safety profiles for endoscopic approaches in obese populations (5).

In the specific context of UBE lumbar surgery, existing cohort studies suggest that obesity per se does not necessarily compromise short-term safety profiles or clinical improvements. Prior biportal cohorts have reported comparable clinical outcomes between obese and non-obese patients and have not identified obesity as an independent predictor of increased perioperative complications (1,2). However, the severity of obesity may represent an important nuance; emerging evidence indicates that obesity severity can influence postoperative outcomes and recovery trajectories following UBE (3). Furthermore, UBE has been directly compared with PELD in obese patients, demonstrating overall effectiveness as a minimally invasive option (4).

Although outcome-oriented studies support the feasibility and safety of UBE in obese patients, detailed technical guidance regarding portal geometry and working corridor management in cases of substantial operative depth remains limited. In morbidly obese patients, the relevant working depth may range from the laminar working area to the target disc trajectory and may approach 80–100 mm. Under such circumstances, accurate skin-based portal simulation may be difficult when external skin contours are incompletely visualized on preoperative imaging. In the present case, portal design was conceptualized as depth-aware and trajectory-based, with intraoperative navigation used as an adjunctive tool for trajectory confirmation and refinement. Recent navigation-assisted transforaminal UBE for upper lumbar disc herniation has also highlighted that intraoperative navigation can provide three-dimensional trajectory information in anatomically challenging lumbar pathology (6).

A central technical observation from this case is that the operative depth alone did not determine the procedural difficulty. Rather, the preservation of triangulation geometry and maintenance of a stable functional working corridor were decisive. As the working depth increases, the vertical component of the triangulation triangle lengthens, and the geometric tolerance of the system decreases. In shallow operative corridors, minor soft-tissue compression may not substantially alter the effective working angles. In contrast, in deep corridors approaching 80–100 mm, even small restrictions at the fascial level may proportionally narrow the functional strike zone at the target anatomy. With a relatively short working depth, the viewing portal and working portal can maintain sufficient triangulation, and minor restrictions at the skin or fascial level have limited influence on the functional working corridor. (B) Exceptionally deep operative corridor in a morbidly obese patient. As the working depth increases, the triangulation geometry becomes elongated, and restrictions at the skin or fascial level may narrow the functional working corridor near the target disc fragment.

In this context, the fascial layer may function as a mechanical gatekeeper of the operative corridor. When the fascial opening is limited, repeated instrument insertion and withdrawal may induce angular convergence and compromise corridor stability. Conversely, adequate fascial release may help preserve the intended portal geometry and maintain a reproducible functional working zone. While adequate fascial release is a recognized principle in UBE surgery, its relative importance may increase as operative depth increases, a phenomenon that may be particularly relevant in patients with morbid obesity. However, the extent of fascial release should be tailored to the operative depth and surgical requirements. Excessive fascial release may increase unnecessary soft-tissue disruption and potentially reduce some minimally invasive advantages. These concepts are summarized in Figure 6.

When skin-level portal spacing and fascial opening are insufficient for the operative depth, the functional working corridor may become restricted, resulting in instrument convergence, reduced working freedom, and impaired irrigation outflow. (B) Depth-adapted portal design. Wider skin-level portal spacing and adequate fascial release may help preserve triangulation, maintain working freedom, and facilitate irrigation outflow in an exceptionally deep operative corridor.

In addition to geometric considerations, irrigation stability is a critical factor in deep operative corridors. UBE relies on continuous saline irrigation to maintain visualization. Increased soft-tissue thickness may elevate resistance at the fascial level, potentially impairing outflow and destabilizing visualization. In the present case, irrigation was performed using a gravity-based system without a pressure-controlled pump. Adequate fascial release may have contributed to a low-resistance outflow pathway and stable visualization without the need for excessive irrigation pressure.

Comparative studies provide additional clinical context. A multicenter retrospective analysis reported that biportal endoscopic discectomy achieved outcomes comparable to those of tubular microscopic discectomy in obese patients with single-level lumbar disc herniation (7). Another retrospective study suggested potential advantages of UBE over open surgery in young, obese patients with lumbar degenerative disease (8). Furthermore, broader endoscopic literature, including full-endoscopic approaches, indicates that endoscopic discectomy can be effective in obese cohorts (9). The present report complements these outcome-based studies by focusing on a practical technical gap that has received limited attention in prior obesity-related endoscopic literature: preservation of triangulation geometry and working-corridor stability under extreme operative depth conditions.

The present case involved an L5/S1 disc herniation, and several anatomical factors specific to this level may influence portal geometry, including iliac crest morphology, laminar slope, interlaminar window dimensions, and the trajectory toward the disc space. Therefore, the specific portal locations and interportal distance reported in this case should not be regarded as universal recommendations. Although the principles of trajectory-based portal planning, adequate interportal spacing, and tailored fascial release may be applicable to other lumbar levels, the exact portal geometry should be individualized according to the anatomical characteristics and operative depth of each case.

This technical note is limited by its single-case design and the absence of a comparative analysis. It does not establish the superiority of UBE over other surgical approaches, nor does it prove the causal effectiveness of any individual technical maneuver. Rather, it presents a practical technical observation that depth-aware portal planning, adequate fascial release, and adjunctive trajectory confirmation may help maintain triangulation and working-corridor stability in an exceptionally deep operative field. The use of O-arm navigation should be regarded as an optional adjunct in selected challenging cases rather than an essential component of the technique. Future studies may clarify whether standardized depth-based portal adjustment improves technical reproducibility and clinical outcomes in morbidly obese patients.

Conclusions

In a morbidly obese patient with a substantial operative depth of approximately 80–100 mm, UBE discectomy was successfully completed using a depth-adapted portal planning strategy combined with adequate fascial management. Anticipation of operative depth, thoughtful adjustment of portal spacing within anatomical limits, and appropriate trajectory planning may help preserve triangulation and working-corridor stability in deep operative fields.

These observations from a single case suggest that operative depth should be considered an important technical variable during UBE planning. Depth-aware portal design and tailored fascial release may improve the technical reproducibility of UBE in anatomically challenging patients. Navigation may serve as a useful adjunct in selected extreme cases but should not be regarded as an essential component of the technique.
