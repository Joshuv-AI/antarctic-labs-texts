Evaluating the printability and buildability of earth-based fiber–biopolymer composites for 3D printing applications
Authors: Suvechha Dhakal, Nitin Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of 3D printing technology in construction has gained significant attention due to its potential to utilize sustainable, earth-based materials. This innovative approach offers a fast, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional construction methods. Moreover, 3D printing presents promising applications in extreme environments, as well as for rapidly deployable structures, including temporary military or disaster-relief housing. This study investigates the fresh properties, printability, and compressive strength of 3D-printed earth mixtures composed of locally available soil, sand, biopolymers, and fibers (polypropylene and hemp). The experiments investigate how variations in composition and proportion affect performance, enabling the optimization of these mixtures for 3D printing. A multi-scale characterization approach is employed. Index properties such as liquid limit, plastic limit, particle size distribution, optimum moisture content, and maximum dry density are measured. Rheological properties, including viscosity, are evaluated using the viscometer to assess workability and extrudability. Printability is analyzed through extrusion quality and buildability, with emphasis on discontinuities, deformation, and layer count. The results indicate that biopolymer and water content, along with fiber reinforcement, have a significant effect on printability. Notably, the mixture containing 1% xanthan gum and 0.5% polypropylene fiber at a water-to-dry ratio of 0.635 was identified as the optimal formulation. This mixture exhibited consistent extrusion with an average filament width of 10.13 mm (10 mm nozzle, triangular bag test) and a printing width of 20.19 mm (15 mm printer nozzle, 2D square structure test), satisfying both extrudability and buildability requirements. Upon drying, the 3D-printed sample showed a 12.66% reduction in total height. Unconfined compression tests revealed that the inclusion of fibers and biopolymers increased the compressive strength by 2.2×. Additionally, water resistance testing showed a marginal weight reduction of only 0.33%. An increase in nozzle diameter resulted in a decrease in the total build height, with the 10 mm nozzle producing the highest number of stable layers. Overall, the findings highlight the potential of well-engineered biopolymer–fiber–soil mixtures to facilitate sustainable and durable 3D-printed earthen construction.

In recent years, earth-based materials have been gaining renewed attention compared to cement because of their economic, environmental, and health benefits. Economically, they offer a cost-effective option since they often utilize easily accessible local resources, such as soil excavated from foundations or silt collected from riverbeds10. Additionally, earthen materials function as natural thermal and moisture regulators, helping to reduce indoor heat fluctuations and balance humidity levels more quickly than most conventional building materials11. However, they have several limitations, including material variability, water sensitivity, excessive settlement, slow structural buildup, and low mechanical strength, which need to be addressed to ensure reliable performance12. So, the addition of stabilizers and other additives is required to overcome such limitations and use earth as a construction material. Incorporating biopolymers as additives can help overcome many of these challenges associated with earth-based materials. They improve both the mechanical strength and chemical stability of earthen construction, making it a more reliable and sustainable building option13. There has also been a growing focus on incorporating fibers in construction to improve material performance and sustainability. When blended with earth-based materials, fibers help overcome some of the traditional weaknesses of earthen structures as they strengthen the material by bridging small cracks, reducing shrinkage, and enhancing resistance to deformation. This not only improves the overall strength and durability of the structure but also makes it more resilient to environmental impacts14–18. However, the application of earthen building materials remains limited in modern construction because of various challenges, such as their high labor demands and relatively slow building process19. The adoption of advanced manufacturing techniques like 3D printing is essential for improving the quality of earthen construction. These technologies enhance detailing, precision, surface finish, and consistency, while also minimizing labor requirements and accelerating the overall building process20,21.

This study explores the development of earth–fiber composites for 3D printing by creating an earth paste enhanced with biopolymers and testing different amounts of fibers, namely, polypropylene and hemp fibers. By examining how well each mix can be printed, through tests on how easily it can be extruded and how well it holds its shape, this study offers an in-depth look at a new generation of fiber-reinforced earth materials for sustainable additive manufacturing. The study is centered on three key objectives aimed at enhancing the application of locally sourced soil in 3D printing of earthen structures. First, it evaluates the geotechnical and rheological properties of locally available soil to determine its suitability for 3D printing applications. Second, it investigates the printability performance and compressive strength of the soil when modified with biopolymers, specifically Xanthan Gum and Locust Bean Gum, as well as reinforcement fibers such as Natural Hemp and Polypropylene. Finally, the research aims to determine the optimal combination of biopolymer, fiber, and water content through experimental assessments focused on extrudability, buildability, and overall printability characteristics.

Background

Earth-based materials and their additives

With the adverse effects of the construction industry, sustainable solutions are being actively developed. Earth-based construction is emerging as a promising alternative to cement-based construction. Several studies have been conducted to incorporate earth-based materials into modern construction. Clay soil has been explored for use in road construction, but it exhibits low bearing capacity, high compressibility, and susceptibility to swelling and shrinkage. To counter these drawbacks, several stabilization methods have been studied, though traditional approaches such as lime and cement stabilization have shown limited effectiveness. However, newer techniques like the use of fly ash have demonstrated improved performance, and the application of modern compaction techniques and geosynthetics helps reduce soil compression and settlement22. Unlike cement concrete, earth-based materials are restricted by their relatively low bearing capacity, primarily due to their fine-grained nature and high water content. This, in turn, decreases shear strength, leading to deformation under load and potential structural instability22. The mechanical behavior of earth-based materials is influenced by various factors, including the moisture content within the mix and the surrounding environmental humidity12. In terms of moisture content, the main challenge lies in determining the optimal water content in the mixture to balance both strength development and extrudability, which is a crucial factor for printability. Since the amount of water directly affects the flowability of the mixture, it must be carefully controlled to ensure smooth and efficient extrusion from the nozzle during printing23. Ongoing research continues to investigate ways to improve these properties14,17,24–26. Selecting an appropriate soil type and particle size is essential for enhancing the mechanical properties of the mix. Clay-rich soils can be blended with earthen materials such as sand and lime to increase compressive strength.

Similarly, fibers and additives can be incorporated to improve tensile strength and enhance the overall strength of the construction material14. The inclusion of long fibers has been found particularly effective in increasing tensile strength. Shrinkage behavior, however, is influenced by several factors, including soil type, water content, and fiber inclusion. Tiwari et al. investigated the effects of natural and synthetic fibers in expansive soils and observed a 90% reduction in upward swelling pressure15. They also demonstrated a significant reduction in shrinkage cracks in fiber-reinforced expansive soils. Natural fibers reduce damage caused by moisture variation by improving the strength, durability, and stability of soil16,17. The addition of fibers helps distribute the soil’s stress-bearing capacity, thereby reducing the likelihood of micro-pore formation18. Furthermore, natural fibers are biodegradable and available at a significantly lower cost, making them an attractive, sustainable alternative. Although biodegradable, fibers tend to lose tensile strength over time as they degrade, with polypropylene fibers showing up to a 30% reduction in strength when exposed to brine and a 7% reduction in open air27. Fibers such as polypropylene have been shown to improve the tensile and shear strength of soils and enhance their reinforcement potential. Tang et al. found that polypropylene fibers increased unconfined compressive strength (UCS), shear strength, and axial strain at failure in both cemented and uncemented stabilized soils28. As fiber content and length increased, the UCS, cohesion, and internal friction angle also increased. Jiang et al. reported that the optimum fiber content was 0.3% and the optimum fiber length was 15 mm29. When combined with silica fume, Tiwari and Satyam observed a significant increase in UCS, with the optimum performance achieved at 0.5% fiber content30. In cement- and fly ash-treated soils, tensile strength also increased at an optimum fiber length of 12 mm and content ranging from 0.5% to 0.75%31. However, it remains difficult to draw definitive conclusions about how natural fibers, such as sawdust, affect the mechanical properties of 3D-printed specimens. While some studies have shown that adding these materials can decrease compressive strength, they have also found reductions in shrinkage and cracking after printing. The addition of sand to soil has been shown to increase maximum dry unit weight while decreasing optimum moisture content when mixed with kaolinite and bentonite clay32. Conversely, the addition of fly ash produces the opposite effect. The inclusion of biopolymers such as xanthan gum has been found to improve the strength of both fine- and coarse-grained soils, with a more pronounced effect in fine-grained soils at a content of 1–1.5%33. Research into other biopolymers has shown that guar gum is more effective than xanthan gum in reducing permeability and increasing shear strength34. A combination of biopolymers and fibers can help counteract the negative effects of biopolymers alone. For instance, Benzerara et al. found that a mix of 1.5% natural fibers and 1% xanthan gum produced maximum strength35. Similarly, Bozyiğit et al. observed a 5.8-fold increase in strength when 1% xanthan gum and 0.5% fiber content were mixed with clay36. Hence, using both biopolymers and fibers can be an effective approach to enhance soil strength and durability.

Concrete and earth-based materials for 3D printing

Recent advancements have explored how large-scale 3D printing of concrete structures can be applied in construction and how it influences both the economy and the environment37. While 3D printing offers significant advantages over traditional construction methods, further research is still required on the direct use of cementitious materials for printing. In addition, investigations into both the fresh and hardened properties of printed materials are essential to fully determine the effectiveness and sustainability of low-cost 3D printing alternatives37. Despite growing interest and progress in 3D concrete printing, the materials currently used remain considerably more expensive than conventional concrete, even though they reduce labor and formwork costs. Moreover, the environmental footprint can be greater, as these mixes often require higher cement content and additives such as silica fume to achieve the desired flow and printability38. Several studies have focused on mitigating this high-emission cement usage by incorporating earth-based materials into construction, which can effectively reduce environmental impact and promote sustainability14,17,24–26. Various earth materials can be blended with cement or cement-like substances such as fly ash, bottom ash, pond ash, and biopolymers, altering their behavior to exhibit concrete-like properties. These mixtures can serve as a low-cost alternative to traditional concrete. This approach not only reduces the carbon footprint associated with cement production but also decreases aggregate consumption, thereby contributing to a more sustainable construction solution.

There has also been a focus on completely eliminating the use of cement, as it is more expensive to produce and transport. An alternative approach utilizing locally available soil has been proposed to provide a low-cost solution25. However, clay-rich earth materials are prone to swelling and shrinkage, making them unsuitable for direct use in 3D printing. Any material used in 3D printing must meet fundamental mechanical performance requirements, including the ability to support its own weight and that of subsequent layers, ensure adequate bonding between layers, and minimize shrinkage. To address these challenges, additives such as fibers and biopolymers can be incorporated. Fibers play a crucial role in improving the cohesion of specimens and minimizing the formation of large cracks caused by significant drying shrinkage, both in cast and 3D-printed samples39. Nevertheless, in 3D printing applications, the addition of fibers can sometimes reduce the printability of the mixture. Natural fibers exhibit a wide range of size distributions, from microscopic lengths to several millimeters or even centimeters. This natural variability enables enhanced nanoscale diffusion and entanglement within the pores of the fiber cell walls, contributing to improved bonding and mechanical performance40. showed that shorter fibers are more effective than longer ones in reducing mix contraction. To enhance the stability and cohesion of the mixtures, biopolymers can be added to the earth-based formulations. Biopolymers are characterized by their high viscosity, gel-forming capability, and water retention properties, and are widely used in industries such as pharmaceuticals and food processing. Their inclusion helps improve the rheological behavior, workability, and printability of earth-based mixtures. However, the incorporation of different additives, such as lime, pozzolana, and fibers, tends to increase the water demand required to produce a printable mix. As a result, printed walls often exhibit shorter single filaments, wider filament widths, and greater deformation in the bottom layers, primarily due to the higher water content.

Further investigation is needed to better understand the influence of fibers on printability, particularly to determine the optimal fiber content when combined with secondary cementitious materials, in order to achieve both high printability and enhanced swelling–shrinkage resistance.

Properties of Polypropylene Fiber and Hemp Fiber.

In this study, two types of reinforcing fibers were selected for evaluation: hemp fiber, a natural fiber known for its high tensile strength and rough surface texture, and polypropylene fiber, a synthetic fiber recognized for its flexibility and chemical resistance. Their physical dimensions and compositions are presented in Table 3. In addition, two biopolymers with distinct natural origins and molecular structures were selected. Both biopolymers were obtained in powder form.

Mixture preparation and W/D ratio determination

Locally extracted soil, sand, biopolymer, and fiber were used in varying proportions for this study. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), all four constituents were obtained either in powdered form or ground as described in Section "Material Collection and Characterization", and dry-mixed. The base composition consisted of approximately 80% soil, while the proportions of sand, biopolymer, and fiber were varied according to Table 4. Specifically, all dry constituents were combined in a 5-L planetary mortar mixer and mixed at the low-speed setting of 140 rpm revolving with 62 rpm planetary action for a total of 5 minutes. The dry constituents were first blended before water was added incrementally and mixing continued until the 5-minute mixing duration was complete. Workability assessment

Extrudability criteria

Extrudability depends on the rheological behavior of the material. To evaluate how well the mixture can be extruded through a nozzle, a simple triangular-bag test was used as shown in Fig. 2(a). The mixture was placed in a triangular bag with a 10 mm opening, which is the same diameter as the printer nozzle, and manually extruded. Each sample was then examined for how easily and uniformly it could be extruded, and whether any gaps, cracks, or water separation appeared along the filament. The width of the extruded filament was measured to ensure printing consistency. An extrusion was deemed successful if the filament remained continuous and the measured width was within 30% of the nozzle diameter (i.e., between 7 mm and 13 mm for the 10 mm nozzle). Mixtures producing filaments outside this range, or exhibiting surface cracking, water separation, or discontinuities, were considered to have failed the extrudability criterion. As noted in Section "Mixture Preparation and W/D Ratio Determination", the W/D ratio was further optimized during this stage, and mixtures that failed to meet the extrudability criteria were deemed unsuitable for printing and excluded from subsequent testing.

The triangular bag test is an adaptation of the manual piping bag extrusion screening approach used in prior studies on earth-based and biopolymer-stabilised printable mixtures14,24,25,43. Equivalent pre-screening methods are established in the concrete 3D printing literature as a practical means of eliminating unsuitable formulations before full-scale printer trials44. The validity of the method in the present study rests on two key considerations. First, the primary pass/fail classification is based on an objective, quantitative criterion: an extrusion is accepted only if the filament width falls within the defined 30% tolerance band of the nozzle diameter (7–13 mm for the 10 mm nozzle), measured directly with a digital caliper. This threshold eliminates dependence on operator judgment from the classification decision. Qualitative observations, surface cracking, water bleeding, and discontinuities, are recorded as supplementary mechanistic descriptors but do not independently determine the pass/fail outcome. Second, the bag test results were cross-validated against the 3D printer-based tests: all formulations satisfying the bag test criterion produced acceptable extrusion during printer trials, while all formulations failing the bag test also performed inadequately under actual printing conditions. This one-to-one concordance confirms the predictive validity of the manual pre-screening procedure for this class of materials.

Viscosity was also measured using a rotational viscometer to assess the material’s resistance to flow and overall suitability. As shown in Fig. 2(c), freshly prepared samples were placed in the mold and rotated at 200 rpm for 20 seconds. This procedure was repeated five times to determine the average viscosity for each mixture. While advanced rheological parameters such as yield stress and thixotropy are recognized as important for characterizing printable earth-based materials, this study adopts a performance-based framework in which flowability, extrudability, and buildability serve as practical proxies for these properties. Buildability assessment

Buildability refers to the capacity of deposited layers to maintain their shape and support the weight of subsequent layers during printing. It was evaluated using two tests: the multilayer manual extrusion test and the green strength test. The multilayer manual extrusion test follows the same procedure as the extrudability test, where the mixture is placed in a triangular bag and manually extruded layer by layer, as shown in Fig. 3(a). At least eight layers were extruded per mixture to assess structural stability under increasing overburden. Mixtures that maintained their shape without collapse or significant base deformation were selected for the printability evaluation.

The green strength test measures the resistance of the fresh mixture to deformation under an applied load, expressed as a height retention index. Within 10 minutes of mixing, a cylindrical mold is prepared. A flat plate with a 2 kg weight is placed on top of the sample for 10 seconds. The initial and final heights are measured, and the height retention index is calculated as the ratio of the final height to the initial height, expressed as a percentage. A value of ≥30% was used as the threshold for adequate buildability in this study. Green strength was measured only for mixtures that satisfied the extrudability criterion, as prior-stage screening eliminated all other formulations from further evaluation.

Extrudability

The control sample (CS), containing no additives, demonstrated satisfactory extrudability. All hemp-fiber-containing formulations (CSXH1–4 and CSLH1–4) failed the initial extrudability test due to nozzle clogging; the average hemp fiber length of 19–38 mm substantially exceeded the 10 mm nozzle diameter, preventing consistent passage of material through the nozzle outlet, consistent with prior observations on natural fibers in narrow-nozzle printing systems24. To assess whether reduced fiber length could address this limitation, hemp fibers were manually trimmed to approximately 6 mm and extrudability tests were repeated on CSXH1 and CSXH2. While the shorter fibers passed through the nozzle without mechanical blockage, the resulting filaments exhibited significant deficiencies: measured widths exceeded the 13 mm upper bound of the extrudability criterion, and extrusions displayed irregular cross-sections, surface gaps, and water bleeding along the filament length. These deficiencies are attributed to the high moisture absorption capacity of hemp fiber, which disrupts the matrix moisture balance and reduces resistance to deformation during extrusion. The CSLH formulations were not retested at the reduced fiber length, as the locust bean gum binder had already demonstrated consistently poor matrix performance across all concentrations; combining it further with high-absorption hemp fiber was not expected to yield improvement.

Among polypropylene-fiber-containing formulations, mixtures with xanthan gum exhibited favorable extrudability at a water-to-dry (W/D) ratio of 0.635, satisfying the objective extrudability criterion defined in Section “Extrudability Criteria” (filament width within 7–13 mm for the 10 mm nozzle). The CSXP2 mixture yielded an average filament width of 10.13 mm when extruded through the 10 mm nozzle. However, increasing the content of biopolymer and fiber, as in CSXP3 and CSXP4, resulted in defects such as surface cracking and discontinuities. In contrast, mixtures incorporating Locust Bean Gum (LBG) exhibited poor extrudability across all tested concentrations. Even at the lowest dosage, significant cracking and separation were observed, and higher water contents did not mitigate these effects. Further reduction of LBG content to 0.25% also failed to improve extrudability. The water content in CSLP2 was therefore increased to 116%, and further testing was performed on both CSLP2 and CSXP2.

The control sample (CS) demonstrated satisfactory buildability, maintaining structural integrity across successive layers. Among the Xanthan gum and polypropylene fiber-modified mixtures, both CSXP2 and CSXP4 successfully completed the test without significant discontinuities. Hardened properties

The hardened 3D-printed samples were evaluated by measuring dimensional changes after drying, as illustrated in Fig. 11. CSXP2 and CSLP2 were selected for comparison, and the results are shown in Fig. 12. In the CSLP2 sample, the bottommost layer thickness decreased by 46.63%, driven by the cumulative overburden of the upper layers during drying. CSXP2 showed a smaller reduction of 26.26%, indicating greater resistance to layer compaction. For the topmost layer, both mixtures showed similar reductions of 24.5% (CSXP2) and 25% (CSLP2), suggesting comparable behavior at lower overburden stress where drying shrinkage is the dominant mechanism. Drying shrinkage in clay-rich materials is governed by moisture loss from the pore structure and is known to increase with higher clay content, water content, and lower aggregate content24. The higher shrinkage observed in CSLP2 can be attributed to its lower viscosity and weaker structural build-up capacity, which allows for greater moisture loss and subsequent shrinkage during drying. In contrast, the xanthan gum in CSXP2 enhances the mixture’s ability to retain moisture and resist shrinkage, resulting in improved dimensional stability.

In terms of longitudinal shrinkage, CSLP2 showed an average length reduction of 10.3%, while CSXP2 underwent a greater reduction of 25.98%. This inverse trend relative to thickness change is attributed to the formation of microcracks and interlayer gaps in CSXP2 during drying, which widened longitudinally without a corresponding change in specimen width, as visible in Fig. 11 (right). Fiber reinforcement is known to reduce crack propagation in 3D-printed earth materials by bridging micro-cracks, but does not eliminate shrinkage-induced deformation entirely14. In terms of total height, CSLP2 exhibited a 57.9% reduction, while CSXP2 showed only 12.66%, reflecting the superior structural recovery of the xanthan gum matrix under increasing layer count.

Mechanical strength and comparison with construction standards

Figure 14 presents the compressive strength of the three printed samples alongside reference values from previous studies14,39. Bhusal et al.39, using a mixture of soil, lime, wheat fiber, and pozzolana, reported a reduction in compressive strength with fiber addition. Carcassi et al.14, working with clay, locust bean gum, and hemp fiber, observed only a marginal strength increase. The mixtures developed in the present study exceeded both reference values, which is attributed to the inclusion of sand, which provides a structural interlocking framework between soil grains32.

The addition of polypropylene fiber and xanthan gum produced a 2.2× increase in compressive strength over the control. The strength gain from xanthan gum is consistent with its known cementation mechanism: upon drying, xanthan gum forms hydrogen bonds with the charged surfaces of clay particles, creating a biopolymer–clay matrix that densifies the pore structure and increases interparticle cohesion33. Weng et al.46 similarly reported that the synergistic effect of xanthan gum and polypropylene fibers produced strength values 1.9 to 2.41 times greater than untreated red clay, attributing this to the combined effects of biopolymer cementation and fiber bridging across micro-cracks. Bozyigit et al.36 also observed a 5.8-fold increase in strength when 1% xanthan gum and 0.5% fiber content were combined with clay, consistent with the proportions used in CSXP2 in the present study.

When comparing mixtures with the same fiber content, the xanthan gum formulation showed greater strength improvement than the locust bean gum mixture, which aligns with the differences in biopolymer–clay bonding mechanisms observed during fresh-state testing. The measured UCS values remain below the compressive strength of conventional concrete, confirming that these formulations are currently suited for non-structural applications only. Water resistance

The water resistance of the printed specimens was evaluated using the spray test described in Section "Water Resistance Test". Water absorption was calculated using Equation 3. CSLP2 exhibited a mass reduction of 0.8%, while CSXP2 showed a significantly lower reduction of 0.33%, representing a 59% improvement over CSLP2.

The superior water resistance of CSXP2 is consistent with the known behavior of xanthan gum in clay-rich soils. Xanthan gum forms hydrogen bonds directly with the charged surfaces of clay particles, creating a biopolymer–clay matrix that acts as a cementation binder and reduces surface porosity33. This bonding mechanism effectively fills inter-particle pore spaces, reducing hydraulic conductivity and limiting the ingress of water into the matrix47. As a result, surface erosion under water exposure is substantially reduced. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that xanthan gum-treated soils remain structurally intact under water exposure, whereas untreated soils disintegrate within hours of submersion34.

The comparatively higher mass loss in CSLP2 reflects the greater moisture sensitivity of locust bean gum, which was also evident during extrudability and buildability testing. Unlike xanthan gum, locust bean gum does not form the same stable hydrogen-bond network with clay particles upon drying, resulting in weaker interparticle bonding and higher susceptibility to surface erosion34. Although both mass reductions are low in absolute terms, the results confirm that untreated or poorly stabilized earthen specimens remain vulnerable to surface erosion under water exposure, and that protective coatings would be required for outdoor applications. The xanthan gum matrix in CSXP2 provides measurably greater resistance to water-induced mass loss, supporting its selection as the optimal formulation for earth-based 3D printing.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed on all repeated measurements to evaluate experimental variability and ensure reliability of the reported results. Table 8 summarizes the sample size (n), mean, and standard deviation (SD) for the three key quantitative outputs: viscosity, filament width, and linear shrinkage. Viscosity was measured with n = 5 repetitions per mixture at 200 rpm for 20 seconds. The low coefficients of variation (CV ≤ 9.1%) across all mixtures confirm measurement reproducibility. The addition of xanthan gum increased viscosity (CV = 3.7%), while locust bean gum reduced it (CV = 6.9%), consistent with the extrudability findings discussed in Section “Extrudability”.

Filament width was recorded at n = 15 locations across the 2D square print structure. CSXP2 achieved a lower CV of 14.0% compared to 15.1% for CSLP2, confirming more consistent extrusion for the optimal mixture. Mini-slump spread represents the average of three diameter readings per sample. Effect of fibers and biopolymers

The experimental results indicate that the locally available soil, when blended with sand, exhibits favorable rheological properties for 3D printing applications, specifically demonstrating satisfactory extrudability and buildability. The control sample (CS) was buildable at a water-to-dry ratio of 0.5. When natural hemp fiber was added, the mixture clogged the nozzle due to the excessive fiber length, which impeded extrusion and rendered the sample unprintable. In contrast, the addition of polypropylene fiber (PPF) allowed the mixture to be extrudable while simultaneously improving its shrinkage behavior. All mixtures modified with xanthan gum were successfully extruded; however, increasing the gum concentration beyond the optimal threshold resulted in the formation of surface cracks and interlayer gaps, ultimately compromising print quality. Conversely, mixtures incorporating locust bean gum (LBG) exhibited poor performance, as evidenced by severe cracking and structural disintegration during extrusion, even at higher water contents. This behavior is likely attributable to the moisture sensitivity of LBG, which may lead to excessive water absorption and subsequent weakening of the material matrix. The observed differences in printability and structural integrity among the various fiber and biopolymer combinations underscore the critical importance of optimizing mixture compositions to achieve desirable performance characteristics in earth-based 3D printing applications.

Effect of nozzle size

Printability was also evaluated using four nozzle diameters: 5 mm, 10 mm, 15 mm, and 20 mm. Since the printability of a material depends on its rheological properties, it is also governed by the shear strain the material experiences during extrusion through the nozzle. This evaluation was conducted on CSXP2 at a water-to-dry ratio of 0.7. No extrusion was observed with the 5 mm nozzle, while extrusion was achieved with the 10 mm, 15 mm, and 20 mm nozzles, as shown in Fig. 15(a–c). The maximum number of layers for each nozzle diameter is given in Table 9. As nozzle diameter increases, the shear strain applied to the material during extrusion decreases, reducing the degree of structural build-up and causing greater lateral spreading of the deposited filament. This results in wider, shorter layers and a lower maximum layer count. Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Freeze-thaw cycle testing was not conducted; therefore, the suitability of these materials for cold climates remains unverified. The absence of long-term weathering data also suggests that outdoor applications would require protective surface coatings to prevent moisture-induced degradation. Significant drying shrinkage was observed, which limits the geometric accuracy and dimensional stability of the printed structures. The measured compressive strength indicates that these formulations are currently suitable only for non-structural applications. Flexural strength testing was not performed in this study and represents an important direction for future work to more fully characterize the structural capacity of these mixtures. Future research should address fiber reinforcement configurations, shrinkage reduction strategies, and long-term durability evaluation to broaden the applicability of these materials in earthen construction. Formal rheological characterization, including yield stress and thixotropy measurements, was also beyond the scope of this study and should be addressed in future work to better understand the time-dependent behavior of these mixtures during printing.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the printability and structural integrity of fiber-reinforced earth-based materials for 3D printing, using locally available soil modified with biopolymers and synthetic and natural fibers. Geotechnical, rheological, extrudability, buildability, and hardened property tests were conducted across sixteen mixture formulations. The mixture incorporating 1 wt.% xanthan gum and 0.5 wt.% polypropylene fiber at a water-to-dry ratio of 0.635 (CSXP2) produced the best overall performance, achieving an average filament width of 10.13 mm (10 mm nozzle, triangular bag test) and a printing width of 20.19 mm (15 mm printer nozzle, 2D square structure test). Upon drying, the sample showed a 12.66% reduction in total height, with deformation decreasing sharply in the upper layers. UCS testing showed a 2.2× increase in compressive strength over the control, exceeding values reported in previous studies on fiber-reinforced earthen materials. Water resistance testing produced a marginal mass reduction of 0.33%, confirming the stabilizing effect of the xanthan gum matrix against surface erosion. Hemp fiber mixtures failed due to nozzle clogging at original fiber lengths; re-testing at 6 mm showed improved extrusion but insufficient buildability. Locust bean gum mixtures exhibited consistent cracking and structural failure across all tested concentrations, attributed to the moisture sensitivity of LBG and its weaker bonding with clay particles. Nozzle diameter evaluation showed that the 10 mm nozzle produced the highest number of stable layers, with build height declining as diameter increased. These results confirm that biopolymer and fiber type, concentration, and water content are the primary variables governing printability in earth-based mixtures. The current formulations are suited for non-structural applications; further work on shrinkage reduction, flexural strength, and long-term durability is required before these materials can be considered for structural use.
