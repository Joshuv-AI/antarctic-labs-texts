Daily Associations Between Perceived Maternal Privacy Invasion and Youth Information Management: How Do Cultural Factors Matter?
Authors: Dan Gao, Yue Wang, Skyler T Hawk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPerceptions of parental privacy invasion can predict increased youth concealment across monthly and yearly timescales. However, daily associations between parental privacy invasion and youth information management may differ from patterns widely examined at broader measurement intervals, with cultural values potentially shaping these associations. This daily diary study examined whether Chinese youths' perceptions of maternal privacy invasion were associated with their concealment and disclosure to their mothers, and whether youths' cultural values moderated these associations.MethodsThese issues were examined in a month‐long daily diary study with 146 Chinese youth (n = 2851 observations, MT1age = 18.74, SDT1age = 0.58, 44.5% male).ResultsDynamic structural equation modeling indicated that youth reported greater secrecy on days when they perceived higher‐than‐usual maternal privacy invasion. Higher‐than‐usual youth disclosure predicted increased privacy invasion the following day. Cross‐level interaction analyses showed that stronger family obligation attenuated the positive same‐day association from perceived privacy invasion to youths' concealment.ConclusionsThese findings suggest a potential day‐to‐day feedback loop in youth–parent privacy boundary negotiations, and family obligation values specific to current assistance to family might shape youths' responses to privacy invasion with less concealment. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the findings for youth information management behaviors in daily life.Keywords: daily diary, family obligation, mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal, privacy invasion, youth information management

The transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood represents a major shift away from daily parental supervision, which can promote privacy boundary renegotiation within the family system and related changes in communication dynamics (Lindell and Campione‐Barr 2017; Petronio 2002). As youth seek greater control over their personal boundaries, they may disclose less and conceal more in response to parents' increasingly intrusive behaviors. Cumulative longitudinal evidence generally suggests that greater perceptions of parental privacy invasion predict increased youth concealment (Dietvorst et al. 2018; Hawk et al. 2013; Peng et al. 2023; Son and Padilla‐Walker 2022). Although the realignment of family privacy boundary expectations may require repeated cycles of iterative adjustment (Collins and Luebker 1994; Petronio 2002; Petronio and Caughlin 2006), extant evidence for reciprocal associations between youth information management and parental privacy invasion remains mixed; studies report conflicting directions of these effects (Dietvorst et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2023), suggesting potential moderating factors. In some cultural contexts where family obligations and interdependence are highly valued, such as China, youths' endorsement of cultural values might shape interpretations of parenting and their use of information management strategies (e.g., Yau et al. 2009; Yeo and Tong 2024). Finally, youth information management shows meaningful day‐to‐day variability (Smetana et al. 2010), but extant studies on youth information management and parental privacy invasion have primarily focused on longer‐term associations. Examining short‐term dynamic processes is crucial to understanding how youth and parents coordinate privacy boundaries in daily life.

Perceived Privacy Invasion and Youth Information Management

Communication Privacy Management theory suggests that discrepant privacy expectations between youth and parents can create coordination asynchronies and boundary turbulence (Petronio 2002; Petronio and Caughlin 2006). That is, parents often consider themselves to have a legitimate right to information about their children's personal lives, whereas youth increasingly assert autonomy and safeguard their privacy by managing the information that they disclose or conceal (Rote and Smetana 2015). This misalignment occurs across different cultures, where Dutch, American, and Chinese youth express stronger disagreement with parents' legitimacy to access their personal information or spaces (e.g., Hawk et al. 2016; Smetana et al. 2006; Tang and Dong 2006). Discrepant perceptions of privacy rights can create a dynamic “negotiation dance,” in which family members must continuously adjust their behaviors in response to each other's actions (Granic et al. 2003; Hollenstein and Tsui 2019). Research has found that parents' active efforts to gain information through various monitoring behaviors, including asking about youths' activities, setting unilateral rules for disclosure, searching through belongings, and entering personal spaces without permission, can potentially elicit youths' perceptions of privacy invasion (Hawk et al. 2016, 2008; Ledbetter and Vik 2012). These perceptions occur when youth experience a loss of control over information, spaces, and property that they view as being under their sole ownership (Petronio 2002). Under such circumstances, youth are motivated to act in ways that restore or fortify their desired privacy boundaries.

One typical way for youth to regulate parents' access to their privacy is through information management, such as disclosure and secrecy. Although parental monitoring has been associated with greater youth disclosure (e.g., Baudat et al. 2020; Hamza and Willoughby 2011), longitudinal studies in European, Asian, and North American contexts suggest that youths' perceptions of parental privacy invasion predict later within‐person decreases in disclosure and/or increases in secrecy (e.g., Hawk et al. 2013; Peng et al. 2023; Son and Padilla‐Walker 2022). Disclosure and secrecy are related but distinct strategies that should not be conceptualized as opposite ends of a single continuum (Frijns et al. 2005). Indeed, studies in Chinese and North American contexts have observed positive associations between youth disclosure and concealment (Peng and Hawk 2025; Villalobos Villalobos Solís et al. 2015). This pattern reflects a dialectical tension between openness and closeness in response to parental privacy invasion, through which youth navigate their dual needs for connection and autonomy (Petronio 2002).

From a dynamic systems perspective, parents adapt and reorganize their behaviors across children's developmental periods to accommodate their changing needs (Granic et al. 2003; Hollenstein and Tsui 2019). An emerging body of research has examined the reciprocal dynamics between youth perceptions of privacy invasion and their information management strategies (Dietvorst et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2023). However, empirical findings regarding these associations remain mixed. Specifically, a three‐wave longitudinal study with 3‐month intervals among Dutch youth found that, after accounting for positive between‐person associations between youths' secrecy and perceptions of privacy invasion, higher‐than‐usual youth secrecy predicted later reductions in perceived privacy invasion at the within‐person level, whereas the reverse pathway was not found (Dietvorst et al. 2018). Parents may construe youths' secrecy as an indication of rejection and distrust, and may react by distancing themselves in the relationship (Finkenauer et al. 2008). In contrast, a three‐wave longitudinal study of Chinese youth with 6‐month intervals simultaneously examined secrecy and disclosure and revealed a different pattern: at the between‐person level, secrecy was not associated with privacy invasion, whereas disclosure was negatively associated with privacy invasion; at the within‐person level, higher‐than‐usual perceptions of privacy invasion predicted increases in secrecy and decreases in disclosure, with no evidence of reciprocal effects (Peng et al. 2023). Although both studies used comparable conceptualizations of privacy invasion and information management, age samples, and analytic approaches (i.e., random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel models), key differences in information management strategies included in the analyses, measurement intervals, and cultural contexts may explain the divergent findings. These inconsistencies highlight the need to further investigate the bidirectional associations between privacy invasion and youth information management across diverse cultural settings and measurement designs.

The Role of Cultural Values

According to Communication Privacy Management theory, cultural values can shape the rule systems that individuals develop to manage privacy expectations (Petronio 2002; Petronio and Caughlin 2006). In cultural contexts that emphasize parent‐oriented interdependent self‐construal (tendencies to integrate relationships with parents into children's own self‐concept; Pomerantz et al. 2009) and family obligation, such as China, parents often claim ownership over youths' private information, and youths may therefore feel obligated to comply with parental expectations by disclosing more information (Hawk and Peng 2025). Although Chinese youth have strong desires for personal privacy (Tang and Dong 2006), their values on interdependent self‐construal and family obligation might navigate how much personal information they decide to disclose to parents. Research has consistently shown that stronger family obligation and interdependence orientations are associated with greater youth disclosure to their parents (e.g., Villalobos Villalobos Solís et al. 2015; Yau et al. 2009; Yeo and Tong 2024). However, it remains unclear whether these cultural values also help youth to respond to parental privacy invasion in ways that preserve openness, rather than pushing them toward decreased disclosure and increased secrecy. While prior research has not focused specifically on privacy invasion, findings have shown that high levels of family obligation values buffered the positive longitudinal links between maternal psychological control and Filipino youths' adjustment difficulties (Dizon and Alampay 2024). Thus, it is plausible that cultural values can serve as a protective factor in youths' responses to parental privacy invasion.

However, portrayals of Chinese youth as highly obligated and interdependent may not align with contemporary young people's experiences (Y. C. Wang and Faldowski 2014). Evidence suggests that Chinese youths' privacy management responses tend to be defensive, in which they decrease disclosure and increase concealment when they perceive parental privacy invasion (Peng et al. 2023). Nonetheless, it remains unknown whether youths' cultural values indeed play a role in these responses. Moreover, broad heterogeneity in traditional values endorsement is prevalent in contemporary Chinese society, partly due to industrialization and globalization (Qi 2016). Therefore, it is crucial to assess youths' cultural values for evaluating the theoretical premises. Given that youth tend to disclose more to mothers than to fathers (Kullberg et al. 2026), and that mothers play a prominent role as primary caregivers in Chinese families (e.g., Ng and Wang 2019), greater attention is needed to youth information management behaviors with mothers and perceptions of maternal privacy invasion.

Micro‐Level Daily Dynamics

While prior research has yielded important insights into longitudinal associations between parental privacy invasion and youth information management (e.g., Dietvorst et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2023), the broader measurement intervals used in these studies might obscure the short‐term and dynamic emotional and cognitive processes underlying daily youth–parent interactions. Indeed, both youth information management and parenting behaviors can fluctuate meaningfully from day to day (e.g., Smetana et al. 2010; Y. Wang, Boele, et al. 2025). Consequently, privacy rule systems are likely to develop through repeated daily interactions, in which both youth and parents gradually learn the boundary limits through trial and error. This perspective aligns with Communication Privacy Management theory (Petronio 2002; Petronio and Caughlin 2006), which emphasizes that cycles of privacy invasion and renegotiation may occur repeatedly because the changing expectations held by different parties require continual realignment. Such processes can unfold in day‐to‐day feedback loops, whereby perceived parental invasive behaviors elicit youths' information management responses, which in turn shape subsequent perceptions of privacy invasion on the following day. To date, however, no research has examined these processes at the daily level. Thus, it is of paramount importance to investigate the privacy “negotiation dance” at a micro‐level timescale and to capture time‐sensitive dynamics of youth–parent interactions.

The Present Study

This preregistered 30‐day daily diary study aimed to extend existing empirical knowledge on the potential bidirectional associations between parental privacy invasion and youth information management, both in daily life and within a specific cultural context. We hypothesized that youth would exhibit lower disclosure than their average levels, and higher secrecy than their average levels, on the same days that they experience higher‐than‐average levels of maternal privacy invasion (Hypothesis 1a and 1b), as well as on the following day (Hypothesis 1c and 1d). We also anticipated that youth perceptions of maternal privacy invasion would differ from average levels on the same days that they exhibit higher‐than‐average levels of daily disclosure or secrecy, as well as on the following day. Nonetheless, given the mixed evidence for the links from youth information management to parental privacy invasion, we adopted an exploratory approach without specifying the valence of the effects (Research Question 1).

Another key question was whether youth's endorsement of traditional family and cultural values (i.e., family obligation values and mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal) would moderate the same‐day and cross‐day associations between daily maternal privacy invasion and youth's daily disclosure and secrecy, respectively. We hypothesized that youth's family obligation values and mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal would play compensatory and protective roles in the context of daily maternal privacy invasion. Specifically, for youth who reported higher levels of family obligation values and interdependent self‐construal, the negative associations between maternal privacy invasion and youth disclosure would be weaker (Hypothesis 2a), as would the positive links between maternal privacy invasion and youth secrecy (Hypothesis 2b). Additionally, we speculated that the extant mixed findings regarding the links from youth information management to maternal privacy invasion might be partly attributable to unexplored moderators such as cultural values. Given the limited research on these moderators, we explored their potential role without a priori hypotheses (Research Question 2).

Methods

Participants

Participants were part of a longitudinal project (https://osf.io/9a7gf/) examining the wellness of first‐year students at a major university in Hunan province, China. A total of 146 students (MT1age = 18.7, SDT1age = 0.58, RangeT1age = 18.0–21.7, 44.5% male, 54.1% from urban cities) completed a baseline survey and a 30‐day daily diary study. All participants were Chinese residents living on campus during the study. The sample included students from nine ethnic groups (91.1% Han), seven majors (46.6% Engineering), and 28 provinces (37.0% Hunan province). Monthly family income ranged from less than 5000 RMB (13.0%) to more than 20,000 RMB (6.2%), with most participants (41.8%) reporting an income between 5001 and 10,000 RMB. The sample matched national averages for ethnicity, religion, and socioeconomic status (National Bureau of Statistics of China 2023), but with an overrepresentation from Hunan province due to the university's location. Detailed demographics are shown in Supporting Information S1: Table S1.

Procedure

This project was approved by the ethical committee of the Chinese University of Hong Kong (SBRE‐23‐0108). The recruitment strategy is detailed in the pre‐registered data collection plan (https://osf.io/9a7gf/) and previous work (Y. Wang, Hawk, et al. 2025). Recruitment occurred in October 2023 through mandatory mental health classes for first‐year university students, reaching 2391 students across 14 classes. Of the initial 340 students who expressed interest, those reporting interactions with their mothers (e.g., online chat, text messages, or video calls) on at least 3 days per week were invited to participate in the daily diary study, resulting in a final sample of 146 participants for the present study. All participants provided informed consent.

Participants completed an online baseline survey in October 2023. They then responded to daily surveys for 30 consecutive days beginning on October 16. Each daily survey was sent via WeChat or QQ (two popular Chinese messaging and social media platforms) and took approximately 5–7 min to complete. Daily surveys were sent between 8 and 11 p.m. based on participant preference, with reminders sent that evening and the following morning. Compensation included two RMB per survey, with bonuses for completing seven consecutive surveys in a row (five RMB) and all 30 surveys (15 RMB), totaling up to 100 RMB. Participants also received a summary report of their responses.

The completion rate was 65.1%, which is typical for intensive longitudinal studies with adolescents (van Roekel et al. 2019). Detailed completion information is presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1 and Table S2. On average, participants had contact with their mothers on 19.5 days (SD = 8.08) out of 30 days. Participants completed measures on privacy invasion (2851 daily reports) and information management behaviors (i.e., disclosure and secrecy; 2631 daily reports) only on days they had contact with their mothers.

Measures

Bilingual speakers translated and back‐translated scales that lacked existing Chinese versions. The complete set of items, reliability, and factor loadings based on (multilevel) CFAs can be found in Supporting Information Materials Sections 3 and 4.

Baseline Measures

Family obligation: Participants' feelings of obligation to their families were measured using an 11‐item scale modified from the Family Obligation Scale (Fuligni and Zhang 2004). The scale assessed (a) current assistance to the family (four items; e.g., “Spend time at home with your family”); (b) respect for the family (three items; e.g., “Follow your parents’ advice about choosing a job or major in college”); and (c) future support to the family (four items; e.g., “Have your parents live with you when they get older”). Participants rated the importance of each item on a scale ranging from 1 (not important at all) to 6 (extremely important). The reliability of the scale was good, Cronbach's α = 0.86. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated an acceptable fit for a three‐factor model (CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.91, SRMR = 0.06, RMESA = 0.08 [0.05, 0.0.11]) and a second‐order model (CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.91, SRMR = 0.06, RMESA = 0.08 [0.05, 0.0.11]). The minimum standardized factor loading was 0.51. See Supporting Information S1: Table S3 for more information.

Mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal: Participants rated the extent to which they incorporated relationships with their mothers as self‐defining, using 10 items adapted from the Parent‐Oriented Interdependent Self‐Construal Scale (Pomerantz et al. 2009). Participants indicated the extent to which each item characterized them (e.g., “My relationship with my mother is an important part of who I am”) using a scale ranging from 1 (Very untrue of me) to 6 (Very true of me). One item was excluded due to low factor loading (see Supporting Information S1: Table S4), resulting in 9 items. The reliability of the scale was good, Cronbach's α = 0.77. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated an acceptable fit for the 9‐item scale, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.92, SRMR = 0.06, RMESA = 0.06 [0.01, 0.0.10]. The standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.31 to 0.84. Two items exhibited relatively lower loadings (0.31 and 0.37), but both exceeded the minimum threshold of 0.30 (Hair et al. 2010) and were therefore retained.

Daily Measures

Perceived privacy invasion: Participants reported daily maternal privacy invasion using a two‐item version of the intrusiveness subscale of the Level of Expressed Emotion (LEE) questionnaire (Hale et al. 2007), measured on a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 100 (Very much). The items included “Today, my mother nosed into my business” and “Today, my mother intruded into my private matters.” Higher mean scores represent greater perceived maternal invasion on that day. The scale showed acceptable internal consistency (indicated by correlations between the two items) at both the within‐ and between‐person levels (r between = 0.95; r within = 0.66).

Information management: Participants self‐reported daily information management behaviors (i.e., disclosure and secrecy) with an adapted Disclosure and Secrecy Scale (Stattin and Kerr 2000) on a VAS ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 100 (Very much). Participants reported on two items: one for disclosure (“Today, I shared information about my personal life with my mother”) and one for secrecy (“Today, I kept a secret about my personal life from my mother”).

Covariates

At the between‐person level, person‐average levels of maternal privacy invasion and youth information management behaviors did not differ based on participants' living area (0 = urban, 1 = rural), their mothers' education or employment status, or family monthly income. Participants' gender (0 = male and 1 = female) was significantly associated with person‐average levels of privacy invasion (r = −0.19, p = 0.022) and disclosure (r = 0.31, p < 0.001), and the number of siblings was significantly associated with person‐average levels of secrecy (r = −0.19, p = 0.027). These two variables were therefore included as covariates in subsequent analyses.

Preregistered Analysis Plan

The preregistered analytical plan, syntax, and final outputs are available at https://osf.io/5yv4z/?view_only=412c8ff6adec4c41a623925372c22206, and the data are available upon reasonable request. The analysis plan was preregistered before the data were examined. We used SPSS 26.0 for data cleaning and calculation of descriptive statistics. Mplus 8.11 (Muthén and Muthén 1998–2017) was used to assess between‐ and within‐family reliability, validity, and bivariate correlations, and to estimate Dynamic Structural Equation Models (DSEM; McNeish and Hamaker 2020). As time (i.e., days) explained less than 2% of the variance in multilevel regression models, we assumed stationarity for daily privacy invasion, youth disclosure, and youth secrecy.

Three DSEM models were fitted to examine the directional associations between maternal privacy invasion and youth information management (i.e., disclosure and secrecy) on the same day (Models 1 and 2; Figure 1) and the following day (Model 3; Figure 1). As presented in the left panel of Figure 1, the total variances of the variables were decomposed into within‐person (i.e., latent within‐person fluctuations) and between‐person (i.e., individual means) components. In Model 1, youth disclosure and secrecy were regressed onto maternal privacy invasion (H1a and 1b), controlling for the autoregressive effect of the previous day's disclosure and secrecy, respectively. In Model 2, maternal privacy invasion was regressed onto youth disclosure and secrecy, controlling for the previous day's privacy invasion. Controlling for the previous day's scores accounts for the stability of each construct over time, helping to isolate the unique effect of one variable on another within the same day. By modeling same‐day paths directionally while accounting for previous‐day effects, observed same‐day paths can be interpreted as potential evidence of temporal effects rather than merely concurrent associations. Model 3 examined four cross‐day paths: from maternal privacy invasion on day t‐1 to youth disclosure and secrecy on day t (H1c and 1d), and vice versa, controlling for the previous day's privacy invasion, disclosure, and secrecy. All autoregressive paths, concurrent predictions, cross‐lagged paths, and residuals were estimated as random effects. At the between‐person level (Figure 2), random effects, individual means (i.e., decomposed between‐person components), and random residuals were correlated.

Subsequently, each of the three base DSEM models was expanded to include two cultural value moderators (i.e., family obligation values and mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal), resulting in six models with cross‐level interactions. Specifically, the two cultural values were modeled as predictors of the same‐day and cross‐day within‐family associations between privacy invasion and youth information management (i.e., disclosure and secrecy), as well as the individual means of the study variables (Figure 2). Significant interaction effects were interpreted using the Johnson‐Neyman (J‐N) technique, which illustrates how within‐family associations between privacy invasion and youth information management behaviors vary across levels of the moderators.

In DSEMs, Bayesian estimation is used by default. To account for unequal time intervals between measurements due to missing data, we set TINERVAL to 1 (representing 1 day). The model was run with a minimum of 5000 iterations and a thinning of 2. We assessed convergence using the Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) factor and trace plots, with a stable PSR below 1.1 or 1.05 (Asparouhov and Muthén 2010) and trace plots resembling a “fat caterpillar” indicating adequate convergence (Hamaker et al. 2018). To address convergence issues, we simplified all cross‐day models by removing the random residuals. The concurrent and time‐lagged associations between maternal privacy invasion and youth information management, as well as the moderating effects of cultural values, were considered significant if the 95% Bayesian credible intervals (CI) of the unstandardized effects did not include zero. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our findings, including doubling the minimal iterations of DSEM models to 10,000, and repeating the analyses, excluding participants with fewer than 10 measurements.

The main analyses deviated from the preregistration in four ways. First, we examined the three non‐preregistered base models before adding cultural values as moderators, as the between‐level moderators could influence within‐level associations. Second, we modeled random residuals to capture individual differences in within‐person variability. Third, we regressed the individual means of the study variables onto cultural values at the between‐person level. Fourth, we used the J‐N technique rather than testing simple slopes for significant moderation effects.

Exploratory Analyses

Exploratory multilevel logistic regression models tested whether higher‐than‐usual maternal privacy invasion was associated with a lower likelihood of youth contacting their mothers the following day. Additionally, we also examined whether different dimensions of family obligation values (i.e., current assistance to the family, respect for the family, and future support to the family) had varying moderating effects on the associations between maternal privacy invasion and youth information management behaviors.

Results

Descriptive Statistics and Correlations

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICCs) for the daily variables (Table 1; ICC = 0.50–0.52) indicated that 50%−52% of the variance in the daily measures was due to stable between‐person variance, with the remaining 48%−50% was due to day‐to‐day fluctuations at the within‐person level. Participants’ perceptions of maternal privacy invasion were significantly and positively correlated with their secrecy behaviors at both the within‐person (r = 0.12, p < 0.001) and between‐person (r = 0.27, p < 0.001) levels, but were not correlated with their disclosure behaviors at either level (r within = 0.02, p = 0.540; r between = −0.10, p = 0.193). Secrecy was negatively correlated with disclosure behaviors at the between‐person level (r = −0.21, p = 0.022), but not the within‐person level (r = −0.02, p = 0.653). Additionally, at the between‐person level, participants’ family obligation values and mother‐oriented interdepend self‐construal were negatively correlated with perceived privacy invasion (r = −0.25/−0.16, p = 0.002/0.049), positively correlated with disclosure (r = 0.20/0.17, p = 0.016/0.050), and showed no significant correlations with secrecy (r = −0.09/0.05, p = 0.297/0.543).

Given that youth information management behaviors in this study were only assessed on days when youths had contact with their mothers, we also examined the correlations between the number of days of contact with mothers and all key study variables. The results showed that contact frequency was not significantly associated with any of these variables (all ps > 0.09; see Supporting Information S1: Figure S2).

DSEM

From Maternal Privacy Invasion to Youth Information Management Behaviors (H1)

As shown in Table 2 (Model 1), significant same‐day positive associations were found from perceived maternal privacy invasion to secrecy while controlling for previous‐day secrecy (β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.11, 0.31]; supporting H1b), but not to disclosure (β = 0.03, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.15]; H1a). Contrary to H1c and H1d (Model 3), no cross‐day associations were found from perceived maternal privacy invasion to youth's information management behaviors (disclosure: β = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.29, 0.30]; secrecy: β = 0.03, 95% CI [−0.17, 0.22]). Hence, youth who perceived higher‐than‐usual maternal privacy invasion on a given day (relative to their 30‐day average) experienced corresponding same‐day increases in secrecy about their daily lives, but no changes in disclosure. The observed effect did not persist to the following day.

Exploratory analyses indicated that higher‐than‐usual maternal privacy invasion predicted a lower likelihood of youth–mother contact the following day (b = −0.01, OR = 0.988, 95% CI [0.980, 0.996], p = 0.003), suggesting that reduced contact may potentially function as a passive or avoidant form of boundary management in response to perceived privacy invasion. From Youth Information Management Behaviors to Maternal Privacy Invasion (RQ1)

As shown in Table 2 (Model 2), significant same‐day positive associations were found from youth's secrecy to perceived privacy invasion while controlling for previous‐day privacy invasion (β = 0.06, 95% CI [0.01, 0.05]. In contrast, no significant same‐day associations existed from youth's disclosure to perceived privacy invasion (β = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.01]). Thus, youth who kept more secrets about their daily lives than usual on a given day also perceived increased maternal privacy invasion that same day. Moreover, significant positive cross‐day associations (in Model 3) were found from youth's disclosure to perceived privacy invasion (β = 0.06, 95% CI [0.004, 0.08]), but not from secrecy to perceived privacy invasion (β = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.09]). That is, youth who disclosed more about their daily lives than usual on a given day perceived increased maternal privacy invasion the following day.

Moderation by Cultural Values (H2 and RQ2)

Contrary to H2, youth's family obligation values and mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal did not moderate the within‐person associations between maternal privacy invasion and youth information management behaviors (Table 2; Models 4−9). That is, the within‐person associations, both same‐day and cross‐day, did not differ depending on the between‐person levels of youth's endorsement of cultural values. However, our exploratory analyses using different dimensions of family obligation values (i.e., current assistance to the family, respect for the family, and future support to the family) as between‐person moderators suggested that current assistance to the family (FO‐C) negatively predicted the same‐day within‐person association from maternal privacy invasion to youth secrecy (β = −0.31, 95% CI [−0.56, −0.01]; see output at https://osf.io/5yv4z/?view_only=412c8ff6adec4c41a623925372c22206). Figure 3 illustrates that, for youth who scored below 5.53 on FO‐C (ranged 2–6), the same‐day positive association from privacy invasion to secrecy was significant but weakened as FO‐C scores increased. That is, youth's endorsement of FO‐C buffered the positive association from perceived maternal privacy invasion to youth concealment.

Sensitivity Analyses

Preregistered sensitivity analyses assessed the robustness of results, first by doubling the number of iterations to estimate the model, and second by excluding participants with fewer than 10 measurements. Detailed results are reported in Supporting Information S1: Tables S5 and S6. The sensitivity analyses revealed consistent findings, with only slight variations in the strengths of the effects. Overall, the main findings were robust.

Discussion

Longitudinal research has suggested that perceived parental privacy invasion is associated with youth information management behaviors (e.g., Dietvorst et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2023), but the direction and valence of these associations vary between studies. Using a 30‐day daily diary design, this study provides preliminary evidence for daily associations between maternal privacy invasion and youth information management. DSEMs indicated that youth reported greater secrecy on days when they perceived higher‐than‐usual maternal privacy invasion, but these effects did not carry over to the next day. Moreover, when youth disclosed more than usual, they perceived increased maternal privacy invasion the following day. Cross‐level interaction analyses further showed that stronger endorsement of spending time with family buffered youths' tendencies to conceal daily experiences on days when they perceived higher‐than‐usual privacy invasion.

Daily Associations Between Perceived Maternal Privacy Invasion and Youth Information Management

Our findings extend prior longitudinal evidence that youths' perceptions of parental privacy invasion are positively associated with increases in secrecy at the within‐person level (Peng et al. 2023) by demonstrating this effect in youths' everyday lives using a daily diary design. Specifically, youths reported greater secrecy from their mothers on the same days that they perceived higher‐than‐usual maternal privacy invasion. This pattern is consistent with Communication Privacy Management theory, which posits that youth strategically manage information when their boundaries are threatened (Petronio 2002; Petronio and Caughlin 2006). From this perspective, secrecy may serve as an immediate self‐protective response when youth experience parental behaviors as intrusive or misaligned with their privacy expectations. During the transition to university, youth often expect heightened autonomy over their daily routines and communication with parents; thus, maternal privacy invasion during this period may be particularly salient to them and can trigger immediate shifts toward greater information withholding. Of note, the present findings pertained to privacy‐boundary processes on days when youth and mothers had contact with one another, which may have excluded forms of passive information management (e.g., contact avoidance or passive secrecy). Indeed, our exploratory findings indicated that youth may manage privacy not only through active disclosure or secrecy during interactions with mothers, but also by avoiding contact, suggesting a potentially passive boundary management strategy that warrants further investigation.

The findings also showed that youths perceived greater maternal privacy invasion on the same days that they reported more secrecy from their mothers, suggesting a bidirectional association between privacy invasion and youth concealment in daily life. Therefore, increases in youth secrecy may elicit proximal stronger perceptions of maternal privacy invasion. Of note, the daily associations between perceived maternal privacy invasion and youth secrecy were observed at the same‐day level. Our analytic approach tested both directions of effect separately while controlling for previous‐day levels of the respective outcomes, which allows for a cautious interpretation in which one variable predicts the other. Nevertheless, these same‐day associations are not fully directional in nature. Furthermore, our cross‐day findings did not provide evidence for bidirectional links between perceived privacy invasion and youth secrecy. Instead, the only cross‐day association observed indicated that youths perceived greater maternal privacy invasion on the day after having disclosed more than usual. This supports the notion that disclosure and secrecy are related but distinct information management strategies (Frijns et al. 2005), and underscores the importance of examining them simultaneously to reveal their unique contributions to privacy invasion.

The positive cross‐day association from youth disclosure to perceived privacy invasion contrasts with prior longitudinal research with Chinese youth that found no such effect over 6‐month intervals (Peng et al. 2023), supporting the view that micro‐level daily dynamics may be distinct from patterns observed over longer macro‐level time windows. When youth disclose more information, they might feel relatively more emotionally exposed or vulnerable (Finkenauer et al. 2025), which could intensify their sensitivity to subsequent parental behaviors or prompt regret about the extent of their disclosure, leading to higher perceived privacy invasion the next day. Alternatively, from a dynamic systems perspective, parents modify and adjust their behaviors throughout youth development to align with their children's evolving needs (Granic et al. 2003; Hollenstein and Tsui 2019). From this perspective, parents may interpret increased disclosure as a signal to become more involved, potentially eliciting more probing behaviors that youth perceive as intrusive (Kerr and Stattin 2000). Although speculative, this interpretation could also explain why youth secrecy did not predict maternal privacy invasion on the following day, as secrecy is less observable to parents than disclosure. Future research could incorporate parent reports or observational methods to capture actual parental behaviors, rather than relying solely on youth perceptions, to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Taken together, our findings suggest a potential temporal feedback loop in youth–parent daily interactions. That is, on days when youths disclosed more information, they perceived increased maternal privacy invasion the following day, which in turn, triggered greater secrecy on the same day. This possible feedback loop would suggest a self‐defeating cycle, in which youth perceptions of parental responses to disclosure can paradoxically undermine subsequent information sharing. This notion highlights the possibility that parental privacy invasion may inadvertently trigger the very secrecy parents aim to prevent. Through recurrent daily interactions, youth strategically calibrate their information management in light of perceived privacy invasion to maintain a balance between disclosure and concealment.

Family Obligations Play a Role

Partly consistent with hypotheses about the moderating roles of youth's family and cultural values, our findings revealed that youth who reported higher levels of family obligation values (current assistance to the family) showed a weaker positive association from maternal privacy invasion to youth secrecy. Previous research generally suggests that parental privacy invasion undermines youth information sharing (Hawk et al. 2013; Peng et al. 2023). However, our findings highlight the importance of youth cultural values in buffering the detrimental effects of perceived maternal privacy invasion. Youth who strongly endorse family obligation may prioritize family harmony and feel obligated to meet parental expectations (Hawk and Peng 2025), which could reduce the extent to which maternal invasion is perceived as threatening and thereby attenuate the likelihood of secrecy.

Interestingly, this moderating effect was observed only for the dimension of current assistance to the family, rather than respect for the family or future support for the family. This may be because items on the current assistance subscale reflect youths' day‐to‐day engagement with family members, which is most directly relevant to daily interactions such as disclosure and secrecy. In contrast, respect for the family and future‐oriented support may be more abstract or distal values that are less salient in daily youth–parent interactions. Though sensitivity analyses confirmed their robustness, caution is still warranted due to the small size of these moderating effects. Additionally, mother‐oriented interdependent self‐construal did not moderate the daily associations between privacy invasion and youth information management. One possible explanation is that interdependent self‐construal can have dual effects. Specifically, while interdependent self‐construal may shape youths' sense of duty to share with parents and adjust privacy boundaries, it may also elevate sensitivity to parental behaviors (Shi et al. 2024; Y. Wang, Boele, et al. 2025), potentially amplifying the deleterious impacts of maternal privacy invasion on youth secrecy.

Overall, family obligation values might help youth respond to parental privacy invasion in ways that maintain openness rather than foster secrecy. Our findings also highlight the distinctions between closely related cultural value constructs, which can have subtle but meaningful differences, particularly regarding their effects on daily youth–parent interactions.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

This study makes significant contributions to the literature on youth information management by examining its daily associations with perceived maternal privacy invasion and the role of youths' cultural values. By adopting a daily diary design, our findings illuminate the day‐to‐day dynamic “negotiation dance” between youth and parents. Importantly, incorporating youths' cultural values captured heterogeneity in these daily processes and provided preliminary evidence that broader cultural orientations might shape how Chinese youth respond to maternal privacy invasion with less concealment.

Despite these strengths, the findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the cultural value moderation effect observed in this study was relatively small. This suggests that more nuanced cultural values might be involved (e.g., family conformity orientations; Hawk and Peng 2025), or alternatively, cultural values might have limited impacts in the daily context of youth information management. The finding thus warrants future replication. Second, to reduce participant burden in the daily diary design, we relied on a small number of items, with each daily construct using fewer than three items. Research has suggested that single‐item measures can be appropriate in intensive longitudinal designs (e.g., Van Hooff et al. 2007). Despite the strong face validity of the items, future research would benefit from more thoroughly validating brief daily measures of maternal privacy invasion and youth information management that better balance psychometric rigor with feasibility in intensive longitudinal designs. Third, the study relied exclusively on youths' self‐reports. At the daily level, youth and parents can hold divergent perspectives (Li and Zheng 2025). Future research would benefit from a multi‐informant design that includes reports from parents or observational data.

Fourth, the present study focused solely on maternal privacy invasion. Communication Privacy Management theory suggests that privacy rules are often constructed and applied based on gendered expectations (Petronio 2002; Petronio and Caughlin 2006), and prior research indicates that youth can respond differently to privacy invasion by mothers versus fathers (Hawk et al. 2009), underscoring the need to consider both parent and youth gender. Fifth, information management strategies are likely to vary depending on the domain of issues. Prior research suggests, for example, that youth feel more obligated to disclose about prudential issues than personal issues (Smetana et al. 2006). Future studies should therefore differentiate between types of activities and topics, as the perceived legitimacy of parental involvement may differ substantially across domains. Finally, the sample consisted of university students living on campus rather than at home, who may have relatively limited daily interactions with their parents; thus, future research is needed to examine whether the present findings generalize to adolescents living at home.

Conclusions

The present findings extend prior longitudinal research by demonstrating that youth–parent privacy negotiations unfold through repeated daily interactions, in which family communication and coordination functions to gradually modify privacy boundaries. The potential day‐to‐day feedback loop indicates that youth strategically regulate the dialectic tension between disclosure and concealment in response to perceived parental privacy invasion during the transition to university. Importantly, family obligation values specific to current assistance to family provide a form of cultural scaffolding, guiding how youth regulate information management behaviors and negotiate privacy boundaries. Collectively, this study calls for future research to investigate youth–parent privacy negotiation at a micro‐level timescale that also incorporates cultural constructs.
