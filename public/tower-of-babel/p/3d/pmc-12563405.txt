Association Between Body Mass Index and Caries Activity Using ORA Test among Children Aged 12–15 Years Visiting a Tertiary Care Hospital in Mangalore City: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: M. Alfiya, Rekha P. Shenoy, Imran Pasha Mohammed, Junaid, Supriya Amanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Objectives:The association between body mass index (BMI) and dental caries has been widely studied, but its relationship with caries activity remains unclear. With changing lifestyles, rising obesity, and dietary shifts, both high BMI and dental caries are increasing concerns in children. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between BMI, oral health status, and caries activity among 12–15-year-old children visiting a tertiary care hospital in Mangalore, Karnataka.Materials and Methods:This descriptive cross-sectional study, conducted from September 2019 to July 2021, included 164 children, categorized as underweight, normal, or overweight/obese using the Indian Academy of Pediatrics growth charts. Caries activity was assessed using the ORA test, which detects microbial oxygen consumption in expectorated milk samples. Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA), and Pearson’s correlation was used to examine associations between BMI, decayed, missed, and filled teeth (DMFT) scores, and ORA test results.Results:A significant negative correlation was found between BMI and ORA test color change time (r = −0.367, P < 0.01), DMFT and color change time (r = −0.846, P < 0.01), and periodontal status and color change time (r = −0.384, P < 0.01).Conclusion:A significant association was observed between BMI and caries activity, emphasizing the need for early identification of high-risk individuals. The ORA test serves as a useful, cost-effective tool for screening oral microbial load in school and community settings.

INTRODUCTION

Oral health reflects overall health, well-being, and quality of life. It encompasses conditions like dental caries, periodontal disease, tooth loss, oral cancer, HIV-related oral lesions, trauma, noma, and congenital anomalies such as cleft lip and palate. As per the Global Burden of Disease Study (2017), around 3.5 billion people are affected globally, with dental caries in permanent teeth being the most common condition.[1]

Dental caries is a chronic disease with prevalence influenced by age, region, and socioeconomic status. Limited data hinder progress toward the WHO’s caries reduction goals.[2] Globalization has increased sugar intake and obesity, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.[3] In India, 36.7% of adolescents (13–19 years) are affected, while in Saudi Arabia, prevalence among 6–8-year-olds is 83%.[24] Untreated caries can lead to pain, feeding and sleep issues, emergency visits, and systemic effects.[5] Globally, 2.4 billion people have caries in permanent teeth, and 486 million children in primary teeth.[6] A meta-analysis shows 52% prevalence among Indian children (3–18 years), with higher rates in rural and tribal areas.[7]

Dental caries is mainly caused by Mutans streptococci, Lactobacilli, and Actinomyces. Oral flora affects caries risk and is linked to systemic issues like coronary heart disease and low birth weight.[8] Microbial diagnosis is limited by low sensitivity and difficulty in differentiating pathogens from normal flora. Caries activity tests such as the Lactobacillus Count, Snyder Test, and Salivary Buffer Capacity are useful but limited by time, cost, and equipment requirements.[910]

The ORA test, introduced by Rosenberg et al. in 1989, is a quick, simple, and noninvasive method to assess oral microbial load via oxygen consumption in milk rinses, where methylene blue is reduced as microbes deplete oxygen.[11] It requires no specialized training and is useful for monitoring oral hygiene, gingival irritation, and plaque. Cost-effective and equipment-free, it suits schools and community settings. However, it lacks specificity, cannot differentiate between conditions like gingivitis or halitosis, identify specific bacteria, or detect early carious lesions, limiting its role as a standalone diagnostic tool.[8]

Over the past two decades, India has faced a double burden of malnutrition, marked by rising urban obesity and persistent rural undernutrition, driven by socioeconomic disparities, globalization, dietary shifts, and sedentary lifestyles.[121314] Body mass index (BMI) helps assess nutritional status.[15] To simplify screening, the Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) introduced color-coded BMI charts in 2015.[16] While several Indian studies have explored the BMI–dental caries relationship,[17181920] limited data exist on its association with caries activity. With changing lifestyles and increased sedentary behavior, especially in urban children, examining this link is crucial. Hence, this study aims to assess the association between BMI and caries activity in 12–15-year-old children attending a tertiary care hospital in Mangaluru, Karnataka.

METHODOLOGY

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2019 to July 2021 to assess the association between BMI and caries activity in 12–15-year-old children attending a tertiary care hospital in Mangaluru, Karnataka. Ethical approval was obtained from Yenepoya (Deemed to be University) Ethics Committee II on March 15, 2021 (Ref No. YEC2/176). Based on BMI prevalence from a prior study,[14] the sample size was calculated, and 164 children aged 12–14 years were selected via simple random sampling. Participants were categorized into underweight (38), normal weight (48), and overweight (78) groups. Children with assent and parental consent were included. Exclusion criteria were lack of consent, fixed orthodontic appliances, or systemic illness. Data collection involved a structured proforma covering demographics, anthropometrics, and ORA test color change time.

Body mass index

Anthropometric data, including height, weight, and BMI, were recorded. Height and weight were measured using a digital scale and stadiometer without footwear. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2) and categorized per IAP growth charts (ages 5–18). Participants were grouped as Group I (underweight), Group II (normal), and Group III (overweight/obese). Oral health was assessed using the WHO Oral Health Assessment Form (2013), and caries status via the Basic Oral Health Survey Methodology (2013).[21]

ORA Test

The ORA test involves rinsing with sterile milk to dislodge microbes and provide a metabolic substrate. It detects leucomethylene blue formation resulting from microbial oxygen depletion. Each child rinsed with 10 mL of ultrahigh-temperature-sterilized, double-toned cow’s milk (3% fat, pH 6.5) for 30 s. The expectorated sample was collected in a sterile beaker, and 3 mL was transferred into a screw-cap tube containing 0.12 mL of 0.1% methylene blue. After mixing, the tube was placed on a stand in a well-lit area, and color change (blue to white) was observed every 15 min for up to 3 h. The time for a 6 mm white ring to appear at the tube’s bottom was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation; categorical data as frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s correlation assessed associations between BMI, DMFT, periodontal status, and ORA test color change time.

DISCUSSION

This study assessed the association between BMI, oral health, and caries activity in 12–15-year-old children attending a tertiary hospital in Mangalore. Participants were categorized using the 2015 IAP growth charts. Unlike WHO charts (ages 5–19), IAP charts reflect regional variations in puberty, diet, environment, and genetics.[22]

This study included 164 participants, with a male predominance, similar to findings by Sharma and Khour, who reported a higher number of male subjects among 196 participants.[23] The selected age group of 12–15 years aligns with previous studies, including that of Reddy et al. in 2018, who studied children aged 8–13 years, and Goyal et al., in 2010, who focused on those between 12 and 18 years.[1214] About 37.8% of participants were overweight, closely matching Goodarz et al., who found 38.2% overweight prevalence.[18] The ORA test showed a strong negative correlation between DMFT and color change time; higher DMFT scores corresponded to quicker color changes, consistent with Gainneos et al.[10]

Bhasin et al. studied 48 schoolchildren in Mangalore and found that the ORA test showed shorter color change times in those with dental caries, with the fastest changes seen in individuals with the highest caries prevalence.[24] These findings are consistent with this study. This study found a significant negative correlation between the number of bleeding sites and the ORA test color change time, indicating higher oral microbial levels with faster color change. These findings align with those of Rosenberg et al. and Saxena et al.[8]

A key limitation of the ORA test is its lack of specificity, as it cannot identify the exact microbial source. However, it effectively detects gingival disorders, periodontal disease, and halitosis. Its simplicity allows auxiliary staff to use it, making it ideal for school health programs. The visible results can motivate practitioners, children, and parents, and its equipment-free nature supports treatment monitoring. Variations in findings between this and other studies may stem from differences in sample size, geography, and hidden factors akin to the iceberg phenomenon, such as environment, lifestyle, and socioeconomic status.

The study’s limitations include a small sample size and the use of convenience sampling. A comparative evaluation of the ORA test with other caries activity tests was also not performed. The findings support incorporating BMI assessment into routine pediatric check-ups for early risk detection. Nutritional education should emphasize its impact on both general and oral health, with BMI included in children’s case histories. Preventive programs must address high BMI and dental caries by reducing fermentable carbohydrate intake and controlling meal frequency. Promoting healthy diets and physical activity can help prevent obesity, reinforcing the dentist’s role in dietary and weight counseling. Public health efforts should strengthen dental and nutritional education to lower the prevalence of these conditions. Integrating weight-related health topics into dental curricula can further support research and practice. The ORA test, being simple and easy to use, is a valuable tool in school health programs, especially when conducted by trained auxiliary staff.

CONCLUSION

This study shows a significant association between BMI and caries activity among school children aged 12–15 years visiting a tertiary care hospital. The study showed that BMI is negatively correlated with the time for color change in the ORA test.
