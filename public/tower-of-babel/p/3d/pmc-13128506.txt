Establishment of a Gastric Cancer Diagnostic Model Based on the Combined Evaluation of Peripheral Blood Immune-inflammatory Nutritional Indicators and Tumor Markers
Authors: Ke Chen, Jinglue Han, Xiaoxue Zhang, He Nie, Lijia Ding, Lin Ji, Huazhen Zhang, Jie Yin, Yunan Zhang, Wenying Tian, Jiale Lv, Qiang Zhan, Fangmei An, Zhifa Lv, Shuping Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop a non-invasive diagnostic model for gastric cancer (GC) by combining serum immune-inflammatory-nutrition indicators with traditional tumor markers to improve early diagnosis efficiency. Our retrospective analysis included 3,464 participants, comprising 833 GC patients and 2,631 chronic gastritis controls, recruited between January 2018 and July 2025. The dataset was split into training and validation sets at a 7 : 3 ratio, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was utilized to screen for characteristic variables. Of the candidate variables, seven key features—age, sex, body mass index, carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 199, systemic immune-inflammation index/albumin (SII/ALB), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI)—were identified and integrated into a multivariate logistic regression model. The analysis indicated that advanced age (OR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.20-1.26) and elevated SII/ALB ratios (OR, 2.63; 95% CI, 1.93-3.58) were significant risk factors, whereas higher PNI values (OR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.09-0.17) were associated with a reduced risk. The model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, achieving an area under the curve of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.91-0.94) in the training set and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.90-0.94) in the validation set. Subgroup analysis further revealed that the seven key markers were significantly associated with Lauren classification, histological grade, and tumor-node-metastasis stage, progressively worsening with disease advancement. Our study underscores the high accuracy and clinical utility of this multimodal model, providing a reliable, non-invasive tool for the early screening and personalized pretreatment assessment of gastric cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer is one of the malignant tumors that seriously threaten human health globally. According to the 2020 GLOBOCAN statistics, there are over 1 million new cases of gastric cancer every year, and the mortality rate ranks fourth among cancer-related deaths worldwide. Although the incidence and mortality rates of gastric cancer have decreased in the past 50 years, it still remains a major public health challenge [1]. The 5-year survival rate of early gastric cancer patients is over 90%, while that of advanced gastric cancer is less than 20%. Therefore, early diagnosis of gastric cancer is particularly important. For late-stage gastric cancer patients, the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system is a core tool for guiding clinical treatment and assessing prognosis, but it relies on postoperative pathological results and is difficult to predict preoperatively [2]. Therefore, finding non-invasive serum markers for diagnosis, preoperative personalized assessment, and prognosis prediction of gastric cancer is of great clinical significance.

Inflammation plays a key role in the occurrence and development of gastric cancer, driving disease progression by promoting tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune escape [3-5]. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) have been confirmed to be closely related to the prognosis of gastric cancer patients, serving as comprehensive indicators reflecting the body's immune-inflammatory status and nutritional status [6,7]. Specifically, SII can serve as an effective predictive indicator for non-curative resection after endoscopic submucosal dissection in early gastric cancer patients [8], while PNI is significantly associated with the incidence of postoperative complications and long-term survival of patients [9]. Additionally, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) as independent predictive factors are not only related to the mortality and incidence of gastric cancer patients, but also closely related to TNM staging, assisting in preoperative staging evaluation [10].

Traditional tumor biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 199 (CA-199), CA-125, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) have certain value in the diagnosis of gastric cancer, but their sensitivity and specificity are limited when used alone, especially with a low detection rate in early gastric cancer [11]. Exploring the combination model of multiple serum markers has become a key direction to improve diagnostic efficiency. In recent years, studies have found that combining immune-inflammatory indicators such as SII and PNI with traditional tumor markers can significantly improve the diagnostic accuracy of gastric cancer [12]. However, there are still limitations: first, each tumor marker is biologically independent, with different mechanisms of expression and elevation, and there is a common problem of lack of specificity. Therefore, in test results, it is common to see one marker abnormally elevated while other related markers are within normal ranges, which presents a challenge for doctors in interpretation and makes it difficult to determine whether the anomaly is due to gastric cancer or other benign diseases or physiological fluctuations, thus affecting the accuracy and efficiency of judgment. Secondly, clinicians cannot intuitively and quantitatively assess the risk of gastric cancer in patients based on the specific values of one or a few tumor markers. Finally, the differences in the expression of tumor markers in patients with gastric cancer at different stages, types, and levels of differentiation are still unclear.

Based on this, the present study aims to compare the expression levels of NLR, PLR, SII, PNI, SII/albumin (ALB), CEA, CA-199, and AFP in gastric cancer patients and chronic gastritis patients, construct a multi-biomarker combination model, clarify their diagnostic value in gastric cancer with different clinical features, and provide scientific basis for non-invasive diagnosis and preoperative assessment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Medical ethics

This study is a retrospective study, so the informed consent of patients was waived. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi People's Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University before implementation, and followed all applicable laws, regulations, and ethical standards. The research protocol has been submitted and approved by the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research of Wuxi People's Hospital (KY23001), and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400085856).

Patient baseline data collection

The transformation of chronic inflammation into cancer is a key mechanism in cancer initiation, chronic superficial gastritis shares a homologous histological background with gastric cancer, the serological prediction model we have established is to accurately identify specific biological markers during gastric cancer and effectively eliminate interference caused by simple inflammatory responses, therefore, in this study, we use the chronic gastritis patients as the control group to the gastric cancer group. This study retrospectively collected patient data from January 2018 to July 2025. The flowchart of the research is shown in Figure 1. The inclusion criteria were: 1) Gastric cancer cohort: patients from the Department of Surgery of Wuxi People's Hospital who underwent surgical treatment and were pathologically diagnosed with gastric cancer postoperatively, totaling 2,186 cases; 2) Chronic gastritis cohort: patients from the Health Management Center of Wuxi People's Hospital who underwent gastroscopy and were ultimately diagnosed with chronic gastritis, totaling 4,172 cases. All enrolled participants finished the study.

Subject demographics, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were recorded for all participants. Patient laboratory indicators include: neutrophil count (NEUT), lymphocyte count (LYM), platelet count (PLT), ALB, AFP, CEA, CA199. Based on data preprocessing, the following inflammation-related factors were included: NLR = NEUT/LYM; PLR = PLT/LYM; SII = NEUT x PLT/LYM; LYM x ALB) SII/ALB = NEUT x PLT/LYM x ALB; PNI = ALB + 5 x LYM.

The pathological information of gastric cancer includes: tumor morphology, Lauren classification, differentiation degree, depth of infiltration, invasion of adjacent tissues, etc. The general characteristics (such as sex and age) and laboratory test indicators of all included subjects were obtained from medical records within 30 days of disease diagnosis.

Gastric cancer pathological diagnostic criteria: The Lauren classification divides gastric cancer into intestinal type, diffuse type, mixed type, and indeterminate type. Pathological features of intestinal type gastric cancer: cancer cells arranged in glandular or gland-like structures similar to the intestine, resembling colorectal cancer, with clear connections between cells, forming well-defined nests. Pathological features of the diffuse type gastric cancer: cancer cells infiltrate diffusely in scattered or small clustered patterns, without forming glandular structures. Pathological features of mixed type gastric cancer: both intestinal and diffuse types coexist within the same tumor. Pathological features of indeterminate type gastric cancer: poor tumor differentiation, lack of clear glandular or diffuse growth patterns, unable to be classified as any of the above types [13]. TNM staging refers to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer [14], and pathological grading refers to the Chinese Medical Association's Gastric Cancer Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines (2021 edition), based on the degree of differentiation of gastric cancer tissue cells into well-differentiated (G1), moderately differentiated (G2), and poorly differentiated/undifferentiated (G3/G4) [15].

Data processing

Multiple imputation was adopted to handle missing values in the dataset of this study. The implementation was based on the mice package, with the random forest algorithm employed as the core imputation method (method = "rf". We set m = 5 imputations to ensure statistical stability and fixed the random seed to guarantee the reproducibility of results. Additionally, visualization verification confirmed that the distribution of continuous variables after imputation was basically consistent with that of the original data, and there was no obvious systematic bias in categorical variables (Fig. S1). Subsequently, a logistic regression model was constructed on the imputed datasets, and the dataset with the lowest AIC was selected for subsequent analyses.

Model development and evaluation

The construction of the gastric cancer risk model refers to the high-risk age of gastric cancer in the Chinese population in the Gastric Cancer Risk Management Public Guidelines (2023 Edition) [16]. Only individuals aged 45-69 years were included, resulting in a total of 3,464 participants—comprising 833 gastric cancer cases and 2,631 patients with gastritis.

To avoid the interference of multicollinearity on the analysis results, this study first calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all included variables, and converted the indicators with collinearity risk into categorical variables. Subsequently, the independent risk factors were analyzed in the training set, and regression models were established using both LASSO regression and multiple logistic regression. Evaluate the reliability of the model using several common evaluation indicators such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. Use calibration curves to evaluate the consistency between the predicted probabilities of the model and the observed outcomes. Evaluate the net benefit of the model at different thresholds using decision curve analysis.

Calculation and justification of the sample size

The sample size was based on estimations by power analysis with the rule “≥ 10 events per predictor variable”. It recommends at least 10 outcome events per predictor variable (EPV) to maintain reliable estimates in logistic regression. In our study, the number of events (gastric cancer) is 833, and the number of predictor parameters (the total number of degree of freedom) is 7, so 833/7≈119, this value satisfies the 10 EPV rule, which indicates that our sample size is enough to maintain reliable estimates in logistic regression model.

Statistical methods

We used LASSO regression to analyze the cleaned data. The LASSO regression was implemented in the R environment (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the glmnetpackage for variable selection and regularization path fitting, obtaining the likelihood values or misclassification rates corresponding to different lambda values, and visualizing the results. Logistic regression analysis was performed using the rms package (version 6.4.0) to construct the model, along with relevant statistical tests including goodness-of-fit tests and model diagnostics. In addition, the Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used to assess the normality of the numerical variables in this study, and none of these variables conformed to a normal distribution (Table S1). Continuous variables with skewed distribution are represented as median with interquartile range and compared using Mann-Whitney U-test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables are represented with numbers as percentages and compared using chi-square test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data availability statement

The data generated in this study are not publicly available due to information that could compromise patient privacy or consent but are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 3,464 subjects were enrolled in this study, and significant differences in demographic and hematological indicators were observed between the gastric cancer group and the gastritis group (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Specifically, the gastric cancer group had a notably higher median age (63.0 vs. 53.0 years) and a lower BMI (22.77 vs. 24.27). For hematological parameters, the gastric cancer group showed significantly decreased LYM (1.62 vs. 1.92), the ALB level (39.30 vs. 45.00), and PNI (47.50 vs. 54.70), whereas NEUT (3.57 vs. 3.23), NLR (2.14 vs. 1.68), PLR (131.09 vs. 116.00), SII (473.75 vs. 370.77), and SII/ALB (11.91 vs. 8.26) were markedly elevated. In terms of tumor markers, AFP (2.57 vs. 3.19), CEA (1.73 vs. 1.48), and CA199 (7.70 vs. 6.60) levels in the gastric cancer group were significantly higher than those in the gastritis group. No significant difference was noted in sex distribution between the two groups (males: 68.26% in the gastritis group vs. 70.47% in the gastric cancer group, P = 0.231). These results indicate that gastric cancer patients are characterized by advanced age, poor nutritional status, and systemic inflammation.

Establishment of logistic regression model for gastric cancer diagnosis

1) Data set splitting

Further evaluation of age, sex, BMI, tumor markers, inflammation, and nutritional indicators on the prediction value of gastric cancer risk and establishment of risk prediction models. First, we split the data into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7 : 3. The training set includes 2,424 study subjects, and the validation set includes 1,040 study subjects. There were no significant differences in variables between the two groups (Table S2).

2) Determine the optimal threshold points

To avoid the interference of multicollinearity on the analysis results, this study first calculated the VIF for all included variables. The results showed that the VIF values of both SII and SII/ALB were greater than 150, which were far higher than the critical value (generally > 10), indicating a serious multicollinearity problem; the VIF values of NLR, PLR and PNI ranged from 3.6 to 5.0, approaching the multicollinearity threshold (VIF = 5); the VIF values of the remaining variables (sex, age, BMI, AFP, CEA, CA199) were all less than 1.2, suggesting no risk of collinearity (Table S3). Given that all the aforementioned indicators were derived and calculated based on hematological parameters, the subsequent analyses of this study adopted the optimal cutoff values of each indicator to convert them into categorical variables, thereby avoiding the impact of multicollinearity on the model. Based on the diagnostic outcome of the study subjects, ROC curves were plotted for NLR, PLR, SII, SII/ALB, and PNI as predictive variables, and the best threshold point of ROC curves was selected based on the Youden index. The results of ROC curve analysis showed that the optimal cutoff value of NLR was 2.39, that of PLR was 163.93, that of SII was 553.68, that of SII/ALB was 12.47, and that of PNI was 50.98 (Fig. 2). Based on the above optimal cutoff values, the study subjects were divided into high-NLR and low-NLR groups (NLR cut), high-PLR and low-PLR groups (PLR cut), high-SII and low-SII groups (SII cut), high-SII/ALB and low-SII/ALB groups (SII/ALB cut), as well as high-PNI and low-PNI groups (PNI cut).

3) Feature selection and construction of logistic regression model

As a type of shrinkage estimation method, LASSO regression achieves variable selection and model complexity adjustment by constructing an optimization objective function containing penalty terms. LASSO regression (λ.1se = 0.0091292) identified 7 key features: age, sex, BMI, CEA, CA199, SII/ALB, PNI. These 7 key variables were ultimately included in the model construction. The 7 key variables selected by LASSO regression were subjected to multiple logistic regression analysis. We also calculated the VIF for the included variables. The results showed that the VIF values of all 7 variables were less than 2.5, indicating no risk of collinearity (Table S3). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis (including 2,424 patients, of whom 1,843 had gastritis and 581 had gastric cancer), after adjusting for confounding factors such as sex, age, BMI, CEA, and CA199, the following variables were found to be significantly associated with the risk of gastric cancer (Table 2): each 1-year increase in age was associated with a significant 23% increase in gastric cancer risk (OR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.20-1.26; P < 0.001); each 1-unit increase in BMI was associated with a 14% reduction in gastric cancer risk (OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.82-0.90; P < 0.001); elevated levels of CEA (OR, 1.08; 95% CI, 1.01-1.15; P = 0.025) and CA199 (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01-1.03; P < 0.001) were also independent risk factors for gastric cancer. In addition, patients with an SII/ALB ratio ≥ 12.47 had a 2.63-fold higher risk of gastric cancer than those with a ratio < 12.47 (OR, 2.63; 95% CI, 1.93-3.58; P < 0.001), whereas a PNI ≥ 50.98 was significantly associated with a reduced risk of gastric cancer (OR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.09-0.17; P < 0.001). Sex did not reach statistical significance in the multivariate model (P = 0.109). These results indicate that advanced age, elevated SII/ALB ratio, low PNI, and increased tumor marker levels are independent predictors of gastric cancer.

4) Model performance validation

The AUC of the model in the training set is 0.93 (0.91-0.94), with a specificity of 0.85 (0.83-0.86) and a sensitivity of 0.85 (0.82-0.88); in the validation set, the AUC is 0.92 (0.90-0.94), with a specificity of 0.85 (0.82-0.87) and a sensitivity of 0.85 (0.80-0.89) (Fig. 3A, Table 3). Calibration curve and decision curve analysis were used to evaluate the clinical utility of the model, showing stable performance between the training and validation sets (Fig. 3B and 3E). Finally, we ranked the importance of the included variables based on the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method. The results showed that the top three variables contributing to the model’s predictive performance were age, PNI, and BMI, respectively (Fig. 3F).

5) The predictive value of the model in the Lauren classification, pathological grades and clinical staging of gastric cancer

In order to further discuss whether there are differences in the 7 key features (age, sex, BMI, CEA, CA199, SII/ALB, and PNI) among gastric cancer patients of different Lauren types, pathological grades, and clinical stages, we analyzed 1,644 gastric cancer patients with complete clinical pathological information in the initial data.

Firstly, we analyzed the differences in clinical pathological features among different Lauren types (intestinal-type, diffuse-type, mixed-type, undetermined-type). The results showed: among 1,644 participants, 997 (60.6%) were in group intestinal-type, 245 (14.9%) in group diffuse-type, 352 (21.4%) in group mixed-type, and 50 (3.0%) in group undetermined-type. Age differed among the groups (P < 0.001), with a median of 72.00 years (interquartile range [IQR], 65.00-77.00) overall: 72.00 (67.00-78.00) in group intestinal-type, 69.00 (61.00-75.00) in group diffuse-type, 71.00 (62.75-77.00) in group mixed-type, and 71.50 (63.75-78.50) in group undetermined-type. Differences were also observed for BMI (median 22.72 overall; IQR, 20.55-24.86; P = 0.011), CEA (median 1.91 overall; IQR, 1.21-3.11; P = 0.003), CA199 (median 8.40 overall; IQR, 5.10-16.00; P = 0.007), SII/ALB (median 12.25 overall; IQR, 7.84-20.68; P < 0.001), and PNI (median 46.10 overall; IQR, 41.75-50.36; P < 0.001). The distribution of sex differed among the groups (P < 0.001), with the proportion of female patients being 24.47%, 42.04%, 26.99% and 20% in groups intestinal-type diffuse-type, mixed-type and undetermined-type, respectively (Table S4).

Secondly, in the analysis of gastric cancer patients with different pathological grades (G1, G2, G3/G4), the results showed: Among 1,644 participants, 194 (11.8%) were in group G1, 627 (38.1%) in group G2, and 823 (50.1%) in group G3/G4. Age differed among the groups (χ2 = 22.58; P < 0.001), with medians of 70.00 (IQR, 66.00-76.00), 73.00 (67.00-78.00), and 71.00 (63.00-77.00) in groups G1, G2, and G3/G4, respectively. BMI also showed a statistical difference (χ2 = 21.55; P < 0.001), with medians of 23.48 (21.55-25.57), 22.86 (20.76-24.98), and 22.31 (20.20-24.61). CEA levels differed (χ2 = 7.07; P = 0.029), with medians of 1.83 (1.13-2.55), 1.91 (1.26-3.04), and 1.96 (1.21-3.38). CA199 levels differed (χ2 = 9.02; P = 0.011), with medians of 7.65 (4.85-13.80), 8.30 (4.95-15.00), and 8.70 (5.20-18.15). SII/ALB differed (χ2 = 63.02; P < 0.001), with medians of 9.64 (6.00-13.52), 11.40 (7.48-18.91), and 13.89 (8.90-24.37). PNI differed (χ2 = 45.70; P < 0.001), with medians of 48.50 (45.30-52.41), 46.25 (41.98-50.52), and 45.45 (41.00-49.80). The distribution of sex differed among the groups (χ2 = 20.50; P < 0.001), with the proportion of female being 25.77%, 21.69%, and 32.32% in groups G1, G2, and G3/G4, respectively (Table S5).

Thirdly, in the analysis of gastric cancer patients with different clinical staging (I-stage, II-stage, III-stage, IV-stage, Undetermined), the results showed: Among 1,644 participants, 763 (46.4%) were in group I-stage, 323 (19.6%) in group II-stage, 483 (29.4%) in group III-stage, 24 (1.5%) in group IV-stage, and 51 (3.1%) undetermined. Age differed among the groups (χ2 = 14.09; P = 0.007), with medians of 72.00 years (IQR, 65.00-77.00) overall, 72.00 (64.00-77.00) in group I-stage, 72.00 (66.50-78.50) in group II-stage, 72.00 (65.00-78.00) in group III-stage and 71.00 (65.75-72.25) in group IV-stage. BMI differed among the groups (χ2 = 47.51; P < 0.001), with medians of 22.72 (IQR, 20.55-24.86) overall, 23.18 (21.08-25.26) in group I-stage, 22.22 (20.20-24.98) in group II-stage, 21.99 (20.09 - 24.22) in group III-stage, and 19.55 (17.60-22.85) in group IV-stage. CEA levels differed among the groups (χ2 = 55.45; P < 0.001), with medians of 1.91 (IQR, 1.21-3.11) overall, 1.74 (1.13-2.66) in group I-stage, 1.95 (1.29-3.15) in group II-stage, 2.29 (1.33-4.21) in group III-stage and 4.00 (1.52-9.42) in group IV-stage. CA199 levels differed among the groups (χ2 = 60.85; P < 0.001), with medians of 8.40 (IQR 5.10-16.00) overall, 7.80 (4.70-12.55) in group I-stage, 9.20 (5.50-18.40) in group II-stage, 10.00 (5.85-23.50) in group III-stage and 23.75 (6.42-159.70) in group IV-stage. SII/ALB ratio differed among the groups (χ2 = 143.55; P < 0.001), with medians of 12.25 (IQR, 7.84-20.68) overall, 9.86 (6.64-15.07) in group I-stage, 15.50 (9.09-27.06) in group II-stage, 15.35 (10.07-27.05) in group III-stage and 13.28 (11.31-21.99) in group IV-stage. PNI differed among the groups (χ2 = 137.63; P < 0.001), with medians of 46.10 (IQR, 41.75-50.36) overall, 48.00 (43.80-51.75) in group I-stage, 44.90 (40.60-49.60) in group II-stage, 43.90 (39.77-47.90) in group III-stage and 44.53 (41.34-47.26) in group IV-stage. No statistically significant difference in sex distribution was observed among the groups (χ2 = 1.05; P = 0.902), with 1,192 males (72.51%) overall: 553 (72.48%) in group I-stage, 231 (71.52%) in group II-stage, 353 (73.08%) in group III-stage and 16 (66.67%) in group IV-stage (Table S6).

Finally, we used the previously constructed model to predict different subtypes of gastric cancer and calculated the prediction accuracy. The results showed that for different Lauren classifications, the accuracy was 0.89 for the intestinal type, 0.77 for the diffuse type, 0.83 for the mixed type, and 0.86 for the undetermined type; for different pathological grades, the accuracy was 0.79 for G1, 0.89 for G2, and 0.85 for G3/G4; for different clinical stages, the accuracy was 0.82 for stage I, 0.89 for stage II, 0.90 for stage III, and 0.83 for stage IV.

DISCUSSION

Based on serology, the methods of diagnosis of gastric cancer is widely used in clinical practice due to its minimally invasive, cost-effective, and easily acceptable characteristics. Previous studies have confirmed that various biomarkers have potential diagnostic value, such as CA724, CEA, CA199, etc [17-22]. Studies have shown that the overexpression of CEA in gastric cancer cells is not only related to the invasiveness and metastatic potential of tumors, but may also affect the prognosis of patients by promoting the proliferation and migration of tumor cells [23]. CA199 has high sensitivity in gastrointestinal tumors. The levels of CA199 in pancreatic cancer, hepatobiliary system cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer are 683 times, 535 times, 279 times, and 115 times the normal average value, respectively [24]. The study showed that the levels of CEA and CA199 are significantly increased in gastric cancer patients, they are closely related to the invasiveness and prognosis status of the tumor [25].

However, the preoperative serum elevation rates of CEA and CA199 were 15.8% and 10.5%, respectively, indicating that they are significantly inadequate in meeting clinical needs [26]. In addition, studies have also pointed out significant regional differences in the sensitivity and predictive performance of tumor markers. For example, research in northeastern and southern China showed that the sensitivity of CEA for diagnosing gastric cancer was 49.6% and 34.78% respectively, while CA199 was 49.6% and 52.61% respectively [27]. Therefore, there is currently a lack of ideal serological diagnostic markers for the diagnosis and preoperative evaluation of gastric cancer.

Inflammation response and nutritional status play important roles in the occurrence and development of tumors. Previous studies have reported that inflammation indicators such as the NLR and PLR, as well as nutritional indicators including the PNI are related to the prognosis of gastric cancer [12]. In this study, we integrated serological inflammatory and nutritional indicators of patients, as well as traditional tumor markers to construct a multidimensional predictive model. We evaluated the expression differences of 7 key variables including age, sex, BMI, CEA, CA199, SII/ALB and PNI in different subtypes of gastric cancer to achieve non-invasive and accurate diagnosis model.

Gastric cancer exhibits marked sex-based disparities globally, with numerous epidemiological studies consistently reporting a significantly higher incidence in males than in females. According to global cancer statistics, gastric cancer ranks as the fifth most common malignancy worldwide; however, its burden is disproportionately borne by men—ranking fourth among cancers in males but only eighth in females—highlighting a pervasive male predominance in risk [28]. In 2019, the number of new gastric cancer cases among adolescent and young adult males (age range unspecified) was 2.1 times that of females (368.9 vs. 178.2 per million). Similarly, both mortality (14,971.6 vs. 12,923.6) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs; 841,920.5 vs. 731,976.3) were approximately 1.1-fold higher in males, further underscoring the systematically greater disease burden in men [29]. Biologically, sex hormones are considered key mediators of this disparity.

Evidence suggests that androgens may exert protective effects through immunomodulatory pathways—for instance, by regulating the activation of group 2 innate lymphoid cells, thereby attenuating Helicobacter pylori–induced gastric inflammation and potentially lowering gastric cancer risk in males [28]. Conversely, estrogen may confer increased susceptibility in certain subtypes: diffuse-type gastric cancer, which is strongly associated with H. pylori infection, demonstrates poorer prognosis in younger women, implying a potential tumor-promoting role of estrogen in this context [30]. Prospective studies on sex hormone metabolites further support this hypothesis, revealing significant associations between specific metabolites (e.g., unnamed steroid derivatives) and both gastric cancer and its precursors, such as atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, suggesting a direct regulatory role of sex hormones in carcinogenesis and their potential utility as biomarkers for high-risk stratification.

Environmental factors, particularly H. pylori infection, are major drivers of gastric cancer; however, they alone cannot fully account for the observed sex differences, implicating host-intrinsic factors such as genetic variation and hormonal milieu [31]. For example, single nucleotide polymorphisms in genes like Toll-like receptor 4 remain significantly associated with gastric cancer risk even after adjustment for age, sex, and CagA serostatus, highlighting the contribution of sex-specific genetic backgrounds [31].

SII/ALB, as an emerging inflammation-nutrition composite index, integrates neutrophils, platelets, lymphocytes, and ALB, reflecting both the intensity of systemic inflammation response and nutritional status. It has shown important clinical value in the diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and treatment response monitoring in gastric cancer [32], but some studies found that the effects of SII/ALB remain controversial due to the limitations of existing research: relevant studies either focused on single inflammatory markers rather than combined inflammation-nutrition indicators [33], lacked standardized optimal thresholds and corresponding diagnostic efficacy data [34], or failed to address the interference of underlying diseases on marker performance [35]. In this study, SII/ALB and PNI exhibited the strongest efficacy in predicting gastric cancer, becoming the core "anchor" of this study model, with the optimal threshold of 12.470 corresponding to a specificity of 0.871, with the high diagnosis efficiency of gastric cancer. PNI, as a classic nutritional assessment index, with the optimal threshold of 50.975 corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.867 and specificity of 0.709, indicates that low PNI is not only a sign of malnutrition but may also weaken the body's anti-tumor immune response, becoming an independent risk factor for gastric cancer occurrence [7,9].

The Lauren classification reflects significant biological heterogeneity in gastric cancer. Our study revealed that all seven variables differed significantly across Lauren subtypes (P < 0.05). Specifically, patients with diffuse-type gastric cancer exhibited distinct demographic features, being significantly younger (median age: 69.00 years) compared to those with intestinal-type (72.00 years). Furthermore, the proportion of females in the diffuse-type group (42.04%) was markedly higher than in the intestinal-type (24.47%) and mixed-type groups. These findings align with previous epidemiological studies, suggesting that diffuse-type gastric cancer may possess a unique genetic background and potential hormonal associations [36,37].

Notably, although diffuse-type gastric cancer is generally associated with a poorer prognosis, its preoperative CEA and CA199 levels (median: 1.60 ng/mL and 7.90 U/mL, respectively) were lower than those observed in the intestinal and mixed types. In contrast, indicators reflecting inflammation and nutritional status, such as SII/ALB and PNI, showed more severe dysregulation in the mixed and undetermined types. This suggests that relying solely on traditional tumor markers may underestimate the risk associated with diffuse or mixed-type gastric cancer. Incorporating host response metrics like SII/ALB and PNI allows for a more comprehensive assessment of the biological characteristics inherent to different histological subtypes.

Pathological grade serves as a direct indicator of tumor malignancy. Our data uncovered a "dose-response" relationship between tumor differentiation and the host's systemic status (Table S5). As tumor differentiation decreased (from G1 to G3/G4), systemic inflammation (SII/ALB) significantly escalated (median: 13.89 in G3/G4 vs. 9.64 in G1, P < 0.001), while the nutritional-immunological status (PNI) significantly declined (P < 0.001). Additionally, a downward trend in median BMI was observed in the poorly differentiated/undifferentiated (G3/G4) group (22.31). These findings imply that poorly differentiated tumors exhibit more aggressive invasive capabilities and metabolic demands, leading to heightened systemic inflammation and accelerated nutritional consumption (a tendency toward cachexia). Consequently, by integrating SII/ALB and PNI, our model effectively captures the disruption of the host immune microenvironment caused by high-grade malignancy [4,5].

In the analysis of clinical staging (Table S6), with the exception of sex distribution (P = 0.902), all variables exhibited progressive changes corresponding to disease advancement. Most strikingly, the tumor markers CEA and CA199 showed an exponential increase in Stage IV patients (median CEA: 4.00 ng/mL; CA199: 23.75 U/mL), intuitively reflecting the substantial tumor burden associated with advanced disease.Crucially, the SII/ALB ratio rose significantly with advancing stage (Stage I: 9.86 vs. Stage II/III: ~15.50), accompanied by a concurrent decrease in PNI. These findings confirm that as the tumor grows and metastasizes, an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines suppresses lymphocyte-mediated anti-tumor immunity and impairs protein synthesis. This "high inflammation-low nutrition" state is not merely a consequence of the disease but may also serve as a fertile soil promoting further metastasis. Therefore, it is suggested that the combination of serum markers can not only be used for the diagnosis of gastric cancer, but also assist in judging whether the tumor has metastasized through dynamic monitoring of indicator changes, assessing treatment effects. For example, a decrease in SII/ALB and an increase in PNI after treatment may indicate disease remission, while a continuous increase in CEA and CA199 may indicate caution against tumor recurrence or metastasis, providing reference for clinical follow-up [2]. It has also been confirmed that the combination of SII, PNI, and CEA has high value in the diagnosis of lymph node metastasis in gastric cancer, further supporting the research results obtained in this study [12].

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the data in this study comes from a single center, and the geographical concentration of the sample source may lead to a certain population bias in the model. In the future, it is necessary to further verify the external validity of the model through multi-center, large-sample studies. Secondly, H. pylori infection is an important risk factor for gastric cancer and may affect the level of inflammatory markers, but this variable was not included in this study, which may affect the completeness of the model to a certain extent. This study did not analyze the diagnostic efficiency of the model in patients with early gastric cancer (Stage I). Although the overall model AUC is high, the changes in serum biomarkers in early gastric cancer patients may be more subtle, and the sensitivity of the model in early diagnosis needs to be further evaluated. Thirdly, this study has potential spectrum bias arising from its case-control design. specifically, the case group comprised pathologically confirmed gastric cancer patients who underwent surgery, while the control group consisted of chronic gastritis patients from a health management center.

Considering that there may be systematic differences in disease severity and clinical characteristics between the two groups, thus the model is more suitable as a diagnostic aid for clinical evaluation rather than for population-based screening. Subgroup analyses stratified by Lauren classification, histologic grade, and TNM stage provide clinical insights but are limited by a small sample size and the risk of multiple comparisons, requiring cautious interpretation of the results.

In conclusion, this study combined the inflammation-nutrition integrated index with traditional tumor markers and clinical features to diagnosis of gastric cancer for the first time, and constructed the gastric cancer diagnostic model using age, sex, BMI, CEA, CA199, SII/ALB, and PNI, it not only has high diagnostic efficiency in gastric cancer diagnosis, but also has certain clinical value in the judgement of different Lauren subtypes, histological grades, and TNM stages of gastric cancer. Furthermore, to address the common issue of multicollinearity in similar models, this study systematically identified and eliminated multicollinearity interference via VIF testing, thereby ensuring the stability of the model. Meanwhile, SHAP analysis was introduced to clarify the ranking of contribution degrees of each variable, which solved the "black box" problem inherent in traditional diagnostic models and enhanced the interpretability and clinical credibility of the results. The results of this study may provide certain reference value for non-invasive diagnosis, preoperative evaluation, and prognostic stratification of gastric cancer.
