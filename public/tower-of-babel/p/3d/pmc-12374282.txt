Characteristics of gut and lung microbiota in patients with lung masses and their relationship with clinical features
Authors: Yanping Yang, Jiacheng Shen, Sulan Wei, Maosong Ye, Xing Zhao, Jian Zhou, Lin Tong, Jie Hu, Yuanlin Song, Shengdi Wu, Nuo Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe exploration of how dysbiosis relates to lung masses is still nascent, with few studies focusing on the microbial characteristics across various sites. Therefore, we categorized the microbiota into feces and bronchoalveolar fluid (BALF) groups to compare microbial characteristics between benign and malignant masses, analyze their clinical correlations, and develop predictive models for lung cancer.MethodsA total of 238 fecal samples and 34 BALF samples were collected from patients with benign and malignant masses and then analyzed by 16 SrRNA. We explored the distinct composition of the gut and lung microbiota and their associations with clinical features. The diagnostic models were constructed using microbial features identified through two approaches: random forest algorithm with five-fold cross-validation and comparative analysis of significantly differential taxa. The performance evaluation was subsequently conducted using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsThere was no significant difference in α-and β-diversity between feces and BALF groups. The relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P = 0.003232) and Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 (P = 0.01316) in feces group and Proteobacteria (P = 0.03654) in BALF group were significantly increased in lung cancer patients. We also found Bacteroides (P = 0.01458) was abundant in NSCLC than those of SCLC in feces group, while the BALF group was dominated by norank_c_Cyanobacteria (P = 0.03384). Smoking history appeared to be related to the distribution of microbiota, with enrichment of Parabacteroides (P = 0.02054) in feces and Prevotella_1 (P = 0.03286) in BALF. Furthermore, the patients with Sellimonas (P = 0.04148) in feces and Alloprevotella (P = 0.04283) in BALF seemed to have better response to chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. For differentiating benign and malignant masses, the combination of Megasphaera and norank_p__Saccharibacteria in BALF demonstrated superior predictive performance, with an AUC reaching 0.8 (95% CI 0.59-1).ConclusionThe microbiota composition significantly differed between benign and malignant masses in both fecal and BALF groups, with minimal evidence supporting microbial migration between these two sites. Our findings suggest that BALF microbiota may serve as a more reliable biomarker for lung masses classification, offering valuable insights for early diagnosis and clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04325-5.

Introduction

The differentiation of benign and malignant lung masses remains a significant diagnostic challenge in clinical practice, as existing diagnostic modalities are associated with notable limitations. Bronchoscopy, while widely utilized, is often challenged by technical difficulties in localizing peripheral or small masses, resulting in a suboptimal diagnostic yield from needle biopsies. Traditional contrast-enhanced chest CT exhibits limited discriminatory capacity, while PET/CT faces challenges in differentiating between infectious lesions and malignant tumors. Gene detection of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), though promising, requires further validation for accuracy and clinical utility. These limitations underscore the need for more refined diagnostic approaches in lung masses characterization.

Recent studies have revealed that gut microbiota, as a crucial symbiotic ecosystem in humans, participates in the pathogenesis of various diseases by regulating host metabolic pathways and immune homeostasis [1–3]. Dysbiosis of this ecosystem has not only been closely associated with metabolic disorders, neuropsychiatric conditions and malignant tumors, but has also been demonstrated to independently predict all-cause mortality risk in populations [4–8]. Emerging evidence has overturned the traditional view of sterile lungs, revealing that even healthy pulmonary tissues harbor complex microbial communities [9]. Notably, distinct microbiome signatures have been associated with various respiratory pathologies, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic bronchitis, and cystic fibrosis [10–13]. The gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts share a mucosal immune system known as the gut-lung axis, with bidirectional communication mediated by interactions between gut and lung microbiota and their effects on local and distal immune functions [14, 15]. Gut microbiota affects lung immunity by regulating inflammation and immune responses, while lung microbiota influences the gut via blood circulation [16, 17].

Current research has identified significant links between gut microbiota and lung cancer, as well as the distinct microbial profiles within the lungs of lung cancer patients. Studies have revealed that the normal function of gut microbiota was decreased in patients with lung cancer, with reduced levels of beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacterium, while Enterococcus was most common in lung cancer [18]. This was similar to the study conducted by Liu et al. [19] that found fewer probiotics in lung cancer patients. Certain gut microbiota, such as Prevotella9, Peptococcus and Actinomycetaceae, are associated with higher risk of specific lung cancer subtypes, adenocarcinoma (ADC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), respectively [20]. Additionally, gut microbiome characteristics have been leveraged to develop predictive models for early lung cancer detection, indicating the diagnostic potential of gut microbiota analysis [21]. As for respiratory microbiota, non-smokers with lung cancer have a higher prevalence of microbiota like Granulicatella, Abiotrophia, and Streptococcus spp. and fewer OTUs relative to that of the control in their sputum [22]. Salivary and BALF samples from lung cancer patients also reveal elevated levels of specific bacteria, such as Capnocytophaga and Veillonella in former and Veillonella and Megasphaera in later, which may serve as potential biomarkers [23, 24]. Moreover, the diversity and composition of lung microbiota differ across lung cancer subtypes, with certain microbiota like Thermus was more prevalent in advanced-stage cancers [25] and others varying by factors like smoking status and cancer type [26].

The mechanisms underlying lung tumorigenesis remain incompletely understood. Although microbiota plays a role in lung cancer development, existing research has focused on single-site microbial analysis, lacking systematic exploration of gut-lung microbiota interactions. Our study aims to characterize the gut and lung microbiota in patients with benign and malignant lung masses, elucidating potential interconnections between these microbiotas and exploring their possible mechanistic roles in lung carcinogenesis. Furthermore, we investigate the relationship between microbiota and clinical characteristics. Based on differential microbiota, we develop a predictive model to provide novel diagnostic insights and methodologies for differentiating benign and malignant masses.

Methods

Patients recruitment

Patients who were admitted for evaluation of lung masses were prospectively enrolled in Zhongshan hospital, Fudan University between January and May 2020. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: pregnant; had undergone any procedure other than bronchoscopy to evaluate the lung mass; combined history of digestive system surgery; acute digestive or pulmonary infections in the past two months; use of antibiotics, intestinal microbial agents, oral laxatives, etc. within two weeks before enrollment; history of cancer of other systems. The baseline characteristics of age, gender, smoking history, treatment efficacy, tumor pathological type and stage were collected, and the clinical efficacy response was evaluated according to the updated RECIST 1.1 [27]. Response (R) was defined if patients had a complete response (CR), partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD) for at least 6 months, while progressive disease (PD) or SD lasting less than 6 months, it is defined as non-response (NR). Participants were categorized according to the TNM staging system into early-stage tumors (stages I/II) and advanced-stage tumors (stages III/IV), where stage I typically represents small tumors confined to their original site while stage II indicates larger tumors or those with limited local spread to adjacent tissues, and advanced-stage tumors (stages III and IV) are characterized by extensive local invasion of surrounding tissues or structures or distant metastasis. All patients had been given full access to information and consented to participate in this study on the day of admission to evaluate the lung mass. The protocol for this prospective study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Board of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (approval number: B2020-019R).

Samples collection

Feces

All enrolled participants provided fecal samples collected by standardized rectal swab procedure. Following a 12-hour fasting period, the perianal area was thoroughly cleansed with soap, water, and 75% alcohol. Using sterile swabs inserted 4–5 cm into the anal canal, samples were obtained by gentle rotation and immediately transferred to sterile tubes. Within 30 min post-collection, approximately 1 g of fresh sample was processed in a biosafety cabinet for gut microbiota analysis, with the remainder stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

BALF

All eligible participants underwent bronchoscopy following nebulized administration of 2% lidocaine for topical anesthesia. Under direct visualization, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed in the bronchus contralateral to the identified pulmonary mass using 20–30 mL sterile 0.9% saline, with approximately 10 mL of BALF successfully obtained per procedure. The samples were aliquoted into 2 mL sterile centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to obtain cellular pellets, which were subsequently stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Microbial DNA was extracted from fecal and BALF samples using the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) according to manufacturer’s protocols. The final DNA concentration and purification were determined by NanoDrop 2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA), and DNA quality was checked by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3 to V4 regions of the 16 S rRNA were amplified by thermocycler PCR system (GeneAmp 9700, ABI, USA). The amplification was conducted using the following program: 5 min of denaturation at 95 °C, 27 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s for annealing at 55 °C, and 30 s for extension at 72 °C, and a final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR reactions were performed in triplicate 20 µL mixture containing 4 µL of 5 × FastPfu Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of FastPfu Polymerase and 10 ng of template DNA. The PCR products were extracted from a 2% agarose gel. AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) was used to further purify the amplified products and QuantiFluor™-ST (Promega, USA) was used for quantifying the DNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Illumina MiSeq sequencing

Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Processing of sequencing data

Raw fastq files were demultiplexed, quality-filtered by Trimmomatic and merged by FLASH with the following criteria: (i) The reads were truncated at any site receiving an average quality score < 20 over a 50 bp sliding window. (ii) Primers were exactly matched allowing 2 nucleotide mismatching, and reads containing ambiguous bases were removed. (iii) Sequences whose overlap longer than 10 bp were merged according to their overlap sequence.

Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered with 97% similarity cutoff using UPARSE(version 7.1 http://drive5.com/uparse/) and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME. The taxonomy of each 16 S rRNA gene sequence was analyzed by RDP Classifier algorithm (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) against the Silva (SSU123) 16SrRNA database using confidence threshold of 70%. Statistical analysis

Chao1 index, Ace index, Shannon index and Simpson diversity index were used for the richness and diversity of samples. Random subsampling was conducted to equalize the read sizes of samples for comparison among samples. The overall phylogenetic distance between two groups was visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Microbiota differences were assessed using PERMANOVA based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices. The method partitions variance components to quantify between-group differences while testing significance through permutation procedures (n = 999). The diagnostic models were constructed using microbial features identified through two approaches: random forest algorithm with ten-fold cross-validation and comparative analysis of significantly differential taxa. The performance evaluation was subsequently conducted using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for differences between categories, and Spearman correlation test was used for association of continuous variables. Continuous variables were compared between groups by t-test, and categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square test. Data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and number (%) for categorical variables. P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS 25.0 was used to perform all statistical analyses in this study.

Results

Characteristics and taxonomic profiles of the patients with benign or malignant masses in two groups

A total of 238 feces and 34 BALF samples were collected from patients with benign and malignant masses, which were divided into feces group and BALF group. In the feces group, 190 individuals were diagnosed with lung cancer and 48 with benign mass-like lesion. According to the pathological type of lung cancer, 155 cases were classified as NSCLC, including 100 cases of ADC, 52 cases of SCC, 1 case of adenosquamous carcinoma and 2 cases of large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, 29 cases were small cell lung cancer (SCLC), and the pathological types of 6 patients were to be determined by further examination. In the BALF group, 26 patients were diagnosed with lung cancer, and 8 were diagnosed with a benign mass-like lesion. In lung cancer patients, 6 patients were diagnosed with SCLC, and 20 patients were diagnosed with NSCLC. Of the latter, 14 patients had ADC, 5 patients had SCC and 1 patient with adenosquamous carcinomas. The baseline characteristics of the patients were shown in Tables 1 and 2 and no significant differences were observed between the two groups.

Comparison of microbiota composition of benign or malignant masses between the two groups

Differences in α- and β-diversity between the two groups

In order to evaluate the species richness and evenness of benign and malignant masses between the two groups, ace index and chao1 index were used to analyze the α-diversity for both feces (Fig. 2A) and BALF group (Fig. 2B), and PCoA and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDs) were used to evaluate the β-diversity for feces (Fig. 2C) and BALF group (Fig. 2D). The results showed that there was no significant difference in α-and β-diversity between feces and BALF groups. Furthermore, statistical analysis using PERMANOVA demonstrated no significant variations in microbiota composition between benign and malignant masses, with minimal effect sizes observed for both gut (R² = 0.004, P = 0.494) and BALF groups (R² = 0.002, P = 0.740).

Distinct composition between the two groups

In feces group, 21 phyla, 438 genera and 1002 species were detected, and Firmicutes to be the most abundant phylum in both benign and malignant masses, followed by Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia. Firmicutes was significantly higher in malignant mass than in benign mass (P = 0.01209) (Figure S1G, S2A). At the genus level, Bacteroides, Escherichia Shigella, Blautia, Faecalibacterium, Prevotella and Subdoligranulum were common (Figure S1H). Species difference analysis showed that Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P = 0.003232) and Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 (P = 0.01316) were more abundant in tumors (Fig. 3A), with Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 exhibited the difference with a 5-fold increase in abundance (Log2FC = 3), suggesting its potential role in tumor microenvironment modulation. And at the species level, uncultured_Blautia_sp._g_Blautia (P = 0.0255) and Fusobacterium_ulcerans (P = 0.04704) showed significant differences between benign and malignant masses (Figure S2B).

In BALF group, 34 phyla, 616 genera and 1037 species were detected. Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria were the most common phyla, and Streptococcus, Prevotella_7, Neisseria and Veillonella were most common genera in the study samples (Figure S1J, K). The relative abundance of two phyla Firmicutes (P = 0.01957) and Saccharibacteria (P = 0.02064) and two genera norank_p_saccharibacteria (P = 0.01658), megasphaera (P = 0.01483) were significant difference between the two groups (Figure S2C, 3B). At the species level, the abundance of Megasphaera_micronuciformis (P = 0.008207) and TM7_phylum_sp._oral_clone_DR034 (P = 0.01956) was significantly higher in benign patients than in lung cancer patients (Figure S2D).

To understand the composition and relative abundance of the benign and malignant groups, we analyzed the microbiota at phylum, genus and species levels. Based on the feces (Figure S1A, B, C) and BALF microbiota heatmap (Figure S1D, E, F), the relative abundances of the 18 most dominant phyla, 30 genera and 30 species were shown, and the relative abundances of dominant species at different levels are revealed according to the mass type (Figure S1G-L).

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to examine the difference of benign and malignant masses microbiota in feces and BALF group at phyla to species level. linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score > 3 was used to measure the influence of species on the difference effect. The results showed that in feces group, Firmicutes (P = 0.01205) at phylum level, Selenomonadaceae at family level (P = 0.03667) and uncultured_Blautia_sp__g__Blautia at species level (P = 0.02566) were significantly increased in malignant mass, while uncultured_bacterium_g__Dorea at species level (P = 0.01576) and Dorea at genus level (P = 0.02229) were more common in benign mass (Fig. 3C, E). In BALF group, Proteobacteria at phylum level (P = 0.03476) and Lachnospiraceae at family level (P = 0.02555) were more abundant in malignant mass, while Firmicutes at phylum level (P = 0.01853) and Megasphaera_micronuciformis at the species level (p = 0.00728) were significantly increased in benign mass (Fig. 3D, F). As shown in the results, the composition and distribution of microbiota in benign and malignant masses were significantly different in feces and BALF groups, suggesting that the microbial environment of the two sites might be independent, and the microbiota interaction between them might be mediated by other pathways, such as activation of immune cells or cytokines.

Relationship between microbiota and clinical features of lung cancer patients

To better understand the relationship between microbiota and clinical features of lung cancer patients, we focused on three aspects of histology, smoking history and response, and analyzed them in the feces and BALF group respectively. In feces group, it was found that one phylum Bacteroidota (P = 0.04098) (Figure S3A) and four genera Bacteroides (P = 0.01458), Butyricicoccus (P = 0.045), Bilophila (p = 0.03991) and Lachnospira (P = 0.009935) (Fig. 4A) were abundant in patients with NSCLC than those of SCLC. Notably, Bacteroides showed a significant 2-fold enrichment in NSCLC (Fig. 4A, S4A). In contrast, norank_c_Cyanobacteria (45-fold enrichment, P = 0.03384) (Fig. 4B, S4B) were more common in NSCLC in BALF group, while the majority of differential bacteria were found in SCLC, which was dominated by Bacteroidota (P = 0.01155) (Figure S3B), and four genera Alloprevotella (P = 0.004225), Treponema_2 (P = 0.03564), Sneathia (P = 0.0009113) and Prevotella_1 (P = 0.004951) (Fig. 4B).

In feces group, smoking was associated with a significant increase in the abundance of one phylum Verrucomicrobiota (P = 0.04545) (Figure S3C) and two genera Parabacteroides (2-fold enrichment, P = 0.02054) and Alistipes (1-fold enrichment, P = 0.01105) (Fig. 4C, S4C). At the genus level of the BALF group, the relative abundance of Prevotella_1 (2-fold enrichment, P = 0.03286) and Comamonas (P = 0.02447) differed significantly based on the smoking history of lung cancer patients, with the former being higher and the latter being lower in smokers (Fig. 4D, S4D).

Furthermore, in feces group, the relative abundance of one phylum cyanobacteria (P = 0.0274) was decreased (Figure S3D) and two genera Enterococcus (P = 0.03947) and Alistipes (P = 0.04282) were increased in the responding patients compared with nonresponding to chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy patients (Figure S3E). Patients who responded were further analyzed and divided into PR_CR and SD groups. Four genera Sellimonas (11-fold enrichment, P = 0.04148), Catenibacterium (P = 0.0139), Allisonella (5-fold enrichment, P = 0.007306) and Catenibacillus (P = 0.005275) were found to be significantly increased in PR_CR patients (Fig. 4E, S4E). For BALF group, PR_CR patients were higher in Bacteroidetes (P = 0.04283) (Figure S3F), Alloprevotella (2-fold enrichment, P = 0.04283) and Capnocytophaga (11-fold enrichment, P = 0.01421) than SD patients (Fig. 4F, S4F).

Predictive value of microbiota in lung cancer patients

To robustly evaluate the predictive value of microbiota for differentiating benign and malignant masses, we implemented a random forest classifier with five-fold cross-validation. The cross-validation results (Fig. 5A) demonstrate consistent model performance across all folds, with the optimal accuracy of 83.33%, precision of 84.09%, recall of 97.37%, and F1-score of 90.24%. While the AUC values showed greater variability, Fold-1 achieved the strongest discriminative capacity (AUC = 0.8075), suggesting potential for optimization through feature selection (Fig. 5B). The feature importance rankings of microbiota across five experimental iterations are shown in Figure S5A, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals presented in Figure S5B. The high recall rates indicate particular utility for predictive malignancy when combined with gut microbiota. Furthermore, we performed the ROC curve with Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 at the genus level. The results demonstrated that both biomarkers achieved an AUC of 0.71 (Fig. 5C). As for BALF group, the combination of Megasphaera and norank_p__Saccharibacteria showed a higher AUC value (AUC = 0.8, 95%CI: 0.59-1) than each alone (AUC = 0.79) (Fig. 5D). Collectively, the differential microbiota in BALF group had good predictive effect, while the feces group needs further study.

Discussion

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted associations between microbiota and cancer-related immune responses. Potential mechanistic links may involve microbial regulation of immune cell activity, modulation of inflammatory cytokine profiles, and alterations in metabolic pathways, which could influence lung cancer development [28–32]. Furthermore, the observed association between antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and elevated lung cancer risk [33] suggests microbiota-mediated carcinogenesis. When combined with chemoimmunotherapy, therapeutic interventions like fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) or short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) supplementation may enhance antitumor immunity by promoting effector T cell activity, as evidenced by recent study [34]. Gut microbiome plays a pivotal role in modulating immune, particularly in promoting M1 macrophage and Th1 lymphocyte differentiation, enhancing the activation of CD4+/CD8+ T lymphocytes and upregulating PD-1 expression on lymphocytes [29, 31]. Mechanistically, gut microbiota and its products traverse the intestinal epithelium into systemic circulation [35], where they can initiate TLR-dependent signaling cascades that amplify T-cell responses in distal tissues [36]. This translocation phenomenon may enhance antitumor immunity through TLR activation or immunological memory, as evidenced in Enterococcus-SCLC interactions [37]. Reduced airway microbial diversity impairs antigen presenting cells (APC) function, dampening anti-tumor immunity [35]. Following recognition by Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways are activated, subsequently promoting Th17 cell differentiation [31]. This triggers compensatory immunosuppression through Treg cells and M2 macrophages expansion and PD-L1 upregulation [38]. Notably, Pasteurella correlates with CD8+ T cell infiltration while suppressing M2 macrophages, suggesting distinct immunomodulatory effects [39]. Current research remains limited, particularly regarding early-stage lesions and comparative multi-site microbiota analyses. Our findings demonstrate statistically significant microbial signatures distinguishing benign and malignant lung masses, suggesting their potential utility as diagnostic biomarkers.

The increase of Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 in the gut had been associated with poor prognosis in various diseases, including lung cancer, mental disorders and hypertension [40–43]. In line with Ni et al. [44], we observed similar enrichment of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group in lung cancer. Low-biomass lung microbiota is known to participate in immune modulation [45], our study revealed Firmicutes dominating healthy controls, mirroring findings from Morris et al. [46] and Segal et al. [47], while significant Proteobacteria enrichment in cancer that parallels the observations of Greathouse et al. [48]. Notably, we identified reduced norank_p_Saccharibacteria and Megasphaera in BALF from cancer patients, showing diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.8). These taxa may represent candidate biomarkers, though their functional roles require experimental validation. While Jin et al. [49] reported tumor burden correlations with airway but not gut microbiota, we similarly found distinct compartment-specific microbial profiles. The observed gut-lung microbiome disparities suggest site-specific ecological niches, though potential systemic connections through immune or metabolic pathways remain speculative in our study design. The microbial signatures we identified could improve lung cancer diagnosis by complementing existing imaging and liquid biopsy methods. Site-specific microbial patterns may guide personalized sampling and treatment strategies. These findings support integrating microbiome analysis into clinical oncology practice to enhance diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic decision-making.

Current evidence on microbiota-lung cancer histopathological types associations remains limited, though the Mendelian randomization study of Ma et al. [50] identified distinct gut microbial signatures across NSCLC, SCLC, ADC, and SCC subtypes, suggesting possible mechanistic links. In our cross-sectional analysis, we observed differential abundances of the genus Bacteroides and phylum Bacteroidota in feces and norank_c_Cyanobacteria and Unclassidied_p_Proteobacteria in BALF between NSCLC and SCLC, which may serve as diagnostic discriminators. Additionally, smoking history correlated with altered abundances of Parabacteroides and Alistipes in feces and Prevotella_1 in BALF, consistent with prior reports of tobacco-induced microbial shifts [51–53]. Emerging evidence suggests associations between gut and lung microbiota alterations and mortality outcomes in lung cancer patients receiving combination therapy [54–56]. Recent studies have demonstrated that probiotic supplementation may potentiate cisplatin’s antitumor effects, with approximately 30% risk reduction observed via immunomodulatory mechanisms [57, 58]. Consistent with the metagenomic sequencing results from Dora et al. [59] demonstrating Alistipes enrichment in long progression-free survival (PFS) patients versus Streptococcus predominance in short-PFS cases, we similarly identified a significant association between Alistipes abundance and positive treatment response in our study. Notably, treatment-responsive patients (PR/CR) exhibited distinct microbial profiles, with fecal Sellimonas, Catenibacterium, and Allisonella along with BALF Alloprevotella and Capnocytophaga enrichment compared to SD cases, suggesting the potential for microbiota-targeted interventions to improve treatment outcomes. Our study identified clinically significant microbial signatures with potential applications in lung cancer management. The distinct microbial profiles observed in fecal and BALF samples effectively differentiated between NSCLC and SCLC subtypes, suggesting their utility as complementary diagnostic biomarkers when integrated with existing diagnostic modalities. Smoking-related microbial alterations demonstrated potential for risk stratification in clinical screening protocols. Notably, treatment-responsive patients exhibited characteristic microbial patterns that may serve as predictive biomarkers for therapeutic monitoring and targets for microbial modulation strategies. The results underscore the potential of microbial profiling to enhance precision oncology approaches, although further validation in prospective clinical studies is warranted.

One potential limitation of this study was the disparity in sample sizes between the feces and BALF group. This discrepancy arises because bronchoscopy, an invasive procedure, was only performed in patients with suspected malignant masses, whereas healthy controls or those with benign masses typically did not undergo this examination. Furthermore, the limited sample size in the BALF group may affect model generalizability. To address this issue, we will subsequently supplement BALF samples, as well as fecal samples from benign masses and healthy controls. This will help improve the comparability and generalizability of our findings across different patient groups. Second, we acknowledge that certain potentially influential factors including lung function, dietary habits, and body composition indices were not systematically examined in the current investigation, which may represent an important limitation when interpreting the observed microbiota differences between benign and malignant lung masses. Finally, future studies should elucidate the molecular mechanisms by how microbiota influence lung masses development, offering insights into early detection and therapeutic targeting.

Conclusion

Our study revealed distinct differences in both feces and BALF groups between patients with benign and malignant masses. Notably, the lung microbiota exhibited a unique composition, showing little evidence of microbial migration from the gut. Certain microbiota was associated with clinical features, offering predictive value for lung cancer. These findings suggest that both gut and lung microbiota could serve as novel biomarkers to differentiate the type of masses. Importantly, lung microbiota may hold greater significance than gut microbiota in the early diagnosis of lung cancer. Further large-scale studies are warranted to validate the role of microbiota in lung cancer patients.
