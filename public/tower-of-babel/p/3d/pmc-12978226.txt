Atmospheric systems drive spatiotemporal divergence of dust and moisture changes across Asia over the past 130,000 years
Authors: Guoqiang Li, He Yang, Xiaojian Zhang, Jiaxin Li, Peirui Du, Dongmei Han, Run Zhou, Yougui Song, Kathryn E. Fitzsimmons, Aditi K. Dave, Shuangwen Yi, Shugang Kang, Thomas Stevens, Jan-Pieter Buylaert, Zhongping Lai, Yixuan Wang, Ji Lai, Zhongfeng Yan, Jin Yang, Xiaoyan Wang, Luo Qin, Siyi Gou, Chunzhu Chen, Chongyi E, Qiuyue Zhao, Leibin Wang, Tao Lu, Yanqing Deng, Lele Shu, Lupeng Yu, Xiangjun Liu, Zhiyong Ling, Qiufang Chang, Haitao Wei, Haichao Xie, Fahu Chen

ABSTRACT
Loess-paleosol sequences across Asia provide a critical archive of past dust deposition and climate dynamics shaped by westerlies and Asian monsoons. We compile luminescence ages and climatic proxies from 107 loess sections to reconstruct dust mass accumulation rates (MARs) and hydroclimate variability over the past 130 thousand years (ka). The results reveal a consistent pattern: high MARs and low moisture during Marine Isotope Stages (MIS) 4 to 2 (71 to 12 ka) and low MARs with high moisture during MIS 5 (130 to 71 ka) and MIS 1 (12 ka to present). These shifts are driven by cooling-induced changes in atmospheric humidity linked to fluctuations in ice volume across glacial-interglacial cycles. Within MIS 5, MIS 4 to 2, and MIS 1, pronounced spatial and temporal contrasts in MARs and moisture emerge between westerlies-dominated northern Iran and central Asia and monsoon-dominated East Asia. Notably, westerly and monsoonal precipitation vary out of phase, driven by insolation-controlled shifts in moisture transport.

INTRODUCTION

The variation and interaction of the mid-latitude westerlies, the East Asian Summer Monsoon (EASM) and South Asian Summer Monsoon (SASM), and the East Asian Winter Monsoon (EAWM) have long been recognized as key drivers of climatic changes across Asia on Quaternary timescales (Fig. 1). Precipitation patterns in western and central Asia are primarily influenced by moisture transported from the North Atlantic, Western Mediterranean, and Caspian Sea, as well as local evaporation, with the westerly circulation playing a critical role (1, 2). In contrast, precipitation in East Asia is predominantly derived from the western Pacific and tropical Indian Ocean, with its variability governed by the intensity of the EASM (3) (Fig. 1). During winter, the strengthening of the Siberian High shifts the westerly jet southward, leading to drier conditions across western and central Asia (3–5). In addition, the intensified Siberian High and its associated EAWM bring cooler temperatures, strong winds, and dust transport into mid-latitude Asia (3). The interaction of these atmospheric systems drives climatic variability across Asia and has profound implications for regional economies and the livelihoods of billions. However, despite extensive research, the precise variations in the intensity of these systems and their influence on regional climate change across different timescales remain controversial (2, 6, 7). This controversy stems largely from differing sensitivities and ambiguities in the interpretation of proxy indices from depositional records (e.g., lake sediments, peatlands, stalagmites, and ice cores), as well as challenges in establishing accurate chronologies, such as dealing with the considerable reservoir effects in radiocarbon dating of lacustrine deposits in Asia (2, 8, 9). Furthermore, a relative lack of well-dated climatic records from western and central Asia, in comparison to East Asia, has hindered effective comparisons between westerlies-dominated regions and their monsoonal counterparts. This gap complicates efforts to assess the relative influence of the EASM and westerlies on climatic changes across different timescales and locations (10). As a result, the variability, interactions, and driving mechanisms of the westerlies, EASM, and EAWM during the late Quaternary remain subjects of active debate, with unresolved questions regarding the contributions of global ice volume and Northern Hemisphere solar insolation to these climate subsystems.

Loess-paleosol sequences deposited during the Quaternary across Asia serve as invaluable archives for reconstructing climate changes over a range of timescales (3, 4, 11–13) and can help resolve these controversies (Fig. 2). These sequences are predominantly found in the arid and semiarid regions of mid-latitude Asia, including the Iranian Loess Plateau, central Asia, the northeastern (NE) and eastern (E) Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, the Shandong Peninsula, and parts of Siberia (10, 11, 14–20). In recent decades, loess records from these regions have become central to paleoclimatic and environmental studies due to their relatively continuous deposition and high deposition rates over both orbital and suborbital timescales (3, 17, 21). Loess-paleosol sequences from the Chinese Loess Plateau, in particular, have been pivotal in investigating variations in the intensity of the EASM and EAWM throughout the Quaternary (3, 13). Similarly, loess-paleosol records from other regions of Asia offer substantial potential for elucidating spatial and temporal differences in climatic and environmental changes, particularly in areas where the westerlies, EASM, and EAWM interact across various spatial and temporal scales (2, 6, 7). Furthermore, the analysis of loess mass accumulation rates (MARs) from sequences in different regions of Asia provides valuable insights into variations in atmospheric dust activity, an important yet still poorly understood component of the climate system (22, 23).

Over the past decade, important advancements in luminescence dating techniques, such as quartz optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) and K-feldspar post-infrared infrared stimulated luminescence (pIRIR), have provided valuable insights into loess deposition over the last two glacial-interglacial cycles (6, 7, 14, 19, 20, 23–26). These methods address the limitations of traditional 14C dating, which is constrained by the scarcity of suitable materials and a narrow age range of 30 to 50 thousand years (ka) for Asian loess deposits (27). When combined with climatic proxy data, these luminescence dating techniques have enabled the reconstruction of spatial and temporal variations in moisture and precipitation across Asia during the late Quaternary. Despite these advances, the spatial-temporal variability of loess deposition and climatic shifts in mid-latitude Asia remains insufficiently understood to establish continent-scale correlations and identify the dominant climatic drivers. This is primarily due to the focus on local or subregional climatic changes in most loess-paleosol records, coupled with variability in the quality and resolution of available age and climatic proxy data. Moreover, these datasets have not been systematically compiled, screened, or interpreted using consistent standards, complicating broader regional comparisons and hindering a comprehensive understanding of climatic processes across Asia.

In this study, we address these gaps using an updated version of the Asian loess luminescence chronologies and climatic proxy indices dataset (28), the most comprehensive compilation of luminescence chronologies and climatic proxies from loess-paleosol sequences across Asia (28, 29). Using 1783 quartz OSL and 1178 K-feldspar pIRIR dating ages, we establish high-resolution chronologies for 107 loess-paleosol sequences across six subregions: northern Iran, central Asia, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, and the Shandong Peninsula (fig. S1 and table S1), using Bacon age-depth modeling (30). Moisture variations are reconstructed from high-resolution chronologies and 18,576 climatic proxy indices derived from 63 loess-paleosol sequences (fig. S1 and table S1). We synthesize and normalize loess MARs and moisture variations across mid-latitude Asia over the past 130 ka (fig. S2). Precipitation changes in the westerlies-dominated central Asia and the EASM-influenced regions of the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and the Chinese Loess Plateau are quantitatively reconstructed using transfer functions. We examine both in-phase and out-of-phase moisture and precipitation divergences and their phase relationships across these subregions. Using climate simulations from the Paleoclimate Modeling Intercomparison Project Phase 4 (PMIP4) (31, 32), we investigate the underlying drivers of spatial-temporal changes in moisture and precipitation. This approach allows us to assess the relative contributions of high-latitude ice volume and low-latitude solar insolation to moisture and dust variability across different regions of Asia, thereby addressing the uncertainties over wide-scale climate dynamics outlined above.

RESULTS

Depositional discontinuities and MAR variations in Asian loess records

The age-depth models for loess records across Asia reveal two prominent features. First, loess deposition was widespread across various subregions of Asia over the past 130 ka. Second, high-resolution age-depth profiles of individual loess sequences (fig. S3) show marked depositional discontinuities, with hiatuses extending up to 70 ka in some loess-paleosol sequences (Fig. 3). Notably, hiatuses exceeding 5 ka in duration are observed in ~14% of sections in northern Iran (n = 7), 56% of sections in central Asia (n = 32), 48% in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau (n = 33), 37% in the Chinese Loess Plateau (n = 19), 29% in NE China (n = 7), and 78% in sections from the Shandong Peninsula (n = 9). These findings underscore the widespread occurrence of depositional discontinuities in loess across different subregions of Asia and the importance of independent dating in identifying them.

The stacked MARs of loess across various regions of Asia generally show higher MARs during the Marine Isotope Stage (MIS) 4 to MIS 2 periods and lower MARs during the MIS 5 and MIS 1 periods (Fig. 3, fig. S4, and data S1). However, within the MIS 5, MIS 4 to MIS 2, and MIS 1 periods, spatial and temporal variations in MARs across these regions are notably distinct. In northern Iran, the stacked MAR slightly decreases from MIS 5e to MIS 5b (130 to 87 ka), followed by an increase from MIS 5b to MIS 5a (87 to 71 ka). Similarly, in central Asia, loess MARs decrease during MIS 5e, followed by a smooth increase from MIS 5d to MIS 5a. In monsoonal-dominated East Asia, loess MARs in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau decrease from MIS 5e to MIS 5c (130 to 95 ka), followed by an increase from MIS 5c to MIS 5a (95 to 71 ka). In contrast, MARs in the Chinese Loess Plateau decrease during MIS 5e (130 to 96 ka), followed by a slight increase from MIS 5d to MIS 5a (96 to 71 ka). MARs in NE China remain generally low, with no discernible trend during MIS 5. Conversely, the Shandong Peninsula exhibits two MAR peaks during MIS 5e and MIS 5a, with generally low values from MIS 5d to MIS 5b.

During MIS 4 to MIS 2, the highest MARs in loess are observed in northern Iran, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and the Shandong Peninsula around 26 ka, coinciding with the early Last Glacial Maximum (LGM). The highest MARs in the Chinese Loess Plateau not only occur somewhat later at around 21 ka but also aligned with the LGM. However, in central Asia and NE China, the highest MARs occur at ~61 ka, corresponding to late MIS 4 (71 to 57 ka) (33), with only minor peaks during the LGM. In the Holocene, MARs across northern Iran and the NE and E Tibetan Plateau show a consistent decreasing trend from the early to mid-Holocene (~12 to 6 ka), followed by an increase from the mid to late Holocene (~6 to 0 ka). In contrast, MARs in the central Asia, Chinese Loess Plateau and Shandong Peninsula show a continuous increase throughout the Holocene, whereas those in NE China are higher during the mid-Holocene (~5 to 3 ka) and lower during both the early and late Holocene (~12 to 5 ka and ~3 to 0 ka, respectively).

Despite the use of a two-level weighting framework to reduce the influence of short-duration records and spatial clustering, the regional stacks remain subject to limitations associated with heterogeneous temporal coverage over the past 130 ka. In particular, long-term glacial-interglacial variability is more strongly constrained by the subset of loess records that span both glacial and interglacial intervals, whereas shorter records primarily contribute information within their limited temporal coverage. As a result, whereas the stacked curves capture consistent first-order regional patterns, such as higher dust accumulation during MIS 4 to 2 relative to MIS 5 and MIS 1, the amplitude and finer-scale structure of long-term variations may be influenced by a smaller number of long, continuous records during intervals of reduced data coverage.

Reconstructing moisture changes across Asia over the past 130 ka

The low-frequency magnetic susceptibility (χIf) of loess-paleosols from the Chinese Loess Plateau has long been used to reconstruct pedogenesis levels, serving as a sensitive proxy for EASM precipitation intensity. This is because the fine-grained ferrimagnetic minerals formed during weathering and pedogenesis are the primary contributors to the magnetic enhancement observed in paleosols (34–36). Similar analyses of magnetic properties in loess deposits across other EASM-influenced regions—such as the NE and E Tibetan Plateau (37, 38), NE China (39), and the Shandong Peninsula (18)—reveal comparable geochemical compositions, background susceptibilities, and mechanisms of magnetic enhancement to those found in the Chinese Loess Plateau. These findings suggest that χIf can also serve as a reliable indicator of moisture changes in these regions.

The χIf variation in loess in westerlies-dominated regions of western and central Asia—particularly in the moist high-mountain areas—can be interpreted as an indicator of pedogenesis driven by effective moisture availability. Here, magnetic enhancement is primarily linked to the formation of ferrimagnetic and ferromagnetic minerals during postdepositional pedogenesis, a process similar to that observed in the Chinese Loess Plateau (40–42). However, in the drier areas of western and central Asia, such as the northern slopes of the Tianshan Mountains and the Junggar Basin, the magnetic parameters of χIf in loess deposits are more closely related to the grain-size distribution of magnetic minerals, which are influenced by wind intensity and the input of magnetic minerals, rather than by pedogenic processes (43). The mass-specific frequency-dependent magnetic susceptibility (χfd) is particularly sensitive to the content of superparamagnetic (SP) grains (<0.03 μm), which are governed by pedogenic intensity (34). Consequently, χfd serves as a suitable proxy for pedogenesis and effective moisture in both relatively dry and moist areas of western to central Asia (6, 21, 43). Therefore, magnetic parameters such as χIf and χfd can be used as indicators of pedogenesis and moisture changes in monsoonal East Asia (44), whereas χfd proves to be a reliable proxy for pedogenesis and moisture variations in western to central Asia (6, 40, 45).

The anhysteretic remanent magnetization susceptibility (χARM) of loess is highly sensitive to the concentration of stable single-domain (SSD; 30 to 100 nm) ferrimagnetic grains, whereas the saturation isothermal remanent magnetization (SIRM) reflects the overall content of remanence-bearing minerals and is influenced by the grain size of ferrimagnetic particles, although it is less sensitive than χARM (46). Consequently, the ratio of χARM/SIRM serves as a sensitive parameter for diagnosing variations in ferrimagnetic grain size, making it a more effective proxy for pedogenesis in weakly developed paleosols (47). To assess the reliability of moisture reconstructions, we analyzed the χARM/SIRM ratios of samples from the XY17 and NLT17 sections in central Asia, as well as from the HS18 and XY18 sections in the NE Tibetan Plateau. This allowed us to further compare the proxy-based moisture reconstructions using χfd for loess in westerlies-dominated regions from northern Iran to central Asia and χIf and χfd for loess in monsoonal East Asia.

The results demonstrate a strong positive correlation between χfd and χARM/SIRM values (r = 0.83, P < 0.0001) in the westerlies-dominated central Asia, whereas a statistically significant but very weak negative correlation is observed between χIf and χARM/SIRM (r = −0.14, P < 0.005) (Fig. 4, A and B). Furthermore, whereas χfd shows a consistent variation with χARM/SIRM across the past 130 ka, χIf exhibits an inconsistent pattern of variation (fig. S5A). In contrast, in the monsoonal regions of the NE Tibetan Plateau and the Chinese Loess Plateau, both χIf and χfd show strong positive correlations with χARM/SIRM (r = 0.90–0.98, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4, C to F), with both proxies displaying consistent variation with χARM/SIRM over the past 130 ka (fig. S5, B and C). These findings provide robust support for the use of χfd as an indicator of moisture changes in westerlies-dominated regions of northern Iran and central Asia, as well as for the use of both χIf and χfd as proxies for moisture variations in monsoonal East Asia. Reconstructing precipitation changes in central and eastern Asia over the past 130 ka

Magnetic parameters, specifically χIf and χfd of loess, have long been regarded as reliable proxies for EASM precipitation on the Chinese Loess Plateau (35, 36). Modern process studies of soil from various regions, including the Chinese Loess Plateau, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, and central Asia, have been used to assess the impact of different climatic variables on soil magnetic properties (38, 41, 44, 45, 48). These studies suggest that annual precipitation, rather than temperature, exerts the dominant effect on soil magnetic enhancement in the Chinese Loess Plateau, as well as the NE and E Tibetan Plateau. Transfer functions based on both χIf and χfd can be used as reliable estimates of mean annual precipitation (MAP) on the Chinese Loess Plateau (44, 48), whereas χfd alone provides a reliable estimate of MAP in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau (38). Similarly, variations in χfd of loess in central Asia covary with precipitation changes, with temperature having a negligible impact on surface soil magnetic properties (41). In addition, a transfer function between MAP and χfd has been successfully established for central Asia (45). Using these transfer functions, we quantitatively reconstruct precipitation changes in EASM-dominated regions (i.e., the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and Chinese Loess Plateau) as well as westerlies-dominated central Asia. The reconstructed precipitation changes in central Asia, the NE Tibetan Plateau, and the Chinese Loess Plateau are shown in Fig. 6 and fig. S7 (data S1).

Modeling of precipitation changes across Asia using PMIP4

Proxy records from loess-paleosol sequences across mid-latitude Asia reveal pronounced spatiotemporal variations in precipitation over the past 130 ka. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we analyze outputs from four experiments in the PMIP4 (31, 32): lig127k (127 ka), lgm (21 ka), midHolocene (6 ka), and piControl (0 ka). The lig127k and midHolocene experiments examine climatic responses to orbital forcing at 127 and 6 ka, respectively, whereas the lgm experiment explores climatic responses under glacial conditions at 21 ka. The piControl experiment, representing the preindustrial climate at 0 ka (1850 CE), serves as a reference for the three experimental scenarios. In this study, we use outputs from three models (AWI-ESM-1-1-LR, INM-CM4-8, and MIROC-ES2L) because they have completed all the aforementioned experiments (table S3).

The lig127k simulations show a substantial increase in annual precipitation over northern Iran, the Chinese Loess Plateau, and NE China, whereas central Asia and the NE and E Tibetan Plateau experience a decrease in precipitation during MIS 5e (127 ka) relative to the present (0 ka) (Fig. 7A). A similar precipitation anomaly pattern is observed during the mid-Holocene (6 ka), albeit with lower magnitude (Fig. 7C). Notably, regions in northern Iran with proxy records exhibit a slight decrease in annual precipitation during the mid-Holocene. During the LGM (21 ka), simulations consistently indicate reduced annual precipitation across different subregions of Asia, including northern Iran, central Asia, the Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, and the Shandong Peninsula, compared to the present (Fig. 7B).

DISCUSSION

Loess depositional processes in Asia over the past 130 ka

Multiple factors influence loess deposition, not least dust/silt production amounts and mechanisms in source regions, sediment availability, transport pathways, and intensity under fluvial, alluvial, and aeolian regimes, as well as aeolian accumulation conditions at depocenters (49–51). A result of this complexity is that discontinuous loess deposition occurs across various regions of mid-latitude Asia, corresponding to distinct episodes from MIS 5 (130 to 71 ka) to MIS 1 (11.7 ka to present) (6, 7, 20, 22, 23, 26, 33, 52) (Fig. 3). The formation of hiatuses, often lacking clear stratigraphic features, can only be identified through closely spaced dating, emphasizing the critical role of high-resolution chronologies in reconstructing paleoclimatic changes from loess-paleosol records across Asia (6, 28).

Depositional gaps in loess-paleosol sequences arise from erosion, limited loess accumulation, or a combination of both processes. Across the arid and semiarid mid-latitude regions of Asia, loess deposition occurred in various subregions throughout nearly all intervals from MIS 5 to MIS 1. However, discontinuities spanning 5 to 70 ka make it unlikely that deposition ceased entirely for such long durations, indicating that erosion played a dominant role in producing these stratigraphic breaks. In the Iranian Loess Plateau, depositional gaps were likely produced by intense erosion episodes that disrupted the sediment budget. Periods of heavy rainfall following arid intervals induced extensive denudation of silty-sandy deposits under sparse vegetation, erasing earlier loess accumulations. These erosion events were probably linked to climatic transitions from the last interglacial to glacial periods (16). Moreover, substantial sea level rise in the Caspian Sea during these periods may have reduced available dust source areas, further diminishing sediment supply and contributing to the formation of large depositional discontinuities (53). In inland Asia, including central Asia, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, and the Chinese Loess Plateau, these hiatuses are likely dominated by enhanced wind erosion during the MIS 4 (71 to 57 ka) to MIS 2 (29 to 11.7 ka) periods (22, 23, 33, 51, 54), especially given the absence of sedimentological evidence for fluvial erosion and the arid and semiarid climate. For instance, in the central Chinese Loess Plateau, loess accumulation has generally been relatively continuous throughout the late Quaternary, with more rapid deposition during glacial periods [e.g., MIS 5d (116 to 105 ka) to MIS 2] compared to interglacial periods [e.g., MIS 5e (130 to 116 ka) and MIS 1]. This pattern is primarily driven by enhanced wind erosion from upwind dust source regions, resulting from a stronger Siberian high-pressure system and the associated intensification of the EAWM (3, 13, 33). However, the northern margin of the Chinese Loess Plateau experienced transitions between erosion and sediment accumulation in response to glacial-interglacial and millennial-scale climate fluctuations. This region underwent erosion during peak glacial periods, leading to depositional discontinuities, particularly during MIS 4 to MIS 2, whereas loess accumulation was primarily observed during interglacial periods (e.g., MIS 5e and MIS 1) (23, 51, 54). These findings highlight the complexity of wind erosion and its role in generating discontinuities in loess-paleosol sequences across various areas of the Chinese Loess Plateau.

Water-involved erosion may also contribute to the discontinuities in loess deposition during humid periods of the late Quaternary (e.g., MIS 5e and MIS 1), particularly in regions with higher precipitation, such as the high mountain areas of central Asia, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, and the southwestern Chinese Loess Plateau. However, further research is required to better understand the relative contributions of wind erosion and water-involved erosion to the formation of depositional discontinuities across different subregions of Asia during the late Quaternary. In coastal East China, such as the Shandong Peninsula, sea level fluctuations during glacial-interglacial cycles have played a dominant role in controlling loess accumulation, due to exposure and flooding of source areas (55).

Over glacial-interglacial cycles, loess deposits in central to East Asia are primarily transported by EAWM winds from dust sources, including deserts, the Gobi, floodplains, and the alluvial fans of northern and northwestern China, as well as the Mongolian Plateau (11, 56–58). Present-day dust storms and deposition serve as analogs for past dust accumulation in monsoonal Asia (11, 56). Therefore, variations in loess MARs over glacial-interglacial cycles in central to East Asia are closely linked to changes in EAWM intensity (3, 13) (fig. S4). Loess accumulation in different subregions of Asia generally follows a pattern of high MARs during MIS 4 to MIS 2 and low MARs during MIS 5 and MIS 1, which covary with EAWM intensity, indicating the dominant role of the Siberian High anticyclone, forced by the Northern Hemisphere ice sheets, in driving orbital MAR changes in Asian loess (4, 23, 59). In contrast to inland drylands, loess accumulation in the Shandong Peninsula is influenced by both terrestrial and marine environmental changes. During the MIS 4 to MIS 2 periods, lower sea levels exposed the continental shelf, which is rich in source material, leading to higher accumulation rates. Conversely, during MIS 5 and MIS 1, rising sea levels reduced dust supply from the shelf, whereas seawater-induced erosion further diminished loess deposition rates in these coastal regions (55).

The observed spatial and temporal variations in loess MARs across the substages of MIS 5 to MIS 1 suggest that loess accumulation in Asia does not respond solely or directly to fluctuations in EAWM intensity. Instead, it reflects the complex interactions between source material availability, vegetation cover, and local geomorphological feedbacks, such as changes in wind speed and direction (60, 61). Although dust transport in East Asia is primarily facilitated by EAWM winds, the westerlies can also contribute to the transport of fine grains from central Asia into East Asia (56, 58). In addition, variations in EASM intensity influence dust source aridity and vegetation cover, further contributing to loess MAR variations in East Asia on millennial timescales (62). In westerlies-dominated regions of central to western Asia, surface winds driven by the Siberian High, particularly the EAWM, predominantly govern suborbital-scale changes in dust MARs over millennial timescales (5). The westerlies may also contribute to dust transport from western to central Asian deserts to downwind regions (15, 63), whereas variations in westerlies precipitation may alter vegetation cover and dust availability, further influencing dust MAR variability (5).

A notable increase in loess MARs occurred during the late Holocene in northern Iran, central Asia, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, and the Shandong Peninsula (Fig. 3). This increase is likely associated with intensified EAWM activity related to the strengthening of the Siberian High and a concurrent decrease in the EASM, which reduced vegetation cover in the upwind source regions of northern China and the Mongolian Gobi Desert (5). In addition, human activities, including farming and grazing, gradually intensified in mid-latitude Asia during the late Holocene, particularly over the past two millennia (64, 65), potentially contributing to increased dust availability (5). Collectively, these factors shape the spatial and temporal variations in loess MARs observed across different regions of inland Asia.

Divergence in moisture change across Asia at multiple timescales

As shown in Fig. 5, the synthesized moisture variability across different subregions of Asia over glacial-interglacial timescales exhibits a coherent pattern: Moisture levels were generally higher during MIS 5 and MIS 1 and lower during MIS 4 to MIS 2. This overall trend is evident in both the westerlies-influenced regions from northern Iran to central Asia and in the EASM dominated regions of the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, and Shandong Peninsula. However, pronounced spatial and temporal divergences occur within MIS 5, MIS 4 to 2, and MIS 1 (Fig. 5 and fig. S6).

In westerlies-dominated northern Iran and central Asia (Fig. 5, C and D), the trajectories of moisture change differ substantially. In northern Iran during MIS 5, moisture generally increased from ~130 to ~82 ka, with relatively wetter intervals at ~130 to 112 ka and ~105 to 82 ka, interrupted by a drier phase at ~112 to 105 ka, followed by a decline from ~82 to ~71 ka (Fig. 5C). In contrast, central Asia experienced an overall decreasing trend from ~130 to ~71 ka, punctuated by wetter episodes at ~120 to 110 ka, ~95 to 86 ka, and ~78 to 71 ka, and drier intervals at ~130 to 120 ka, ~110 to 95 ka, and ~86 to 78 ka (Fig. 5D). During MIS 4 to 2, these contrasts became more pronounced: Northern Iran registered a general moisture increase from MIS 4 to late MIS 2, with superimposed millennial-scale oscillations, whereas central Asia remained persistently dry, showing only a modest rise from MIS 4 and early MIS 3 to mid-late MIS 3 and MIS 2. In the Holocene (MIS 1), both regions experienced a general increase in moisture from the early to late Holocene, with northern Iran additionally exhibiting frequent millennial-scale fluctuations during the mid-to-late Holocene (Fig. 5, C and D).

In inland Asia, moisture increases with elevation, from basins to high mountain regions, due to orographic effects (66). Reconstructed moisture changes in central Asia reveal a marked dichotomy between the high mountain areas (e.g., Tianshan Mountains) and lowlands (e.g., Junggar Basin) over the past 130 ka. Moisture variations, as indicated by the proxy index χfd, show a distinct decrease from MIS 5 to MIS 4 to MIS 2, followed by an increase from MIS 2 to MIS 1 in high mountain and intermountain basin regions (e.g., Yili Basin). However, loess records from basins (e.g., Junggar Basin) and the slopes of the Tianshan Mountains consistently show low χfd values, suggesting that arid conditions prevailed in the lowlands of central Asia throughout the last two glacial-interglacial cycles (6, 66). The limited westerlies precipitation and high evaporation in the lowland regions are likely responsible for the persistent aridity during the MIS 5 and MIS 1 periods (66). These findings underscore the crucial role of increased precipitation in the high mountain areas of arid central Asia in driving hydrological and climatic changes over various timescales.

In EASM-dominated East Asia, moisture variations during the MIS 5 substages display a consistent pattern. Specifically, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, along with the Chinese Loess Plateau, exhibit increased moisture during ~125 to 116 ka, ~106 to 95 ka, and ~83 to 77 ka, corresponding to MIS 5e, MIS 5c, and MIS 5a, respectively. These wetter intervals are interspersed with drier periods at ~116 to 106 ka and ~95 to 83 ka, corresponding to MIS 5d and MIS 5b, respectively (Fig. 5, E and F). Although the resolution of loess records from NE China and the Shandong Peninsula is lower, the moisture trends observed in these regions align with those from the NE and E Tibetan Plateau (Fig. 5, G and H). In the Shandong Peninsula, moisture increased from ~130 to ~89 ka, with peaks at ~116 and ~94 ka. After this, moisture levels generally remained low during ~89 to 82 ka and ~78 to 71 ka, with a relative moisture increase between ~82 and ~78 ka.

During the MIS 4 to MIS 2 period, moisture levels remained relatively low across different regions of East Asia. Specifically, moisture in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau remained consistently low throughout this period. In contrast, the Chinese Loess Plateau and NE China recorded relatively higher moisture levels during MIS 3 compared to the preceding MIS 4 and MIS 2. In the Shandong Peninsula, moisture increased from MIS 4 to MIS 3 and then decreased during MIS 3, followed by an increase during MIS 2. Notably, a pronounced increase in moisture occurred during the late MIS 2 (~20 to 12 ka), as indicated by pedogenesis (18). On the basis of quartz OSL ages and changes in chroma and the chemical index of alteration, it is found that the paleosols in the central Shandong Peninsula, aged ~15 to 8 ka, were aggradational soils formed concurrently with dust accumulation, indicating a moist climate from the last deglaciation to the early Holocene (18). In contrast, paleosols aged ~20 to 15 ka are nonaggradational soils, attributed to the deep weathering of underlying last glacial loess, with a weathering depth of 40 to 50 cm. These findings suggest that moisture during MIS 2 was likely lower compared to the early Holocene in the Shandong Peninsula (18).

The Holocene exhibits a generally consistent moisture pattern across EASM-dominated regions, including the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, and NE China. This pattern is characterized by dry conditions during the early Holocene, a moisture increase during the mid-Holocene, and a subsequent decline from the mid-Holocene to late Holocene (~5 ka onward; Fig. 5, E to G). In contrast to other regions of East Asia, where Holocene moisture levels peak similarly to those during MIS 5, the maximum Holocene moisture in the Shandong Peninsula is noticeably lower than during MIS 5 (Fig. 5H). Furthermore, moisture levels in the Shandong Peninsula remained relatively stable overall, showing a slight decreasing trend from the early to mid-late Holocene, with reductions at ~7 to 6 ka and ~4 to 3 ka. The moisture changes in the Shandong Peninsula exhibit a clear asynchrony compared to other EASM-dominated regions during the Holocene, and the underlying mechanisms driving this discrepancy remain unclear, warranting further investigation. Despite this discrepancy and the relatively modest Holocene moisture increase compared to MIS 5 in the Shandong Peninsula, the overall consistency of moisture variations across East Asia over the past 130 ka suggests that EASM dynamics have been the primary drivers of moisture changes in the region during the last two glacial-interglacial cycles.

As illustrated in Figs. 5 and 6, the reconstructed changes in westerlies-driven precipitation in central Asia and EASM-driven precipitation in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and Chinese Loess Plateau show generally consistent variations with moisture reconstructions from these regions over the past 130 ka. This suggests that precipitation has been the primary driver of moisture variability in the arid and semiarid regions of inland Asia. However, quantitative reconstructions of late Quaternary precipitation changes in northern Iran, NE China, and the Shandong Peninsula remain unavailable due to the lack of established transfer functions linking precipitation with magnetic proxy indices. To address this gap, further studies of precipitation-sensitive proxies, alongside more well-dated loess-paleosol records from these regions, are crucial for reconstructing high-resolution precipitation changes during the late Quaternary.

Overall, precipitation patterns in EASM-dominated East Asia (e.g., NE and E Tibetan Plateau and Chinese Loess Plateau) and westerlies-dominated central Asia exhibit a general trend of dry conditions during MIS 4 to MIS 2 and wetter conditions during MIS 5 and MIS 1, closely aligning with global ice volume and greenhouse gas variations (6, 7). During the MIS 5 and MIS 1 periods, precipitation changes in EASM-dominated regions, including the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and the Chinese Loess Plateau, show consistent variation over the past 130 ka. On millennial timescales, precipitation changes in central Asia, influenced by the westerlies, display an out-of-phase relationship with precipitation changes in EASM-dominated regions (Fig. 6). Specifically, westerlies-driven precipitation in central Asia was relatively high during MIS 5d and MIS 5b but decreased during MIS 5e, 5c, and 5a, in contrast to EASM precipitation in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and the Chinese Loess Plateau, which increased during MIS 5e, 5c, and 5a and decreased during MIS 5d and MIS 5b (6, 7, 23, 40). During the Holocene (MIS 1), westerlies-driven precipitation in central Asia increased from the early to late Holocene and peaked in the late Holocene, whereas EASM precipitation increased from the early to mid-Holocene and then declined, with maxima at ~8 ka in the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and ~4 ka in the Chinese Loess Plateau.

During MIS 4 to 2, precipitation associated with the westerlies in central Asia generally decreased, exhibiting millennial-scale fluctuations from MIS 4 to early MIS 3 (71 to 54 ka), followed by a modest increase during mid–late MIS 3 and MIS 2 (54 to 12 ka). In contrast, regions dominated by the EASM, such as the Chinese Loess Plateau, recorded an overall increase in precipitation from MIS 4 to MIS 3, reaching a maximum around 37 ka before declining during late MIS 3 and MIS 2. The NE and E Tibetan Plateau display a broadly similar precipitation evolution but with smaller amplitudes (Fig. 6). These results further indicate spatially asynchronous precipitation changes within EASM-influenced regions during MIS 4 to 2 (Figs. 6 and 8).

Cross-correlation analyses further substantiate an out-of-phase relationship between EASM-derived and westerlies-derived precipitation during MIS 5, MIS 4 to 2, and MIS 1 (Fig. 8). Significant positive correlations between precipitation variability on the NE and E Tibetan Plateau and the Chinese Loess Plateau are observed during MIS 5 (r = 0.70, P < 0.001), MIS 4 to 2 (r = 0.20, P < 0.001), and MIS 1 (r = 0.87 and 0.79, P < 0.001), collectively indicating the persistent dominance of EASM precipitation over the past 130 ka (Fig. 8, I to L). In contrast, EASM precipitation on the Chinese Loess Plateau exhibits significant negative correlations with westerlies-driven precipitation in central Asia during MIS 5 (r = −0.10, P < 0.05; Fig. 8E), MIS 4 to 2 (r = −0.39, P < 0.001; Fig. 8F), and the mid-to-late MIS 1 (r = −0.29, P < 0.05; Fig. 8H). Likewise, EASM precipitation on the NE and E Tibetan Plateau is negatively correlated with westerlies-driven precipitation in central Asia during MIS 5 and the mid-to-late MIS 1 (r = −0.25 and −0.69, P < 0.001; Fig. 8, A and D), whereas no significant correlation is detected during MIS 4 to 2 (r = 0.02, P > 0.05; Fig. 8B). Together, these results demonstrate a statistically robust and persistent out-of-phase relationship between EASM and westerlies precipitation throughout MIS 5, MIS 4 to 2, and MIS 1.

Mechanisms behind orbital-scale precipitation changes across Asia

As shown in Fig. 7, PMIP4 simulations indicate that precipitation across Asian subregions was consistently lower during the LGM than during the preindustrial period, primarily due to increased ice volume and reduced greenhouse gas concentrations. This pattern supports a continent-wide glacial-interglacial pattern characterized by wetter interglacials and drier glacials. However, during the MIS 5 and MIS 1 periods, the simulations reveal pronounced spatial heterogeneity. Precipitation changes diverged between the westerly dominated eastern parts of central Asia and the EASM-dominated regions, including the Chinese Loess Plateau and NE China. Substantial differences also emerged within both circulation domains, for example, between northern Iran and eastern Central Asia within the westerly regime, and among the Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, and the Shandong Peninsula within the EASM domain. Moreover, northern Iran displayed opposing precipitation trends between MIS 5 and MIS 1. Overall, the PMIP4-simulated precipitation anomalies align closely with quantitatively reconstructed precipitation records from loess-paleosol sequences, except for discrepancies observed over the NE and E Tibetan Plateau during MIS 5e and the mid-Holocene.

Precipitation variation is primarily driven by changes in moisture transport (2). In northern Iran and central Asia, moisture is predominantly transported by the westerlies from the North Atlantic, Mediterranean Sea, Black Sea, and Caspian Sea throughout the year (figs. S8A and S9A). In monsoonal Asia, which spans the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, and NE China, moisture is mainly transported by southerly winds from the tropical Indian Ocean and northwestern Pacific during the warm season, concentrated in June to September (2) (fig. S8A), whereas during the cold season (October to March), it is transported by westerlies originating from the North Atlantic (fig. S9A). During the LGM, moisture transport weakened compared to the present. Both the westerly moisture transport to northern Iran and central Asia, and the monsoonal moisture transport to the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, and NE China, showed a reduction during the warm season (fig. S8C). In the cold season of the LGM, westerly moisture transport also weakened across Asia (fig. S9C). This overall weakening of moisture transport contributed to a uniform reduction in precipitation across Asia during both warm and cold seasons of the LGM (fig. S10, B and E). As a result, precipitation changes across Asia followed an in-phase relationship on the glacial-interglacial timescale.

Within the interglacial periods (MIS 5e and mid-Holocene), pronounced variations in moisture transport were simulated across different regions of Asia relative to the present. In northern Iran, specifically in regions southeast of the Caspian Sea with proxy records, summer-season westerly moisture transport was notably enhanced during MIS 5e (fig. S8B), leading to a marked increase in precipitation in this region (fig. S10A). Although winter-season precipitation decreased due to weakened westerly moisture transport during this period (fig. S9B), the magnitude of the reduction in winter precipitation was smaller than the summer-season increase (fig. S10, A and D). As a result, northern Iran experienced anomalous wetting during MIS 5e (Fig. 7A). In contrast, during the mid-Holocene, the reduction in cold-season precipitation was slightly greater than the increase in summer-season precipitation (fig. S10, C and F), leading to a minor drying trend (Fig. 7C). These seasonal precipitation changes were linked to variations in westerly moisture transport (figs. S8D and S9D). In eastern central Asia, westerly moisture transport weakened during both the warm and cold seasons of the MIS 5e and mid-Holocene periods (figs. S8 and S9). Consequently, eastern central Asia experienced drying during both interglacial periods (fig. S10).

The wetting of the Chinese Loess Plateau and NE China during MIS 5e and the mid-Holocene was primarily driven by increased warm-season precipitation (fig. S10, A and C), whereas reduced cold-season precipitation had a dampening effect (fig. S10, D and F). During these periods, the EASM and associated southerly moisture transport were markedly enhanced (fig. S8, B and D), resulting in increased precipitation over the Chinese Loess Plateau and NE China (fig. S10, A and C). Despite the intensification of monsoonal moisture transport, precipitation over the Shandong Peninsula decreased substantially during the warm seasons of MIS 5e and the mid-Holocene (fig. S10, A and C). This decrease is likely due to the northward displacement of the western Pacific subtropical high, which led to anomalous subsidence over the region (fig. S8, B and D).

Over the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, model simulations show a decrease in both warm-season and cold-season precipitation during the MIS 5e and mid-Holocene periods compared to the present (fig. S10). Precipitation in this region is primarily influenced by southwesterly moisture transport from the tropical Indian Ocean during the warm season (fig. S8A) and by westerlies from the North Atlantic during the cold season (fig. S9A). During MIS 5e and the mid-Holocene, southwesterly moisture transport weakened during the warm season (fig. S8, B and D), resulting in reduced warm-season precipitation (fig. S10, A and C). Similarly, westerly moisture transport weakened during the cold season, leading to a decline in cold-season precipitation (figs. S9, B and D, and S10, D and F). However, hydrogen isotopic data from long-leaf wax (n-alkanoic acid, δ2Hwax) records from Qinghai Lake suggest enhanced southwesterly moisture transport to the NE Tibetan Plateau during the mid-Holocene, driven by a stronger SASM (67). This indicates potential biases in the modeling of warm-season atmospheric circulation over the NE and E Tibetan Plateau during the mid-Holocene and possibly also during MIS 5e, which may contribute to the discrepancy between the PMIP4 simulations and our reconstructed precipitation data for this region.

Orbital-scale precipitation variations across Asia over the past 130 ka were closely linked to fluctuations in atmospheric moisture transport. The moisture flux is governed by both wind patterns and atmospheric humidity. During the LGM, both the EASM and SASM experienced substantial weakening, whereas the westerlies intensified markedly (fig. S11). These concurrent changes in moisture transport were therefore likely not driven by wind anomalies but were instead associated with an overall reduction in atmospheric humidity (fig. S12, B and D). Cooling, resulting from the expansion of ice sheets and reduced greenhouse gas concentrations, played a crucial role in decreasing precipitation by suppressing evaporation, thus lowering atmospheric humidity (6, 7). Consequently, the in-phase relationship of precipitation changes across Asia during the LGM can largely be attributed to the suppression of evaporation caused by cooling.

During interglacial periods (MIS 5e and mid-Holocene), weakened westerlies over central Asia were associated with an anomalous cyclone around the Tibetan Plateau during the warm season (fig. S11, A and C). This anomaly was likely induced by upward motions over the Tibetan Plateau (68), which were probably driven by the northward migration of the South Asian high (69). This migration was a result of enhanced latent heating, caused by the intensification of SASM precipitation in response to increased summer insolation (fig. S10, A and C). In contrast, minor variations in the westerlies toward northern Iran during the warm season (fig. S11, A and C) were linked to enhanced westerly moisture transport. This enhancement was likely caused by increased atmospheric humidity due to warming-induced evaporation under higher summer insolation (fig. S12, A and C). These distinct mechanisms governed changes in westerly moisture transport during the warm season of the MIS 5e and mid-MIS 1 periods, leading to an out-of-phase relationship in precipitation changes between northern Iran and central Asia.

Over the Chinese Loess Plateau and NE China, moisture transport is dominated by the EASM. The EASM was strengthened during the MIS 5e and mid-Holocene periods, driven by increased summer insolation (fig. S11, A and C), which amplified the land-ocean thermal contrast (fig. S12, A and C). Meanwhile, moisture transport over the NE and E Tibetan Plateau is controlled by the SASM (fig. S8A). The PMIP4 simulations fail to capture the enhanced SASM over the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, leading to substantial biases in the representation of precipitation changes in this region during the MIS 5e and mid-Holocene. Cold-season precipitation generally decreased from northern Iran to NE China during both MIS 5e and the mid-Holocene (fig. S10, D to F), implying a common underlying driver. This reduction likely reflects decreased atmospheric humidity caused by suppressed evaporation under Northern Hemisphere cooling, which, in turn, resulted from reduced winter insolation (fig. S12, D to F). These results suggest that spatial heterogeneity in precipitation across Asia was primarily governed by changes in warm-season dynamics. Together, the out-of-phase precipitation relationship between central Asia and the EASM-dominated regions, including the Chinese Loess Plateau and NE China, was mainly driven by orbital modulation of summer monsoon intensity through its control on surface temperature. Warm-season precipitation in northern Iran was primarily influenced by variations in surface evaporation over the North Atlantic and the Mediterranean, whereas precipitation in central Asia was modulated by variability in the Asian summer monsoon. Both processes were regulated by summer insolation-driven surface temperature changes, contributing to pronounced precipitation differences between northern Iran and central Asia.

Our reconstructions indicate that EASM precipitation exhibits a similar rhythm to summer insolation but with a 2- to 5-ka lag, suggesting a delayed response of precipitation changes to insolation forcing (7, 23). This lag is likely influenced by high-latitude ice forcing (70) and low-latitude oceanic feedbacks (68, 71). During the MIS 4 to MIS 2 periods, the spatiotemporal uniformity of precipitation changes across Asia is primarily attributed to reduced atmospheric humidity, resulting from surface cooling caused by expanded ice sheets and decreased greenhouse gas concentrations. During the MIS 5 and MIS 1 periods, high-latitude factors contributed to the lagged response of EASM precipitation, driven by interactions between EASM circulation and the westerlies (7, 70). During periods of high June insolation at 30°N (e.g., MIS 5e and early MIS 1), strong sea-land thermal contrasts intensified the EASM, enhancing water vapor transport from the tropical Indian Ocean and northwestern Pacific to East Asia. At the same time, high-latitude cooling strengthened the westerlies, which suppressed upward motion and precipitation in East Asia (70). This created a negative feedback loop, where stronger mid-latitude westerlies during high insolation periods weakened EASM precipitation, leading to a delayed peak response of EASM precipitation to insolation forcing (7).

Oceanic feedbacks also play a critical role in shaping precipitation responses to orbital forcing. Community Earth System Model (CESM) simulations suggest that tropical Indian Ocean cooling around 6 ka contributed to a decrease in summer half-year (April to September) precipitation in central Asia, consistent with patterns observed in loess proxy records (71). This cooling led to a reduction in upper-level westerlies south of the westerly jet axis, causing the jet to shift northward during the mid-Holocene. This shift reduced the meridional thermal gradient between the tropical Indian Ocean and northern Eurasia, weakening the South Asian high and promoting anomalous downward motion, thereby diminishing summer half-year precipitation over central Asia during this period (70, 71). Conversely, the weakening of the westerly jet generated upper-level anticyclonic anomalies over East Asia, fostering anomalous upward motions and enhancing EASM precipitation during the mid-Holocene (68).

In summary, spatiotemporal variations in precipitation at orbital timescales across Asia were primarily governed by changes in surface temperature. During the MIS 4 to MIS 2 periods, expanded ice sheets and decreased greenhouse gas concentrations were the dominant drivers of surface temperature changes, resulting in pronounced cooling across the Northern Hemisphere. This cooling weakened both westerly and monsoonal moisture transport by reducing evaporation and lowering atmospheric humidity, contributing to the spatiotemporal homogeneity of precipitation changes across Asia. In contrast, during the MIS 5 and MIS 1 periods, surface temperature variations were mainly influenced by summer insolation. These temperature changes markedly affected monsoon and westerly circulation systems, as well as evaporation-related atmospheric humidity. The combined effects of altered atmospheric circulation and humidity led to pronounced divergence in moisture transport, contributing to the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of precipitation changes across Asia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Loess-paleosol sequence distribution in Asia

Loess deposits are primarily found in the mid-latitudes of Asia (Fig. 2 and fig. S1). In western Asia, loess is distributed across the northern Iranian Loess Plateau, bordered by the Kopet Dag Mountains to the north, the Alborz Mountains to the south, and the Caspian Sea to the west. Here, loess accumulates along the northern foothills of the Alborz Mountains, with thicknesses exceeding 60 m (16, 72). In central Asia, loess is mainly found between the Asian high mountains and adjacent deserts, particularly on windward slopes, fluvial terraces, alluvial plains, and desert margins. The thickness of loess in these regions ranges from several meters to over 100 m (10). Sandy loess is predominantly distributed along the peripheries of central Asia’s deserts—such as the Taukum, Muyunkum, Kyzylkum, and Saryyesik Atyrau deserts—whereas finer silty loess is more common in the piedmonts of the Alborz, Kopet Dag, Hindu Kush, and Tianshan mountains (10).

In eastern Asia, the Chinese Loess Plateau hosts the most extensive and some of the thickest loess deposits, covering ~500,000 km2. The plateau spans about 700 km from north to south and 1200 km from east to west (11). The western section of the plateau typically shows loess thicknesses ranging from 200 to 300 m, with the thickest deposit reaching ~505 m in the Jingyuan region. In the eastern section, loess thickness ranges from 100 to 200 m, extending from Liupan Mountain to the Lvliang Mountains (17). Loess is also found along the Huangshui River valley, the Qinghai Lake basin, and the Gonghe Basin in the NE Tibetan Plateau, as well as sporadically across other regions, including the upper Yellow River valley, the Hengduan Mountains basin, and the Yarlung Zangbo River basin (17, 73). Loess accumulation in these areas ranges from a few meters to over 100 m, with the thickest deposits in the NE Tibetan Plateau (17). In Northeast China, loess is found primarily along the river basins of the Songhua and Liaohe rivers, where thicknesses generally do not exceed 100 m (14). In eastern China, loess is distributed in the piedmonts of the Taishan and Lushan mountains, as well as in the intermontane basins of the northern Shandong Peninsula (18). In the Shandong Peninsula, loess thickness typically ranges from a few meters to several tens of meters.

The northern Iranian Loess Plateau experiences a Mediterranean-type climate, characterized by hot, dry summers, and mild, humid winters. Precipitation in this region is primarily driven by the westerlies, which account for ~85% of the total annual precipitation. Most precipitation occurs during winter and spring. The mean annual air temperature decreases from ~18°C in the northern lowlands to ~15°C toward the southern part of the region and further declines with increasing elevation along the Alborz Mountains. The MAP shows a pronounced latitudinal gradient, varying from over 800 mm in the south (near the Alborz Mountains) to less than 300 mm in the north (near the Kopet Dag Mountains), within a span of ~80 km (16, 72).

Central Asia exhibits a typical temperate continental climate, marked by notable annual and diurnal temperature variations, as well as uneven seasonal and regional precipitation distributions. This climate is influenced by the westerlies, the Siberian High, and the Polar Front from the north (10). The spatial distribution of water vapor transported by the westerlies varies substantially between high mountain ranges and lowland regions of central and western Asia, with pronounced orographic effects that increase precipitation with elevation (10). For example, precipitation in the Yili Valley Basin in the Tianshan Mountains ranges from 400 to 800 mm/year, whereas the northern slopes of the Tianshan Mountains exhibit a precipitation gradient from ~300 to 200 mm/year to less than 150 mm/year with decreasing elevation. The MAP in the Junggar Basin ranges between 75 and 150 mm/year (6, 10). Consequently, loess deposits in high-precipitation areas such as the Yili Valley Basin show well-developed paleosol horizons, whereas loess from low-precipitation areas, such as the northern Tianshan slopes and the Junggar Basin, exhibit less developed or absent paleosols (6, 20, 26).

In East Asia, the precipitation regime in loess-accumulating regions of the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, Chinese Loess Plateau, and NE China is primarily influenced by variations in the EASM, which contributes more than 80% of the annual precipitation in the form of summer rainfall (May to September). During the winter (December to March), precipitation is minimal, and the prevailing EAWM, driven by the breakdown of the Siberian High, brings frequent dust storms (12). For example, the Chinese Loess Plateau exhibits a semiarid climate, with variations in EASM intensity resulting in MAP ranging from over 700 mm in the southeast to less than 200 mm in the northwest. Mean annual temperatures range from over 15°C in the southeast to less than 5°C in the northwest (62). Similarly, the EASM influences precipitation in the NE Tibetan Plateau, with MAP and temperature reaching ~330 mm and ~7°C, respectively, in recent decades (7).

Luminescence dating measurements

Luminescence ages reported in this study were obtained through pretreatment and measurement procedures conducted under subdued red or yellow light conditions at various luminescence dating laboratories worldwide. Coarse-grained quartz and K-feldspar fractions for loess samples were pretreated using standard heavy liquid separation and HF etching, whereas fine-grained quartz samples (4 to 11 μm) were prepared by settling the <63- or <38-μm fractions using Stokes’ law and hexafluorosilicic acid etching. Detailed procedures for quartz and K-feldspar sample preparation and the measurement facilities are provided in text S1.

The equivalent dose values for quartz OSL samples were primarily determined using the single-aliquot regeneration (SAR) protocol (74). For K-feldspar and polymineral samples, equivalent doses were determined using the K-feldspar pIRIR dating technique. This method, using the pIR50IR290 (25), pIR200IR290 (75), and pIR50IR170 (76) signals, was applied to both coarse-grained (e.g., 63 to 90 μm) K-feldspar and fine-grained (e.g., 4 to 11 μm) polymineral samples from loess sections. Dose recovery tests were conducted on representative samples from each loess-paleosol section to assess the suitability of the quartz OSL and K-feldspar/polymineral pIRIR dating methods for each sequence. The total dose rate for each quartz sample was calculated on the basis of the concentrations of radioactive elements in the sample, with a minor contribution from cosmic rays. Detailed descriptions of the quartz OSL dating measurements, K-feldspar/polymineral pIRIR dating methods, as well as the measurement of U, Th, K, and Rb concentrations and dose rate calculations are provided in text S2. The quartz OSL and K-feldspar pIRIR ages for each sample were determined by dividing the measured equivalent dose value by the annual dose rate.

Proxy indices: Magnetic parameters of χlf, χfd, and χARM/SIRM

Magnetic susceptibility measurements were performed on loess samples that were air-dried and placed into 10-cm3 plastic containers. Magnetic susceptibility was measured at both low frequency (470 Hz, χlf) and high frequency (4700 Hz, χhf) using a Bartington Instruments MS2B meter or an AGICO Kappabridge MFK2. The percentage of mass-specific frequency-dependent magnetic susceptibility (χfd) was calculated using the following equation (Eq. 1)χfd=χlf−χhfχlf×100%(1)

We conducted χARM and SIRM measurements on a total of 1168 loess-paleosol samples from central Asia and the NE Tibetan Plateau, including 475 and 190 samples from the XY17 and NLT17 sections of central Asia and 368 and 135 samples from the HS18 and XY18 sections of the NE Tibetan Plateau. Loess samples were air-dried and gently ground to a uniform consistency to ensure the original particle properties were not damaged. A 5-g portion of each sample was then placed into 8-cm3 plastic containers. For ARM measurements, anhysteretic remanences were induced using a LAD-3 or LAD-5 demagnetizer in a peak alternating field of 100 mT, with a dc field of 0.05 mT superimposed, and were measured using a JR-6A spinner magnetometer. The χARM was estimated by dividing the ARM value by the biasing field strength. The SIRM was imparted using a MMPM10 or IM10-30 pulse magnetizer, applying a 1-T dc field, and measured using a JR-6A spinner magnetometer. The χARM/SIRM ratio was calculated by dividing the measured χARM by the SIRM.

Data sources and selection criteria for loess-paleosol sequences

To reconstruct loess deposition and moisture changes across Asia, we compiled an extensive dataset that includes quartz OSL and K-feldspar pIRIR ages, along with environmental proxy data (χlf, χfd, and χARM/SIRM) from loess-paleosol records spanning the past 130 ka. The age and magnetic parameter data from 107 loess-paleosol sequences were taken from the updated Asian loess luminescence chronologies and proxy indices dataset (28). This dataset provides high-resolution chronologies and proxy data from loess sequences across six major regions of Asia: northern Iran, central Asia, the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, the Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, and the Shandong Peninsula.

The loess-paleosol records were selected on the basis of the following criteria:

1) Quartz and/or K-feldspar pIRIR ages have a sampling resolution of ≤1 m per loess-paleosol profile/section.

2) The reliability of quartz OSL and K-feldspar pIRIR ages was validated through luminescence characteristic analysis and/or internal comparisons.

3) Quartz OSL ages do not exceed their respective upper limits: 50 ka for samples from western to central Asia (26, 27) and 70 ka for samples from monsoonal East Asia (77, 78); K-feldspar pIR50IR170, pIR50IR290, and pIR200IR290 ages do not exceed their respective upper limits of 50, 150, and 250 ka (6, 7, 23, 27, 40, 73).

4) Magnetic parameters of χfd are used as proxies for moisture changes within the loess-paleosol sequences from northern Iran and central Asia, whereas magnetic parameters of χIf and χfd serve as proxies for moisture changes within the loess-paleosol sequences from the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, Chinese Loess Plateau, NE China, and the Shandong Peninsula (6, 7, 22, 23, 59).

A total of 107 loess-paleosol sections were selected across these regions based on these criteria (fig. S2 and table S1). These include 7 sections from northern Iran (1.24 to 54 m depth), 32 from central Asia (1.1 to 44 m depth), 33 from the NE and E Tibetan Plateau (0.8 to 36.2 m depth), 19 from the Chinese Loess Plateau (1.9 to 39.8 m depth), 7 from NE China (3.2 to 17.1 m depth), and 9 from the Shandong Peninsula (4.5 to 20 m depth). Detailed location and depth information for each section are provided in table S1.

Comparison of quartz OSL and K-feldspar pIRIR ages

A comparison of quartz OSL and K-feldspar pIRIR ages (including pIR50IR170, pIR50IR290, and pIR200IR290) from loess samples across different Asian regions reveals strong agreement for samples younger than ~50 ka, supporting the reliability of both dating methods within this temporal range (14, 26, 76) (Fig. 9). However, for samples older than 50 ka from western to central Asia and older than 70 ka from East Asia, quartz OSL ages tend to underestimate the age compared to K-feldspar pIR50IR290 and pIR200IR290 ages, likely due to signal instability and/or field saturation in the high-dose region (78). As a result, quartz OSL ages for samples younger than 50 ka from western to central Asia and younger than 70 ka from East Asia are used for loess-paleosol chronologies. The K-feldspar pIR50IR170 ages show good consistency with quartz OSL ages for samples younger than 50 ka and general consistency with pIR200IR290 ages for samples younger than 35 ka. A discrepancy between pIR50IR170 and pIR200IR290 ages was observed for two samples with ages of 40 to 50 ka, which may be due to the uncertainties in the pIR50IR170 and pIR200IR290 ages. The K-feldspar pIR50IR170 ages exhibit an underestimation compared to both quartz OSL ages and K-feldspar pIR200IR290 ages for samples older than 50 ka, likely due to the instability of the pIR50IR170 signal (6, 7, 73, 76). As a result, K-feldspar pIR50IR170 ages for samples younger than 50 ka are used for the determination of loess-paleosol chronologies (Fig. 9).

Previous studies have shown that the pIR200IR290 signal can be used to date Asian loess samples up to 250 to 300 ka, but ages greater than 250 to 300 ka are likely underestimated due to signal saturation (6, 29, 40). The saturation of the pIR200IR290 signal for samples older than 250 ka is also indicated by the lack of pronounced age increases with depth in loess records from central Asia and the Chinese Loess Plateau (6, 23). A comparison of pIR50IR290 and pIR200IR290 ages reveals good consistency for samples younger than 150 ka, indicating that pIR50IR290 ages for samples younger than 150 ka can be reliably used for loess-paleosol chronology determination (40) (Fig. 9D). pIR50IR290 ages older than 150 ka may be affected by signal instability (75). In this study, we only synthesized loess-paleosol sequences spanning the past 130 ka, and both pIR50IR290 and pIR200IR290 ages are reliable within this time range as they have not reached their upper limits and can be used for chronology determination and age-depth modeling reconstruction.

Age-depth modeling of loess-paleosol sequences in Asia

Age-depth models for loess sections were established using the Bacon age-depth modeling approach (30), which uses Bayesian statistics to reconstruct accumulation histories for deposits. This approach helps reduce age-depth model error estimates by considering the stratigraphic positions of the ages. Using closely spaced quartz OSL and/or K-feldspar pIRIR ages from each section, a total of 107 age-depth models were generated for loess-paleosol sequences across Asia (fig. S3). These include 7 models from northern Iran, 32 from central Asia, 33 from the NE and E Tibetan Plateau, 19 from the Chinese Loess Plateau, 7 from NE China, and 9 from the Shandong Peninsula. The age-depth models were generated at 1- to 2-cm intervals and span the past 130 ka. All age-depth models for the loess sequences account for deposition discontinuities caused by hiatuses in the age-depth profiles, which are revealed through high-resolution luminescence dating of loess-paleosol sequences. A detailed list of the parameters used in Bacon age-depth modeling for each section is provided in table S2.

Reconstruction, normalization, and synthesis of loess MARs and moisture changes in Asia

The loess MAR for each loess-paleosol sequence was calculated as follows (Eq. 2)MAR(gcm−2ka−1)=H×ρΔt(2)where H denotes the thickness of the depositional unit (cm), ρ is the bulk density of the loess or paleosol (g cm−3), and Δt is the time interval represented by the layer (ka). For loess and paleosol layers, densities of 2.01 and 1.94 g cm−3, respectively, were adopted (79).

To reduce potential biases arising from uneven site distribution and heterogeneous record lengths, all loess-paleosol MAR series were interpolated to a uniform 100-year resolution and subsequently normalized to a standard z-score (80) using the following formula (Eq. 3)Z=X−μσ(3)where X is the interpolated MAR value, μ is the mean MAR, and σ is the SD of the time series.

Loess-paleosol sequences were aggregated into 1° × 1° grid cells. When multiple records occurred within the same grid, a grid-level time series, Zg(t), was computed as the weighted mean of all normalized records in that grid, with each record weighted by its temporal coverage relative to the total study interval (130 ka) using the following formula (Eq. 4)Wr=(Li130)arec(4)where Li is the valid temporal coverage (ka) of record i within grid g. The exponent arec = 0.5 was applied to moderately reduce the influence of short-duration records while preserving the relative importance of longer, more continuous sequences.

The grid-level MAR series were then combined across grids to produce regional mean MARs. Each grid was assigned a temporal-span weight calculated using the following formula (Eq. 5)Wg=(Lg130)agrid(5)where Lg represents the total valid temporal coverage within grid g. The exponent agrid = 0.5 was applied to similarly balance temporal representativeness and spatial equity among grids.

The regional mean MAR at each 100-year time slice t was obtained using the following formula (Eq. 6)MARregional(t)=∑gWgag(t)Zg(t)∑gWgag(t)(6)where ag(t) = 1 if the grid contains valid data at time t and 0 otherwise.

This two-level weighting framework minimizes the influence of densely clustered records or short-duration loess-paleosol records within individual grid cells and assigns lower weights to grids that contribute limited temporal coverage to the regional synthesis.

The robustness of the two-level weighting framework was evaluated through sensitivity analyses incorporating single-level weighting, alternative exponent values, different grid resolutions, and grid-origin offsets (table S4). Regional MAR curves generated using single-level weighting, alternative exponent values (a = 0.3 to 1.0), different grid sizes (1° and 2°), and grid-origin offsets (1° grids with integer-degree and 0.5° offset origins) were highly consistent with those from the baseline configuration (arec = agrid = 0.5; 1° grid; two-level framework), with correlation coefficients (r) ranging from 0.99 to 1.00 and root mean square error (RMSE) values between 0.00 and 0.11 (fig. S13). These results confirm the reliability of regional mean MAR estimates derived from the two-level weighting framework.

Moisture changes for each loess-paleosol record were reconstructed using the age-depth models and proxy indices of χfd for samples from northern Iran and central Asia and χlf and χfd for samples from East Asia. Similar to the MAR values, the original proxy data were interpolated to a 100-year resolution. The moisture index was derived by normalizing the moisture-sensitive proxies to the standard z-score (80) using the formula (Eq. 7)Z=X−μσ(7)where X is the interpolated proxy value, and μ and σ represent the mean proxy value and SD of the time series, respectively.

The regional mean moisture index was generated through a spatiotemporal synthesis of normalized z-scores using the same two-level weighting framework applied in the regional MAR reconstruction, providing representative moisture estimates for Asian subregions. The robustness of this framework was evaluated through sensitivity analyses incorporating single-level weighting, alternative exponent values, different grid resolutions, and grid-origin offsets (table S4). Regional moisture curves generated using single-level weighting, alternative exponent values (a = 0.3 to 1.0), different grid sizes (1° and 2°), and grid-origin offsets (1° grids with integer-degree and 0.5° offset origins) were highly consistent with those from the baseline configuration (arec = agrid = 0.5; 1° grid; two-level framework), with r ranging from 0.99 to 1.00 and RMSE values between 0.00 and 0.09 (fig. S14). These results confirm the reliability of regional mean moisture estimates derived from the two-level weighting framework.

Climate simulations using PMIP4

To explore the mechanisms underlying orbital-scale changes in Asian precipitation over the past 130 ka, we used climate simulation data from four experiments conducted as part of the PMIP4 framework (31, 32). These experiments include the lig127k, lgm, and midHolocene simulations, which are specifically designed to investigate climate conditions during the MIS 5e (127 ka), the LGM (21 ka), and the mid-Holocene (6 ka), respectively. In addition, the piControl simulation for 1850 CE serves as the reference experiment against which the three paleoclimate simulations are compared. The lig127k and midHolocene simulations are primarily driven by orbital parameters, with only minor changes in greenhouse gas concentrations, whereas the lgm simulation is primarily driven by the expanded ice sheets, lower sea levels, and reduced greenhouse gas concentrations, with minimal variations in orbital parameters at 21 ka. Although dozens of models participated in PMIP4, only three models have completed all of the aforementioned experiments. These three models were specifically selected for inclusion in this study (table S3). To minimize climate drift errors, we selected results from the final 100 years of each experiment segment for analysis. The data were standardized through bilinear interpolation to a spatial resolution of 1° × 1°, and multimodel mean (MMM) results were computed to reduce uncertainties associated with individual models.
