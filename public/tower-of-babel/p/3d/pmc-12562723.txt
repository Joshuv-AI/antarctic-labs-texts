Barriers to influenza vaccination in older adults with chronic diseases: Insights from a COM-B model–based meta-analysis
Authors: Luo Jiang, Jin Zhao, Hui Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated barriers to influenza vaccination among older adults with chronic diseases through a systematic review of 21 studies (391 467 participants, 13 countries) following PRISMA guidelines. According to the COM-B framework, barriers in the capability (OR = 1.47), opportunity (OR = 2.07) and motivation (OR = 1.35) dimensions significantly influenced influenza vaccination behaviors. The strongest predictors included no prior vaccination history (OR = 3.17), exposure to negative vaccine advice (OR = 2.96), and limited healthcare utilization (OR = 2.3). Findings emphasize that structural interventions targeting the opportunity dimension-particularly government-funded vaccination programs- exhibit the greatest potential for improving influenza vaccination coverage. Prioritizing policy-level initiatives over individual-level education campaigns may yield more equitable and sustainable outcomes in this high-risk group.KEYWORDS: Chronic diseases, older adults, influenza vaccination, meta-analysis, COM-B model

Introduction

Influenza poses a significant global public health threat, particularly for older adults with chronic conditions, who face an elevated risk of severe related complications and mortality.1 Previous estimates indicated that 3–5 million cases of influenza-associated severe illness and hospitalizations2,3 and 291 243–645 832 deaths4 occurred each year, with over 20% of hospitalizations2 and over 60% of deaths4 occurring in people aged 65 years and older. WHO has also been committed to enhancing influenza vaccination coverage among older adult populations.5 In accordance with World Health Assembly Resolution 56.19,6 all Member States have pledged to strengthen immunization efforts targeting older adults and individuals with chronic medical conditions. Chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and respiratory conditions exacerbate the risks associated with influenza, underscoring the importance of vaccination as a critical preventive measure.7–9 Numerous studies have demonstrated that influenza vaccination is highly effective at reducing influenza-related hospitalizations, particularly among individuals with preexisting cardiovascular and stroke conditions.10 Despite the well-established benefits of influenza vaccination for reducing morbidity and mortality rates, vaccination coverage remains suboptimal, particularly for older adults with chronic diseases. During the 2018–19 influenza season, approximately 68.1% of older adults in the United States received the influenza vaccine. This coverage increased significantly to 75.2% in the 2020–21 season; however, it declined to 69.7% during the 2023–24 season,11 falling below the WHO-recommended threshold of 75%.6 Vaccination rates are even lower among older adults with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, with rates falling below 60%.12 According to a report13 by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), vaccination coverage among older adults was documented across 19 EU/EEA countries during the 2020–21 influenza season. Among these, Denmark was the only country to achieve a vaccination rate of 75%, whereas most other countries reported coverage ranging between 50% and 70%. In contrast, Latvia recorded an exceptionally low rate of just 4.5%. Similarly, in China, vaccination coverage rates among older adults with chronic diseases remain alarmingly low, often falling below 30%.14

Several factors contribute to low vaccination rates in this population, and understanding these barriers is critical for improving vaccine acceptance rates. Common obstacles include concern about vaccine side effects,15–19 the underestimation of influenza risks,18,20 poor access to healthcare services,15–22 and a lack of healthcare provider recommendations.23,24 Moreover, unhealthy behaviors such as smoking and physical inactivity further complicate the situation and serve as additional vaccination barriers.1,25 Classifying these barriers to influenza vaccination according to relevant behavioral change theories can help clarify the main direction of the barriers and facilitate the subsequent development of targeted intervention measures.26,27

Professor Michie proposed the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior (COM-B) model, an important integrative framework for behavior change theory. Based on a systematic analysis of 19 behavior change frameworks,27 the model deconstructs the mechanism of behavior into the interaction of three core components: (1) Capability: psychological ability (knowledge, cognitive skills) and physiological ability (physical function) with emphasis of the cognitive and physiological basis of individual behaviors; (2) Opportunity: physical environment (time, resources, geographical accessibility) and social environment (cultural norms, social support) with focus on the external conditions for behaviors; and (3) Motivation: automatic motivation (emotional impulse, habit) and reflective motivation (rational decision-making, goal setting) that reflect the mechanisms driving behaviors.

The COM-B model breaks through the limitations of traditional linear models via the “capability-opportunity-motivation-behavior” circular feedback system.26 In health behavior research, the COM-B model has demonstrated strong explanatory power, while existing studies have confirmed its application value in scenarios such as vaccination28 and chronic disease management.29 Using this model, we identified and classified multilevel barriers to influenza vaccination behavior among older adults with chronic diseases and mapped them in three dimensions: capability, opportunity, and motivation. This approach provides a structured framework for analyzing factors influencing vaccination behavior and offers theoretical support for the design of targeted interventions.

Given its substantial public health implications, it is crucial to systematically review and quantify the modifiable barriers to influenza vaccination among older adults with chronic diseases. This meta-analysis aimed to identify and assess these barriers by ultimately providing interventions that can increase vaccination acceptance and improve the health outcomes of this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

This meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO and conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines that ensure a systematic and transparent approach to study selection and data extraction processes.30

Study scope

The PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases were searched for studies published between database inception and August 2024. Only studies examining the barriers to influenza vaccination acceptance among older adults with chronic diseases were considered for inclusion.1

Eligibility criteria and literature search

We included studies based on the pre-defined PICOTS (population, intervention, comparator, outcome, time, and study design) criteria: (1) the study population consisted of individuals aged ≥60 years with at least one chronic disease; (2) the study specifically reported on barriers to influenza vaccination for older adults with chronic diseases; (3) the study reported influenza vaccination rates within the target population and quantified the impact of barriers; (4) the study design was observational study. Exclude criteria based on the following criteria: (1) the study population included non-older adults with chronic diseases; (2) studies with ambiguous data or incomplete data or those lacking quantifiable outcomes; (3) non-English literature; (4) duplicate studies.

The literature search employed relevant Medical Subject Heading terms and text words. The detailed search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Material. A manual search was performed of the reference lists of the included articles to identify additional relevant studies that might have been overlooked.

Study screening

A researcher conducted the literature search and screened the search results. Two independent researchers screened the titles and abstracts of the identified articles. Studies that met the research objectives were subjected to full-text review. Any uncertainties regarding inclusion were resolved through discussions with a third researcher.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Key data extracted from the selected studies included first author, publication year, country/region, sample size, age of study population, and barriers to influenza vaccination. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated for each barrier; if not available, data that could be converted into the OR were extracted. When conversion was not possible, the data were summarized descriptively.

The included studies used cross-sectional cohort or case-control designs. The quality of these observational studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, a widely recognized tool for evaluating non-randomized studies and meta-analyses.31 Discrepancies in study quality assessment were resolved through discussion with a third author.

Statistical analysis

The data were standardized by conversion to a consistent form. When necessary, the OR (1/OR) and corresponding CI (1/upper CI and 1/lower CI) were flipped to ensure effect-size uniformity across studies.32

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 11.0. A fixed-effects model was used for barriers with low heterogeneity (I2 < 50%). For barriers with higher heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 50%), a random-effects model was used. The I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test were used to assess inter-study heterogeneity.33 Pooled OR and 95% CI were calculated for each COM-B dimension and specific barrier to quantify their impact on influenza vaccination behaviors. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test, with values of P ≤ .05 indicating potential bias.

The full-text review identified 11 barriers as outcome indicators: (1) concern about vaccine side effects, (2) no previous vaccination history, (3) unhealthy habits (smoking, history of smoking, alcohol consumption, lack of exercise), (4) no/rare use of medical services, (5) lack of vaccination information, (6) perceived barriers, (7) advice from others, (8) self-perceived health status, (9) low perceived benefit, (10) needle phobia, and (11) frequency of physical examination. Each barrier was then classified according to the COM-B model. The weighted effects of the different barriers were calculated across three dimensions. The quality assessment scores of all 21 included articles were ≥6, meaning that no obvious risk of bias was identified. The quality evaluation scores of the included studies are presented in Table S2.

The analysis of influenza vaccination rates across different countries based on the COM-B model did not reveal distinct patterns between countries with low (<75%) and high (≥75%) vaccination coverage. In countries with lower vaccination rates, no capability-related data were reported. Barriers were statistically significant in both the opportunity dimension (OR = 2.566; 95% CI, 1.992–3.305) and the motivation dimension (OR = 1.650; 95% CI, 1.193–2.283). Countries achieving higher vaccination rates demonstrated statistically significant outcomes across all three domains: capability (OR = 1.474; 95% CI, 1.186–1.833), opportunity (OR = 2.429; 95% CI, 1.602–3.684), and motivation (OR = 1.209; 95% CI, 1.049–1.393). No statistically significant differences in barriers were observed across dimensions between countries with low and high influenza vaccination rates. It is noteworthy that countries with higher vaccination rates universally implemented free vaccination policies, whereas those with lower rates lacked such policies (Table S3). This policy characteristic aligns with the opportunity dimension in the COM-B model framework.

Descriptive analysis of barriers to influenza vaccination

Among the 11 identified barriers, three – concern about vaccine side effects, lack of vaccination information, and needle phobia – could not be quantified using the OR. Therefore, these barriers were descriptively analyzed.

Concern about vaccine side effects

Concern about vaccine side effects was reported by five studies. The data showed substantial variation, with 28% of individuals in the Jiménez-Garcia et al.16, 28.6% in the Bacurau et al.18, 40% in the Poeppl et al.17, and 6.3% in Saiphoklang et al.15 studies reporting vaccination refusal due to concern about vaccine side effects. However, no significant intergroup differences were observed.

Lack of vaccination information

A lack of information was mentioned by two studies. According to Gorska-Ciebiada et al.22, 59% of individuals did not receive the influenza vaccine due to a lack of information, whereas according to Poeppl et al.17, only 5% of participants cited this reason.

Needle phobia

Needle phobia was a barrier according to two studies (Poeppl et al.17 and Saiphoklang et al.15), but its impact was minimal, with only approximately 1% of participants affected.

The influence of advice from others on vaccination decisions was also explored. Two studies did not provide OR, but descriptive data indicated that 3.27% of individuals in the Jiménez-Garcia et al.16 study refused vaccination because of a lack of recommendations from others, whereas 81.3% of individuals in the Saiphoklang et al.15 study reported that advice received from others influenced their vaccination decisions.

Analysis of barriers to influenza vaccination based on COM-B model

Capability

The capability dimension included two barriers: self-perceived health status and lack of vaccination information. The combined OR for this dimension was 1.474 (95% CI, 1.186–1.833).

Opportunity

The opportunity dimension included factors related to physical and social environments. Barriers such as no/rare use of medical services, perceived barriers, advice from others, and physical examination frequency were grouped into this dimension. The combined OR was 2.068 (95% CI, 1.409–3.034).

Motivation

This dimension included barriers such as concern about vaccine side effects, no previous vaccination history, unhealthy habits (smoking, alcohol use, lack of exercise), needle phobia, and low perceived benefits. The combined OR was 1.347 (95% CI, 1.081–1.677).

Based on the above classification results, the barriers affecting vaccination behavior were quantitatively analyzed in different dimensions of the COM-B model. Finally, the effect values of the three dimensions were obtained, namely opportunity (comprehensive OR = 2.068; 95% CI, 1.409–3.034), motivation (comprehensive OR = 1.347; 95% CI, 1.081–1.677), and capability (comprehensive OR = 1.474; 95% CI, 1.186–1.833) (Table 3).

Meta-analysis of specific barriers to influenza vaccination

Of the 11 identified barriers, three could not be quantified (lack of vaccination information, concern about vaccine side effects, and needle phobia). The remaining eight barriers were included in the meta-analysis, of which the following had significant findings (Table 4): no previous vaccination history (OR = 3.172; 95% CI, 1.159–8.682), no/rare use of medical services (OR = 2.319; 95% CI, 1.494–3.596), advice from others (OR = 2.395; 95% CI, 1.154–4.054), perceived barriers to vaccination (OR = 1.301; 95% CI, 1.049–1.161), and low perceived benefits of vaccination (OR = 1.197; 95% CI, 1.039–1.378), and unhealthy habits (OR = 1.339; 95% CI, 0.985–1.820). Self-perceived health status and physical examination frequency also have important impacts on vaccination decisions. Individuals whose self-perceived health status is “normal” (OR = 1.496; 95% CI, 1.034–1.811) or “good” (OR = 1.463; 95% CI, 1.117–1.916), as well as individuals who underwent physical examinations less frequently (OR = 2.180; 95% CI, 1.709–2.781), faced significant barriers to influenza vaccination.

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

Influenza vaccination rates across countries and regions

Guided by the WHO’s recommendation to target 75% influenza vaccination coverage in older adults,6 this study employed this value (75%) as the cutoff to dichotomize coverage levels into high and low. Four countries included in this study surpassed this threshold: South Korea, Brazil, the United States, and Thailand. Another group of countries and regions – Denmark, Germany, Taiwan, Israel, Spain, and Italy – achieved vaccination rates above 50%, yet remained below the 75% benchmark. In contrast, Poland, Austria, and China are all below 40%, with markedly lower coverage than the other countries. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in national vaccination policies and social environments.42,47–50 In countries where influenza vaccination coverage exceeds 50%, governments have typically established free vaccination policies, which correlate with steadily increasing immunization rates among older adults. For instance, from the 2018–19 to the 2023–24 influenza seasons, vaccination coverage among the older adults in the United States remained stable at approximately 70%.11 Meanwhile, Denmark achieved a rate of 75% during the 2020–21 season,13 meeting the WHO recommendation. By contrast, Poland48,50 and Austria49 do not provide nationwide free influenza vaccination for older adults, and China only offers free vaccines to older adults in select developed cities.47,51 It is noteworthy that the influenza vaccination rates among older adults in two East Asian countries – South Korea (85.10%) and China (4.70%) – highlight the pronounced disparity resulting from divergent policy approaches. Since 2005, South Korea has provided free influenza vaccinations to all individuals aged 65 and older,52,53 a policy that has consistently maintained coverage above 80% in this population. Prior studies confirm higher vaccination rates in Chinese regions with free policies compared to non-covered areas,51 Although direct quantification of policy effects was infeasible, the opportunity-focused vaccination policies of COM-B model may explain these variations. Further research with expanded datasets is warranted to validate these findings. However, the COM-B model-based analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in the opportunity (OR = 2.429 vs. 2.566) or motivation (OR = 1.209 vs. 1.650) dimensions between countries with high and low vaccination rates. Potential contributing factors were analyzed, prioritizing limitations in statistical power due to sparse data from low-coverage countries of this study.

Interpretation based on COM-B model

An analysis using the COM-B model revealed that all three barrier dimensions significantly influenced influenza vaccination behavior. This highlights the crucial role of external environmental support (opportunity), attitudes and beliefs of older adults regarding vaccination (motivation), availability of health information, and a health self-assessment (capability).

Capability barriers

Older adults with chronic diseases who perceive their health as good may underestimate the risk of influenza, leading to lower vaccination acceptance rates.54 The lack of accessible and accurate information about influenza and vaccination limits their ability to make informed decisions.55 This issue is particularly prevalent in communities with insufficient health education or in which information dissemination is inadequate. Improving the availability and accuracy of vaccination-related information is crucial for addressing this barrier.

Opportunity barriers

This dimension underscores the importance of external support in vaccination decision-making processes. Previous studies56,57 have confirmed that these external factors significantly contribute to low vaccination rates, emphasizing the need for enhanced doctor – patient communication, increased the accessibility and convenience of medical services, and reduced vaccine costs.

Motivation barriers

These psychological barriers in this dimension significantly reduced the willingness of older adults with chronic diseases to receive influenza vaccination. In particular, concern about vaccine side effects and low perceived benefits of vaccination are strong deterrents.58 Previous study34 also revealed that older adults who had never been vaccinated were less likely to change their stance, highlighting the importance of addressing motivational barriers to vaccination campaigns.

Meta-analysis results

The consistent associations of these factors identified in this meta-analysis suggest that they represent persistent barriers to vaccination across diverse populations.59 For example, individuals with no previous history of influenza vaccination were less likely to be vaccinated,34 indicating strong continuity in vaccination behaviors. Similarly, no/rare use of medical services was associated with lower vaccination rates, as individuals with limited contact with healthcare professionals were less likely to receive recommendations or education on vaccination. Therefore, their vaccination decisions are more susceptible to influence from their immediate social networks,60 including family and friends. Other barriers to influenza vaccination include financial and accessibility concerns.57 In countries without universal free vaccination policies, the associated financial cost presents a substantial barrier to improving immunization coverage. These findings underscore the importance of addressing both individual and social barriers to increase vaccination rates.

Unhealthy habits such as smoking were also identified as significant barriers, with smokers being less likely to accept vaccinations. In this study, however, a history of smoking was not found to be a barrier to vaccination. We speculate that this may be because individuals who have undergone smoking cessation often adopt more health-seeking behaviors, potentially making them more inclined to vaccinate than those who continue to smoke. Optimistic self-perceived health was also a notable barrier, with individuals who viewed their health as good or normal being less likely to perceive a need for vaccination (OR = 1.496 and 1.463, respectively). This finding suggests that individuals who feel healthy may underestimate the risk of influenza, reducing their motivation for vaccination. Finally, low perceived benefits and perceived barriers were significant obstacles to vaccination, as many older adults did not recognize the value of the vaccine or felt that the vaccination process was too cumbersome.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the included studies were all observational, and the use of self-reported data introduced potential selection and information biases, which may limit the ability to make causal inferences.61 Second, the studies included in this analysis were performed in diverse countries and regions with differences in culture, healthcare systems, and social contexts, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Third, certain barriers such as needle phobia lacked sufficient data for the quantitative analysis, and their impact was only qualitatively addressed. And finally, although the COM-B model provides a robust framework for analyzing vaccination behavior, the data used in this study were insufficient to enable full elucidation of the complex dynamics of vaccination decision-making.

Future research directions

In countries or regions without free vaccination policies, policy support and financial incentives should serve as primary measures to enhance immunization coverage. In settings with limited or partial fiscal subsidies, it is crucial to assess the real-world effectiveness of diverse policy interventions – such as varying levels of medical insurance reimbursement and the strategic allocation of vaccination sites – and to incorporate these insights into broader economic evaluations. This evidence-informed strategy will better guide the formulation of vaccination policies tailored to local conditions. For countries or regions that have already established free vaccination programs, complementary efforts in public awareness and health education remain crucial. Future research should build on this foundation and explore the specific barriers within each dimension in more detail, particularly the influence of socioeconomic factors on vaccination behavior. Longitudinal studies across multiple influenza seasons would also provide valuable insights into the temporal dynamics of vaccination behaviors.62 Further research is also required to validate the applicability of the COM-B model in different vaccination contexts to ensure that future interventions are grounded in a robust theoretical framework.

Conclusion

While barriers operate across COM-B dimensions, structural interventions targeting the opportunity dimension-particularly government-funded vaccination programs- exhibit the greatest potential for improving influenza vaccination coverage among older adults with chronic diseases. These findings suggest that priority should be given to structural interventions targeting the Opportunity dimension for this population, such as incorporating influenza vaccination into national immunization programs for older adults with chronic diseases. Simultaneously, further research is needed to refine our understanding of these barriers and develop more targeted interventions.
