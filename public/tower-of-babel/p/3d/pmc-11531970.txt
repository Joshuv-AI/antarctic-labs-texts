Fibroblast activation protein constitutes a novel target of chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy in solid tumors
Authors: Sikun Meng, Tomoaki Hara, Yutaka Miura, Hideshi Ishii
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith recent advances in tumor immunotherapy, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR‐T) cell therapy has achieved unprecedented success in several hematologic tumors, significantly improving patient prognosis. However, in solid tumors, the efficacy of CAR‐T cell therapy is limited because of high antigen uncertainty and the extremely restrictive tumor microenvironment (TME). This challenge has led to the exploration of new targets, among which fibroblast activation protein (FAP) has gained attention for its relatively stable and specific expression in the TME of various solid tumors, making it a potential new target for CAR‐T cell therapy. This study comprehensively analyzed the biological characteristics of FAP and discussed its potential application in CAR‐T cell therapy, including the theoretical basis, and preclinical and clinical research progress of targeting FAP with CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumor treatment. The challenges and future optimization directions of this treatment strategy were also explored, providing new perspectives and strategies for CAR‐T cell therapy in solid tumors.Keywords: CAR‐T therapy, FAP, solid tumors, TME

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR‐T) cell therapy has emerged as a highly promising cancer immunotherapy recently. It involves genetically engineering a patient's T cells to express CARs that recognize a specific antigen, thereby directing the modified T cells to “strike precisely” at the target. It has achieved quite satisfactory clinical results in several hematological cancers, a success that is eager to be replicated in solid tumors. However, CAR‐T cell therapy has been sluggishly advancing in the complexity of solid cancers, for which the lack of suitable antigens, poor infiltration of the tumor, and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) are the three inescapable obstacles.

With the increasing emphasis on the TME's role in solid cancers, it is generally accepted as a significant factor related to cancer onset and progression, as well as to drug interactions, recurrence, and metastasis. The TME is usually described as hypoxic and acidic, consisting of immune cells such as macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer cells, and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs); stromal cells such as cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), pericytes, and mesenchymal stromal cells 1 , 2 ; and other noncellular components such as the extracellular matrix (ECM), blood vessels, lymphatic organs or nodes, nerves, and chemokines. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The importance of fibroblast activation protein (FAP) has been repeatedly highlighted along with intensive efforts in cancer research, particularly the widespread use of sequencing technologies, including single‐cell sequencing. 7 , 8 FAP is a transmembrane serine protease and highly expressed in nearly all subtypes of CAFs in the TME and even directly in tumor cells in some cancers; however, it is rarely expressed in normal tissues. 9 Accumulating evidence shows that FAP exerts enzymatic and nonenzymatic effects, linking various molecules in tumors to regulate biological processes throughout tumor development. 10 Given its exclusive expression and indispensable contribution to solid tumor pathology, therapeutic approaches targeting FAP have sparked considerable attention in oncological research.

As mentioned above, restrictions such as limited infiltration and suppressive immune microenvironment substantially hamper the application of CAR‐T cell therapy in solid tumors. Accordingly, FAP serves as a specific target for CAR‐T cell therapy while simultaneously assisting in dismantling the physical barriers and tumor‐promoting TME by sniping at CAFs and even tumor cells themselves. Over the past decade, many research teams have appreciated and pursued the concept of targeting FAP for CAR‐T cell therapy. 11 Therefore, we outlined the basic principles of CAR‐T cell therapy and the challenges encountered in solid tumors while introducing FAP as a potential target to revolutionize the treatment landscape. Through a comprehensive review of existing research and progress in this field, we seek to reveal the advantages of integrating FAP‐targeted strategies into CAR‐T cell therapy and its effect on the future treatment of solid cancers, expecting to open new horizons for further research and applications.

CAR‐T CELL THERAPY

The fundamental principle of CAR‐T cell therapy involves enhancing the precision and potency of T cells by introducing a CAR that recognizes a specific antigen. 12 , 13 A CAR is a synthetic receptor composed of several key components: (1) single‐chain variable fragment (scFv), which is the extracellular recognition part of the CAR, usually derived from a monoclonal antibody scFv, and responsible for identifying and binding to specific tumor antigens; (2) transmembrane domain, which is involved in anchoring the CAR on the T‐cell surface; and (3) intracellular signaling domain, which participates in activating the T cell and triggering an immune response. 14 , 15 This design enables CAR‐T cells to recognize a broad range of antigens, including proteins, glycolipids, and carbohydrates, independently of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules and unrestricted by the downregulation or deletion of MHC expression. 16 , 17 Endowed with this capability, CAR‐T cell therapy has been regarded with tremendous anticipation in various cancers, particularly those that traditional immunotherapies find challenging to recognize and attack.

Development overview of CAR‐T cell therapy

In 1989, Gross et al. 13 initially proposed a scheme for modifying T‐cell receptors directly based on antigens. Over the past three decades, CAR‐T therapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in treating hematological malignancies. Since the Food and Drug Administration's approval of Kymriah for B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia in 2017, six CAR‐T cell therapies have been marketed, extending their reach to non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma and multiple myeloma. Despite its success in hematological cancers, CAR‐T cell therapy faces numerous challenges in solid tumors, including the complexity of the TME, immune suppression, tumor heterogeneity, and limitations in T‐cell infiltration and persistence. To enhance efficacy, longevity, and safety while addressing these challenges, CAR‐T cell therapy has undergone several generations of evolution 18 (Figure 1A).

Harnessing chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy against fibroblast activation protein‐positive cancer‐associated fibroblasts (FAP+ CAFs) in solid tumors. (A) Evolving CAR‐T cell therapies. CAR‐T cells basically consist of a singular extracellular targeting domain (scFv), a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain. Second‐ and third‐generation CAR‐T cells are equipped with co‐stimulators to augment the cytotoxic capabilities of T cells. Fourth‐generation CAR‐T cells are capable of secreting immunomodulatory molecules to mitigate the effects of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Fifth‐generation CAR‐T cells adopt a dual‐specific module to transform themselves into universal CAR‐T cells, in order to achieve greater controllability and practicality. (B) CAFs in the complex tumor microenvironment of solid tumors. Characterized by the consistent expression of FAP, CAFs play a dual role in TME, not only creating a formidable barrier against therapeutic agents but also exerting immunosuppressive effects that facilitate tumor growth and resistance to therapy. The targeting of FAP+ CAFs with CAR‐T‐cell therapy emerges as a promising strategy, offering the potential to disrupt these defensive fortifications and significantly enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in the battle against solid tumors.

First‐generation CAR‐T cells comprise a singular extracellular targeting domain, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular CD3ζ signaling domain. Although T cells can be activated through this design, their proliferation and persistence are constrained by the absence of co‐stimulatory signals. Expanding on this foundation, second‐generation CAR‐T cells incorporated a co‐stimulatory signal domain, such as CD28 or 4‐1BB (CD137). 19 This advancement makes it the most popular type of CAR‐T cell therapy in clinical practice by improving T cells' activity, capacity for proliferation, and durability. Third‐generation CAR‐T cells contain two or more co‐stimulatory signal domains, e.g., combining the domains of CD28 and 4‐1BB. 20 Fourth‐generation CAR‐T cells, also referred to as “TRUCKs” (T cells redirected for universal cytokine killing), are engineered to release immunomodulatory molecules, such as cytokines, in addition to equipment with targeting and co‐stimulatory domains. This feature is intended to improve the tumor microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of the therapy. 21 In 2012, Urbanska et al. innovatively introduced the “AND‐OR‐NOT” gating logic into CAR‐T cells, pioneering the fifth‐generation universal CAR‐T cells (UCAR‐T). The novel architecture splits the CAR's external segment into two components: a unique receptor on the T‐cell surface and a dual‐specificity targeting module. This module is adept at both homing in on the antigen and interacting with the T cell's unique receptor, setting the stage for highly adaptable CAR‐T cell applications. 22 , 23

Potential toxicities of CAR‐T cell therapy

Although CAR‐T cell therapy has achieved remarkable success in cancer treatment, it is still associated with certain adverse effects and possible toxicities. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) are the most prominent. 24 CRS is an excessive immune response triggered by the release of large amounts of cytokines such as interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), interleukin (IL)‐6, and IL‐10 upon CAR‐T cell activation, leading to symptoms such as fever, hypotension, and tachycardia, and, in severe cases, hemodynamic instability and organ damage. ICANS is a neurological side effect associated with immune effector cells, which is closely related to increased cytokine levels in the central nervous system, particularly in patients with high tumor burden, and can manifest as cognitive dysfunction, headache, and seizures. Moreover, on‐target off‐tumor (OTOT) toxicity in CAR‐T cell therapy, where CAR‐T cells correctly identify their intended target antigens (on‐target) that are also present on normal cells (off‐tumor), leads to undesirable reactions and tissue damage. The occurrence of the “OTOT” effect is often related to the selection of target antigens. Ideal CAR‐T cell therapy targets should be tumor‐specific, expressed only on the tumor cells and not or minimally on normal cells. However, most potential CAR‐T cell therapy targets are also expressed to some extent in normal tissues, increasing the risk of OTOT toxicity. To mitigate this, researchers meticulously select target antigens when developing CAR‐T cell therapies, considering more precise targets or establishing CAR structures that can accurately differentiate between tumor and normal cells. In addition, efforts are underway to devise tactics for managing CAR‐T cell activity, enabling swift termination or reversal of the effects on the detection of the OTOT phenomenon.

FIBROBLAST ACTIVATION PROTEIN

Fibroblast activation protein plays an increasingly vital role in solid cancer research. As a protein highly expressed in CAFs of most solid tumors, FAP is crucial in constructing and maintaining the TME. Exploring FAP's biological properties and its potential mechanisms in facilitating tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion will undoubtedly provide key insights for targeted therapies in solid tumors (Figure 1B).

FAP expression

Fibroblast activation protein is overexpressed in CAFs across various solid tumor types while exhibiting minimal expression in healthy adult tissues. 25 , 26 , 27 CAFs, which are the key cellular components of the TME, form and remodel the ECM, which affects the tumor's physical properties. They also interact with tumor cells and the TME, supporting and influencing tumor development, growth, and metastasis. Leveraging the limited proliferation capacity, eradicating CAFs can create a sustainable, impoverished TME, laying a strong foundation for the reconstitution of the endogenous immune system. 28 FAP overexpression in CAFs correlates with tumor development, progression, and invasiveness, making it a promising biomarker for CAFs. In cancers with a strong desmoplastic reaction, such as lung, pancreatic, hepatocellular, and breast cancers, FAP expression is closely associated with local tumor invasion, increased risk of lymph node metastasis, and poor prognosis. 29 Intriguingly, in certain stages of cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, osteosarcoma, esophageal adenocarcinoma, and epithelial ovarian cancer, FAP can also be expressed on tumor cells, potentially signifying local invasion, distant metastasis, and increased treatment resistance. 11 Jiang et al. reconstructed a functional human liver in immunodeficient mice using primary human hepatocytes and induced hepatocellular carcinoma (iHCC) that replicated the histological structure and gene expression characteristics of clinical hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples by introducing specific tumor‐related genes. Transcriptomic analysis revealed the expressions of FAP and another transmembrane glycoprotein MUC1 in iHCC but not in the normal liver, and the effective clearance of iHCC cells by CAR‐T cells targeting both antigens indirectly demonstrates its accessibility. 30

Functional aspects of FAP

Fibroblast activation protein functions in the TME through both enzymatic and nonenzymatic mechanisms, collectively facilitating tumor progression. FAP, a type II membrane‐bound protein, has a single transmembrane region, with its N‐terminus (amino end) inside its cytoplasm and the C‐terminus (carboxy end) which houses its enzymatic activity, external to the cell. As a dipeptidyl peptidase (DPP), FAP is a proline‐selective serine protease (endopeptidase) that can hydrolyze the glycine–proline (Gly–Pro) bond, located in two amino acids or more from a protein's N‐terminus. This effect is sometimes described as gelatinase activity caused by gelatin's rich Gly–Pro bonds, and it is similarly applicable to its precursor, collagen. Indeed, structural proteins in the ECM, such as collagens I and III, contain abundant Gly–Pro bonds, suggesting that FAP's endopeptidase activity may remodel the ECM. Moreover, substrates such as fibroblast growth factor 21, colony‐stimulating factor‐1, neuropeptide Y, and C–X–‐C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CXCL5) undergo catalysis by FAP, significantly affecting metabolism, ECM, and immune responses. The distinction between the enzymatic and nonenzymatic roles of FAP in ECM remodeling is not fully clear, and various hypotheses have been proposed. Beyond endopeptidase activity, FAP directly influences cell signaling pathways, altering cell cycles to promote cell growth and cancer transformation.

FAP and tumor vasculature

In tumors, the vasculature serves as a transport pathway not only for nutrients, such as in normal tissues, but also as an entry point for tumor cells into the bloodstream, making it a crucial indicator for cancer diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis assessment. Ebert et al. analyzed the Ivy Glioblastoma Atlas Project dataset and discovered strong FAP expression in microvascular proliferative regions and proliferating vessels obtained through laser capture microdissection, suggesting that FAP expression is a distinct feature of tumor angiogenesis, specifically prominent in glioblastomas. 31 A previous study found that FAP‐positive stromal cells upregulated factors promoting angiogenesis, such as angiopoietin‐2, endothelin‐1, insulin‐like growth factor binding protein 2, and vascular endothelial growth factor. 32 Hence, FAP is a potential target for tumor angiogenesis, and inhibition of FAP activity could suppress tumor vascular development and progression. 29

FAP and immunosuppression

The expression of FAP on CAFs can activate the PTEN/Akt and MAP2K/ERK pathways, leading to an increase in glycogen synthase kinase 3β, nuclear factor‐kappa B, and chemokines like C‐C motif ligand‐2 and CXCL8. This elevation contributes to the recruitment of immunosuppressive cell populations such as regulatory T cells, tumor‐associated macrophages, and MDSCs. 33 , 34 Concurrently, cytokines and chemokines secreted by CAFs, such as transforming growth factor‐β and CXCL12, can inhibit the activation and recruitment of T lymphocytes at the cancer site. 35 , 36 Through this dual mechanism of action, FAP's expression and activity in the TME play a crucial role in tumor immune evasion and immunosuppression, exacerbating the challenges faced by immunotherapies.

The association between FAP expression and poor prognosis is most evident in colorectal, pancreatic, liver, and breast cancers. 37 , 38 FAP is considered a significant diagnostic target, and various therapeutic approaches emphasize the importance of depleting FAP+ cells in controlling tumor growth. Notably, some noncancerous diseases, such as arthritis, fibrosis, cardiovascular diseases (e.g., atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction), and metabolic disorders, are closely linked to FAP upregulation. Beyond its role in tumor stroma, FAP also expresses in cells within inflammatory tissues during wound healing and maintains homeostasis in fat tissue, pancreas, stromal cells, and skeletal muscles. The pathologically specific nature of FAP confers its promising potential in research on heart fibrosis, 39 Achilles’ tendon injury, 40 and pulmonary fibrosis. 41

FAP and immunotherapy

Compared with the highly variable tumor‐associated antigens on tumor cells, FAP's expression in the TME is relatively stable, making it an attractive target for immunotherapy. Targeting CAFs is a strategy to activate the TME to increase immune cell infiltration and transform the TME into an immune‐sensitive environment. Since FAP is also highly expressed in some cancer cells, FAP targeting can potentially weaken the TME while eliminating cancer cells in certain cancers. 30 Various FAP‐targeted immunotherapies have been explored, such as radiotherapy, CAR‐T cell therapy, tumor vaccines, and antibodies; however, most are still in the preclinical research stage, with few advancing to clinical trials. In addition, beyond therapeutic purposes, these approaches can target the tumor stroma for tracing treatment and even offer diagnostic advantages from a clinical perspective. 42 Undeniably, as a broad‐spectrum target for tumor diagnosis and intervention, FAP has gained extensive recognition and attention for providing a new strategy for activating the TME.

FAP‐TARGETED CAR‐T CELL THERAPY

Fibroblast activation protein is unique not only manifested in its specific and widespread expression in the TME but also in its crucial role in regulating various processes of tumor development, including aspects related to poor prognosis and cancer metastasis. This section focuses on the research progress of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy in solid tumors, reviewing related preclinical and clinical trials, and discussing current challenges and optimization strategies. In particular, how FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy can dismantle the physical barriers and tumor‐favoring immune microenvironment in solid cancers, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of CAR‐T cells in solid tumor treatment, will be explored.

CAR‐T cells reverse impoverished TME by targeting FAP + cells

According to Bruni et al., in solid tumors, a “hot” immune environment characterized by dense immune infiltration, effective antigen presentation, and immune activation mechanisms is conducive to enhancing the efficacy of cancer treatments, reducing recurrence rates, and slowing cancer progression while potentially reducing resistance to certain treatments and offering better therapeutic prospects for patients with cancer. 43 , 44 In mouse models xenografted with human lung cancer cells, the infusion of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells successfully disrupted the formation of tumor foci, angiogenesis, and duct‐like structures in tumor nodules. 29 In mouse xenograft and syngeneic models of pancreatic cancer, FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy achieved matrix cell depletion rates of 62% and 91%, respectively, with significant reductions observed in key ECM components such as collagen, hyaluronan, and versican. 29 In another study, AE17.ova cells formed tumors in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised mice, and FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells more effectively inhibited tumor growth in the immunocompetent mouse model. 20 Further study found that mice treated with FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells had significantly higher numbers of tumor necrosis factor‐α‐producing CD4+ T cells and CD69+ and IFN‐γ+CD8+ T cells than the control group, indicating that targeting FAP with CAR‐T cells activated endogenous T cells and increased their tumor infiltration, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity. 20

A phase I clinical trial (NCT01722149) on malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), a rare and highly aggressive malignancy originating in the pleural tissue, 45 was conducted. In preclinical trials targeting MPM, FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells demonstrated antigen specificity in vitro and in vivo and inhibited the growth of FAP‐positive human MPM cells in the peritoneal cavity of mice, significantly prolonging their survival. 19 Twenty‐one days before the clinical trial, researchers extracted CD8‐positive T cells from patient peripheral blood, transduced them with a second‐generation CAR gene targeting FAP through retrovirus, and then administered a single injection of 1 × 10 6 redirected T cells into the patient's pleural effusion. Over 35 days of continuous monitoring, systemic expansion of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells was detected in the blood of all three patients, with two experiencing postoperative thromboembolic events that were resolved and deemed unrelated to the CAR‐T cell therapy, potentially linked to cancer activity, chemotherapy, or catheter placement. The trial's preliminary results revealed positive indications of safety and initial efficacy, laying the groundwork for further development of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy in clinical applications. 46 , 47 , 48 , 49

Combination therapy

Cancer treatment, as one of the most challenging medical issues, often requires a combination of methods. Within this multimodal intervention framework, CAR‐T cell therapy targeting FAP can dismantle the TME, paving the way for other treatments and enhancing antitumor efficacy. Recent research has increasingly favored the combination of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy with other treatment modalities.

Some studies have indicated that while the combination of CAR‐T cell therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) is generally safe, its effectiveness is quite limited. 50 , 51 Primary resistance to ICI in some patients is associated with low levels of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), 52 , 53 whereas a higher level of these lymphocytes is typically observed in those who respond well to ICI therapy. 54 , 55 Given that FAP+CAFs reduce T‐cell levels in tumor tissues, 56 , 57 , 58 FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy might enhance T‐cell infiltration in tumors, 20 increasing the likelihood of a positive response to ICI therapy. Indeed, Gulati et al. 59 attempted to block programmed cell death protein‐1 during FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy and found that the combination significantly inhibited tumor growth in humanized tumor‐bearing mice and prolonged their survival. Because TILs can attack various tumor antigens, they may be more effective against highly heterogeneous solid tumors. Briefly, FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy enhances immune infiltration to assist this diverse antigen recognition ability, and ICIs prevent immune escape by blocking immune checkpoints, contributing to a more comprehensive eradication of tumor cells, rendering it a promising solution.

Interestingly, many studies have explored the combination of two types of CAR‐T cells that target FAP and another antigen, likely capitalizing on the ability of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells to clean the TME. Kakarla et al. 60 first demonstrated the antitumor effect of combining FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells with those for cancer cells on the A549 lung cancer model by recognizing EphA2 antigens. In contrast, a study in 2022 exhibited the synergistic effect in lung metastatic cancer mouse models of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells combined with CAR‐T cells targeting Nectin4, a potential therapeutic target in various solid tumors, implying broader applicability in more types of solid tumors. A related phase I clinical trial (NCT03932565) unfolded in 2019, aiming for breakthroughs in the treatment and prognosis of advanced malignant solid tumors. 21 In 2023, Liu et al. proposed a sequential therapy strategy that combined CAR‐T cells targeting FAP and CLDN18.2 (claudin 18.2) to treat pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Briefly, FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells first eliminated CAFs in the TME, followed by the introduction of CLDN18.2‐targeted CAR‐T cells to directly target and kill cancer cells. This sequential therapy had significantly higher antitumor effects than a mixture of both CAR‐T cells infused simultaneously. Notably, FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy not only increased cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration but also indirectly enhanced the effect of subsequent CLDN18.2‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy by inhibiting the recruitment of MDSCs. 61 Overall, this combined approach attacks multiple tumor biomarkers (i.e. FAP and Nectin4) to reduce tumor cell resistance to single treatments, offering a more comprehensive and effective therapeutic method.

In addition to FAP, mesothelin has also emerged as a significant target in the tumor microenvironment. Mesothelin is a tumor‐associated antigen overexpressed in several malignancies, including MPM, pancreatic cancer, ovarian cancer, and some lung cancers. 62 Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of mesothelin‐specific CAR‐T cells in inducing antitumor activity. For example, mRNA‐engineered T cells targeting mesothelin have shown feasibility and safety in clinical trials without causing off‐tumor toxicity. 63 These findings suggest that mesothelin, similar to FAP, could play a crucial role in improving the efficacy of CAR‐T cell therapies in solid tumors.

Challenges

Currently, in its infancy, FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy remains a developing field. To identify key challenges supposed to be addressed in the future, this chapter attempts to synthesize and analyze issues that have arisen and may emerge, based on existing research.

Although FAP is not or is rarely expressed in normal tissues, attention must be paid to the potential “OTOT” risks of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy. A study in 2013 reported severe cachexia and bone toxicity in mice treated with FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells, which was attributed to the attack on bone marrow stromal cells highly expressing FAP. 64 This highlighted the need for consideration of FAP's heterogeneity in different cells 65 , 66 and the selection of more specific scFv to reduce “off‐target” aftermath in the design of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T therapies. As proof, a subsequent study optimized the scFv targeting FAP to mitigate similar toxicities. 67

In various human tumors, the expression of FAP in CAFs may vary in origin, quantity, and distribution, which may lead to distinct responses to treatments among different tumors or even at different stages. 68 In addition, this heterogeneity is challenging to extrapolate from in vitro studies to in vivo contexts. 69 The heterogeneity of CAFs stems from their origins; indiscriminate eradication of CAFs is not recommended because they are a highly plastic cell group with context‐dependent behavior that dynamically changes over time. 70 CAFs interact with other cell types in the TME, such as immune cells, and the complexity of these interactions indicates that simply clearing CAFs may not suffice for cancer treatment and could even have negative consequences. Furthermore, some CAF types exert tumor‐suppressive functions, 69 , 71 , 72 such as apCAFs, which can present antigens to CD4+ T cells and are thus considered to participate in antitumor processes, and more evidence is expected to fully elucidate their antitumor effects. 73

The overactivation of the immune system with FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy poses risks of CRS and ICANS, highlighting the importance of precise dosing and timing. For different cancer types, a detailed assessment from multiple perspectives, including CAR‐T cell persistence, stability post‐TME remodeling, and potential systemic immune responses, is essential to determine critical parameters, minimize potential risks, and ensure treatment safety and efficacy. Moreover, CAR‐T cell therapy still faces challenges such as limited response in patients with an immunocompromised state, lengthy and expensive manufacturing processes, and limited cell sources.

Optimization strategies

Nonetheless, the growth of CAR‐T cell therapy has introduced numerous innovative design concepts and strategies that can assist in improving the dilemmas encountered in the fight against solid tumors (Figure 2).

Future evolutionary directions of FAP CAR‐T therapy. (A) Precise modifications of each domain of CAR through gene editing platforms assist in reducing the risks of “OTOT” risk. (B) Schematic illustration of logic gates and Bi‐specific T‐cell engager (BiTE) mechanism. In Khanali et al.'s study, when the scFv/IL‐12Rβ2 receptor of CAR‐T cells recognizes tumor‐associated antigen 1 (TAA1), the expressions of TAA2 and TAA3 receptors are triggered. CAR‐T cells then target tumors only presenting with TAA1 in conjunction with TAA2 or TAA3. BiTEs are engineered antibodies that engage T cells to recognize and attack cancer cells. BiTEs can be designed for both CAR‐T cells and endogenous T cells to direct them to a certain target. In Loureiro et al.'s study, target modules enable meticulous control over antigen recognition of universal CAR‐T cells, acting as switches to integrate gate logic for targeted activation and therapeutic intervention. (C) Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) facilitate the delivery of FAP CAR mRNA into T cells through endocytosis, bypassing the nucleus for direct cytoplasmic translation and subsequent expression on the cell membrane. As the mRNA is not integrated into the genome and is degraded naturally, this approach is more manageable in terms of risk and efficacy.

Precise design of CAR structure through gene‐editing platforms

Gene‐editing platforms such as CRISPR‐Cas9, TALEN, and ZFN, which utilize molecular biology techniques, enable precise modifications of specific DNA sequences, allowing scientists to finely design each CAR domain. Das et al. 67 enhanced the specificity of the scFv in their FAP‐targeted UCAR‐T cells using TALEN editing. In addition, by knocking out T‐cell receptors and MHC‐related genes, they improved the survival and functionality of CAR‐T cells in allogeneic environments. Such optimizations can reduce “OTOT” risks, minimize rejection reactions, alleviate CAR‐T cell exhaustion, and enhance treatment safety.

Precise control of CAR‐T cell activation using logic gates and switch systems

In the age of information, the intersection of biotechnology and computational logic is redefining the forefront of cancer treatment. Khanali et al. designed a versatile type of T cell that can attack tumor cells and indirectly target CAFs, ingeniously integrating “AND‐OR‐NOT” logic. When the scFv/IL‐12Rβ2 receptor of CAR‐T cells recognizes tumor‐associated antigen 1 (TAA1), the expressions of TAA2 and TAA3 receptors are triggered. CAR‐T cells then targeted tumors only presenting with TAA1 in conjunction with TAA2 or TAA3, thereby mitigating the risk of “OTOT” effects. Recognition of TAA1 also induces the expression of anti‐FAP Bi‐specific T‐cell engager (BiTE) genes in CAR‐T, directing endogenous T cells to clear CAFs, enhancing immune infiltration, and improving TME. 74 Loureiro et al. applied the concept of segmented control using a “switch” system to a universal CAR‐T cell (UniCAR‐T cells) that recognizes FAP through target modules (TMs). UniCAR‐T cells, engineered with the UniCAR E5B9 epitope and unresponsive to surface antigens, are directed toward FAP via TMs of different sizes. In the absence of TMs, these T cells remained inert and harmless, only becoming active and promoting cytotoxicity in the presence of TMs linked to specific recognition molecules, which is FAP in this case. In in vitro (2D and 3D models), TMs effectively and specifically redirected UniCAR‐T cells, showing improved cytotoxicity and cytokine release. In vivo, UniCAR‐T cells demonstrated antitumor activity in the presence of TMs, even achieving tumor‐free mice. The high specificity accumulation of size‐expanded TMs in tumor tissues showed potential in tumor‐tracing diagnostics. The flexible pharmacokinetics of small molecule modules make CAR‐T cell activity more controllable, potentially avoiding excessive immune activation‐induced CRS and neurotoxicity and are more suitable for personalized treatment schemes. 75 Overall, logic gate control strategies ensure CAR‐T cell activation only under specific conditions with “AND‐OR‐NOT” logic, reducing the risk of off‐target attacks, and switch control systems allow external signals to modulate CAR‐T cell activity, enhancing treatment safety and controllability.

Nanoparticle‐based mRNA expression in CAR‐T cells

The 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Dr. Karikó and Dr. Weissman, pioneers of the mRNA COVID‐19 vaccine. mRNA technology, which is renowned for not altering the genome, is considered safer and invaluable in swiftly addressing emerging diseases. Inspired by this technology, Rurik et al. developed an mRNA‐based FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy, demonstrating its potential in treating cardiac diseases and offering a new direction for CAR‐T cell therapy optimization. They encapsulated the mRNA of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) targeting T cells via CD5. The endocytosis mechanism releases mRNA into the cytoplasm, enabling direct ribosomal CAR‐T expression for DNA‐free in vivo editing. Applied in a mouse model of heart failure, this CD5/LNP‐FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell approach resulted in effective mRNA delivery, CAR‐T cell generation, and a notable reduction in cardiac fibrosis. Echocardiography showed significant improvement in cardiac function. 76 Given the production period and cost of traditional ex vivo‐edited CAR‐T cells, this mRNA‐LNP method offers convenience, controllability in dosage and timing, and enhanced penetration caused by vascular transport, presenting an ingeniously conceived system.

Exploring the mechanisms of CAR‐T cells' efficacy and side effects using multiple approaches

Efficacy analysis is crucial for therapeutic improvement and innovation. Lee et al. developed a tool using the radiotracer named [18F]AlF‐FAPI‐74 to monitor responses to FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy, which was validated in a rodent lung cancer model for tracking FAP expression changes. This method, which enabled noninvasive imaging of the TME, provided predictive and pharmacodynamic response biomarkers for FAP‐targeted therapy. 77 In FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy, unraveling the complexities of the TME and molecular patterns is key to unlocking the mystery of therapeutic outcomes. Fortunately, current sequencing technologies allow for unbiased, high‐throughput, single‐cell resolution analyses of cellular heterogeneity and molecular patterns. Multiomics analyses, including genomics, epigenomics, transcriptomics, T‐cell receptor profiling, metabolomics, and proteomics, provide real‐time dynamic insights at different levels, supporting the optimization of targets for FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy, precise determination of T‐cell states, and elucidation of related toxicity mechanisms. The combined application of these technologies not only helps in precisely tuning and optimizing the design and function of CAR‐T cells but also provides critical biological information for personalized therapy, thus advancing treatment strategies for specific tumor types. 78 , 79 , 80

CONCLUSIONS

FAP‐targeted CAR‐T therapy remains largely unexplored, and more clinical trials are needed to confirm its clinical efficacy and safety. Although FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cell therapy primarily does not target tumor cells, the role of FAP and CAFs in the TME should not be underestimated, and the transformative effects of the TME in tumor development cannot be overlooked. Choosing FAP, preferentially expressed in tumor tissues, particularly in the TME, as the target for CAR‐T cell therapy follows a “simplifying complex problems” approach, seamlessly dovetailing with the three major challenges of CAR‐T cell therapy in solid cancers: infiltration, specific antigen identification, and immunosuppressive TME, potentially opening new avenues in the fight against solid tumors.
