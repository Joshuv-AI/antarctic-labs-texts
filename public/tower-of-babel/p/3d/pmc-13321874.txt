Microbiome and metabolites impact enteric and central nervous systems in ALS
Authors: Erykah I Walton, Jun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) has been linked to gastrointestinal symptoms and alterations in the gut microbiota. The enteric nervous system (ENS) coordinates intestinal function and sits at the host–microbe interface. The mechanisms by which luminal changes relay to the central nervous system (CNS), where motor neurons reside, have yet to be completely defined. In this narrative review, we first present evidence from ALS patient cohorts and preclinical models alongside mechanistic studies of infection, dysbiosis, and related neurodegenerative diseases to discuss how the microbiota and its metabolites may affect the ENS and CNS in ALS. Next, we propose a plausible mechanism of ALS pathogenesis through the gut–microbiome–brain axis. We further offer a summary of clinical trials that have studied the impacts of the microbiota on human ALS. Finally, we discuss future directions for studies of microbiota–ENS–CNS interactions in ALS. Better understanding of the dynamic interactions among the microbiota, microbial metabolites, neuroactive metabolites, and inflammation through the ENS/CNS in ALS will provide innovative insights into ALS prevention and treatment.Keywords: ALS; dysbiosis; enteric nervous system; central nervous system; inflammation, microbiota, microbiome, gut–microbiota–brain–axis, microbial metabolites, neurodegenerative diseases

Introduction

ALS is a progressive motor neuron disease (MND) first described in 1869 by Jean-Martin Charcot, 1 It is marked by degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons and becomes fatal when muscle weakness leads to paralysis of respiratory muscles. 2 While considered a rare disease, ALS incidence continues to rise, with cases projected to reach 38,000 annually in the United States by 2030. 3 The exact etiology of ALS remains unknown. Several genes associated with ALS have been identified, including SOD1, TDP-43, FUS, ANG, ATXN2, VCP, OPTN, and C9orf72. 1 , 4 While genetic mutations have been identified in many cases of familial ALS, incomplete penetrance along with sporadic ALS being more common suggests that there are environmental or lifestyle mechanisms driving the risk of ALS. 5

An emerging view is that the gut microbial community is one such environmental factor that plays critical roles in ALS. 6-11 Further, as we have noted in our previous review, a myriad of gastrointestinal (GI) disorders exist among patients with ALS, including pancreatic exocrine dysfunction, delayed gastric emptying, slowed colonic transit times, and constipation. 6 The presence of dysbiosis and GI dysfunction in ALS patients points to a shared interface: the enteric nervous system (ENS). The ENS is the primary local controller of gut motility and digestive functions 7 , 8 and rests in the similar intestinal environment that is subject to microbes and their metabolites. The gut–microbiota–brain axis describes the communication between the gut and the microbiota that reside there and the brain through immune, metabolic, endocrine, and neural systems. 9 , 10 Alterations in any of these pathways may contribute to disease. The gut–microbiota–brain axis offers a framework for understanding how the microbiota could impact intestinal function and influence neural systems and other organs relevant to ALS.

In this review, we will discuss how the gut microbiota and their associated metabolites affect the nearby ENS and how ENS-linked changes may be transmitted to the central nervous system (CNS), where ALS pathology predominates. We will summarize the existing evidence and discuss mechanisms of ALS pathogenesis through the gut‒brain-microbiota axis. Because ALS is a clinical diagnosis, 11 we will further offer a summary of clinical trials that modulate microbiota in patients with ALS. Finally, we will discuss limits of current studies and propose future directions for studies of microbiota–ENS–CNS interactions in ALS. Understanding interactions between the ENS, CNS, GI, and microbiota will enhance the diagnostic repertoire and inform innovative therapies to improve quality of life for patients with ALS.

Methods

This narrative review synthesizes literature on the relationships among the gut microbiota and its metabolites, the enteric nervous system, and the central nervous system, all within the context of ALS. The goal of this review was to integrate evidence from ALS microbiome studies, ENS and CNS physiology, the microbiota‒gut‒brain axis, and translational applications of the microbiota and evaluate how gut microbiota‒ENS‒CNS interactions may contribute to ALS pathogenesis.

The literature search was organized around the major topics of the manuscript. Relevant peer-reviewed literature was identified primarily through PubMed, with additional targeted searches performed using Google Scholar and reference lists from selected primary research articles and reviews. The sources included studies of the human ALS microbiome, ALS animal models, enteric and central nervous systems, microbiota‒gut‒brain axis, and microbial metabolites. Review articles were used to establish foundational concepts and locate additional primary sources. Searches were narrowed to studies published from 2020 onward to assess the ALS microbiome and gut‒brain axis literature published before/in June 2026, although older studies were included if they were foundational or necessary for a mechanistic context. The search terms included “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,” “ALS,” “microbiome,” “microbiota,” “dysbiosis,” “enteric nervous system,” “central nervous system,” “gut‒brain axis,” “microbiota‒gut‒brain axis,” “microbial metabolites,” “intestinal barrier,” and “neurodegenerative disease.” Additional searches were conducted during manuscript writing to address specific gaps. Clinical trial information was gathered separately using ClinicalTrials.gov and ALS clinical trial resources, as detailed in ALS Focused Microbiome and Biomarker Clinical Trials.

ALS pathogenesis

ALS is a motor neuron disease whose central pathology is the degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. 2 , 4 At the cellular level, ALS pathogenesis is thought to be multifactorial, involving several mechanisms, including glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, protein aggregation, and neuroinflammation. 12 ALS-associated genes, such as SOD1, TDP-43, C9orf72, and FUS have been linked to pathological processes such as impaired clearance of free radicals, protein misfolding and aggregation, disordered RNA metabolism, and defects in axonal and cytoskeletal transport. 13 Neuroinflammation is also a feature of ALS and is characterized by the activation of microglia and astrocytes, infiltration of T cells, elevation of complement system factors, and a compromised blood‒brain barrier (BBB). 14 Together, these processes are thought to contribute to motor neuron dysfunction and eventual neuronal death, resulting in progressive neurodegeneration. 14 Given that ALS is multifactorial, gene‒environment interactions are likely to play a role, and peripheral factors that influence inflammation, barrier integrity, or neural signaling may be potential contributors to disease risk or progression. The gut microbiota and its involvement with the ENS and CNS may represent one such influence, as explored throughout this review.

Microbiome and ENS in health

ENS overview

The ENS innervates organs along the gastrointestinal tract 15 , 16 and can operate independently of the CNS to run digestive functions. 8 , 17 The major functions of the ENS include controlling gut motility, secreting gastric hormones, facilitating nutrient absorption, regulating intestinal blood flow, and controlling fluid movement and nutrient absorption. 7 , 8 ENS functions, such as gut motility, can be compromised in diseases that affect the intestine, including ALS. 18 , 19 Enteric neurons are the primary effectors that coordinate gut physiology in the ENS and include approximately 20 neuron types 16 that aid in digestion-centric functions such as control of the lower esophageal sphincter, propulsion of gastric contents, and intestinal motility. 15 , 17 Enteric neurons emerge from two plexuses that form the core circuitry of the ENS and help autonomously control gut motility, secretion, and blood flow. The myenteric plexus is embedded between the longitudinal and circular muscle layers, while the submucosal plexus lies in the lumen beneath the mucosa. These locations map onto function: myenteric circuits primarily control motility via smooth muscle activity, whereas submucosal circuits primarily regulate fluid secretion and mucosal blood flow. 7 While enteric neurons and their associated circuits drive the effector functions of the ENS, enteric glial cells (EGCs) are non-neuronal cells that support the ENS. 16 EGCs are important because of their ability to interact with a diverse set of cells within the ENS, including the neuronal cells of the myenteric and submucosal plexus, digestion-associated cells (e.g., enterocytes, enteroendocrine cells), and immune cells. 17 One such support role is the ability of EGCs to regulate the growth and development of enteric neurons 20 or alter the motility capabilities of enteric neurons through intercellular signaling mediated by connexin-43. 7

The ENS interacts with a diverse set of cells along the intestinal tract to receive and respond to information about the luminal contents. Enteroendocrine cells (EECs) are responsible for nutrient sensing and respond via the secretion of hormones such as secretin, cholecystokinin, and gastric inhibitory peptide. 21 EECs relay what they sense in the lumen to the ENS and are the connection of luminal contents to the neural response. 7 The sensing capabilities of EECs extend beyond sensing nutrients to the microbiota and their metabolites. For instance, EECs can sense bacterial derived short chain fatty acids via their free fatty acid receptors and offers one such route for the ENS to respond to the microbial environment within the gut. 22 These interactions with EECs illustrate that the ENS is equipped to detect luminal cues, including microbiota-derived signals, and convert them into appropriate enteric neural responses.

Microbiome and microbial products that signal to ENS

The human microbiome is often defined as a community of microorganisms “that inhabit a particular environment and especially the collection of microorganisms living in or on the human body”. 23 Microbiomes, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, phages, and archaea, 24 have distinct physio-chemical properties, functions, and interactions with their environments. 25 In this review, we will focus on bacterial-ENS interactions, while acknowledging that work is also being done with other microorganisms, including viruses 26 and fungi. 27 Given that the vast majority of bacteria in the body are found in the colon, and the ileum is the third most populous region, 28 we focus on how bacteria in the gut influence the ENS and, subsequently, the development of ALS.

The ENS participates in a bidirectional interaction with gut microbiota. Within the gut wall, enteric neurons and glia express pattern recognition receptors, namely, Toll-like receptors (TLRs), enabling the ENS to detect microbial products at the mucosal surface. 7 In particular, TLRs 3, 4, and 7 have been found in the neurons and glial cells of mouse enteric plexuses and the human ileum. 29 Interestingly, TLRs are not solely for microbe detection. For example, TLR2 can promote colonic neurogenesis in a microbiota-dependent manner. 30 EECs provide a complementary sensory route by converting luminal microbial metabolites into hormonal and neural signals. For instance, microbial fermentation products such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) can activate free fatty acid receptors (FFAR1/FFAR2/FFAR3) on EECs in the duodenum and proximal gut and promote the secretion of hormones, such as glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1). 31 This type of environmental sensing is also apparent at the transcriptional level of enteric neurons. A recent study profiled enteric neurons in gnotobiotic and type-2 immunity-modeled mice. The study identified transcriptional responses in enteric neurons across various microbial states and immune challenges, finding that gastrin-releasing (Grp)-hi neurons expand in the colon under germ-free conditions and that Neuromedin U (Nmu)-hi sensory neurons were transcriptionally responsive to the type 2 immune mediators IL-13 and leukotrienes. 32 It remains unclear whether luminal metabolites activate enteric neurons directly or act indirectly through the epithelial (e.g., EEC-mediated signaling). In full-thickness mouse proximal colon preparations, SCFAs (e.g., acetate, propionate, and butyrate) or serotonin (5-HT) were applied to the mucosal surface, and Ca²⁺ activity was monitored in either the epithelium or the ENS. All three SCFAs and 5-HT triggered epithelial Ca²⁺ responses, whereas enteric neuronal Ca²⁺ responses have been reported for propionate and 5-HT in both the myenteric and the submucosal plexuses. 22 These findings demonstrate epithelial and enteric neuronal responsiveness to luminal metabolites, but they do not themselves determine whether signaling to neurons is direct or mediated through epithelial intermediates.

Nonetheless, the gut microbiota impacts the ENS via their metabolites in various ways. Studies suggest that the ENS is sensitive to shifts in the gut microbiota, which can have consequences for normal ENS physiology. 33 Bacteria produce a variety of metabolites, among the well-studied ones being SCFAs. SCFAs are microbial metabolites that are formed from the fermentation of dietary fiber in the colon. 34 SCFAs are sensed by the host through acting on G-protein coupled receptors of EECs. 7 It should be noted that there are metabolite-specific effects of SCFAs, and not all SCFAs prompt the same response from the ENS. In one study, the magnitude of enteric activation differed among acetate, butyrate, and propionate despite similar epithelial Ca²⁺ response amplitudes, with propionate producing the most robust myenteric neuronal Ca²⁺ responses. 22 Thus, this study illustrates that host responses to SCFA are variable.

SCFAs have a variety of functions within the ENS. SCFAs are reported to promote neurogenesis by increasing the levels of the receptor for the serotonin precursor 5-HT4, which is known to be involved in neurogenesis. 35 SCFAs can indirectly modulate the ENS through direct interactions with cells of the immune system. Hamilton et al. 36 studied how spinal cord injury (SCI) can alter the microbial profile and affect post-SCI recovery in enteric neurons. There was a decrease in SCFA-producing bacteria in the human and mouse models following SCI. Mice that were provided fiber supplementation via inulin during the early recovery phase at 14 d post injury showed slight recovery of intestinal motility and enteric atrophy, compared to mice without inulin treatment. Dietary supplementation with inulin and the downstream production of SCFAs increased IL-10 levels, and through an IL-10 mediated pathway prevents atrophy of the ENS following SCI. 37 This study shows that enteric function following injury can be improved through dietary fiber supplementation and subsequent microbiome production of SCFAs. SCFAs might have value in ENS repair, preventing reactive gliosis, increasing muscularis macrophage (MM) activity as related to motility, and promoting neurogenesis within the ENS.

Other metabolites can shape ENS signaling in intestinal physiology. In MM, beta-hydroxybutyrate may prevent pyroptosis and thereby help to preserve gut motility. 38 Legan et al. 39 tested whether administering a tryptophan (Trp)-synthesizing bacterium, Bacillus subtilis R0179, could modulate intestinal motility via serotonergic pathways. After one week of gavage in healthy mice, live R0179 increased colonic tissue Trp and the serotonin metabolite 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), and it accelerated colonic motility, while the whole-gut transit time was unchanged. These prokinetic effects were not reproduced by heat-inactivated R0179 or by a tryptophan synthase-null B. subtilis strain (1A2), which depends on 5-HT4 receptor signaling. 40 Another study looking at tryptophan from a different angle showed that a tryptophan-enriched diet in mice promotes the production of microbiome-derived tryptophan metabolites (e.g. indole-3-ethanol) that can activate dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) in intestinal epithelial cells. DRD2 signaling reduced actin pedestal formation required for attaching and effacing (AE) pathogens, conferring protection against Citrobacter rodentium. 41 Together, these studies illustrate that microbiome-derived Trp metabolites can interface with canonical neurotransmitter‒receptor pathways in the gut, directly influencing motility via serotonergic signaling and shaping epithelial host defense via dopaminergic receptor signaling. Thus, microbial metabolites have the potential to impact intestinal physiology through multiple gut regulatory layers in conjunction with the ENS. These studies illustrate that gut microbiota produced metabolites can facilitate a variety of functions within the ENS, sometimes supporting normal function and at other times promoting dysfunction.

While the gut microbiota can produce metabolites according to their inherent metabolic needs and capabilities, they can also produce neurotransmitters that speak directly in the language of enteric neurons and intestinal cells. The gut microbiota can directly synthesize neurotransmitters, e.g. serotonin, and modulate the function of intestinal cells. 42 Similarly, the gut microbiota can secrete neurotransmitter-like molecules and even produce catecholamines that modulate ENS function. 43 Just as the gut microbiota can secrete neurotransmitters, they are also responsive to them, as in the case of stress-induced catecholamines. The release of norepinephrine in patients following surgery led to an increased abundance of P. aeruginosa and consequent gut sepsis. 43

While specific metabolites changes have not been defined in these studies, they highlight that mere gut microbiota composition can also influence the ENS. 20 For instance, changes in the gut microbiome composition can influence glial populations and alter enteric glial abundance. When enteric glia detect microbes via TLRs, activated TLR signaling leads to activation of the S100B/RAGE pathway, which drives the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inflammation.

The microbiome is known to be integral to the development of the gastrointestinal tract, as the postnatal establishment of the gut microbiota is associated with the final migration of glia into mucosal sites such as the lamina propria and villi in mice. 20 Studies have explored how the developing microbiome can affect the adult ENS. Germ-free mice showed a reduction in glia: neuron ratio, highlighting the significance of the gut microbiota in development. 36 The effects of ENS development in mice treated with antibiotics were investigated in HuCD+ (pan neuronal marker) and choline acetyltransferase (ChAT+) neurons and S100B+EGCs in the myenteric plexus of the colon. On embryonic day 18.5 in antibiotic-treated mice, there was a decrease in these proliferation markers. As pups, offspring treated with antibiotics experienced higher levels of inflammatory markers. Antibiotics not only decreased beneficial bacteria, such as Lactobacillus intestinalis or L. reuteri in these mice, but also exhibited an immature ENS and a vulnerable intestinal barrier in the colon. 44 Taken together, these studies show that microbial signals can influence ENS function.

The ENS participates in a bidirectional interaction with the gut microbiota and is not merely responsive to microbes but is a key determinant of the luminal environment. Blot et al. 27 found that EECs were dispensable for survival in adult male mice under a standard chow diet. 21 EEC-deficiency led to decreased lipid absorption and a shifted microbiota community, most notably with a decrease in SCFA-producing bacteria, such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, thus reshaping the gut microbiota. 21 Griffiths et al. 2024 studied the influence of gut-associated ChAT+ (cholinergic) or TH+ (catecholaminergic) neurons on the intestinal microbial community structure. They used an ENS tropic AAV and Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs (DREADD) to selectively activate these neurons in GI tissue in vivo. The activation of ChAT+ neurons caused a reduction in alpha diversity and a shift in microbial taxa in fecal and cecal samples, while activation of TH+neurons reflected diversity measures similar to those of controls. Differential-abundance analysis further showed that ChAT+ activation was associated with Verrucomicrobia enrichment, which was driven by Akkermansia muciniphila. 39 These results indicate that selectively activating ENS-associated cholinergic neurons is sufficient to remodel the intestinal microbiome in vivo. ENS neurons may also interact with microbes through their antimicrobial properties, which can support the residence of some microbes, as it eliminates others. Enteric neurons secrete IL-8, which can have an impact the production of antimicrobial peptides in goblet cells. 7

In summary, the ENS is a branch of the peripheral nervous system that facilitates intestinal functions and bidirectionally communicates with the gut microbiota for various functions, including development, homeostasis, and immune responses (Figure 1).

ALS microbiome and ENS

An emerging view is that the gut microbiota is one such environmental factor that could play a novel role in ALS. 45-48 In this section, we summarize human microbiome studies in ALS and evidence from animal models that have begun to address causality, including reported ENS pathology and candidate mechanisms linking dysbiosis to enteric dysfunction.

Altered microbiome in human ALS cohorts

Microbiome changes can be observed starting at the phylum level in ALS patients. 45 , 49-51 Early studies have found a reduction in Firmicutes and the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio in ALS, 45 , 49-51 although the F/B ratio is not a consistent readout and has been replaced by updated functional studies in microbiome research. Niccolai et al. profiled the stool microbiota by 16S rRNA sequencing in a cohort study of 19 ALS patients spanning multiple clinical phenotypes (5% bulbar, 47% classic, 21% flail arm/leg, 26% UMNp) and 9 age-matched healthy family caregivers. Medium progressors displayed a higher F/B ratio than controls and other ALS progression subgroups, but without statistical significance or a significant relationship to progression rate or survival. 51 Similarly, Zeng et al. reported an increase in Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Firmicutes in a cohort of ALS patients and aged-matched caregiver controls. 50

Studies comparing ALS patients with cohabitating spouses are common throughout the ALS literature, helping to reduce confounding given their shared household environment. In one representative study, the gut microbiome profiles of sporadic ALS patients and their cohabiting spouses were compared, along with a set of healthy cohabiting couples. Notably, no ALS participants were taking Riluzole at sampling. 16s rRNA sequencing of rectal swabs showed that ALS patients had higher alpha diversity than spouse controls and overall differences in community composition. While ALS patient microbiome composition was more diverse in terms of the number of taxa, they experienced reductions in certain species, such as Prevotella spp., whose predominance the authors note is associated with higher fiber intake. There was a reduced representation of enzymes in several metabolic pathways, including butanoate metabolism, which is key for butyrate production. Interestingly, there was no observed decrease in butyrate-producing taxa. Finally, ALS patients did not have different levels of inflammatory biomarkers (e.g. IFN-γ, IL-1β, and LPS) in their stool or serum from healthy controls. 52 Studies such as this using spouse controls provide evidence that ALS-associated differences may exist despite shared environmental factors, supporting microbial shifts that may be associated with ALS.

Variations in the microbiota have also been observed among patients with different ALS subtypes. Kim et al. stratified patients by spinal onset ALS (sALS) and bulbar-onset ALS (bALS) 29 and profiled their fecal and oral microbiomes to determine whether dysbiosis patterns vary by clinical phenotype. They found that sALS exhibited gut dysbiosis primarily associated with an increased fecal F/B ratio, while bALS exhibited oral dysbiosis was associated with a decreased oral F/B ratio. They also found that a higher fecal F/B ratio was strongly associated with greater disease severity, as noted by lower ALSFRS-R scores in those with sALS, while this fecal F/B severity was not significant in bALS. 53 Rather severity in bALS was tracked with the oral F/B ratio. These studies suggest that in addition to differences that exist between ALS patients and controls, the sampling site and phenotype might be key determinants for ALS microbiomes. A different study investigated whether the microbiota and SCFA levels differ across ALS subtypes. Stool samples were collected from 16 recently diagnosed ALS patients (5 bulbar, 11 spinal) and 12 spouse/relative controls. Compared to controls, ALS patients exhibited increased Enterobacter, Clostridium, Veillonella, Dialister, Turicibacter, and Acidaminococcus with decreased Prevotella, Lactobacillus, and Butyricimonas. At the subtype level, spinal-onset ALS was associated with higher Fusobacteria and Tenericutes compared with bulbar-onset ALS. Fecal SCFA concentrations were not significantly different by diagnosis or subtype, although spinal ALS showed a trend toward lower propionate. 54 This study highlights that there is heterogeneity in the microbiome early in ALS among ALS subtypes and that factors such as dysphagia can influence the microbiome. Thus, eating habits that are present in early disease and lifestyle adjustments that are present later in disease are important factors to consider when observing an altered gut microbiome in ALS patients.

Beyond mere community compositional shifts, dysbiosis in ALS may also alter host metabolic characteristics and potentially influence disease. One study reported a reduced abundance of multiple butyrate-producing species in ALS, while another observed ALS-associated community differences, including lower Prevotella and predicted shifts in pathways related to butyrate metabolism, 52 , 55 although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely defined. A study using shotgun metagenomics and fecal metabolomics on a cohort of 20 ALS patients and 20 healthy family caregivers reported differences in predicted functions using KEGG-based functional annotation. The ALS samples showed reduced representation of pathway categories for the metabolism of other amino acids, folding/sorting/degradation, nucleotide metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism. 50 While this study does not establish causality, these findings support an association between ALS and altered microbial functions. Niccolai et al. examined links between microbial taxa and host/microbial readouts by collecting paired serum and fecal samples from 19 ALS and 9 caregiver controls. After 16S rRNA stool profiling, they quantified SCFAs and measured several inflammatory cytokines in both the serum and the stool. The absolute fecal SCFA concentrations did not significantly differ between the ALS and controls, but there were differences in taxa-SCFA correlations. Controls showed many genera that were negatively correlated with propionic acid, whereas the ALS samples included genera positively correlated with acetic acid. Immunologically, several serum cytokines were significantly lower in ALS than controls, including IL-8, IL-15, MCP-1, and VEGF-A. In the stool, IL-2 was significantly higher in the ALS patients, while IL-1β was higher but not significantly. Finally, IL-21 was reduced in patients with a fast-progressing phenotype compared to slow or medium progressors. 51

Dysbiosis has implications for not only altering the gut microbiota composition but also impacting microbial metabolites detected at sites beyond the gut. Guo et al. profiled the fecal microbiome of ALS patients and integrated baseline stool data with plasma metabolomics data in a longitudinal case‒control study. Functional analysis of the microbiome revealed enrichment of pathways related to lipid metabolism (e.g., linoleic acid metabolism) and amino acid metabolism (e.g., tryptophan metabolism), while there was depletion of pathways such as pyrimidine metabolism. Acylcarnitines and other fatty-acids were associated with worse ALSFRS-R scores. 56 This study revealed that alterations in the gut microbiome can affect host metabolism outside of the gut and potentially play a role in disease progression. In another study that included serum sampling of ALS patients, Niccolai et al. measured multi-length chain free fatty acids (FFA) and found distinct signatures that could distinguish them from healthy controls. In particular, ALS patients presented with higher overall FFA levels, lower SCFAs, and differences in nearly all medium- and long-chain fatty acids, with the exception of t2-ethylhexanoic acid, octanoic acid, and octadecanoic acid. 57 Whether collected from the stool or the serum, these studies demonstrate that microbial metabolite signatures can distinguish ALS patients from healthy controls and suggest the potential clinical utility of assays that detect microbial metabolites in this population.

Several reviews have synthesized taxa-level microbiome differences reported in human ALS cohorts in greater depth, and we therefore will not recapitulate those broad taxonomic summaries here. 6 , 47 , 58-60 While we have reviewed relatively recent studies demonstrating differences in microbiota composition in ALS patient cohorts (Table 1), there are limitations that need to be addressed when discussing microbiome studies in ALS. It is first important to note that not all studies have identified microbiome differences in ALS patients compared with healthy controls. A study found that the fecal microbiome of patients with motor neuron disease, including those clinically diagnosed with ALS, was not significantly different from age, sex, and BMI matched controls 61 Across human studies, ALS was associated with an altered community structure rather than any single microbiome signature. Human studies of the ALS gut microbiome are heterogeneous and are limited by being observational cross-sectional studies. There are challenges in recruiting patients for ALS clinical research to achieve a large sample size. Without the ability to collect intestinal tissue samples, there are also limits in understanding how the ENS is affected by dysbiosis in patients. In summary, we have presented studies from 2020 (Table 1) demonstrating differences in microbiota composition across human AD cohorts. Owing to limitations related to heterogeneity, causality, and mixed findings, continued studies are needed to resolve how the microbiome is affected in ALS and how dysbiosis may influence disease outcomes.

Altered microbiome and ENS in ALS animal models

Animal models provide an opportunity to study the mechanisms and early changes in the intestinal microbiome before ALS symptoms are observed. The gut microbiome has been shown to be altered across multiple mouse models of ALS, e.g., SOD1G93A mice, 46 , 48 , 62 , 63 C9orf72 mice, 64 and the TDP43 A315T model. 65 These findings motivate investigations into whether gut microbial changes instigate or amplify GI dysfunction/ENS pathology in ALS models. Below, we summarize evidence for dysbiosis in ALS models and then discuss GI phenotypes and ENS pathology in the context of disease.

Dysbiosis is an early feature of ALS mouse models. In our previous studies, we demonstrated that there may be a relationship between dysbiosis and ALS disease progression through longitudinal sampling of SOD1G93A mice at 1, 2, and 3 month timepoints. 46 , 48 We observed species-level changes in SOD1 mice that accompanied ALS progression. For instance, Enterorhabdus murus and Bacteroides acidifaciens were more abundant in 2 and 3 month SOD1 mice compared to at one month. 62 We also detected dysbiosis in SOD1 mice based on a reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria, which began occurring as early as 2 months. 46 , 48 These results demonstrate that there are distinct microbial community compositions across disease progression and highlight the combination of genetic risk with perturbations in the gut microbiota. Another group investigated the gut microbiota composition across disease progression in SOD1 mice on two differing strains, 129Sv_G93A and C57Ola_G93A, found no differences in the gut microbiota composition with disease progression. Niccolai et al. 2024 found that both SOD1 strains differed from their respective littermate controls at 12 weeks, with the C57Ola_G93A strain showing decreases in taxa such as Erysipelotrichaceae and Faecalibaclum, while the 129Sv_G93A showed subtle differences in taxa such as Lachnospiraceae. When the gut microbiota composition was assessed later in disease at 14/16 weeks for 129Sv_G93A mice or 16/22 weeks for C57Ola_G93A mice, the gut microbiota composition was stable over time and did not suggest that the microbiota changes that accompany disease progression. 66 These results demonstrate that while specific microbiota changes are strain dependent, there is consistency in SOD1 mice showing divergent gut microbiota profiles from controls and further provide evidence that the gut microbiota is impacted in ALS.

The role of antibiotic exposure to gut dysbiosis in ALS has also been studied. While finding different microbiota compositions in SOD1 mice and controls, Blancher et al. 2019 also found differences between antibiotic-treated SOD1 mice and antibiotic controls, including enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae and Sutterella. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing of SOD1 mice suggested varying functional pathways that were associated with dysbiosis and included reductions in genes encoding enzymes for tryptophan, NAM, and nicotinamide metabolism. SOD1 mice also showed poor performance on Rotarod and grip strength tests, suggesting that dysbiosis is not merely present but can exacerbate the motor symptoms of ALS. 63 It should be noted that a different study using the C9orf72 ALS mouse model found that exposure to antibiotics was associated with a reduction in inflammatory phenotypes (e.g., elevated cytokine levels and splenomegaly). In this case, it appears that antibiotics are not a driver of dysbiosis and suggests that in some cases, microbial manipulation is able to ameliorate the hallmark inflammation that accompanies ALS. 64 These studies taken together illustrate the presence of dysbiosis in the context of ALS and convey that dysbiosis does not appear to exist silently but can potentially impact functional pathways on the microscopic scale and motor symptoms on the macroscopic scale.

In addition to alterations in microbial composition and function, ALS model mice also display alterations in intestinal barrier structure. We found early alterations in the intestinal epithelium of SOD1 mice, which showed reduced tight junction (TJ) protein ZO-1 and adherin junction protein E-cadherin at 2 months, well before ALS symptoms are expected to manifest. TJ proteins are known to regulate barrier functions. These changes were not just structural, as these mice experienced increased evidence of leaky gut via a permeability assay. 48 In TDP43 mice, there is increased intestinal permeability compared to WT mice. In TDP43 mice, we observed significant downregulation of ZO-1 and Claudin-5 at the protein level in the colon of TDP43 mice, thus leading to higher intestinal permeability. 65 These results suggest that GI barrier integrity could be an early change in ALS pathology.

Intestinal motility has been shown to be altered in ALS mice. Whether this is ENS intrinsic or secondary to ALS pathology is unclear. As early as two months, there was SOD1 protein observed in the small intestine and lumbar spinal cord of the mice. This increase in SOD1 was not isolated however, there was also an increase in glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in the small intestine and lumbar spinal cord of SOD1G93A mice at the same time point. This was also associated with a reduction in smooth muscle and enteric glia markers in the intestine and spinal cord of 2 month SOD1 mice, which functionally showed slowed intestinal motility. 62 We also found the enhanced GFAP expression in the colon of TDP43 mice. 65 GFAP is expressed in the glia cells. Increased GFAP in the ENS, spinal cord, brain, and serum of ALS patients, served as a biomarker of ALS. 67 , 68 Being that motility is a major ENS function, and these results suggest that ALS pathology may be associated with ENS dysfunction in ALS. ALS pathology may be a factor in ENS dysfunction, as displayed through markers for reactive enteric glia and consequent reductions in intestinal motility.

Few studies have directly measured ENS endpoints after microbiome manipulation, but the altered microbiome may still have implications for the ENS. In an SOD1G93A model, there was an accumulation of SOD1G93A in the myenteric ganglia, which was associated with an increase in caspase 3 and apoptosis and cell death in these neurons. By 90 and 120 d, the mice showed neuronal loss and glial cell expansion in the myenteric plexus, along with an increase in pro-inflammatory immune markers, such as TNF-alpha and IL-1 beta, within the myenteric plexus and mucosa. Interestingly, this study only showed TJ protein Claudin 1 was affected in the colon of G93Amice. 69

In summary, dysbiosis has been reported in ALS mouse models, although patterns do not overlap neatly with human data. Most human ALS is classified as sporadic (~90%), whereas much of the mechanistic mouse literature relies on transgenic models that express ALS-linked mutations and reflect familial ALS etiology. Among these, the SOD1G93A line is one of the most widely used ALS models. However, SOD1 mutations account for only a subset of familial cases and a minority of ALS overall, failing to reflect the etiology of sporadic ALS. Other commonly used models are built around other ALS genes, such as TARDBP/TDP-43, FUS, and C9orf72. Each model captures different aspects of ALS pathology: SOD1 lines show prominent motor dysfunction, TARDBP/TDP-43 lines emphasize protein inclusions, FUS lines highlight disruptions in RNA metabolism, and C9orf72 models show immune and inflammatory phenotypes. However, ALS-linked mutations can be heterogeneous across patients, and findings observed in models may not generalize to patients. 70

Taken together, ALS has been characterized by gut dysbiosis in both human patients and mouse models. Several factors should be considered when comparing microbiomes in patients with ALS and healthy controls. Firstly, only 10%–20% of the microbiome is shared between individuals at the genus or species level. However, these taxonomic differences are often alleviated since there are overlapping functions across different species. While two individuals may appear to have different predominant species, it is worth investigating the metabolic and functional profiles of these bacteria to determine whether they share commonalities (i.e., functional redundancy). 71 Of course, there are variations across studies, so alterations in the gut microbiome so there should also be consideration of patient demographics, patient diets/lifestyles, co-morbid conditions, and drug use. 42 , 71 Another set of considerations that are more relevant to ALS than other neurodegenerative diseases is that its heterogeneous presentation (e.g. spinal ALS vs bulbar ALS) and variations in disease course time may allow for even more variations in the gut microbiome. 72

Potential mechanisms in the ENS and propagation to CNS in ALS

Given the presence of gut dysbiosis and GI symptoms in ALS, we now turn to how the gut may be involved in disease. Local intestinal changes may affect ALS outcomes through a variety of routes. First, changes in the microbiota and metabolites can remodel enteric neurons and glia. Second, barrier dysfunction can connect luminal events to immune activation and systemic exposure to microbial products. Third, neuro-immune pathways in the gut can relay microbial signals from the gut lumen. These local changes, including altered microbiome and ENS, can then impact the CNS through neural, metabolite-related, immune, and barrier mechanisms. Importantly, these intestinal mechanisms may contribute to the some of the pathological processes in ALS, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, excitotoxicity, BBB dysfunction, and altered microglial/immune activity. While direct mechanistic evidence in ALS remains limited, these routes offer plausible explanations for how GI dysfunction and dysbiosis could be connected to ENS and CNS dysfunction.

ENS remodeling by infection/dysbiosis

Microbiota disturbances can disrupt the normal physiology of the ENS by altering the environment required for enteric neuron function. Enteric infection is one such way that the gut microbiome can be disturbed. In mice, infection with Salmonella typhimurium led to a loss of neurons in the myenteric plexus of the ileum and colon and was associated with long-lasting reductions in motility. 73 A similar loss of enteric neurons was also seen following infection with Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, suggesting that enteric neuron loss is a shared outcome of infection with various enteric pathogens. There were also persistent changes in enteric neurons, as indicators of enteric neurogenesis were not detected in myenteric neurons 4 months after infection was cleared. 73 These results suggest that enteric infection can both injure enteric neurons and delay their recovery. Although not tested in ALS, the observation that enteric infections can result in ENS remodeling provides a plausible explanation for how microbiota disturbances might contribute to the GI dysfunction that precedes neurodegenerative diseases.

Beyond infection, gut dysbiosis can also be sufficient to drive enteric neuronal loss. Antibiotic administration in mice was associated with a reduction in enteric neurons in the ileum and colon, with specific loss of neuronal subsets, including CART+ neurons. This effect was mediated by the NLRP6 and caspase-11 pathways, linking gut dysbiosis to cell death. Neuron numbers were able to recover following antibiotic withdrawal, suggesting that antibiotic-induced dysbiosis can be reversed when microbiota are allowed to recolonize. 74 This study supports gut dysbiosis as a mechanism that increase susceptibility of ENS effectors to cell death. These results motivate testing of whether dysbiosis in ALS may similarly engage inflammasome pathways in the ENS and contribute to early GI dysfunction and systemic inflammation that could affect disease progression in the CNS.

As illustrated by Vicentini et al., dysbiosis can lead to downstream effects on the intestinal structure and injury to underlying ENS neurons. Antibiotic-induced dysbiosis in mice resulted in structural intestinal changes, such as longer intestines and larger ceca, alongside enteric changes, including the loss of HuC/D+ neurons in the colonic myenteric plexus and slowed motility in the small intestine. Interestingly, these morphological differences were reversed when the microbiota could re-colonize the gut. 75 The gut dysbiosis that occurs in ALS may then be a plausible factor for triggering enteric neuron loss and lead to early GI dysfunction. Finally, dysbiosis may also influence ALS risk through the involvement of extra-intestinal organs in metabolizing bacterial products. A large UK Biobank prospective cohort found that pre-existing liver disease and MRI-derived markers of liver injury/steatosis were associated with a higher risk of developing ALS. 72 This suggests that gut-derived metabolites that are processed by the liver and distributed to the rest of the body could contribute to the distant pathology of ALS seen in the CNS.

Taken together, these studies show that changes in the gut microbiota as a result of infection or dysbiosis can lead to remodeling of the ENS. This remodeling is associated with many consequences, including enteric neuron loss, delays in neurogenesis, reductions in motility, systemic inflammation, and physiological changes in organs along and outside the GI tract. In ALS, dysbiosis and gastrointestinal symptoms are commonly observed, but their link remains unresolved. It is still unclear whether dysbiosis precedes ALS pathogenesis or if ALS pathology contributes to dysbiosis. The infection and dysbiosis studies offer a hypothesis for how microbiota disturbances may contribute to early GI dysfunction through loss of ENS neuron function, and future ALS studies could assess how microbiota shifts affect these ENS outcomes.

ENS and the intestinal epithelial barrier

The intestinal epithelial barrier is the contact point for luminal contents, whose information makes its way to enteric neurons, which can signal to the rest of the body. The intestinal epithelial barrier is composed of an epithelial monolayer of cells, mucus layer, and intercellular junction proteins, which are reviewed in detail by Odenwald and Turner. 76 When barrier integrity is compromised, luminal products can access shielded neural and immune cells and expose the gut to inflammation that can then become systemic and affect the CNS.

A major means of epithelial barrier regulation in the gut comes from the activity of EGCs. 77 Projections from enteric glia contribute to the sheath surrounding the intestinal epithelial barrier, mirroring the role of astrocytes in regulating the BBB in the CNS. 20 , 78 EGCs support the intestinal barrier by promoting the proliferation of epithelial stem cells, with GFAP+ EGCs driving cell renewal through Wnt ligands. 79 EGCs also play a role in the injury response and are involved in the migration of epithelial cells during wound repair in vitro. 80

Under inflammatory conditions, EGCs roles can shift from barrier-promoting to pro-inflammatory in a state known as reactive gliosis and be detected by increased GFAP and S100β. 81 For instance, systemic LPS administration resulted in the detection of GFAP and S100β in multiple segments of the intestines, indicating reactive gliosis. 82 Reactive gliosis can also be a feature of neurodegenerative diseases, as seen Parkinson's disease (PD). Duodenal biopsies in patients with PD revealed α-synuclein pathology alongside cell morphology that reflected evidence of reactive enteric gliosis. 83 These studies demonstrated that EGCs can adopt a reactive phenotype in the setting of neurodegenerative diseases and that when dysfunctional EGCs pivot away from maintaining barrier integrity, they can become a source of vulnerability, potentially in diseases such as ALS. Reactive enteric gliosis can have direct consequences for barrier integrity. In one study assessing Gulf War Illness induced dysbiosis, there was an increased presence of activated EGCs, alongside a decrease in the tight junction protein occludin and an increase in Claudin-2. Interestingly, treatment with antibiotics reversed this finding as occludin levels increased and claudin-2 levels decreased. 84 This study suggests that an altered microbiome can induce a reactive phenotype in enteric glia, which can then affect the intestinal barrier and enable systemic insults.

In additional to EGCs, enteric neurons are believed to play a role in regulating the intestinal barrier. The release of neurotransmitters, such as 5-HT from enteric neurons, is associated with epithelial proliferation. 85 Another study found increased expression of ZO-1, increased transepithelial electrical resistance, and decreased dextran permeability in epithelial monolayers in vitro following in vitro co-culture with enteric neurons. 86 These studies indicate the vital role of enteric neurons and neurotransmitters in promoting epithelial proliferation and an impermeable epithelial barrier. Enteric neurons can influence the composition of the microbiota at the epithelial barrier, which is critical for preventing gut dysbiosis. A study by Lei et al. found that chronic alcohol use is directly toxic to VIPergic neurons, which regulate host-mediated fucosylation of the epithelial surface. The loss of VIPergic neurons from the ileal and colonic submucosal plexuses led to decreased host fucosylation of the epithelium, preventing the colonization of the gut epithelium by the commensal B. bifidum and overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria E. faecalis. 87 This study has demonstrated that the loss of enteric neurons can alter the microbiome at the level of the intestinal epithelium. Although the impact on the epithelial barrier due to enteric neurons loss has not been directly tested in human ALS, mouse models have shown the loss of myenteric neurons in the colon and ileum of SOD1 mice. 69 These findings indicate that the loss of enteric neurons in ALS could compromise epithelial barrier integrity, promoting dysbiosis or enabling exposure to systemic inflammation, which could have consequences for disease progression.

Enteric glia and neurons have important roles in maintaining homeostasis at the intestinal epithelial barrier and motivate investigations into how these roles are impacted during ALS. Loss of barrier integrity could be a plausible link that bridges luminal microbes to systemic immune activation that influences the CNS in ways that drive ALS progression.

Neuro-immune pathways

The axons of enteric neurons are in close proximity to immune cells, 7 enabling neurons of the ENS to be responsive to microbial perturbations that affect immune signaling. 7 Both systemic (e.g. elevated cytokines, CD4+ T cell counts) and central inflammation (e.g. microglial activation, immune cell infiltration) are present in ALS 88 and warrant an evaluation of how the ENS may be initiating or sustaining inflammation.

The gut microbiota shape the immune environments, influencing the CNS via a gut‒immune–brain axis. In a multiomics study, Wang et al. used Mendelian randomization (MR) to test whether specific gut microbial taxa are causally associated with ALS risk and reported that 16 taxa are linked to higher risk, most notably Pasteurellaceae and Bacteroides vulgatus, and 16 taxa are linked to lower risk, including p_Firmicutes.c_Clostridia and Ruminococcus lactaris. MR further suggested that the protective association of p_Firmicutes.c_Clostridia is partly mediated through higher levels of CD33+HLA-DR myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). The authors then observed reduced circulating MDSCs in ALS patients by flow cytometry alongside lower anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β1. Fecal qPCR showed depletion of Firmicutes/Clostridia in ALS patient and showed that these taxa correlated positively with MDSC abundance and TGF-β1 levels. 89 Overall, the study reflects immune dysregulation in ALS and proposes that the gut microbiota may contribute to ALS pathogenesis via a microbiome-immune mechanism.

As with the intestinal barrier, enteric neurons are highly involved in directing and responding to the immune system. Muller et al. investigated the role of muscularis macrophages (MM) and enteric neurons, finding a loss of coordinated muscle contractions and intestinal motility when in the colonic rings from MM-depleted mice. They further found that MM secrete bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) and that the close anatomical position of the MM and enteric neurons caused enteric neurons to secrete colony stimulatory factor 1 (CSF1), which is key for macrophage development. Finally, they observed that microbes can influence this MM-enteric neuron interaction, showing a decrease in Bmp2 expression following antibiotic treatment, while there was an increase in Csf1 expression following LPS treatment. 90 Another study examined the interaction between the ENS and regulatory T cells (Tregs) and found that enteric neurons inhibited Treg differentiation by secreting IL-6. This IL-6-mediated interaction was found to be altered by the microbiota, as colonization with Clostridium ramosum led to decreased IL-6 levels 1 week after colonization and a reduction in enteric neuron populations 2 weeks after colonization. 91 These studies demonstrate how the ENS interacts with the immune system to control intestinal motility and immune cell differentiation and how commensal bacteria can regulate these interactions under homeostatic conditions. Because dysbiosis and GI dysfunction are commonly reported in ALS, these studies motivate testing of how altered gut microbiota can impact immune‒ENS interactions and potentially be a mechanism for enabling systemic inflammation.

EGCs play an active role in communicating with the immune system and may function as antigen presenting cells as they have been noted to express MHC II receptors. ECGs are particularly responsive to inflammatory cytokines and initiating reactive gliosis phenotypes that can drive inflammation in the GI tract. 92 In one study, active infection with the helminth Heligmosomoides polygyrus induced reactive gliosis and the expression of IFNγ-related genes in EGCs. This IFNγ signaling in EGCs was associated with the recruitment of innate and adaptive immune cell populations, including neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes, and CD8+ T cell recruitment, as well as an enhanced type 2 response. EGCs were found to be responsive to IFNγ signaling through the expression of the receptors Ifngr1 and Ifngr2 and had a CXCL10-mediated response to initiate tissue repair following infection. 93 These results indicate that EGCs are involved in local immune responses and create an inflammatory environment in the gut but also have mechanisms to curtail the damaging effects of inflammation through tissue repair. Thus, this study inspires future studies related to how EGC immune interactions may become dysregulated in ALS, contributing to persistent gut inflammation and impaired ENS recovery that may drive the systemic inflammation characteristic of ALS.

Taken together, these studies demonstrate the role of the ENS in interacting with cells of the immune system, particularly those that are responsible for generating inflammation. Aberrations in ENS regulation over immune tolerance can result in inflammation, which is central to the development and progression of ALS.

Gut to CNS propagation

Another question that has been persistently explored in gut‒brain axis research is how intestinal microbes influence the brain, which is located a considerable distance away. Several proposed mechanisms involve translocation, indirect actions via inflammation or bacterial-derived metabolites, effects on nutrient absorption in the GI tract, manipulation of exogenous drug metabolism, or actions in conjunction with the ENS. 94 Against this context, microbiota-driven changes in the gut may propagate to the CNS and shape ALS pathology. We focus on three potential channels for gut to CNS propagation that may have particular relevance to ALS: neural signaling, bacterial metabolites, and immune mechanisms that may initiate or amplify neuroinflammation.

Neural signaling is one such communication channel between the gut microbiota and the CNS. Besides the production of neural compounds by the gut microbiota, it has been suggested that other microbial metabolites can mediate their effects through the innervated gut and vagus nerve and communicate microbiota and luminal information with the CNS. 43 , 95 The vagus nerve is the longest in the body and serves as an essential facilitator between the brain and the intestines, enabling peripheral organs to communicate with the brain. 96 Vagal afferent nerves relay information from the intestinal lumen into the brain via the nucleus tractus soltarius, 97 while efferent fibers coordinate brain-to-gut control. Both work together to drive intestinal reflexes and signal hunger and satiety. 35 Vagus afferent fibers do not cross the epithelium and thus do not directly interact with microbes and instead coordinate with enteroendocrine cells that interact with luminal contents. 98 Thus, in ALS, luminal changes such as gut dysbiosis could be sensed by EECs and relayed to the vagus nerve, where they could then be rapidly routed to the brain. Future studies are needed to test how dysbiosis in ALS can affect vagus nerve activity and influence motor neuron vulnerability.

Another neural mechanism linking the gut to the brain happens through ENS‒CNS coupling. While the ENS can coordinate many gastrointestinal functions through intrinsic local circuits, it remains tightly linked to the CNS through bidirectional signaling in the gut–brain axis. 35 The coupled work of the CNS and ENS is essential for many processes, such as the vago-vagal reflex, to coordinate the propulsion of luminal contents 8 or in defecation, where the CNS monitors movements of the stomach while the ENS focuses on movements of the small and large intestines. 17 The stress pathway, which is mediated through the production of cortisol in the hypothalamic pituitary (HPA) axis, serves as an example of CNS‒ENS connection. 99 As such, the CNS and ENS cover distinct regions to maintain homeostasis. In general, the esophagus and stomach are richly innervated with vagal efferent sources and lack a submucosal plexus, while the distal intestine and colon innervations are derived from intrinsic ENS networks. 13 , 43 Importantly, the ENS and CNS share some structural similarities that can connect their functionality, despite their different anatomical locations. The ENS possesses CNS-like characteristics with enteric glia expressing S-100B and GFAP markers found on astrocytes, 20 the EEC-derived serotonin pool allowing serotonergic signaling in enteric neurons similar to that in central ones, 15 and non-fenestrated myenteric that resembles those of the blood–brain barrier. 15 Thus, there are similarities in the glial, neurotransmitter, and neurovascular systems of the ENS and CNS that may serve to connect their functionality.

It has even been suggested in the context of neurodegenerative diseases that these two branches of the nervous system are not siloed. For instance, alpha-synuclein inclusions were found in neurons of both the myenteric and submucosal plexuses of Parkinson's disease patients. The authors hypothesized that misfolded protein could then move along connected, long-axon projection pathways from the ENS into the brainstem, including vagal nuclei such as the dorsal motor nucleus. 100 Thus, the connections between the ENS and CNS offer a route for gut changes to transmit to the brain, where they may impact pathology in neurodegenerative disease.

Microbial metabolites provide a humoral route by which the gut environment can influence the CNS. These metabolites can cross from the gut epithelium into systemic circulation and communicate with the brain, influencing ALS progression. A recent study examined the production capabilities of several commensal gut microbiota of neuroactive metabolites in vitro. Several strains, including L. lactis and E. coli Nissle generated GABA when grown in media containing glutamine and glutamate. Commensals were found to have varying production capacities for the tyrosine pathway, with L. lactis, B. producta, and E. coli Nissle producing L-dopa, while E. faecalis generated tyramine and dopamine. In contrast, all commensals consumed tryptophan from the media, but none generated serotonin. 101 This study shows that the gut microbiota can generate neuroactive compounds involved in CNS signaling and suggests that the gut microbiota can communicate with the CNS in this way. Given that microbes can produce neurotransmitters and that ALS pathology is marked by excess glutamate and insufficient gamma aminobutyric acid 73 that creates hyperexcitability cortical neurons, 102 studies that specifically examine glutamate- or GABA-producing bacteria could further link the microbiota to ALS pathology. Other bacterial metabolites can influence the CNS by mimicking host-derived molecules, as in the case of carnitine. Microbe-produced mimics 3-methyl-4-trimethylammoniobutanoate (3M-4-TMAB) and 4-trimethylammonio pentanoate (4-TMAP), which have been linked to diabetes and preeclampsia, resemble the structure of carnitine. These mimics associate with carnitine in the white matter of the CNS and inhibit its role in fatty acid oxidation, which is critical for brain function. 95 On the other hand, bacterial metabolites such as SCFAs can activate the sympathetic nervous system or regulate serotonin release from the mucosa. 103 , 104 Sphingolipids can affect inflammation, and endocannabinoid-like lipids from bacteria may also play a role in neuroinflammation. 105 These studies demonstrate the diverse effects of bacterial produced metabolites on host neurological processes, which are mediated through molecular mimicry, direct effects on multiple branches of the nervous system, and participation in neuroinflammation. They suggest testable mechanisms that may explain how dysbiosis and the presence of particular microbial metabolites in ALS could lead to downstream neuroinflammation.

Immune routes are yet another way for the gut microbiota to interact with the CNS. As early as development, the gut microbiota can alter the maturation and characteristics of cells in the CNS. A study by Huang et al. 2023 used single-cell sequencing of the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex to demonstrate how the gut microbiota can influence the development of microglial cells. They found that GF mice had fewer microglial cell populations in the hippocampus than SPF mice and fewer other glial cell populations, including astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte precursor cells. Transcriptomic analysis revealed several hundred differentially expressed genes in SPF and GF mice, with the hippocampal microglia of GF mice showing enrichments in neuroinflammation and complement-related pathways. 106 These studies indicate that the gut microbiota have the potential to shape CNS immune cell development and associated function. Another study showed that the gut microbiota may impact immune cell distribution and activity in the brain. Golomb et al. used CITE-seq to investigate the transcriptomic changes in Ly6c+ monocytes and ILCs in the context of aging and gut dysbiosis. Antibiotic-induced and age-related gut dysbiosis in mice was associated with increased microglia subsets, ILC-like cells with altered transcriptional profiles, and increased neuroinflammation. 107 These studies illustrate that the microbiota can tune the development and presence of CNS-resident inflammatory cells, as well as those that infiltrate the CNS. They relate to ALS given the increase in neuroinflammation and infiltrating inflammatory immune cells such as macrophages and T cells 88 and motivate studies that further investigate microbiota affects immune-affiliated cells in ALS.

The microbiota may drive neuroinflammation through their influence on support cells of the CNS, as Cox et al. 108 investigated the role of the gut microbiota on microglial populations in SOD1 mice. Low-dose antibiotics were administered to a SOD1 mice to suppress the microbiota, while another group of SOD1 mice were co-housed with WT mice to augment the microbiota. Antibiotic-treated mice exhibited body weight loss, accelerated disease progression, and decreased survival time. In contrast, co-housed SOD1 mice did not appear to have any protective effects. Microbiome analysis revealed no effect of cohousing while there was depletion of several taxa in antibiotic treated SOD1 mice, including Akkermansia and select butyrate producers such as Ruminococcus. Transcriptional analysis of microglia in the spinal cord showed that antibiotic exposure was associated with a downregulation of five genes regulating microglia activation and decreased pathways in TGF-B signaling. There was an upregulation of genes related to microglial neurodegeneration and pathways related to neuroinflammation. This study showing the damaging effects of antibiotics in SOD1 mice contrasts literature in C9orf72 mice, where antibiotics appear to delay ALS progression. 64 This reflects some of the heterogeneity seen in human microbiome studies and suggests that the interaction of genetics and the microbiota drive specific responses that cannot be easily generalized. Taken together, these studies reflect that intestinal alterations in the context of ALS can affect cells of the CNS and may explain how distal gut microbes could affect CNS cells involved in ALS progression.

While there are investigations into how gut microbial communities influence the CNS, there are fewer investigations on how the CNS influences the microbiota residing in the gut. One example of a top-down brain-to-gut effect can be seen in depression, where chronic activation of neurons in the lateral habenula (LHb) induces changes in the gut microbiota. Wang et al. 2025 chronically activated excitable glutaminergic LHb neurons in mice and observed differences in beta diversity and greater variation in taxa composition over time. 89 While the mechanism has yet to be fully elucidated, these results suggest that the brain can influence the composition of the gut under certain conditions. The CNS may also communicate with bacteria through neurotransmitter receptors in the intestine. For instance, norepinephrine was shown to increase the virulence of Campylobacter jejuni by facilitating its iron uptake and disruption of tight junction proteins in Caco-2 cells. 109 These studies show that the brain can signal back to the gut and influence microbes in the gut, indicating that a vulnerable CNS in the setting of ALS could further perturb the intestinal environment and initiate a cycle of inflammation.

Evidence from other neurodegenerative diseases supports shared routes

Because mechanistic evidence in ALS remains limited, we draw on other related neurodegenerative disorders to consider frameworks for how the ENS and microbiome may interact. While these comparisons cannot be assumed to be generalizable across different diseases, they help to provide a scope of plausible ENS-microbiome mechanisms that may also occur in ALS.

It is possible that similar mechanisms of ENS to CNS transmission occur in ALS. In a case series using antemortem non-CNS tissue samples from individuals who later developed ALS, pTDP-43 aggregates were detected at several sites, including in GI tissue, lymph nodes, and skin. Notably, a gallbladder specimen positive for pTDP43 was collected 2 y before the ALS diagnosis, suggesting that some peripheral GI/enteric pathology can precede the clinical recognition of ALS. 110 Because these ENS‒CNS connections exist, a later section summarizes the proposed routes by which gut microbes may influence ENS and CNS physiology and can be contextualized when considering ALS.

Several studies have identified gastrointestinal changes that occur in PD years before diagnosis and have even characterized how the ENS is affected during the active disease state. 111 In biopsy samples isolated from patients with PD, the expression of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-6 and TNF-α) was associated with reactive enteric glia in the colon. 112 In trying to define the mechanism of ENS to CNS transmission, others have purported that EECs and glia may have an association that facilitates the movement of proteins from within EEC to the ENS and CNS. In the case of PD, it has been proposed that prions and alpha synuclein found in EECs may be able to translocate through the ENS or vagus nerve into the brain. 35 , 113

AD has also been brought into the field's conversation about the gut‒brain axis. One study characterized the gut microbiome of AD patients and observed an altered composition with overall less diversity, increased proinflammatory bacteria (e.g. Shigella, Escherichia), and reduced anti-inflammatory bacteria (i.e. Eubacterium rectale). They propose that alterations in gut permeability or the BBB enable peripheral inflammatory factors and bacteria to enter the brain. 71 As pertains to the ENS, it has been proposed that amyloid beta plaques themselves can cause harm and can affect neurotransmitter receptors, such as the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR). 114 Within the amyloid precursor protein (APP) model of AD, APP mice can experience amyloid beta accumulation in enteric neurons, which affects motility and responses to inflammation. Mechanistically, misfolded proteins may be able to spread from the ENS, reaching the enteric plexuses through M cells or DCs in Peyer's patches. 42

Finally, similar findings of altered gut microbiota composition were seen in a mouse model of multiple sclerosis (MS). Researchers transferred fecal matter from the small intestine of MS patients into germ-free mice to identify bacteria that may initiate MS pathology. They identified Eisenbergiella tayi and Lachnoclostridium as disease-causing candidates. 115 In a germ-free background, this study demonstrated the influence of the gut microbiome in initiating disease.

Together, evidence from PD, AD, and MS supports a recurring theme of early gastrointestinal dysfunction and remodeling of ENS cells (Figure 2). Given that these gastrointestinal alterations related to the ENS can precede or accompany neurodegenerative pathology, it motivates testing of these pathways in ALS.

These changes can increase intestinal permeability and trigger both local and systemic inflammation. Changes in the gut may then reach the CNS through a variety of routes, including neural signaling, bacteria produced metabolites, and immune infiltrating cells/mediators. Applications: gut microbiome-targeted therapies in ALS

Given that the gut microbiome may be dysregulated in ALS, correcting imbalances through microbiome-targeted therapies may reduce the ALS burden. In one study where antibiotics were administered to C9orf72−/− mice, there was an improvement in rotarod performance and a reduction in inflammatory measures. Those treated with antibiotics throughout life did not experience infiltration of myeloid cells into the spinal cord. 64 While antibiotics themselves may not be a viable treatment in humans, their use in mice provides proof of concept that correcting dysbiosis in general may alleviate ALS symptoms and the associated inflammation caused by bacteria.

Community replacement: fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

One way to target dysbiosis is to replace a shifted microbial community with one from a healthy donor using FMT. Clinically, FMT is best established to correct recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection. 116 FMT is generally well-tolerated and has thus been explored as a strategy to shift community composition and microbial function in ALS.

A recent randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 27 patients suggested the current state of FMT in clinical translation for the treatment of ALS. While there was no change in the ALSFRS-R nor quality of life, motor function, or respiratory function, patients receiving FMT did report reductions in anxiety, depression, and constipation. A microbiome shift was also observed in patients with FMT treatment (e.g., increased Bifidobacterium at week 15). 117 While limited by sample size and duration, these data suggest that FMT may shift the microbiome in ALS and may have the potential to affect gastrointestinal motility and mood disorders.

Yan et al. reported two late-onset classic ALS case reports requiring tracheostomy and mechanical ventilation who underwent two rounds of FMT with paired metagenomic and untargeted metabolomic profiling. In Case 1, the patient began ventilator weaning within days, transitioned to noninvasive ventilation and resumed oral intake after the first FMT, and after the second FMT showed modest strength gains of being able to stand with assistance, along with an ALSFRS-R increase. In Case 2, ventilatory requirements decreased after the first FMT, and the patient progressed to prolonged daily breathing off the ventilator while after the second FMT he used regained limited ambulation and handwriting, tolerated oral intake without choking and had associated weight gain, and also showed improved ALSFRS-R score. In both cases, pre-FMT stool microbiota differed from those of the donor in alpha/beta diversity and composition, and post-FMT profiles shifted toward that of the donor with increased diversity. Shared taxonomic changes included enrichment of donor-associated taxa, particularly Bacteroides species (e.g., B. vulgatus) and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii after FMT. Functional profiling revealed increases in pathways related to arginine/histidine metabolism, coenzyme A biosynthesis, and glycolysis, while metabolomics showed post-FMT enrichment of metabolites mapping to arginine biosynthesis, along with a reduction in metabolites associated with branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis. 118 These results in ALS patients experiencing respiratory failure suggest that FMT may be used as a therapy late in disease, when symptoms are thought to be irreversible. However, because these are case reports with limited follow-up, the findings are best viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than causal evidence of efficacy.

Washed microbiota transplantation (WMT) is a standardized form of FMT that employs automated washing/microfiltration to reduce impurities and improve dose consistency. 119 Lu et al. reported a 48-y-old woman with ALS who experienced deterioration after antibiotics and became wheelchair dependent. After WMT, she showed improved muscle strength and regained the ability to walk with assistance. Following several rounds of FMT, the stool profiles shifted toward donor samples, with increases in taxa such as Prevotella and Ruminococcus. No adverse events have been reported. 120 As a single case, it cannot establish efficacy, but it provides an early clinical context for microbiota transplantation strategies in ALS and evidence of safety.

While trial outcomes have not been made available, the FETR-ALS study was one of the earliest randomized clinical trial protocols testing FMT in ALS and is 121 NCT03766321. Participants were planned to receive donor FMT at baseline and 6 months, with immunologic and clinical measures followed through 12 months. This trial was designed to test how microbiota replacement can influence circulating regulatory T cells (Tregs) and modulate the enteric immune environment. FMT studies in human are limited and this clinical trial raises an important question about how the gut microbiota may influence the immune system and, in turn, ALS outcomes.

The microbiota composition of 2 ALS patients was compared to that of a healthy individual who later served as a FMT donor. In Case 1, the five most abundant species pre-FMT were Enterococcus faecalis, Collinsella aerofaciens, Bacteroides stercoris, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Bacteroides uniformis, while in Case 2 Enterococcus faecium, E. faecalis, Clostridium clostridioforme, Clostridium innocuum, Klebsiella michiganensis and Flavonifractor plautii. These bacteria look markedly different from those observed in healthy donors. 118 This case report indicates that even with the heterogeneity that each individual's microbiome inherently carries, patients with ALS show a distinct profile of bacteria that differs from someone without ALS.

FMT in mice has shown promise in related conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease, 105 spinal cord injury, 122 and Parkison's disease. 123 While FMT studies in ALS mouse models remain limited, Burberry et al. found that disease severity in C9orf72 knockout mice was dependent on their microbial environment. Mice housed under conditions associated with “pro-survival” outcomes showed reduced inflammatory and autoimmune features compared with mice maintained in a more pro-inflammatory setting. When the authors depleted the gut microbiota using antibiotics or transferred microbes from the protective environment to affected mice via FMT, they could reduce systemic inflammation. 64 These studies support a link between the gut microbiota and inflammation that may influence ALS susceptibility and progression.

Community modulation: probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics

Beyond community replacement with FMT, the microbiome can also be modulated using prebiotics, probiotics, or synbiotics. These approaches aim to shift microbial community structure and function by supplying live beneficial organisms to the host (probiotics), selectively nourishing resident microbes (prebiotics), or pairing both in a single intervention (synbiotics). 124

Di Gioia et al. administered multistrain lactic acid probiotics (Streptococcus thermophilus, Lactobacillus fermentum, Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. delbrueckii, Lactobacillus plantarum, and Lactobacillus salivarius) to ALS patients for 6 continuous months or after 3 months of placebo. Overall, the intervention did not cause significant shifts in the gut microbial community or show differences in clinical measures such as the ALSFRS-R scores, BMI, or FVC%. There were no drastic differences in the gut or fecal microbiota composition between the 6-month probiotic and 3-month probiotic groups, only an increase in the Rikenellaceae family after 6 months of probiotic treatment. 125 Given the modest and variable results, this study suggests that there may be a time-dependent effect of probiotics and calls for studies that follow longer probiotic administration times.

In SOD1 mice, Xin et al. administered a commercially prepared probiotic mixture “LBE” composed of Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium longum, and Enterococcus faecalis beginning at 60 d and assessed enteric pathology alongside central disease features. In the colon, LBE treatment was associated with an increase in the tight-junction protein Claudin-1 and reduced TNF-α–positive cells in the myenteric ganglia. Whole-mount imaging of the myenteric plexus showed that probiotic treatment was associated with preservation of the enteric neural architecture, with increased pan neuronal marker PGP9.5 and reduced GFAP signals, which is consistent with lower enteric glial activation in both the colon and ileum. Notably, LBE decreased abnormal SOD1 signal and reduced caspase-3 labeling within the enteric ganglia, suggesting attenuation of enteric proteinopathy and cell-stress/apoptotic pathways. The spinal cord showed greater neuronal/motor neuron survival and reduced astrocytic and microglial activation markers in LBE-treated transgenic mice. These histologic changes were accompanied by improved motor performance, slower weight loss, delayed hindlimb disability onset, and prolonged survival, 69 supporting the idea that microbiome-directed probiotics can modulate both enteric and central neuropathology in this ALS model.

Zhang et al. administered an 8-strain probiotic VSL#3 (Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus paracasei, Lactobacillus helveticus, Bifidobacterium breve, Bifidobacterium longum, Bifidobacterium infantis, and Streptococcus thermophilus) to TDP43 mice. The microbiota community composition shifted in TDP43 mice following probiotic treatment, as evidenced by an increase in butyryl-CoA transferase, a gene necessary for butyrate production. There was also an increase in the butyrate producer Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens, a reduction in the inflammatory Bacteroides fragilis, and decreased serum LPS alongside decreased inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-17). Functionally, probiotic-treated TDP43 mice performed better on Rotarod, exhibited decreased weight loss, and had decreased gastrointestinal transit time. In the intestine, probiotic treatment decreased TDP43 and activated the glial marker GFAP while increasing the smooth muscle marker aα-SMA and the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Claudin-5. Similar findings were observed in the spinal cord, with decreased TDP43 aggregation and GFAP markers found alongside increased Claudin-5. 65 These results suggest that probiotics can modulate the microbiota in specific ways, in this instance towards butyrate production that serves to improve the intestinal barrier, reduce inflammation, and decrease TDP43 pathology in the ENS and CNS.

Song et al. assessed the use of Galactooligosaccharides 126 yogurt prebiotics in mice to determine whether they could improve the uptake of B vitamins in SOD1 mice, which may be involved in alleviating homocysteine pathways that may contribute to neurotoxicity in ALS. Starting at 56 d, SOD1 mice that received prebiotic treatment exhibited delayed disease onset and extended survival time compared to controls. At 130 d, there was increased serum folate and vitamin B12 and decreased homocysteine levels, with no difference in vitamin B6 levels. Treated mice showed preservation of motor neurons and suppression of microglia and astrocyte activation in the lumbar spinal cord. Outside the CNS, treatment decreased skeletal muscle denervation. 127 While the mechanism is framed as gut-mediated, microbiota composition and intestinal changes were not measured. However, the results still suggest that prebiotic administration can shift host metabolites with the potential to affect ALS inflammation and spinal cord pathology.

Human studies with prebiotics in ALS are limited, but dietary intervention offer a related way to examine prebiotic-like effects, since fiber-rich can supply bacteria with fermentable substrates needed for metabolite production. A recent prospective study examined whether a Mediterranean diet intervention (50% carbohydrates, 30% fats, 20% protein) alters the plasma levels of six SCFAs in ALS and how these metabolites are related to the clinical features measured by the ALSFRS-R subscores. ALS patients were followed for 12 months, with assessments at baseline (T0), after 6 months of natural progression (T1), and after a subsequent 6-month dietary intervention (T2). At T0, ALS patients showed higher circulating acetic, propionic, butyric, and 3-hydroxybutyric acids versus controls, while 4-methyl-valeric acid was lower and hexanoic acid was not significantly different. At T1, higher acetic acid and 3-hydroxybutyric acid were associated with lower ALSFRS-R fine motor/upper-limb subscores, whereas butyric acid correlated positively. Acetic acid was also positively correlated with higher depression scores. Following the Mediterranean diet phase, plasma acetic and propionic acid decreased significantly, 4-methyl-valeric acid decreases significantly, while butyric acid decreases without statistical significance, and 3-hydroxybutyric acid and hexanoic acid show no clear diet-linked changes. 128 These findings suggest that Mediterranean-style dietary modulation can shift circulating SCFA profiles in ALS with the fiber-rich background of this diet, potentially supporting SCFA production by bacteria, although it cannot be established whether these metabolite changes translate into altered ALS progression.

Community outputs: bacterial metabolites

SCFAs

Because SCFAs can provide local beneficial effects in the intestines and communicate along the microbiota‒gut‒brain axis, 34 they have become a major focus in ALS host‒microbiome studies.

Fontdevila et al. profiled the fecal microbiota and SCFAs in a small cohort of recent-onset ALS patients and matched controls. Alpha diversity did not differ, but there were subtle shifts: Enterobacter, Clostridium, and Veillonella species members increased while there were decreases in Prevotella, Lactobacillus, and Butyricimonas. When stratified by ALS subtype, additional differences emerged, for instance higher Fusobacteria/Tenericutes in spinal ALS. Despite these shifts, SCFA concentrations were not significantly different overall, but there were correlations observed in how SCFAs are related to community composition, For instance a positive correlation between Verrucomicrobia and acetic and propionic acid levels in patients with spinal ALS. This study provides an example of how ALS microbiome findings can look inconsistent across cohorts owing to small sample sizes and subtype variation but does provide proof of concept of SCFA causing microbiota shifts at the species level and that their effects on different ALS subtypes cannot be clustered together.

Ogbu et al. used longitudinal fecal metabolomics over a 13-week period to assess how metabolites shift in SOD1 mice with ALS progression and butyrate treatment. Across time, there were differences spanning pathways in carbohydrates (e.g. glucose metabolism), amino acids/proteins (e.g. tryptophan metabolism), lipids (e.g., sphingomyelins), and xenobiotics metabolism (e.g. O-sulfo-L-tyrosine). Around disease onset in SOD1 mice, there were alterations in the neuroactive metabolites histamine and tryptophan, such as 1-ribosyl-imidazoleacetate and 3-indoxyl sulfate, respectively. When the metabolome was compared in butyrate-treated SOD1 mice, there were reductions in select histidine- and tryptophan-related metabolites, such as 1-methylhistamine and indole-3-carboxylate, respectively. 129 These results indicate that ALS pathology is reflected in the fecal metabolome and that supplementation with a bacterial-derived metabolite such as butyrate can reshape some of the metabolic patterns in ALS.

SCFAs can also be harnessed to alter ALS pathology in mice. In one study where SOD1 mice were found to have a decreased abundance of butyrate producing bacteria, treatment with the SCFA butyrate appeared to influence the composition of the gut microbiota for the better. Following treatment, the mice were observed to have increased levels of butyrate producing bacteria such as Butyrivibrio at the genus level and Butyrivibrio fibrosolvens and Eubacterium at the species level, as well as evidence of the CoA enzyme, an intermediate of anaerobic bacteria that produce butyrate. Importantly, treatment delayed reduced weight loss and prolonged survival, appearing to delay signs of ALS progression. 46 A later study showed treated 2 month old SOD1 experiencing early dysbiosis with sodium butyrate. Treatment was associated with decreased aggregation of SOD1 in the small intestine and lumbar spinal cord, improved motor function, and decreased GFAP markers in the intestinal and spinal cord locations, compared to untreated SOD1 mice. 62

Another potential therapeutic approach is combining traditional approaches with microbiome-targeted approaches. The potential synergistic effects of the FDA-approved ALS medication Riluzole and bacterial-produced SCFA sodium butyrate were examined in SOD1G93A mice. Mice with dual treatment exhibited increased motor performance, as demonstrated by Rotarod time, increased grip strength, and the most drastic reduction in SOD1 aggregation in the intestines compared to those treated with only Riluzole or only sodium butyrate. 130

Non SCFA metabolites

Finally, microbiome-based therapies for ALS can also be framed in terms of community outputs, with a focus on microbial metabolites and bioactive products from specific taxa. These products may modulate ENS activity and gastrointestinal function and, in turn, influence downstream immune and neuroinflammatory pathways relevant to disease progression in ALS. One study found an association between a particular bacterial strain and ALS severity using 16 s rRNA and shotgun metagenomics analyses. After observing that Akkermansia muciniphilia was reduced in SOD1 mice, especially with disease progression, researchers treated SOD1 mice with this bacteria. Metagenomic profiling revealed increased levels of the metabolite NAM in the spinal cords of A. muciniphilia mice, suggesting that the gut microbiome can travel to motor neurons in the CNS. These mice exhibited reduced signs on brain atrophy on T2 weighted MRI, reduced loss of cell numbers in the spinal, and prolonged survival times compared to untreated SOD1 mice. 63 This study opens the door for the investigation of other metabolites produced by the gut microbiota in ALS, which may be able to interact with distant motor neurons. These results suggest that commensal strains may be useful in slowing disease progression by producing microbial metabolites that can interface with the CNS.

Zhang et al. asked how genetically predicted difference in the gut microbiota composition and their associated metabolites found in the blood influence ALS risk. They identified two microbes associated with increased ALS risk, an unclassified Enterobacteriaceae and unclassified Acidaminococcaceae, as well as microbiome-produced metabolites linked to higher ALS risk, including several gamma-glutamyl amino acids. These metabolites in particular are relevant to ALS because they may contribute to glutamate-driven excitotoxity. 131 This study points to the microbiome as a source of metabolites that can influence ALS-associated changes.

A multiomics study conducted by Gong et al. 2022 investigated whether the gut microbiota differed between ALS patients with cognitive impairment (CI) versus those without and healthy controls. There was a difference in six metabolic pathways associated with bile acid metabolism in ALS patients with cognitive impairment, and when bile acids were directly quantified in patients, there were lower levels of primary bile acids in ALS patients with CI, among them cholic acid. There was an increased abundance of species within Ruminococcaceae that play a role in converting bile acid forms, suggesting that microbiota production of bile acids may have relevance for CI in ALS. 132 This study highlights the understudied cognitive involvement in ALS patients and suggests how the gut microbiota may play a role in symptoms.

Gautam et al. 2025 paired shotgun metagenomics with metabolomics to compare ALS patients with household controls. Overall alpha diversity was similar, but ALS patients exhibited reductions in beneficial bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which generate SCFAs in the intestines and GABA for the modulation of the CNS, respectively. On the metabolite side, there were differences in tyrosine, glutamate, and SCFAs, and upregulations in functional pathways such as lipid metabolism, while there were several downregulated pathways, such as oxidative phosphorylation. Several metabolites have been found to track with ALS severity, including a correlation between ALSFRS-R and both carnitine and glutamate. 133 Taken together, this study shows the myriad of ways in which metabolic function differs in ALS and suggests that using these differences in metabolites may serve as potential biomarkers to predict ALS risk.

Metabolomic studies may have implications for other neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) beyond ALS. A multi-disease multiomics study by Christopher et al. 2025 compared stool 16S data and metabolomics in patients with ALS, Alzheimer's disease, and Parkinson's disease to look for shared metabolites that may be present in the disease. Several taxa were shifted across all three NDDs, including increased Desulfovibrio and Eubacterium. Several gut metabolomic differences were observed in ALS, including increases in histadinol and CDP-choline, with drastic decreases in all other metabolites, such as kyneuric acid and creatine. The most consistent shared metabolite was DHPS, found about 9 times lower than that of the controls across all three NDDs and was correlated with metabolites involved in neurotransmitter, bile acid, and vitamin B metabolism. This study broadly studies the microbial and metabolomic differences present in NDDs to understand whether there may be global alterations present. It identified signatures that were unique to each NDD and importantly found a microbially derived metabolite shared between the three. 134 Further studies are needed to reveal whether there are global microbiota changes that may signal the development of NDDs.

ALS focused microbiome and biomarker clinical trials

ALS is a rare, quickly progressive, and universally fatal disease. To establish a national database for collecting ALS case data, Congress passed the ALS Registry Act and authorized the creation of the National ALS Registry in October 2008. 135 The National ALS Registry collects demographic and lifestyle data from ALS patients in the United States, assesses risk factors through survey distribution, and facilitates recruitment into clinical trials. Building on this, the CDC launched the National ALS Biorepository in January 2017 to link Registry data with biospecimens for research use. 136 The existence of these public health infrastructures offers a practical bridge for people living with ALS to locate and enroll in clinical trials, which is essential for rapidly progressive disease. While not an explicit focus of the ALS Registry or Biorepository, standardized microbiota sampling could help determine whether there are signatures in ALS that could inform diagnosis or prognosis.

Understanding ALS is of utmost importance, as there are currently no cures. The Food and Drug Administration has approved three drugs for slowing ALS progression: Riluzole in 1995, Edaravone in 2017, and Tofersen in 2023. 4 , 137-139 Riluzole provides neuroprotection by inactivating voltage-gated sodium channels and blocking N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, thus preventing the transmission of excitatory glutamatergic signals within the CNS. 140 Edaravone is a costly treatment whose exact mechanism is unknown and functions best in the early stages of ALS. It works as an antioxidant and relieves oxidative stress in ALS 11 , 141 Tofersen is an antisense oligonucleotide that is effective for patients with SOD1 mutation and prevents the toxic accumulation of SOD1 protein in neurons by targeting its mRNA. 138 Consideration of how the microbiome may affect ALS pathogenesis and progression is warranted, given the heterogeneity in disease presentation among patients (Kargbo et al., 2023, FDA n.d.). DOI: 10.1021/acsmedchemlett.3c00221. 4 , 137-139 ).

Shafieinouri et al. 2025 analyzed data from biobanks to determine how having a non-AD or PD disorder affecting the gut or brain affects the risk for developing either AD or PD. They found those without co-morbidities and those who later developed AD or PD were more likely to have an increased polygenic risk score, which indicates that there is a genetic component in these isolated cases. When comparing proteomic biomarkers in individuals with only AD or PD versus those with a co-morbidity plus AD or PD, they found a potential biomarker for predicting AD risk, PSG1. Such biomarkers may be helpful for predicting ALS risk, as their dataset revealed that neurodegeneration risk was present 15 y before AD or PD onset when another co-morbid condition affecting endocrine, metabolic, digestive, or nutritional pathways was present. For AD risk, they also identified several GI disorders that are implicated in an increased risk of developing AD (e.g., gastritis, non-infectious colitis, and pancreatic secretion disorders). 142 Similar findings may be true in ALS, where co-morbid GI conditions years before ALS symptom onset may present.

In summary, we identified 20 ALS clinical trials that were microbiome-related or discussed biomarkers. The dates shown in the table represent the most recent trial record updates available at the time of this review. One notable pattern was that several studies evaluated the use of antibiotics in ALS but not primarily for antimicrobial activity (NCT00047723, NCT04840823, and NCT00349622). Rather, they assessed the impact of these antibiotics' potential anti-inflammatory or neuroprotective effects on the CNS. The trials did not include microbiota profiling however, many were initiated before the gut microbiome was considered a relevant factor in neurodegenerative disease. Another theme that emerged from the number of biomarker-focused trials turned up in our search is the ongoing need for biomarkers that enable earlier and accurate ALS diagnosis. Among the biomarker studies we identified (NCT07091799, NCT06318598, NCT06803784, and NCT06875739), only one trial (NCT06856850) incorporated microbiome analysis. There are currently no active trials that are designed primarily to evaluate microbiota-derived signatures as diagnostic biomarkers. These observations highlight a gap in clinical translation and point to microbiome-based biomarkers as a priority for future investigations.

Limits and future directions

While the etiology of ALS is multifactorial and complex, the proposed pathogenic mechanisms agree on motor system degeneration that results in motor neuron loss in the motor cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord. Eisen et al. describes a “dying-forward” view in which ALS may begin with failure of the corticomotoneuronal system and hyperexcitability in the motor cortex that then spreads down descending pathways to affect lower motor neurons in the brainstem and spinal cord. They also emphasize that progression likely reflects interactions among genes, aging, and the ALS exposome, which has environmental and lifestyle influences. 143

In this context, one possibility is that ALS-associated gut dysbiosis could contribute to the exposome through gut-derived signals that may have the potential to modulate neuro-associated pathways, an idea that remains to be directly tested in ALS. ALS-specific evidence linking bacterial metabolites to cortical hyperexcitability, as described by Eisen, is limited. However, gut-derived metabolites are established modulators of neuronal excitability in other contexts, providing a plausible justification to test in ALS. Within ALS studies, microbial community alterations warrant investigation into how ALS patients may be functionally impacted by dysbiosis.

A recent Mendelian randomization study by Changqing et al. examined the relationships among the gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and ALS. There were four microbial taxa associated with ALS and five cytokines associated with ALS. However, there was no link between these ALS-associated gut microbiota and ALS-associated inflammatory cytokines. 144 These results suggest that the gut microbiota affect ALS outcomes independent of inflammatory cytokines and may involve other immune pathways. Studies that clarify how dysbiosis may drive immune involvement through other mediators (e.g. immune cells populations, microbial metabolites) are much needed, given the role of neuroinflammation in driving ALS pathology.

Given that most patients with ALS take drugs such as Riluzole to manage their symptoms, it is important to consider how the microbiome may facilitate or reduce the efficacy of these drugs. Boddy et al. 2021 suggested that metabolites produced by gut microbes may impact the absorption and bioavailability of ALS drugs such as Riluzole. 94 A study by Zimmerman et al. 2019 identified Riluzole as one of many orally administered drugs that was metabolized by the gut microbiota during in vitro screening. 145 Because the gut microbial composition differs across individuals, these findings suggest that Riluzole's microbial metabolism may allow for its effects to vary. Beyond Zimmermann, there are currently no studies assessing how ALS drugs such as Riluzole affect the microbiome and affect ALS drug absorption. Similarly, investigations have not assessed how the microbiome in turn can be affected by these ALS drugs or therapies. A study assessing the gut microbiota and AD pathology through biomarkers found in the CSF microbiome reported that the majority of patients who participated were taking medications such as ACh inhibitors (donepezil or rivastigmine) or an NMDA receptor antagonist (memantine) to manage AD symptoms. The authors acknowledged that no studies exist on how these AD medications impact the gut microbiota, which may be especially important given that they can cause GI upset and symptoms. 146 Similarly, many patients with ALS take Riluzole, but it is not known how this medication may impact the gut microbiota. Therapies may be able to help manage neurological systems but could inadvertently have the potential to further disturb the gut microbiota and create other symptoms, which is not ideal considering that gut dysbiosis has already been detected in ALS patients. Thus, these gaps in how the microbiome affects the absorption of ALS therapies and how these therapies, in turn, may alter the microbiome are necessary to understand whether ALS treatments are both efficacious and non-harmful to patients.

To move forward, we need studies that establish mechanisms directed at how specific microbiome and microbial metabolites contribute to the microbiome‒ENS‒CNS interface in ALS, define how the microbiome communicates with the immune system and influences neuroinflammation, and how medications for ALS or co-morbid conditions impact the microbiome. The microbiome-ENS-CNS axis will offer abundant opportunities for future ALS research.
