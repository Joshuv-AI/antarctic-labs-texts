The Distribution Pattern of Pyrolysis Products from Thermally Thick PMMA under Constant Thermal Radiation in a Cross Forced Airflow
Authors: Dimeng Lai, Lizhong Yang, Baisheng Liao, Hongzhou He, Wenfeng Shen, Yangui Chen, Zhongqing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the ever-expanding applications of polymers, the issue of polymer fires has garnered increasing attention in academic circles. Compared to the situation in a static air environment, the ignition of polymers in airflow presents a more complex scenario, based on the fact that different air flow velocities alter the accumulation of pyrolysis products of the polymers in space. This study addresses the impact of airflow on PMMA (poly­(methyl methacrylate)) pyrolysis under constant heat flux through experimental, analytical, and numerical methods. Measurements of gas temperature, mass flux, and ignition delay time were performed to evaluate the influence of airflow and heat flux. The findings reveal a dynamic relationship between heat flux and airflow velocity: Increased heat flux enhances pyrolysis gas production, while higher airflow velocity enhances the transport capacity of pyrolysis gas. When gas production lags behind transport capacity, gas phase temperature remains uniform; exceeding transport capacity leads to local high temperature. This phenomenon impacts ignition delay time: At low airflow velocity, the shortest ignition delay time (approximately 0.44 times the maximum value) occurs at the sample’s rear edge, whereas at high airflow velocity, it shifts to the sample’s center. A condensed phase analytical model and a gas phase numerical model were developed. The distribution trend of the pyrolysis gas was computed using this model. The result validated the conclusion drawn from the measured gas phase temperature. The proposed analytical–numerical model helps to reduce the complexity of the calculation, compared to the traditional solid–gas numerical model. The research results are helpful for predicting the polymer ignition situation under air flow.

Introduction

As polymers are widely used in human society, their heating and ignition properties have also become a topic of widespread concern. The ignition problem of solid combustibles was broken down into three subproblems: (1) generation of combustible gases; (2) mixing process of combustible gas and oxygen; (3) ignition limit. Figure illustrates the schematic of the two-dimensional PMMA ignition problem. Pyrolysis of polymer molecules on high-temperature-exposed surfaces typically generates volatile production from combustible solids. Ambient airflow impacts fuel surface pyrolysis by altering the barrier mechanism for pyrolysis gas and increasing convective cooling effects. Additionally, the spatial distribution of a pyrolytic volatile component is significantly affected by ambient airflow, resulting in varying timeframes to reach lean flammability limits at different locations. Research has been conducted on polymer ignition behaviors under concurrent airflow conditions with an aim to increase or decrease ignition probability.

Schematic of the two-dimensional PMMA ignition in airflow.

Fundamental principles and theories for pyrolysis studies in quiescent air have been established, forming the foundation for airflow-conditioned research. Markstein emphasized radiation as the primary heat transfer mode, while Agarwal and Lattimer developed a systematic approach to measure kinetics and thermodynamic parameters related to thermal degradation of polymer materials. , Quintiere et al. − confirmed critical surface temperature and critical mass flux as ignition criteria, and Stoliarov et al. provided experimental data on ignition delay time and critical mass flux for PMMA under varying thicknesses and radiative heat fluxes (23–75 kW/m2). Furthermore, Kashiwagi identified that the pyrolysis volatile component had absorption effects on heat flux in gas phase processes. Delichatsios noted a decrease in the critical mass flux of plywood with increasing oxygen concentration (14–22%). Galgano and Di Blasi highlighted the influence of pilot position on ignition delay time. Regarding pyrolysis modeling, Lautenberger and Fernandez-Pello and Gong et al. investigated solid heat transfer, combining surface and depth absorptions, and developed numerical models. Gong et al. provided approximate analytical solutions predicting surface temperature and mass flux of condensed phases, showing good agreement with experimental results. Galgano and Di Blasi tested various models’ sensitivity in predicting PMMA ignition and established a numerical simulation model integrating gas phase ignition processes. However, due to airflow’s impact on the ignition process, conclusions drawn in quiescent-air studies require re-evaluation.

In the literature, few studies have addressed the forced convection effect. Zhou et al. developed a PMMA ignition model in airflow, calculating gas phase temperatures. Fernandez-Pello et al. − conducted successive studies on combustible ignition characteristics in airflow, establishing corresponding mathematical models. Gong et al. , investigated autoignition phenomena in thick PMMA exposed to parallel forced convective airflow. Lai et al. proposed an analytical approach to predict condensed phase surface temperature within airflow. However, gas phase process exploration primarily relies on numerical simulation methods lacking experimental verification, and classical numerical models incorporating both gas–solid coupling are computationally complex. The analytical approximation equation ignores the gas phase process, resulting in errors in the predicted values. In summary, the heat transfer complexities arising from solid–gas phase interactions due to forced convection remain inadequately understood.

This study investigates relationships between condensed phase mass flux, gas temperature distribution, ignition delay time, pyrolysis gas distribution, and airflow velocity based upon experiments and analytical and numerical simulations. The adopted analytical approach effectively predicts the unsteady mass flux caused by PMMA pyrolysis within flowing air and constant thermal radiation. The results of the analysis equation are added to the gas phase numerical simulation, which reduces the complexity of the traditional calculation. The results help to control the ignition process of polymer materials in the air flow.

Experimental Section

All experiments used the forced flow ignition test (FFIT) configuration displayed in Figure . This setup predominantly comprises a minuscule wind tunnel, a radiation platform, and corresponding supportive structures. Preliminary trials indicated that the airflow subsequent to the wind tunnel approximates a consistent laminar flow, and the radiation platform delivers a nearly uniform radiant heat flux. Radiant heat fluxes were measured using a water-cooled heat flux gauge. The heat flux on the sample, positioned horizontally on a thermally insulating holder, was directed perpendicularly to its surface, with a fixed 100 mm separation from the heater’s base. A 4 mm electrical spark, functioning as the ignition source, was situated downstream of the airflow, specifically 5 mm above the holder’s surface. A more comprehensive exposition of the experimental apparatus can be found in prior scholarly works.

Configuration of the experimental setup (unit: mm).

Clear PMMA, a transparent polymer extensively encountered in everyday applications, was chosen as the subject material for our experimental investigation. Considering that a large number of studies also take PMMA as the research object, sufficient data information is provided for comparative analysis. When observing the pyrolysis and ignition process, the material thickness should encompass the pyrolysis zone and the preheating zone and also be sufficient to support the presentation of the exponential absorption attenuation process of radiation in the semitransparent body. ,, Consequently, thermally thick PMMA (5 × 5 × 2 cm3) was chosen, where the internal temperature of the material exhibits variations along the thickness direction.

Within the heat flux range of 10–20 kW/m2, ignition susceptibility shows increased sensitivity to ambient conditions like airflow velocity, atmospheric pressure, and oxygen concentration compared to higher heat flux levels. Therefore, the investigation focused on heat flux levels of 11.53, 15.68, 19.30, and 22.15 kW/m2, and airflow velocities of 0.5, 0.8, 1.15, and 1.43 m/s. The ignition source was positioned 0, 15, 30, and 45 mm horizontally from the PMMA’s center, taking advantage of air flow to transport pyrolysis gas to the sample’s trailing edge, enhancing ignition probability. Temperature was monitored using S-type thermocouples mounted on a precise displacement platform, enabling transient temperature readings in localized areas of the sample.

After the heater reached thermal equilibrium, continuous airflow was introduced. Once heat flux and airflow stabilized, the heater was sheltered. The sample was then elevated to the designated position and exposed to radiation. Sample mass was measured using an electronic balance before ignition. Tests were repeated more than three times to ensure reliability, with nonignition defined as no ignition within 15 min.

Results

The solid fuel ignition process comprises pyrolysis, fuel-oxidizer mixing, and chemical reaction stages. Solid materials decompose thermally, generating pyrolysis gas that disperses into the air and mixes with oxidizers until reaching the lean burn limit and igniting. Therefore, solid mass flux determines the rate of pyrolysis gas production. Due to the higher temperature of pyrolysis gas compared to air, the spatial distribution pattern of pyrolysis gas is obtained indirectly by measuring the gas phase temperature above the solid. The ignition time is the result of the concentration of all factors.

Mass Loss and Mass Loss Rate

As volatile compounds continue to escape from the solid material, mass loss progressively increases, as depicted in Figure a. The pyrolysis rate, indicated by mass flux, shows a slight increase with accumulated heat, as shown in Figure b. When the mixture in the vicinity of the ignition source attains the lean limit, ignition takes place. Simultaneously, owing to the thermal feedback effect of the flame, the pyrolysis process is expedited, and the mass flux exhibits a sudden upsurge. Notably, prior to the ignition point, the curves corresponding to different ignition positions largely overlap. This finding suggests that alterations in the ignition position have a negligible influence on the mass flux, thereby indicating that the pyrolysis process remains unaltered. The inflection points of these curves signify the ignition delay time, which varies according to different ignition positions. This variability suggests that the ignition position has an impact on the mixing and chemical reaction processes. Consequently, under identical airflow velocity and heat flux conditions, modifying the ignition position will not undermine the accuracy of the predictions for the condensed phase using the previous model, which does not encompass the mixing and chemical reaction processes.

Mass loss and mass flux at 19.30 kW/m2 and 1.43 m/s.

Figure presents the critical mass flux measured at an ignition distance of 30 mm, which is considered an ignition criterion due to its relatively stable characteristics. ,,, The critical mass flux under all four heat flux conditions increases with airflow velocities within the range of 0.5 to 1.43 m/s, a trend consistent with previous studies. Notably, higher heat flux levels correspond to a more pronounced increase in critical mass flux as airflow velocity rises. Specifically, when the heat flux is 22.15 kW/m2, the critical mass flux increases by approximately 85% as the wind speed increases from 0.5 to 1.43 m/s. In contrast, when the heat flux is 15.68 kW/m2, the corresponding increase in critical mass flux is only about 29% under the same airflow conditions. This indicates that under high heat flux conditions, the influence of air flow becomes more significant when using critical mass flux as an ignition criterion. The occurrence of ignition necessitates that the combustible mixture near the pilot reaches the lower flammability limit. Airflow influences the ignition process in three primary ways: first, it affects the generation of pyrolysis gas through convective cooling; second, it transports pyrolysis gas downstream, thereby altering its spatial distribution; and third, the rapid influx of air leads to the dilution of pyrolysis gas. A lower airflow velocity results in reduced convective cooling and diminished transport capacity of pyrolysis gas, defined as the mass of pyrolysis gas conveyed from the center of the sample to its downstream region per unit time. When the pilot is located close to the sample, a decrease in transport capacity causes the combustible mixture surrounding the pilot to reach the lower flammability limit more quickly. An increase in incident radiation flux significantly enhances the yield of pyrolysis gas. For airflow velocities below 0.5 m/s, the absorption and attenuation of incident heat flux by pyrolysis gas must be considered. These effects prolong the ignition delay time and consequently increase the critical mass flux. The critical mass flux of black PMMA exhibits minimal variation across different airflow rates (0.4–1.2 m/s), which can be attributed to changes in transmittance affecting the absorption coefficient and subsequently redistributing the external heat flux acting on the material.

Spatial Distribution of Gas Phase Temperature

Figure illustrates the measured temporal temperature distribution under a heat flux of 22.15 kW/m2 and an airflow velocity of 1.43 m/s, highlighting the influence of the pyrolysis process on the spatial distribution of gas phase temperature (°C). The coordinate system originates from the center of the upper surface of the sample, with the X-axis denoting distance from the sample center (along the sample tail) and the Y-axis indicating distance from the upper surface. As pyrolysis initiates, the space above the sample gradually fills with pyrolysis gas, causing a rise in temperature. Density differences cause the heated gas to rise, elevating the upper layer temperature while reducing the lower layer temperature due to heat absorption by the sample’s upper surface, resulting in temperature stratification. Notably, the temperature peak consistently occurs 1.5 cm away from the upper surface. To further analyze heat flow and airflow’s impact on gas phase temperature, additional temperature data at 1.5 cm are provided.

The difference between the blank group and the experimental group was only that the experimental group placed samples, while the blank group did not. For an airflow of 1.43 m/s, slight smoke from the insulation surrounding the sample led to temperature increases at all locations. This indicates that pyrolysis gas accumulates primarily in the 1–2 cm area of the trailing edge. The elevated heat flux accelerates the production of pyrolysis gas, while the reduced airflow impedes its downstream transport, leading to localized accumulation.

Ignition Delay Time at Different Pilot Locations

Ignition delay time is a critical parameter in ignition studies. Figure presents the ignition delay times of PMMA at various pilot positions under two airflow velocities: 0.8 and 1.43 m/s. In this figure, the dashed lines represent the average values, while the shaded regions illustrate the range between the maximum and minimum values obtained from repeated experiments. The ignition delay time primarily depends on the duration required for the combustible gases near the pilot to reach the lean flammability limit (with chemical reaction induction time considered negligible , ). As the radiant heat flux increases, the ignition delay time decreases. This phenomenon occurs because higher incident heat flux elevates the condensed-phase temperature and accelerates the pyrolysis process, thereby reducing the time required for ignition. For different ignition positions, it was observed that under an airflow velocity of 0.8 m/s, the shortest ignition delay consistently occurred at all four heat flux levels at a distance of 15 mm from the sample center. Under a heat flux of 11.53 kW/m2, the ignition delay time at 0 mm exhibited significant variability (the maximum value being approximately twice the minimum), which can be attributed to the transport of a large quantity of pyrolysis gas downstream by the airflow. A necessary condition for central ignition is that the pyrolysis gas mass flux approaches the lower flammability limit. When the airflow rate increased to 1.43 m/s under a heat flux of 15.68 kW/m2, the ignition delay time remained relatively constant across different positions; however, the uncertainty remained high. Specifically, the maximum ignition delay time was approximately 2.25 times the minimum value (at 15 mm). Under conditions of low heat flux and high airflow rate, pyrolysis gas production is limited, but the transport capacity of the air is strong, preventing gas accumulation in the space.

Ignition delay time at different pilot locations at airflow velocities (a) 0.8 m/s and (b) 1.43 m/s.

At lower heat flux and higher air flow velocity, the pyrolysis gas produces relatively slower. However, due to the strong air transport capacity, the pyrolysis gas does not accumulate spatially, and ignition depends on the random occurrence of locally elevated concentrations of pyrolysis gas. In contrast, under higher heat flux conditions, the shortest ignition time occurs in the central region. This suggests that airflow significantly influences the spatial distribution pattern of pyrolysis gas: Under low airflow velocities, pyrolysis gas tends to accumulate downstream, whereas under high airflow velocities, dispersion prevents significant accumulation, resulting in the highest concentration near the original production site.

Discussion

The spatial uniformity of gas phase temperature indirectly reflects the distribution of pyrolysis gas. Analytical simulation of the condensed phase and numerical simulation of the gas phase were employed for a comprehensive assessment of pyrolysis gas distribution.

Simulation Results

It is difficult to accurately acquire the gas phase concentration field without influencing the experimental outcomes. This is because during data collection, gas extraction is necessary, and this very process inherently modifies the spatial accumulation mechanism of pyrolysis gas. Consequently, the specific concentration values derived from numerical simulations lack practical significance. Instead, the fraction of the mixed gas (that is, the proportion of fuel mass within the mixed gas) exhibits a qualitative tendency. This qualitative trend is employed to indirectly analyze the spatial distribution pattern of pyrolysis gas.

The mixture fraction obtained from numerical simulation at 22.15 kW/m2 and 1.43 m/s is depicted in Figure . Owing to the heating effect of the heat flux, a pyrolysis gas mass (2.5 s) emerges above the sample, swiftly diluted by airflow (7.1 s). The interplay between heat flux and airflow velocity engenders a repetitive process over time, impeding the stable appearance of the pyrolysis gas mass above PMMA. As time progresses, the mass flux of the pyrolysis gas phase gradually increases.

Conclusions

This study experimentally investigates the piloted ignition of thermally thick PMMA under constant incident heat flux and external forced airflow. Analytical and numerical models, considering the effect of radiation reduction, are employed to simulate experimental conditions. The analytical models are validated by their close agreement with experimental data. Key findings include the following:1.Different pilot positions and airflow velocity have no significant effect on the mass flux.2.As heat flux increases, a dynamic interplay emerges between heat flux and airflow velocity. Elevated heat flux augments pyrolysis gas production, whereas increased airflow velocity enhances its transport capacity. When pyrolysis gas production falls below transport capacity, the gas phase temperature remains uniform. Conversely, if the rate of thermal decomposition gas generation surpasses transport capacity, a singular accumulation phenomenon arises.3.The shortest ignition delay time occurs at the rear edge of the sample under an airflow velocity of 0.8 m/s, and a shift to the center with the shortest ignition delay time when airflow increases to 1.43 m/s.4.Numerical simulations yield mixture fractions affirming the nexus between localized high gas phase temperatures and the singular accumulation of pyrolysis gas.
