Knowledge of fertile windows and associated factors among female high school students in Warra Jarso district, Ethiopia using a mixed-methods approach
Authors: Dursa Hussein, Mengistu Tadesse, Mulugeta Mukuria, Derara Girma Tufa, Befekadu Tesfaye Oyato, Hana Israel Gesisa, Seifu Mohammed Ibrahim, Meron Seyoum Lakew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the fertility period is crucial for delaying pregnancy. Many female students lack accurate knowledge, leading to unintended pregnancies and unsafe abortions, contributing to maternal death and school dropout. Most research focuses on adult women, overlooking the unique challenges faced by adolescents. This highlights the need for targeted studies on younger females. Therefore, this study aimed to assess knowledge of fertile windows and associated factors among female students of high schools in Warra Jarso District, North Shewa Zone, and Oromia, Ethiopia. An institution-based cross-sectional study with a convergent mixed-methods approach was conducted among high school students in Warra Jarso from March 01 to April 01, 2024. A simple random sampling technique was used to select 417 study participants. The quantitative data were collected using self-administered questionnaires, and focus group discussions were used for the qualitative part. Epi Data version 4.6 was used for data entry, and SPSS version 26.0 software was used for data analysis. The multivariable logistic regression model included variables with a P value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis. The degree of association was expressed using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) at a P value < 0.05. The magnitude of fertile window knowledge was 24.9% (95% Cl: 20.9, 29.0). Age group (17–19 years) (AOR = 2.1, 95% CI = 1.9, 8.8), having sex (AOR = 3.0, 95% CI = 1.4, 4.7), history of pregnancy (AOR = 3.2, 95% CI = 1.2, 8.8), contraceptive use (AOR = 9.0, 95% CI = 4.1, 19.3) and a positive attitude towards fertility knowledge (AOR = 5.0, 95% CI = 2.6, 9.4) were significantly associated with knowledge of the fertility window. Three main themes emerged from the qualitative analysis: Personal experiences and attitudes towards fertility as enablers while misconceptions about ovulation and fertility as barriers. The level of knowledge about the fertile window among participants was relatively low. Factors such as being in the 17–19 age group, having had sexual experience, a history of pregnancy, contraceptive use, and a positive attitude toward fertility knowledge were significantly associated with higher levels of knowledge about the fertile window. Additionally, the qualitative study identified the enablers and barriers to knowledge of fertile windows. The implementation of comprehensive sexual and reproductive health programs with a focus on fertility awareness is recommended for female high school students.Keywords: Knowledge, Fertile period, Fertile windows, Students, Associated factors, Warra Jarso, EthiopiaSubject terms: Biological techniques, Developmental biology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Understanding the fertile window, the period of a woman’s menstrual cycle when conception is most likely, is crucial for effective family planning, particularly for those choosing natural methods1. Accurate knowledge of this period enables informed decisions regarding pregnancy timing and prevention, reducing the risk of unintended pregnancies and subsequent complications, including unsafe abortions2. Maternal mortality remains a significant concern globally, particularly in regions like sub-Saharan Africa where unsafe abortions contribute substantially to this alarming statistic3. Lack of awareness regarding the fertile window is a major contributing factor4. While modern contraceptives exist, many women discontinue use due to concerns about side effects, underestimating the likelihood of conception, or simply lacking sufficient knowledge5.

This knowledge gap is widespread. Studies across diverse populations—from the United States and Australia to Turkey and sub-Saharan Africa—consistently reveal a significant proportion of women lacking an accurate understanding of their fertile window3,6–8 This deficit extends to adolescents, with alarmingly low accurate knowledge rates reported in various African studies9. The consequences are substantial, leading to unintended pregnancies, economic burdens on individuals and healthcare systems, and negative impacts on educational and career opportunities. Furthermore, limited knowledge about the fertile period contributes to higher fertility rates, with negative social and economic repercussions10.

While prior studies in Ethiopia have examined fertile window knowledge among reproductive-age women or adolescents broadly4,11,12, our study specifically targets female high school students—a subgroup facing unique vulnerabilities, such as limited access to sexual education, peer pressure, and cultural taboos that disproportionately impact their reproductive health literacy. Previous research has predominantly relied on quantitative methodologies, which may overlook contextual barriers like, misconceptions linking fertility to lunar cycles11,13. By employing a convergent mixed-methods design, this study not only quantifies knowledge gaps but also identifies socio-cultural and educational drivers of misinformation, offering evidence for targeted school-based interventions in rural districts like Warra Jarso.

Methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted in the North Shewa Zone of the Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia, from March 1 to April 1, 2024, among female high school students in Warra Jarso District. The schools involved were Goha-Tsion High School, Filiklik High School, and Tulu Milki High School. Warra Jarso District is situated in the Northern part of the North Shewa Zone, approximately 52 km from Fiche, the capital of the zone, and 186 km from Addis Ababa, the country’s capital city. According to estimates from the Warra Jarso District Education Office, there will be 3,828 students in the district in 2024, with 1,936 being female.

Study design

An institution -based cross-sectional study design with a convergent mixed-methods approach was employed. A convergent parallel mixed methods is a type of design in which qualitative and quantitative data are collected in parallel, analysed separately and then merged during interpretations14. In this study, the quantitative study was conducted to determine the magnitude of fertile window knowledge and associated factors, while the qualitative study was conducted to explore the enablers and barriers to fertile window knowledge among female high school students.

Population

All students in Warra Jarso District high schools were the source population, and all randomly selected students in the three selected high schools of Warra Jarso District constituted the study population. A qualitative study was conducted among purposively selected students from high schools in Warra Jarso District.

Eligibility criteria

All high school students attending regular classes in Warra Jarso District during the study period were included in the study, while remedial high school students were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated by using the formula for estimating the proportion of a single population in the Epi Info STAT CALC cohort. Version 7.2, with the assumptions of a 95% confidence interval (CI), a margin of error (d) of 0.05, and a prevalence of knowledge of the fertile period among female students of 0.50 since no prior study has been conducted in the country. Considering a 10% nonresponse rate, the final sample size was 422. For the qualitative part of the study, a total of 28 students were selected using a purposive sampling technique. One focused group discussion (FGD) was conducted in each of the three selected high schools, with 8–10 participants per group, based on factors such as age, grade level, residence, marital status, and willingness to participate.

Sampling procedures

For the quantitative component, a stratified simple random sampling technique was employed. The six public high schools in Warra Jarso District were first listed, and three were selected using the lottery method. Within each selected school, students were stratified by grade level (Grades 9 to 12), and the number of participants from each grade was allocated proportionally based on student enrollment. From a total of 64 sections across the selected grades, 50 sections were randomly selected using the lottery method. Finally, a simple random sampling technique was used to select individual female students from each selected section using a student list as the sampling frame.

For the qualitative part of the study, a total of 28 students were selected using a homogeneous purposive sampling technique to ensure participants shared similar characteristics, such as age, grade level, residence, and marital status. This approach aimed to create a comfortable environment for open discussions and to generate in-depth insights. Three FGDs were conducted, one in each of the three selected high schools: Goha-Tsion (10 participants), Filiklik (10 participants), and Tulu Milki (8 participants).

Data collection procedures (tools, techniques, and personnel)

For the quantitative part, a structured questionnaire was developed from various sources of literature15–18. The questionnaire consisted of six parts including sociodemographic characteristics, Misconceptions and sources of information about the ovulation period, sexual-related characteristics, pregnancy, abortion and contraception use-related factors, knowledge of the fertility window, and attitudes towards the fertile window. Data were collected using a self-administered structured questionnaire. Seven BSc nurses served as data collectors, supported by two BSc public health supervisors during the data collection process.

For the qualitative part, data were collected in the local language (Afaan Oromo) by using semi-structured FGD guides. The PI prepared the guides in English, translated them into Afaan Oromo, and checked by advisers for more clarity. The FGD guides list a few discussion points, such as enablers and barriers to knowledge about the fertile period, with several follow-up probes used to address this issue. The FGDs were moderated by an experienced health professional and note-taker. During the discussion, notes were taken, and their voices were recorded using a tape recorder. Each experiment lasted 60–90 min at each selected site.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Knowledge of Fertility Window.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic characteristics (age, marital status, religion, wealth index, grade level).

Sexual-related characteristics (ever made sex, age at first sex, with whom 1st sex, reason for 1st sex, number of sexual partners).

Pregnancy, abortion, and contraception use-related factors (use of modern contraceptives, chance of becoming pregnant, number of pregnancies, ever abortions, number of abortions, reasons for abortion).

Attitudes toward the fertile window

Operational definitions

Knowledge of Fertile window Knowledge of the ovulation period was assessed by asking respondents a single question, “When do you think the ovulation period of a woman is?” Respondents who answered that the fertile period is in the middle of the menstrual cycle are categorized as having good knowledge of the fertile period, while respondents who answered that the fertile period is “during her period,” “after the period ends,” “before a period begins,” “at any time,” or “I don’t know” are categorized as having poor knowledge15.

Fertility window The fertility period, also known as the fertile window, refers to the timeframe during which a woman’s menstrual cycle is most likely to conceive a pregnancy if she engages in sexual intercourse19.

Attitudes towards the fertile window Attitudes were assessed using a total of 10 questions specifically related to the fertile window. Participants who scored at or above the mean value of these questions were categorized as having positive attitudes towards the fertile window, while those who scored below the mean were classified as having negative attitudes20.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality for the quantitative study, one day of training was given for data collectors on information about the data collection tools research objective, eligible study subject procedures, and interview methods. The questionnaire was initially developed in English and then translated into the local language (Afaan Oromo) to ensure comprehension by the students. To maintain consistency and accuracy, a different language expert conducted a back-translation into English.

The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the total sample size (at Gerba Guracha High School) to assess its clarity, consistency, and appropriateness16,21.

On each data collection day, the authors examined some per cent of the collected data, and any forwarded problems were immediately solved. Subsequently, during the data entry and analysis stage, a software tool called Epi-Data was utilized for data entry. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The attitude-related questions yielded a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.80, while the knowledge-related questions yielded a value of 0.76, indicating acceptable reliability for both sections.

To ensure the quality of data in the qualitative part of this study, the principles of trustworthiness were carefully applied. The following components were meticulously addressed:

Credibility This was achieved through prolonged engagement with participants, member checking, and triangulation of data sources. Validating findings with participants and using multiple data sources enhanced the accuracy and authenticity of the results.

Transferability Detailed descriptions of the research context, participant demographics, and study methodology were provided to enable readers to assess the applicability of findings to other settings.

Dependability A comprehensive audit trail was maintained to document the research process, decisions made, and any changes implemented, thereby ensuring transparency and reproducibility.

Confirmability Reflexivity was practiced by the principal investigator, who maintained a reflective journal to account for personal biases and assumptions. Peer debriefing sessions were also conducted to provide external validation of the findings.

Data processing and analysis

For the quantitative part, the filled-out questionnaires were checked for completeness, coded, and entered into the computer using EPI Data Version 4.6 software, exported to SPSS Version 26.0 for cleaning and checking for missing values, and finally analyzed. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were calculated. Then, binary logistic regression was performed to assess the crude relationship between the independent variables and the dependent variable. All variables with a p-value < 0.2 were candidates for multivariable logistic regression to control for possible confounding effects.

To ensure model validity, diagnostic checks were performed. Multicollinearity was tested using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all variables showing VIF values less than 5. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.7), indicating a good fit. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by adjusting the p-value threshold for including variables in the multivariable model from < 0.2 during the bivariate analysis. The results remained stable, suggesting the robustness of the findings.

To determine the independent influence of each variable on the outcome variable, multivariable logistic regression was used. The final results of the associations were presented as the AORs with 95% CIs, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

For qualitative data, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated into English, and then coded manually. The data were reviewed for similarities and differences, and categorized under major themes and subthemes based on the FGD guide. Thematic analysis was applied, and the findings were summarized manually. Thematic saturation was reached by the third FGD, as no new codes or themes emerged, indicating that an adequate depth of information had been obtained.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Review Board of Salale University (Ref. No: IRB/911/16). The study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki on ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects. For participants aged 18 years and above, written informed consent was obtained before data collection. For participants under the age of 18, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and verbal assent was obtained from the minors themselves. All information collected from study participants was treated with strict confidentiality, and identifying information was not recorded to ensure anonymity.

We applied thematic analysis to the FGD transcripts. After coding and categorizing responses, three overarching themes emerged: (1) personal experiences and demographic factors shaping fertility knowledge, (2) attitudes toward learning about fertility, and (3) misconceptions and cultural barriers. Each theme is described below with illustrative quotes. Data saturation was reached by the third FGD as no new themes emerged.

Enablers to Fertile Window Knowledge

The findings of this study were discussed under three major themes: Personal Experiences demographic factors and attitudes toward fertility.

Theme 1: personal experiences and demographic factors shaping fertility knowledge

Many students reported that their knowledge about the fertile window was influenced by life experiences such as age, sexual activity, pregnancy history, and contraceptive use. Older participants tended to be more confident in discussing reproductive health, reflecting greater exposure to information. Students who had engaged in sexual activity or experienced pregnancy described seeking information to avoid unintended pregnancies. Those who used contraceptives reported learning about fertility through counseling and health services.

“Older girls in my class seem more confident talking about these things—they’ve probably learned more from their families or friends” (Student 6, FGD 1).

“When I started having sex, I had to learn more about my body so I wouldn’t get pregnant accidentally” (Student 12, FGD 1).

“After my first pregnancy, I realized how important it is to know when you’re most likely to conceive” (Student 4, FGD 2).

“After I started using birth control pills, I had to understand my cycle better so I wouldn’t miss any days” (Student 5, FGD 3).

These findings echo our quantitative results showing that students aged 17–19, those who had experienced sexual activity or pregnancy, and those who used contraceptives had significantly higher knowledge of the fertile window.

Theme 2: positive attitudes toward learning about fertility

Students with positive attitudes toward reproductive health described fertility knowledge as essential for planning their lives, focusing on education, and preventing early pregnancy. Such attitudes motivated them to seek accurate information from teachers, peers, or online sources despite cultural taboos.

“I think knowing about fertility is important for planning your future—it’s something everyone should learn” (Student 14, FGD 2).

“For me, learning about the fertile window isn’t just about avoiding pregnancy—it’s about planning my life. If I know my cycle, I can focus on my studies without worrying” (Student 16, FGD 2).

"Understanding the fertile window is like having a tool to make better decisions about my future” (Student 8, Group 1–3).

“I plan to go to college and start a career first, so knowing when I’m fertile will help me avoid getting pregnant too early” (Student 11, FGD 3).

This theme supports our quantitative finding that a positive attitude was associated with a fivefold increase in correct fertile window knowledge.

Barriers to fertile window knowledge

Barriers to fertile window knowledge were explored and categorized under one theme as follows:

Theme 1. Misconceptions and cultural barriers

Despite some accurate knowledge, misconceptions about ovulation timing and fertility remained widespread. Several participants believed that pregnancy could occur at any time of the month except during menstruation, or associated fertility with lunar cycles (such as the full moon). Cultural taboos also limited open discussion of reproductive health at home and in schools.

“We don’t talk about these things openly at home; it’s considered shameful” (Student 8, FGD 1).

“The biology teacher mentioned something about ovulation once, but it wasn’t explained very well” (Student 7, FGD 2).

“I thought everything I read on social media was true until my friend told me otherwise” (Student 5, FGD 3).

“I looked online for answers, but there were so many different opinions that I didn’t know what to believe” (Student 12, FGD 3).

These misconceptions reflect barriers identified in previous studies and underscore the need for clear, culturally sensitive reproductive health education for adolescents.

Factors associated with fertile window knowledge

Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with fertile window knowledge. Variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression. The results showed that age group (17–19 years) (AOR = 2.1, 95% CI = 1.9–8.8), having had sex (AOR = 3.0, 95% CI = 1.4–4.7), history of pregnancy (AOR = 3.2, 95% CI = 1.2–8.8), contraceptive use (AOR = 9.0, 95% CI = 4.1–19.3), and having a positive attitude towards fertility knowledge (AOR = 5.0, 95% CI = 2.6–9.4) were significantly associated with fertile window knowledge (Table 7).

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The integration of quantitative and qualitative data provided a more comprehensive understanding of the factors associated with knowledge of the fertile window among female students. The quantitative results showed that students aged 17–19, those who had experienced sexual activity or pregnancy, those who used contraceptives, and those with positive attitudes toward fertility knowledge were significantly more likely to have higher levels of fertile window knowledge.

These statistical associations were further enriched by the qualitative data. Participants in the FGDs consistently reported that learning often occurred after encountering real-life reproductive experiences. Several girls stated that they only became aware of their fertility cycle after becoming pregnant or after visiting a health center for contraception. This suggests that knowledge may be reactive rather than proactive—a finding not fully captured through the survey alone.

For instance, while the quantitative analysis showed that contraceptive use was significantly associated with better knowledge of the fertile window, this was supported by FGD participants who shared that counseling received during contraceptive visits helped them better understand their fertility cycle. Furthermore, although the quantitative data demonstrated that a positive attitude was strongly associated with better knowledge, qualitative narratives revealed that cultural taboos, limited communication, and inadequate school-based reproductive health education often undermined students’ ability to access accurate information. This highlights a critical gap: even when girls are motivated to learn, structural and social barriers can impede their access to fertility knowledge.

The qualitative data also provided context for the relatively low overall knowledge level (24.9%) observed in the survey. Participants explained that fertility-related topics are often considered inappropriate for unmarried girls, contributing to misinformation, silence, and confusion about the fertile period.

Together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating reproductive health education into schools early—before girls become sexually active. The mixed-methods approach enabled the researchers not only to identify key associated factors statistically, but also to understand the why and how behind these associations.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the magnitude of knowledge of fertile windows and associated factors among female high school students in Warra Jarso District, Oromia, Ethiopia. The study found that 24.9% of students had good knowledge of the fertile window. Factors such as age (17–19 years), sexual activity, a history of pregnancy, contraceptive use, and a positive attitude toward understanding fertile windows were significantly associated with fertile window knowledge. The qualitative findings enriched these results by identifying personal experiences, attitudes, and misconceptions as important enablers and barriers.

The proportion of students with good knowledge of the fertile window in our study 24.9% (95% CI: 20.9–29.0) is comparable to findings from the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (23.5%) and studies conducted in Wolaita Sodo, Ethiopia, and other countries such as Haiti (24.1%) and Pakistan (25%)15,16,20,22. However, it is higher than what was reported in Ghana (14.2%), Kenya (18.3%), Uganda (15%), and Saudi Arabia (12.2%)5,9,23,24. These differences may reflect variations in education, cultural norms, socio-cultural taboos, and study populations’ characteristics affecting access to reproductive health information. On the other hand, the prevalence of good knowledge in our study is lower than that reported in Cameroon (49.3%)19, East Sikkim in Asia (51.2%)25, and Bangladesh (41.2%)26. These variations may reflect differences in educational systems, access to sexual and reproductive health education, and cultural norms surrounding fertility awareness.

The proportion of female students in our study who had good knowledge of the fertile window (24.9%) is also lower than what has been reported in some high-income countries. For example, a population-based survey in Australia found that about 31% of reproductive-age women correctly identified the fertile window7. Similarly, studies from the United States have shown that only 20–30% of women could accurately determine the timing of ovulation6. A systematic review from Europe also reported higher awareness levels, ranging from 40–60% in some settings27. These findings indicate that, although gaps in fertility awareness exist globally, adolescents in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia are at an even greater disadvantage. Limited access to comprehensive reproductive health education and cultural taboos surrounding open discussion of fertility may partly explain these differences.

In our qualitative analysis, three key themes emerged: personal experiences, misconceptions about fertility, and positive attitudes toward learning. These findings align with a study conducted in Denmark, which suggested that personal experiences, such as facing pregnancy, significantly influence knowledge about fertility27. However, unlike findings from a study conducted in two United States metropolitan centers among women from low- and high-resource settings presenting for fertility care28, which reported that misconceptions were less prevalent among urban populations, our study revealed widespread misunderstandings about ovulation timing. This indicates a need for targeted educational interventions in rural settings.

Additionally, the importance of positive attitudes toward learning about fertility is similar to findings from South India29, which found that students who feel empowered are more likely to seek out information from various sources. These insights reinforce the necessity of fostering supportive environments that encourage open discussions about reproductive health.

In this study, students aged 17–19 years were about twice as likely to have good knowledge of the fertile window compared to those aged 14–16 years. This finding is consistent with studies from Ethiopia15,16, Sub-Saharan Africa30, Turkey8, Nnorthern Nigeria31, and Cameroon19. Older students may have greater exposure to reproductive health education, peer discussions, or personal experiences that increase their awareness.

Students who had ever engaged in sexual activity were about three times more likely to have knowledge of the fertile window compared to those who had never had sex. This is consistent with studies in Ethiopia, Turkey, and Cameroon8,16,19. Sexual experience may increase information-seeking behavior to avoid unintended pregnancy. The qualitative findings supported this, with students stating that they only learned about their cycle after becoming sexually active.

Similarly, students with a history of pregnancy were about three times more likely to have good knowledge compared to those who had never been pregnant. This suggests that lived reproductive experiences may drive greater awareness of fertility, a pattern also highlighted in the qualitative data. Contraceptive use was strongly associated with fertility awareness, with contraceptive users being nine times more likely to have good knowledge compared to non-users. This is supported by other studies in Ethiopia, Turkey, and Cameroon8,16,19. The qualitative data indicated that counseling during contraceptive use often provided important information about the menstrual cycle and fertility.

Finally, students with a positive attitude toward fertility knowledge were five times more likely to have good knowledge compared to those with negative attitudes. This aligns with evidence from Ethiopia32, Cameroon19, Australia7, and East Sikkim25. The qualitative findings further highlighted that positive attitudes motivate adolescents to actively seek accurate information, while cultural taboos and misconceptions remain significant barriers.

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings strengthens the interpretation. Quantitative associations (e.g., sexual activity, pregnancy, contraceptive use, attitudes) were explained in greater depth through qualitative narratives, showing that fertility knowledge among adolescents is often reactive rather than proactive. These insights emphasize the importance of early, school-based fertility education to ensure adolescents gain accurate knowledge before encountering reproductive experiences.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The data were collected through both quantitative and qualitative research approaches, which add value to strengthen the study design. As a cross-sectional study, a cause-and-effect relationship cannot be established to identify an actual predictor. Since the data were self-reported, and included some sensitive issues there may be social desirability bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while the knowledge of the fertile window in this study was higher than in some countries, it was still considerably lower than in others, suggesting the need for improved and context-specific sexual and reproductive health education. The findings from this study concluded that factors such as age (17–19 years), sexual activity, a history of pregnancy, contraceptive use, and a positive attitude towards understanding fertile windows were identified as significantly associated with fertile window knowledge. Additionally, the qualitative study identified the enablers and barriers to knowledge of fertile windows. Therefore, the implementation of comprehensive sexual and reproductive health programs aimed at providing accurate, age-appropriate, and comprehensive sexual and reproductive health education for all adolescent and adult female students, with a special focus on awareness of the fertility period. The Regional Health Bureau and Education Bureau should work collaboratively by designing strategies to increase the knowledge of female students about their fertility period.
