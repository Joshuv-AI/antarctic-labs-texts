Prevalence and Predictors of Nomophobia among Medical Students of a University in South India
Authors: S Yogeshkumar, Sasitharan Muthusamy, Rohit D. Bamane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Nomophobia, the fear of being without mobile phone contact, is recognized as a behavioral addiction characterized by anxiety when individuals are unable to access their smartphones or mobile networks. This phenomenon is increasingly observed among medical students, whose academic and personal reliance on smartphones is significant. The present study aims to determine the prevalence of nomophobia and identify the factors predicting its occurrence among undergraduate medical students at a University in Belagavi, South India.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted over 1 year among 416 undergraduate medical students using a universal sampling technique. After obtaining informed consent, data were collected using a predesigned structured questionnaire covering sociodemographic details and smartphone usage patterns. Nomophobia was assessed using the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q). Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS v23.0, and P values <0.05 considered statistically significant.Results:The median (interquartile range) age of participants was 21 (2) years, and the overall prevalence of nomophobia was 97.4%. Significant associations were observed between nomophobia and age group, frequency of smartphone checks, and the number of applications installed. Among the four dimensions of nomophobia, the highest mean score was observed for not able to communicate (4.16 ± 1.54), followed by not able to access information, losing connectedness, and giving up convenience.Conclusion:The prevalence of nomophobia among medical students is alarmingly high and consistent with other studies. These findings highlight the urgent need for awareness, targeted interventions, and support systems to mitigate smartphone-related anxiety and its potential impact on student wellbeing and academic performance.

INTRODUCTION

Nomophobia, an amalgamation of “NO MObile PHOne phoBIA,” characterizes a psychological condition marked by individuals’ apprehension about being disconnected from mobile phone connectivity.[1]

This unique fear stems from a behavioral addiction toward smartphones, accentuated by anxiety related to the detachment from the mobile network or the unavailability of a smartphone. Defined as a special phobia, nomophobia has been associated with individuals who exhibit compulsive smartphone-checking behaviors, reaching a point where the urge becomes overpowering and even hallucinations of phone ringing occur when it is not. Though not formally recognized in the DSM-5, it has been proposed as a specific phobia aligning with the DSM-IV definitions.[2]

With smartphones omnipresent among professionals and students, it becomes imperative to scrutinize the adverse effects of excessive mobile phone use. Numerous studies have explored the prevalence of mobile phone addiction and nomophobic behaviors, particularly among medical students in India,[3456789] emphasizing the need for comprehensive assessment and intervention.

Recent research suggests that nomophobia, an escalating mental health concern, is particularly prevalent among male adolescents. There is a substantial correlation between nomophobia and adverse mental health outcomes, including heightened levels of depression and anxiety, suicidal tendencies, and a notable decline in overall quality of life.[101112]

The findings of some of the recent studies highlighted the widespread exploration into the potential correlations between symptoms of nomophobia and various psychiatric or physical health concerns such as alterations in sleep patterns, impacts on learning and attention abilities, academic performance outcomes, and the implementation of coping strategies.[13141516]

In light of these considerations, the present study focuses on estimating the prevalence and predictors of nomophobia among medical students at a university in South India. Being a unique study of its kind in South India, the study seeks to enhance our understanding of nomophobia within the context of medical education, paving the way for targeted interventions and support strategies.

METHODOLOGY

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 416 undergraduate medical students over a period of 1 year from February 2023 to January 2024. Ethical clearance was obtained from JNMC Institutional Ethical Committee (Reference no. MDC/JNMCIEC/82) dated 14.10.2022.

The study utilized a universal sampling approach, recruiting students who owned smartphones and willingly chose to take part. A google form was meticulously created with the required information and circulated among the first- to fourth-year medical students. Approval from the respective department heads was acquired in advance, and students provided digital informed consent prior to the commencement of the form-filling process.

The study utilized a prevalidated Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q),[17] containing 20 questions, designed for college students. The questionnaire had four dimensions for assessing nomophobia including not being able to access information (4 questions), losing connectedness (5 questions), not being able to communicate (6 questions), and giving up convenience (5 questions). The answer for each question was rated using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (7).

The total score on the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q) ranged from a minimum of 20 to a maximum of 140. Based on the scores, participants were categorized as No Nomophobia for scores up to 20, scores between 21 and 59 represented mild nomophobia, scores ranging from 60 to 99 indicated moderate nomophobia, and scores between 100 and 140 signified severe nomophobia.

Data on sociodemographic profile such as age, gender, year of study, and type of residential area were obtained. Also, details pertaining to smartphone usage such as years of usage, using Internet, time and duration spent on smartphone in a day, frequency of checking the smartphone in a day, and the number of additional applications installed in the phone were also obtained.

All the data obtained in the google forms were extracted to Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v23.0. Results were tabulated and represented using Descriptive and Inferential statistics. All P values <0.05 were considered to be significant.

Also, the patterns of smartphone usage among the study participants indicate that a significant proportion of the students report long-term smartphone use, with 41.3% using a smartphone for 5 years or more, while a vast majority of them had access to an Internet connection (95.2%). In terms of daily usage, 30.0% spent a minimum of 2 to 3 hours on their smartphones. Checking frequency was also notable as 34.1% reported checking their phones between 10 and 20 times a day, while 24.3% checked at least once every hour. Additionally, a substantial percentage of participants (32.5%) reported having 20 or more additional applications installed on their smartphones.

Figure 1 outlines the prevalence of nomophobia within the medical student population. An overwhelming 97.4% of the students exhibited signs of nomophobia. Among them, 20% experienced mild nomophobia, while 63.2% and 14.2% displayed moderate and severe nomophobia, respectively. These findings suggest that virtually every student in the study demonstrated some level of nomophobia. Conversely, only a minimal 2.6% of students were found to be devoid of nomophobic tendencies.

DISCUSSION

Our study revealed an exceptionally high overall prevalence of nomophobia, reaching 97.4%. Additionally, notable associations were identified between nomophobia and various factors, including the age group of students, number of times the smartphone is checked in a day, and the number of applications installed on smartphones. These findings underscore the multifaceted nature of nomophobia, indicating that it is influenced by diverse aspects such as age group influence, smartphone usage patterns, and application preferences among the participants.

Moreover, an in-depth analysis of the reasons behind nomophobic tendencies unveiled that the primary concern among participants was the fear of being unable to communicate, as reflected by the highest mean score. Subsequently, other significant reasons included anxiety about the inability to access information, the fear of losing connectedness, and apprehension about giving up convenience. These insights into the specific triggers for nomophobia provide valuable context for understanding the nuanced psychological aspects contributing to this prevalent phenomenon among the study participants.

The prevalence in our study aligns with findings from similar studies,[491819] conducted among college students, suggesting that nomophobia is not exclusive to medical students but extends across various academic disciplines.[20] The consistent prevalence observed when employing the nomophobia questionnaire (NMP-Q) indicates the universality of this issue among students, regardless of their specific field of study. A meta-analysis[21] demonstrated that a substantial proportion of the population experiences moderate nomophobia, aligning with the parallel observation in our study.

However, it is crucial to note discrepancies in prevalence figures among different studies,[62223] revealing substantially lower prevalence rates. Upon closer examination, these variations could be attributed to the utilization of different questionnaires in data collection, especially among similar study participants. This underscores the importance of the questionnaire used in determining the reported prevalence of nomophobia. The choice of assessment tools may introduce variability in the data, emphasizing the need for standardized instruments to ensure consistency in evaluating nomophobia across diverse study populations.

Our study revealed a significant correlation between the age group and nomophobia, consistent with similar observations in studies conducted both within India[24] and internationally.[2526] Interestingly, in contrast to a few studies that established associations between nomophobia and variables such as gender,[27] and year of study,[28] our study did not yield similar findings in these specific domains.

Also, our study revealed association between nomophobia and the number of times the smartphone is checked in a day, and the number of applications installed on smartphones. A similar association was also observed in other studies involving college students.[829] While our study looked at the smartphone usage and nomophobia, a few other studies[730] have assessed nomophobia among Internet addicts. In our study, there was no association between nomophobia and using mobile phone with Internet.

The strengths of the study are due to its high prevalence detection and the comprehensive association of triggers, strengthening the external validity of the research and ultimately adding contribution to the nomophobia literature.

However, there exists certain limitations such as cross-sectional design, self-reported data, limited exploration of Internet use, and the usage of NMP-Q tool, which might have some bias as the students cannot rate less than one in the Likert scale and failing to do will undoubtedly result in bias, leading to overestimation of the results as commented in the recent literature.[31]

The findings of this study highlight critical implications for educational institutions, mental health professionals, policymakers, and researchers to address nomophobia as a significant mental health issue, particularly in high-stress environments like medical schools. Awareness programs should be incorporated into the curriculum to educate students on the impact of excessive smartphone use, while universities can implement policies encouraging healthy usage patterns, such as digital detox workshops and time management training. Mental health support systems should integrate nomophobia screening and provide counseling to address associated anxieties.

Also, the study recommends that future studies should explore the intricacies of nomophobia assessing the impact that it has on academic performance and sleep patterns among different demographic populations and thereby develop intervention strategies for addressing nomophobia.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study unveils an alarmingly high prevalence of nomophobia (97.4%) among medical students, with significant associations identified with smartphone usage patterns. The primary trigger, fear of communication loss, provides insight into the psychological elements that underlie and contribute to the phenomenon of nomophobia. The complex nature of these dynamics emphasizes the necessity for carefully tailored interventions to effectively address nomophobia within academic environments.
