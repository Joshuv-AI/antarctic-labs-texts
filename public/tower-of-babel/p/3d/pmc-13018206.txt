Sleep awake detection from leg-worn wearables using deep sensor fusion
Authors: Yumna Anwar, Kanika Bansal, Murat Kucukosmanoglu, Quang Dang, Cody Feltch, Justin Brooks, Nilanjan Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Restful sleep is essential for health, yet many children with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) experience disturbances such as delayed sleep onset, shorter total sleep time, frequent awakenings, and daytime fatigue. Accurate detection of these issues is important for clinical care, but existing tools have limitations: polysomnography is costly and complex, while wrist devices often miss subtle movement or physiological changes. This study introduces a deep learning approach using data from RestEaze, a leg-worn multimodal wearable that records photoplethysmography (PPG), motion from accelerometer and gyroscope, and temperature signals. Overnight recordings were collected from 14 children referred for ADHD evaluation. A Support Vector Machine (SVM) using handcrafted features was implemented to establish a traditional baseline. Two convolutional neural network (CNN–BiLSTM) models were then developed, employing early and late-fusion of raw multimodal inputs to classify sleep and wake states in short windows. The late-fusion model achieved an area under the ROC curve of 90.94% in five-fold cross-validation. Derived metrics included total sleep time, wake after sleep onset, sleep onset latency, and awakenings. A temporal label-smoothing method further improved consistency. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of leg-based multimodal sensing and deep learning for noninvasive sleep monitoring in pediatric neurodevelopmental populations.

Introduction

Restful sleep is vital for maintaining overall health and well-being. It serves as a physiological and behavioral marker for a wide range of conditions, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), insomnia, sleep apnea, and neurodevelopmental disorders such as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)1–3. According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), an estimated 50 to 70 million Americans experience sleep disturbances, which contribute to numerous chronic health issues and impaired quality of life4. Sleep disturbances are particularly critical in people with ADHD, where disturbed sleep can exacerbate core behavioral symptoms such as inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. ADHD is frequently comorbid with other disorders and sleep disturbances rank among the most common1.

Sleep disturbances are multidimensional in nature. In children with ADHD, polysomnographic studies have revealed disrupted sleep characterized by elevated limb movements and a higher incidence of hypopnea and apnea events5,6. Actigraphy-based assessments also reported increased sleep onset latency and reduced total sleep time, difficulty falling asleep, difficulty waking, fragmented sleep, and daytime sleepiness1. These multidimensional disturbances suggest the need for sleep assessment tools that capture both sleep architecture and motor-related disturbances with sufficient temporal and physiological resolution.

Although polysomnography remains the gold standard for sleep assessment, it is costly, time intensive, and requires overnight monitoring in a controlled laboratory setting, which may not reflect the natural sleep patterns of an individual7,8. This makes long-term, real-world sleep tracking impractical for many individuals. In response to these limitations, wearable sleep tracking devices have become an attractive alternative. Many commercial wearables rely on wrist-based accelerometry to estimate sleep-wake patterns9. Although these devices offer convenience, they struggle to accurately detect fragmentation, depth, and subtle physiological changes in sleep. They also lack the ability to identify leg-related movements, making them particularly limited for conditions such as Restless Leg Syndrome, where leg movement plays a key role during sleep disturbances.

Recent advances in sleep–wake classification have spanned both classical machine learning and deep learning approaches using wearable sensor data. Large-scale benchmarks such as those by Palotti et al.10 demonstrated the effectiveness of ensemble and recurrent models applied to actigraphy, establishing a strong foundation for wrist-based sleep detection. Subsequent work introduced lightweight convolutional networks trained on accelerometer signals11 and convolutional architectures applied directly to photoplethysmography (PPG) signals12, showing that both motion and cardiovascular cues can yield robust performance under controlled conditions. Other studies employing handcrafted PPG features with traditional classifiers such as k-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, and random forests13 achieved reliable discrimination but were limited by small sample sizes and reduced generalizability. More recently, gradient-boosted tree models integrating PPG and activity features14 have emphasized scalability to larger cohorts but continue to face challenges in maintaining balanced accuracy across populations. Collectively, these findings highlight that while single-modality systems can perform well, their robustness is constrained by motion artifacts, sensor placement variability, and limited physiological context, underscoring the value of multimodal sensing strategies for improving sleep–wake detection in real-world settings.

To address this gap, there is growing interest in wearable technologies that incorporate a broader range of physiological signals and support more granular analysis of sleep behavior. Leg-based sensing, in particular, holds promise due to the established relationship between lower-limb movement and sleep disruptions. Therefore, in this study, we used the RestEaze device, a leg-worn wearable that integrates a 3-D accelerometer, gyroscope, temperature sensor, and PPG to classify sleep and wake states using a deep learning architecture. This multimodal configuration enables the capture of dorsiflexions and complex leg movements, providing a richer physiological context for sleep classification.

The RestEaze device has previously been utilized in studies investigating leg movements and cortical arousals during sleep. Jha et al.15 demonstrated that machine learning analyses of leg-movement signals recorded by RestEaze could effectively distinguish movements associated with cortical arousals from those without. Building on this work, Bansal et al.16 examined the relationship between cortical arousals and leg movements in children with ADHD using the same device, reporting strong correlations between leg-movement features and EEG-defined arousal events. More recently, Kucukosmanoglu et al.17 introduced a multimodal machine learning framework integrating accelerometry, gyroscope, photoplethysmography (PPG), and temperature signals to detect cortical arousals. Collectively, these studies established the reliability of the RestEaze platform for arousal and movement characterization. In the present study, we extend this work by leveraging the same multimodal sensing platform to classify sleep and wake states, providing a comprehensive evaluation of its applicability for sleep–wake monitoring. We used overnight RestEaze data from 14 children undergoing clinical evaluation for ADHD and developed a deep learning–based sleep–wake classifier using a CNN–BiLSTM architecture. To integrate multimodal sensor data, we implemented two fusion strategies: (1) early-fusion, where inputs from all sensors are concatenated and passed through a single model, and (2) late-fusion, where each sensor stream is processed independently through sub-models before being combined for final classification.

In addition to frame-wise predictions, we derive several clinically relevant sleep metrics from the model outputs, including total sleep time (TST), wake after sleep onset (WASO), sleep onset latency (SO), and sleep efficiency (SE). These derived measures enable interpretable analysis of sleep quality and disruption patterns. By combining leg-based sensing with temporal deep learning, this study contributes to the development of accessible, non-invasive tools for real-world sleep monitoring.

Results

The classification model was evaluated on data segmented into fixed-length windows, each labeled as either sleep (1) or awake (0). Across all participants, the dataset showed a strong imbalance, with sleep segments occurring approximately five times more frequently than awake segments. To account for this, performance metrics were selected to reflect sensitivity to class imbalance, including Receiver Operating Characteristic - Area Under the Curve (ROC–AUC), F1 score, and specificity at fixed sensitivity levels. The model was trained on raw signals from four sensor modalities, PPG, gyroscope, accelerometer, and temperature, and evaluated using five-fold cross-validation with subject-level splits to ensure generalization between individuals. To mitigate the effects of class imbalance during training, we applied binary focal loss, which helped improve the model’s ability to detect the minority awake class.

Evaluation of sleep metric agreement

In addition to classification performance, our main outcomes of interest for sleep analysis are summarized by key sleep metrics. TST refers to the total duration of sleep across the night, WASO measures the time spent awake after initially falling asleep, SE represents the ratio of TST to time in bed, and SO is the time taken to transition from wake to the first sustained sleep period. To assess agreement between predicted and reference values, Fig. 1 shows the Bland–Altman plots for the early-fusion model across all subjects, and Fig. 2 shows the corresponding plots for the late-fusion model. Each point in the plots represents a single subject’s overnight recording, where the x-axis shows the mean of the predicted and actual values, and the y-axis shows their difference. The red horizontal line denotes the mean bias, and the upper and lower blue lines indicate the limits of agreement (mean difference ± 1.96 standard deviations). These visualizations help identify systematic bias and quantify variability in model-predicted sleep metrics relative to PSG reference values.

As shown in Fig. 1, the early fusion model displays greater dispersion but smaller overall bias across several metrics, particularly for TST and WASO, as reflected by the wider spread yet smaller mean differences in the Bland–Altman plots. In contrast, Fig. 2 shows that the late fusion model produces narrower limits of agreement and more uniform distributions across subjects. Overall, both approaches achieved comparable performance; however, the late fusion model demonstrated slightly greater consistency across evaluation metrics and attained a higher ROC–AUC score. Performance improvement through label smoothing

In highly imbalanced settings, even a small percentage of misclassified sleep windows can result in a large number of false awake predictions, which in turn distorts the representation of the minority awake class. These isolated errors often appear as brief, fragmented wake periods that are inconsistent with surrounding context. To address this issue and improve the consistency of sleep estimates, we applied a post-processing step to the late-fusion model that smooths predicted labels using temporal context. For each window, one neighboring window before and one after are considered; if both neighbors share the same state, the current label is reassigned accordingly, while ties leave it unchanged. This procedure reduces brief, inconsistent wake predictions and enhances the overall continuity of the sleep–wake profile, leading to more physiologically plausible outputs.

To assess the impact of the proposed smoothing algorithm, we re-evaluated model predictions using Bland–Altman analysis for TST, WASO, SE, SO, and F1 score. The F1 score increased from 90.75 ± 1.68 SD to 92.23 ± 1.66 SD in the late-fusion model; the ROC–AUC, and the sensitivity at a specific threshold of specificity remained unchanged, since the smoothing algorithm affected the prediction labels, not the prediction probability. As shown in Fig. 3, the application of temporal label smoothing led to improved agreement with the ground truth. For TST, the mean difference decreased to +11.18 min, indicating a reduced underestimation bias compared to the pre-smoothing result. The limits of agreement also narrowed, suggesting greater consistency across subjects. In the case of WASO, the smoothing algorithm significantly reduced the overestimation bias, with the mean difference improving to –29.59 min. For SE, smoothing reduced both dispersion and bias, indicating more stable and accurate estimation of total sleep time. In contrast, smoothing had no measurable effect on SO, as the initial transition to sleep was already stable and met the requirement of two consecutive sleep predictions. This confirms that the smoothing algorithm primarily improved the continuity of post-onset sleep by reducing fragmented or isolated false wake predictions, rather than altering the timing of sleep onset itself.Fig. 3Bland–Altman plots comparing predicted and ground-truth values for key sleep metrics across subjects using the late fusion model after applying the label-smoothing algorithm: (a) TST, (b) WASO, (c) SE, and (d) SO.

Ablation study and explainability

We conducted an ablation analysis using the late-fusion CNN–BiLSTM model with attention to further evaluate the relative importance of sensing modalities and the contribution of temporal modeling to performance, Each variant was retrained under identical hyperparameters and evaluated using five-fold subject-wise cross-validation. This analysis quantified how individual sensor modalities and architectural components influenced the overall classification performance.

Sensor-level contribution

In the late-fusion framework, feature representations from each sensor branch were passed through a trainable attention layer that learned normalized modality weights19. These weights represent the relative importance of each sensing modality during decision making and were extracted from the trained model to quantify their contributions to the final prediction. Discussion

In this study, we present a CNN–BiLSTM-based model for distinguishing between sleep and wake states using leg-worn sensor data. The model enables the derivation of clinically relevant sleep metrics such as TST, WASO, SE, and SO, which are particularly informative in populations with ADHD and other neurodevelopmental conditions frequently associated with sleep disturbances. These measures may support future efforts in long term sleep monitoring, characterization of comorbid sleep related symptoms, and clinical assessment or early intervention.

Model performance and robustness

The proposed model achieved an average ROC–AUC of 90.94% across five-fold cross-validation. At a sensitivity of 85%, the model attained a specificity of 83.36%, indicating reliable performance in distinguishing between sleep and wake segments under a class-imbalanced setting. While this demonstrates promising discrimination capability, performance variability across folds suggests that additional training data could further stabilize generalization across individuals. We acknowledge that motion artifacts, particularly in the PPG signal, could affect classification stability. To mitigate these effects, the PPG data were band-pass filtered (0.2–5 Hz) to remove baseline drift and high-frequency noise, and a quality-control layer excluded segments with low signal-to-noise ratio, inconsistent HRV, or unreliable peak detection. These steps reduced motion-related interference and improved overall model robustness. Future work may incorporate adaptive filtering20,21 or feature selection to further enhance resilience under naturalistic conditions.

Comparison with existing approaches

Several recent studies have reported state of the art approaches for binary sleep–wake classification using wearable sensor data. Banfi et al.11 introduced a lightweight convolutional network (LightCNNA) trained on raw wrist accelerometer signals, achieving 89.2% sensitivity, 92.0% specificity, and an F1 score of 90.9% on a private dataset of 81 subjects. Habib et al.12 proposed a CNN trained directly on raw PPG signals from 10 participants with sleep disordered breathing, reporting 94.2 ± 11.9% accuracy, 94.4% sensitivity, and 93.1 ± 13.8% F1 score. Motin et al.13 evaluated three classical machine learning classifiers, KNN, SVM, and RF, trained on 72 handcrafted PPG features from 10 subjects, achieving accuracies of 83.8–85.2% with sensitivities of 87.8–87.9% and specificities of 73.6–77.7%. While Banfi’s approach benefited from a moderately large dataset, both Habib and Motin’s studies were limited by small sample sizes and reliance on a single physiological signal. More recently, Almeida et al.14 developed an XGBoost based model trained on PPG and activity features from 2237 subjects, achieving 91.2% sensitivity but only 53.7% specificity, illustrating the challenge of maintaining balanced classification in large but heterogeneous datasets.

In comparison, our multimodal CNN–BiLSTM model was trained on leg-worn sensor data from 14 pediatric participants, integrating complementary signals from accelerometer, PPG, gyroscope, and temperature modalities. The model achieved a ROC–AUC of 90.94 ± 2.45, F1 score of 90.75 ± 1.68, sensitivity of 85%, and specificity of 83.36 ± 5.25, comparable to previously reported results despite using a smaller cohort. Unlike prior single modality methods, this approach leverages multimodal integration to capture both movement and physiological dynamics relevant to sleep–wake transitions. However, the relatively small dataset size limits the generalizability of these findings, and further validation across larger and more diverse populations will be essential. Phase 2 of this work will include expanded data collection and refinement of model parameters to improve cross subject robustness and deployment readiness in real-world sleep monitoring settings.

Relation to sleep staging literature

Most related work in sleep analysis focuses on multiclass staging, such as REM, N1, N2, N3, Wake, using data from healthy adults or individuals with sleep disorders22–24. While multistage labeling offers a more granular view of sleep architecture, our focus on binary sleep wake classification aligns with applications where coarse sleep structure is sufficient. In this application of overnight sleep analysis in children undergoing ADHD evaluation, detailed staging may be less essential when the focus is on capturing overall sleep continuity and disruptions through summary metrics. Existing literature also commonly relies on wrist-worn PPG sensors or clinical PSG recordings22,23,25, and often use engineered features derived from heart rate variability (HRV), signal entropy, or frequency-domain characteristics13. In contrast, we use a leg-worn multimodal sensor, which captures movement-related features potentially more relevant in pediatric populations prone to restless sleep. The model used in this research is trained directly on raw sensor signals, reducing reliance on domain specific feature engineering and helping preserve temporal and physiological patterns that might otherwise be lost during manual feature extraction. This approach may also improve adaptability across datasets with varying signal characteristics. Though our dataset is smaller than many adult-focused studies, it is uniquely representative of pediatric populations undergoing ADHD evaluation, a group often underrepresented in existing literature.

Error trends and label smoothing

When computing sleep metrics such as TST, WASO, SE, and SO from each participant’s overnight model predictions, we observed a noticeable error trend attributable to the model’s tendency to overestimate awake segments. As illustrated in the hypnogram in Fig. 5, the predictions frequently included brief, fragmented awake episodes that did not appear in the ground truth. Although the model demonstrates strong classification performance, with both sensitivity and specificity exceeding 80%, the class imbalance between sleep and awake states is approximately 5:1 on average, which results in a disproportionate number of sleep misclassifications. Specifically, even a small percentage of false wake predictions can outnumber the correctly predicted true awake instances, thereby distorting derived sleep metrics. These misclassified awake windows are often isolated and surrounded by correctly predicted sleep, suggesting they stem from model uncertainty rather than meaningful transitions. This observation forms the basis for our post-processing approach, which leverages the predictions and confidence scores of neighboring windows to correct isolated misclassifications. By incorporating temporal context, the smoothing algorithm helps reduce spurious wake segments and improve the accuracy of derived sleep parameters.

This smoothing approach markedly reduced errors across key sleep metrics. The absolute bias in total sleep time decreased from 52.39 to 11.18 min, while the bias in wake after sleep onset declined from 70.55 to 29.59 min. These improvements highlight the effectiveness of incorporating local temporal context to refine predictions and enhance the reliability of derived sleep metric estimates.

Architectural design and computational considerations

The late-fusion architecture we adopted is a design choice we made for its advantages in a multimodal context. The architecture allows the extraction of modality specific features that may otherwise be diluted in an early-fusion setup. It also provides a natural way to handle differences in sampling rates, since each subnetwork can operate on its own input scale and structure prior to fusion. However, this approach also introduces greater model complexity, both in terms of architecture and training. It can lead to higher computational cost, more demanding training requirements, and potential challenges in deployment, especially in resource-constrained environments. Although larger transformer-based architectures could potentially achieve higher accuracy, they are computationally expensive and unsuitable for low-power embedded systems While recent transformer-based frameworks such as FECT-OSA26 demonstrate advanced multimodal fusion capabilities, their high computational and memory demands may make them impractical for embedded or on-device deployment; our focus remains on optimizing lightweight architectures suitable for real-time processing under strict power and hardware constraints.

Sensor contribution

The modality-level analysis indicates that motion-derived inputs, particularly from the gyroscope and accelerometer, were the most influential in differentiating sleep and wake states. These modalities provided robust discriminative cues, suggesting that leg movement alone serves as a strong indicator of behavioral state transitions. In contrast, temperature and PPG offered supportive physiological context rather than primary classification power, consistent with the higher susceptibility of PPG to motion artifacts and the lower temporal resolution of temperature measurements. This distribution of influence mirrors the attention patterns learned by the late-fusion CNN–BiLSTM model, where motion-related channels consistently received greater weighting. Moreover, the dependence on temporal modeling emphasizes that sleep–wake discrimination relies not only on sensor diversity but also on the model’s ability to capture sequential continuity and smooth physiological transitions over time.

Limitations and future work

While the proposed approach shows promising results, it is important to acknowledge the significant dependence of such models on both the quality and availability of training data. Acquiring overnight sleep recordings, especially from participants with neurodevelopmental conditions, is logistically challenging and resource intensive. In addition to the limited availability of such datasets, the process of manual annotation also introduces further constraints. Sleep staging on short, window-level segments requires precise and consistent labeling, and human annotation is both labor intensive and susceptible to inter rater variability and error. Because our model makes predictions at the granularity of brief time windows, even minor inconsistencies in labeling can have a pronounced effect on training and evaluation.

Another important limitation of this study is that all participants were children with clinically confirmed ADHD. This population was selected intentionally, as ADHD is associated with higher rates of sleep disturbance, increased movement, and delayed sleep onset, providing a challenging and informative context for evaluating model performance. However, these same characteristics may limit the generalizability of the findings to non-ADHD or typically developing populations. Because the intended use of the RestEaze device includes broader consumer and clinical applications, future studies will focus on validating the system in larger and more diverse cohorts, including healthy adults and children, to ensure robustness and general applicability.

Handling class imbalance and data augmentation

To address the class imbalance between sleep and wake segments in this study, we employed Binary Focal Loss18, which adaptively down-weights well-classified majority (sleep) samples and emphasizes harder-to-classify minority (wake) instances. This approach mitigated imbalance effects while preserving the natural temporal distribution of sleep and wake transitions. Given these data limitations, future work may explore advanced data-level augmentation strategies to further enhance model generalization. Approaches such as stratified resampling, synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE), and generative adversarial network (GAN)-based data augmentation could be applied to enrich minority wake samples while preserving temporal and physiological structure. GANs, in particular, offer the potential to generate realistic physiological sequences that capture the statistical properties of wake periods, thereby expanding the diversity of training data and improving model robustness in underrepresented conditions.

Moreover, it is also important to recognize that the boundary between sleep and wake states is inherently ambiguous, even under ideal conditions. Despite the precision of our window level annotations and modeling efforts, there exists a natural gray area between clear sleep and wakefulness, such as periods of light sleep, brief arousals, or motionless wake, that are challenging to define with certainty. Furthermore, the awake state while in bed tends to be relatively underrepresented in most overnight recordings, compounding the difficulty of accurately detecting and evaluating these segments. These inherent ambiguities highlight the greater utility of aggregated sleep metrics, such as TST, WASO, and SE, over strict window wise classification accuracy. In this context, our post-processing approach serves as a principled method to reduce isolated misclassifications and enhance the reliability of derived metrics. Rather than enforcing overly rigid boundaries between states, it acknowledges the probabilistic nature of sleep transitions and offers a more robust representation of sleep architecture. An alternative yet equally valid framing is to redefine the task itself by modifying the labeling strategy, for instance, excluding micro-awakenings below a certain duration and using longer window sizes (e.g., 3–5 min) with stricter majority thresholds. This perspective may reduce label noise and capture more stable and clinically relevant sleep–wake patterns.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a leg-worn multimodal sensor combined with a late-fusion CNN–BiLSTM architecture can reliably distinguish sleep and wake states in pediatric ADHD populations. By integrating motion, temperature, and cardiovascular cues, the model achieves high agreement with polysomnography-derived metrics while maintaining low complexity suitable for wearable deployment. Incorporating temporal label smoothing further improves the reliability of derived sleep parameters. Future work will expand validation to larger and more diverse cohorts, enabling scalable, at-home sleep monitoring and early detection of sleep-related disturbances.

RestEaze system and dataset for sleep monitoring

In this study, we used sensor data collected from the RestEaze system27, a leg-worn wearable designed for sleep monitoring. RestEaze captures multi-modal physiological and movement data, including PPG, gyroscope, accelerometer, and temperature signals, providing a comprehensive dataset for sleep analysis. PPG is a technique that uses light to measure changes in blood volume under the skin and is commonly used to infer heart rate and variability. Gyroscopes detect angular velocity, while accelerometers measure linear acceleration. The system is specifically designed to monitor leg movements, which play a crucial role in identifying sleep fragmentation, arousals, and movement-related sleep disorders (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Overview of the data collection process using the RestEaze leg-worn sensor.

Overnight sleep data were collected from 14 children between January 2024 and February 2025 using the RestEaze system as part of a study investigating sleep-related movement patterns and sleep disruption in pediatric patients undergoing evaluation for ADHD. Participants were community-dwelling children between the ages of 5 and 18 years with a clinically confirmed ADHD diagnosis established via structured interview and/or the ADHD Rating Scale-5 (ADRS-5). The screening process included a comprehensive review of medical and psychiatric records, medication and allergy history, and, when feasible, corroboration from family members. This rigorous process helped ensure that participants did not have confounding conditions or medication histories (e.g., antipsychotic, opioid, or benzodiazepine use) that might independently contribute to leg movement during sleep.

Sensor signals were recorded from both the right and left legs to ensure comprehensive capture of leg movement and physiological activity during sleep. Signal preprocessing

Following the data collection, the sensor data undergoes a structured signal processing pipeline, as shown in Fig. 8, before being fed into the model. The process begins with filtering, where high-pass filters (cutoff: 0.2 Hz) are applied to the gyroscope, and accelerometer signals to remove baseline drift and low-frequency noise. For the PPG signal, a band-pass filter (0.2–5 Hz) is used to suppress both baseline drift and high-frequency artifacts while retaining physiologically relevant cardiac components.

Due to PPG’s susceptibility to motion artifacts, we implemented a window-level quality-control procedure before model training. Each 60-second window was evaluated using three criteria. First, a spectral SNR was computed around each detected peak using a ±0.4-second window. Signal power was defined within the cardiac band (0.2–5 Hz) and noise power outside this band. Windows were retained only if the average peak SNR exceeded 5 dB; empirical inspection confirmed that signals below this threshold contained no discernible physiological information and were dominated by noise. Second, the mean heart rate was constrained to 40–120 bpm. Third, windows with RMSSD outside 20–200 ms were rejected, indicating poor signal quality. Only windows meeting all criteria were retained. After filtering and z-score normalization, temperature readings (0.2 Hz) were aligned to the higher-frequency sensor windows (25 Hz) without resampling.

All sensors in the RestEaze device are synchronized through a shared onboard microcontroller and clock, ensuring precise temporal alignment across modalities. During validation, the data from both the PSG and RestEaze devices were tightly synchronized within milliseconds using a pseudorandom sequence generated by the RestEaze device, which was transmitted wirelessly and integrated into the PSG system. No measurable temporal drift was observed during overnight recordings, and synchronization verification was included in preprocessing to ensure consistent alignment across all modalities.

Model architecture

The windowed sensor inputs serve as the input streams to our sleep–wake classification model. For the baseline SVM model, we extracted features from four physiological signals: heart rate, gyroscope, accelerometer, and temperature. On each signal, key statistical features were computed, including minimum, mean, median, maximum, standard deviation, range, skewness, and kurtosis. In addition, for the heart rate signal, we calculated HRV features in both the time and frequency domains, such as the mean PP interval, pNN50, LF/HF ratio, etc. The extracted features are used as inputs to the SVM model. For raw input architecture, we explore both early and late-fusion strategies for modeling multimodal sleep data. As illustrated in Fig. 9, early-fusion involves concatenating all sensor streams at the input level into a single tensor, which is then processed jointly through a shared model. In contrast, late-fusion processes each modality through separate sub-networks before integrating their outputs. Given its ability to preserve modality-specific patterns and accommodate differing input characteristics, we adopt the late-fusion architecture in our proposed approach.Fig. 9Comparison of early-fusion and late-fusion strategies for multimodal sensor data integration.

Comparison of early-fusion and late-fusion strategies for multimodal sensor data integration.

An overview of the late-fusion model architecture is shown in Fig. 10. Signals from the accelerometer, gyroscope, PPG, and temperature sensors are fed into dedicated sub-models designed to capture the distinct temporal and physiological patterns present in each modality. Given the relatively higher temporal resolution and larger input dimensionality of the PPG, gyroscope, and accelerometer signals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1500 \times 3 \times 1)$$\end{document}(1500×3×1), these sub models are constructed with greater capacity. Specifically, they consists of two convolutional blocks. The first block includes two consecutive Conv2D layers with 8 and 16 filters, followed by a max-pooling operation. The second block comprises a single Conv2D layer with 32 filters and a subsequent max-pooling layer. This configuration allows the model to progressively extract increasingly abstract features while reducing temporal dimensionality. These are followed by BiLSTM layers and a GlobalMaxPooling operation to summarize temporal dynamics across the window. In contrast, the temperature input has a smaller size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(12 \times 1 \times 1)$$\end{document}(12×1×1) and a single channel. Accordingly, its sub-model uses a lightweight Conv1D-based architecture with two convolutional layers containing 8 and 16 filters, respectively, followed by BiLSTM of 32 units each direction and a global max pooling layer. This differentiation in model complexity allows each sub-network to be appropriately tailored to the nature of the input signal. The outputs of all sub-models are concatenated to form a unified latent representation, which is passed through fully connected layers of 64 units to produce the final binary sleep–wake classification.

Regularization and overfitting control

To minimize overfitting given the limited dataset size, several regularization strategies were applied throughout the network. We used the batch size of 32. Each convolutional block included Batch Normalization, LeakyReLU activation, and Dropout layers (with dropout rates between 0.2 and 0.3) to enhance generalization and prevent co-adaptation of features. A Global Max Pooling layer was used to reduce model complexity prior to the dense layers. Training employed early stopping based on validation loss to prevent overtraining. All hyperparameters, including dropout rate, learning rate, and number of filters, were tuned exclusively within the training folds of the cross-validation procedure to ensure no information leakage from the test data.Fig. 10Multisensor late-fusion model architecture for sleep state classification. Model training and evaluation

We used five-fold cross-validation with subject-level grouping: all data from each subject, including both legs and all recording windows, were kept exclusively in a single fold to avoid any data leakage. This ensures the model was never trained or validated on overlapping or temporally adjacent data from the same participant.

Neighborhood majority voting-based label smoothing

To enhance temporal consistency and reduce spurious state transitions, we implemented a neighborhood majority voting-based label smoothing method (Algorithm 1). For each prediction window (60 seconds), the algorithm evaluates the predicted labels and associated probabilities of neighboring windows to refine transient predictions. In our implementation, we use one neighboring window before and one after the current window. If both neighboring windows are classified as sleep, and the current window is predicted as awake with a confidence exceeding a dynamic threshold, the label is reassigned to sleep. Conversely, if both neighboring windows are classified as awake, and the current window is predicted as sleep, the label is reassigned to awake. When the number of sleep and awake neighbors is equal, no change is made to preserve the original prediction. We empirically found that using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=1$$\end{document}n=1 (one window on each side) provides an effective balance between smoothing and responsiveness to short physiological transitions. This localized smoothing procedure mitigates short, noise-induced fluctuations while maintaining genuine physiological transitions. Because the algorithm incorporates one future window, it introduces a maximum latency of 1 window shift (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{shift}$$\end{document}Δshift = 30 seconds). This modest delay is acceptable for overnight sleep monitoring applications, where minute-level responsiveness is sufficient and strict real-time immediacy is not required.

To ensure that the smoothing process did not obscure physiologically meaningful fragmentation, we visually examined the hypnograms of ADHD participants before and after smoothing. The characteristic short, recurrent awakenings typical of ADHD remained preserved, while mostly isolated, single-window misclassifications were corrected. Because the algorithm operates over a narrow temporal context, it effectively mitigates spurious transitions without obscuring genuine sleep fragmentation.
