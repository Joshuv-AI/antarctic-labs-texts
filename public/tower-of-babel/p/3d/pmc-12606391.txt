Developing and Refining a Knowledge Assessment Instrument for Individuals Involved in Managing Occupational Heat Stress: Paper A
Authors: Emily J. Tetzlaff, Bruce E. Oddson, Kristina‐Marie T. Janetos, Robert D. Meade, Glen P. Kenny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionOccupational heat stress poses a critical threat to workers' health, safety, and productivity. To effectively manage this hazard, occupational health and safety (OHS) representatives must comprehensively understand heat stress, heat‐associated injury and disease outcomes, and heat mitigation measures to protect workers. We developed the Human and Environmental Physiology Research Unit‐Occupational Heat Stress Knowledge Assessment Test (HEPRU‐OHSKAT) as an initial step toward facilitating research in this area.MethodThe HEPRU‐OHSKAT was developed using a mixed‐methods approach. Preliminary items were developed after reviewing the academic and gray literature. A standardized content validity assessment was conducted with heat stress and OHS experts (n = 9); a trial distribution to refine and test feasibility was performed with an internal pilot group (n = 18). The instrument was then distributed to individuals involved with OHS (n = 216) and members of the general working population (n = 101) in Ontario, Canada (n = 317). Item analysis and item response theory modelling were used to refine the scale and scope of the instrument.ResultsThirty‐two items were developed for the preliminary instrument. Following expert consultation, a review of content validity, and the internal pilot, 31 items were retained in four subscales: General Heat Stress Knowledge (n = 8), Recognizing the Signs and Symptoms of Heat Stress and First Aid (n = 7), Exposure Limits and Heat Monitoring Practices (n= 13), and Workplace Controls for Heat (n = 3). Following item selection, the broader instrument was reduced to 20 items grouped into one scale.ConclusionsThe HEPRU‐OHSKAT is the first instrument to assess knowledge of heat stress among those responsible for OHS. The instrument showed good reliability and internal consistency across knowledge categories.Practical ApplicationsThe instrument can help OHS representatives better evaluate knowledge of, and manage training about, heat stress.

Introduction

Due to rising global temperatures [1, 2], workers in various sectors are increasingly exposed to high heat conditions. Heat‐exposed workers are at an elevated risk of pathophysiological conditions (e.g., heat stroke, acute kidney injury, adverse cardiovascular events) [3, 4, 5, 6], and potentially death [7]. Further, heat‐exposed workers are also at risk for impaired work performance and cognitive function [3, 4, 8], which in turn lead to an increased risk of work‐related injuries [3, 4, 9, 10]. The International Labour Organization recently estimated that among the almost 2.4 billion workers exposed to excessive heat (~70% of the global workforce), there are nearly 23 million heat‐related injuries, 19,000 fatalities, and 2 million disability‐adjusted life years lost annually [7]. Developing innovative approaches to prevent injuries and diseases caused by heat exposure in the workplace is critical.

Numerous safety and labour boards worldwide have implemented, or are in the process of developing, regulatory changes to address occupational heat stress. For instance, the Biden‐Harris administration in the United States had initiated an interagency effort to create a nationally mandated heat stress standard to protect both outdoor and indoor workers [11]. Although progress on the federal heat standard is still under development, several US states, including Minnesota, Washington, California, and New Mexico, have already established heat regulations to safeguard workers' health [12]. Similarly, national occupational health and safety boards in Asia (e.g., China and Thailand), Africa (e.g., Gabon, Mozambique, Cameroon, and South Africa), Central and South America (e.g., Costa Rica and Brazil), Europe (e.g., Germany, Spain, and Cyprus), and the Middle East (e.g., Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, United Arab Emirates, Oman, and Saudi Arabia), have also made strides in enacting and improving longstanding heat regulations [7, 13]. In Canada, provincial safety ministries are also working to enhance heat stress protections, including the Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development (MLITSD) in Ontario, which has proposed creating a heat‐specific regulation under the Occupational Health and Safety Act [14] to establish a more comprehensive approach to managing heat stress in the workplace.

For a heat regulation to be effective, the individuals responsible for ensuring implementation and compliance, typically occupational health and safety (OHS) personnel and front‐line workers, must maintain a working knowledge and understanding of heat stress. Furthermore, OHS personnel need to be able to translate the regulation within the context of their workplace and develop and implement effective heat stress management programs, policies, and controls to mitigate adverse effects on their workforce. However, there is currently no available resource to assess OHS personnel's knowledge of occupational heat stress, associated injuries and diseases, and mitigation measures to protect worker safety. Although some studies have assessed basic knowledge in this area, they have typically been conducted among specific working populations (e.g., general laborers [15], construction workers [16]) and have been small in scope (n = 10 [15], n = 89 [16]). Further, the studies to date have been conducted (and sometimes subsequently validated) within specific geographic regions (Iran [15], Egypt [16]) that are typically arid/semi‐arid to desert climate zones, which may affect their applicability in more temperate climate zones such as Canada and the US).

In the absence of an instrument that assesses knowledge of the core elements of occupational heat stress management, our understanding of the capacity of OHS personnel to implement new management policies and regulations remains limited. Therefore, this study was an initial, exploratory step towards the development, piloting, and refinement of an instrument that measures knowledge relevant to individuals involved in managing and controlling occupational heat stress. To develop and refine the proposed instrument for subsequent validation while considering the recent movement toward heat‐specific regulation in the province of Ontario, Canada, we chose to trial the instrument in this region.

Methods

The study was approved by the University of Ottawa Research Ethics Board (REB#H‐11‐22‐8660) and was conducted in two phases. In the first part, we developed the Human and Environmental Physiology Research Unit‐Occupational Heat Stress Knowledge Assessment Test (HEPRU‐OHSKAT) at the University of Ottawa (Ottawa, Canada) (Paper A). In the second part, we distributed the instrument to OHS personnel and workers in Ontario to pilot the instrument for subsequent refinement (see companion Paper B).

Instrument Development

Iteration 1: Academic and Gray Literature Review

To develop the initial iteration of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT, a review of current academic literature related to OHS and heat stress was conducted, and existing instruments for assessing the heat stress knowledge of the public [17, 18, 19] and front‐line workers were considered [16, 20]. This included our own recent work, which systematically reviewed and analyzed the content of over 500 occupational heat stress guidance documents from 155 safety agencies, associations, and labour ministries across Canada that are available online [21]. The recent proposal by Ontario MLITSD was also examined to ensure that the details of the regulatory proposal were thoroughly captured.

The literature review identified four core competency areas essential for effectively managing occupational heat stress. These categories addressed: (A) heat stress and associated physiological responses, (B) signs and symptoms of heat strain along with appropriate first aid measures, (C) exposure limits and monitoring practices, and (D) heat‐mitigation controls aimed at protecting worker health and safety. Each category encompasses several related constructs (Table 1). The first iteration of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT included 32 items (q) (Figure 1).

Iteration 2: Expert Review for Content Validity

The initial HEPRU‐OHSKAT was evaluated by an ad hoc expert panel to assess its content validity and identify opportunities for refinement. The expert reviewers were invited based on their knowledge of OHS, human thermoregulation, and occupational medicine. Using a standardized evaluation form (Supporting Information: File 1A), thirty‐two experts were invited to review the HEPRU‐OHSKAT and determine whether the items adequately assessed the required knowledge necessary for the management of occupational heat stress (i.e., are the items relevant to occupational heat stress management?) and whether the items sufficiently covered the scope of content that an individual involved in heat stress management should understand (i.e., are all essential topics related to occupational heat stress management included in the instrument?). The reviewers were also requested to evaluate the relevance of each item on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = very irrelevant, 2 = irrelevant, 3 = relevant, 4 = very relevant). The content validity index (CVI) was subsequently calculated as the proportion of raters scoring each item as 3 or 4. A CVI above 0.8 was considered adequate [22, 23]. Additional comments concerning the accuracy of the statements and correct responses (i.e., true or false), along with readability, clarity, and appropriateness, were also recorded (e.g., do you find this item confusing or misleading?) (Supporting Information: Appendix 1A). The reviewers were given approximately 1 month to respond (between April 2nd and April 26th, 2024, inclusively).

Responses were received from 9 of the 32 invited experts (28%) (see Acknowledgments). Based on the content validity assessment, 31 items scored above 0.8 CVI (1.0: n = 28, 0.89: n = 3), indicating their acceptability for inclusion. One item (Part B: q5) scored 0.67 and was removed from the instrument. Regarding accuracy, complete agreement existed on the correct response for most items (n = 20). None of the experts indicated disagreement with any of the responses; however, some were uncertain about the accuracy of answers for a few items (Part A: q3, q6; Part B: q4, q6, q7, q8; Part C: q2, q4, q5, q7, q8; Part D: q1), which accounted for only 5% of the total votes (n = 288). Five additional items were added based on open feedback and reviewer recommendations, and wording modifications were made. The second iteration of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT included 36 items (Figure 1).

Iteration 3: Internal Pilot for Feasibility

The HEPRU‐OHSKAT was recreated using SurveyMonkey's online platform (San Mateo, California, USA). An internal pilot study was conducted among 18 undergraduate, graduate, and post‐doctoral fellows at the HEPRU to determine the average time required to complete the assessment, test the online system, and gather additional feedback between May 2 and May 8, 2024, inclusively. The average completion time was 30 min. Five items that were deemed redundant were removed. Other minor modifications were made based on respondent feedback (e.g., adjusting settings to allow for forward and backward navigation and reorganizing dropdown response options to randomize further). As a result of these adjustments, the third iteration of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT included 31 items (Figure 1).

Instrument Description

The HEPRU‐OHSKAT instrument presents items in various formats, including multiple choice, multi‐select, true or false, matching, and reordering. Most items are close‐ended (with pre‐selected responses) except for one open‐ended, written response based on a presented case study. A copy of the complete HEPRU‐OHSKAT can be found in Supporting Information: File 1B. The following subsections outline the four knowledge categories and their associated items.

Part A: General Heat Stress Knowledge

The first section is composed of eight items used to assess the respondent's general knowledge of heat stress. The items focus on testing knowledge of thermoregulation, mechanisms of heat exchange, sources of heat stress in the workplace, seasonality of risk, and individual factors that may place certain workers at greater risk (e.g., age and health).

Part B: Knowing the Signs and Symptoms of Heat Stress and First Aid

This section contains seven items to assess the respondent's understanding of the signs and symptoms of heat‐related illnesses and first aid. These items address heat strain, individual variability in symptom presentation, and the severity of heat‐related illnesses. Additionally, items specifically focus on heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and heat‐related accidents and injuries that may occur in the workplace were included.

Part C: Exposure Limits and Heat Monitoring Practices

This section consists of 13 items to evaluate the respondents' understanding of heat stress exposure limits and monitoring strategies. It emphasizes environmental factors that should be monitored to effectively assess heat stress, along with items on standard measures of heat exposure (i.e., wet bulb globe temperature), testing instruments, acclimatization considerations, and workload (i.e., work effort/intensity). Items about the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) heat stress management guidelines are also included to assess respondents' comprehension of the necessary assumptions for effectively implementing this strategy and to illustrate using the associated reference tables.

Part D: Workplace Controls for Heat

The final category includes three items to assess respondents' awareness of methods and procedures for controlling heat exposure in their workplaces. This section references the hierarchy of controls and the order of effectiveness for reducing heat exposure hazards. It also tests respondents' understanding of specific engineering and administrative controls that can be implemented to reduce the duration, frequency, or intensity of heat exposure experienced by workers performing their duties in hot environments.

Distribution to the Target Population

A purposive sample of external participants was recruited to pilot and assess the HEPRU‐OHSKAT. This sample included individuals aged 18 and older who are involved in occupational heat stress management and those representing the general working population. For OHS, the inclusion criteria specified that participants must be employed or engaged in OHS roles, such as industrial hygienists, health and safety specialists, occupational nurses or physicians, and representatives from joint health and safety committees. Individuals working in any workplace governed by the Ontario Occupational Health and Safety Act and experiencing heat stress (e.g., mining, construction, agriculture, manufacturing, laundries, and bakeries) were considered eligible. Individuals employed by federally regulated industries, including airlines, banks, federal civil services, post offices, radio and television stations, interprovincial railways, and interprovincial trucking, were not eligible to participate. For members of the public, the inclusion criteria mandated that they be currently employed at any workplace governed by the Ontario Occupational Health and Safety Act. Individuals employed in federally regulated industries and those not presently in the formal workforce, such as retirees, were also ineligible to participate.

For OHS and public respondents, participants were recruited through email distribution and online promotional strategies (i.e., LinkedIn, X [formerly known as Twitter], HEPRU website). All ministry‐funded health and safety associations (n = 6; e.g., Public Services Health & Safety Association) and research center partners within Ontario's health and safety system (n = 6; Center for Research in Occupational Safety and Health) were contacted to seek support for recruitment. In addition, various national safety groups (n = 7, e.g., Board of Canadian Registered Safety Professionals) and magazines (n = 2, e.g., Canadian Occupational Safety Magazine) were also approached for support in online promotion and distribution via email networks to enhance outreach (n = 21).

The self‐administered instrument was available on SurveyMonkey from May 25 to July 31, 2024. On the landing page of the online platform, participants provided their informed consent, affirming that they had read and understood the background information on the study and consented to participate.

Analysis

Descriptives

The number of respondents and percentage of the total sample were calculated based on individual response rates to each item. We noted that some respondents had zero scores (i.e., some participants completed the demographic section but not the actual knowledge‐based items; n = 79) and removed these participants from the data set. Thus, 238 respondents were included in the analysis (Figure 2).

Scoring

The HEPRU‐OHSKAT is scored using a partial‐credit approach, with each correct answer awarded 1 point, each “I do not know” answer awarded 0.5 points, and each incorrect answer awarded 0 points. This scoring system was selected to reduce the effects of random guessing and encourage metacognitive accuracy, which is a respondent's ability to assess the limits of their own knowledge [24]. This distinction is especially important in OHS contexts, where misjudged confidence can have significant implications for worker safety. The partial‐credit approach has precedent in the knowledge assessment literature within various domains [25, 26] to differentiate between true knowledge, lack of knowledge and misinformation [27]. Research suggests that such methods can reduce random guessing, improve score validity and provide a more nuanced picture of respondent competence [28]. While it is possible that partial credit could be misused to inflate scores compared to traditional dichotomous scoring or formula scoring methods that penalize incorrect answers, the trade‐off is acceptable when the goal is to promote learning and self‐awareness rather than simply differentiate between high and low scores [27]. We therefore opted for this approach as it is well‐aligned with the study's goal of evaluating both factual knowledge and the respondents' awareness of their own knowledge limitations.

Scores were summed across items within each knowledge category. Potential category scores ranged from 0 to 13 for General Heat Stress Knowledge, 0 to 32 for Signs and Symptoms of Heat Stress and First Aid, 0 to 40 for Exposure Limits and Heat Monitoring Practices, and 0 to 15 for Workplace Controls for Heat. The cumulative score across categories ranged from 0 to 100. Full scoring instructions are provided in Supporting Information: File 1C. Note that after principal component factor analysis item reduction (see Dimensionality of the Instrument in the Results), the full instrument was reduced to a single 20‐item scale with a cumulative score range of 0–58.

Item Analysis

All data were analyzed using Stata statistical software (Version 17) [29]. Supporting Information: File 1D presents all descriptive results. For reporting purposes, the item numbers were retained from the original instrument layout; however, adjusted numbering is presented for the revised instrument.

Item Characteristics

Response frequencies, means, and standard deviations were screened [30]. The frequency with which incorrect options were chosen was assessed for multiple‐choice and true–false items. Options selected by less than 5% of participants were deemed "nonfunctional distractors” and identified for modification [31, 32].

Readability

To assess readability, we used the online tool WebFX (https://www.webfx.com/tools/read-able/#enter-text-tab). The readability score for the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Flesch‐Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) [33], Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) [34], Gunning‐Fog Index (GFI) [35], and Automated Readability Index (ARI) [36], and text statistics, including sentences, words, complex words, percentage of complex words, average words per sentence and average syllables per word [37] were recorded.

Item Difficulty and Discrimination

Item difficulty and discrimination were assessed using a graded response model (IRT). Items showing poor discrimination or having a poor fit for their difficulty parameter were considered for removal or alteration [31, 38]. However, as individual items have utility beyond their contribution to the total knowledge score (i.e., observing that responses to an item are consistently poor provides valuable information about gaps in knowledge), we employed pragmatic decision‐making before removing or modifying any items [39].

Internal Consistency

Item‐to‐total correlations and Cronbach's coefficient alpha (α) were employed to assess internal consistency. Item‐total correlation refers to the correlation of an item with the rest of the scale, excluding that item. A correlation below 0.20 was deemed poor; Cronbach's α evaluates inter‐item consistency and ranges from 0.0 to 1.0. If the exclusion of an item significantly raises Cronbach's α, then omitting that item enhances the scale's homogeneity, which is advantageous. Consequently, it is recommended that Cronbach's α be above 0.70 but not exceed 0.90.

Construct Validity

Construct validity was assessed by performing known‐group comparisons; that is, by statistically comparing mean scores of groups that we expect to do well with mean scores from participants whose knowledge should not be as high [40]. We classified the groups as OHS personnel (including full‐time and part‐time OHS professionals, practitioners, or workers currently or previously engaged in OHS) (Group A) and the general working population (those not involved in any capacity in OHS) (Group B). This classification aided in determining whether the instrument measured the intended construct [31].

Factor Analysis

Principal component factor analysis was conducted to determine whether the underlying factors identified statistically within the collected data aligned with the theoretical factors we intended to measure using the HEPRU‐OHSKAT. The four constructs included (A) general heat stress knowledge, (B) signs and symptoms of heat stress and first aid, (C) exposure limits and heat monitoring practices, and (D) workplace controls for heat.

Results

Descriptives

A total of 331 individuals accessed the HEPRU‐OHSKAT link; however, 8 (2%) did not consent to participate in the study and were automatically exited from the online assessment. Additionally, six responses were removed as they were submitted from individuals outside Ontario. Therefore, 317 eligible respondents participated. Most respondents took approximately 30 min to complete the HEPRU‐OHSKAT. The average completion rate was 55% (n = 182 incomplete submissions); 25% scored zero (n = 79; these individuals may have completed the demographic section but no scorable items) (Figure 2). The maximum number of responses recorded for a given item was 238 responses (OHS: n = 161, GWP: n = 77), and the lowest response rate was 177 responses (OHS: n = 124, GWP: n = 53).

Readability

The readability of the instrument varied substantially between items from grade one to university‐level text. There were 86 words per item on average, with 15.5 complex terms (17%). Based on all scales, the instrument was considered above the recommended grade 6 reading level. The FRE scale was 54.9, the FKGL was 8.8, the GFI was 11.0, the SMOG was 8.5, and the ARI score was 8.0.

Item Difficulty

The instrument items reflected a high difficulty level (Table 2). In Part A, item q1 was noted for having an exceptionally low score (< 0.3). In Part B, items q2a, q2d, and q6 were low scoring. Items q4c, q8, and q13b from Part C also had low scores. Similarly, items q1a, q4d, q5e, q2b, q2c, and q3 from Part D were identified as low scoring.

Item Discrimination

The instrument did not perform as expected in distinguishing between groups based on presumed competence (i.e., being OHS professionals vs. other workers) (Table 3). Several individuals identified as OHS professionals scored at or near zero on the HEPRU‐OHSKAT, and both OHS role and year of experience explained only minimal variance in total scores. These findings suggest that the current version of the instrument may not adequately assess professional‐level competence in its present form.

A follow‐up analysis was conducted to identify whether any sub‐categories of the instrument (Part A–D) were more effective at distinguishing between groups. Of the four subscales, items in Part D: Workplace Controls for Heat were the only items that showed consistent score differences between OHS professionals and other workers, indicating potential utility for differentiating between groups. The other subscales (Part A–C) did not demonstrate significant discriminatory power. These results suggest there may be a need for further refinement of item content and difficulty to improve the construct validity of the instrument.

After removing the identified poor‐performing items and subjects with a total score equal to zero, the revised measure, with 45 items, had an alpha of 0.94 (Supporting Information: Appendix 1B). Further, an effect of the OHS role was noted (based on OHS involvement reported in Safety Specific Background: q16), but not expertise (based on Canadian or international safety‐related certifications or designations reported in Safety Specific Background: q17), such that people with an OHS role tended to get higher scores. The difference between OHS and non‐OHS is significant but overlaps considerably (Table 3).

Dimensionality of the Instrument

The instrument items exhibit a clear unidimensional factor structure under principal component analysis (PCA). The first component accounts for 53% of the variance, with a potential second factor explaining about 6% and a third at 3%. Items that may potentially load on the second and third factors include q3c, q3d, q4, q5, and q6 from Part A, and q1a, q1c, q1d, and q1e from Part D. However, none of these items load even half of their potential variance on such a factor. Following the removal of the poor‐performing items, it was again determined through factor analysis that the results strongly suggest a single‐factor model, with the first factor covering 46% of the total variance and a potential second factor covering about 7% (Supporting Information: Appendix 1C). These findings indicate that although the test was initially designed to group questions into four distinct categories (General Heat Stress Knowledge, Signs and Symptoms of Heat Stress and First Aid, Exposure Limits and Heat Monitoring Practices, and Workplace Controls for Heat), the items function psychometrically as one latent construct. That is, the four categories are not statistically distinct and only represent conceptual content groups. This indicates that there are no significant subtests and that reporting anything less than the total score is unnecessary. We therefore removed the subgroups from the revised instrument and scoring (Table 4).

Selection of Items

The IRT Graded Response Model (GRM) was used to assess the difficulty and discrimination of the items by analyzing respondent scores across items to estimate their underlying ability. The instrument estimates a very low total score for individuals with relatively weak ability but very high scores for those with greater ability, indicating that the HEPRU‐OHSKAT is appropriate. However, it also suggests that the scores are likely binary (i.e., a respondent will receive either a high or low score, with few individuals scoring in between) and that the instrument may lack sensitivity to expertise. After removing all respondents with total scores below 11, a re‐estimation was performed. The re‐estimated results showed a more reasonable instrument profile; however, mean scores were still not different enough to use the OHS group to ensure validity. This suggests that our analyses are significantly weakened by including too many respondents without knowledge of heat stress. Supporting Information: Appendix 1C presents the items sorted by discrimination capacity for participants with a total score > 10. Based on these findings, we removed items where the discrimination of high and low knowledge was poor, or the difficulty made it unlikely that the item added value to the instrument. Discussion

The initial aim of this study was to create an instrument capable of assessing the competence of individuals involved in the management of occupational heat stress. However, the results indicate that the current version of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT is not yet suitable for formally assessing the competence of existing OHS professionals. Although the items selected demonstrated good face and content validity through the construction of the instrument and expert review, respondents scored much lower than expected across all four knowledge categories. Identifying this unanticipated knowledge gap among individuals responsible for managing occupational heat stress presents a key finding that underscores the urgent need for targeted training and resources (explored further in Paper B). Rather than suggesting that the individual items are inappropriate, these findings indicate that the HEPRU‐OHSKAT, as an instrument, in its current form, may not be effective for evaluating professional competence or for use in high‐stakes settings such as certification or compliance audits. Instead, the HEPRU‐OHSKAT is more appropriately used as a diagnostic and developmental tool, helping organizations identify training needs, raise awareness, and build capacity in occupational heat stress management. As such, this study should be viewed as an initial development phase, laying the foundation for future validation studies that will assess the instrument's suitability for broader applications.

It is also important to note that the instrument cannot be fully demonstrated to have external validity due to limitations of the sample. In particular, the external expertise of the participants was not established, given the significant difference between observed scores and the standards set by experts. However, our item analysis suggested that the instrument items can differentiate between weak and strong underlying knowledge. Nonetheless, the instrument's ability to demonstrate construct validity was limited. Total scores showed weak associations with OHS role and years of experience, and three of the four knowledge subdomains failed to distinguish between OHS professionals and members of the general working population. Only the Part D: Workplace Controls subscale demonstrated the expected discrimination. This finding suggests that portions of the instrument may not yet sufficiently capture the depth of knowledge expected among experienced OHS professionals and should be refined in future validation efforts. As a result, we propose that future iterations remove some items with weak discrimination or inappropriate difficulty. This revision likely improved the measure (i.e., shorter and better responsiveness). That said, the sample participants came from 17 industries in Ontario, and some of them were OHS professionals responsible for evaluating risk from heat stress. Therefore, this raises concerns about their perception of the instrument's difficulty. In contrast, the content experts consulted during instrument development, who admittedly did not write the HEPRU‐OHSKAT under time pressure or the same constraints, all know the answers and would be considered to have perfect scores. Although these data cannot be used for item analysis, they suggest that the participant sample indicates a general lack of expertise in this knowledge category, even among those who should be competent (see Paper B).

Limitations and Future Studies

This study has limitations that underscore the necessity for further investigations. First, as this study details the initial development and distribution of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT, subsequent analysis will be required to validate the revised knowledge assessment instrument. This validation will ensure that the final version is optimized for user ease, compliance in completing all items, and reflection of the most essential heat stress competency items.

Second, although we endeavored to ensure validity throughout the instrument's design, we did not assess test‐retest reliability in this study. Our reliability analysis was limited to internal consistency, as measured by Cronbach's α, and did not include an evaluation of the instrument's temporal stability over time. The reported reliability should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Establishing test‐retest reliability will be a necessary component of future validation work. This could involve administering the instrument to the same group of respondents after a 1–2‐week interval and assessing stability using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs; with values of ≥ 0.75 generally considered indicative of good reliability) [41]. This step will be critical for confirming the consistency of the HEPRU‐OHSKAT over time and strengthening its utility in both research and applied settings.

Third, to ensure that all respondents were governed by the same OHS legislation when assessing the instrument, the pilot population was restricted to the province of Ontario. Therefore, although the instrument was designed broadly based on international peer‐reviewed literature, it may not yet be applicable globally due to variations between jurisdictions with differing OHS legislation, heat stress regulations, or standards. Future studies should consider expanding the recruitment efforts to facilitate interprovincial and international validation and comparisons.

Lastly, considering ongoing research into the physiological impacts of occupational heat stress and changes in heat management standards and guidelines due to policy reform, the HEPRU‐OHSKAT will require regular revision to ensure content accuracy and revalidation if any changes are made.

Conclusion

The HEPRU‐OHSKAT was developed through a multi‐step process that included a review of both academic and gray literature, consultations with experts in heat stress and OHS, and a standardized assessment of content validity alongside internal piloting. As the first step towards developing an instrument designed to evaluate knowledge of heat stress among individuals involved in OHS, the HEPRU‐OHSKAT has demonstrated reliability and internal consistency across its four knowledge categories. Following subsequent revisions and validation, the application of this tool as a standardized instrument will help inform future training and education needs, thereby better supporting on‐site representatives in implementing heat management controls at their workplaces. As rising temperatures continue to affect workers across various sectors, continuing to pursue the development of this instrument could be valuable for identifying knowledge gaps among those responsible for health protection. Safety ministries, safety agencies and associations, and/or individual worksites could integrate the HEPRU‐OHSKAT within their policies and practices.
