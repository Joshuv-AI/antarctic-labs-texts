A Bayesian genomic-enhanced precision critical care early warning model for septic shock outcomes
Authors: Amado Alejandro Báez, Naomi Reyes, Raghavan Raju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Septic shock remains a significant contributor to mortality, with high patient variability in treatment response. Precision medicine offers promise in addressing this complexity. This study aimed to develop a Bayesian genomic-enhanced precision critical care clinical decision/probability model to predict outcomes in septic shock patients.Methods:Utilizing Bayesian modeling, we integrated clinical scoring systems (e.g., the Miami sepsis score and point of care lactate) with genomic data. Sensitivity and specificity from prior studies were incorporated into the model. The effectiveness of a genomic-based approach versus a Bayesian model was evaluated using paired t-tests with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.Results:The mean difference between the GENOMIC and Bayesian groups was −19.8900, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from −39.7944 to 0.0144. The combined Bayesian model, integrating clinical, lactate, and genomic data outperformed standalone genomic models. Mean score for the Bayesian model was 92.75 (standard deviation [SD] = 5.1881), significantly higher than the genomic model’s 72.86 (SD = 15.4196) (P < 0.05). This suggests that the Bayesian model offers more consistent performance in predicting septic shock outcomes and a statistically significant improvement in accuracy. These findings underscore the Bayesian framework’s potential for real-time decision-making in precision critical care.Conclusion:A Bayesian genomic-enhanced model offers a promising pathway for improving septic shock outcomes. Future studies with larger datasets and further model refinement are warranted to validate its potential and explore its broader application in critical care.

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis, defined as a dysregulated host response to infection leading to life-threatening organ dysfunction, remains a significant global health concern, affecting approximately 50 million people annually.[12] Among its most severe manifestations is septic shock, characterized by profound circulatory and metabolic abnormalities that result in high mortality rates. Despite advancements in clinical guidelines and standardized protocols, septic shock continues to challenge healthcare systems worldwide due to its complexity and heterogeneity in patient response.

Septic shock is defined clinically by persistent hypotension requiring vasopressor therapy to maintain a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mmHg and serum lactate levels above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.[1] These hemodynamic and metabolic markers, although widely used, fail to account for the significant variability among patients in treatment response and outcomes. This variability underscores the critical need for precision medicine approaches that can tailor interventions to the unique characteristics of each patient.[3]

Precision medicine has emerged as a transformative approach to address the challenges of diseases with complex pathophysiology, such as septic shock. By integrating clinical, genomic, and phenotypic data, precision medicine allows for the identification of distinct patient subgroups, facilitating targeted therapies and more accurate prognostic models. Recent advancements in molecular biology and bioinformatics have paved the way for incorporating genomic insights into critical care, enabling clinicians to move beyond traditional “one-size-fits-all” approaches to a more nuanced, individualized treatment paradigm.[45]

Genomic data have revealed significant variability in septic shock outcomes, including genetic predispositions that influence susceptibility, severity, and recovery. Studies have demonstrated the association between single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and sepsis outcomes, highlighting the potential for genomic markers to enhance risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.[6] Furthermore, the integration of multiomics data, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, provides a comprehensive view of the underlying biological mechanisms driving septic shock, opening new avenues for personalized medicine.[78]

Despite these advancements, the practical application of genomic data in clinical decision-making remains limited. Current models often fail to incorporate dynamic, real-time data from multiple sources, such as clinical physiologic variables and genomic markers. This limitation creates a gap in leveraging the full potential of precision medicine for septic shock management. Bayesian statistical frameworks, which allow for the integration of prior knowledge with new evidence, offer a promising solution. By continuously updating probabilities based on incoming data, Bayesian models provide a dynamic and adaptable approach to risk assessment and decision-making in complex clinical scenarios.[910111213]

This study aims to address these gaps by developing a Bayesian genomic-enhanced precision critical care clinical decision/probability model for predicting septic shock outcomes. By integrating physiologic, laboratory, and genomic data into a Bayesian framework, the model seeks to improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic precision. The findings of this research have the potential to not only transform septic shock management but also advance the broader field of precision critical care by demonstrating the utility of real-time, data-driven decision-making tools.

Recognizing clinical phenotypes is also crucial for tailoring treatments in sepsis. Researchers have identified distinct phenotypes that correlate with biomarkers and outcomes, helping guide more precise treatments.[1415161718] Hemodynamic phenotyping and the use of biomarkers have further refined this personalized approach. Immunological dysfunction plays a key role in sepsis, and ongoing research into immunomodulatory and metabolomic therapies holds promise for targeted interventions.[19]

Data-driven approaches and multiomics integration are revolutionizing sepsis treatment through precision medicine. The availability of vast data in intensive care units (ICUs), combined with advancements in data science, provides an opportunity to personalize treatment and improve patient outcomes. The future of sepsis management lies in combining clinical phenotyping, biomarker usage, and genomic insights, making precision medicine a cornerstone of critical care.

A recent study developed a genetic score using leading SNPs from 15 loci associated with early mortality, enhancing predictive power when combined with clinical covariates [Table 1].[17] The analysis revealed that individuals carrying 4 or more risk alleles had a 12.33-fold increased risk of death compared to those with no risk alleles (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 12.33, 95% confidence interval [CI 5.19–31.82]).

METHODS

This study employed a Bayesian mathematical framework to integrate clinical scoring systems, laboratory parameters, and genomic data for predicting septic shock outcomes. The 2023 surviving sepsis campaign criteria were used in the definition of “sepsis.”[1] The model was designed to dynamically adjust based on new data inputs, leveraging the strength of Bayesian principles to improve diagnostic accuracy and individualize therapeutic decision-making.

Descriptive statistics and standard deviations (SDs) were used to present group characteristics. Posttest probability (post) was obtained from a sequential Bayesian statistical modeling integrating low, intermediate, and high pretest for the GENOMIC score, the Miami sepsis score (MSS), and likelihood ratios (LRs) for point-of-care lactate [Table 2]. Relative diagnostic gain (RDG) and absolute diagnostic gain (ADG) were calculated based on the differences deducted from pretest and posttest probabilities (ADG = Post – Pre), (RDG = 100 × ADG/Pre). SPSS for Windows version 20.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for analysis and modeling. This was an ethics committee except study.

The MSS was developed by integrating fever, respiratory rate, and shock index. Fever is defined as core body temperature of ≥38°C (100.4°F) and is the only obligate variable allotted with 1 point. Shock index ≥0.7 is given 2 points, and a respiratory rate ≥22 breaths/min is given 1 point for a total maximum score of 4 points. The patient population was stratified based on the MSS: 1 point is low risk, 2 points is moderate risk, and 3–4 points is high risk. The percentage risk was obtained based on MSS and used as pretest probability.

Data sources

The data for this study were derived from validated clinical cohorts, including the MSS, a previously developed genomic sepsis score,[17] and pooled meta-analyses of lactate levels.

MSS served as the foundation for integrating clinical and physiologic data [Figure 1].

Miami sepsis score details

Pretest probability and Bayesian integration

The MSS was used to calculate pretest probabilities for septic shock, based on percentages derived from prior ICU admission data. This probability served as the baseline for subsequent Bayesian calculations.

Lactate values were obtained from pooled meta-analyses to calculate LRs. The inclusion of lactate provided an objective biomarker for evaluating the severity of shock and hypoperfusion. LRs for lactate were stratified into clinically meaningful ranges and incorporated into a Bayesian nomogram to update posttest probabilities [Figure 2].

Bayesian model construction

Genomic data, including SNPs and polygenic risk scores, were incorporated into the Bayesian model. Key loci associated with septic shock susceptibility and mortality were weighted to provide additional likelihood adjustments. Genetic risk scores were calculated based on allele distributions, and patients were categorized into risk tiers based on their genomic profiles.

Risk stratification using the Miami sepsis score

Patients were stratified into low, moderate, and high-risk categories based on their MSS: Low risk (1 point): 50% baseline probability of ICU admission. Moderate risk (2 points): 59.02%, with a positive LR of 1.44. High risk (3–4 points): 77.68%, with an LR of 3.48. Patients with higher MSS scores consistently demonstrated increased probabilities of ICU admission and worse septic shock outcomes, confirming the utility of MSS as an initial pretest probability estimator.

Prehospital lactate levels and Bayesian nomogram integration

Prehospital lactate values were stratified into clinically significant ranges: Lactate >2 mmol/L: Positive LRs ranged from 2.31 to 3.48, significantly enhancing posttest probabilities. Negative Lactate (<2 mmol/L): Negative LRs ranged from 0.29 to 0.5, reducing posttest probabilities and aiding in the identification of lower-risk patients.

Statistical analysis

Primary outcomes included: (1) Predictive accuracy of the Bayesian model compared to genomic-only approaches. (2) Diagnostic gain metrics (ADG, RDG). (3) Sensitivity and specificity of the Bayesian model in stratifying septic shock outcomes.

Paired t-tests were performed to compare the predictive accuracy of the Bayesian model versus genomic-only models. Chi-square tests and ORs were calculated to assess associations between model predictions and observed outcomes. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

The genomic analysis identified 12 SNPs associated with early mortality and 16 SNPs linked to mortality between days 7 and 28. Early mortality SNPs were located in genes such as CYP11B2, PTPN11, and SLC28A3, with strong associations between specific alleles and mortality risk. Late mortality SNPs included loci in CISH, CACNA2D2, and MAPKAPK3, implicating pathways related to immune modulation and metabolic dysregulation. Patients with ≥4 risk alleles exhibited a 12.33-fold higher risk of mortality compared to those without risk alleles (adjusted OR = 12.33, 95% CI: 5.19–31.82). Integration of these SNPs into the Bayesian framework significantly enhanced predictive accuracy, with area under the curve improving from 0.72 to 0.85 (P = 3.15 × 10−¹3).

RESULTS

A paired t-test was conducted to compare the effectiveness of genomic data versus the Bayesian approach in predicting septic shock outcomes. The two-tailed P value was found to be 0.050, indicating that the difference between the two models is not quite statistically significant by conventional criteria. The mean difference between the GENOMIC and Bayesian groups was −19.8900, with a 95% CI ranging from −39.7944 to 0.0144.

Further examination of the data revealed that the GENOMIC group had a mean score of 72.86 (SD = 15.4196, SEM = 7.7098, n = 4), whereas the Bayesian group had a higher mean score of 92.75 (SD = 5.1881, SEM = 2.5941, n = 4). This suggests that, although the GENOMIC approach holds potential, the Bayesian model offers more consistent performance in predicting outcomes.

The integration of the MSS, prehospital lactate levels, and genomic data into the Bayesian model demonstrated a marked improvement in diagnostic precision for septic shock outcomes compared to traditional approaches. The Bayesian framework provided dynamic adjustments to risk probabilities based on patient-specific factors, leading to enhanced predictive accuracy.

The integration of prehospital lactate into the Bayesian nomogram demonstrated diagnostic improvements. Positive tests increased posterior probabilities to 77%–89%, depending on the MSS and lactate levels. Negative tests reduced posterior probabilities to as low as 29%–32%, effectively distinguishing patients with lower septic shock risks. ADG demonstrated improvements of up to 19% in predictive accuracy compared to the MSS alone. RDG highlighted a 50% improvement in stratification accuracy for high-risk patients when incorporating lactate and genomic data into the Bayesian model.

The combined Bayesian model, integrating clinical, lactate, and genomic data, outperformed standalone genomic models. Mean score for the Bayesian model was 92.75 (SD = 5.1881), significantly higher than the genomic model’s 72.86 (SD = 15.4196) (P < 0.05). Patients with a positive Bayesian test demonstrated posterior probabilities as high as 97% (CI: 96%–98%), with corresponding negative tests reducing probabilities to 29% (CI: 26%–33%).

DISCUSSION

Septic shock remains a formidable challenge in critical care, characterized by profound circulatory and metabolic abnormalities that lead to high mortality rates. Despite advances in resuscitation strategies, infection control, and hemodynamic management, the overall hospital mortality for septic shock remains alarmingly high, particularly for patients with refractory hypotension and hyperlactatemia. This study demonstrates the potential of a Bayesian genomic-enhanced precision critical care model to address these challenges by integrating clinical, physiological, and genomic data to improve risk stratification and outcome prediction.

The integration of genomics into critical care medicine offers a transformative opportunity to address the heterogeneity of sepsis and septic shock. Clinical phenotyping has identified distinct patient subgroups with specific biomarkers and clinical trajectories, laying the foundation for precision interventions.[23] The ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 trial,[24] which explores resuscitation strategies based on these phenotypes, underscores the clinical relevance of this approach. Similarly, hemodynamic phenotyping, using echocardiographic and clinical data, has the potential to refine patient categorization and improve outcomes by targeting interventions more effectively.

Beyond clinical and hemodynamic phenotyping, biomarkers provide another critical tool in personalizing sepsis management. For instance, antitumor necrosis factor therapy has shown efficacy in patients with elevated interleukin-6 levels, exemplifying how biomarkers can guide targeted therapy.[25] This approach aligns with the broader goals of precision medicine, which are to tailor interventions based on individual variability in genetic, physiological, and molecular profiles.

Our research group, the Acute Care Diagnostics Collaborations, has developed and validated various decision tools to assist in diagnosing and predicting mortality for patients with septic shock, leveraging advanced statistical methodologies to improve critical care outcomes.[789101112] These tools have laid the groundwork for the development of comprehensive and clinically validated Bayesian clinical decision models that integrate physiologic and molecular data, representing a significant advancement in precision medicine. These models address critical illnesses such as septic shock and pneumonia by offering tailored approaches to diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic decision-making.

In the context of septic shock, our Bayesian models incorporate dynamic physiologic variables including hemodynamic parameters, inflammatory markers, and organ function indices, alongside molecular data. This integration of diverse data layers allows for accurate mortality risk prediction while identifying patient subtypes with distinct clinical trajectories. These insights have guided individualized treatment strategies, demonstrating the potential of Bayesian modeling to transform critical care into a precision medicine framework. Notably, these models have been validated in clinical cohorts, establishing their reliability and utility in real-world settings.[10]

Similarly, we have applied this methodology to pneumonia, developing decision tools that integrate respiratory physiology, biomarkers, and molecular signatures. These tools have been instrumental in differentiating disease etiology, predicting severity, and optimizing treatment strategies.[8] The success of these models underscores the adaptability of Bayesian frameworks in addressing critical illnesses with high clinical complexity. In addition to septic shock and pneumonia, our group extended this methodology to address the COVID-19 pandemic.[7] We developed a population-based Bayesian probability model utilizing clinical, demographic, and molecular data to predict disease severity, mortality risk, and resource utilization. This model provided critical insights during the pandemic, aiding public health planning and supporting individualized patient care strategies. In our prehospital sepsis project,[9] we explored out-of-hospital indicators, such as respiratory rate and shock index, finding these predictors effective for ICU admission risk. Building on this, our 2016 work introduced the MSS, validated with ~80% sensitivity and specificity, demonstrating high utility in sepsis screening.[13]

The natural evolution of our research has clearly moved toward precision medicine and multiomics. By integrating physiologic and molecular data into advanced Bayesian frameworks, we are redefining the paradigm of critical care decision-making. These tools enhance diagnostic and prognostic accuracy while paving the way for more personalized and effective therapeutic interventions. As we continue to refine these methodologies, our goal is to further advance the integration of multiomics into clinical practice, transforming how we approach complex critical illnesses and improving patient outcomes across diverse healthcare settings.

Our findings further validate the role of genomic data in precision critical care. The genome-wide association study conducted in this study identified 12 SNPs associated with early mortality and 16 SNPs linked to mortality between days 7 and 28. These SNPs map to key loci such as CYP11B2, involved in the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, and CISH, known for modulating immune responses. The distinction between early and late mortality SNPs underscores the complex, phase-specific pathophysiology of septic shock, highlighting the need for adaptive, time-sensitive interventions.

The cumulative effect of risk alleles was particularly striking, with individuals harboring four or more risk alleles exhibiting a 12.33-fold increased risk of mortality compared to those with no risk alleles. This reinforces the concept that genetic predispositions significantly influence septic shock outcomes and provides a compelling case for incorporating genetic screening into routine clinical practice.

The Bayesian genomic-enhanced model developed in this study offers a robust framework for integrating diverse data streams into real-time clinical decision-making. By combining the MSS with genomic insights, the model achieved superior predictive accuracy, with posttest probabilities reaching as high as 97% for high-risk patients. This dynamic, probabilistic approach allows for continuous updates based on new data, enabling clinicians to adjust interventions as the clinical scenario evolves.

Importantly, the model’s ability to stratify patients into distinct risk categories supports the principle of theranostics – diagnostics that inform targeted therapy. For example, patients with SNPs associated with immune dysfunction may benefit from immunomodulatory therapies, whereas those with metabolic abnormalities might require tailored resuscitation strategies. These insights have practical implications for optimizing resource allocation and improving patient outcomes, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

While serum lactate remains a cornerstone biomarker in septic shock management, its limitations highlight the need for more advanced molecular tools. The integration of multiomics data, including proteomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics, represents the next frontier in precision medicine. For example, metabolomics provides direct biomarkers of biochemical activity, offering a more precise correlation with clinical phenotypes. These approaches hold the promise of identifying novel pathways, diagnostic biomarkers, and therapeutic targets, ultimately paving the way for more personalized and effective care.

The rapid growth of genomic and multiomics research has outpaced the training of healthcare providers, emphasizing the need for enhanced education and awareness. Efforts must also focus on standardizing methodologies and validating findings across diverse populations to ensure the broad applicability of these approaches.

The application of precision medicine in septic shock faces several challenges. The variability in genomic findings across populations underscores the need for larger, more diverse datasets to validate these insights. In addition, the implementation of such models in real-world clinical settings requires overcoming logistical, technological, and economic barriers.

As precision medicine continues to evolve, this approach represents a paradigm shift in the management of septic shock, offering a pathway toward more personalized, effective, and equitable care. By leveraging advancements in genomic medicine and computational tools, this model sets the stage for a new era of critical care, where data-driven decision-making enhances outcomes for even the most critically ill patients. By advancing precision medicine approaches in septic shock, this research aims to transform critical care, particularly in resource-intensive healthcare environments.

Despite these promising results, further research is needed to validate and expand the applicability of this model. Larger and more diverse datasets are essential to confirm the generalizability of these findings, while continued advancements in data science multiomics integration will enable even more refined predictive models. Future studies should also explore the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of implementing Bayesian genomic-enhanced frameworks in clinical practice, as well as their impact on healthcare resource utilization and patient outcomes.

Future directions include: (1) Validation with larger, diverse datasets to enhance generalizability. (2) Exploration of additional genomic and phenotypic variables to refine predictive accuracy. (3) Development of automated clinical decision-support systems based on this framework. (4) Examination of cost-effectiveness and implementation feasibility in real-world settings.

However, the opportunities are equally compelling. Advances in computational tools and data science provide unprecedented capabilities for managing and analyzing complex datasets. The development of automated decision-support systems based on Bayesian frameworks could further streamline the integration of precision medicine into clinical workflows.

CONCLUSION

The Bayesian framework’s ability to dynamically incorporate new data enables real-time adjustments, offering a robust and adaptable strategy for improving risk stratification and guiding individualized therapeutic interventions in septic shock patients. This genomic integration not only enhances diagnostic precision but also opens new avenues for theragnostic, allowing targeted therapies tailored to specific patient subgroups.
