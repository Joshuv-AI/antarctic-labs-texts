Ectopic pancreatic tissue in the small intestine: An uncommon finding following trauma

Introduction

Ectopic pancreatic tissue (EPT), also called heterotopic pancreas (HP), refers to the existence of pancreatic tissue out of the primary pancreatic tissue. EPT and main pancreatic tissue aren't vascularly or anatomically connected, and EPT has its own vascular and ductal system. In 1729, Jean-Schultz made the first report on EPT [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. It is a rare congenital malformation that can affect both abdominal and extra-abdominal parts of the body [5], although it predominantly involves the upper gastrointestinal tract [6]. EPT is typically discovered during radiologic imaging or surgical procedures, often conducted for unrelated conditions [7,8].

In this report, we present a rare case of EPT located in the submucosal area of the small intestine, discovered incidentally after the patient experienced abdominal pain following trauma from a car accident. The study adheres to SCARE guidelines [9] and comprehensively discusses the clinical types, potential causes, and treatment options for EPT.

Case presentation

In October 2023, a 56-year-old male presented to our emergency department following a high-speed car collision, during which he sustained multiple traumas. The patient reported that he had struck the vehicle in front of him at approximately 60 km/h, causing the steering wheel to impact his abdomen. His chief complaint upon arrival was abdominal pain. He had no significant medical history, denied smoking and recreational drug use, and had no known allergies. On physical examination, the patient exhibited tenderness in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen. Initial vital signs were within normal limits, with a blood oxygen saturation of 99 % on room air, blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg, heart rate of 84 bpm, and a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15.

A Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) of the abdomen, performed by an experienced radiologist, revealed the presence of free fluid in all abdominal and pelvic cavities, including the splenorenal space and around the bladder. Subsequent abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrated moderate interloop, perisplenic, and perihepatic free fluid. The jejunal mesenteric tissue was hazy, and the increased wall thickness of the intestinal loops suggested mesenteric damage, although no air or fluid levels were observed in the intestines. Initial laboratory tests revealed a white blood cell count of 5700 cells/μL, a hemoglobin level of 10.7 g/dL, and a platelet count of 104,000 cells/μL.

The patient was admitted to the surgical ward with a diagnosis of hemoperitoneum, suspected to be due to a rupture of the mesentery of the small intestine. During an exploratory laparotomy, approximately 1 L of hemorrhagic fluid and 0.5 L of clotted blood were removed from the peritoneal cavity. Moreover, a non-expanding, non-pulsating hematoma was identified in zone II of the abdomen. Further exploration of the small intestine, from the ligament of Treitz to 100 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve, revealed extensive mesenteric destruction and a 30 cm segment of the small intestine without viable blood supply. This segment was resected, and an end-to-end anastomosis was performed.

Throughout pathological assessment, grossly, the specimen consisted of a portion of the small intestine measuring 23 × 2.5 cm surrounded by congested and hemorrhagic meso-intestinal fat. Upon sectioning, a 1.5 cm submucosal mass was identified, located 4 cm from the resected proximal margin (Fig. 1). Histopathologic examination revealed a submucosal lesion comprising pancreatic ducts and acini (exocrine pancreatic tissue) beneath erosive small intestinal mucosa. The adjacent intestinal wall showed congestion, with evidence of a perforated area. (Fig. 2a).

The postoperative course was uneventful, with no complications reported. The patient was discharged in stable condition. At the follow-up visit, the patient reported no new symptoms, and the surgical site was healing well with no signs of infection or complications.

Discussion

EPT is an uncommon embryologic abnormality characterized by the presence of pancreatic tissue that is separate from the main body of the pancreas [7]. EPT is often discovered incidentally, making it difficult to accurately determine its true prevalence [5]. However, estimates suggest that EPT is found in approximately 0.5 % to 13.7 % of autopsies and 0.2 % to 0.8 % of abdominal laparotomies [3,10]. While EPT can occur at any age, it is most commonly detected in the fifth and sixth decades of life and predominantly affects males. The most frequent locations include the duodenum (28 %), stomach (26 %), and the jejunum (16 %). Less commonly, it has been identified in the ileum (3 %), ileal or jejunal diverticula (3 and 0.4 %, respectively), and Meckel's diverticulum (5 %). EPT has also been reported in more unusual locations, such as the gallbladder or bile ducts, umbilicus, fallopian tube, and even within a mediastinal teratoma [4,11,12].

The etiology of EPT remains unclear, though several theories have been proposed to explain its development. One hypothesis suggests that during embryonic development, pancreatic tissue may become separated as the dorsal and ventral pancreatic buds rotate and fuse, leading to ectopic tissue formation. Another theory posits that embryonic tissue buds may become misplaced as they penetrate the bowel walls, resulting in EPT [13]. The most likely explanation is that during the normal development of the pancreas from various evaginations, one or more of these evaginations may remain within the bowel wall. Another idea is that the migration of embryonic remnants, combined with the concurrent development of the gastrointestinal tract, could contribute to the formation of EPT [14]. While in the current literature, there is no evidence suggesting that trauma has a role in the formation or progression of EPT, it may facilitate the incidental discovery of the lesion. In our case, the patient's acute injury resulted in hemoperitoneum and necessitated surgical resection, leading to the histological identification of EPT. Although trauma is not causally linked to the existence of EPT, it was instrumental in uncovering a lesion that might have otherwise remained undiagnosed, highlighting trauma's role as an indirect factor in revealing EPT in emergency settings.

In contrast to our case, some patients may present with nonspecific symptoms that vary depending on the lesion's size and location. The literature suggests that lesions larger than 2 cm in diameter are more likely to be symptomatic, with pain being the most common complaint. In more severe cases, EPT can lead to complications such as intestinal obstruction, perforation, peritonitis, intussusception, ileus, or gastrointestinal bleeding, which often require a multidisciplinary approach due to the complexities involved in diagnosis and treatment. Despite its rarity, EPT has also been linked to malignant transformations, including adenocarcinoma, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), and solid pseudopapillary tumors [8,[15], [16], [17]].

Over a decade after Jean Schultz first documented EPT, Klob provided histological confirmation in 1859, marking a significant advancement in understanding the condition. In 1909, Von Heinrich introduced the initial classification system for EPT, which was later refined by Fuentes in 1973. The classification now includes four types: Type I, which contains all components of the normal pancreas; Type II, known as the canalicular variety, composed solely of pancreatic ducts; Type III, consisting exclusively of acinar tissue (exocrine pancreas); and Type IV, which includes only islet cells (endocrine pancreas) [18,19]. Based on the histological findings, our case is compatible with type III EPT, which consists of an exocrine pancreas with acinar glands and pancreatic ducts (Fig. 2b).

EPT is often asymptomatic, which makes detection through physical examination challenging. Endoscopic biopsies also pose difficulties, as the lesion is frequently located too deeply in the submucosa to be sampled effectively. Therefore, EPT is rarely detected before surgery, and definitive diagnosis generally relies on histological examination. Surgical resection remains the preferred treatment for both symptomatic and incidental cases of EPT, as it helps prevent potential future complications [17]. Surgical intervention for EPT can be performed using either open or laparoscopic techniques, depending on the lesion's location and the available surgical resources. Surgical resection, combined with histologic evaluation, is crucial for distinguishing an ectopic pancreas from malignant tumors [20,21].

Conclusion

This case underscores the rare occurrence of EPT in the submucosal region of the small intestine, diagnosed incidentally following a traumatic injury. Although EPT is often asymptomatic, it can lead to significant complications, necessitating surgical intervention for both symptomatic cases and incidental findings, particularly since diagnosis is frequently made postoperatively through histological examination. While trauma is not causally linked to the formation of EPT, it prompted surgical evaluations that ultimately revealed a lesion potentially capable of causing severe complications if left undiagnosed. Given the variety of symptoms associated with this condition, EPT should be included in the differential diagnosis when a lesion is identified in the small bowel.
