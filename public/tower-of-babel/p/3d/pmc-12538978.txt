Ethnographic study of Buruli ulcer wound management practices in a traditional therapeutic setting in Ghana
Authors: Edmond Kwaku Ocloo, Daniel Okyere, Emmanuel Afreh Kyei, Ishaque Mintah Siam, Adwoa Asante-Poku, Richard Akuffo, Jennifer Palmer, Tara Mtuy, Rachel Pullan, Stephen L Walker, Dorothy Yeboah-Manu, Collins S Ahorlu, Eric Koka, SHARP Collaboration
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBuruli ulcer (BU) is a skin-related neglected tropical disease (skin NTD) considered to be a disease of the poor. This study explored BU wound management in a traditional therapeutic setting in the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana and described the social interactions observed.MethodEthnographic data about the practices of a herbalist renowned for his experience in treating BU wounds were obtained using direct observations, photography, and informal conversations.ResultsAt this therapeutic setting, we observed wounds cleaned and dressed using gloves, gauze, antiseptic solutions, non-sterile scissors, and a petrol and bark preparation supported with prayers. Most clients of the traditional healer indicated that they experienced their BU as a stubborn wound that needed powerful medicine to cure it, and believed the wounds might have supernatural origins. Key reasons clients provided for seeking care at the traditional therapeutic setting included trust in the traditional healer, his practices, respectful care, a friendly and non-stigmatising environment, low-cost and flexible payment options, and people’s opinions about the potency of traditional plant medicines.DiscussionOur findings suggest that the traditional healer enjoyed substantial public legitimacy from his clients due to his perceived interest in helping affected individuals achieve cures using culturally and financially appropriate ways to manage wounds. However, we also observed the use of non-sterile procedures and unregulated preparations, which may be potentially deleterious. The willingness of the traditional healer to collaborate with the formal healthcare system to meet the health needs of people with wounds could form the basis for future collaborative approaches between the two healthcare systems to address inequities regarding clients’ access to care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02640-x.Keywords: Skin NTDs, Buruli ulcer, Wounds, Stigma, Traditional healer

Introduction

Traditional healing practice is common across Africa [1]. In Ghana, the traditional healer-client ratio in rural areas stands at 1:200 [2] whereas the doctor-patient ratio is nearly 1:20,000 [3] And the nurse-patient ratio is around 1:350 [4].

The national health insurance scheme in Ghana does not cover all treatment costs related to wound care at health facilities [5, 6]. Affected individuals often have to make out-of-pocket payments for dressing materials and drugs [6]. Traditional healers typically do not charge in advance for the services they provide. They are compensated either in cash or in kind when clients recover from their health conditions [7, 8].

Across Ghana, traditional medicine is often regarded as more effective than biomedicine because it is rooted in indigenous cultural and moral value systems and is aligned with traditions and religious beliefs [8, 9]. Research and interventions aimed at neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) still largely neglect social, ecological, and other contextual elements promoting the presence and management of the diseases in specific populations and places [10]. These factors are crucial to understanding affected individuals’ decision-making about care and potential delays in seeking biomedical treatment.

The sociocultural factors contributing to the delay in seeking biomedical treatment by people living with BU have been extensively studied [11–15]. A study of BU patients in the Ga West and Ga South municipalities of Ghana found supernatural explanations to be common among rural people, particularly when healing is protracted or complications occur [13, 16]. Some supernatural beliefs ascribed as causes of BU include sorcery, witchcraft, and curses [13, 16]. A community survey in the Ga West Municipality found that more than half of the study participants (53%) had no understanding of the aetiology of BU, although only 5% mentioned witchcraft or curse as the cause [16]. Such beliefs and a lack of understanding of the cause of BU may lead affected individuals to seek the aid of traditional healers or prayer camps before seeking biomedical treatment [8, 16, 17].

In some settings, certain wounds are believed to be better managed by traditional methods and even aggravated by biomedicine [18, 19]. Affected individuals holding these beliefs are not likely to initially seek treatment from the formal health system [20]. A study conducted in a hospital setting in Ghana found some patients who believed wound healing was influenced by the age or sex of the care provider at health facilities often leading them to dress the wounds themselves at home for fear of a lack of wound healing [13].

High direct and indirect costs associated with formal healthcare and fear of stigma associated with anatomical and functional differences due to wound debridement or amputation are other important factors influencing the choice of traditional treatment over biomedical care [21]. In addition, accessibility and perceived treatment failure of biomedical care are important factors shaping treatment preference [22].

We conducted an ethnographic study of a traditional healing setting alongside formative research of the public healthcare system in Atwima Mponua District, Ghana. Although quantitative methods may provide valuable data on specific aspects of wound management, ethnographic observation offers the depth and context required to understand the complex interplay of cultural, social, and individual factors that shape traditional wound management practices [23, 24]. Observation allowed the researchers to look beyond statements of ideal behaviours so that the discrepancy between the real and ideal behaviour could be described, assessed, and explained [25].

This study was conducted within the broader context of research intended to understand the lived experiences of people with skin NTDs in the district. It was undertaken with a prominent healer to understand his practices, what attracted people to him, and to possibly inform future interventions.

Theoretical framework

This study examined traditional wound management in the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana through the lens of medical pluralism. Medical pluralism was a concept developed in the 1970s–1980s to offer innovative ways of understanding different medical practices and their relations with biomedicine [26]. Medical pluralism refers to the availability of various medical approaches, treatments, and institutions that people can access when seeking health care, including combining biomedicine with traditional or alternative medicines [26]. Healthcare systems in all human societies consist of a dyadic core involving a healer and a patient [27]. All healthcare systems are composed of ideas and practices that aim to improve health and reduce illness [28]. Societies develop unique medical systems to address their health issues, shaped by their natural environment, religion, and social and political structures [27].

A society’s medical system encompasses all medical subsystems, which generally compete or sometimes cooperate or collaborate [27, 29]. Medical pluralism allows traditional healing practices to coexist with modern biomedical care [29]. Examining how people manage illness by navigating between home remedies, evidence-based medicine, traditional healing, and other treatment options will reveal the presence of a certain level of medical pluralism in all modern societies. Each medical system has its own unique understanding of disease causes, pathogenesis, and appropriate methods for diagnosis and healing. Studying traditional healing as an ‘alternative’ to care through the epistemologically dominant formal health sector requires researchers to highlight the ‘perceived’ effectiveness of individual medical approaches by exploring patients’ and care providers’ feelings, personal experiences, and opinions as they rationalise their use of alternative medicines [30]. We drew on medical pluralism to explain why, even though biomedical care for BU is available in Ghana, people still seek services from traditional healers.

Materials and methods

Study setting

The study was conducted between November 2021 and October 2022 in a community called Nkroma, located in the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana. The community has its own CHPS compound and is about a 15-min drive from the district hospital. The Atwima Mponua District is located in the southwestern part of the Ashanti Region of Ghana and had a population of 155,254 in 2021 [31]. The district capital is Nyinahin, which is about 45 km from the regional capital, Kumasi, and lies on the main Bibiani-Kumasi Highway. Akan ethnic people are the largest group in the district, with Mamprusi, Ewe, Fante, and Ga people contributing smaller numbers. Twi is the most widely spoken language, and Christianity is the most common religion. Common livelihoods include agricultural activities (farming and fishing), commerce, and small and medium-scale industries, including lumber, metal works, cassava processing, palm oil processing, alcohol (akpeteshie) distillation, corn milling, and mining [31].

The district has 17 health facilities, comprising ten health centres, 6 community-based health planning and services (CHPS) compounds, and a district hospital. Traditional healers, including herbalists and fetishists, are a convenient resource for many people with skin conditions, including individuals with BU. Despite the reported high utilisation of the services of traditional healers in the district, there is no organised network of such practitioners.

Study design and data collection

This study was based on an ethnographic design. We conducted observations at the traditional therapeutic setting of a well-known healer identified as AK (pseudonym) to understand interactions among persons with BU and between the clients and AK. Buruli ulcer was generally referred to as Kisikro in the local Twi language by both AK and his clients. AK was described as having experience in treating various complicated skin conditions, including BU.

The study was conducted between November 2021 and October 2022 by EKO with assistance from DO and EAK in a community called Nkroma, located in the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana. Twelve visits were made to the traditional therapeutic setting to observe and interact with the participants.

Data were collected through direct observations, photography, and informal interviews with AK and ten clients with wounds presumed to be BU. Detailed fieldnotes were taken at each visit to the therapeutic setting. Photographs were taken to document wound care practices. Informal conversations with clients provided information on their decisions to receive treatment at the traditional healer’s setting rather than the formal healthcare system.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was carried out to identify common themes using MAXQDA software. Quotes are presented in the results section to show dominant views. These themes include the traditional healer, the wound dressing arena, wound dressing procedures, factors influencing traditional wound care over biomedical care (respectful care and supportive interactions in the traditional healer setting, the effectiveness of traditional wound care, local beliefs associated with illness causation, and treatment costs), and relationships with other healthcare providers in the district. Analysis was carried out by the lead author and validated by other co-authors (EK, DO, EAK).

Ethical approval

This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research, University of Ghana (IRB 00001276), and the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (22604). The purpose of the study was explained to all participants. All participants consented to participate in the study. The final draft of this paper was shown to the traditional healer, who consented to its publication. Further, the findings of this study have been reported following the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (S1).

Healing story of the healer

AK did not learn his traditional healing or wound management practices through an apprenticeship or formal education. According to AK, his current skills and knowledge of traditional healing practices were revealed to him through a series of dreams ten years before our study. AK mentioned that an old man kept appearing in his dreams and showing him herbs to treat specific illnesses and diseases. AK said he received encouragement from an uncle to pursue traditional healing practices. AK’s uncle told him the old man who kept showing him herbs in his dreams might be his grandfather, who was an excellent herbalist.

AK said he treated BU wounds, other wounds, haemorrhoids, and fever due to various causes. Although his children currently do not have the traditional healing skills, they undertake errands for him when he is caring for clients. He said his family was very supportive in caring for clients.

The wound dressing area

AK treats people in his home. There are signs directing people to his therapeutic setting, but many clients hear of his reputation through the testimonies of former clients. Clients are treated in either a partially completed building or the open compound of AK’s house (see Figs. 1 and 2). The building is one of seven family living areas under construction, with a roof and walls, but no doors or windows. The dressing area is approximately 5 m from the kitchen, and approximately six metres from this building is a well that serves the household and neighbours. Only the client and their caretaker(s) are allowed inside the dressing area. On wound dressing days, other clients (up to four) wait on a bench about two metres from the dressing area. The compound is not walled and typical of others in the village, boundaries are not demarcated by fences. As such, treatment activities are somewhat public, and clients may be observed by passersby. Nevertheless, clients did not seem worried about being observed.

Wound care procedures

Observations revealed that wound dressing normally starts at 6:30 am and ends at 8:00 am, as AK farms after attending to clients. The mean time to dress a wound was approximately eight minutes. Some of the materials and equipment used for dressing the wounds, such as hydrogen peroxide, antiseptic solutions, cotton wool, bandages, gloves, and gauze were available in pharmacies. Clients procure hydrogen peroxide, antiseptic solutions, cotton wool, and bandages, while the healer procures gloves, scissors, and razor blades.

AK uses a preparation known as “mmoto” and “etsi” in the Twi and Ewe languages, respectively. AK keeps the complete recipe and ingredients used for the preparations a secret, but divulged that it includes petrol and burnt tree bark. AK uses gloves when they are available, but they are not a requirement for wound dressing.

First, the bandage covering the wound was removed. Adherent gauze and cotton wool were irrigated with the “mmoto” to detach them from the wound bed (Fig. 3). Hydrogen peroxide was subsequently poured onto the wound and wiped clean with cotton wool. Scissors and razor blades were used to remove slough or necrotic tissue from the wound. New gauze and cotton wool dressings were applied and soaked with “mmoto”. Finally, the wound was bandaged.

Pouring of petrol and bark (Mmoto) preparation onto the cotton wool covering the wound before exposing it

The traditional healer placed the cotton wool, gauze, and dead necrotic tissues removed during the procedure into a polythene bag, which he buried or burnt after completion of the day’s dressings. AK sprinkled the “mmoto” on the footrest prior to performing the next client’s wound dressing. No attempt was made to clean the scissors between clients.

As described by AK, the special petrol and bark preparation made for the wound dressing is used for a similar purpose as the antiseptic solution purchased from pharmacy shops. According to him, both the purchased antiseptic solution and the special petrol and bark solution he prepares kill microorganisms responsible for skin infections. However, he claimed that the antiseptic solution acts at a more superficial level. He believes the petrol and bark preparation is more powerful and penetrates deeply to “uproot” organisms that have taken hold in the body and cause chronic ulcers:

That medicine [mmoto] is very powerful and helps in uprooting the organism from the wound. It kills the organism in the wound. Without that medicine, the wound would not heal. The substances I used to prepare it [mmoto] kill Buruli ulcer and other causal agents of stubborn skin diseases.

AK explained that he prays over the petrol and bark preparation during its preparation with the hope that it will be effective in treating skin ulcers and warding off any spiritual forces that may be associated with the condition:

Oh yes, I say prayers a lot for my clients to recover from their sicknesses fast. I am a devoted Christian, so I don’t joke with prayers at all. Though the petrol-based solution is a powerful medicine that kills the organisms causing the wounds, I believe strongly that prayers also help in getting clients healed. Some may be afflicted with the illness spiritually, so I say prayers on the petrol and bark solution when I am preparing it because I believe that prayers could help ward off the spiritual forces behind the diseases. I don’t discuss the prayers I say while preparing the medicine with my clients.

Respectful care and supportive interactions in the traditional healer setting

At AK’s treatment setting, many participants described the shame and social exclusion they felt because of their wounds. A woman with wounds on both her hand and leg reported how she concealed her wounds with clothing when she left her compound. At home, she felt that her wounds scared her children to the extent that they were rejecting her:

I mostly wear long dresses to cover the wound on my leg, and also cover my hand with a handkerchief since a lot of people in the community ask questions about my wound. I am simply not comfortable responding to such questions. [… At home]. My own children don’t want to come near me. I have a baby who is not even a year old but has stopped breastfeeding because of my leg […]. If I call him, he just runs away from me. I was even telling my mother yesterday that my children have abandoned me in my sickness. For the secondborn, he does not even want to see my face. When he sees me, he shouts kakai (scary person) and runs away. [34-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

Despite many clients describing feelings of shame and social exclusion at home and in the community, they were observed at AK’s healing setting chatting among themselves. The appearance of their individual wounds did not seem to be a barrier to their interaction when they met at the healing centre. Another woman presumed to be affected by BU described a sense of solidarity with other clients receiving care:

After all, we are all here to get treated for the same sickness- basically, wounds. I am here with a wound, so why should I distance myself from other people with wounds? How would I feel if people I am obtaining treatment with withdrew from me? After all, the powerful herbal preparations [petrol and bark solution] the traditional healer uses to dress the wound prevent it from smelling, so why should I withdraw from other patients? It is uncalled for to do that! [45-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

It was observed that clients expressed empathy towards each other when they went for a change of dressing for their wounds. AK and his household members were also often seen expressing empathy with the clients, especially when they expressed pain. Clients were often heard routinely saying “sorry, sorry” to other clients crying out during dressing changes, in recognition of each other’s pain. Furthermore, in all his conversations with the clients, AK emphasised his belief that they would be healed soon, and clients often narrated how reassuring this was for them.

Older clients described the respectful and culturally appropriate care AK administered, and contrasted this to their previous experiences at hospitals:

At the hospital, you are talked to as if you don’t know the importance of your health. You are shouted at if the nurse feels you have done something wrong. It is quite embarrassing to see a nurse who is young enough to be your daughter or son shouting at you and making unwelcoming remarks about your condition, all because you are sick, and he or she must take care of you. You will not experience such treatments here at AK’s place. The man treats you with respect and care to ensure that you feel loved. It is this that keeps us coming here every day to dress our wounds [43-year-old man presumed to have BU].

The traditional healer won’t talk to you anyhow, unlike the health facility, where any little boy or girl clothed in a uniform can say anything to you without considering your age. Nurses must be re-oriented on how to relate with patients. Their relationships with patients are very poor. Such behaviours are turning a lot of people away from the hospital [52-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

Trust in the effectiveness of traditional wound care

AK’s clients indicated they had more trust in the effectiveness of traditional wound care than in formal healthcare. No participant was receiving care from health facilities while seeking care from AK.

Had it not been for the traditional healer, I think my leg would have been amputated by now. When I went to the hospital for treatment, they mentioned that they would amputate my leg. This got me, my husband, and other close relations terrified, and I decided not to continue with treatment over there […]. Just about one and a half months into treatment here, I can say that my condition has improved greatly [43-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

AK’s clients maintained that traditional healers use herbs procured from their immediate environment, which are more potent than medicines available at health facilities.

I believe local herbal preparations are more effective than hospital medicines. Herbs and plants are more powerful than hospital medicines and cure all sorts of diseases, including stubborn wounds. I will choose a traditional treatment (wound care) anytime over the health facility [64-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

Participants’ other reasons for employing the services of traditional healers/herbalists rather than the formal healthcare system included being comfortable with their services due to an established relationship.

Local beliefs associated with illness causation

It emerged from our interactions with the participants that they believed evil spirits, curses, and spells played a role in their skin conditions. This underpinned their desire to seek the services of traditional healers whose treatments they believed were potent enough to combat such forces.

I just got pricked by a mosquito coil stand, and it turned into this big wound. I can’t fathom how a small prick from a mosquito coil stand could turn into this kind of wound. I believe there is something behind it. So, I think this condition (Buruli ulcer) can only be treated by the traditional healer [34-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

Another client expressed his opinion about his condition and the best place to seek treatment:

I strongly believe that a certain man who always mentions to me that my crops are doing better than his afflicted me with this disease. […] Even though I share boundaries with other people too, it is only this man who complains […]. Three months ago, […] I became upset and told him that I didn’t understand why he kept asking me that question. This got him infuriated. He then told me that I am just a year or two older than his lastborn [… and]. I have disrespected him, so he would certainly prove to me that we are not co-equals. Exactly seven days later, I woke up with pains in my right foot, and it has turned into this disease. […] So, it is only the traditional healer who can treat me. [28–year–old young man presumed to have BU].

Treatment costs

Financial concerns also played a critical role in AK’s clients’ preferences for traditional care over biomedical healthcare. Cheaper treatment by the traditional healer compared to the health facilities was one of the reasons participants cited for choosing AK’s care. AK’s clients reported that the national health insurance scheme did not cover some medicines and wound dressing materials when they visited the formal health facilities. As a result, regardless of their choice of treatment setting (traditional or hospital care), they were still required to buy or pay for their wound dressing materials. However, they believed that herbal treatments were cheaper overall.

Treatment at the hospital is very expensive. At the hospital, I paid for virtually everything, but here [traditional healer’s place], the little you give to the traditional healer, he will attend to you. Within 3 months, I spent more than Gh¢3,000.00 (50) for the close to two and half months that I have been coming. […]. I have seen a lot of improvement in my condition here [36-year-old man presumed to have BU].

The traditional healer does not charge for daily wound dressing. He accepts whatever you give him. Even with active national health insurance, you still have to pay for dressing and medicines at the hospital. So, do you think someone like me, who is struggling to even cater for my children, would go to the hospital for wound care? I am here because the traditional healer didn’t mention any big amount of money for me to pay before commencing treatment on me [34-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

We observed clients paying between Gh¢5.00 and Gh¢20.00 ($0.5-$2.00) to AK on several occasions after wound dressing. AK informed us that he does not charge for performing wound dressing, but clients reimburse him for wound dressing materials he provides when their own have run out. Clients reimbursed him when they had money.

The participants also said payment for treatment provided by AK was much more flexible than health facilities. At the traditional therapeutic setting, clients said they were allowed to make payments in tranches while treatment was ongoing. In addition, payments could be made in cash or kind. Farm produce and livestock were sometimes used to pay for the traditional healer’s services, which is impossible at the health facility.

Here, if you come and you don’t have money, the man (traditional healer) will not sack you. He would attend to you and ask you to pay later when you have the money. He accepts payment in cash or kind (food crops, fowls, goats, among others). Treatment here is cheaper than at the health facility, where you would even be asked to make an upfront payment or risk not being attended to [45-year-old man presumed to have BU].

The traditional healer doesn’t demand that we pay before he attends to us. Once your family members accompany you to him and negotiate the payment terms with him, he will treat you. What makes the traditional healer comfortable to initiate treatment on a patient is the presence of a caretaker or family member to assure him of payment on a later day. For instance, when he charges you Ghc500.00 (10) for the start, he will accept it and commence treatment on you with the hope that you will pay later when you have it [32-year-old woman presumed to have BU].

Relationship with other healthcare providers in the district

AK expressed his desire to earn recognition from the district health officials. AK stated that some community members had told him that some staff at local health facilities knew about his good work in wound care, but he regretted that they did not seem ready to collaborate with him. AK indicated that he does not accept clients with conditions for which he has no treatment (for instance, hypertension) and, instead, advises them to visit the health facility. AK intimated he was ready and willing to collaborate with the formal healthcare system to meet the health needs of people with wounds and was interested in discussing opportunities for collaboration with district health officials. AK identified pain management as a potential area where collaboration would be useful, as he could not provide analgesia for wound dressing:

I would be extremely glad if you could discuss with the nurses at the community health planning and services (CHPs) centres to give injections to my clients to relieve them of pain after I dress their wounds. This is because my herbal preparation is strong, making them experience pain anytime I apply it to their wounds. I believe we are all in the service of meeting the health needs of our people.

Discussion

Our study reveals several important characteristics of traditional care for people presumed to have BU at a popular therapeutic site in the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana, which have implications for skin health interventions. Among the many attractions drawing people to AK’s care was a belief in the effectiveness of his cure. Many people attributed magical causes to their wounds, as is common in other parts of Ghana and West Africa [10, 11, 19], and seeking care from AK was appropriate on these grounds. In our formative research, we identified other healers, such as fetishists, who more directly engaged with the magical and spiritual components of disease by warding off evil spirits to promote recovery [6]. AK identified more as a herbalist and conducted no such overtly spiritual rituals while seeing clients. However, belief in the healing powers of supernatural forces still permeated his practice, lending strength to the promise he made to everyone that they would be cured and trust in him, more generally. For instance, the potency of his special dressing preparation was derived from undisclosed but trusted materials derived from multiple sources, including petrol and other plant-based sources, which he imbued with healing powers through Christian prayer. He was also known to have received his healing powers mystically from an ancestor who was himself well-respected and trusted as a healer.

Other key attractions of AK’s care were the convenience for his clients of visiting his home for regular morning dressing changes; the social support people received from him and fellow clients attending his home service; the respectful care he delivered, especially for older patients; and the affordability and flexibility in managing costs associated with care.

As found in other contexts, people presumed to have BU in this study reported experiences of substantial stigma linked to both the appearance of their wounds and their assumed spiritual causes [11, 22, 32–34]. Clients at AK’s home, however, supported each other by acknowledging each other’s psychological distress by being friendly and trying to distract each other from the pain they all felt during dressing changes. Older people, especially, described AK’s care as respectful and compassionate, and detailed major deficiencies that formal health services in the district had in delivering culturally appropriate care for their age group.

Stock-outs and charging for dressing materials at public health facilities in the district create a large financial burden on people with BU and other chronic ulcers, even for those who may have national health insurance [35]. AK’s practices seem broadly similar to those of other traditional practitioners in Ghana who are compensated with a small (monetary) gift if patients truly recover rather than all charges upfront [6, 8]. Receiving remuneration directly from clients and in small amounts may also reinforce AK’s enactment of compassion, since he must demonstrate respect for people to keep them returning [35]. This is unlike staff in health facilities, who will receive their salaries at the end of the month regardless of how many patients they treat.

Beliefs about the safety, effectiveness, and appropriateness of care are always influenced by complex historical, social, political, and economic factors [36, 37]. In Ghana, traditional medicine is often regarded as effective because it is rooted in the cultural and moral value systems of the people. Belief in magical aetiologies is rarely the main or only factor driving people towards or away from certain providers of care [8, 38].

Feeling safe in healthcare settings also relates to people’s sense of fairness and the quality of healthcare provider-patient relationships [39–41]. In programmes seeking to address low use of or resistance to healthcare services, therefore, successful community engagement typically involves demonstrating genuine respect for existing local disease logics (such as the need for medicines to be powerful enough to ‘uproot the organism causing BU’), listening and taking time to appreciate the many constraints people have using healthcare, and consulting widely among affected people and trusted authorities (including traditional practitioners) to find acceptable solutions [36, 37].

Like other ethnographic studies of traditional skincare [42, 43], we observed some practices that may cause harm, delay care-seeking, aggravate wounds, and challenge collaboration with traditional practitioners. Secondary infection risks associated with traditional cutting practices and the conditions under which local healing mixtures are prepared are a concern [13]. Caustic and toxic agents commonly used in traditional wound preparations in Ghana, such as petrol and gunpowder, can also harm the natural barrier functions of the skin and delay wound healing [13]. There is a council responsible for promoting, controlling, and regulating traditional and alternative medicine practices in Ghana [44]. However, we are unaware of any public health programmes in Africa or elsewhere that actively incorporate healers/herbalists using unregulated preparations to implement traditional wound management practices such as these. It is unlikely that the Ghana Health Service would collaborate with AK to support him with pain management for his clients, as this would mean tacitly accepting his wound management practices, which they see as harmful. Some might even call for him to be shut down or sanctioned.

Nevertheless, the legitimacy that traditional healers have cannot be ignored, and there may be other domains of collaboration that would be acceptable to all parties [45, 46]. Other studies reported instances where traditional healers were asked to participate in facility-based care by training them in disease recognition, accompanying clients they suspected to have BU, and offering spiritual and moral support, but not engaging in wound management [13, 43]. In Ghana, harm reduction programmes seeking to work with respected local healers like AK could begin by acknowledging a shared interest in the well-being of affected individuals and many shared practices used to treat and control secondary infections, such as the use of antiseptics, gloves, clean gauze, and equipment. The willingness of AK to collaborate with the formal healthcare system to meet the health needs of people with wounds could form the basis for future collaborative approaches between the two healthcare systems to address inequities regarding clients’ access to care.

Contrary to the perception that asking traditional healers to refer people to health facilities might work against their perceived economic interest, anthropological research in other parts of Africa suggests that symbolic motivators, such as having a good reputation in the community, can be just as important to healers, and these can be built into community-based intervention designs [38, 41, 43].

This study has some limitations. It is a case study of a single herbalist and his clients, and this might limit our understanding of the diversity of traditional care for ulcers in the district. While the study offers valuable insights, focusing on a single healer limits the generalisability of the findings. Traditional healing practices may vary among different practitioners [47], so future research that includes multiple healers would, therefore, help to capture a wider diversity of practices and perspectives within the community. It would also be helpful to understand the perspectives of biomedical healthcare providers about the role traditional medicine and traditional healers play in BU care. This would provide a fuller picture of the different actors involved in the treatment process and how multiple systems of medicine interact to influence care seeking. Furthermore, we relied on the healer’s and affected people’s working assumptions of their BU diagnoses, and did not independently conduct additional BU tests. While we found important age-related care concerns for older people with ulcers, no children were observed at the traditional healing site, and there may be important differences in their use of traditional care for BU and other BU-like skin problems. We recommend that future studies engage more participants to compare practices and social interactions across traditional healing settings.

Conclusions

Through a study of a popular herbalist known for his expertise in managing BU wounds, we examined traditional care practices and their attractiveness to many people in the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana to inform future interventions. We identified respectful care, a friendly and non-stigmatising environment, low-cost and flexible payment options, and deep trust in the healer’s locally-prepared wound cleansing solution as key to his popularity. It also demonstrates characteristics of skin care that are important for future health systems interventions to adopt.

We also observed potentially deleterious practices such as the use of non-sterile cutting and cleaning materials, and the application of a petrol-based solution for wound cleansing. This presents health risks that may make collaboration with the Ghana Health Service difficult.

Nevertheless, other elements of the traditional wound care ritual, such as the use of over-the-counter antiseptic solutions and clean gauze, suggest a genuine interest in keeping patients safe by controlling infections. This represents common ground that harm reduction education or other collaborative interventions could engage with.
