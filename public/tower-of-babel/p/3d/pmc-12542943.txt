A multistep platform identifies spleen-tropic lipid nanoparticles for in vivo T cell–targeted delivery of gene-editing proteins
Authors: Xiaoya Lu, Yining Zhu, Christine Wei, Leonardo Cheng, Kailei D. Goodier, Jiayuan Kong, Xiangyu Gao, Di Yu, Xiang Liu, Yuanmuhuang Long, Jinghan Lin, Jingyao Ma, Yunhe Su, Hai-Quan Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are a promising nonviral delivery system for gene-editing proteins, but optimal formulations remain underexplored. Unlike messenger RNA–based approaches, ribonucleoprotein delivery enables immediate genome editing without relying on endogenous translation. However, intracellular delivery remains a major challenge due to protein size, charge variability, and susceptibility to denaturation and degradation. Here, we present a multistep screening platform to optimize LNP formulations for gene-editing protein delivery, focusing on in vivo T cell targeting. Through in vitro screening of a composition library, we identified top-performing candidates. In vivo screening in Ai9 mice revealed a spleen-tropic LNP formulation that preferentially targets T cells, enabling efficient gene editing in vivo. Using this LNP formulation, we achieved targeted knockout of CCR5 and PD-1 in splenic T cells, supporting potential applications in HIV resistance and cancer immunotherapy. Furthermore, a machine learning–guided mechanistic study revealed key design principles for LNP-based protein delivery, highlighting unexplored opportunities for in vivo genome-editing therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Protein-based gene-editing tools, particularly ribonucleoproteins (RNPs), have emerged as a promising approach for treating genetic, infectious, and oncological diseases (1–4). A key challenge that remains in the current mRNA-based gene-editing systems is the inefficient assembly of CRISPR proteins with their corresponding single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) into functional units as sgRNA may degrade before the mRNA is translated into an active protein (5). Unlike mRNA-based delivery systems, the direct delivery of preassembled RNPs does not rely on the endogenous translation machinery to produce gene-editing proteins, enabling immediate activity upon delivery while also avoiding potential immune responses triggered by exogenous mRNA and minimizing off-target effects due to shorter intracellular persistence (6–8). Although these advantages make RNPs highly attractive for precise and efficient genome editing, the intracellular delivery of RNPs and other gene-editing proteins remains a major challenge, hence limiting their therapeutic potential (9).

The efficient intracellular delivery of gene-editing proteins, including RNPs, poses unique challenges due to several inherent barriers. These proteins are large and charged, preventing passive diffusion across the lipid bilayer of the cell membrane (10, 11). In addition, they are highly susceptible to enzymatic degradation in the extracellular environment and within endosomal compartments following cellular uptake (12). Overcoming these delivery obstacles is critical to maximizing the therapeutic potential of RNP-based technologies. Although a variety of delivery vehicles—including viral vectors, electroporation, and polymeric nanoparticles—have been explored, each comes with limitations related to efficiency, toxicity, or scalability (13–18). Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which have demonstrated remarkable success in delivering nucleic acids as evidenced by their use in mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines, offer a promising alternative for the delivery of gene-editing proteins (19, 20). However, the composition of LNPs has yet to be optimized to accommodate the distinct physicochemical properties of proteins that differ substantially from nucleic acids. Unlike nucleic acids, which carry a uniform negative charge, proteins exhibit variable surface charges and conformations, posing challenges for encapsulation and efficient cellular delivery (21). LNPs’ modular composition, which typically consists of an ionizable lipid, helper lipid(s), cholesterol, and a poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)–lipid, allows for tunability to address these challenges, making them a suitable carrier for protein delivery (22). However, the optimal composition of LNPs for in vitro and in vivo protein delivery remains underexplored (23–27). Systematic optimization of LNP formulations is required to achieve efficient intracellular delivery of gene-editing proteins like RNPs and Cre recombinase.

One particularly difficult yet highly valuable application of gene-editing therapeutics is in vivo T cell engineering. T cells play a central role in adaptive immunity and are critical targets for treating infectious diseases, autoimmunity, and cancer (28–30). Traditional ex vivo approaches, such as adoptive T cell therapy, involve isolating T cells from a patient, genetically modifying them, expanding them in culture, and reinfusing them back into the body. Although effective, this process is labor-intensive, costly, and constrained by patient-specific variability, limiting its scalability and accessibility (31, 32). In contrast, direct in vivo gene editing of T cells offers notable advantages, including greater scalability, reduced manipulation-induced dysfunction, and the potential for broader clinical applications (33, 34). However, achieving efficient in vivo gene editing in T cells remains a major challenge due to their dynamic circulation, complex microenvironment, and the need for selective targeting (35). Overcoming the barriers to in vivo T cell gene editing would enable more accessible and effective immunotherapies.

In this study, we leveraged our high-throughput, multistep screening platform to optimize LNPs for gene-editing protein delivery (Fig. 1) (36). By screening 486 formulations in vitro, we identified top candidates on the basis of gene-editing efficiency in human embryonic kidney (HEK) Ai9 cells and used a cluster-mode screening approach for in vivo evaluation in Ai9 mice, leading to the discovery of a spleen-tropic formulation with a preference for T cell gene editing. To evaluate its therapeutic potential, we used LNP-encapsulated RNPs to achieve functional knockout of CCR5 and PD-1 in splenic T cells, demonstrating applications in both HIV resistance and cancer immunotherapy. In a B16– ovalbumin (OVA) tumor model, PD-1 editing enhanced antitumor efficacy, validating the platform for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Further investigations into biodistribution and cellular uptake provided mechanistic insights into in vivo protein delivery. In addition, by optimizing LNPs for Cre recombinase, we explored whether a universal LNP composition is viable for different proteins, offering key design principles for enhancing LNP-based gene-editing therapies.

RESULTS

In vitro screening of LNP-mediated RNP delivery using HEK Ai9 cells

We designed a library of 486 unique LNP formulations by systematically varying lipid compositions while maintaining particle stability. This library incorporated six different helper lipids—dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium propane (DOTAP; A), dimethyldioctadecylammonium (DDAB; B), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE; C), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC; D), 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphate (14PA; E), and 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1’-rac-glycerol) (18PG; F)—alongside cholesterol, 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG–PEG-2000), and (6Z,9Z,28Z,31Z)-heptatriaconta-6,9,28,31-tetraen-19-yl 4-(dimethylamino)butanoate (DLin-MC3-DMA), the ionizable lipid used in the Food and Drug Administration–approved Onpattro formulation. These variations generated a diverse range of LNPs for optimizing RNP delivery. To assess their delivery efficiency, we encapsulated RNPs and conducted high-throughput screening (HTS) in HEK Ai9 cells. Gene-editing efficiency was quantified using flow cytometry by measuring tdTomato fluorescence, which is activated upon successful deletion of the stop sequence. Before the LNP assembly, we tested the range of ethanol percentage impact on the protein activities using luciferase as a surrogate. We titrated the ethanol percentage by adjusting the volume ratio of the luciferase aqueous solution to ethanol, which served as the solvent for lipids, and assessed the effect of ethanol content on luciferase activity. The relative luciferase activity plateaued at an aqueous-to-ethanol volume ratio of 85:15 (fig. S1). We then selected a volume ratio of 9:1 for all subsequent experiments.

As shown in Fig. 2 (A and B), the delivery efficiency of the tested formulations varied substantially. The most potent formulation achieved ~14.3 ± 8% tdTom+ cells following treatment, demonstrating a robust gene-editing efficiency. In comparison, the commercially available CRISPR-Max reagent achieved a ~6% editing efficiency at the same RNP dose. This represents a more than twofold improvement over the CRISPR-Max benchmark. Notably, formulations containing the cationic helper lipids DDAB and DOTAP yielded the highest number of successful edits, suggesting that positively charged lipids enhance RNP delivery, likely by promoting stronger complexation with the slightly negatively charged RNPs. This unexpected result may be due to the faster release of cargo once the LNPs reach the cytosol, suggesting its intricate balance with the need to achieve sufficient condensation and complexation stability during the LNP assembly and delivery process. In addition, 18PG contains unsaturated acyl chains that may increase membrane fluidity, a property shown to enhance endosomal escape by facilitating the formation of nonlamellar, fusogenic structures and membrane destabilization (37, 38). In contrast, zwitterionic helper lipids such as DOPE and DSPC showed minimal editing activity, reinforcing the idea that charge interactions play a critical role in determining LNP performance for protein delivery. These findings highlight the importance of lipid composition in optimizing LNP formulations for efficient intracellular delivery of gene-editing proteins.

To further analyze the HTS results, we used a machine learning (ML) pipeline that trains models on the LNP formulation data to predict gene-editing efficiency outcomes (Fig. 2, C and D). Using Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values, we quantified the relative importance of formulation features and identified some key design rules. Each dot represents an individual LNP formulation, with the color corresponding to the relative value of the feature in that formulation. The position of the dot on the x axis reflects the impact of the feature value on the model’s output, where a positive x value indicates a positive contribution to the predicted gene-editing efficiency. We have revealed that (i) formulations with a higher combined percentage of helper lipid and ionizable lipid [e.g., (IL+HL wt %)] around 50 to 80% showed improved gene-editing efficiency, likely due to enhanced particle stability and endosomal escape capabilities; (ii) unsaturated lipid tails (e.g., higher number of double bonds) were preferred as their fluidity promotes endosomal escape and intracellular delivery; (iii) the absence of negative charges in helper lipid was favorable as cationic (e.g., DOTAP and DDAB) usually outperformed anionic lipids, consistent with the observed trends in HTS; and (iv) formulations with a lower lipid-to-protein ratio demonstrated higher gene-editing efficiency, suggesting that excessive lipid content may hinder RNP release or cellular uptake.

Cluster-mode in vivo screening in Ai9 mice identifies T cell–targeting gene-editing LNP formulations

The gene-editing efficiency of RNP-loaded LNPs in vivo often differs from that observed in traditional in vitro assays due to the complexities of biological environments and additional delivery barriers. To identify the optimal formulation for T cell–targeted delivery, we selected the most effective LNPs on the basis of in vitro transfection efficiency and tested them via intravenous injection. To streamline the in vivo screening process while reducing the number of animals, time, and cost, we used a cluster-mode screening approach. Specifically, the top 40 formulations with the highest gene-editing efficiency in HEK Ai9 cells were first grouped into eight clusters, each containing five formulations, for initial testing. The top-performing clusters were then selected, and the individual formulations within these clusters were further analyzed to identify the most potent ones. This method allowed for an efficient initial evaluation of in vivo performance, guiding the selection of top-performing formulations for further validation.

Each pooled cluster was first tested in Ai9 mice, which express tdTomato upon successful deletion of the stop codon upstream of the tdTomato expression cassette by stop sequence–targeting RNPs. Mice received a systemic intravenous injection of 50 μg of sgAi9-containing RNPs per dose. As the result showed that, although most clusters showed no detectable gene-editing activity in the liver and lung, they exhibited high gene-editing efficiency in the spleen, particularly in CD45+CD3+ T cells (Fig. 3A). The top two performing clusters, DOTAP I and 18PG I, showed up to 6.5 ± 3% and 5 ± 3% of tdTom+ CD45+CD3+ T cells, respectively, while exhibiting minimal gene-editing activity in nonlymphoid organs. Specifically, cluster DOTAP I showed 0.2 ± 0.04% and 0.43 ± 0.2% gene-editing efficiency in hepatocytes and lung epithelial cells, respectively, whereas cluster 18PG I exhibited 0.02 ± 0.01% and 0.11 ± 0.07% gene-editing efficiency in these cell populations (fig. S6). Although gene editing was observed in other immune cell populations within the spleen, T cells represented most edited cells, accounting for 82 ± 13% and 78 ± 9% of all tdTom+ CD45+immune cells for the top two clusters–DOTAP I cluster and 18PG I cluster (fig. S7), respectively. This result underscores the optimized LNP formulations’ strong T cell–targeting capability and their potent gene-editing activity in T cells.

To further identify the most effective individual formulation for T cell–targeted delivery, we evaluated 15 formulations from the top three clusters identified in the initial screening (table S1). Each formulation was administered to Ai9 mice at a dose of 50 μg of LNP-encapsulated sgAi9 RNPs. Although delivery efficiency varied substantially among formulations, F48 emerged as the top candidate, achieving 7.8 ± 0.7% gene-editing efficiency in T cells, followed by formulations A41 and F47, which showed 7.0 ± 2.3% and 4.8 ± 2.4% transfection, respectively (Fig. 3B). Further analysis of the edited cell populations revealed that 69 ± 11% of tdTom+ cells were T cells (Fig. 3C), demonstrating a strong preference for T cell–specific editing. Formulation F48 achieved this high T cell specificity with minimal background expression in other cell types within the spleen, liver, and lung. These results showcase the ability of F48 to achieve targeted gene editing in T cells while avoiding off-target effects in other organs (Fig. 3D). We also measured the size, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of the top 15 sgAi9 RNP formulations (table S2). Our best-performing formulation, F48, exhibited a slightly negative zeta potential of −2.07 mV and achieved a relatively high EE of 73.8%, outperforming most other formulations in the clusters. To further elucidate the mechanism of cellular uptake of the F48 formulation, we conducted an uptake pathway study in HEK Ai9 cells using Cy5-labeled LNPs. Our results indicate that the major pathways of internalization for F48 are caveolae-mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis; both mechanisms were reported in other mRNA-loaded LNPs (39, 40) (fig. S8).

To further validate these findings, we performed confocal microscopy imaging on spleen tissue sections from Ai9 mice treated with the optimized formulation F48 (Fig. 3E and fig. S9). The tissue was stained for CD45 and CD3 to identify immune cell populations. The imaging revealed bright tdTomato expression colocalized with CD45+ and CD3+ immune cells, confirming successful gene editing in T cells within the spleen. These results provide visual confirmation of the spleen-tropic gene editing pattern and the high gene editing of formulation F48 for T cells.

Functional gene editing of CCR5 and PD-1 in C57BL/6 mice

To assess the therapeutic potential of our optimized LNP platform, we conducted functional gene editing of two clinically relevant targets, CCR5 and PD-1, in C57BL/6 mice. CCR5 serves as a co-receptor for HIV entry, and its knockout can confer resistance to HIV infection, whereas PD-1 is an immune checkpoint receptor whose knockout can enhance antitumor immunity by reversing T cell exhaustion.

We first evaluate the efficiency and durability of the CCR5 knockout. F48 LNP-encapsulated sgCCR5 RNPs were administered to C57BL/6 mice via systemic intravenous injection. The treatment regimen consisted of two doses, administered on day 0 and day 7, to enhance gene-editing efficiency. Blood samples were collected on day 5 and day 10 to monitor CCR5 expression in peripheral immune cells, and spleens were harvested on day 15 for comprehensive analysis (Fig. 4A). Flow cytometry was performed on immune cells isolated from the blood (day 5 and day 10) and spleen (day 15) to quantify CCR5 surface expression. The results demonstrated a substantial, time-dependent reduction in CCR5 expression in T cells (CD3+ and CD3+CD4+) (Fig. 4B). The F48 LNP treatment group showed 45 and 40% reduction in CD4+ T cells on day 5 and day 10, respectively. The results were further validated through Western blot analysis. Compared to the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group, CCR5 protein levels decreased by 54 and 49% following F48 LNP treatment on day 10 and day 15, respectively, yielding consistent results with the flow cytometry data (Fig. 4C and fig. S11). This confirmed the successful knockout of CCR5 at both the protein and cellular levels. These results demonstrate that our LNP platform can achieve efficient and durable CCR5 knockout in vivo with potential applications in HIV resistance and other therapeutic contexts.

We next investigated the functional knockout of PD-1 in C57BL/6 mice using our optimal LNP formulation. Given that the mouse PD-1 knockout sgRNA sequence is well documented in the literature, we anticipated higher gene-editing efficiency compared to CCR5. Mice were immunized with 10 μg of OVA mRNA (mOVA) LNP to stimulate the PD-1 expression in the spleen 14 days before the RNP injection. On day 14, mice were administered a single dose of 50 μg of LNP-encapsulated sgPD-1 RNPs through intravenous administration, and the spleens were harvested 10 days postinjection for analysis (Fig. 4D). Flow cytometry revealed a 70% reduction in PD-1 expression in CD45+CD3+ T cells and an 85% reduction in CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 4E). The reduction of PD-1 in CD8+ T cells is most obvious as PD-1 is a key immune checkpoint receptor that suppresses T cell activity; its knockout can enhance antitumor immunity by reversing T cell exhaustion and restoring cytotoxic function. Western blot analysis of splenic protein lysates further confirmed a decrease in PD-1 protein levels, validating the knockout at the molecular level (Fig. 4F and fig. S13). Compared to the PBS group, PD-1 protein levels decreased by 60% following F48 LNP treatment on day 10. These results demonstrate the ability of our LNP platform to achieve efficient and functional PD-1 knockout, highlighting its potential for cancer immunotherapy. We also compared the gene-editing efficiency between CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets. For both CCR5 and PD-1 knockouts, CD8+ T cells accounted for ~61 and ~70%, respectively, of the total edited CD3+ T cells (fig. S14). These results suggest that this LNP platform is more effective in editing CD8+ T cells, a subset crucial for antitumor immune responses.

Motivated by this finding, we next evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of our system in a tumor model by using a B16-OVA melanoma model in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 5A). Mice were subcutaneously vaccinated with 3 × 105 B16-OVA tumor cells and treated with mOVA with Moderna LNPs on days 4 and 11. Mice either received intravenous injection of F48 LNPs loaded with sgPD-1 RNPs (50 μg) or intraperitoneal injection of anti–PD-1 antibody on days 5 and 12 as ICB therapies. Mice treated with sgPD-1 RNP LNPs showed substantially reduced tumor growth compared to both control groups, with and without anti–PD-1 antibody (Fig. 5B), indicating the efficacy of gene editing of the RNP LNPs in enhancing antitumor responses. Tumors were harvested on day 18, and flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed a 27.5 ± 9.0% and 35.3 ± 11.5% reduction in PD-1 expression on CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells, respectively, in the sgPD-1 RNP LNP–treated group (Fig. 5, C and D). These findings confirmed successful in vivo editing of Pdcd1, leading to reduced PD-1 expression and improved tumor suppression. We also assessed PD-1 expression on CD3+CD4+ T cells within the tumors (fig. S15). However, no substantial difference was observed between the sgPD-1 RNP LNP–treated group and the anti–PD-1 antibody-treated group, suggesting that the editing or turnover of PD-1 in CD4+ T cells may be less responsive under these conditions.

Systematic screening of LNP formulations for efficient Cre recombinase delivery

To demonstrate the versatility of our LNP platform, we next evaluated its ability to deliver Cre recombinase, a physiologically distinct protein compared to RNPs. Unlike RNPs, which are slightly negatively charged and have a relatively higher molecular weight (150 kDa), Cre recombinase is smaller (38 kDa) and has a higher isoelectric point, resulting in a more neutral and positive charge under physiological conditions. We conducted the same HTS process using the 486-formulation library and identified a higher number of positive hits in formulations containing 18PG and 14PA helper lipids (Fig. 6, A and B). These anionic lipids likely facilitated better complexation with Cre recombinase due to is slightly positive charge.

To further analyze these trends, we applied the same ML pipeline used for RNP screening. Key findings from the screening revealed that formulations with 80% combined ionizable and helper lipid content showed higher gene-editing efficiency, and we have seen the similar trend in RNP formulations (Fig. 6C). In addition, a medium lipid-to-protein ratio of 20 achieved optimal gene editing, striking a balance between cargo loading and delivery efficiency. Reducing the PEG concentration also improved gene editing, likely by minimizing steric hindrance and enhancing cellular uptake and endosomal escape. Similar to our RNP results, formulations with unsaturated helper lipid tails (e.g., DOPE and 18PG) outperformed those with saturated tails, likely due to improved membrane fluidity and endosomal escape. These results highlight the adaptability of our LNP platform for delivering diverse protein cargoes, including Cre recombinase, while providing insights into the formulation parameters that drive efficient gene editing.

To identify the most effective formulations for Cre recombinase delivery in vivo, we followed the same workflow and conducted a cluster-mode screening study using the top 50 formulations, grouped into 10 clusters (Fig. 6D). The results revealed that the top 15 formulations were predominantly composed of DDAB, 18PG, and 14PA helper lipids, overlapping our in vitro findings (table S3). We then harvested the spleen, liver, and lung to check the gene-editing efficiency of the cell populations of these major organs. Similar to the delivery of RNPs, gene-editing events were primarily observed in the spleen, with 15 ± 4% of CD45+ immune cells showing tdTomato expression after being treated with the top formulation F53, indicating successful Cre-mediated recombination (Fig. 6E). We also evaluated the size, zeta potential, and EE of the top 15 Cre LNP formulations (table S4). The best-performing formulation, F52, exhibited a near-neutral zeta potential of −0.73 mV and achieved an EE of 95%, indicating highly efficient encapsulation of Cre recombinase.

Further analysis of the tdTomato-positive cell populations revealed a distinct distribution compared to RNP delivery. Although RNP-mediated gene editing was largely restricted to T cells, Cre recombinase delivery resulted in a broader range of edited immune cell types, particularly B cells and CD11c+ dendritic cell (DC)–like cells (Fig. 6F). This suggests that Cre recombinase, due to its smaller size and neutral charge, may have greater accessibility to diverse immune cell populations within the spleen. The optimal formulation achieved efficient gene editing across multiple cell types, demonstrating the versatility of our LNP platform for delivering diverse protein cargoes.

The biodistribution of top-performing formulations reveals cell type–dependent intracellular trafficking

To assess the biodistribution and cellular uptake of our LNPs, we formulated the top-performing RNP LNP (F48 LNP) and Cre LNP (F53 LNP) with Cy5-labeled DMG-PEG and tracked their distribution using IVIS imaging at 6, 24, and 48 hours postinjection. Unexpectedly, both LNPs exhibited similar biodistribution patterns, with most of the signal accumulating in the liver (~60%) and less than 10% detected in the spleen across all time points (Fig. 7, A and E, and figs. S18 and S19). The RNP LNP had a particle size of around 200 nm (Fig. 7D), similar to nucleic acid–based LNPs, which may explain their comparable biodistribution. The Cre LNP also measured around 200 nm (Fig. 7H), with a polydispersity index (PDI) similar to nucleic acid–based LNPs, further supporting the observed similarity in distribution patterns. We also assessed the in vivo safety profile of the lead LNP formulations carrying either RNP or Cre recombinase by systemically administering the LNPs into C57BL/6 mice. Mice were monitored for changes in body weight and serum cytokine levels as indicators of systemic toxicity. No significant weight loss was observed in LNP-treated mice compared to the PBS control group over a 7-day period postinjection (fig. S20), indicating minimal systemic toxicity. Furthermore, serum levels of key cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-10, IL-4, and IL-12, all remained within baseline levels, with no statistically significant elevation observed at 1, 3, and 7 days posttreatment (fig. S21). These findings suggest that the lead LNP formulations are well tolerated in vivo and do not elicit acute systemic inflammation under the tested conditions.

Despite the substantial accumulation of LNPs in the liver, gene-editing efficiency remained low, suggesting that efficient intracellular trafficking, including cellular uptake and endosomal escape, did not sufficiently occur in liver cells. Conversely, although only a small fraction of LNPs (7.6 ± 0.27%) localized to the spleen, a substantial level of gene editing was observed, indicating that the LNPs successfully entered target cells and completed intracellular processing.

Flow cytometry analysis further revealed distinct cellular uptake profiles within the spleen. RNP LNPs were predominantly taken up by T cells, with 48 ± 7.8% of Cy5+ immune cells at 6 hours, followed by a gradual decline over time (Fig. 7, B and C). In contrast, Cre LNPs showed higher uptake levels in B cells (21 ± 2.9% of Cy5+ immune cells) and DC-like CD11c+ cells (53 ± 8.2% of Cy5+ immune cells) at 6 hours, with a similar decreasing trend over time (Fig. 7, F and G). These findings suggest that the initial uptake of LNPs by specific immune cell populations plays a crucial role in determining the subsequent efficiency of gene editing.

The distinct uptake profiles of RNP LNPs and Cre LNPs highlight the importance of tailoring LNP formulations for targeted gene editing in specific immune cells. Although protein-LNPs exhibit similar accumulation levels in the liver and spleen as mRNA-LNPs, their ability to achieve spleen-targeted delivery may be attributed to the drastic changes in LNP physical properties induced by the protein cargo, particularly its enhanced endosomal escape capacity. This property likely makes protein-LNPs more suitable for immune cell uptake and processing compared to liver cells, further emphasizing the need for cargo-specific LNP design in therapeutic applications.

DISCUSSION

Although LNP-mediated RNP delivery for in vivo gene editing has been reported, most studies have primarily targeted the liver and lung (23, 41). A spleen-tropic, T cell–targeting LNPs for RNP delivery has not been reported previously. Furthermore, existing studies often repurpose LNP formulations originally designed for nucleic acid payloads, lacking a methodical optimization of LNP compositions for protein delivery. This is a major limitation as proteins differ fundamentally from nucleic acids in terms of size, charge, and stability, necessitating tailored optimization of the delivery system. This study reports a screening workflow for systematic optimization of LNP composition for protein delivery using T cell–targeted gene editing as the first case study.

In this study, we used an in vivo cluster-mode screening strategy by pooling five LNP formulations per group to identify candidates with strong gene-editing activity and favorable selectivity. One limitation of our cluster-mode screening approach is the potential for false negatives due to signal dilution or overlapping uptake when multiple LNP formulations are delivered together. In theory, a modestly effective formulation may be masked within a cluster containing stronger candidates, especially in the absence of single-particle barcoding. However, our results indicate that top-performing LNPs generally dominate the signal and are consistently identified in the downstream validation step. Moreover, our two-stage screening strategy, combining high-throughput in vitro prioritization with focused in vivo clustering, helps enrich for potent formulations and reduces the likelihood of missing hits. Although we acknowledge that some individual LNP candidates may be overlooked during cluster screening, the overall identification of functionally robust LNPs is not compromised. Compared to barcoding-based approaches, which require a complex formulation process and decoding framework, this method offers a streamlined, scalable, and faster alternative for finding effective formulations across diverse tissues.

Our biodistribution data revealed that most LNPs accumulated in the liver after intravenous administration, consistent with a previous report of LNP-mediated nucleic acid delivery (36). However, despite this liver tropism in terms of in vivo LNP distribution, the gene-editing events were predominantly observed in T cells within the spleen. This discrepancy can be attributed to the distinct endosomal environment of the T cells, which may favor protein delivery and endosomal escape for these studied LNPs, compared to hepatocytes. T cells exhibit a more acidic endosomal pH and a higher density of endosomal escape machinery, both of which could enhance the proton sponge effect of ionizable lipids, leading to more effective cytosolic release of RNPs (42). These findings highlight the importance of tailoring delivery systems to exploit the unique biological features of target cell types and suggest that LNPs can be further optimized to enhance gene-editing efficiency by taking advantage of cell-specific endosomal environments and immune cell properties.

We have previously demonstrated the use of ML to optimize LNP formulations for nucleic acid delivery (43). In this study, we extend this approach to protein delivery, marking the first application of ML analysis to evaluate LNP formulations for gene-editing proteins. The results revealed that the feature importance trends varied between different protein cargoes. For example, a lower percentage of PEGylated lipids was crucial for achieving high gene-editing efficiency with Cre recombinase, but this feature was less impactful for RNP delivery. This difference likely stems from the distinct physicochemical properties of the two proteins, such as size, charge, and stability, which influence their interactions with LNP components. Our ML analysis identified the total percentage of the ionizable lipid and helper lipid as the most influential feature across both cargos. In addition, the ML model highlighted that unsaturated lipid tails and higher ionizable lipid content were consistently important across both cargo types, potentially underscoring their role in enhancing endosomal escape and particle stability. These findings illustrate that ML analysis can provide cargo-specific insights into formulation optimization, enabling the design of tailored LNPs for diverse therapeutic applications. Future models trained on broader, more diverse datasets may uncover additional or context-specific features that influence targeting, potency, or safety.

Understanding the factors that govern cellular tropism is essential for optimizing LNP-mediated gene delivery. In our study, we observed T cell tropism with certain LNP formulations, raising the question of whether this effect is primarily driven by the formulation or by the nature of the cargo. We acknowledge that both components can influence organ and cell-specific delivery. A prior work has demonstrated that lipid composition and properties, including lipid type, charge, and LNP particle size, influence spleen-targeted delivery. On the other hand, protein-based cargos, such as RNPs, are more stable than mRNA and do not require intracellular translation, which may influence their intracellular trafficking and retention. Together, these factors likely contribute to the observed differences in organ and cell-type targeting. To isolate the contributions of formulation versus cargo, we performed a cargo-swapping experiment, delivering RNPs with the F53 LNP formulation (originally used for Cre) and Cre with the F48 LNP formulation (originally used for RNPs). However, the F53 LNPs exhibited a lower EE of RNPs (~52%), whereas F48 LNPs loaded with Cre formed larger and polydisperse particles (~6.3 μm), likely compromising biodistribution and cellular uptake. These findings underscore the physicochemical compatibility constraints between cargo and formulation, which complicate direct comparisons. Nonetheless, we believe that, with optimization to achieve proper encapsulation and particle characteristics, cell-type specificity, including T cell targeting, can be modulated through formulation design.

The successful functional knockout of CCR5 and PD-1 in T cells serves as a robust proof of concept for the efficacy of our RNP-encapsulated delivery system. The CCR5 knockout model demonstrates the potential of this approach for HIV prevention as CCR5 serves as a co-receptor for HIV entry and its deletion confers resistance to HIV infection (44). Similarly, the PD-1 knockout model showcases the potential of this system for tumor immunotherapy as PD-1 is a key immune checkpoint receptor that suppresses T cell activity; its deletion can enhance antitumor immunity by reversing T cell exhaustion, thereby boosting the adaptive immune responses against tumors (45, 46). In our B16-OVA tumor model, systemic delivery of sgPD-1 RNPs via LNPs significantly suppressed tumor growth and reduced PD-1 expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, confirming effective in vivo editing. We also observed a CD8+ T cell–skewed editing following systemic administration of our LNPs for PD-1 knockout. Given that CD8+ cytotoxic T cells are the primary mediators of antitumor activity, the efficient editing in this T cell subset may have contributed to the observed therapeutic effect in the B16-OVA model. These results emphasize the translational potential of our platform for checkpoint gene editing in solid tumors.

These findings underscore the promising usage of our formulations in achieving precise and efficient gene editing of T cells, with implications for both infectious disease and cancer immunotherapy. Further investigations could evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CCR5 and PD-1 knockout LNPs in preclinical models, as well as the potential for targeting other immune checkpoints or disease-related genes in T cells, which could open emerging avenues for precision medicine in immunotherapy.

In summary, we demonstrated the potential of LNP-encapsulated RNPs for efficient T cell gene editing in the spleen in vivo. By optimizing LNP formulations along with an ML-guided approach, we identified key lipid components and formulation parameters that enhance the in vivo delivery efficiency of gene-editing proteins. Our findings highlight the importance of spleen targeting and the unique endosomal environment of T cells in achieving efficient gene editing. The successful functional knockout of CCR5 and PD-1 serves as a proof of concept for the therapeutic potential of our platform. These insights pave the way for future studies aimed at further refining LNP formulations, assessing therapeutic efficacy in preclinical models, and expanding the applications of this platform to other immune cell types and disease targets. Overall, our work contributes to the advancement of protein delivery and gene-editing technologies, offering a versatile and efficient platform for immune cell–specific therapies with broad implications for both infectious disease and cancer immunotherapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

DLin-MC3-DMA was obtained from MedKoo Biosciences. DSPC, DOPE, 18PG, 14PA, DOTAP, and DMG–PEG-2000 were from Avanti Polar Lipids, and cholesterol was from Sigma-Aldrich. The Cre recombinase (SCR508) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, and CRISPR-Cas9 (CAS-EE109) was purchased from Kactus. Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX(CMAX00015) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The HEK Ai9 cells, a tdTomato reporter cell line, were a gift from K. Leong Lab.

Animals and cell culture

All animal procedures were performed in compliance with protocols approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. MO23E31). C57BL/6 mice (both sexes, 6 to 8 weeks old) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Ai9 mice (male and female, 6 to 8 weeks old) were bred in-house at the Johns Hopkins Animal Facility and randomly assigned to experimental groups. Mice were provided access to standard feed and water. Mice consumed 4 to 5 g of food (~120 g/kg body weight) and 3 to 5 ml of water (~150 ml/kg body weight) daily on overage. The diet typically contained 5% fiber, 20% protein, and 5 to 10% fat. Housing conditions were maintained at 18° to 26°C with 30 to 70% relative humidity and a minimum of 10 air changes per hour. Mice were housed in standard shoebox cages with corncob bedding.

For experiments in Ai9 mice and C57BL/6 mice studies, the LNPs were given intravenously via lateral tail vein injection at a predetermined dose per mouse. For intravenous injection, the LNP suspensions were concentrated to 250 μg protein/ml by an Amicon Ultra-2 centrifugal filter (MilliporeSigma) unit with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 100 kDa. The RNP and Cre LNP formulations were prepared as described above and administered via intravenous injections at a protein dose of 50 μg per mouse.

HEK Ai9 cells, a derivative of HEK 293 cells engineered to harbor the Cre-inducible Ai9 tdTomato reporter cassette, were cultured under standard conditions. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, catalog no. 11965-092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, catalog no. 26140-079), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, catalog no. 15140-122), and 2 mM l-glutamine (optional, Gibco, catalog no. 25030-081) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured at ~70 to 80% confluency using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, catalog no. 25300-054). The medium was changed every 2 days.

LNP screening, production, and characterization

LNPs were prepared by mixing an organic phase containing the lipid components with an aqueous phase containing the protein cargo. The ethanol phase was prepared by dissolving a mixture of ionizable lipid (DLin-MC3-DMA), cholesterol, DMG–PEG-2000, and a helper lipid (DOTAP, DDAB, DOPE, DSPC, 14PA, or 18PG) at predetermined ratios in ethanol. The ethanol phase for the 486 formulations was synthesized in a 96-well plate using a liquid handling robot (Mantis Liquid Dispenser). LNPs were then prepared by mixing the aqueous phase into the ethanol phase at a 9:1 volumetric ratio. For RNP LNPs, the aqueous phase was prepared by incubating Cas9 protein and sgRNA at a 1:1 molar ratio for 10 min in PBS at room temperature (sgRNA sequences provided in table S5). For Cre LNPs, Cre recombinase was dissolved directly in PBS. For high-throughput in vitro screening, LNPs were dosed without dialysis. For in vivo screening, LNPs were formulated in 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tubes through pipette mixing. When synthesized for cluster mode, LNPs were dialyzed using a 100-kDa MWCO cassette against PBS for 6 hours at 4°C and stored at 4°C before injection. LNP hydration radius, PDI, and zeta potential were characterized using dynamic light scattering (ZetaPALS, Brookhaven Instruments) at 25°C, with measurements taken in triplicate. Reported diameters are represented as intensity averages.

The EE of the LNPs was evaluated as follows. LNP samples were spun through a 300-kDa MWCO ultrafiltration tube to separate the free protein from the encapsulated. The resulting flow-through was then dialyzed against PBS using a 10- to 14-kDa MWCO dialysis tube to remove any residual free lipids. The protein concentration in the dialyzed fraction was quantified using the BCA assay, and the EE was calculated on the basis of the ratio of free protein to the total protein input.

ML model training and interpretation

ML model training, optimization, feature refinement, and interpretation were implemented following previously published methods used for pDNA LNP structure-function relationship analysis. Briefly, a highly predictive ML model to predict RNP LNP in vitro delivery efficiency from corresponding formulation features (compositional and chemical) was identified by testing the performance of a panel of various ML models that were trained and hyperparameter optimized on our library screening dataset. Model performance was assessed by calculating the average mean absolute error (MAE) across five repetitions of a fivefold shuffled cross-validation procedure. The best-performing ML model underwent further feature refinement by iteratively removing input parameters and tracking model performance to remove parameters that may be nonimpactful or adversely affecting model performance.

Top ML models were then interrogated using the SHAP package (v0.42.1) to quantify critical design considerations for RNP LNPs. Specifically, SHAP calculates the contribution of each input parameter to model output providing insight into how the ML model predicts RNP LNP transfection efficiency. SHAP summary plots were generated to visualize the relationships between feature values and their effects on model predictions. RNP LNP design rules were extracted by identifying feature value ranges associated with high average SHAP values, indicative of a strong positive impact on model output (transfection efficiency). These ranges provide actionable guidelines for optimizing LNP formulations.

Tissue and blood processing and cell isolation

To isolate cells from the liver, lungs, and spleen of Ai9 mice, harvested tissues were placed on 40-μm cell strainers and mechanically dissociated using the plunger of a 3-ml syringe in PBS. The resulting cell suspensions were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min at 4°C. Pelleted cells were resuspended in ACK lysis buffer and incubated at room temperature for 5 min to lyse red blood cells. Cells were then pelleted again by centrifugation (300g, 5 min, 4°C), washed once with RPMI 1640 medium, and washed two more times before staining for flow cytometry. All procedures were carried out under light-protected conditions.

For immune cell isolation from blood in C57BL/6 mice, 200 μl of blood was collected in EDTA-coated tubes and transferred to 15-ml tubes containing 5 ml of ACK lysis buffer. After brief vortexing, samples were incubated at room temperature for 3 min, centrifuged (300g, 5 min, 4°C), and washed twice with RPMI 1640 medium before use in flow cytometry or Western blot analysis. Spleen cell isolation in C57BL/6 mice followed the same protocol used for Ai9 mice: mechanical dissociation, red blood cell lysis with ACK buffer, and sequential washing and pelleting steps before downstream applications.

Antibodies and staining for flow cytometry

Antibody panels used for flow cytometry are listed in table S6. All antibodies were diluted according to the ratios specified in the table. Isolated cells, prepared as described in the previous section, were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Dead Cell Stain Kits for 30 min on ice in the dark. Then, cells were pelleted and resuspended in 100 μl of antibody cocktail diluted in flow cytometry staining buffer (eBioscience) on ice, protected from light, for 1 hour. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 200 μl of eBioscience staining buffer for flow cytometry analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer, and results were analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for cytokine detection

C57BL/6 mice (n = 4) were injected with one dose of RNPs and Cre LNPs equivalent to 50 μg of the cargo protein. On days 1, 3, and 7, an aliquot of 150 μl of blood sample was drawn from the tail vein. Levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, IL-4, and IL-12 were measured using uncoated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Invitrogen and BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Supernatants were diluted at a volume ratio of 1:3. Optical densities were determined at a wavelength of 450 nm in a plate reader (Bio-Rad).

Western blot

Cells were harvested and lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for protein quantification using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of total protein (15 μg per lane) were mixed with 4× LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen) and 10× Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen), boiled at 80°C for 10 min. Samples were loaded onto a Bolt 4 to 12% Bis-Tris Plus Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and run in Bolt MES Running Buffer at 200 V for 35 min. Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the iBlot 3 Dry Transfer System. Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk in PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-CCR5 antibody (1:1000 dilution, Invitrogen, PA5-78949), anti–PD-1 antibody (1:1000 dilution, Invitrogen, PA5-20350), and anti–α-tubulin antibody (1:4000 dilution, Invitrogen, PA5-19489).

After washing with PBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit, 1:10,000 dilution, Invitrogen 32460) for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were then washed and developed using the SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Imaging System. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH), and relative protein expression levels were normalized to α-tubulin.

Confocal microscopy

Mouse spleens were harvested and embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek) for cryosectioning. Cryosections were cut at a thickness of 10 μm using a cryostat and mounted onto glass slides. Sections were then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 hour. For immunostaining, sections were blocked in 10% normal donkey serum in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-CD3 (1:200 dilution, Abcam, ab135372) and anti-CD45 (1:200, GeneTex, GT0014). After washing 3 × 5 min in PBS, sections were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-mouse (1:1000 dilution, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., 715-165-151) and Cy5-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, 711-175-152). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 15 min at room temperature. Sections were mounted with fluorescence mounting medium (e.g., ProLong Gold Antifade, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and coverslipped.

Imaging was performed using a Zeiss Apotome 3 microscope with 20× and 63× objective lenses, and images were acquired using Zeiss Zen software. Image processing and analysis were conducted using ImageJ software.

Statistics

Unpaired t tests were performed when comparing two groups. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel and Prism 10 (GraphPad). A difference was considered significant if P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).
