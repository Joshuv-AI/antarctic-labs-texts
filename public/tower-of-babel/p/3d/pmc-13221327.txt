Adoption, determinants, and economic efficiency of insect-based technologies for organic waste recycling into fertilizer in Kenya
Authors: Perpetual G Mugendi, Beatrice W Muriithi, Raphael Gitau, Dennis Beesigamukama, Shaphan Y Chia, Teresia G Wamwondwe, Kassie Menale, Chrysantus M Tanga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInappropriate handling of the abundant organic waste generated annually has far-reaching impacts on the environment, economies, and societal well-being. Insect-based technologies are gaining traction in the recycling of organic waste and the recovery of valuable macro- and micronutrients. However, there is little information about the costs and benefits of using black soldier fly (BSF) in recycling organic waste into fertilizer compared to conventional composting. This study determined factors influencing the adoption of BSF and its cost-effectiveness in recycling organic waste using logistic regression, cost-benefit analysis, and sensitivity analysis. A household survey was conducted among 222 households in Kiambu County, Kenya. Results revealed that membership in waste management groups, access to recycling information, and perception of diversification of recycled products significantly and positively influenced the adoption of BSF. Households adopting BSF recycle 3-fold higher volumes of organic waste annually and generate 5-fold higher return on investment than those using conventional composting. Our findings reveal that BSF is a profitable and potentially economically efficient technology for managing organic waste and promoting greener economies. These results should inform research and policy decisions aimed at developing and promoting efficient waste management technologies as well as integrating frass fertilizer production into smallholder cropping systems.

Introduction

Globally, over two billion tonnes of organic waste are generated annually in urban and peri-urban areas, with only 16% of this waste being recycled [1]. In Kenya, 70–80% of the waste generated daily is mainly organic [2]. Yet only 10% of the total organic waste is disposed of correctly in designated dumpsites and recycling facilities [2]. The improper disposal of organic waste leads to economic losses, public health risks, and environmental degradation [3, 4]. Additional threats include unpleasant odors, the release of greenhouse gases such as methane and carbon dioxide, breeding sites for disease-causing organisms, and disruption of biogeochemical cycles [5–7]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for local solutions and technologies that efficiently bio-convert organic waste into valuable products while promoting environmental restoration [8].

Efficient bioconversion of organic waste in closed-loop economies is multifaceted due to the inefficiency of recycling and composting technologies, inadequate collection and disposal infrastructure, and weak enforcement of laws and regulations, which often pave the way for illegal littering and dumping [9]. Moreover, there is a lack of public awareness of the potential economic benefits of efficient and cost-effective waste management practices [10]. Conventionally, organic waste is managed through composting to produce bio-fertilizers [11]. However, conventional composting methods are slow, and if not adequately controlled, they can have adverse effects, including direct contamination of surface and drinking water, the transmission of zoonotic diseases, and the emission of methane, carbon dioxide, and offensive odors [12, 13]. Therefore, there is a need for efficient, sustainable, and circular-based bioeconomy interventions for managing organic waste, which can be achieved through insect-based technologies.

The use of insect-based technologies, such as the black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens, BSF), to recycle organic waste into larval biomass for feed and nutrient-rich organic fertilizer with a minimal environmental footprint has gained global attention [14–18]. BSF larvae feed on a range of organic wastes, including food waste (food scraps and any inedible portions of food), livestock waste (manure), and market waste (vegetable and fruit refuse) [19]. The larvae have a high affinity for organic waste and promote closed-loop organic waste management by reducing volumes [20, 21], pathogen concentrations [22], greenhouse gas emissions [23], and heavy metal concentrations [24].

The by-product of this waste bioconversion process called “frass” consists of a mixture of uneaten substrates, insect feces, insect shed exoskeleton and dead insects. Frass has excellent fertilizing properties due to its rich organic matter, macro and micro-nutrients, and trace elements [25–28]. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of frass to improve soil health [29], boost plant growth and yield [14, 27, 30], suppress diseases and pests [31], increase farmers’ profits, and reduce the environmental burden of global warming and the use of synthetic fertilizers [16]. However, some studies have found that the application of BSF can inhibit plant growth due to the phytotoxicity of fresh frass; therefore, they recommend further post-processing to produce stable frass fertilizer [32]. Frass is potentially a promising fertilizer; however, further multidisciplinary research is required to fully evaluate its potential [33].

Despite the steady increase in literature promoting circular bio-economies and sustainable agricultural production through the use of BSF in recycling organic waste into frass, information on the economic aspects of its production is limited [34]. Existing studies have identified the nutritional composition of frass from different waste sources [35], socioeconomic benefits of insect-based feed in poultry production [36], economic value of using frass in maize production BSF as an organic waste recycler [16, 37], and cost benefits of BSF as an alternative fish ingredient [38]. All these studies show the potential benefits of adopting BSF in bio-converting waste into animal feed or frass fertilizer. However, there is limited research on the economic efficiency of producing frass compared to conventional composting among households, which is relevant when exploring the adoption and scalability of frass production.

This paper addresses the knowledge gap by identifying the determinants of adopting BSF in recycling organic waste and estimating the cost-effectiveness of recycling organic waste into organic fertilizer using BSF larvae compared to conventional composting methods. We further discuss the externalities (immaterial benefits) associated with recycling organic waste into fertilizer among households based on existing literature. The study provides information on the economic benefits of using BSF in recycling organic waste compared to conventional composting, thereby supporting the development of this novel frass fertilizer.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Kiambu County, Kenya (Fig. 1). The county was purposively selected based on previous promotional efforts on recycling organic waste conducted by ICIPE and the Kenyan Government in the area.

Map of households recycling organic waste into fertilizer using BSF and conventional composting in Kiambu, Kenya

Data collection and sampling procedure

The study utilized both primary and secondary data. The primary data was collected through a household-level survey using a semi-structured questionnaire with approval from the Egerton University Ethics and Review Committee (EUREC). The data entailed sociodemographic characteristics such as (age, years of schooling, gender, size of household, size of land for recycling, perceptions), institutional factors (such as distance to the source of waste, distance to the main market outlet, group membership, access to credit, access to extension services). Other variables included waste streams and types (such as livestock waste, market waste, farmyard waste, and kitchen waste), production requirements (including start-up capital, operational and maintenance costs), and marketing (including market price and main market outlets). A pretest was conducted to test the validity of the questionnaire. The secondary data was collected from reviews of relevant published and grey literature from research institutions and government agencies.

This study targeted households that were already engaged in recycling organic waste into fertilizer, using either BSF technology or conventional composting methods. This approach allowed us to investigate factors that influence the adoption of BSF among households with comparable baseline practices. While the study area had been exposed to promotional initiatives by ICIPE and the Kenyan Government, these efforts were community-wide and non-discriminatory. They did not involve selective targeting of farmers based on socioeconomic or demographic characteristics. Therefore, the sampling approach does not introduce selection bias in relation to the explanatory variables in our model.

The study employed a multistage sampling technique. In the first stage, Kiambu County was purposively selected, as detailed in the previous section. In the second stage, two wards from each of the 12 sub-counties in Kiambu County, where the majority of organic fertilizer producers reside, were purposively selected. Using a list of households recycling organic waste into fertilizer provided by icipe and the County Department of Agriculture, Livestock, and Fisheries, a sample of the survey respondents was then randomly selected at the third stage. Proportion probability to size technique was used to determine the sample size to be randomly selected for each ward.

The sample size was calculated at a 95% confidence level and 0.05 precision, as suggested by [39] when the population is known.

where n is the sample size, N is the target population, and e is the acceptable margin of error. The sample of 222 households comprised 105 that used BSF, and the remaining 117 households used conventional (windrow) composting to recycle organic waste into fertilizer. This sample size was representative of households that recycle organic waste into fertilizer, and the methodology can be replicated in other regions or countries in studies conducted in similar contexts where exposure to promotional initiatives has occurred. Moreover, the sample was derived from a heterogeneous population with no significant demographic and socioeconomic characteristics that could affect the results. The EXCEL tool and STATA software were used to conduct descriptive analysis and econometric models.

Model specification

Logistic regression model

The study adopted a logistic regression model to determine the factors influencing the choice of BSF for recycling organic waste into fertilizer. These factors include household characteristics, institutional factors, and perceived benefits [40–43]. The promotion of BSF technology in the study area was executed through broad-based community and public training sessions. These initiatives did not entail selective targeting based on socioeconomic or institutional attributes. As such, there is no evidence that socioeconomic factors, such as education level, household size, or group affiliation, were utilized as criteria in the dissemination of information. We, therefore, treat these variables as exogenous in our regression model.

The logistic model was preferred over Ordinary Least Squares and Probit because it corrects the linearity problem by transforming the dependent variable into logs, allowing for the modeling of nonlinear dependence and a more straightforward interpretation [44]. Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA) methodology

This study adopted an allocative efficiency perspective and concepts from the cost-benefit analysis theory to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of using BSF compared to conventional composting. Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) indicators were used in estimating the cost-effectiveness of using BSF in recycling organic waste into fertilizer compared to conventional composting. This study adopted the approach used by Mogaka et al. [45] and [46] and employed four decision indicators: net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), return on investment (ROI), benefit-cost ratio, and sensitivity analysis to test for the robustness of the NPV.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis identifies critical variables whose positive or negative variations have a significant impact on the NPV of the recycling method. In this study, the optimistic-pessimistic scenario was applied. It involved assigning optimistic (most favorable) and pessimistic (least favorable) values and computing the effect on NPV, ceteris paribus. Based on the assumptions of a perfect market, an arbitrary 10% change in the selected variables was used. Critical variables were variables in which a variation of +/- 10% of the value in the base case had a more than 10% variation on NPV [48].

Results

Determinants of adopting BSF in recycling organic waste into fertilizer

The sociodemographic and institutional characteristics were statistically and significantly different among households using BSF and conventional composting (Table 1). The average age of heads of households using BSF was 44 years, with 16 years of formal schooling and 2 years of experience in organic waste recycling. Their counterparts, the household heads involved in windrow composting, were, on average, 56 years old, with 11 years of education and 5 years of experience in organic waste recycling. Both practices have been introduced in recent years by the County Government and ICIPE, respectively, with BSF being the latest.

The logistic regression model demonstrated a good fit to the data (Table 2). The model’s results indicated a good fit to the data as the predictors included in the model adequately explained the adoption of BSF in recycling organic waste (pseudo-R2 value of 0.796). Variables that significantly influenced the household choice of BSF technology in recycling organic waste into fertilizer included the age of the household head, number of years spent schooling, size of land explicitly used for recycling organic waste into fertilizer (production unit), number of social associations/ groups a household head belongs, access to agricultural/environmental extension information, and household perception of recycling organic waste into diverse products. It is important to note that the perception of recycling organic waste into diverse products reflects a general attitude towards recycling rather than an attitude towards BSF specifically. As such, it reflects farmers’ predisposition towards organic waste management prior to selecting the method of recycling.

While the study was conducted in a study area where BSF technology had been promoted, it is crucial to emphasize that the initiatives were intended for general outreach rather than targeting specific groups of farmers. Cost-effectiveness of using BSF in recycling organic waste into fertilizer

The initial capital investment for BSF is, on average,31-fold ($232.4) higher than conventional composting ($7.6) (Table 1), spread evenly across a period of 10 years. The contribution of the various cost components to the total investment cost is illustrated in (Fig. 2a and b). There is a variation in the cost components between the two methods, which is driven by the variation in the production process. Labor costs constitute the most significant proportion of variable costs in both methods, at 53.28% for BSF and 54.14% for conventional composting (Fig. 2c and d), revealing the labor-intensive nature of recycling organic waste.

On average, households using BSF recycle 3.4-fold higher (38.5 tonnes) volume of organic waste annually than households using conventional composting (11.5 tonnes). Recycling 1 MT of organic waste with BSF yielded a substantially 36-fold higher average profit ($417.82) and 3-fold higher gross margin (55.4%) than using conventional composting, which yielded an average profit of $11.25 and gross margin of 16.6% (Table 3) without accounting for the market value of family labor. In a scenario where we account for family labor as an opportunity cost of time, income is reduced by 21% and 80% among households using BSF and conventional composting, respectively, highlighting the significant contribution of family labor, especially among households using conventional composting.

Discussion

Determinants of choice of adopting BSF technology for recycling organic waste into fertilizer

The results above reveal that household characteristics, institutional factors, and perceived benefits significantly influence household choice to adopt BSF technology for recycling organic waste. The age of the household head was a significant negative predictor. The marginal effects (Table 2) indicate that as the age of the household head increases by one year, the probability of adopting BSF for recycling organic waste into fertilizer decreases. This implies that households reduce their likelihood of adopting technologies as the head of the household ages. The plausible reason for this outcome is the high labor requirement for sourcing and pre-treating the waste before recycling using BSF larvae. Since older farmers have less energy than younger ones, they are likely to adopt a recycling approach that requires minimal effort and time. Our findings are consistent with those of Mwangi and Kariuki [49] and Zondo [50], who highlight that older farmers are risk-averse compared to their younger counterparts.

Household size was a significant positive predictor. The marginal effect indicates that a one-member increase in household size increases the household’s probability of adopting BSF. This implies that farmers with larger families are more likely to adopt BSF. Labor is an essential input in recycling organic waste. Larger families translate to more labor available for transforming the waste into fertilizer Zondo [50]. The findings were consistent with [51, 52], which stated that larger family-sized households were more likely to adopt agricultural technologies that required a more labor-intensive workforce.

The number of years spent in schooling demonstrated a statistically significant positive association. The marginal effect (Table 2) suggests that an additional year of education increases the household probability of selecting BSF. Farmers with higher levels of formal education are more cognizant of new technologies within relatively short durations [53]. The findings were consistent with those of Sennuga et al. [54], indicating a strong link between education levels and the ability to make informed choices. This is because education empowers farmers to interpret, perceive, and respond to new technologies and practices, thereby justifying their efficient production performance. Hence, farmers with higher education are more likely to embrace BSF technology for recycling organic waste.

The size of land used for recycling organic waste into fertilizer (production unit) demonstrated a statistically significant negative association. The marginal effect indicates that an increase in the size of land required for recycling organic waste by a square pace (th acre) reduces a household’s probability of adopting BSF. This implies that the less land requirement for recycling increases the likelihood of using new technologies like BSF. This is because land is a scarce resource, especially among vulnerable households and those working in informal sectors. These results are consistent with those of Hanboonsong et al. [55] and Kibaara et al. [39], who reported that insect-based technologies for managing organic waste are efficient in space.

The number of social associations or groups to which a household head belonged was a significant positive predictor. The associated marginal effect (Table 2) suggests that an increase in the number of social associations or groups to which the household head belonged increases a household’s probability of adopting BSF. This reveals that belonging to a social group fosters social networking and knowledge exchange about the advantages of new technologies [56]. The findings complement those of Sseguya et al. [57], who observed that belonging to a group fosters peer learning and the consolidation of inputs through community banking models, thereby encouraging the adoption of technologies.

Access to agricultural and environmental extension information on recycling organic waste was a significant positive predictor of the outcome. The corresponding marginal effect indicates that households that accessed extension information had an increased probability of adopting BSF. These results suggest that access to extension information enhances awareness and increases the likelihood of adopting BSF technology for managing organic waste. Our findings were similar to those of [50], which reported that extension agents are essential service providers who empower farmers with relevant agricultural and environmental technologies to enhance their productivity and profit margins. This emphasizes the importance of extension information in disseminating and promoting agricultural technologies and innovations.

Respondents’ perception of recycling organic waste into diverse products was a significant positive predictor of their attitudes toward recycling. The marginal effect indicates that respondents who perceived recycling of organic waste as a potential diversification process for soil amendment, animal protein source, and bioenergy were more likely to choose BSF than those who did not perceive recycling as having potential diversified products. The results suggest that perception is a crucial factor influencing the adoption of insect-based technologies. These findings were consistent with those of Liu et al. [28] and Salam et al. [58], which stated that the household perception of using BSF technology to convert organic waste into diverse, economically viable products improved its adoption.

Economic efficiency of using BSF larvae in recycling organic waste into fertilizer

The cost-benefit analysis indicators and sensitivity analysis results reveal that using BSF in recycling organic waste into fertilizer is economically efficient compared to conventional composting. The high NPV is attributed to the reduced time for recycling organic waste using BSF and the high selling price per kg of frass. It reveals the high potential of BSF as an emerging and profitable livelihood opportunity for households living in peri-urban areas where organic waste is abundant. These results are consistent with a study by Munthali et al. [38] and Groeneveld et al. [59], which found that BSF farming was economically viable as an alternative animal feed ingredient. A study by Beesigamukama et al. [16] on the economic value of frass found that investing in frass fertilizer production among farmers rearing insects increased their income significantly. Moreover, value addition increases frass marketability and value, resulting in higher profit margins.

The multiple recycling cycles (7–8 times annually) and higher volumes of organic waste recycled (7.5 MT annually) indicate that households recycling organic waste using BSF recycled more waste compared to those using conventional composting methods. To a large extent, these findings unveil the potential of BSF technology in managing organic waste. Moreover, food and market waste, which constitutes a large proportion of organic waste illegally disposed of [60], was the primary type of waste recycled using BSF, further emphasizing the role of BSF in promoting bio-circular economies, especially in urban and peri-urban areas. Additionally, the BSF labor cost per man-day was higher compared to conventional composting, implying that BSF offers job opportunities for unskilled and semi-skilled labor. On the other hand, labor accounts for a large proportion of operating costs [34], which necessitates investing in local solutions that reduce labor requirements, such as waste segregation at the source, to increase the gross margins accrued from recycling waste.

The difference in IRR between the two recycling methods is attributed to the variations in cash flow and initial capital requirement. BCR attempts to summarize the overall monetary value of an investment. The higher BCR for using BSF implies that the overall monetary benefits of recycling organic waste into frass are greater than those of conventional composting. The ROI indicates that BSF’s profitability was 5-fold higher than that of conventional composting. This work presents the first discussion on the cost-effectiveness of recycling organic waste using black soldier fly (BSF) larvae compared to conventional composting. The CBA indicators indicate that recycling organic waste into fertilizer is a cost-effective approach. Additionally, it can provide a secondary source of income for farmers, cushioning them against high fertilizer prices while promoting regenerative agriculture.

Externalities associated with recycling organic waste into fertilizer

The cost-effectiveness of recycling organic waste into fertilizer using BSF larvae also involves determining immaterial benefits (externalities), such as the effects on soil health, crop yield, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and the social impact of adopting BSF compared to conventional composting. Recycling organic waste generates both positive and negative non-monetary externalities, which are critical in determining the economic efficiency of recycling organic waste. To explore these externalities, we conducted a systematic literature review using the Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar search engines to identify relevant literature on the use of BSF in organic waste management. After removing duplicates and screening for relevance, we reviewed a total of 35 documents (20 review papers and 15 experimental journal articles) that explicitly discussed the externalities of composting technologies.

The reviewed literature suggests that frass affects bacterial growth and soil enzymes, leading to a significant impact on soil health and fertility [61]. BSF-composted fertilizer could be categorized as an NPK compound fertilizer with an average composition of 3.4% N, 2.9% P2O5, and 3.5% K2O and a C/N ratio of 13–16 [15, 26, 62]. Additionally, frass is a suitable soil amendment that influences the soil’s fungal and bacterial composition and rapidly releases Nitrogen needed for plant absorption [63]. This implies that frass is a potentially efficient substitute for promoting soil health.

The effect of frass on plant growth and health varies depending on the crop varieties and frass type [35]. This is because the nutritional composition of frass is greatly determined by the feed substrate (organic waste) used [64]. Germination indexes improved when frass fertilizer was used compared to conventional compost because frass is a more stable organic fertilizer [12, 18]. Moreover, the rich nutrients and organic matter in frass fertilizer reduce the need to use chemicals for plant disease protection [65], and crops produce higher yields compared to compost from conventional composting [16, 66]. These findings suggest that frass could be a partial or complete substitute for expensive inorganic fertilizers while promoting efficient organic waste management [67].

Recycling organic waste using BSF larvae reduces global warming potential by 50% compared to conventional composting; thus, BSF technology is environmentally friendly and conserves environmental resources [68]. Additionally, recycling organic waste using BSF larvae reduces odor and greenhouse gases (CH4, NH3, and N2O) associated with the bioconversion of organic waste [69, 70]. Moreover, the short waste recycling duration associated with BSF larvae reduces N2O emissions compared to conventional composting, which takes longer [71]. The total ammonia emitted from BSF composting was 0.12 to 10% that of conventional composting, implying that less overall Nitrogen is lost during BSF composting [72]. These results indicate that recycling organic waste using BSF has a lower environmental footprint compared to conventional composting, making it a more environmentally efficient approach.

Using BSF in recycling organic waste promotes social interaction through learning from one another and creates employment opportunities through the demand for additional labor. Social interaction attracts the technical know-how required for adopting and implementing new technologies. Adopting these technologies creates job opportunities, ultimately leading to social impact [73, 74]. Although the additional labor cost implies a welfare loss to the farmer, the employment created benefits society, resulting in improved societal welfare and economic gain.

Conclusion and policy recommendations

This study discusses the factors influencing the adoption of BSF and its cost-effectiveness in recycling organic waste into fertilizer. We demonstrate that BSF has significant potential in recycling organic waste, particularly in urban and peri-urban areas, due to the diverse nature of the organic waste being recycled and the multiple possible production cycles. For every tonne of waste recycled, BSF was profitable and cost-effective, with a 5-fold higher return on investment than conventional composting. Moreover, the use of BSF in recycling organic waste has lower GHG emissions, while the frass fertilizer promotes regeneration of soil health, which increases crop yield.

It is essential to highlight the economic potential of utilizing BSF in promoting the recycling of organic waste within a circular and green economy framework. Increasing the uptake of BSF in recycling organic waste into fertilizer will require awareness campaigns and capacity-building programs among households, governments, civil society organizations, and private sector actors in the waste management and fertilizer industries. These results should inform further BSF feasibility studies, efficient waste management programs, and livelihood diversification efforts in areas with high waste abundance.

Limitations of the study and future research

This study offers valuable insights into the socioeconomic and institutional factors that influence the adoption of BSF in recycling organic waste, as well as the economic benefits it provides compared to conventional composting methods. However, several limitations should be acknowledged to inform interpretation and guide future research. First, the study’s sample was drawn from a population where both ICIPE and government stakeholders had promoted the recycling of organic waste. The methodology can be replicated in similar contexts where the population has been exposed to outreach and promotional initiatives.

Second, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to entirely rule out reverse causality of key explanatory variables such as perceptions and access to information. While we believe this risk is mitigated by the randomized nature of promotion campaigns among adopters and non-adopters in the same region, future research should consider using longitudinal or experimental designs to establish robust causal relationships. Third, while our cost-benefit analysis covered the economic side of recycling organic waste, it did not include locally measured environmental or social benefits. Instead, we reviewed relevant literature to highlight these externalities. Although this provides valuable context, we acknowledge that not having this data from our sample is a limitation. Future research should consider a more comprehensive assessment of the environmental and social benefits of recycling organic waste using BSF technology compared to conventional composting.
