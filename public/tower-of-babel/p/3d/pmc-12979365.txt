Hybrid Quantum-Classical Algorithm for Robust Optimization via Stochastic-Gradient Online Learning
Authors: Debbie Lim, Joao F. Doriguello, Patrick Rebentrost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Optimization theory has been widely studied in academia and finds a large variety of applications in industry. The different optimization models in their discrete and/or continuous settings have catered to a rich source of research problems. Robust convex optimization is a branch of optimization theory in which the variables or parameters involved have a certain level of uncertainty. In this work, we consider the online robust optimization meta-algorithm by Ben-Tal et al. and show that for a large range of stochastic subgradients, this algorithm has the same guarantee as the original non-stochastic version. We develop a hybrid quantum-classical version of this algorithm and show that an at most quadratic improvement in terms of the dimension can be achieved. The speedup is due to the use of quantum state preparation, quantum norm estimation, and quantum multi-sampling. We apply our quantum meta-algorithm to examples such as robust linear programs and robust semidefinite programs and give applications of these robust optimization problems in finance and engineering.

Introduction

Robust optimization and online algorithms

Robust convex optimization is a form of convex optimization with the additional restriction that the optimization data/parameters belong to an uncertainty set and all constraints must hold for every parameter in the set. Moreover, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {D}\subseteq \mathbb {R}^n$$\end{document} and the uncertainty set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {U}\subseteq \mathbb {R}^d$$\end{document} are both convex. Such a restrictive setting addresses the natural issue of data inaccuracies in many convex optimization problems, which can lead to substantial fluctuations in the solution. Robust convex optimization was first introduced by Ben-Tal and Nemirovski (1998), which had since then been explored and studied in more depth (Ben-Tal and Nemirovski 2002; Ben-Tal et al. 2009; Bertsimas et al. 2011). Despite its theoretical and empirical achievements, the computational cost of adopting robust optimization to large scale problems can become highly prohibitive. In order to tackle this issue, Ben-Tal et al. (2015) proposed meta algorithms to approximately solve the robust counterpart of a given optimization problem using only an algorithm for the original optimization formulation. Their approach for achieving an efficient oracle-based robust optimization was to reduce the problem to a feasibility question and use a primal-dual technique based on online learning tools such as online (sub)gradient descent (Zinkevich 2003).

The work of Ben-Tal et al. (2015) is one of the several recent results that employed online algorithms in the development of learning and optimization algorithms (Bottou 1999; Hazan et al. 2007; McMahan and Streeter 2010; Shalev-Shwartz 2012; Hazan 2016; Hazan et al. 2016; Fotakis et al. 2020; Arora 2009). Unlike offline algorithms which return a solution based on a complete set of input data, online algorithms produce an output based on partial input data. Feedback in the form of a loss function is received from (a possibly adversarial) nature and the online algorithm uses information from the feedback, together with other pieces of input data, to update its strategy and outputs a better solution in the next iteration. This process continues in a sequential manner throughout all iterations. There are two quantities often associated with online algorithms: the offline loss and the regret. The offline loss is defined as the minimum loss suffered when choosing the same strategy that minimizes the losses throughout all iterations. On the other hand, the regret is the difference between the offline loss and the loss from some sequence of strategies. Besides taking computational resources such as time and space into account, the design of online algorithms also aims to minimize the regret. One of the attributes of online algorithms is that their regret bounds hold even for worst-case inputs.

Our results

In this work, we are interested in providing a quantum speedup for robust optimization in the online and oracle setting of Ben-Tal et al. (2015). We stay close to the near-optimal classical framework of Ben-Tal et al. to show possible theoretical advantages. The near-optimal setting of this algorithm makes “dequantization” hard as to our knowledge, no asymptotically better algorithm exists. To this end, we formally specify the input model which allows us to consider the total time complexity of the algorithm. In finance, the Global Maximum Return Portfolio (GMRP) problem aims to maximize the return of an investment without considering the variance of the market. Related work

Online convex optimization seeks a solution from a convex set that optimizes a convex objective function in an online manner (Shalev-Shwartz 2012; Hazan 2016). The commonly used convex optimization techniques include variants of (sub)gradient descent, mirror descent, coordinate descent (Zinkevich 2003; Hazan 2016; Flaxman et al. 2005; Helmbold and Warmuth 2007; Wang and Banerjee 2014), the multiplicative weight update method by Arora and Kale (2007), and Newton’s method (Schraudolph et al. 2007). Applications of these techniques can be found in Arora et al. (2012), Helmbold and Warmuth (2009), and Helmbold et al. (1998). Online convex optimization has wide applications in various fields such as telecommunication (Belmega et al. 2022), aviation (Patel and Goulart 2011; Liu et al. 2017), and finance (Li and Hoi 2014; Kim and Giannakis 2017),

Freund and Schapire (1997) showed that the multiplicative weight update method can be applied in the general decision-theoretic setting of online learning, and thus in a more general class of learning problems. The same technique was also used to develop a boosting algorithm that does not require the performance of the weak learning algorithm to be known a priori. The goal of such boosting algorithms is to convert a weak and inaccurate learner into a stronger and more accurate one. Ben-Tal et al. (2015) provided two online meta-algorithms for solving a robust optimization problem: the first algorithm, which is the main algorithm of this work, approximately solves the problem using the dual subgradient method, assuming that noise vectors belong to a convex uncertainty set. Their second algorithm removes the convexity assumption on the uncertainty set but assumes some linearity properties on the constraint functions, and uses a “pessimization oracle” to find the worst-case noise assignment. The survey by Bertsimas et al. (2011) compiled the results of robust linear, quadratic, semidefinite, and discrete optimization on different uncertainty sets, such as ellipsoidal, polyhedral, cardinality constraint, and norm uncertainties.

In the quantum setting, the runtime of a number of classical online optimization algorithms have been improved quadratically. The quantum AdaBoost algorithm by Arunachalam and Maity (2020) achieves a quadratic speedup in the Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension of the weak learner’s hypothesis class as compared to the classical AdaBoost by Freund and Schapire (1997). The quantum AdaBoost is capable of achieving such an objective in a short time using quantum techniques such as amplitude amplification and estimation (Brassard et al. 2002) and quantum mean estimation (Brassard et al. 2011; Nayak and Wu 1999). Subsequently, Izdebski and de Wolf (2023) provided a substantially faster and simpler quantum boosting algorithm based on the SmoothBoost algorithm by Servedio (2001). In the context of zero-sum games, van Apeldoorn and Gilyén (2019) showed how to efficiently sample from the Gibbs distribution, leading to a quadratic speedup compared to its classical counterpart (Grigoriadis and Khachiyan 1995) in terms of the dimension of the payoff matrix. Furthermore, for the task of classifying n d-dimensional data points, Li et al. (2019) proposed a sublinear time quantum algorithm quadratically better than the classical algorithm by Clarkson et al. (2012). Quantum SDP solvers were developed using the Arora-Kale method (Brandao and Svore 2017; van Apeldoorn et al. 2020).

The computational model and oracles

We use the quantum circuit model (Nielsen and Chuang 2010) whereby an application of a quantum gate is equivalent to performing an elementary operation. In both classical and quantum settings, we assume an arithmetic model that ignores errors from fixed-point representation and each elementary operation takes constant time. The query complexity of a classical/quantum algorithm with some input length is the maximum number of queries the algorithm makes on any input. The time complexity of a classical/quantum computation is in terms of the number of basic gates performed. Definition 1

(Noise memory) Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_1,\dots ,u_m\in \mathbb {R}^d$$\end{document} be a sequence of m vectors that is stored in a classical memory of size O(md). In the quantum setting, we say that we have quantum access to the noise memory if there exists a unitary operator U such that queries can be made in superposition in O(1) time, i.e.,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} U: |{i}\rangle |{j}\rangle |{\bar{0}}\rangle \rightarrow |{i}\rangle |{j}\rangle |{(u_i)_{j}}\rangle , \quad \forall i\in [m],j\in [d], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and updates take O(1) time, as in the classical memory. Our abstract model of noise memory model can be broken down into more standard models, e.g., the classical noise memory can be identified as a random-access memory (RAM), whose entries can be queried in constant time. The quantum noise memory, on the other hand, can be more commonly described as a quantum-readable classical-writable classical memory (QRAM) (Giovannetti et al. 2008b, a; Allcock et al. 2024), which is accessed in superposition by the quantum computer. Even though the O(1) access time might be too strong of an assumption compared to traditional RAM or QRAM models wherein a query requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\text {poly}\log {md})$$\end{document} time, such an overhead would only be polylogarithmic and so we opt to omit it for the sake of clarity. We mention that most QRAM circuits have T-gate count of O(md) and T-gate depth of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\log {md})$$\end{document} (Giovannetti et al. 2008b, a; Arunachalam et al. 2015; Babbush et al. 2018), with new proposals achieving a T-gate count of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\sqrt{md})$$\end{document} and a T-gate depth of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\log \log (md))$$\end{document} (Mukhopadhyay 2025).

(2015), we assume access to a subgradient oracle to access an entry of the subgradient of the constraint functions, a projection oracle to update a noise vector in the noise memory by projecting it back onto the set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {U}$$\end{document}, and an optimization oracle to solve the original non-robust convex optimization problem.

Applications and examples

In this section, we provide a few examples to which our hybrid quantum-classical algorithm can be applied: robust linear and semidefinite programming problems. We shall focus on ellipsoidal uncertainty, being the most common model of data uncertainty since it has a simple parametric representation, can be easily handled numerically, and there are probabilistic arguments which allow the replacement of stochastic uncertainty with ellipsoidal deterministic uncertainty (see Ben-Tal and Nemirovski 1998, 1999 for more information). Moreover, this choice also allows us to compare our results with that of Ben-Tal et al. (2015).

Ben-Tal and Nemirovski (1997) and Ben-Tal et al. The compliance — potential energy stored in the deformed truss — measures the stiffness of the truss. The lesser the compliance, the more stiff the truss is, and hence a greater ability to withstand the weight of the load.

Discussion and conclusion

We studied the problem of robust online convex optimization, where the noise vectors belong to a convex uncertainty set, and all constraints have to be satisfied for all the noise vectors. The online essence of this problem arises from the existence of an optimization oracle that returns a solution based on the noise vectors that are being updated at every iteration.

We devised a hybrid quantum-classical meta-algorithm for the aforementioned problem that runs in time\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{O}\left( \frac{(\sqrt{G_1G_\infty }/G_2)\sqrt{md}\mathcal {T}_\nabla + \min \{G_1G_\infty /G_2^2,m\}\mathcal {T}_{\mathcal {P}} + \mathcal {T}_\epsilon }{\epsilon ^2} \max \{F, D^2G_2^2\}\right)$$\end{document}as compared to the corresponding classical meta-algorithm by Ben-Tal et al. (2015) that runs in time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\big (\frac{D^2G_2^2}{\epsilon ^2}( md\mathcal {T}_\nabla + m\mathcal {T}_{\mathcal {P}} + \mathcal {T}_\epsilon )\big )$$\end{document}. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_1 G_\infty$$\end{document} is comparable to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_2^2$$\end{document}, then we obtain a quadratic improvement in terms of the number of noise vectors m and their dimension d. If, however, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_1 G_\infty = O(mG_2^2)$$\end{document}, then we only achieve a quadratic speedup in d. The speedup is due to techniques such as quantum state preparation, quantum norm estimation, and quantum multi-sampling. Even if, on the one hand, the quantum advantages provided are at most quadratic and depend on quantities like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_\infty$$\end{document}, on the other hand, we argue that our results also show that the meta-framework by Ben-Tal et al. cannot be easily quantized to obtain large speedups, which is a useful consideration, since this framework could be see as a relatively natural candidate for quantization.

The time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal T_{\mathcal {P}}$$\end{document} required by the projection oracle to map the vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u^{\prime (t)}_1, \dots , u^{\prime (t)}_m$$\end{document} back to the convex set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal U$$\end{document} depends on the type/shape of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal U$$\end{document}. For the error analysis, we reproved, in Lemma 1, an upper bound on the regret of the online stochastic (sub)gradient descent (Hazan 2016), where the assumption on the subgradient oracle is slightly changed. Using Lemma 1, we showed that our hybrid quantum-classical algorithm produces a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\epsilon$$\end{document}-approximate solution to the robust optimization problem. We then applied our algorithm to solving robust LPs and robust SDPs whose noise vectors belong to an ellipsoidal uncertainty set, including explicit examples in finance and structural design.

For future work, one can consider more general robust optimization problems where the uncertainty set is not necessarily convex, e.g., robust quadratic programming. Some applications of quadratic programs in finance include the Global Minimum Variance Problem (GMVP) (Goldfarb and Iyengar 2003) and Markowitz mean-variance portfolio optimization problem (Markowitz 1952). A classical meta-algorithm for such robust optimization problems has been proposed by Ben-Tal et al. (2015), called dual-perturbation robust optimization algorithm. It would be interesting to see if quantum techniques could improve the algorithm’s total runtime.
