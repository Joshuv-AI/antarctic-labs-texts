Anemia and iron deficiency in post-kidney transplantation: an unsolved challenge
Authors: Jose Portolés, Rainer Oberbauer, Michele F Eisenga, Aleix Cases, Jolanta Małyszko, Gabriel Choukroun, Marta Crespo, Kai-Uwe Eckardt, Roberto Minutolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnemia and iron deficiency (ID) are common and significant complications in kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) that can affect their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and outcomes. Current anemia guidelines equate the post-transplant situation with the anemia associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) in non-transplanted persons, not acknowledging relevant differences ranging from pathophysiology to clinical manifestation. Nephrologists caring for these patients tend to pay less attention to post-transplant anemia (PTA) and ID than in non-transplanted persons with CKD. In this narrative review we summarize the available evidence about PTA and ID and their specifics in KTRs, including associations with patient and graft survival and poorer HRQoL. The prevalence of anemia is higher in KTRs than in non-transplanted patients with CKD for a given level of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) due to kidney transplant (KT)-specific pathophysiological factors. ID should be detected and corrected in KTRs using oral or intravenous (IV) iron. Some IV iron formulations are associated with an increased risk of hypophosphatemia a typical complication in KTRs. Current guidelines suggest the same hemoglobin targets for erythropoiesis stimulating agent therapy in transplanted and non-transplanted patients, despite the fact that a higher hemoglobin target has been associated with a slower estimated GFR decline in KT. There are insufficient data to recommend the widespread use of hypoxia-inducible factor–prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors in PTA. Red blood cell transfusions should be avoided to minimize alosensitization. We call for increased awareness and targeted trials on anemia and ID in KTRs, accounting for the diverse and specific profiles of these patients.

INTRODUCTION

Anemia and iron deficiency (ID) are common complications in kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) which can affect their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and outcomes. However, the evidence is more limited than in dialysis, or in non-dialysis-dependent (NDD) or dialysis-dependent (DD) chronic kidney disease (CKD). Specific guidelines for diagnosis and management of anemia and ID in KTRs are lacking. Current anemia guidelines equate the post-transplant situation with the anemia in non-transplanted CKD patients, not acknowledging relevant differences ranging from the pathophysiology to clinical manifestation [1–3]. Nephrologists caring for KTRs often focus their efforts on adjusting immunosuppression, prolonging graft survival, and treating infectious or tumor complications, frequently paying little attention to post-transplant anemia (PTA) and ID [4].

In this narrative review, we searched for available evidence from the latest KDIGO guidelines (2012) [1] to March 2025 on the following aspects: PTA and ID incidence, its clinical effects, and how to treat it with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), iron supplements, red blood cell (RBC) transfusions, or new therapies such as hypoxia-inducible factor–prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors (HIF-PHI). When multiple sources were available, we selected those with higher methodological quality, prioritizing meta-analyses, randomized clinical trials (RCT) and large epidemiological studies. For some aspects of the review, the evidence was scarce and of less statistical robustness. We have been cautious in our recommendations, acknowledging when only observational studies support a statement and the evidence from RCT is lacking.

EARLY AND LATE PTA

The definition of PTA is similar to that in non-transplanted adults: hemoglobin (Hb) levels ≤13 g/dL for men and Hb ≤12 g/dL for women, according to the World Health Organization, the American Society of Transplantation and the KDIGO anemia guidelines [1, 5]. PTA typically shows a biphasic pattern occurring early, in the 3 months after kidney transplantation and in the long term, frequently in parallel to a loss of graft function [6].

Early after the surgical procedure, many transplant patients experience anemia. With the restoration of kidney function during the first 3 months, Hb levels gradually rise and may achieve normal values within 12 months [7]. During the early post-transplant period, anemia is influenced by preexisting low Hb levels. Anemia can also be aggravated by blood losses during surgery or frequent blood sampling as well as ID, immunosuppression and viral infections [6, 8, 9]. Aggressive fluid therapy in the early postoperative period may lead to dilutional anemia, as a contributing factor. Delayed graft function (DGF), defined as the failure of the graft to start functioning and resulting in the need for dialysis within the first week after transplantation, can aggravate PTA [8, 9].

A recent meta-analysis including 38 233 patients from 84 studies estimated a mean prevalence of early PTA of 59.8% (range 37.7%–77.2%). After this period, a marked decline in PTA prevalence is observed, reaching its nadir between 24 and 36 months posttransplant, with a median of 27.2% (range 20.5%–39.1%) [6]. Similarly, other studies report a prevalence of PTA of 84%, 40% and 26% at 1, 3 and 6 months, respectively [10–12].

Late PTA is predominantly caused by decreased erythropoiesis as graft function declines, with a pathogenesis similar to that in non-transplanted CKD patients. However, PTA is more frequent than in native CKD with the same estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), because of additional kidney transplant (KT)-specific factors, such as immunosuppressive and antiviral drugs, recurrent episodes of acute or chronic rejection, or a proinflammatory state (Fig. 1) [6, 13, 14]. In European studies, late PTA prevalence ranges from 25% to 41% [6, 11].

The above-mentioned large meta-analysis identified the following risk factors for PTA: low pre-transplant Hb levels, poor allograft function, DGF, acute rejection episodes, high serum interleukin-6 levels [15], cytomegalovirus (CMV) infections, renin–angiotensin system blockade (both angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin-receptor blockers) [13], some immunosuppressive drugs [calcineurin inhibitors (CNI)], mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors and thymoglobulin, as well as African-American ancestry of the transplant recipient [6]. mTOR inhibitors induces an antiproliferative effect and a decreased iron availability, promoting microcytic anemia. Their use is associated with a 2.31-fold higher risk for PTA (95% Confidence interval (CI) 1.00–4.53) [6].

This corresponds to findings in cancer patients, where a meta-analysis with 5436 individuals estimated a 6.03-fold (95% CI 2.76–13.14) risk for anemia as compared with no mTOR inhibitor use [14]. In very rare and severe cases, thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)—characterized by haemolytic anemia—can occur. TMA in transplant recipients is typically triggered by ischemia–reperfusion induced complement activation and the use of mTOR inhibitors or CNI [16].

The incidence of late PTA at 3, 5 and 10 years after transplantation was reported to be 42%, 35% and 93%, respectively [17]. In late stages, PTA prevalence rises in parallel with graft dysfunction. Patients with KT failure who start dialysis exhibit a 67% prevalence of PTA (with hematocrit levels <30%), with a mean hematocrit of 27.5%, similar to non-transplanted CKD patients that start dialysis, although the use of ESA was higher in KTR [18].

PTA AND CLINICAL OUTCOMES

PTA is associated with the composite outcome of all-cause mortality and graft loss [19]. A French cohort study of 4217 KTR found that PTA was associated with an increased risk for all causes and cardiovascular (CV) mortality for patients in CKD stages G1T to G3T, but not in more severe stages of CKD, while graft survival was similar in anemic and non-anemic patients [20]. Another retrospective multicenter study over 639 KTRs identified Hb at first month as an independent factor associated with graft lost but not for mortality, after correct by eGFR, maximum reactive antibody and rejection episodes [21]. A recent large meta-analysis of 16 463 KTR, also concluded PTA was associated with all-cause mortality {pooled risk ratio (RR) [95% confidence interval (CI)] 1.72 (1.39, 2.13)}, graft loss [2.28 (1.77, 2.93)], CV death [2.06 (1.35, 3.16)] and CV events [1.33 (1.10, 1.61)] (Table 1) [8].

While there is no direct analysis of the impact of PTA on HRQoL and fatigue in KTR, several RCT and a prospective study have shown that targeting KTR to higher Hb levels with ESA results in improvements in several dimensions of HRQoL [20, 22, 23]. In a recent prospective, single-centre study of 871 KTRs, Hb levels were inversely related to muscle mass, muscle strength and functional capacity. Patients in the lowest quartile of Hb levels for their age and sex clearly showed the poorest outcomes in all these parameters [24].

ID IN KTRs

ID is one of the most important contributors of PTA, but it is often underrecognized and undertreated in this setting [25–28]. ID is defined according to the KDIGO guidelines as a serum ferritin <100 ng/mL and a transferrin saturation index (TSAT) <20% or a ferritin between 100 and 300 ng/mL and a TSAT <20% [1]. It is highly prevalent in KTRs and may contribute to both early and late PTA [27]. Increased iron needs result from enhanced erythropoiesis due to increased erythropoietin (EPO) production by the functioning allograft and blood losses from several sources: surgery-related, frequent blood sampling, recovery of the menstrual cycle in women, and gastrointestinal bleeding in the presence of antiplatelet or anticoagulants agents. Low-grade inflammation and/or the use of immunosuppressive drugs, like mTOR inhibitors and CNI, increase the production of hepcidin, thus reducing iron availability (iron-restricted erythropoiesis) [27, 29]. The common chronic use of proton-pump inhibitors in these patients may further reduce intestinal iron absorption [30].

The prevalence of ID in KTRs ranged from 11% to 47%, depending on the definition used, time after KT and whether only anemic KTRs were evaluated [4, 27, 29, 31, 32]. Ayerdem et al., using the classical definition of ID in CKD, found a prevalence of 30%–37% in two KTR populations, independent of the CKD stage or the presence of anemia [31]. A recent multicentric study in Spain reported a prevalence of 18.5% in anemic KTRs, which was similar in early and late PTA, but higher in anemic patients treated with ESA [4]. Several studies have found that, after KT, iron parameters tend to decrease in parallel with increases in Hb and some patients develop ID, as expected [21, 32].

The prevalence of ID in KTRs appears to be lower than in NDD-CKD patients, which is around 50%, independent of CKD stage and the presence of anemia [28, 33–35]. This apparent difference may be due to the fact that most KTRs, before transplantation were anemic and received ESA and iron supplements.

CLINICAL CONSEQUENCES OF ID IN KTRs

Several observational studies have shown the relevance of ID, independent of anemia, on the outcomes of KTRs (Fig. 2). The association of ID with graft survival is controversial. One study showed an association of graft survival with higher serum ferritin levels [36]. In contrast, no association between graft failure and the percentage of hypochromic RBCs (as a marker of iron status) was found in another study [37]. Furthermore, the iron status before KT was found not to be related to DGF [38].

HRQoL was assessed using the Short Form 36 (SF-36) and EuroQol Five-Dimensions 3-level (EQ5D-3L) questionnaires; depression using Patient Health Questionnaire-9; attention problems by using Trail Making Test part B, Controlled Oral Word Association Test and Digit Span Backward; memory using Digit Span Forward and 15 Words Test; anxiety using 6-item State-Trait Anxiety Inventory; and fatigue using Checklist of Individual Strength 20 Revised questionnaire.

On the other hand, ID has been associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in KTRs, independent of anemia and eGFR [37–42], with an estimated risk ranging between 77% and 106% (Table 1). This increased risk is mainly due to cardiovascular mortality (95% increase), most likely due to the crucial role of ID in cardiomyocyte energy metabolism and contractility, while ID increased Fibroblast growth factor FGF-23 (discussed latter) and increases platelet count and aggregability (Table 1) [40–42]. As expected, ID has also been associated with ESA resistance in anemic KTRs [4], as in CKD patients [33].

ID, but not anemia, has also been associated with an estimated 53% higher risk of severe fatigue, a 100% higher risk of major depressive symptoms, and a 51% higher chance of being at risk for sick leave/work disability [41]. Furthermore, ID has been associated with worse cognitive scores in this population, including memory, mental speed, and attention and executive functioning [40].

These results agree with previous studies that reported the deleterious effects of ID on adverse hard outcomes and HRQoL, independent of anemia, in NDD-CKD patients (Fig. 2) [43–45]. Since these studies are observational in nature, there is an unmet need for randomized controlled trials assessing whether the correction of ID, independent of anemia, has beneficial effects on outcomes in KTR. Until then, we feel that treatment of severe ID (serum ferritin <30 μg/L or TSAT <20%) can be considered, especially in symptomatic KTR, based on the observational results and the clear mechanistic rationale to correct ID prior to the occurrence of anemia, as discussed below.

ROLE OF ID BEYOND ANEMIA IN KTRs

Iron is known to have myriads of functions in the human body besides being an essential requirement for erythropoiesis. Iron plays, for example, a crucial role in DNA synthesis, electron-chain transport, and cellular proliferation and differentiation, among others [46]. As a result, iron is essential for muscles (e.g. for myoglobin synthesis and mitochondrial function) [25], for the brain (e.g. for myelin formation and neurotransmitter synthesis) [46], for the heart (e.g. for cardiomyocyte contractility) [47], and for the kidney (proximal tubular cells require high amounts of ATP from the mitochondria) [48]. Since anemia is merely an end phase of depleted iron stores, it would be logical to intervene and start treatment for ID as soon as possible, although confirmation would require adequate randomized clinical trials in this setting.

THE INTERPLAY BETWEEN ID AND FGF23 IN KT

An important consideration is the interplay between ID and FGF23, a key regulator of vitamin D and phosphate metabolism [49]. ID simultaneously increases the transcription and cleavage of FGF23, leading to higher levels of C-terminal FGF23 (cFGF23) fragments, while levels of the biologically active intact FGF23 (iFGF23) remain largely unchanged [50]. In KTR, the increased mortality risk associated with ID has been largely attributed to the underlying biological process driving the production and cleavage of iFGF23, or to the increased levels of the cFGF23 fragments [51]. Although these fragments are allegedly assumed to be inactive, the cFGF23 fragments per se have been shown to function as antagonists to iFGF23 by competing for receptor binding, which can reduce phosphaturia and exacerbate soft tissue calcification [52]. Moreover, cFGF23 fragments have been identified as contributing to the enlargement of cardiomyocytes and the development of ventricular hypertrophy [53]. Additionally, cFGF23 fragments play a crucial role in iron homeostasis by inhibiting hepcidin secretion, thereby promoting increased iron absorption [54]. In addition to ID, factors such as increased EPO levels, ESA administration and inflammation are known to concurrently increase FGF23 transcription and cleavage [55–59]. Since the cleavage is impaired with kidney function deterioration, the result will be an accumulation of biologically active FGF23, which is strongly associated with graft loss and increased CV and all-cause mortality risk in KTRs [60]. However, the causative role of excess FGF23 with respect to outcomes remains a subject of ongoing debate, although correcting ID is known to result in decreased levels of total FGF23 [59].

TREATMENT OF ID IN KT

The evidence for the efficacy of iron supplementation in anemic iron deficient KTR is more limited than in NDD-CKD or DD-CKD. Anemic KTR with ID can be successfully treated with either oral or intravenous (IV) iron preparations [61]. Although oral iron preparations may be preferred due to their ease of administration, their efficacy is limited by the common gastrointestinal side effects (abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea, especially in the case of concomitant treatment with mycophenolate mofetil), limited intestinal absorption due to increased hepcidin levels, slower Hb response and limited compliance [61]. In fact, IV iron supplementation is superior to oral iron in increasing Hb and ferritin levels in CKD patients [61, 62]. A RCT on 104 anemic KTR demonstrated that a single dose of 500 mg of IV iron polymaltose did not result in more rapid resolution of anemia compared with continuous daily oral iron. Both irons were safe and effective in the management of PTA [63]. Oral iron supplements may disturb the gut microbiota [64, 65]. Gut microbiota influences immunity, resistance to infection, inflammation and metabolism of the host. Several studies in KTR suggest a role of the gut microbiota for diarrhea, graft survival, infection risk, metabolism of immunosuppressive drugs or development of posttransplant diabetes [66]. The potential impact of oral iron supplementation on the gut microbiota after KT has not been analyzed but deserves further studies. However, in a small RCT assessing the effects of oral iron supplementation in new KTR, there were no differences in the rates of acute rejection, infection and gastrointestinal intolerance in patients receiving oral iron vs those who did not receive oral iron [67].

IV iron supplementation may be more effective and with better gastrointestinal tolerance than oral iron and is safe in KTR [68, 69]. However, IV iron formulations are not devoid of adverse events, such as hypersensitivity reactions (rare with the new formulations), risk of iron overload or bacterial growth [70]. Therefore, IV iron administration should be avoided in case of an acute infection, according to guidelines and consensus documents that mention that IV iron may favor the growth of bacteria while hampering the host response [70]. One recent study found that IV iron supplementation does not increase the risk of de novo infection immediately after KT [71]. Furthermore, some IV iron formulations, such as iron polymaltose, saccharated iron oxide and ferric carboxymaltose, are known to increase iFGF23 levels and, as a result, a decrease in phosphate levels, likely due to mechanisms related to their carbohydrate shells [72–74]. Since KTR are prone to develop hypophosphatemia, especially in the early post-transplant period, caution should be advised with the use of these IV iron formulations, in KTR and phosphate levels should be monitored.

USE OF ESAs AND Hb TARGETS IN PTA

A consensus on the Hb threshold for the initiation of ESAs or HIF stabilizers in persons with PTA does not exist [1, 2]. However, ID should be corrected in the presence of anemia with no Hb threshold. The number and sample size of studies in KTR are too small to provide solid guidance on Hb targets in this population, especially regarding safety. Based on RCT results in NDD-CKD patients, it is recommended not to target normal Hb levels when using ESAs and to initiate ESAs only when Hb levels fall below approximately 10 g/dL [75, 76].

Early ESA administration did not show an association with improved kidney function, as measured by either sCr or eGFR at Months 1, 3 and 6. Early ESA administration failed in demonstrate improvement in DGF incidence [pooled odds ratio (OR) 1.18 (0.80 to 1.75)], or rejection rates or graft loss and death [6].

A renoprotective effect of perioperative ESA administration has been suggested, potentially mediated by direct activation of EPO receptors on graft cells. However, a meta-analysis including 450 KTRs failed to confirm this hypothesis and estimated a non-significant RR of 0.96 (0.83–1.10) for DGF in seven small RCTs vs no ESA [77].

The evidence of the beneficial effects of ESA treatment targeting to higher Hb levels in PTA has been investigated in few RCTs with lower sample sizes compared with NDD-CKD [20, 78, 79]. Three of these studies (N ranging from 55 to 153 patients) reported that using ESAs to target higher Hb levels was associated with a slower decline of eGFR [20, 78, 79], as well as with an improved the HRQoL, whereas a fourth study did not confirm these findings [6, 22]. Notably, these trials did not find an increased risk of CV events or mortality for the high Hb target group, unlike previous RCTs in patients with NDD-CKD, likely because of the lower cardiovascular risk of this population compared with the CKD population [75, 76].

A recent meta-analysis pooled four RCTs (up to 652 patients) comparing high versus low Hb target (mean 12.5 vs 11.4 g/dL) with a median follow-up period of 2 years (range 21–27 months) [6]. A higher Hb target resulted in a higher eGFR at the end of the studies (Fig. 3), a slower decline in eGFR [difference 3.1 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI 0.91 to 5.24), P = .026] and a lower risk of death [pooled OR 0.36 (95% CI 0.14 to 0.89), P = .040]. However, no benefit was observed in the rates of graft losses or acute rejection. The authors considered that this benefit is supported by data indicating the immunosuppressive properties of ESAs, independent of erythropoiesis, and concluded with a recommendation of a target Hb level of 12.5 g/dL as a strategy to delay the decline in eGFR [6].

Of note, this is considerably above the target Hb concentration in CKD and to the KDIGO adapted EBPG position statement, in which the recommended target range of Hb in anemic NDD-CKD patients treated with ESA is between 10 and 12 g/dL, but should be individualized, while intentionally targeting Hb values above 13 g/dL with ESAs should be avoided [1, 2]. This is a reasonable range as has been shown to be consistent with current practice in a large observational study of KTRs [79, 80]. As indicated in previous papers, about 40% of all KTR need ESAs at some time during their post-transplant course to achieve this Hb target, but others experience a spontaneous stabilization of Hb levels above 12 g/dL without ESAs, without evidencing an increased cardiovascular risk [19]. However, Heinze et al. suggested that mortality significantly increases in patients who receive ESAs and have Hb levels above 14.7 g/dL compared with patients with the same Hb not receiving ESA therapy [79].

The KDIGO 2025 clinical guideline for anemia in CKD is under public review and cannot be used as reference yet, also because the issue of ESA use in PTA is not specifically addressed. Therefore, recommendations need to be derived from previous position statements [1, 80, 81].

In analogy to NDD-CKD patients, the ESA dose should be carefully considered to allow for the lowest dose needed to achieve a stable Hb level between 10 and 12 g/dL and, in accordance with the conclusions of the meta-analysis, individualize the target Hb based on the presence of comorbidities and functional status.

HIF STABILIZERS IN PTA

HIF-PHIs or HIF stabilizers are competitive inhibitors of HIF prolyl hydroxylases, enzymes involved in the degradation of HIF [82]. HIF-PHIs increase endogenous EPO production. In addition, HIF-PHI may further increase erythropoiesis via additional mechanisms including: (i) upregulation of EPO receptors in erythroid precursors; (ii) increased intestinal iron absorption; (iii) increase in iron uptake by proerythrocytes; and (iv) inhibition of hepcidin production in the liver [83, 84]. These oral compounds have an entirely different mechanism of action, making them another family of drugs, different from ESA [85, 86].

An early phase 1/2 single-dose study comparing EPO production in a small group of patients on dialysis with native kidneys with bilateral nephrectomy and controls provided evidence that EPO production can be stimulated in extrarenal sites (presumably the liver) and the diseased, nonfunctioning, fibrotic kidneys [87]. These data proved that a disturbance of the renal oxygen-sensing mechanism, rather than a loss of renal EPO-producing cells, was the main cause of renal anemia. Although not directly tested, this concept is likely also true for KTRs.

Several HIF-PHIs have been developed, tested in phase 3 programs and approved in many countries for the treatment of anemia in CKD [82, 88, 89]. The rationale behind their development included the ease of oral administration, easier production and increased stability of these small molecules, as compared with the ESA biologicals, as well as their potentially synergistic effects in iron availability. The lower plasma EPO concentrations achieved by subtle, intermittent activation of endogenous EPO production by HIF-PHIs were hypothesized to be safer than the high, supraphysiological concentrations achieved following IV or even subcutaneous injections with current ESAs. However, the phase 3 programs in both NDD-CKD (all excluding KTR) and DD-CKD do not support the supposed improved safety, especially CV. While studies comparing different HIF-PHIs with ESAs have overall shown similar efficacy in increasing/maintaining Hb levels, HIF-PHIs have been associated with increased rates of major CV events in some studies [82, 84, 88, 90–92].

Pooled post hoc analysis [93] and several metanalysis consistently shown that there is no increased risk of adverse outcomes in different settings, placebo or active controlled RCT [94–96]. Most recent meta-analysis including 23 studies with 15 144 NDD-CKD patients found heterogeneous results and no significant differences in the risk of CV events in either placebo- or ESAs-controlled studies, leading them to consider an acceptable cardiovascular safety profile for HIF-PHI that is not different from that of current ESAs [96, 97].

No head-to-head comparisons of different HIF-PHI are available, but study data indicate that the different compounds have different profiles and risk/benefit ratios that require an individual assessment. Clinical trials suggest that HIF-PHI increase iron availability [89], but the effects on iron metabolism and/or need for iron therapy were not primary endpoints in these clinical trials.

A recent RCT tested roxadustat in 150 patients with PTA (Hb 9–11 g/dL, mean 10.4 g/dL) without ESAs for at least 1 month before and with stable renal function for 3 months (eGFR 50.2 mL/min/1.73 m2) in a 6-month study [98]. Patients were randomized to receive oral iron and roxadustat, adjusted by weight according to label, or iron alone as control group. According to pre-specified targets, efficacy was superior in the roxadustat group with a higher 12-week Hb (Hb 12.2 vs 11.2 g/dL, P < .001). The change in Hb levels from baseline to Week 12 was higher in the roxadustat arm [+1.04 (95% CI 0.58–1.51 g/dL), P < .001], as well as the achievement of Hb >12 g/dL with an increase from baseline >1 g/dL (79% in the roxadustat arm vs 46% in the control arm). The authors reported similar adverse event profiles in both arms and no differences on tacrolimus serum levels. A retrospective study by Ishiyama et al. compared the efficacy and safety of HIF-PHIs in 43 KTRs and 39 non-transplanted CKD patients, of which the former primarily received daprodustat and the latter roxadustat for 6 months [99]. The study concluded that HIF-PHIs were effective in increasing Hb levels in KTR, although KTR experienced a higher rate of thrombotic events (18.6% vs 2.6%, P = .049). Nakamura et al. reported a retrospective analysis of the use of HIF-PHIs in 26 anaemic KTRs, of which 18 had previously received ESA and concluded that HIF-PHI were effective and safe for RTx patients regardless of prior ESA use [100]. All these cohort studies concluded that HIF-PHIs were effective, but given the lack of appropriate control group, the small sample size and short follow-up any conclusion about safety appears premature in KTR.

Thus, although HIF-PHIs appear to be effective in KTR, we suggest a careful consideration of benefits and risks and a short-term duration of HIF-PHI, due to lack of sufficiently large, RCT that assessed the safety in this population in comparison with no treatment or other treatments of anemia (iron and ESA).

RBC TRANSFUSIONS IN KTRs

The French nationwide registry report that 27.7% of the 12 559 patients who underwent a KT received a packed RBC (PRBC) transfusion during the first 14 days posttransplant [101]. Similarly, a subsequent meta-analysis, that included 10 studies involving 32 817 kidney transplant recipients, reported a transfusion rate of 29% at Week 1, between 18% and 43% at Month 1, and between 61% and 63% during the first year [102].

There are no clear Hb thresholds in PTA that indicate the prescription of RBC. It is intuitive that severe post-transplant surgical hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability requires transfusion. However, for subacute and well-tolerated degrees of anemia the situation is less clear. There are some comorbidities, such as ischemic heart disease, which may justify higher transfusion rates.

For other indications, organ specific guidelines have been established such as in upper gastrointestinal bleeding. In this setting, transfusion to a higher threshold of 10 vs 8 g/dL caused harm, i.e. increased risk of death and bleeding and thus should be avoided [103]. Accordingly, in cardiac surgery a restrictive transfusion strategy of 7–8 g/dL has shown to be safe and was associated with 24% less PRBCs consumption [104, 105]. Although the optimal Hb threshold for transfusion is far from clear, guidelines recommend for chronic heart failure a threshold also of 8 g/dL in the absence of severe ischemic heart disease [1, 104]. The presence of pre-transplant anemia, ID upon admission, surgical reintervention and deceased donor donation were associated with an increased risk of RBC transfusion after KT. Further, there was an association between a higher number of PRBC units transfused and impaired kidney graft function 6 months after hospital discharge [106]. The normal half-life of erythrocytes is 120 days, but can be reduced to 30 days after a transfusion in the absence of bleeding due to in vitro storage damage. The greatest degradation of transfused RBC occurs during the first hours after transfusion and studies showed that up to 25% of transfused erythrocytes disappear within the first day [107].

When considering ESAs or HIF-PHIs as an alternative to RBC transfusions to increase low Hb levels, we should remind that erythropoietic process takes up to weeks and thus an immediate rise in Hb levels cannot be expected [108].

The potential anti-HLA sensitization of the KTR via the donor blood is more worrying than the reduced half-life of transfused erythrocytes. Even though PRBC do not usually express HLA class I or II, residual leukocytes in PRBC units carry HLA. HLA class I antigens are expressed on all nucleated cells and platelets but not on erythrocytes, while the HLA class II antigens are expressed on antigen presenting cells (APCs). Sensitization against donor HLA-peptides occurs via some remaining donor APCs or other nucleated cells in the PRBCs [102], especially if the patient is immunocompetent and receives multiple transfusions. Spensley et al. [102] analyzed HLA sensitization in 50 KT candidates transfused from 105 different donors who were HLA typed for the study. They found that transfusion-specific antibodies occurred in 23% of patients with no circulating HLA antibodies at the time of transfusion and in 50% of patients with circulating HLA antibodies, but do not inform of the number and diverse origin of the RBCs [109]. Of the 34 patients who underwent a KT, the majority received a kidney with at least one shared HLA specificity with a transfusion donor. These patients had higher rates of rejection, specifically antibody-mediated rejection, at 3 years. There is also data showing that the development of donor-specific antibodies after PRBC transfusion is associated with a poorer graft prognosis [109]. A previously discussed meta-analysis showed that post-transplant RBC transfusions are associated with lower patient survival [6.0 95% CI (1.70–21.17)], allograft loss [OR 1.73 95% CI (1.24–2.41)], increased rejection rates [OR 1.42 95% CI (1.04–1.94)] and more frequent development of donor-specific antibodies [OR 1.73 95% CI (1.24–2.41)]. The authors therefore recommended the strict application of patient blood management guidelines [109, 110].

Although the strength of the anti-HLA sensitization due to PRBC is lower than after a pregnancy or a previous transplant, it can and should be avoided to prevent the development of donor-specific anti-HLA antibodies [111].

Therefore, we should minimize RBC transfusions, i.e. using ESAs or HIF-PHIs. If transfusions are necessary, then a leukocyte- and thrombocyte-depleted and gamma-irradiated RBC should be recommended [112, 113].

Besides the risk of HLA-sensitization, a potential but very rare risk is the transmission of infection with pathogens such as CMV, hepatitis virus, dengue, HTLV-1, parvovirus B19, plasmodium, toxoplasma and treponema from the PRBC. The risk certainly depends on the geographic region and the serological and molecular diagnostic methods used to screen for these pathogens. A recent study from China reported a transfusion-transmissible infection prevalence of 1.07% in 230 225 blood donors between 2015 and 2019. The most common pathogens were HIV, HBV, HCV and syphilis with rates of 0.08%, 0.40%, 0.19% and 0.40%, respectively [114]. In western industrialized countries, the rates are much lower. The US Transfusion‐Transmissible Infections Monitoring System (TTIMS) study group reported a prevalence of HIV of about 2.6 per 100 000 donations (0.002%) among 26 million donations between 2018 and 2019 (pre COVID-19) and a slightly lower rate during COVID-19 [115]. The HBV prevalence was 6/100 000 and HCV between 16 and 10/100 000 in the pre and COVID-19 era. However, the residual risk based on these incidence rates are 1/2.3 million for HIV, 1/2.6 million for HCV and 1/1.5 million donations for HBV, reflecting very low risk to recipients [116].

IN SUMMARY

Considering the reviewed evidence, we conclude that PTA and ID are common in KTRs and are associated with an increased risk of worse patient and graft survival and poorer HRQoL. Nevertheless, they have received comparatively less attention so far The prevalence of anemia is higher in KTRs than in non-transplanted patients with CKD for a given eGFR due to KT-specific pathophysiological factors. ID should be detected and corrected in KTRs using oral or IV iron formulations. Some IV iron formulations are associated with a higher risk of hypophosphatemia, which may be clinically relevant, especially in the early posttransplant period. Current anemia guidelines propose the same recommendation for PTA as for CKD related anemia in non-transplanted patients, although in small studies a higher Hb target with ESA has been associated with a slower decline in eGFR in the graft, without safety concerns. There is insufficient evidence to recommend the widespread use of HIF-PHI in PTA. RBC transfusion policy should be restrictive to minimize the risk of HLA sensitization. Overall, a proactive strategy for PTA detection and management, from the waiting list throughout the post-transplant period appears worthwhile. We call for increased awareness and targeted trials on anemia and ID in KTRs, considering the diverse and specific profiles of these patients.
