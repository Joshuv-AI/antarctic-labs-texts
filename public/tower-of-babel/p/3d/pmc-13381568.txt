Fully cove-edged bilayer nanographene with parallel displaced stacking
Authors: Xin-Jing Zhao, Hui-Zheng Fang, Jiang-Feng Xing, Wei-Hang Geng, Xiao-Fei Li, Qiong Wu, Rong-Jie Xie, Zhen-Lin Qiu, Liu-Bin Feng, Wen-Long Yan, Ye Yang, Jun Zhu, Jun Qian, Yuan-Zhi Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Owing to the lack of precise atomic-level control offered by existing fabrication techniques over the edge structures in bilayer graphene, experimental studies on the edge effects in bilayer graphene structures have so far largely remained unexplored. In this study, two fully cove-edged bilayer nanographenes (CBGs) are synthesized via organic synthesis approach. One of the prepared CBGs contains a cavity defect. The bilayer stacked structures of CBGs are unambiguously characterized. Two CBGs exhibit a parallel displaced stacking arrangement with tunable interlayer overlapping, which is controlled by the steric hindrance at the cove edges. The different interlayer overlapping results in different interlayer electronic coupling. The decrease in the energy gaps is linked to the cove edges, which leads to a redshift in the optical absorption and emission spectra of the CBGs, which is further used for the NIR-II bioimaging.

Introduction

Graphene is a zero-gap semiconductor that is comprised of a single layer of carbon atoms. One way to achieve bandgap opening in graphene is through lateral confinement, which leads to the formation of graphene nanoribbons when limited in one dimension and to graphene quantum dots when it is constrained in two dimensions1,2. The edges play a crucial role in shaping the electronic and optical properties of these graphene-based materials with finite lateral dimensions3–6, giving rise to various unique phenomena including magnetism, localized spin, and quantized transport7–18. For instance, the zigzag and armchair edges (Fig. 1) are well-known for exhibiting markedly different electronic properties in comparison to each other. The zigzag edges lead to localized spin density, resulting in open-shelled characteristics, a low energy gap, and the formation of localized states7–9. Conversely, the armchair edge is often associated with a fully benzenoid conformation that offers high stability, localized Clar sextets, and a larger energy band gap18–22. Both zigzag and armchair edges maintain a planar geometry without distorting the graphenic skeleton. On the other hand, edges of other types such as cove and fjord edges can be viewed as extensions of hexagonal rings at the zigzag or armchair edges. As opposed to that, the terminal groups present at these edges, even as small as hydrogen atoms can exhibit significant local steric hindrance. It results in a contorted skeleton that can substantially influence the interlayer stacking structure23,24.Fig. 1Different edge structures of graphene and nanographenes.The different edges of graphene and nanographenes with armchair edges (HBC) and cove edges (c-HBC and 1).

Multilayer graphene is formed by stacking different number of graphene layers. Bilayer and trilayer graphene structures have been shown to exhibit remarkably distinct electronic and optical properties in comparison to their single-layer counterpart25–29. Some of these properties include bandgap opening, bound excitons, and even superconductivity. Notably, the electronic properties of bilayer graphene are strongly linked to its edge structure. Prior theoretical studies focusing on bilayer graphene with zigzag or armchair edges have revealed fascinating edge-induced phenomena, including valley-dependent transport30,31, electric field-controlled magnetism, and spin effects32,33. Moreover, bilayer graphene with edges other than zigzag or armchair edges, for instance, cove-edges and fjord-edges, will exhibit a contorted edge conformation, distinct from their planar counterparts. However, the preparation of atomically precise edges in bilayer graphene remains a challenging task for existing fabrication techniques34–36. Thus, a detailed experimental investigation into the edge-induced properties of bilayer graphene has not been feasible so far. Graphene quantum dots obtained through rational organic synthesis, also known as nanographenes37–39, are viable building units for preparing bilayer graphene structures with atomically precise edges. Although some bilayer nanographene structures based on armchair edges have been obtained through a van der Waals assembly40–45 or a covalent linkage of nanographenes46–49, those with cove or fjord edges have so far largely remained unexplored. However, a cove or fjord-edged bilayer nanographene structure can be prepared by combining the synthesis process of cove or fjord-edged nanographenes with controlled bilayer assembly.

In this study, two nanographenes (1 and 2) with fully cove-edged structures were successfully synthesized. Notably, structure 2 had a central cavity defect. 1 and 2 were self-assembled into discrete bilayer structures and were denoted as (1)2 and (2)2, respectively. This was confirmed through the mass spectrometric and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopic analyses. Additionally, single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) of (2)2 provided clear evidence of the wave-shaped structure of 2, along with its parallel displaced stacking arrangement. The results of the DFT calculations clearly showed that the parallel displaced stacking structures were energetically more favorable in comparison to the eclipsed stacking structure, owing to the staggered conformation of the cove edges. Also, both structures (1)2 and (2)2 exhibited a redshift in their absorption and emission spectra in comparison to the corresponding armchair-edged bilayer nanographenes (ABGs). Remarkably, (1)2 displayed a near-infrared emission extending up to a wavelength of 1050 nm, which facilitated their use in NIR-II bioimaging. On the other hand, the photoluminescence (PL) quantum yield obtained for (2)2 was nearly twenty times higher than that for the (1)2 structure, which is associated to the introduction of a cavity defect at the center.

Results

The introduction of bulky groups such as mesityl or dimethylphenyl, in a perpendicular conformation, at the periphery of extended nanographenes has proven to be an effective method for assembling nanographene sheets into discrete stacks. The synthesis of CBG requires the preparation of extended nanographene sheets featuring cove edges and perpendicular peripheral groups. The contorted hexabenzocoronene (c-HBC) is the only fully cove-edged nanographene that has been reported so far24,50, even though several studies on different nanographenes containing cove edges can be found in literature51–54. Cove-edged graphene nanoribbons have also been achieved through surface-assisted synthesis techniques23,55–60. The next fully cove-edged nanographene larger than the c-HBC has been predicted theoretically61 but has not been studied experimentally so far.

The oligomeric phenylene precursor (3) for the extended cove-edged nanographene was designed and synthesized through the trimerization of 1,2-bis(2”,6”-dimethyl-2′-(naphthalen-1-yl)-[1,1′:4′,1”-terphenyl]−4-yl)ethyne (5), as shown in Fig. 2a. The introduction of six naphthalenyl groups to compound 3 was pivotal for constructing the cove-edged structure. Iron(III) chloride (FeCl3) was employed for the cyclodehydrogenation of 3. After purification, a blue-violet component was obtained. Mass spectrometric analysis revealed a dominant peak at 4648.418 Da (see Fig. 2c and Fig. S1), which corresponded to the molecular weight of the expected dimeric structure of (1)2. This confirmed the spontaneous interlayer stacking and the successful formation of CBG after the cyclodehydrogenation process. The successful synthesis of the CBG (1)2 provided further motivation for the exploration of its counterpart with a central cavity defect. A macrocyclic template was employed to introduce this defect into the nanographene structure. The acquisition of precursor 4 via Suzuki coupling between 8 and macrocyclic 6 is illustrated in Fig. 3a. In addition to mass spectrometry (see Fig. S3) and NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S4) of 4, its molecular structure was further characterized by SCXRD (Fig. S5). The naphthalenyl groups exhibited an alternating configuration that deviated in both the upward and the downward directions relative to the plane of the inner macrocycle, and yet they were seen to extend in the same direction. This arrangement was conducive to the subsequent cyclodehydrogenation process. To achieve cyclodehydrogenation of 4, DDQ with 1.7% TfOH was employed, which led to the graphitization of 4 into 2. The mass spectrum of the purified product showed a single peak at 4684.700 Da (Fig. 3c and Fig. S6) as was expected, which had twice the molecular weight of 2. This indicated that 2 had also formed the discrete dimeric (2)2, similar to the (1)2. The observation of distinctive signals in the 1H NMR spectra of the purified product, which corresponded to the peripheral mesityl groups (refer to Fig. 3b and Fig. S7) further confirmed the presence of (2)2 in the solution. Both (1)2 and (2)2 CBGs were soluble in organic solvents such as tetrachloroethane, chloroform, THF and carbon disulfide. The single crystals of (2)2 could be grown from their tetrachloroethane solutions through the diffusion of hexane vapor and measured by SCXRD. The SCXRD analysis provided the precise geometric structure of (2)2 (Fig. 4), revealing its characteristic fully cove-edged structure. In the crystalline state, the dimeric structure of (2)2 was confirmed. Since adjacent (2)2 complexes interacted with each other via C-H···π interactions without any π-π interaction, it further established (2)2 as a discrete dimer in its solid state. Remarkably, the bilayer structure of (2)2 exhibited a parallel displaced stacking pattern, with a displacement distance (X) of 4.9 Å (Fig. 4a). This was in stark contrast to the eclipsed bilayer structures observed in (armchair-edged bilayer nanographene) ABG. The hydrogen atoms within the inner cavity of (2)2 served as probes for investigating the stacking structure of (2)2 in solution via NMR spectroscopy. The resonance peaks of these hydrogen atoms were shifted upfield by 1.06 ppm relative to those in the eclipsed stacked holey nanographene (Fig. S10). The theoretically calculated 1H NMR peaks for monomeric 2 and dimeric (2)2 further confirmed this high field shift of the proton peaks within the cavity after undergoing a displacement in the bilayer stacking structure (Fig. S11). The phenyl rings at the cove edges in (2)2 adopted an up-and-down staggered conformation, with an average torsion angle of 40.8° (Fig. 4a). This highly deviating pattern can be attributed to repulsion from the terminal hydrogen atoms present at the cove edges. On the other hand, the inner region of (2)2 displayed a planar structure, with an average deviation of only 0.09 Å from the mean plane. This suggested that the structural perturbation caused by the nonplanar cove edges was confined only to the peripheries. Consequently, the cove edges interacted with the planar inner region in (2)2, avoiding any unfavorable steric hindrance between the cove edges of the stacked layers. The highly deviating conformation of the cove edges also resulted in closer interlayer contacts, with the shortest interlayer C···C distance measured to be 3.16 Å (Fig. 4b) in the dimeric structure of (2)2. This distance was shorter than that found in typical π-π dimers (3.3–3.4 Å).

Theoretical calculations were performed to investigate the π stacking of the structures here. Initially, the stacking structures of these CBGs were optimized at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-31G(d) level (Fig.5a and S12), starting with the crystal structure of (2)2. The optimized structure of (2)2 closely matched with the experimental one, displaying similar interlayer (3.27 Å for the optimized structure and 3.34 Å for the experimental one) and displacement distances (Xopt = 4.84 Å for the optimized structure and Xexp = 4.90 Å for the experimental one). Likewise, the optimized structure of (1)2 also exhibited a displaced stacking conformation with an interlayer distance of 3.33 Å and a displacement distance of Xopt = 4.59 Å. Subsequently, various stacking conformations of (1)2 and (2)2 with varying X values were computed and analyzed (Fig. 5b). The obtained values of relative energy indicated an increase in energy upon both increasing or decreasing the value of X, confirming the energetically stable nature of the displaced stacked conformations of (1)2 and (2)2. The interlayer binding energies of (1)2 and (2)2 were also calculated and were found to be −153.34 kcal mol−1 for (1)2 and −142.17 kcal mol−1 for (2)2 structure. These values of (1)2 and (2)2 were comparable to those of the eclipsed stacked armchair-edged counterparts (−159.02 kcal mol−1 and −146.91 kcal mol−1, respectively, Fig. S14), despite the displaced conformation of (1)2 and (2)2 that led to a reduction in the interlayer overlapping area.

The optical properties of (1)2 and (2)2 were explored (Fig. 6). Two absorption bands were observed centered around 400 nm and 593 nm for (1)2 (Fig. 6a). The most prominent absorption peak at 593 nm corresponded to the S0-S30 transition. A weaker absorption shoulder at 715 nm was attributed to the S0-S5 transition, while the S0-S1 transition, which was calculated to occur at 782 nm remained invisible due to its symmetrical forbidden nature. On the other hand, (2)2 exhibited a similar absorption profile to (1)2 but with blue-shifted peaks at 361, 484, 517, and 558 nm (Fig. 6a), which were assigned to the S68, S32, and S17 transitions, respectively. Consequently, the optical band gap for (1)2 was determined to be 1.73 eV, whereas its value increased to 2.22 eV for the (2)2 with an increase of 0.49 eV. The PL spectrum of (1)2 displayed three distinct vibronic peaks at 788, 869, and 981 nm, corresponding to different vibronic transitions. Interestingly, the emission spectrum for (1)2 extended up to 1050 nm into the NIR-II region (Fig. 6b). Owing to bandgap opening, the PL spectra of (2)2 were blue-shifted by 191 (Fig. 6b), with three vibronic lines occurring at 597, 636, and 698 nm. Both the absorption, as well as the emission spectra of these two CBGs exhibited a noticeable redshift in comparison to the ABGs, indicating a narrower energy gap. This was consistent with previous observations in nanographenes and graphene nanoribbons containing cove edges.

Notably, the large Stokes shifts of 0.52 and 0.48 eV observed for (1)2 and (2)2, respectively, originate from the formally forbidden S0-S1 transition associated with their optical absorptions. For (1)2 and (2)2, the emission spectra displayed well-resolved vibronic progressions, with the first vibronic line being most intense and subsequent lines diminishing sequentially. This evolution is indicative of weak or negligible H-type interlayer coupling62,63, consistent with their parallel-displaced stacking geometries. Furthermore, transient absorption measurements (Figs. 6c, d and Fig. S15) revealed that the two CBGs exhibit singlet lifetimes of 45 ± 2 ns for (1)2 and 42 ± 2 ns for (2)2, respectively, which are comparable to their fluorescence lifetimes—41.8 ns for (1)2 measured at 788 nm and 38.6 ns for (2)2 measured at 597 nm—obtained from time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy (Fig. S16). In contrast, both (1)2 and (2)2 display exceptionally long triplet lifetimes: within the 200 ns instrumental time window, virtually no decay is observed, indicating that the bilayer architecture effectively stabilizes the excited states (Fig. S16).

The PL quantum yields for two CBGs were determined through absolute measurements using an integrating sphere (Table S1). Their values were found to be just 1.6% for (1)2, whereas it was measured to be 27% for the (2)2 structure. This observed enhancement in photoluminescence in (2)2 is attributed to bandgap opening induced by the inner defect, consistent with the energy-gap law. From the fluorescence lifetimes and quantum yields, the radiative (kr) and nonradiative (knr) decay rate constants were obtained: kr = 0.38 ×106 s−1 for (1)2 and 7.0 × 106 s−1 for (2)2; knr = 2.4 × 107and 1.9 × 107, respectively. These results reveal that while knr remains on the order of 107 s−1, kr is greatly enhanced from 0.38 × 106 to 7.0 × 106 s−1, owing to the gap opening.

The NIR-II emission of (1)2 enabled its application as a fluorescent dye for near-infrared bioimaging (Fig. 6e and Fig. S17). To improve its aqueous dispersibility, the hydrophobic (1)2 was encapsulated with amphiphilic DSPE-mPEG2000 to afford nanodots (Fig. S18). The nanodots of (1)2 was used as a fluorescent probe in combination with the NIR-II imaging technology for macro imaging system, fluorescence confocal microscopy, and three-photon fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 6e and Fig. S17). The fluorescent probe exhibited excellent performance. By utilizing NIR-II emission of (1)2, we achieved in vivo whole-body vessel imaging of mice at the macroscopic level, achieving resolution of vessels as small as 0.19 mm in width, and realized three-dimensional imaging of cerebral and hepatic vessels in living mice at the microscopic level. Specifically, vessels with a diameter of 1.48 μm were clearly resolved at a depth of 300 μm in liver, the highly scattering organ, with the maximum imaging depth reaching 500 μm.

Discussion

Two CBGs were synthesized through a bottom-up organic approach, marking a significant advancement in fully cove-edged nanographenes beyond the c-HBC structure. The successful preparation of bilayer stacked structures was confirmed through mass spectrometry, NMR spectroscopy, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Notably, the stacking conformation of the CBGs was characterized by a parallel displaced arrangement, in contrast to the eclipsed stacking of ABGs. To the best of our knowledge, these CBGs synthesized in this work represent the pioneering instances of bilayer nanographenes with edges quite different from the armchair configuration. This displaced stacking was attributed to the staggered nonplanar structure of the cove edges and can be tuned by the stereo hindrance of edge structures. The presence of the cove edges also contributed to a reduction in the energy gap and brought about a redshift in the absorption and emission spectra of the prepared CBGs. Additionally, the tunable parallel displaced stacking led to a tunable interlayer coupling in these structures. These findings highlight the critical role of the edge structure, not only in influencing the stacking conformation but also in shaping the electronic properties and the interlayer coupling within bilayer graphenic materials.

Methods

Characterizations for materials

NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Spectrometers at 298 K in the solvents indicated. Chemical shifts are expressed in ppm units relative to TMS (0.00 ppm, 1H). High-resolution mass spectra were recorded on a Bruker time of flight mass spectrometer coupled with matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization source (autoflex max MALDI- TOF MS) using trans-2-[3-(4-tert-Butylphenyl)−2-methyl-2-propenylidene] malononitrile (DCTB) as the matrix. Silica gel (300–400 mesh) was used for column chromatography. Ultraviolet/visible absorption spectra were recorded using a Agilent Cary 5000 UV-Vis-NIR spectrometer. Fluorescence spectra were acquired on a FLS-980 Fluorescence Spectrophotometer in the solvents indicated. Single crystal X-ray diffraction data was obtained by Agilent Supernova Diffractometer. DLS measurements were performed using a NanoBrook Omni (Brookhaven, USA) instrument.

Materials

All materials including solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. All the reactions were carried out under the Argon atmosphere.

Synthesis of compound (1)2

A solution of compound 3 (11.86 mg, 0.005 mmol) in DCM (12 mL) was degassed by argon bubbling for 10 min. Then iron(III) chloride (175 mg 1.08 mmol) was added. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h under continuous bubbling with argon, the reaction was stirred another 11 h under argon atmosphere and quenched by the addition of triethylamine. The crude products were further purified by silica gel column chromatography (DCM/petroleum ether = 1:1, CS2, DCM as eluent) to afford (1)2 (1.2 mg, 10%) as a blue solid.

Synthesis of compound (2)2

Compound 4 (12 mg, 0.005 mmol) and DDQ (31 mg, 0.135 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM (12 mL). After stirred at 0 °C for 5 min under argon, CF3SO3H (TfOH) (0.2 mL) was added dropwise via a syringe. The reaction was stirred at 0 °C for 1 h and quenched by trimethylamine (2 mL). The resulting mixture was extracted with water/dichloromethane. The organic phase was dried with Na2SO4, and concentrated in vacuum. The crude products were purified by column chromatography (DCM/PE = 1:1, CS2, DCM as eluent)) to obtain compound (2)2 (2.3 mg, 20% yield) as an orange solid.

Animal handing

All experiments used female C57 mice weighing approximately 20 g. Laboratory animals were fed with clean water and standard laboratory food. The animals were kept at 24 °C on a 12-h day/ night cycle. All animal testing procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (#ZJU20240028).

In vivo imaging

Prior to imaging, a 1% sodium pentobarbital solution was prepared in saline and administered via intraperitoneal injection (100 μL) for anesthesia, followed by intravenous tail vein injection of 150 μL fluorescent probe.

For cerebral vascular imaging, cranial window surgery was performed one day prior to imaging. Briefly, the scalp and a portion of the skull were removed, the dura mater was carefully excised, and a cranial window was created by sealing the exposed brain with a glass coverslip. For liver imaging, a small section of the liver was exposed and immobilized between a glass slide and a coverslip under the objective lens for observation.

Macroscopic imaging was performed using an SD640 camera for detection, with excitation provided by a 665 nm continuous-wave laser. For three-photon imaging, a customized scanning microscope (Ultima 2p plus, Bruker, USA) equipped with a 25× water-immersed objective (XLPLN25XWMP-SP, Olympus, Japan) was used. The excitation wavelength was 1700 nm. The excitation source was a noncollinear femtosecond optical parametric amplifier (NOPA-VISIR, Spectra-Physics, USA), pumped by a regenerative amplifier (Spirit-70, Spectra-Physics, USA). Confocal imaging was conducted using a scanning microscope (FVMPE-RS, Olympus, Japan) equipped with a 25× water-immersed objective (XLPLN25XWMP-SP, Olympus, Japan), with a 671 nm continuous-wave laser for excitation. A near-infrared photomultiplier tubes (H12397A-75, Hamamatsu, Japan) was employed for fluorescence detection.

Transient absorption measurement

The sample for the transient absorption measurements was dissolved in tetrachloroethane at a concentration of 1 × 10−5 mol L−1. The measurements were carried out using a nanosecond transient absorption spectrometer (Eos, Ultrafast Systems). The pump pulses were generated by a femtosecond Ti:sapphire laser (Astrella, Coherent) operating at a 1 kHz repetition rate, which pumped an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS) to produce specific excitation wavelengths at 593 nm and 484 nm. The probe beam was provided by a nanosecond photonic crystal fiber-based supercontinuum laser, with the probe pulse duration determining the instrument’s time resolution. The pump–probe delay was electronically controlled, and the transmitted probe light was collected using a fiber-coupled CMOS detector.

DFT calculations

The optimized structures of (1)2, and (2)2 were calculated at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-31 G(d) level by Gaussian1664–68. The interlayer binding energies were calculated at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-31+G(d) level with BSSE (basis set superposition error) correction using the Boys and Bernardi’s counterpoise technique69. Time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were performed to understand the excited states and simulate spectra of (1)2 and (2)2 in gas phase at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/3-21G level70.
