Safety of pulsed-field ablation combined with IVUS-guided pulmonary vein stenting during atrial fibrillation ablation: a case report
Authors: Tomasz Jadczyk, Lorenzo Caratti di Lanzacco, Julian Jarman, Jonathan Hill, Vias Markides
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) is an uncommon but serious complication of atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation. Angioplasty with stent implantation is the standard treatment for severe PVS; however, restenosis and re-occlusion remain frequent challenges. Pulsed-field ablation (PFA), a novel non-thermal ablation modality for pulmonary vein isolation, causes minimal structural damage and may therefore be particularly suitable and safer for complex redo procedures involving previously stenosed pulmonary veins.Case summaryA 50-year-old man underwent pulmonary vein isolation using radiofrequency in February 2023. A few months later, he developed breathlessness, and a computed tomography scan showed a stenosis of the left inferior pulmonary vein (LIPV) and normal coronary arteries. He also had recurrent arrhythmias. In March 2024, with a cerebral protection device in situ and following pre-dilatation of a tight stenosis of the LIPV, an 8 × 19 mm Omnilink Elite (Abbott) stent was deployed with a good angiographic result. Reconnections of all pulmonary veins except the LIPV were targeted with radiofrequency energy but the LIPV was not treated for fear of causing further problems with stenosis.He was reviewed in December 2024 and was free from breathlessness but had recurrent palpitations. By spring 2025, he had also become breathless, and imaging showed subtotal occlusion of the LIPV stent with reduced perfusion of the left lower lobe despite continuation of edoxaban and clopidogrel after initial stenting. In May 2025, he was admitted for intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided balloon angioplasty with a 7-mm drug-eluting balloon following pre-dilatation, which successfully restored stent patency. PFA was then performed on all pulmonary veins, including around the previously stented LIPV, avoiding direct catheter contact with the stent. The procedure and recovery were uneventful, and the patient’s symptoms improved markedly. He remained free from arrhythmias in April 2026 (∼1 year follow-up).DiscussionThis case highlights the complexity of managing AF ablation-related PVS and underscores the role of IVUS in guiding revascularization. It also demonstrates the safety of PFA (in particular Varipulse™, J&J MedTech) near a stented pulmonary vein, achieving effective electrical isolation with minimal additional structural injury.

Introduction

Pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) is an uncommon complication of atrial fibrillation (AF)1 ablation, with a current incidence of approximately 0.5%–1%.2 It results primarily from thermal injury to the venous endothelium and adjacent myocardium, followed by inflammation, fibrosis, and progressive neointimal hyperplasia. Clinical presentation is often non-specific and may include dyspnoea, pleural effusions, or recurrent pulmonary infections.3 Pulmonary vein stenting is recommended4,5 for symptomatic patients with significant stenosis; however, restenosis occurs in up to 22% of cases within the first year.5 Notably, redo AF ablations using radiofrequency or cryothermal6 energy may exacerbate existing stenosis. Pulsed-field ablation (PFA) is a non-thermal ablation technology that selectively targets myocardial tissue while sparing adjacent vascular structures.7,8 We report a case in which PFA was safely applied to a re-stented pulmonary vein immediately after Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided revascularization of a near-occluded stent,9 providing an effective combined mechanical and electrical solution.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old man with persistent AF underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in February 2023. In the months following the procedure, he developed progressive exertional dyspnoea and paroxysms of recurrent arrhythmia. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left and right ventricular systolic function, with no significant valvulopathies. In February 2024, cardiac computed tomography (CT) with pulmonary venography revealed severe stenosis (>70%)4 of the left inferior pulmonary vein (LIPV), with a residual lumen measuring 3 × 6 mm, while the remaining pulmonary veins appeared normal (Figure 1).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography pulmonary venogram performed in February 2024 demonstrating severe stenosis (>70%) of the left inferior pulmonary vein at its ostium, with a residual lumen of approximately 3 × 6 mm (arrow). The remaining pulmonary veins are patent.

Based on these findings, the patient was scheduled for interventional treatment of PVS. In March 2024, transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) confirmed a narrow residual LIPV channel calculated at 2–3 mm with turbulent flow (peak velocity 120 cm/s) and excluded thrombus in the left atrial appendage. With Sentinel™ (Boston Scientific) cerebral protection device in situ and following pre-dilatation of the LIPV stenosis with a coronary balloon, an 8 × 19 mm Omnilink Elite™ (Abbott) vascular balloon-expandable stent was deployed with a good angiographic result. Redo ablation of all other pulmonary veins was then performed using radiofrequency energy, while the LIPV was intentionally left untreated to avoid further injury. He was reviewed in December 2024 and was free from breathlessness but had recurrent palpitations. By spring 2025, he had also become breathless, and imaging showed subtotal occlusion of the LIPV stent with reduced perfusion of the left lower lobe despite continuation of apixaban and clopidogrel after initial stenting. A month later, a cardiac-gated contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated near-complete occlusion of the LIPV stent with markedly reduced flow. The scan also revealed a mild left-sided pleural effusion, diminished venous perfusion of the left lower lobe, and reduced lobar volume (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

Given these findings and the recurrence of symptoms, a multidisciplinary team recommended repeat intervention to restore LIPV patency and achieve durable electrical isolation of all pulmonary veins.

In May 2025, under TOE guidance (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2) and with cerebral embolic protection using a Sentinel™ device (Boston Scientific), the left atrium was accessed using a CARTO VIZIGO™ Bi-Directional Guiding Sheath (J&J MedTech). IVUS demonstrated both thrombus and circumferential neointimal hyperplasia within the occluded LIPV stent (Figure 2), findings that were also visualized fluoroscopically (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3). There was a flow turbulence in the left superior pulmonary vein (LSPV) without significant stenosis.

Intravascular ultrasound images of the left inferior pulmonary vein during the procedure. The images suggest circumferential neointimal hyperplasia and thrombus formation within the occluded left inferior pulmonary vein stent.

The lesion was treated with sequential balloon dilations using progressively larger balloons: 4.5 × 20 mm, 6 × 20 mm, and finally 7 × 40 mm [shortest available] drug-eluting balloon (DEB) with the proximal half of the balloon in the left atrium (Figure 3).

Fluoroscopy left anterior oblique 30° view: angioplasty of left inferior pulmonary vein using a 7 × 40 mm drug-eluting balloon. The wire is positioned distally in the left inferior pulmonary vein. CARTO VIZIGO™ Bi-directional Guiding Sheath (J&J Medtech) was used for angiography and angioplasty.

Final angiography demonstrated restoration of venous flow with only a thin residual circumferential rim of tissue, approximately 1 mm in thickness (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). After completion of LIPV balloon angioplasty, angiography demonstrated no significant stenosis of the LSPV (see Supplementary material online, Video S2).

Immediately after re-angioplasty, redo PVI was performed using PFA with the Varipulse™ (Biosense Webster) system. All pulmonary veins were successfully treated and re-isolated, including the previously stented LIPV, where energy applications were delivered antrally around the vein without direct catheter contact within the stent (Figure 4 and Supplementary material online, Figure S4). During the procedure, a run of roof-dependent atrial tachycardia was observed and targeted with PFA application at the superior antrum of the LSPV (Figure 5). The high posterior wall was subsequently completely isolated using PFA. The procedure was completed without complications. Of note, the Sentinel™ filters retrieved small pink tissue fragments measuring 1–2 mm, likely representing embolized thrombotic or neointimal material, underscoring the importance of prioritizing cerebral protection (consistent with a previously published case report10). The patient was discharged on edoxaban, with aspirin prescribed for one month and clopidogrel for a minimum of six months. The procedure and recovery were uneventful, and the patient’s symptoms improved markedly. While he remained arrhythmia-free after this, he developed increasing breathlessness and a repeat CT scan showed a new severe stenosis of the LSPV while the LIPV stent remained widely patent. Following a discussion between his consultant, multidisciplinary team and the patient,11 he had a final pulmonary vein intervention with 8 × 40 mm InPact Admiral DEB (Medtronic) to a short LSPV trunk with early quadrifurcation in November 2025 with good angiographic result and symptomatic relief. Due to progressively worsening breathlessness reported by the patient, CT imaging performed in April 2026 demonstrated progression of recurrent left-sided pulmonary vein disease. The LIPV stent remains patent but shows increasing circumferential in-stent restenosis compared with the November 2025 scan. The LSPV, previously occluded, has recanalized; however, persistent focal stenosis near the left atrial insertion remains and appears more severe than on the April 2025 study. Iodine perfusion maps suggest impaired left lung perfusion related to the pulmonary venous stenoses, while right lung perfusion is preserved.

Fluoroscopy view: Varipulse™ catheter in proximity to the stent in the left inferior pulmonary vein with no interruptions of pulsed-field ablation delivery during the procedure. CARTO VIZIGO™ Bi-directional Guiding Sheath (J&J Medtech) was used for pulsed-field ablation.

Discussion

This case illustrates the safe application of PFA immediately following IVUS-guided revascularization of a near-occluded pulmonary vein stent. Although a case of PFA using Farapulse 8 years after pulmonary vein stenting has previously been described,12 to our knowledge, this is the first reported case not only demonstrating safe use of Varipulse™ (Boston Scientific), but also of concomitant PV intervention and PFA demonstrating safe and effective PFA application in proximity to a metallic stent in a pulmonary vein without adverse thermal or mechanical effects. As this case demonstrates, stenoses can be late and progressive and can involve multiple pulmonary veins. These structural changes can lead to severe luminal narrowing or occlusion, often presenting with non-specific symptoms such as exertional dyspnoea, pleuritic discomfort, or recurrent respiratory infections. Current ESC guidelines recommend intervention for symptomatic patients with significant stenosis.1 Although balloon angioplasty and stenting can restore venous patency, re-stenosis occurs in up to 20% of cases within 12 months.5

IVUS provides high-resolution, real-time assessment of intraluminal anatomy and stent architecture, offering a major advantage over angiography alone.13 It allows direct visualization of thrombus, neointimal hyperplasia, and stent under-expansion, enabling tailored balloon sizing and more effective lesion modification. In this case, IVUS confirmed circumferential neointimal proliferation and thrombus as the mechanisms of re-occlusion, guiding successful sequential balloon dilations and DEB therapy.

PFA is a non-thermal ablation modality that induces selective electroporation of myocardial tissue while preserving surrounding vascular, neural, and oesophageal structures.14 As a result, PFA minimizes the risk of collateral injury or worsening of existing fibrosis. Although theoretical concerns exist regarding electrical field distortion and heating near metallic structures, preclinical and clinical data indicate these effects are negligible when energy delivery avoids direct catheter–stent contact.6 In this case, PFA achieved complete antral isolation of all pulmonary veins, including the previously stented one, without procedural complications and could be performed concomitantly with PV intervention.

The subsequent development of severe LSPV stenosis later in the clinical course warrants consideration. Although a contributory effect of PFA cannot be entirely excluded, current experimental and clinical evidence suggests that PFA produces minimal endothelial injury and preserves vascular architecture, with a markedly lower propensity for pulmonary vein narrowing compared with thermal ablation modalities. In contrast, delayed and progressive PVS is a well-recognized sequela of prior radiofrequency ablation, driven by chronic inflammatory remodelling, fibrosis, and neointimal hyperplasia that may evolve over months or even years. The timing, progressive nature, and occurrence in a patient with prior thermal ablation therefore favour a mechanism consistent with delayed post-radiofrequency PVS rather than a direct effect of PFA.

As a single-patient observation, this report cannot establish generalizable safety or efficacy. The long-term interaction between PFA energy and metallic stents remains to be fully characterized. Future registry data and multicentre experiences will be essential to validate this dual-modality approach and delineate procedural best practices.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of performing PFA immediately following revascularization of a stented pulmonary vein, achieving effective electrical isolation. However, maintaining long-term venous patency remains a significant challenge.
