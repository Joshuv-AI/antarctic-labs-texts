Sellar chondroma in a pediatric patient treated endoscopically: illustrative case

Chondromas are benign cartilaginous tumors that originate from synchondroses, mainly in the short tubular bones of the hands and feet. Intracranial chondromas are exceptionally rare, representing less than 1% of intracranial tumors (0.2%–0.3%).1 Among these, the skull base is the most common site. Although they may occur across a wide age range (15–60 years), with a peak incidence in the 3rd decade,2 occurrence in the pediatric population is exceptionally rare. They may be isolated lesions or associated with genetic syndromes such as Ollier disease or Maffucci syndrome.3,4

Sellar chondromas were first described in 1851 by Hirschfeld5 and later classified by Falconer according to their location into three types: sellar, parasellar, and clival.6 Regarding their origin, it is postulated that they derive from ectopic chondrogenic cells in the synchondroses of the skull base or from chondromatous metaplasia of pluripotential fibroblasts located in the sellar diaphragm.2,7

We present the case of a 16-year-old female patient with a sellar chondroma managed via a transsphenoidal endoscopic approach and adjuvant radiosurgery, with the aim of highlighting the diagnostic challenges, imaging and histopathological characteristics, and therapeutic considerations of this exceptionally rare entity in the pediatric population.

Illustrative Case

Patient Information

A 16-year-old female patient, with no relevant past personal or family medical history and normal psychomotor development with age-appropriate academic performance, presented to the emergency department with a few-month history of secondary amenorrhea, hyporexia, and asthenia accompanied by progressive headache and visual field deficit.

Physical and neurological examination revealed a conscious, oriented, and cooperative patient with vital signs within normal limits. Confrontation visual field testing demonstrated heteronymous bitemporal hemianopsia. Visual acuity was 20/40 in the right eye and 20/60 in the left eye. Funduscopic examination showed bilateral optic discs with sharp margins and no papilledema. The pupils were 2 mm bilaterally, equal, and reactive to light. There was full range of extraocular movements in all directions, no nystagmus or diplopia, and the remaining cranial nerves were unremarkable. Motor strength was 5/5 in all extremities and sensation was intact.

A laboratory workup revealed primary hypothyroidism, for which levothyroxine replacement was initiated and imaging studies were requested.

Diagnostic Assessment

Imaging Workup

Contrast-enhanced brain MRI demonstrated a well-defined intra- and suprasellar lesion measuring 3.6 × 2.9 × 2.5 cm, with lobulated morphology and regular contours. Signal intensity was heterogeneous, with hypointense areas on T1-weighted sequences and hyperintense areas on T2-weighted sequences, and heterogeneous contrast enhancement following gadolinium administration. Differential Diagnosis

Based on the clinical and radiological findings, chordoma, craniopharyngioma, sellar meningioma, atypical pituitary adenoma, and chondrosarcoma were considered as differential diagnoses.

Surgical Treatment and Postoperative Course

Endoscopic transsphenoidal resection of the sellar tumor was performed using the following technique (Video 1). A bilateral endonasal endoscopic approach with the four-hands technique was utilized. A drill-assisted wide sphenoidotomy was performed following identification of key anatomical landmarks. On opening of the sellar floor, a tumor of firm, pearly-white, and lobulated consistency was encountered. Resection was carried out via internal debulking followed by extracapsular dissection. Complete resection could not be achieved, as multiple tumor portions were found to be firmly adherent to the medial wall of the right cavernous sinus. Attempts at further dissection resulted in low-flow venous bleeding, posing unacceptable risk to the adjacent neurovascular structures. Decompression of the optic chiasm was accomplished. Sellar floor reconstruction was performed using a multilayer technique with a dural graft and a pedicled nasoseptal flap.

The patient had a favorable postoperative course without immediate complications. Neither diabetes insipidus, CSF leak, meningitis, hemorrhage, nor new neurological deficits developed. The patient was managed in the intensive care unit for 24 hours, during which hormonal stress coverage with intravenous hydrocortisone and levothyroxine replacement were administered. She was discharged on postoperative day 3 requiring only analgesics, with no hormone replacement therapy at the time of discharge.

Histopathological Study

Macroscopic examination revealed multiple irregular tissue fragments with a pearly-white appearance, irregular surface, and firm consistency, collectively measuring 1.5 × 1.5 cm (Fig. 2A). Microscopic analysis demonstrated mature cartilage with a hypocellular chondroid matrix, with chondrocytes arranged in small clusters displaying round to oval nuclei, without evidence of necrosis, mitotic activity, or infiltrative growth pattern. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for S100 protein and SOX-9 and negative for CK AE1/AE3, IDH-1, brachyury, and GFAP. The Ki-67 proliferation index was less than 1%. These morphological and immunophenotypic findings are consistent with a histopathological diagnosis of chondroma (Fig. 2B–D). Follow-Up and Outcomes

At the 1-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated progressive improvement in visual acuity, reaching 20/25 in both eyes. Visual field testing showed gradual expansion of the visual fields with approximately 70% recovery of the bitemporal defect, along with complete resolution of headache. One-month postoperative MRI demonstrated residual tumor adherent to the right cavernous sinus (Fig. 3), consistent with intraoperative findings, without compression of the optic chiasm or hypothalamus. Endocrine assessment revealed normalized pituitary function, with no requirement for ongoing hormone replacement therapy. The lesion extends 2.2 mm beyond the lateral tangential line of the cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery, consistent with cavernous sinus involvement, and produces displacement of the pituitary gland and infundibulum to the left.

At 3 months, the patient was referred to the oncology and radiation oncology teams for evaluation, and adjuvant radiosurgery was initiated for treatment of the residual tumor.

A total dose of 30 Gy in 5 fractions (6 Gy per fraction) was prescribed, delivered on alternate days. Treatment was planned and administered on an Elekta Versa HD linear accelerator using 6 MV of photon energy in flattening filter-free mode, enabling high-dose rates and reduction of total irradiation time, with the potential benefit of minimizing intrafraction motion.

Immobilization was achieved using a rigid thermoplastic mask specifically designed for cranial radiosurgery, combined with a 6 degrees of freedom robotic couch (Hexapod system), ensuring submillimetric precision. Daily imaging verification with image-guided radiation therapy was performed prior to each fraction.

The dosimetric plan was developed using a volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) technique with five coplanar dynamic arcs of 80° each, with couch rotations at 10°, 350°, 310°, 50°, and 90°, optimizing dose conformity and steepness of the dose gradient. Coverage of 100% of the prescribed dose to 99% of the planning target volume (PTV) was achieved, with adequate conformity and a rapid dose fall-off toward adjacent tissues (Fig. 4). From a radiobiological standpoint, the fractionation scheme was selected in consideration of the indolent behavior of cartilaginous tumors and their presumed low α/β ratio. For quantitative analysis, an α/β value of 2 Gy was adopted, representative of slow-growing tumors and late-responding tissues, in which an increase in dose per fraction yields a proportionally greater biological effect.

A surveillance protocol was established, consisting of brain MRI every 6 months for the first 2 years followed by annual MRI for 5 years, ophthalmological evaluation every 3 months during the 1st year, and endocrine assessment every 6 months for 2 years.

Discussion

Observations

Sellar chondromas represent an exceptional diagnostic and therapeutic challenge in neurosurgery, particularly in the pediatric population. Fewer than 20 cases have been reported,2,6 underscoring the rarity of this entity and the limited evidence available to guide clinical decision-making.

Our patient presented with the classic triad of sellar tumor manifestations: headache, visual field defects, and hormonal alterations.8 Bitemporal hemianopsia secondary to optic chiasm compression is a characteristic finding of suprasellar lesions and was present in our case. Other features commonly associated with intrasellar lesions with extrasellar extension, such as hydrocephalus or cranial nerve palsies, were absent in our case.

Craniopharyngioma was initially considered the leading diagnosis given its well-known predilection for the sellar region and its higher prevalence in this age group. However, following the intraoperative findings and histopathological confirmation, a thorough review of the imaging characteristics of intracranial chondromas described in the existing literature revealed that the radiological findings in this case were indeed consistent with the diagnosis of chondroma.9,10

MRI demonstrated the characteristic “arcs and whorls” pattern described in the literature,11–13 resulting from alternating hyaline cartilage and calcifications within the chondroid matrix. The presence of a hypointense signal on T1-weighted sequences and a hyperintense signal on T2-weighted sequences, along with heterogeneous peripheral enhancement, is a typical finding that helps differentiate chondroma from other sellar lesions.12,13

Complementary CT showed internal calcifications and bone remodeling without aggressive destruction, characteristics that favor the benign nature of the lesion and help distinguish it from malignant processes such as chondrosarcoma.14

The differential diagnosis of sellar lesions in young patients includes pituitary adenomas, craniopharyngiomas, meningiomas, chordomas, and chondrosarcomas.15,16 The rarity of sellar chondroma limits its initial consideration, and preoperative misdiagnosis is common.9 The distinction between chondroma and low-grade chondrosarcoma can be particularly challenging. While some authors suggest that sellar chondromas might represent low-grade chondrosarcomas,17,18 predominant evidence supports their benign nature.19,20 In our case, the absence of nuclear atypia, mitoses, hypercellularity, and an infiltrative pattern, together with a Ki-67 proliferation index less than 1%, confirmed the diagnosis of benign chondroma.

Microscopically, these tumors are composed of lobules of mature hyaline cartilage separated by fibrovascular septa containing chondrocytes in lacunae within a hyaline matrix with multiple foci of calcification. The matrix may contain islands of bone with Haversian systems. They show positivity for S100 protein. Unlike chondrosarcoma, chondromas do not present atypia, mitoses, hypercellularity, or an infiltrative pattern.17–19

Complete resection remains the cornerstone of management, providing the best long-term disease control, symptomatic relief, and lowest recurrence rates.21,22 In recent years, the transsphenoidal endoscopic approach has gained popularity due to its minimally invasive nature and satisfactory results.10,14 In our patient, this approach allowed subtotal macroscopic resection with minimal morbidity, offering better visualization, magnification of anatomical structures, less trauma to normal tissues, and faster recovery compared to traditional transcranial approaches. Complete resection could not be achieved, as multiple tumor portions were firmly adherent to the medial wall of the right cavernous sinus. Attempts at further dissection resulted in low-flow venous bleeding, at which point resection was discontinued to avoid life-threatening vascular injury. This decision was made once adequate optic chiasm decompression had been accomplished and sufficient tissue for histopathological diagnosis had been obtained.

Given the benign biological behavior of chondromas, cytotoxic chemotherapy has no established role and is not recommended.21–23 Adjuvant radiation therapy is generally reserved for cases of subtotal resection or radiological progression, with evidence largely extrapolated from skull base chondrosarcoma series.24–26 In our patient, adjuvant radiosurgery was indicated given the subtotal resection and adherence of residual tumor to the right cavernous sinus.

Lessons

Sellar chondroma should be included in the differential diagnosis of heterogeneous sellar lesions in the pediatric population, even when imaging initially suggests a more common entity such as craniopharyngioma. The characteristic MRI arcs and whorls pattern, combined with CT evidence of calcifications and nonaggressive bone remodeling, is a key imaging feature that should raise suspicion for this diagnosis. Since preoperative diagnosis is rarely achieved, definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological confirmation, including immunohistochemical analysis to exclude chondrosarcoma and chordoma.

When gross-total resection is not achievable due to neurovascular involvement, adjuvant radiosurgery represents a safe and effective complementary strategy. The prognosis following surgical management is generally favorable, with low recurrence rates.15, 18 Long-term surveillance with periodic MRI, ophthalmological evaluation, and endocrine assessment is essential, as these tumors can recur years after surgery.

This report has limitations inherent to a single case report with a relatively short follow-up of 3 months. The natural history of sellar chondroma in the pediatric population remains poorly characterized due to its exceptional rarity, and long-term oncological and functional outcomes cannot yet be fully assessed. The strengths of this case include comprehensive clinical, radiological, and histopathological documentation, diagnostic confirmation through thorough immunohistochemical analysis, and demonstration of the feasibility and safety of the endoscopic transsphenoidal approach in a pediatric patient, contributing to the limited literature on this rare entity.
