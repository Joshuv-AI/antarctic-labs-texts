Infection control challenges during 4 years of COVID-19 in a French psychiatric institution (2020-2024)
Authors: Philippe Gaspard, Martin Martinot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•COVID-19 poses significant infection control challenges in psychiatric facilities.•Its impact varied across psychiatric specialties and according to age.•Psychogeriatric, specialized, and adolescent units experienced high infection rates.•Infection control strategies evolved in response to viral transmission trends.•Tailored infection control measures are essential for psychiatric settings.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has posed unprecedented infection control challenges, particularly, for psychiatric facilities, due to the nature of patient interactions [1]. Psychiatric services encompass a diverse range of specialized units, reflecting the complexity of mental health care. These include acute care units for general adult psychiatry, where patients can leave freely, and closed units designed for individuals who need more intensive psychiatric supervision. Specialized psychiatric beds are also available for children, adolescents, and geriatric populations. In addition to these acute care units, long- or medium-stay units are part of medicalized, specialized, and rehabilitation residential care facilities [2]. Many patients with psychiatric disorders face additional barriers to accessing general health care services [3].

Studies on the effects of COVID-19 in psychiatric settings have typically focused on the impacts of the pandemic within a narrow scope of psychiatric practice, offering a limited analysis of the diverse challenges faced by various psychiatric services [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

During the early phase of the pandemic, psychiatric facilities faced an unprecedented crisis, marked by a lack of knowledge about the SARS-CoV-2 virus, overwhelming demand for protective equipment, and varying levels of medical and paramedical staff capacity to implement infection control measures. Simultaneously, societal lockdowns, widespread public communication efforts, and the mobilization of people to limit the spread of the virus that contributed to potential psychiatric disorders in some individuals [9]. As the pandemic progressed, the emergence of new variants, a better understanding of the virus, the development and rollout of vaccines and treatments, and the introduction of the health pass in France, alongside increased staff training and improved organizational responses, shifted the context, potentially altering outbreak risks and unit responses [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Eventually, the pandemic entered a new phase, characterized by a less virulent yet more transmissible virus with endemic circulation in communities, becoming a regular aspect of patient care and infection control strategies [14].

A comprehensive, long-term approach to mental health care is essential for understanding SARS-CoV-2 transmission in psychiatric facilities during the COVID-19 pandemic and strengthening preparedness for future health crises and airborne infection prevention. Therefore, our study aimed to analyze COVID-19 infection control challenges over 4 years across acute and residential psychiatric care settings.

Materials and methods

Design and setting

In this study, we analyzed data collected between March 2020 and May 2024 from 20 units at Rouffach Psychiatric Hospital in Southern Alsace, France. The study encompassed six psychiatric care settings: four acute care units—general adult, closed, psychogeriatric, and adolescent psychiatry—and two residential care units, which included medicalized residential/rehabilitation and specialized care. Virological surveillance, patient data, and activities

Three types of data were used for surveillance: real-time reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), point-of-care antigen-detecting rapid diagnostic tests (Ag-RDTs), and patient admission and discharge records. Nasopharyngeal samples were collected based on medical prescriptions. Virus identification was initially performed using the real-time RT-PCR VIASURE SARS-CoV-2 S gene RT-PCR Kit (CerTest Biotec, Zaragoza, Spain). Starting in May 2023, the BD MAX respiratory panel for the BD MAX system (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was also used. The results of positive virological tests were transmitted electronically to the unit and the infection prevention control team daily to ensure proper verification and implementation of hygiene measures.

Ag-RDTs became available in the units starting in October 2020, with the COVID‐VIRO test (AAZ‐LMB, Boulogne‐Billancourt) and BIOSYNEX COVID-19 Ag BSS tests (Biosynex Swiss SA, Fribourg, Switzerland). The pharmacy tracked the distribution of rapid COVID-19 tests, recording the order date, the requesting unit, and the quantity dispensed. Rapid testing was primarily used for situations requiring quick results, with the protocols to follow-up with a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test for confirmation. If RT-PCR confirmation was performed, only the PCR result was considered for surveillance. In cases without molecular confirmation, only positive Ag-RDT results were reported to the hygiene team for COVID-19 surveillance, whereas negative Ag-RDT results were excluded from the process. The number of direct hospital admissions, discharges, and individuals present on any given day was tracked using data from the information system of the institution. For RT-PCR tests and positive Ag-RDTs, sex, age, and psychiatric care setting were recorded. For patients with multiple positive tests, only one positive result per epidemic wave was retained, assuming each wave corresponded to a new infection episode.

Data analysis

Chronological data analysis was conducted from week 9 of 2020 to week 21 of 2024, with the pandemic timeline structured around virus waves, infection control measures, and vaccination campaigns. The COVID-19 pandemic was categorized into four phases: the initial SARS-CoV-2 outbreak (waves 1 and 2), the vaccination phase (waves 3 and 4), the regulated social activity phase involving sanitary and vaccine passes (waves 5-7), and the seasonal community circulation phase (waves 8 and beyond), as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

The percentage of positive RT-PCR (positive tests/total RT-PCR tests) and the ratio of positive Ag-RDTs per 100 tests ordered by unit were calculated. In acute care units, positive tests were analyzed based on the admission date, considering the delay between admission and test execution. A binary classification was used: tests that returned positive results within the first 5 days after admission were classified as inpatient positive admissions (IPAs), whereas tests that returned positive results after this 5-day period were classified as inpatient positive post-admissions (IPPs) [15]. Outbreaks were defined as occurring when three positive in-unit tests were identified within 7 days and ending when no new cases were detected for 7 consecutive days. Each outbreak was described by the activity of the unit affected, the pandemic phase, the duration of the outbreak (measured from the first to the last day), the total number of tests performed (positive and negative), and the attack rate (calculated as the number of patients tested positive divided by the total number of patients treated, multiplied by 100). The time to reach 50.0% of the cases was also calculated for each outbreak.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test and Wilcoxon and the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P ≤0.05. Multivariable associations between patient characteristics and SARS-CoV-2 positivity were assessed using logistic regression with cluster-robust (Huber–White) standard errors to account for within-patient correlation of repeated tests. Statistics were performed using R software (Version 4.3.2, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Overview of virological testing

Over the 4-year surveillance period, a total of 6552 RT-PCR tests and 3314 Ag-RDTs were conducted, with positive rates of 7.7% (502 positive RT-PCR tests) and 1.1% (38 positive Ag-RDTs). Of the 540 total positive tests, 361 (66.9%) were linked to outbreak investigations, whereas 179 (33.1%) occurred outside the outbreak periods (Supplementary Table 2). In acute care units, 412 positive tests were recorded, with IPAs accounting for 18.4% (76 of 412), whereas IPPs accounted for 81.6% (336 of 412). Among IPP cases, 78.9% (265 of 336) were detected during outbreaks. In residential care units, 128 positive tests were identified (127 IPPs and one IPA), with 75.0% (96 of 128) occurring during outbreaks. Inpatient positivity patterns in acute and residential care during outbreaks

The outbreak analysis, conducted over the 4-year surveillance period, covered 13 acute care units and seven residential care units, following the defined timeline (Supplementary Table 5).

In the early waves (1 and 2), five outbreaks were observed in acute care units: two occurred in psychogeriatric units during wave 1 (15.4% of acute care units, two of 13) and three occurred in general adult and closed units during wave 2 (23.1%, three of 13). Wave 1 saw an average of 12.7 admissions per week (AW) (140 admissions over 11 weeks), whereas wave 2 saw a higher rate of 36.8 AW (957 admissions over 26 weeks). In residential care units, four outbreaks were identified in wave 1, affecting 57.1% (four of seven) of the units. However, during the vaccination phase (March to September 2020), despite the high admission rates in waves 3 and 4 (42.6 and 42.1 AW, respectively), no outbreaks were reported in either acute or residential care units.

Starting in October 2021 (wave 5), acute care units experienced frequent clusters, with 76.9% (10 of 13) of units reporting at least one outbreak during the pass regulation and community circulation phases. During these phases, AW ranged from 36.9 to 39.3. In residential care units, 57.1% (four of seven) experienced outbreaks during the pass regulation phase, whereas 42.9% (three of seven) experienced outbreaks during the community circulation phase. The analysis of the median number of outbreaks per unit across psychiatric activities during the 2020-2024 surveillance period revealed that psychogeriatric units experienced four outbreaks per unit, closed units had three, adolescent, general adult, and medicalized residential/rehabilitation care units had two, and specialized residential care units experienced one (P = 0.01).

The analysis of median outbreak attack rates across affected units is presented in Table 5. For each psychiatric activity, median attack rates showed no significant differences across the various pandemic phases. However, comparing median attack rates across different psychiatric activities throughout the entire pandemic period, significant differences were found (P < 0.01). Lower median outbreak attack rates were observed in general adult care units (25.0%, N = 193 patients exposed during outbreak periods) and medicalized residential/rehabilitation care units (28.6%, N = 127). Higher median attack rates were recorded in adolescent care units (76.9%, N = 21), specialized residential care units (72.5%, N = 78), and psychogeriatric care units (50.0%, N = 225). Discussion

Our study examines IPP and IPA rates, as well as outbreaks, across different psychiatric activities and pandemic waves, from the emergence of COVID-19 and its initial confusion to community-level management and seasonal circulation.

Rouffach Hospital Center, located in northeastern France, is near Mulhouse, where a large religious gathering of approximately 2000 people occurred from February 17 to 24, 2020. This event catalyzed the rapid spread of the epidemic across the region in early March [16], contributing to a high positive admission test rate of over 6.0% in acute care units during wave 1. This initial phase underscores the critical importance of managing and organizing admissions as a fundamental component of any pandemic risk strategy in psychiatric settings, particularly, in environments with limited access to virological testing and incomplete knowledge of the pathogen [17]. A centralized admission management system is essential to limit admissions when possible and allocate patients based on room availability, the presence of active outbreaks in specific units, the risk of severe disease, the availability of diagnostic tests, and adequate ventilation [18]. At Rouffach, this system was implemented in a dedicated pavilion separate from other care units. It included a medical office, treatment room, waiting area, and two restrooms to facilitate patient assessment and triage. This coordinated approach directs patients to the most appropriate care settings, optimizes infection control measures, and minimizes the risk of cross-contamination. Centralized admissions are particularly effective in settings with limited resources and staff shortages, enabling optimized patient management based on clinical and psychiatric stability before integration into care units [19]. In addition, this approach considers the complexity of individual patient statuses, including behaviors, adherence to hygiene protocols, and varying levels of vulnerability and fragility while easing the burden on healthcare teams during crises [20].

During the first wave, surgical masks were worn only in cases of symptomatic infection or outbreaks, whereas continuous mask use was implemented during the second wave. In addition, the hospital adopted the national strategy of screening, isolation, contact tracing, and protection [21]. Because contact tracing was not feasible in specific units, large-scale screening campaigns were conducted whenever a positive case was detected. However, a key challenge in psychiatric care is the difficulty of consistently enforcing isolation measures for some patients. During wave 2, closed units were identified as high-risk clusters, leading to the implementation of filtering facepiece 2 (FFP2) masks for staff and mandatory surgical masks for patients.

Between waves 3 and 4, the widespread rollout of COVID-19 vaccines significantly transformed pandemic management, including in psychiatric units. This period was a concerted effort by health care workers to support patient vaccinations [22,23]. Upon admission, the patients’ vaccination status was assessed, and vaccination was offered during their stay. The results indicated no outbreaks in psychiatric units during this phase, likely due to a combination of barrier measures, vaccination campaigns, and low virus circulation, reflected by a low positive test rate among admissions (0.5%) despite sustained activity (an average of 42.6 AW). Intra- and extra-unit care was progressively regulated based on room size, natural ventilation, and health passes during wave 4 [24]. These adjustments allowed essential care for psychiatric patients to resume while ensuring safety.

During waves 5-7, activities gradually normalized, with AW ranging from 36.9 to 39.3. This shift was facilitated by high vaccination coverage in the general population and the introduction of health and vaccination passes to regulate intra- and extra-unit interactions. Psychiatric units were able to reopen communal spaces, such as cafeterias, under strict regulations, including limits on the number of participants and improved ventilation measures. However, this period was also marked by the emergence of Omicron variants [25]. A previous study (2005-2012) in the same hospital identified a 25% contamination rate of units during winter flu seasons. Conversely, the Omicron variants had an unprecedented impact, affecting over 70% of the units [26]. The Omicron variant was highly mutated, allowing it to evade immunization, especially among elderly patients whose immunity was waning. By this stage of the pandemic, medical considerations, such as the reduced severity of infections in vaccinated populations and the need for social interaction, led to the adoption of lighter strategies. However, these measures proved less effective, particularly, with the emergence of the highly contagious Omicron variant. These strategies included the widespread use of antigen testing, despite its reduced sensitivity and the risk of false negatives, increased reliance on double-occupancy rooms, and group activities such as meals, regulated by theoretical participant limits based on room size but without considering air circulation rates. Recommendations for improved ventilation were issued without systematically monitoring their implementation or effectiveness [27]. In addition, adherence to barrier measures significantly declined during this period, further complicating infection control efforts [28]. Psychiatric units, except for psychogeriatric and specialized residential units, typically accommodate patients at low risk. Consequently, infection control measures were occasionally relaxed by health care providers despite the occasional presence of patients at high risk of mortality.

In the final phase (waves 8 and beyond), COVID-19 management adapted to normalizing community circulation by gradually lifting restrictions [29,30]. At the studied institution, the mandatory use of masks in units was lifted in March 2023, except in cases of symptoms, outbreaks, or a national flu alert. The epidemic impact during this seasonal phase mirrored that of the previous regulatory phase, with approximately 70% of psychiatric units affected in both cases. This highlights the continued high epidemic risk posed by a highly contagious virus in the absence of immunity, regardless of the regulatory measures or standard hygiene protocols in place for seasonal epidemics.

Between 2020-2024, psychogeriatric activities were at a high risk of outbreaks and elevated infection attack rates, primarily due to the vulnerability of elderly patients and their prolonged lengths of stay, which increased exposure. Many patients admitted to these units had come from nursing homes or hospitals where the virus had a significant impact [31]. The challenges in enforcing barrier measures among patients with severe cognitive impairments in psychogeriatric and specialized residential care contributed to outbreaks during wave 1. In addition, the lack of systematic testing upon admission during wave 1, combined with behavioral disorders, played a role in the spread of infections [7]. In the multivariable analysis, however, the psychogeriatric unit itself was not independently associated with SARS-CoV-2 positivity once age was taken into account, suggesting that the higher infection rates observed in these units were largely driven by the advanced age and vulnerability of patients rather than by a specific unit-related transmission context.

To mitigate outbreaks in psychogeriatric and specialized residential care, it is crucial to implement proactive measures—including widespread testing. Providing additional support, such as FFP2 masks for health care workers (because patients often could not maintain physical distance), enforcing strict visitor policies, maintaining cleaning protocols, reorganizing spaces, and offering regular staff training, is also essential [32]. In addition, close monitoring of vaccination coverage among patients and health care is vital [33].

In our study, the multivariable analysis showed that positivity rates were similar between men and women but increased with age, consistent with age-dependent susceptibility to infection, whereas sex differences are mainly reported for disease severity. In addition, specific risks associated with certain psychiatric care settings were identified [34,35]. Closed units were associated with higher odds of SARS-CoV-2 positivity at admission (IPAs) and during hospitalization (IPPs), whereas adolescent units and residential care units were associated with increased odds only for IPPs, compared with general adult acute care units. These settings combine several factors that may facilitate viral transmission, including frequent close interactions between patients, shared communal spaces, prolonged lengths of stay in residential care, and difficulties in consistently applying infection control measures among patients with acute psychiatric symptoms or behavioral disorders. In addition, admissions to closed units are often performed under emergency conditions, which may limit the possibility of thorough screening at entry and increase the risk of introducing infection into the unit, as suggested by the multivariable analysis. By contrast, general adult acute care units showed the lowest risk of positivity in the multivariable model, likely reflecting shorter lengths of stay, greater patient autonomy, and better adherence to hygiene measures. From an organizational perspective, these units may, therefore, represent lower-risk environments, where optimizing patient flow and reducing patient density can help limit transmission. Maintaining flexible admission policies and integrating outpatient care may further support adaptation without compromising psychiatric care [36].

Ventilation likely could contribute to transmission and the units were equipped with single-flow mechanical ventilation systems providing ≤1 ACH, markedly below recommended values for health care facilities (approximately 6 ACH for patient rooms) [[37], [38], [39]]. This low ACH in our institution may have facilitated transmission, particularly, in units characterized by prolonged stays, shared spaces, and challenges in adherence to infection control measures, such as psychogeriatric, closed, adolescent, and residential care units. Although ventilation was not directly measured at the room level in our study, these findings support the integration of ventilation performance, including ACH targets, into infection prevention strategies in psychiatric settings.

Beyond the inherent variability in viral transmissibility, the challenges posed by adherence to infection control measures, and the intrinsic limitations of preventive strategies, the broader social context played a significant role. Although public health measures, such as lockdowns and mass vaccination campaigns, helped control outbreaks, the gradual relaxation of these measures from late 2021 onward also facilitated transmission, increasing the risk of the virus entering psychiatric facilities. This highlights the dual impact of social dynamics as a tool for containment and a pathway for the spread of infection [40,41].

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single psychiatric hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings with different patient populations, organizational structures, or infection control practices. Second, its retrospective design spans a prolonged period and involves multiple factors, such as human factors, personal protective equipment, vaccination, treatments, and diagnostic tools—some of which were not examined. Third, the analysis was limited to patients and residents because no data were available on health care worker infections or immune status, which may have contributed to transmission dynamics within the hospital.

Conclusion

Effective infectious disease control in psychiatric settings requires a comprehensive approach integrating structured admission protocols, enhanced protective measures—especially for high-risk units such as psychogeriatric, closed, and specialized residential units—and swift outbreak containment measures, including isolation and widespread testing. Furthermore, the active involvement of health care workers and patients in infection prevention efforts through adherence to hygiene protocols and vaccinations is essential. The adaptability of psychiatric services is also crucial, necessitating flexible, patient-centered care solutions. Optimizing ventilation systems and increasing access to single-occupancy rooms should be prioritized to align psychiatric infection control with broader health care standards. However, patient behaviors and social dimensions of psychiatric care must be considered, highlighting the need for tailored infection prevention strategies.
