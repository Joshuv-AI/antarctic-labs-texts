How important is intraoperative blood pressure for surgical outcomes?
Authors: Bon-Wook Koo
Published: 2026

Intraoperative blood pressure is one of the most modifiable physiological variables during organ transplantation; however, its importance should be interpreted in context rather than by a single universal threshold. In this issue of the Korean Journal of Anesthesiology, Katayama et al. [1] showed that not all cases of hypotension in liver transplantation have the same prognostic weight. Their data support the simple but important principle that both the depth and duration of hypotension are critical.

In their retrospective cohort of 1063 adult liver transplant recipients, nearly all patients experienced intraoperative hypotension; however, only moderate-to-severe hypotension was independently associated with increased 1-year mortality [1]. Prolonged exposure to a mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 65 or < 60 mmHg was not independently linked to mortality, whereas exposure to an MAP < 55 mmHg for 43 min or longer, MAP < 50 mmHg for 14 min or longer, MAP < 45 mmHg for 13 min or longer, and MAP < 40 mmHg for 5 min or longer were associated with worse survival [1]. These findings extend earlier work showing that an MAP < 55 mmHg is associated with increased 30-d mortality after liver transplantation, whereas a sustained MAP < 50 mmHg and marked blood pressure lability are linked to early mortality and graft failure [2,3].

Why are liver transplant recipients potentially able to tolerate lower pressures than other surgical patients? End-stage liver disease is characterized by a hyperdynamic circulation with low systemic vascular resistance and relatively low baseline arterial pressure. Thus, extrapolating a rigid MAP target of 65 mmHg from general non-cardiac surgery to liver transplantation may be overly simplistic. Nevertheless, the present study strongly suggests that there remains a lower physiological limit below which compensation fails and prognosis worsens [1,2]. The association among deeper hypotension, longer exposure, and higher intraoperative lactate levels further supports the theory that severe hypotension may reflect or contribute to clinically important hypoperfusion [1].

Intraoperative blood pressure is equally important in kidney transplantation. Intraoperative hypotension has been associated with slow and delayed graft function, and recent data suggest that the clearest signal may emerge around an MAP of 55 mmHg [4]. In addition, slow graft function has been reported at higher MAP thresholds in some analyses [5]. This is clinically plausible because a newly reperfused kidney allograft depends on adequate perfusion pressure and many recipients have chronic hypertension, which may shift autoregulation to the right. Accordingly, blood pressure targets during kidney transplantation should not rely on an isolated absolute number alone but should also reflect the recipient’s baseline hemodynamics and the need to preserve graft perfusion [4,5].

Hypotension has even more immediate implications for heart transplantation. The newly transplanted heart is highly sensitive to ischemia-reperfusion injury, conditions involving right ventricular loading, and primary graft dysfunction. In this setting, hypotension may be an early warning sign of low cardiac output and impending multiorgan hypoperfusion rather than a passive numeric abnormality [6]. Early mortality after heart transplantation is driven largely by primary graft dysfunction, low cardiac output states, and right ventricular failure rather than by the MAP alone; thus, hypotension often serves as a visible marker of these critical abnormalities [6]. Perioperative management must therefore focus not only on maintaining an adequate MAP, commonly at least 60–65 mmHg, but also on the simultaneous assessment of cardiac output, filling pressures, lactate, and end-organ perfusion [7].

The broader message is clear: though intraoperative blood pressure is critically important, its significance is organ-, patient-, and phase-specific. The goal is not to defend a single magic number but to develop transplantation-specific blood pressure strategies that integrate the MAP with the physiology of the organ at risk.
