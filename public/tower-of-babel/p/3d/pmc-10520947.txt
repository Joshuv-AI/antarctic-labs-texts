Nickel induces blood-testis barrier damage through ROS-mediated p38 MAPK pathways in mice
Authors: Yuxin Zeng, Qing Yang, Yujuan Ouyang, Yanbin Lou, Hengmin Cui, Huidan Deng, Yanqiu Zhu, Yi Geng, Ping Ouyang, Lian Chen, Zhicai Zuo, Jing Fang, Hongrui Guo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Nickel (Ni) is an essential common environmental contaminant, it is hazardous to male reproduction, but the precise mechanisms are still unknown. Blood-testis barrier (BTB), an important testicular structure consisting of connections between sertoli cells, is the target of reproductive toxicity caused by many environmental toxins. In this study, ultrastructure observation and BTB integrity assay results indicated that NiCl2 induced BTB damage. Meanwhile, BTB-related proteins including the tight junction (TJ), adhesion junction (AJ) and the gap junction (GJ) protein expression in mouse testes as well as in sertoli cells (TM4) were significantly decreased after NiCl2 treatment. Next, the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) was co-treated with NiCl2 to study the function of oxidative stress in NiCl2-mediated BTB deterioration. The results showed that NAC attenuated testicular histopathological damage, and the expression of BTB-related proteins were markedly reversed by NAC co-treatment in vitro and vivo. Otherwise, NiCl2 activated the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. And, NAC co-treatment could significantly inhibit p38 activation induced by NiCl2 in TM4 cells. Furthermore, in order to confirm the role of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway in NiCl2-induced BTB impairment, a p38 inhibitor (SB203580) was co-treated with NiCl2 in TM4 cells, and p38 MAPK signaling inhibition significantly restored BTB damage induced by NiCl2 in TM4 cells. These results suggest that NiCl2 treatment destroys the BTB, in which the oxidative stress-mediated p38 MAPK signaling pathway plays a vital role.

Synopsis

Minimal research exists on the male reproductive toxicology of environmental pollution Ni. This study reveals the detail mechanism of Ni-induced blood-testis barrier damage.

Introduction

Nickel (Ni) is an essential micro-element for animals, and the demand for Ni in animals is extremely low. Micronutrients are good for the body, but excessive amounts can be harmful [1]. Hence, excessive ingestion of Ni above the metabolic requirement will have toxic effects [2]. At the same time, Ni is a silvery-white metal with good mechanical strength and is easily ductile. Ni belongs to the ferromagnetic materials, with magnetic, electrical, thermal and corrosion resistance, and its chemical properties are stable and not easily oxidized [3]. Therefore, Ni is often used in steel, Ni alloys, electroplating and Ni-cadmium batteries, and is also widely used in aerospace, defense, and machinery manufacturing [4,5]. In addition, Ni can be used as a catalyst in the chemical and food industries [6]. The widely industrial use of Ni lead to extensive environmental contamination in air, water, and soil, and then increase the risk of human overexposure to Ni [7]. Usually, the average Ni levels of the environment in general areas are in the range of 0. 00001∼0. 003 μg/m3, while in Ni industrial areas the range can reach 0. 07∼0. 77 μg/m3 [8]. Meanwhile, it has been reported that the safe concentration of Ni in drinking water should be less than 70 μg/L, while in Ni industrial areas, the concentration of Ni in drinking water can reach 70–200 μg/L [9]. In addition, excessive Ni content in soil is one of the most serious problems of Ni contamination. Ni levels in soil near industrial areas have ranged from 3 mg/kg to 1000 mg/kg [10]. It has been found that Ni can cause relevant occupational hazards through inhalation, oral or dermal contact [11,12]. Ni exposure occurs through various routes in different workplaces, and exposure to Ni also ranges from micrograms to milligrams [13]. The inhalation route is the most common form of Ni exposure. Chronic bronchitis and COPD constitute the largest proportion of occupational morbidity in jobs such as Ni mining and Ni electrolysis workers [14]. Lung and nasal cancers have also been found in the Ni occupationally exposed population [15]. In addition, Ni exposure also occurs through oral exposure. The main sources of oral exposure to Ni include water and food contaminated with Ni compounds [16]. Ni contaminants released into the environment from industrial processes are enriched in soil, water resources, and crops, resulting in toxicity to humans through direct and indirect oral ingestion of Ni-containing food and water resources [17,18].

Ni toxicity has attracted more attention in recent years since the widespread growth of Ni contamination [19]. Previous researches have demonstrated that excessive Ni exposure could induce hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, pulmonary toxicity, and reproductive toxicity [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]].

In recent years, widespread global attention has begun to focus on the rising prevalence of infertility with the increase in environmental pollution. In particular, male infertility accounts for 50% of infertility. Decline in sperm quantity and quality is a critical factor in male infertility [27]. At the same time, there is growing evidence that Ni has male reproductive toxicity. The clinical data have showed the association between urinary metal concentrations and semen quality, especially, there is a positive correlation between urinary Ni and sperm malformation rate [28]. Zhou et al. [29] also have demonstrated that Ni environmental exposure result the increased in sperm DNA damage. A study by Pandey et al. [30] has revealed that oral administration of Ni sulfate (NiSO4) was able to precipitate histological changes in the testes and epididymis and reduce sperm viability and sperm count in mice. Sun et al. [31] have found that Ni treatment disrupts the development of testes and inhibits fecundity in Spodoptera litura Fabricius.

The toxicological mechanism of Ni-induced male reproduction damage remains to be fully understood. Ni overexposure decreases sperm quality and increases malformation [32,33]. Also, the results of Liu et al. [34] have showed that Ni nanoparticles treatment cause apoptosis and DNA damage in germ cells. Meanwhile, our previous studies have demonstrated that after gavage with NiCl2 (7. 5/15/30 mg/kg) for 14 and 28 days respectively, Ni could accumulate in the testes of mice, and, disruption of hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis plays a crucial role in NiCl2-induced spermatogenic disorder in mice [35].

Male fertility depends on the production of large numbers of healthy, high-quality sperm during spermatogenesis, and the blood-testis barrier (BTB) is a testicular structure that is absolutely crucial for the successful production of sperm [36]. The BTB is a physical barrier between the testicular spermatogenic tubules and the blood vessels, established mainly by sertoli cells including tight junctions (TJ), gap junctions (GJ) and adherens junctions (AJ). BTB not only prevents the penetration of blood toxins into the spermatogenic tubules that can cause sperm damage, but also prevents the immune system in the testis from recognizing germ cells as foreign, whose integrity provides a stable spermatogenic internal environment that is critical for spermatogenesis [[37], [38], [39]]. There is growing evidence that the BTB may be a target for the reproductive toxic effects of environmental toxicants. It has confirmed that heavy metal such as Cd, Pb, As and Cr could impair BTB [[40], [41], [42]]. Up to now, it is not clear whether BTB is the target of Ni toxicology. Ni and Ni-containing compounds are able to penetrate the BTB and accumulate in the testes [43]. And it has been reported that Ni has an apoptotic effect on Sertoli-germ cells [44]. Cytokines sustain the ratio of spermatogenic cells to sertoli cells by regulating the process of apoptosis, at the same time, it can induce the reorganization of BTB and apical ES to ensure the dynamic balance of BTB. Our previous studies have shown that NiCl2 induces testicular cell apoptosis through oxidative stress. However, the detailed mechanism of the effect of NiCl2 on BTB has not been reported yet.

P38 MAPK signaling pathway can be involved in regulating BTB construction. Studies have confirmed that heavy metals such as Cd can activate p38 MAPK, which lowers the amount of expression of proteins at the corresponding site of BTB, which in turn causes the destruction of BTB [45]. Reproductive damage brought on by environmental pollution is significantly influenced by oxidative stress [46]. It has been reported that ROS can disrupt the integrity of BTB and lead to reproductive toxicity [47]. In addition, it has been investigated that some environmental toxicants, such as DEHP, PM2. 5, can cause oxidative stress, which increases ROS, to activate the p38 MAPK signaling pathway, which directly impairs the integrity of BTB [48]. So p38 MAPK signaling pathway may be crucial in ROS-induced BTB injury.

Ni acts on spermatogenic cells, either by having to cross the BTB first or by disrupting the function of the sertoli cells and further acting on the germ cells. Thus, the BTB is the target of many environmental toxicants. However, more detailed studies are needed on whether Ni can induce reproductive toxicity by inducing damage to testicular sertoli cell intercellular junctions through the ROS-p38 MAPK signaling pathway. In this study, the BTB function is measured in vivo and vitro experiment after treated with NiCl2. Furthermore, the detail mechanism of Ni-induced BTB damage is investigated, including oxidative stress and p38 MAPK pathway [49].

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

NiCl2 came from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co, Ltd. (Chengdu, China). N-acetylcysteine (NAC) (A9165) and SB20358 (M9281) was provided by Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai, China). 2′, 7′-dichloro fluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) (S0033 M) was provided by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). EZ-Link™ Sulfo–NHS–LC-biotin (21335) was provided by Thermo Scientific (American), Streptavidin-Fitc (S3762) was provided by Sigma (Shanghai, China).

Antibodies involved in the current research included: rabbit anti-ZO-1 (21773-1-AP, Proteintech, American), rabbit anti- N-cadherin (YM0465, immunoway, American), rabbit anti-Occludin (27260-1-AP, Proteintech, American), rabbit anti-F11R (ab270446, abcam, UK), rabbit anti-p38 (YP0203, immunoway, American), rabbit anti-p-p38 (2971, immunoway, American), rabbit anti-Claudin-11 (YT6134 immunoway, American), rabbit anti-Connexin-43 (26980-1-AP, Proteintech, American), mouse anti-F-actin (ab205, abcam, UK).

Animals and treatments

One hundred and twenty healthy ICR male mice were obtained from Dashuo Biotech (Chengdu, China). They were 8 weeks old and fed with unlimited amounts of standard rodent food and water, and kept under controlled temperature (22–25 °C), humidity (50–60%) and light conditions (12:12 hlight/dark light). Following adaptation feeding for a week, 120 mice were divided into 4 groups of 30 mice each at random and administered by gavage at 1 mL/100 g, while the control group was given distilled water by gavage daily and the experimental group animals were given 7. 5, 15 and 30 mg/kg NiCl2. 6H2O by gavage for 14/28 days, then the mice were humanely executed and their testicular samples were collected for subsequent experimental analysis. The Ni doses in this study were reported with reference to previous laboratory studies [35], it was known from pre-tests that the LD50 for gavage of NiCl2. 6H2O in mice was 306. 11 mg/kg, so 1/40, 1/20, and 1/10 of the LD50 were selected for the low, medium, and high dose groups in this study, respectively.

In addition, to examine how oxidative stress affects BTB injury caused by NiCl2, we randomly divided 40 healthy ICR male mice into four groups with the same feeding standards and gavage doses. In the NiCl2 and NAC + NiCl2 groups, we gavaged 30 mg/kg of NiCl2, and in the control and NAC groups, we gavaged identical volumes of distilled water. Mice in the control and NiCl2 groups received similar doses of PBS intraperitoneally, while mice in the NAC and NAC + NiCl2 groups received intraperitoneal injections of NAC (100 mg/kg body weight/day) [50]. The mice underwent treatment for 28 days before being executed humanely and having samples of their testicles taken for further experimentation. The Sichuan Agricultural University's Animal Care and Ethics Committee accepted the protocol, and the tests were carried out in accordance with it (approval number: 2012-024, Chengdu, China).

Pathologic observation

Mice were dissected on days 14 and 28 of the experiment, and samples of testes and epididymis were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, treated with graded alcohol and xylene, paraffin embedded, then stained using hematoxylin-eosin. Sections were observed using an Olympus microscope (Olympus, Japan) to assess tissue damage.

Ultrastructure observation

Testis were fixed with 2. 5% glutaraldehyde, rinsed with 0. 1 M phosphoric acid rinse solution, then fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide and rinsed again, dehydrated with acetone, and embedded with embedding agent. Then the ultrastructure was evaluated, and the prepared ultrathin sections were stained with 3% uranium acetate and lead citrate. Ultrastructural micrographs were acquired using a JEM-1400 flash transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan).

BTB integrity

EZ-Link™ Sulfo–NHS–LC-Biotin tracer (21335, Thermo, American) was dissolved in PBS containing 1 mM calcium chloride (10 mg/ml). Mice were anesthetized, 50 μl of this working solution was injected into the testes, the wounds were sutured, and the mice survived for 30 min before the testes were removed and cut into 5-μm cryosections. After the sections were completed, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, then washed three times with PBST, closed with 5% fetal bovine serum for 1 h, and then incubated with Streptavidin-Fitc (S3762, Sigma, Shanghai, China) for 1 h. The sections were washed three times with PBST [51,52]. Finally, after blocking with Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), testicular sections were photographed with a confocal microscope (Olympus).

Cell culture

ATCC provided the mouse Sertoli cell (TM4) cell line (CRL-1715). TM4 cells were grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). In order to assure cell nutrition and maintain cells in logarithmic growth phase, cells were passaged 1/4 when the cell growth density was between 80% and 90%.

Assessment of cell viability

The viability of TM4 cells was measured using MTT assay. TM4 cells were spread in 96-well plates. We treated cells with gradient concentrations of NiCl2 for 12 h and 24 h, and then cells incubated by MTT (ST316, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 4 h. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured by microplate reader. In the in vitro TM4 cell dose determination, based on the half inhibitory concentration of NiCl2 on TM4 cells (IC50 = 3 mM, 24 h) and the pre-test assay, the NiCl2 treatment concentration for the low, medium, and high dose groups were determined to be 0. 75 mM, 1. 5 mM, and 3 mM, respectively. Cells were collected at the 12 h/24 h of NiCl2 treatment, respectively.

Determination of oxidative damage and antioxidant parameters

The testicular samples were homogenized with 9 vol of pre-cooled 0. 9% NaCl2 solution using a homogenizer. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and total protein levels were measured using a Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to Bradford's method. The supernatant was subsequently used to detect T-AOC (Cat. no. A015), SOD (Cat. no. A001-1), GSH (Cat; no. A061-1) and MDA (Cat. no. A003-1). All kits were purchased from Nanjing Jianjian Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China), and the assays were performed according to the kit instructions.

ROS detection by fluorescence staining and flow cytometry

For the ROS detection by fluorescent staining, images were captured using fluorescence microscopy after cells were treated with 10 m/L DCFH-DA (S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and blocked with Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

For ROS detection by flow cytometry, TM4 was digested down with trypsin, pre-chilled PBS washed twice, and the cells were suspended in PBS with the concentration adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/ml. DCFH-DA (10 μm/L) was diluted into working solution with PBS, added dropwise to the flow tube of cell suspension, incubated for 30 min and after the incubation was completed, the cells were washed by centrifugation and then PBS, and repeated 3 times After incubation, the cells were centrifuged and washed with PBS, and then repeated 3 times to remove the excess DCFH-DA working solution, and then analyzed by flow cytometry.

All of the above methods are based on the study reported by Guo et al. [53].

Immunofluorescence

Mouse testis were cut into 5 μm frozen sections, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and washed three times with PBS. Sections were punched with 0. 2% Triiton-100c for 15 min and then washed three times with PBS. After blocking with 5% fetal bovine serum for 1 h, sections were incubated with primary antibody overnight. After washing with PBS for 15 min, sections were covered with secondary antibodies. Finally, sections were mounted using Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Images were obtained using confocal microscopy (Olympus).

Western blotting

Testis were homogenized in RIPA buffer, and TM4 cells were lysed in NP40. After that, cell lysates were centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C (ST2045, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). BCA protein assay reagent was then used to measure the protein concentration (P0011, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Different size proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, then rinsed in secondary antibody for 1 h. Protein bands were observed with Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS + System with ECL kit (P0018A, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Significant differences in protein expression were determined using ImageJ2x software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

RNA from testis and TM4 cells was extracted using the UNlQ-10 Column Trizol Total RNA Isolation Kit (B511321, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed according to the instruction operation of PrimeScript® RT reagent Kit With gDNA Eraser kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan), and the resulting cDNA was stored at −20 °C for use.

The primers were designed according to the requirements for primers in qRT-PCR test and verified by BLAST, and β-actin was selected as the internal reference gene, and the primers used in this qPCR experiment are shown in Table 1. After the primer design was completed, the qRT-PCR reaction was carried out using SYBR Ex TaqTM II Master Mix Kit (DRR820A, Takara, Japan), and the reaction program was as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing for 30 s for 44 cycles; 95 °C for 10 s; 72 °C for 10 s (the rate of change in temperature was 0. 5 °C/s) were detected using LightCycler®480 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8. 0 and SPSS software (version 21. 0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare data between groups. Data were represented as means ± standard deviation. P < 0. 05 indicates a significant difference.

Additionally, as shown in Fig. 1 b, NiCl2 treatment induced abnormal histopathological variation of epididymis, such as reductio of the sperm count, and exfoliation of germ cells in lumens. Besides, intercellular spaces enlarged and epithelial cells exfoliated, along with appearance of cytoplasmic and intercellular vacuoles in epithelial cells of epididymis.

The BTB structure consists of TJ, GJ, AJ, bridging granule and actin filament between Sertoli cells. Morphologically, it is a typical "sandwich" structure consisting of Basal Es, TJ, endoplasmic reticulum membrane and actin filament bundle. This structure is one of the important morphological indicators for the observation and evaluation of BTB at subcellular level [54]. As shown in Fig. 1 c, the transmission electron microscopy showed that in the control group, there was a typical "sandwich" structure of BTB between the Sertoli cells, showing a high density of dense lines. Compared with the control group, the ultrastructure of BTB in the 7. 5 mg/kg group did not change significantly, and only some of the dense BTB structures began to be blurred and disordered (black triangle). In the 15 mg/kg group and 30 mg/kg group, abnormal BTB structures could be seen, with breaks in the BTB structure (black arrows), widening of tight junction gaps (white arrows), appearance of vesicles (*), absence of actin bundles (white triangles), and different degrees of expansion of the endoplasmic reticulum (pentagons). These results indicate that Ni chloride can disrupt the ultrastructure supporting intercellular BTB, suggesting that BTB function may be impaired.

As shown in Fig. 1 d, to further explore whether NiCl2 can disrupt BTB function, we used a biotin assay. In control testis, we observed biotin signals only on the lateral basement membrane of the germinal tubules, but not in the lumen of the germinal tubules. However, the biotin signal penetrated the germinal tubules in the NiCl2 group, and the green fluorescence intensity of the biotin signal penetration increased with time and dose.

The BTB is mainly composed of several coexisting junction types [55]. This study measured the changes in the expression of the BTB junction-related proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, N-cadherin, Connexin-43 and F-actin) in mouse testis tissue induced by NiCl2 treatment. NiCl2-induced changes in BTB proteins were confirmed by IF staining as shown in Fig. 2 a, b and c, the results showed that N-cadherin, Occludin and Connexin-43 were present in the testis and expressed in the spermatogenic epithelium near the basal compartment in a linear distribution. The three proteins were also expressed at all levels of germ cells, between germ cells and Sertoli cells, as well as between testicular interstitial cells. NiCl2 treatment significantly reduced the expression of these signals, but the location of these proteins was not changed. As shown in Fig. 2 d-k, the protein levels of ZO-1, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, N-cadherin, Connexin-43 and F-actin were significantly reduced in the three NiCl2-treated groups on days 14 and 28 of the experiment when compared to the control group (P < 0. 05). Meanwhile, the mRNA expression levels of BTB-associated proteins expression in the testis were also decreased (Supplemental Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 3 a, the control group had an irregular shape, with many slender protrusions around the cells. However, when compared with the control group, NiCl2 treatment caused some damage to the cells. For example, the quantity of TM4 cells sharply dropped, the gap between cells widened and the number of cells with rounded morphology increased. The role of ROS in NiCl2-induced BTB damage in the testis and TM4 cells

In mice testis, NiCl2 treatment can cause oxidative stress, however, the role of ROS in NiCl2-induced BTB damage is still unclear. For investigate whether there is an association between the ROS and BTB damage, the oxidative stress inhibitor was used in mice testis and TM4 cells experiment.

Fig. 4 a, b, c, and d demonstrates that T-AOC activity, SOD activity, and GSH content were inhibited dramatically in the NiCl2-treated group when compared to the control group (P < 0. 05). Additionally, the MDA concentration was considerably higher (P < 0. 05) in the NiCl2-treated group. Discussion

Ni (Ni), a widespread environmental pollution, which toxicology is becoming more common. Male infertility is a rising problem in global health, with 50% of infertility cases due to male factors infertility [57]. In addition, Ni has been demonstrated to be poisonous to the testicles [23]. Our previous studies have shown that Ni can accumulate in the testis, result in apoptosis, sperm malformation, spermatogenic disorders, cause reproductive damage in the mouse testis. Notably, BTB is an essential component of the reproductive system, it creates a stable internal environment for spermatogenesis [58]. The destruction of BTB can induce spermatogenesis disorders or infertility. However, it is still unclear exactly how NiCl2 toxicity and BTB are related. This work sought to elucidate the probable processes underlying the action of NiCl2 on the BTB from in vivo to in vitro.

The BTB is mainly composed of TJ, GJ and AJ [59]. TJ is the largest form of junction in BTB and is formed by a variety of tight junction proteins (Claudin-11, JAM family member F11R, ocludin, etc.) connected to the actin cytoskeleton via ZO-1 [60]. Among them, claudin-11 is essential for spermatogenesis, and knockdown of claudin-11 has been shown to cause sterility [61,62]. Whereas F11R knockdown is fertile but has no testicular phenotype [63], Occludin knockout degenerates and reduces fertility [64]. GJ is a plasma membrane channel on adjacent cell membranes, connexin-43 is the predominant gap junction protein, and in the presence of damaged spermatozoa, Connexin-43 expression is altered, affecting BTB kinetics [65]. AJ is a testis-specific type of attachment, and ectoplasmic specialization (ES) is a form of attachment in AJ that confers the ability for sperm cells to adhere to sertoli cells and transit through the germinal epithelium, the most characteristic ultrastructural feature of BTB, including calmodulin (N-cadherin) [66]. F-actin is a structural component of actin in the cytoskeleton of sertoli cells. F-actin could connect with ZO-1 to ensure the reticular integrity of the TJ and influence ES, sperm adhesion processes [67]. Defects in the cell junction proteins that establish BTB may lead to the impairment of BTB function, which may initiate an immune response against germ cells, ultimately causing male infertility [54].

The results of this experimental study showed that NiCl2 treatment induced abnormal histopathological variation of testis and epididymis. At the same time, NiCl2 can also the structure and integrity of BTB. Also, ZO-1, N-cadherin, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, Connexin-43 and F-actin protein expression was significantly reduced in mouse testes by gavage of NiCl2 for 14 and 28 days. Moreover, in vitro study, NiCl2 treatment decreased cell activity, caused a significant decrease in the levels of ZO-1, N-cadherin, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, Connexin-43 and F-actin proteins, and the extent of the decrease was proportional to the NiCl2 treatment concentration. It suggests that exposure to NiCl2 can impair the integrity of BTB by causing harm to TJ, GJ, and AJ both in vivo and in vitro. Similarly, some environmental pollutants (Cd, As, Pb, Fluoride, SO2) have been shown to induce disruption of BTB connections [38,41,[68], [69], [70]].

Then, the putative mechanism of BTB degradation caused by NiCl2 was investigated. Oxidative stress is caused by increased ROS production or decreased antioxidant capacity in the body. Sustained oxidative stress can trigger apoptosis or necrosis. In accordance with our prior research, NiCl2 gavage for 14 and 28 days resulted in a significant increase in MDA content and a significant decrease in T-AOC and GSH, as well as an increase in ROS levels in the testis. Also, NiCl2 treatment can lead to prove that oxidative stress occurred in the testis [35]. Therefore, we used the antioxidant N-Acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC) co-treatment with NiCl2 to analysis the role of ROS in Ni-mediated BTB damage in testis and TM4 cells. The outcomes demonstrated that NAC co-treatment dramatically reversed the testis' histological abnormalities and halted the loss of BTB integrity and proteins associated to BTB including ZO-1, N-cadherin, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, Connexin-43 and F-actin in the testis of mice. In vitro, NiCl2 treatment increased ROS content in TM4 cells, while NAC co-treatment enhanced cell activity, reduced ROS content, and significantly increased protein levels of ZO-1, N-cadherin, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, Connexin-43 and F-actin. These results demonstrate that oxidative stress are crucial in NiCl2-disrupted BTB junction integrity in testis and TM4 cells. Ye et al. [71] have reported that obese mice exhibit significant oxidative stress that disrupts BTB integrity, whereas the antioxidant metformin has restored obesity-induced BTB structural and permeability damage as well as disordered BTB-associated proteins by ameliorating oxidative stress. Wu et al. [72] have found that bisphenol AF (BPAF) decreases sperm quality, possibly due to enhanced oxidative stress to disrupt BTB function, which induces testicular damage. The above findings suggest that oxidative stress is involved in the process of Ni toxicity to the BTB. And substances with similar antioxidant effects as NAC may be able to treat the BTB damaged through oxidative stress to some extent.

It has been found that the signaling during spermatogenesis and BTB reconstruction is intimately correlated with p38-MAPK signaling pathway [56]. Meanwhile, it has been demonstrated that the large amount of ROS produced by oxidative stress can activate p38-MAPK signaling pathway which induce apoptosis, impaired spermatogenesis, and impaired BTB integrity in the testis [[73], [74], [75]]. Therefore, this experiment explored the relationship between ROS-p38 MAPK and NiCl2-induced BTB junction disruption. In this study, both in vivo and in vitro experiment findings indicated that NiCl2 treatment greatly increased p-p38 MAPK expression. Otherwise, our results also indicated that ROS participated in NiCl2-activated p38 MAPK pathway. Han et al. [76] findings are consistent with our findings in that NiSO4 can cause testosterone synthesis disorder in rat Leydig cells through ROS and p38 MAPK, while Gan et al. [77] have demonstrated that selenium nanoparticles can inhibit the p38MAPK pathway in rats testis by alleviating oxidative stress, which could alleviate Ni-induced testosterone synthesis disorder. Moreover, we found that co-treatment with the p38 MAPK signaling pathway inhibitor SB203580 in TM4 cells reversed NiCl2's negative impact on cell viability. Also, the levels of p-p38 protein expression were reduced and the levels ZO-1, N-cadherin, Occludin, F11R, Claudin-11, Connexin-43 and F-actin proteins were markedly elevated. These data confirm that the p38 MAPK pathway involve in the BTB injury induced by NiCl2. Similarly, Lu et al. [78] have identified that after PFOA exposure, testes and SCs showed elevated p-p38/p38 MAPK ratios. Zhou et al. [40] have found that in Cd-exposed support cells, occludin levels and cell barrier function have been recovered when the p38 pathway was inhibited by SB203580 pretreatment. Also, betaine protects from diabetes-induced BTB malfunction via controlling the oxidative stress-mediated p38 MAPK pathway [52].

Our results showed that NiCl2 treatment induced BTB injury by down-regulation of BTB-related protein expression in mouse testis as well as in Sertoli cells TM4. The p38 MAPK signaling pathway, which is activated by oxidative stress, was also crucial to the process. This study will assist in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the adverse NiCl2-induced effects in male reproductive function. Future studies could be directed toward inhibiting oxidative stress and the p38 MAPK pathway. Exploring the potential of drugs with efficacy similar to NAC and SB203580 to provide new insight into the direction of male reproductive toxicology therapy for Ni toxicity.
