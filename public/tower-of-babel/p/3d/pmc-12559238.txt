Job Stress and Ethical Decision-Making Among Healthcare Workers in Post-Pandemic Infectious Disease Control
Authors: Feng-Jen Tsai, Fauzi Budi Satria, Ting-Fu Chang, Yu-Chun Chen, Cheng-Ta Yang, Chenyi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the post-pandemic era, healthcare workers face moral dilemmas balancing individual rights with public health concerns. Taiwan’s COVID-19 measures, while effective, have raised privacy concerns, highlighting these challenges. An observational survey of 169 healthcare workers evaluated moral judgment across Non-Moral, Impersonal Moral, and Personal Moral Dilemmas, considering sociodemographic factors, employment details, pandemic experience, empathy, and job stress. The study found job stress significantly impacts moral decision-making. For Impersonal Moral Dilemmas, higher job stress—often resulting from lack of sleep or lower job security—pushes professionals towards utilitarian decisions, possibly due to diminished empathy, indicating a preference for societal benefits over individual welfare. Conversely, in Personal Moral Dilemmas involving personal connections, heightened emotional conflicts arise. A reversed pattern was observed in how job stress affects utilitarian choices based on the dilemma’s nature: higher job stress leads to utilitarian choices in impersonal dilemmas, while lower job stress prompts utilitarian sacrifices of personally connected individuals in personal dilemmas. These results underscore the complex relationship between healthcare professionals’ psychological well-being, empathy, and work-related stress, highlighting the ethical navigation challenges in healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22055-6.Keywords: Moral dilemmas, Healthcare, Job stress, Utilitarian ethical choicesSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has presented a moral quandary in global resource allocation. Balancing community benefit maximization with individual rights is a pressing challenge in the post-pandemic era. Taiwan, a society known for collectivism, has received international commendation for its effective quarantine measures in response to the COVID-19 outbreak, although these measures have also raised privacy concerns1–3. A significant impact of culture on the spread of COVID-19 has been found, with cultural collectivism and moral decision-making also identified as predictors of the response to COVID-194,5.

While frontline healthcare workers face moral dilemmas in making decisions about infectious disease control, they inevitably encounter ‘mortality salience’—the awareness of one’s own mortality—which significantly impacts neuropsychological processing due to the life-threatening nature of such diseases. This heightened awareness, triggered by exposure to severe illness or the risk of death, can meaningfully influence both behavior and moral judgment6. They frequently confront moral dilemmas, such as deciding whether to prioritize individual rights or the broader public health and social welfare when addressing health issues7. Adapting the traditional moral dilemma paradigm, this study highlights the debate between two distinct moral philosophies: utilitarianism and deontology. Utilitarianism prioritizes outcomes that maximize overall well-being, even at the expense of individual rights, whereas deontology emphasizes adherence to moral rules or duties, holding that harming others is never acceptable—even if it could lead to greater overall benefits. This debate, associated with varying degrees of cognitive and emotional modulation within brain networks, influences an individual’s moral decision-making8–10. For example, in a “Delegation of Human Resources” dilemma, optimal outcomes may require infringing upon the rights of a few (healthcare workers); for instance, by redeploying your team to support staff at specialized hospitals, thus ensuring adequate care for a greater number of patients with infectious disease X. Utilitarianism deems such sacrificial actions permissible or even obligatory, while deontology opposes them on principle, regardless of the reasons11,12. This is because utilitarianism is society-oriented, justifying actions by their outcomes, while deontology prioritizes individual rights, asserting they must never be violated13. The moral decision-making dilemma becomes more acute when those affected are personally connected to the decision-maker. This is particularly evident in scenarios like the “Decision to Lock Down the Hospital,” where one must weigh the potential benefits to the general population against increased risks to individuals within the hospital, including loved ones, such as the decision-maker’s spouse. Typically, a utilitarian decision in a personal moral dilemma is tempered by heightened emotional bias, which affects the cognitive evaluation of the outcome’s greater benefit. However, the concept of mortality salience, inherent in personal healthcare moral dilemmas, has been found to increase self-enhancement—the psychological tendency to preserve or elevate one’s self-worth, particularly when facing existential threats. This is particularly evident when individuals take credit for positive events or outcomes that align with the views of authoritative worldviews14. Sacrificing personal interests to improve societal welfare aligns with the principles of ethical training for healthcare workers. This selflessness may also intersect with the varying influences of mortality salience, which can depend on occupational roles and cultural standards6,15. Meanwhile, it is essential for healthcare workers (HCWs) to possess empathy alongside knowledge and skills16–19. Research indicates that higher levels of empathy among HCWs lead to improved their performance and help patients feel safer to express their concerns20. Despite the well-acknowledged importance of empathy, HCWs continue to face challenges in effectively incorporating it into their daily interactions. Moreover, recent investigations also unveiled a diminishing trend in both Empathy and Morality among HCWs16,19 that may impair their Moral Judgment. This “diminishing trend in morality” does not reflect inherent ethical failure but is attributed to systemic stressors—such as long hours, understaffing, and institutional pressures—that undermine empathy and ethical decision-making in clinical practice21,22 .

In practice, both, utilitarian and deontological principles are used in medical decision-making. However, choosing between these two approaches is difficult as deciding whether to prioritize individual rights or public health and social welfare in addressing health issues is still debatable7. As the forefront of combating infectious diseases, individual and societal values play a pivotal role in guiding HCWs’ actions and choices7. In infectious disease management, the intricate interplay between empathy and moral reasoning puts the HCWs in ethical dilemmas. In addition, their responsibilities also make them exposed to high levels of stress, stemming from demanding workloads, exposure to high-risk environments, and the ethical weight of their decisions. According to Terror Management Theory23, the awareness of one’s mortality (mortality salience) activates death anxiety, which can provoke affect-laden, hot-system responses. These responses often manifest as defensive behaviors aligned with culturally valued worldviews24,25. In the context of healthcare, such mechanisms may drive HCWs under high job stress to favor deontological choices in personal moral dilemmas — choices that reinforce professional identity and ethical norms centered on patient care and individual rights. On the other hand, while mortality salience can trigger affect-laden, defensive reactions, the concept of death reflection introduces a more deliberative, ‘cool-system’ pathway26. This reflective stance toward mortality has been shown to foster generativity and prosocial risk-taking. In healthcare settings, such reflective processes may help explain why some HCWs, particularly those experiencing lower levels of burnout, are more inclined to make utilitarian decisions that favor the broader social good over personal or relational attachments.

Numerous studies conducted over the past few decades27 have aimed to assess the levels of Empathy, Job Stress, and Moral Judgment among HCWs. Surprisingly, despite this extensive body of research, there appears to be a notable gap in our understanding, as no study, to the best of our knowledge, has comprehensively evaluated these factors among HCWs, particularly when considering the unique context of infectious disease control.

Especially with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, it has brought into focus the substantial challenges to HCWs. Several studies28,29 conducted during the pandemic have revealed a significant increase in stress levels among HCWs. This rise in stress can be attributed to a multitude of factors29, including a surge in their workload, especially during the initial stages of the pandemic when resources were scarce. This dire situation also heightened their own risk of contracting the virus, thereby placing not only their lives but also those of their families and loved ones in jeopardy. Moreover, the workplace environment for HCWs witnessed a disturbing escalation in incidents of violence, encompassing verbal abuse, threats, harassment, and even ostracization. Physicians, in particular, grappled with sleep deprivation and an overwhelming increase in their workload, both of which directly contributed to a pervasive sense of burnout29.

By bridging these research strands, this study aims to examine how empathy and job stress influence the moral decision-making processes of healthcare workers (HCWs) in Taiwan, particularly in the context of infectious disease control. We enrolled physicians and nurses involved in frontline patient care and hypothesize that lower levels of empathy and higher levels of job stress are associated with a greater likelihood of making utilitarian decisions. Drawing on Terror Management Theory (TMT) and dual-process moral psychology, we articulate the following predictions:

H1 – HCWs with lower levels of empathy, measured through pain and unpleasantness ratings, will be more likely to make utilitarian choices in non-moral dilemmas (NMD). This is based on the dual-process theory, which posits that in low-emotion scenarios, moral decisions are primarily driven by cognitive, rather than affective, processing. Previous studies have shown that individuals with reduced empathic concern tend to favor utilitarian outcomes30.

H2 – Greater job stress, particularly indicated by shorter sleep duration and lower perceived job security, will be associated with more utilitarian responses in impersonal moral dilemmas (IMD). Stress may impair emotional regulation and empathy, thus prompting more outcome-focused judgments in scenarios that are emotionally neutral.

H3 – Lower levels of client-related burnout will correspond with a higher likelihood of utilitarian decisions in personal moral dilemmas (PMD). While TMT suggests that mortality cues under stress provoke defensive, deontological responses, those experiencing less burnout may be more capable of engaging in reflective, deliberate reasoning—what McAdams and de St. Aubin describe as “death reflection”26—which can promote generativity and prosocial risk-taking, even in emotionally charged situations.

Understanding how empathy and job stress shape moral choices in healthcare settings is critical for informing institutional policy and training. While this study does not take a normative stance on whether utilitarianism is the ethically superior approach, we assume that the capacity to make impartial, outcome-focused decisions can be especially important in public health crises where resources are limited and trade-offs are inevitable. Identifying factors that influence such decision-making can help institutions better support HCWs’ ethical reasoning and psychological resilience under pressure.

Methods

Study design and participant involvement

This research was carried out from April 2020 to June 2021. Generally, during this period, the world suffered from the COVID-19 pandemic. However, compared to most countries, Taiwan was relatively safer1,31. This study involves 169 HCWs, including nurses and doctors from two hospitals in Taipei. Each of them was provided with various types of questionnaires to assess their Job Stress, Empathy, and Moral Judgement regarding infectious disease control. During the questionnaire design phase, a participant joined the development committee. Participants were informed that if they wished to learn about the study’s results and interpretation, they would receive this information via email after the study’s publication. Additionally, a summary of the results, designed for a non-specialist audience, would be made available in a study newsletter upon request. The sample size was estimated using G*Power prior to the data collection. To detect a small to medium effect size in the linear multiple regression model with 95% power (f2 = 0.1, with alpha = 0.05, number of predictors = 6), a sample size of 132 participants was required. All the questionnaires that were used in this study has passed the reliability and validity tests (Cronbach’s α >0.8). All participants provided written informed consent approved by the Taipei Medical University – Joint Institutional Review Board (N202005035) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Independent variables

Independent variables in this study are HCWs’ characteristics, Empathy, and Job Stress level. In the context of HCWs’ characteristics, we analyze their Demographic background, Employment Status, Pandemic-related Experience, and last month Mental Health Status. We identified HCWs’ Demographic background by asking them about their Sex, Age, Educational Level, Marital Status, and Child Status. For Employment Status, we collected information about respondents’ Income, Occupation, Position, Service Unit, Department of Service, and Length of Work. Then, regarding Pandemic-related Experience, we asked respondents whether they had received any training on pandemics or had personally handled a pandemic. Meanwhile, to assess HCWs’ last month Mental Health status, respondents were asked about their mental feelings and their average sleeping hours per day for the recent one month.

Next, to measure respondents’ Job Stress, we used the Job Content Questionnaire (JCQ) and Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) questionnaire32. The JCQ is a globally-used and scientifically proven instrument to measure employee work organization and psychosocial job content32. In this study, we used the validated Chinese version of JCQ (C-JCQ) consisting of 31 items for 8 indicators; Job Control (9 items), Psychological Workload (7 items), Physiological Workload (1 item), Job Security (1 item), Workplace Justice (6 items), Labor Participation (2 items), and Social Support from Supervisor Support (4 items) and Peers (1 item)33. The Cronbach’s α of C-JCQ questionnaire is 0.805 for our study. Participants responded on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Higher agreement with psychological demand items indicates higher job stress, while higher agreement with decision latitude and supervisor support items reflects greater perceived control and social support, respectively.

Each indicator is assessed by the sum of the scores for each item measured using a Likert scale where a score of 1 indicates strong disagreement and a score of 4 indicates strong agreement. Then, the 169 respondents’ average score of each C-JCQ indicator is used as the cutoff point. Respondents who scored below the average were labeled “Less Agree” and those who scored above were labeled “More Agree”.

Furthermore, to measure HCWs’ Job Stress level, we also use CBI questionnaire. The CBI is an instrument to measure burnout in different dimensions34. This study used the validated Chinese version of CBI (C-CBI) consisting of 19 questions for 3 different dimensions; Personal (6 questions), Work-related (6 questions), and Client-related burnout (7 questions)35. Apart from personal burnout, client-related burnout specifically refers to emotional exhaustion resulting from interactions with patients, capturing the interpersonal strain inherent in caregiving roles. In contrast, work-related burnout pertains to stress associated with general occupational demands and organizational pressures, such as workload, staffing, and administrative responsibilities. And the Cronbach’s α of C-CBI questionnaire is 0.948 for our study.

The questions are all scored on a 5-point scale in which a value of 5 indicates a very exhaustive experience. Each dimension of burnout status is measured by the sum of the scores for each question. Then, the average scores of 169 respondents for each dimension are used as the cutoff point for the respective dimension. Those whose scores are above the cutoff point are labeled “Often” while those whose scores are below are labeled “Rare”.

To assess empathy for pain among HCWs, we used two main empathy scales: the Pain Intensity Rating and the Unpleasantness Rating17,18. Participants viewed a series of images depicting individuals in physically painful scenarios and rated each image on a 10-point scale, with higher scores indicating stronger empathic responses. Each scale included 10 items, and responses ranged from 1 (lowest) to 10 (highest). Scores from the 10 items in each scale were summed to produce total scores for Pain Intensity and Unpleasantness. The average score for each scale was used as the cut-off point: participants scoring below the average were categorized as “Less Pain” or “Less Unpleasantness,” while those scoring above—indicating higher empathic responses—were categorized as “More Pain” or “More Unpleasantness.”

Dependent variables: moral decision

In this study, HCWs’ Moral Judgment regarding infectious disease control is measured by validated questionnaire that have been passed through expert review process. There are three indicators being measured in the Moral Judgement questionnaire; Non-Moral Dilemma (NMD), Impersonal Moral Dilemma (IMD), and Personal Moral Dilemma (PMD) (Supplementary materials). NMDs involve everyday preference scenarios without moral implications (e.g., choosing between two travel routes); IMDs present moral situations that require utilitarian decisions but entail less direct physical or emotional involvement (e.g., sacrificing one stranger to save several others); and PMDs depict high-conflict moral scenarios in which participants must evaluate actions involving direct personal harm or intense emotional conflict (e.g., sacrificing one’s own child to save many others). Each indicator consists of 8 questions presented in white SurveyCake style, pt 18, on a white screen. Each dilemma item in the MJ questionnaire was followed by a binary choice. Participants were asked to respond “yes” or “no” to the proposed action. A “yes” response indicates a utilitarian judgment (endorsing the harmful action to maximize overall outcomes), while a “no” response reflects a deontological stance (rejecting harmful actions regardless of the consequences). Because there are 24 questions for three indicators (NMD, IMD, and PMD), the score range for each indicator is 0 to 8. The average score of 169 respondents for each indicator is used as the cut-off point for classification. Respondents with scores higher than the cut-off point are classified as Utilitarian, while those with scores lower than the cut-off point are classified as Deontological.

We used expert review approach to obtain the content validity of all the variables and measure except standardized questionnaire as C-JCQ and C-CBI. Six related experts were invited for the review. The review experts included two medical doctors and medical ethicists, three neuro-psychologist, and an infectious disease epidemiologist. And the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) of each item was above 0.78 and used in the study. The Cronbach’s α of Empathy questionnaire is 0.929 in our study. And the Cronbach’s α of Moral questionnaire is 0.712 in our study.

Results

Table 1 contains information about the characteristics of 169 respondents who participated in this study. The majority of respondents (87% are female), under 30 years old (59.2%), have a college degree (95.9%), are unmarried (60.9%), and do not have children (74.6%). While the majority of respondents are nurses (91.1%), the majority work on the ward (81.7%) and in the emergency department (60.4%), have worked for at least 5 years (50.3%), and earn between $40,000 and $100,000 NTD per month (82.8%). Furthermore, those with pandemic-related training (91.7%) and only once dealing with a pandemic (48.5%) made up the majority of those with pandemic-related experience. Meanwhile, according to the self-mental assessment, slightly more respondents (51.5%) have had good mental feelings in the last month and slept at least 7 h (56.8%).

Table 2 then describes the results of the job stress and empathy assessment of HCWs. According to the respondents’ answers to the JCQ questionnaire, slightly more HCWs (52.1%) disagree that they have job control and that there is justice in their workplace (54.4%). The majority of respondents agree that their workload is physiologically (92.9%) and psychologically (62.1%) exhausting accompanied by a sense of job insecurity (66.3%). Furthermore, the majority of them agreed that there is labor union participation (59.2%), supervisor support (70.4%), and peer support (85.8%). Moreover, based on their responses to CBI questions, the majority of them rarely experience personal (51.5%), work-related (51.5%), or overwork (64.5%) burnout. However, the majority of them (57.4%) experienced client-related burnout while working. Then, when shown pictures to assess their empathy, the majority of respondents (65.7%) and (74%), respectively, were classified into less painful and less uncomfortable groups.

Table 3 describe the respondents answers to Moral Judgment questionnaires. According to Table 3, the majority of respondents answered “Yes” to almost all the questions either in NMD, IMD, or PMD questionnaire. The majority of respondents only gave more “No” answers to NMD questions about “Investment Offers,” IMD questions about “Suspect Cases,” and PMD questions about “Vaccine Trials”, “Vitamins”, and “Home Quarantine”. Based on their preferences, the majority of respondents in Impersonal Moral Dilemma (IMD) (57.4%) and Personal Moral Dilemma (PMD) (65.1%) questions are classified as Utilitarian. Meanwhile, the majority of respondents (52.7%) are classified as Deontological in Non-Moral Dilemma (NMD) questions.

Next, we conducted Chi-Square test to identify the factors associated with HCWs’ Moral Judgment (Table 4). In the results, we found there is a significant relationship between demographic factors (Education), employment status (Occupation), pandemic-related experience (Training), and Empathy status with their preferences in answering NMD questions (p<0.05). Furthermore, respondents’ mental health status (Sleep hour) and their job stress assessment (Job Security) were associated factors in their preference in answering IMD questions (p<0.05). In addition, our study also found that Job Stress (Workplace Justice, Support from supervisors and peers, Client-related burnout) was a factor associated with respondents’ preference in answering PMD questions in Moral Judgment questionnaire (p<0.05).

We then used multivariate logistic regression to see how these associated factors relate to HCWs’ preference for Moral Judgment (Table 5). In the context of NMD questions, Utilitarian decision is more likely to be those with experience in pandemic training and who are perceived to be less uncomfortable in empathy assessment (OR=4.02; p<0.05). The likelihood of having Utilitarian decision in IMD questions increases if HCWs have fewer sleep hours (OR=2.24; p<0.05) and believe their job is insecure (OR=2.73; p<0.05). In the meantime, HCWs who rarely experience client-related burnout are more likely to be classified as Utilitarian in PMD questions (OR=2.32; p<0.05).

Discussion

This study found that there is a relationship between Empathy, Job Stress and Moral Judgment regarding infectious disease control among HCWs in Taiwan. We found that Empathy was only associated with NMD while Job Stress was associated with IMD and PMD. The significant relationship between Empathy and NMD decision is consistent with previous research that indicate Utilitarian tend to be Less Empathetic30. The literature36,37 explains that although empathy helps a person manage the nuances of morality by taking another’s perspective in knowing and determining the right thing to do in a given situation, empathy cannot always describe a person’s moral behavior and may even interfere with moral judgments by introducing partiality when involving relatives and members of certain groups. This may also explain the absence of a significant relationship between Empathy and Moral Dilemma decisions (IMD and PMD) in our study. Although empathy did not significantly predict moral decisions in impersonal or personal dilemmas, this may reflect professional adaptation. Healthcare workers are often trained to regulate emotional responses to maintain objectivity, especially in ethically complex settings. Suppressing empathic arousal in favor of procedural reasoning may be necessary in clinical decision-making17,18,21,22. Still, empathy may function as a contextual mechanism shaped by job stress. High stress can dampen empathic sensitivity21,22, potentially shifting moral reasoning toward more detached approaches. In this sense, empathy may not act as a direct predictor, but as a conduit influenced by stress, particularly in emotionally salient dilemmas (PMD) versus neutral ones (IMD). Importantly, in our study, job stress and empathy reflect distinct constructs: job stress (via C-JCQ) involves structural and cognitive strain, while empathy (via pain/unpleasantness ratings) captures affective resonance. This distinction may explain their differing roles in moral decision-making across dilemma types.

In the context of NMD, the decision to choose is not based on reasons of morality, but purely on the basis of preference, desire, or satisfaction38. In this study, the majority of respondents answered “Yes” to 7 out of 8 questions. Only 1 question had a majority of respondents answering “No”, which was about “Investment offer (A7)”. This question asked whether respondents would still invest if they knew that the investment was not promising enough to make money. Referring to the content of question A7, although the majority of respondents answered “No”, the actual direction of the majority of respondents’ answers is coherent and consistent with the other 7 questions that indicate a preference for taking an Utilitarian decision.

Previous studies mentioned that Empathy is one of the factors that are also important in determining Moral Dilemmas decisions (IMD and PMD)30. It is noteworthy that our findings indicate a significant association between moral decision-making and Job Stress, rather than with Empathy. This significant correlation aligns with prior research39,40, which suggests that a Utilitarian decision is linked to Job Stress. More interestingly, the direction in which Job Stress modulates moral decision-making depends on the level of emotional bias inherent in different types of moral dilemmas (Fig. 1). In the context of Impersonal Moral Dilemma (IMD), those classified as Utilitarian tend to be those who has Higher Job Stress (Sleep <=7 h or/and who feel Less Job Security). Consistent findings suggest that increased job stress could diminish clinical empathy41. Consequently, the observed positive correlation between utilitarian decisions and job stress within the context of Impersonal Moral Dilemma (IMD) could be due to decreased empathy when making decisions that involve sacrificing a few less personally significant individuals for the greater societal good. In contrast, when faced with Personal Moral Dilemmas (PMDs) that require the sacrifice of individuals with whom they have a personal connection, healthcare professionals often experience intensified emotional conflicts due to heightened mortality salience processing6. Such decision-making processes are likely to be influenced by the self-enhancement values intrinsic to healthcare professionals, which align with their ethical training and the ethos of their profession, as well as by the prevailing authoritative worldviews and cultural norms6,15.

Job stress modulates utilitarian moral decisions in medical settings, contingent upon the degree of emotional bias inherent in impersonal versus personal moral dilemmas. Elevated job stress, characterized by diminished job security, was correlated with an increased propensity for utilitarian decisions in impersonal moral dilemmas. Conversely, reduced job stress, marked by lesser client-related burnout, was linked to a higher inclination toward utilitarian choices in personal moral dilemmas.

The interplay of these personal and contextual factors exerts a composite influence on healthcare professionals as they navigate these challenging PMD decisions, resulting in a notable majority opting for utilitarian solutions (65.1% as opposed to 34.9%). This tendency towards utilitarianism can be influenced by job stress and is more pronounced among those with lower levels of client-related burnout. Interestingly, lower burnout from client interactions may transform into motivation, prompting a willingness to sacrifice personally significant individuals for the greater good of aiding more clients.

Our findings — particularly the decreased utilitarian decision-making under stress in personal dilemmas — may be interpreted through the lens of Terror Management Theory. Job-related stress in HCWs, coupled with constant exposure to mortality, likely heightens death anxiety, triggering protective adherence to deontological norms. This aligns with previous research24,25, suggesting that in emotionally salient scenarios, such as personal moral dilemmas, HCWs might prioritize ethical principles that affirm their professional role and societal expectations. However, not all responses to mortality cues are defensive. The concept of death reflection offers a complementary perspective. Unlike the reactive, ‘hot-system’ response described by TMT, death reflection involves a deliberative, ‘cool-system’ process that fosters generativity and prosocial risk-taking26. This framework may help explain our finding that HCWs with lower levels of client-related burnout were more likely to make utilitarian choices in personal dilemmas. With reduced emotional exhaustion, these individuals may be better equipped to reflect on the broader implications of their actions, leading to moral decisions that prioritize collective welfare over personal ties.

Based on Moral Psychology Theory, moral decisions are the product of either one of two different mental processes; automatic-emotional process and conscious-controlled process. Automatic-emotional process is faster than Conscious-controlled process in terms of making moral decision. Conscious-controlled process take longer time in making decision since it use more cognitive function than emotional function42,43. Literature explain that Utilitarian decision is generated from Conscious-controlled process while Deontological decision is mostly made from Automatic-emotional process. It may explain why people with lower stress level prefer a Utilitarian decision. People with lower stress level tend to have more time to think before making moral decision meanwhile people with high stress level tend to make decision emotionally42,43.

In Moral Dilemma scenarios (IMD and PMD), there is no universally accepted right or wrong answer. The main different between IMD and PMD questions is whether or not the respondent’s family/closest person is involved in the scenario. In the PMD questionnaire, the family/closest person of the respondent was involved in the scenario whereas in the IMD questionnaire they were not. The involvement of family/closest person in PMD scenario let the respondent with high Job Stress level to take Deontological decision. Corroborating previous findings, HCWs in Taiwan are generally unwilling to cause harm when their own relatives are involved (PMD), but are more willing to accept minor sacrifices for greater benefits when no close personal connections are at stake (IMD)43. We believe this pattern may be influenced by bioethics education and the professional pledge taken prior to entering the field44.

This study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of the findings may be constrained by the sample composition, which was predominantly female (87%) and composed mostly of nurses (92%) from two hospitals in Taipei. Given the focus on healthcare workers in the capital city, the results may not fully capture the moral decision-making patterns of HCWs in suburban or rural areas, where cultural norms, professional roles, and work environments may differ. Moreover, gender may also shape moral reasoning. As Gilligan notes, women tend toward a “care ethics” approach, while men favor “justice” reasoning, encompassing both utilitarian and deontological views. This limits generalizability to more gender-diverse samples45. Second, all data were self-reported and collected in a single session, introducing the potential for common-method bias and inflated associations due to shared method variance. Future research should consider employing time-separated assessments and/or multi-method data collection to address this issue. Third, we used dichotomized predictors in our regression models to manage sample size limitations and model complexity. However, this approach may have reduced the variance captured and limited the explanatory power of some variables. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples could benefit from retaining continuous variables to uncover more nuanced relationships.

A serendipitous insight on moral dilemma responses

An unexpected pattern emerged in Table 3: unlike other IMD items that received majority “yes” responses, the “Suspected Cases” item was predominantly answered “no” (63.9%). Similarly, PMD items showed a near-even split—some (e.g., “vaccine trials,” “vitamins”) drew majority “no” responses, while others (e.g., “euthanasia,” “asking for leave”) leaned toward “yes.” Preliminary comparisons with an unpublished nursing student dataset suggest these patterns may be influenced by clinical experience. Vocational nurses and students responded differently to these items, indicating that professional exposure may shape moral judgments. These findings suggest that standard moral dilemma categories (IMD, PMD, NMD) may overlook important contextual and occupational factors. Future studies using factor analysis and comparative designs could help refine moral judgment models for healthcare settings.

Conclusion

There is a meaningful relationship between empathy, job stress, and moral judgment in the context of infectious disease control among healthcare workers (HCWs) in Taiwan. While empathy was primarily associated with responses to Non-Moral Dilemma (NMD) scenarios, job stress influenced decision-making in both Impersonal Moral Dilemmas (IMDs) and Personal Moral Dilemmas (PMDs)—but in opposite directions. Higher levels of stress were linked to more utilitarian judgments in IMDs, whereas lower stress levels were associated with greater utilitarian responses in PMDs. It is essential to acknowledge the moral dilemmas faced by healthcare professionals. Their decisions likely emerge from a convergence of multiple, often implicit, influences—such as cultural norms, mortality salience, and self-enhancement motives—shaped by professional ethics and institutional expectations. These broader factors intersect with individual psychological states, particularly empathy and work-related stress, underscoring the complex and multifaceted nature of moral reasoning in clinical practice.
