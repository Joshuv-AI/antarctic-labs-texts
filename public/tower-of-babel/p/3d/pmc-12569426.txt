Prevention of Unbearable Situations and Harms Protocol: A Quality Improvement Initiative in Neurological Delirium
Authors: Mi Sun Kim, Sungyang Jo, Hyung Ja Kim, Sun Young Pyun, Seonok Kim, Sun Mae Park, Yong Seo Koo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeHyperactive delirium in neurological wards frequently results in patient harm, falls, device removal, and caregiver injuries, alongside significant staff burnout. To address inconsistent management practices, we developed the Prevention of Unbearable Situations and Harms (PUSH) protocol, a structured multidisciplinary approach to proactively manage hyperactive delirium. This study evaluated the effectiveness of the PUSH protocol in reducing delirium-related adverse events (primary outcome), and improving clinical workflow, staff burnout, and satisfaction (secondary outcomes).MethodsWe conducted a pre-post study in three phases: pre-implementation (November 2021–May 2022), pilot testing (June–August 2022), and post-implementation (September 2022–March 2023). The PUSH protocol includes Standard Care (routine delirium prevention), Preparation (anticipatory medication planning for patients at risk, Nursing Delirium Screening Scale ≥2), and Action (rapid response for escalating delirium) phases. Outcomes included delirium rates, hyperactive delirium episodes, adverse event incidence, medication response times, and staff burnout and satisfaction surveys. We compared pre- and post-implementation data using Poisson regression.ResultsAmong 2,457 patients, 174 developed delirium during 2,958 observed person-days. Unbearable situations or harms decreased from 5.6 to 4.2 events per 100 person-days, with a fully adjusted incidence rate ratio of 0.539 (95% confidence interval: 0.368–0.788, p=0.001). Response times to sedative medication administration improved markedly (16 to 0 min; p<0.001). Staff burnout significantly decreased (32 to 27; p<0.001), and satisfaction notably increased (3 to 4; p=0.009).ConclusionsThe PUSH protocol significantly reduced delirium-related harms, enhanced clinical workflow efficiency, decreased staff burnout, and increased satisfaction, supporting its broader implementation in neurological settings.

INTRODUCTION

Delirium is a common and serious acute confusional state occurring in 10%–30% of hospitalized adults, with even higher prevalence among neurological patients.123 Hyperactive delirium, characterized by agitation, restlessness, and impulsivity,4 is particularly problematic because of serious adverse events, including patient falls,5 inadvertent removal of catheters or therapeutic devices,67 and injuries to patients and caregivers.8 These incidents increase morbidity and hospital length-of-stays and elevate healthcare costs9 and staff burnout.101112

Neurological wards face unique challenges in the management of delirium because of the need to balance sedation with ongoing neurological assessment.1315 Sedative medications, often required to keep patients comfortable or safe, can suppress consciousness and mask subtle neurological changes. This is especially problematic in conditions such as stroke16 or traumatic brain injury,15 where frequent neurological examinations are critical to detect deterioration. Additionally, underlying neurological conditions can mimic or exacerbate delirium, complicating timely diagnosis.17 For example, poststroke confusion, postictal states after seizures, or encephalitic processes may present with delirium-like symptoms.1617 Even chronic disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) pose challenges: clinicians must be cautious with antipsychotic medications commonly used for delirium because these can worsen motor symptoms in PD and other movement disorders.1819 Thus, neurologists often hesitate to use sedatives or antipsychotics aggressively, fearing they might confound the clinical picture or harm neurologically vulnerable patients.1320 These complexities contribute to significant variability and caution in delirium management on neurological services.12162021

In the absence of effective strategies, delirium in neurological patients can have devastating consequences for both patients and staff.117222324 Untreated or poorly managed delirium is associated with worse clinical outcomes, including prolonged hospital stays, long-term cognitive decline, higher rates of institutionalization, and increased mortality.172324 A heavy burden is also placed on caregivers and families who struggle with fluctuating patient lucidity and behavioral disturbances. In the acute neurology ward setting, the repercussions of delirium often extend beyond the health of the patient to pose safety risks: patients in a hyperactive delirious state may accidentally remove essential medical devices, fall, or become physically aggressive.20 Such “unbearable situations” have tangible impacts on healthcare providers.23 Indeed, our experience in the neurology unit has witnessed incidents of feeding tubes and intravenous lines being pulled out and nursing staff experiencing injuries while trying to calm agitated delirious patients. These extreme results underscore the urgency of timely and effective delirium management tailored to neurological patients, which go beyond merely reducing the incidence of delirium but also to preventing dangerous escalation.16

However, there remains a critical gap in formal guidance for delirium management specific to neurological settings.16 Although comprehensive protocols exist for delirium in general medical wards, intensive care units, and for postoperative care, no equivalent guidelines are available to address the nuances of delirium in patients with primary neurological illnesses.1622 In practice, this void has led to inconsistent approaches and uncertainty among clinicians and nurses when facing delirium on neurology floors.24 The lack of standardized assessment tools validated for patients with neurological deficits further contributes to delayed recognition and intervention.20 Recognizing this unmet need, experts have begun to call for the development of delirium management guidelines tailored to neurological populations. For instance, recent consensus statements in stroke and PD care highlight the importance of specialized strategies in these contexts.1625 In line with these recommendations, our team sought to establish a more structured delirium management approach in the neurology ward. By addressing sedation practices, staff training, and early intervention protocols, our goal was to improve patient safety and clinical outcomes and ultimately fill the critical gap in delirium management in neurological care.

We developed the Prevention of Unbearable Situations and Harms (PUSH) protocol, a structured, multidisciplinary framework tailored to neurological wards to fill this gap. PUSH emphasizes proactive delirium recognition through standardized assessment (Nursing Delirium Screening Scale [Nu-DESC]), anticipatory ordering of individualized “as-needed” medications that consider neurological comorbidities, and prompt coordinated responses involving nursing staff and security resources. We thought systematic preparation and structured intervention would reduce adverse incidents, mitigate delirium management unpredictability, and alleviate caregiver burnout.

Our study objectives were: 1) to develop and implement the PUSH protocol specifically tailored to neurological wards; 2) to evaluate its effectiveness in reducing delirium-related adverse events (primary outcomes: unbearable situations or harms, hyperactive delirium episodes); and 3) to assess its impact on healthcare provider burnout and satisfaction (secondary outcomes).

METHODS

Patients

All patients admitted to the neurological ward during the study period were eligible for the study if they could reliably undergo assessment using the Nu-DESC.2627 Individuals younger than 18 years or in a semicomatose or comatose state were excluded because their level of consciousness precluded valid delirium screening. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB No. 2020-1675). For the pre-implementation phase, which involved retrospective review of electronic medical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived. For the post-implementation phase, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The authors report no conflicts of interest.

Study design

We adopted a pre-post study design with three sequential phases: pre-implementation (November 2021–May 2022), during which usual care served as the baseline; pilot (June–August 2022), wherein a draft version of the PUSH protocol was tested alongside standard care to identify practical challenges (e.g., delayed “as-needed” [PRN] medications, oversedation risks); and post-implementation (September 2022–March 2023), when the refined protocol was introduced. Data collected prospectively during the post-implementation period were compared with data from the pre-implementation to assess changes attributable to the PUSH protocol. Throughout all phases, nurses performed Nu-DESC assessments2627 three times per day (once per shift). The Nu-DESC is a rapid, 5-item observational scale used to screen for delirium symptoms, including disorientation, inappropriate behavior, inappropriate communication, illusions/hallucinations, and psychomotor retardation.27 Each item is scored from 0 to 2, with a total score ≥2 indicating possible delirium. The Nu-DESC is a widely used, validated tool demonstrating good sensitivity (76%–81%) and specificity (73%–90%) in general clinical populations.2627 Nurses can administer the Nu-DESC within approximately 2–3 min. Although widely validated in clinical practice, the Nu-DESC has not been specifically validated in neurological populations. Each patient with at least one completed Nu-DESC assessment contributed to the daily person-time calculation, which was used to compute incidence rates for delirium and other outcomes. Nursing logs and the incident-reporting system of the hospital were cross-referenced to ensure the accuracy and completeness of data.

This study was conducted at the Asan Medical Center, a tertiary hospital with 2,715 inpatient beds as of 2021. The neurological ward has 51 inpatient beds, including an 8-bed acute stroke unit. Staffing comprised 21 neurologists, 12 residents, and 32 ward nurses. The average hospital stay in this setting was approximately 5–6 days. Before designing the study, instructions for standard delirium care were routinely provided to physicians and nurses; however, delirium screening using Nu-DESC had been inconsistently applied in routine practice. After initiating our study, the Nu-DESC screening tool was uniformly administered to all patients throughout their hospitalization during both the pre- and post-implementation periods.

Development of the PUSH protocol

The PUSH protocol (Fig. 1) was developed collaboratively by neurologists, residents, nurses, security staff, and hospital administrators to proactively address hyperactive delirium in a neurological ward. Insights from the pilot phase led to refining its three key components—Standard Care, Preparation, and Action—in a tiered framework. The PUSH protocol involved systematic delirium screening and structured interventions. Under Standard Care, patients received routine delirium-prevention measures (e.g., orientation, adequate hydration, early catheter removal, and family engagement).

Nurses performed Nu-DESC screening every 8 h to promptly identify signs of delirium. When the Nu-DESC scores reached ≥2 (Preparation), nurses proactively collaborated with daytime physicians to prescribe individualized PRN medications tailored to each patient’s neurological status and alerted family members to potential restraint needs. These anticipatory orders were intended to facilitate prompt medication administration during nighttime, reducing uncertainty and delays caused by duty physicians unfamiliar with specific patient conditions.

In addition, sedative recommendations were guided by predefined options outlined in the PUSH protocol (Fig. 1), which stratified drug choices and dosages according to the electrocardiogram (EKG) findings and neurological comorbidities of the patient to minimize adverse effects.

If delirium escalated despite these precautions (Action), nurses coordinated with the security team for timely assistance during acute delirium-related aggression or violent behaviors and with physicians for approval of PRN medication administration. Physical restraints were used only if immediate harm was imminent. This structured approach balanced timely delirium control with the need for ongoing neurological evaluation.

Clinical variables and outcomes

The Nu-DESC scores were recorded every 8 h to identify patients with delirium (defined as a score of ≥2) during the pre-implementation and post-implementation periods. The primary outcome was the occurrence of “unbearable situations or harms,” which included removal of therapeutic devices (e.g., peripherally inserted central catheters, Foley catheters, intravenous lines), physical or verbal harm toward nurses, self-injury by the patient, refusal of nursing care such as rejecting medications or vital-sign checks, and environmental harm (e.g., soiled sheets, spilled fluids). These events were recorded at 2-h intervals and repeated at each 2-h mark if the event persisted or until the patient was discharged. Additional patient-level variables included age, sex, occupation, marital status, diagnosis, admission route, number of concurrently prescribed medications, comorbidities, presence of pain, use of analgesics, sleep deprivation, and presence of indwelling catheters. These factors were examined as potential confounders influencing delirium frequency or severity.

Sleep deprivation was defined as patients consistently experiencing difficulty sleeping throughout their hospitalization and was typically reflected in repeated nursing assessments and the need for frequent sleep medication use during the admission. Presence of pain was defined by patients who persistently reported pain symptoms throughout the hospital stay requiring ongoing analgesic treatment. Both variables were assessed by nurses based on clinical judgment and routine observations during inpatient care; no validated scales (e.g., Insomnia Severity Index or pain rating scales) were used for these assessments.

Assessment of physician and nurse workload, burnout, and satisfaction

We evaluated the impact of the PUSH protocol on medical staff through objective workload measures and using subjective surveys evaluating burnout and satisfaction. Objective workload was assessed by measuring intervals from nurses’ recognition (notification) of hyperactive delirium (Nu-DESC item #2 positive) to physician prescription and subsequent administration of sedative medications. Additionally, security team calls and arrival times were documented.

Subjective evaluations were conducted through anonymous surveys administered before and after PUSH implementation. Burnout was measured using a validated 10-item version of the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OLBI),2829 which assesses emotional exhaustion and psychological disengagement on a 5-point Likert scale (1, strongly disagree; 5, strongly agree). Satisfaction with delirium management was measured using a single-item question: “Overall, how satisfied are you with current delirium management?” answered on a 5-point Likert scale (1, very dissatisfied; 5, very satisfied). Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential.

The same clinical staff (physicians and nurses) participated in pre- and post-implementation surveys to minimize variability. This design allowed for consistent assessment of burnout and satisfaction while controlling for personnel differences potentially influencing outcomes.

Statistical analyses

Demographic and clinical variables were summarized using means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Univariate analyses were conducted to explore associations between each independent variable and the count of “unbearable situations or harms.” A Poisson regression model was employed, with person-years as the denominator, to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for adverse events. The multivariable Poisson regression models included three tiers of adjustment: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex), and Model 3 (further adjusted for additional clinical variables found to significantly differ between groups: marital status, sleep deprivation, use of sleep medication, hypertension, cardiac disease, and cerebrovascular disease). IRRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived to determine the PUSH protocol’s effectiveness in reducing delirium-related harms.

Continuous variables with paired measurements (e.g., burnout scores, satisfaction scores) were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, reflecting the paired nature of these data. Continuous variables with independent measurements (e.g., response times for sedative administration) were assessed for normality; owing to non-normal distributions and the presence of outliers, they were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Categorical variables (e.g., number of sedative medications administered per episode) were compared using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. No missing data were observed for primary outcomes, and no sedation-related complications occurred during the study.

RESULTS

Pilot testing and protocol refinements

During pilot testing, adherence to the PUSH protocol varied initially, with some physicians reluctant to follow nurse-initiated recommendations. One neurology staff member (Y.S.K.) contacted physicians to address these adherence challenges, identify misunderstandings, and refine communication. For example, based on physician feedback, standard PRN medication orders (“set-orders”) were integrated into the electronic health record system, accompanied by guidance on commonly prescribed medications and precautions. Additionally, concerns relative to antipsychotic use were addressed through explicit clarifications in the protocol, reassuring physicians that PRN medication orders represented proactive preparedness rather than obligatory administration. Neurological assessments or physician reviews were explicitly allowed and documented before medication use. Three hospitalists who participated in the study took responsibility for coordinating PRN prescriptions during the Preparation phase, further improving adherence. These refinements after the pilot phase substantially improved physician acceptance and implementation consistency.

Demographics

A total of 2,457 patients were admitted to the neurology ward during the study period (Fig. 2). Their mean age was 65.0±14.5 years, with 1,368 men (55.7%) and 1,089 women (44.3%). Of these patients, 1,224 (50.8%) were admitted through the emergency room, and 1,155 (47.9%) were admitted from outpatient clinics. During the pre-implementation phase (Pre-PUSH), 1,258 patients were enrolled, whereas 1,154 patients were enrolled during the post-implementation phase (Post-PUSH). Of the total sample, 174 (7.2%) developed delirium (Nu-DESC≥2) at some point during hospitalization (94 in the pre-implementation period, 80 in the post-implementation period). Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of these 174 patients. Notably, more men were in the post-implementation group (65.0% vs. 48.9%, p=0.033), whereas mean ages of participants did not differ significantly (77.0±8.6 years vs. 74.5±11.0 years, p=0.094). Additional significant differences were observed between the two periods in terms of marital status, sleep deprivation, use of sleep medication, and past medical history (including hypertension, cardiac, and cerebrovascular diseases).

All patients who developed hyperactive delirium were identified with a Nu-DESC total score of ≥2 in assessments preceding the event. Specifically, none of the hyperactive delirium events occurred in patients who had a preceding Nu-DESC score of ≤1, underscoring the reliability of Nu-DESC screening within the PUSH protocol framework.

Effects of the PUSH protocol on clinical outcomes in delirium

Delirium occurrence

In the pre-implementation phase, 94 patients who developed delirium experienced 564 delirium events over 1,462 person-days (38.6 events per 100 person-days). In contrast, in the post-implementation phase, 80 patients had 277 episodes over 1,496 person-days (18.5 events per 100 person-days), representing a highly significant decrease (χ2(1)=145.26, p<0.001) following PUSH implementation.

Hyperactive delirium

Hyperactive episodes (Nu-DESC item #2) occurred at a rate of 18.7 per 100 person-days (273 events over 1,462 person-days) in the pre-implementation group compared with 14.6 per 100 person-days (218 events over 1,496 person-days) in the post-implementation group (Table 2). Thus, the post-implementation group had a 22% reduction in hyperactive delirium compared with the pre-implementation group (IRR 0.780; 95% CI 0.653–0.932, p=0.006). After adjusting for age and sex, the reduction reached 25% (IRR 0.741; 95% CI 0.616–0.892, p=0.002). After further adjustment for clinical confounders in Model 3, the incidence of hyperactive delirium remained significantly lower (adjusted IRR 0.657, 95% CI 0.536–0.804, p<0.001).

Subgroup analyses by diagnosis showed that the reduction in hyperactive delirium was most prominent in patients admitted with cerebrovascular disease. Among this subgroup, the adjusted IRR for hyperactive delirium (Model 3) was 0.528 (95% CI 0.414–0.674, p<0.001) (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). In contrast, patients without cerebrovascular disease showed no significant change in the incidence hyperactive delirium (Model 3 IRR 1.067, 95% CI 0.715–1.593, p=0.751) (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Unbearable situations or harms

Among patients who developed delirium (Nu-DESC≥2), “unbearable situations or harms” occurred at a rate of 5.6 per 100 person-days (82 events over 1,462 person-days) in the pre-implementation group but only 4.2 per 100 person-days (63 events over 1,496 person-days) in the post-implementation group (Table 2). This reduction was marginally non-significant in the crude analysis (IRR 0.751, 95% CI 0.5411–1.043, p=0.087) but became significant after adjusting for age and sex (IRR 0.667, 95% CI 0.474–0.938, p=0.020). After full adjustment for baseline confounders in Model 3, the reduction remained robust and significant (IRR 0.539, 95% CI 0.368–0.788, p=0.001).

The largest reduction was seen in environmental harms: from 4.4 to 1.2 events per 100 person-days, with a fully adjusted IRR of 0.198 (95% CI 0.112–0.350, p<0.001). Use of physical restraints also decreased significantly, with a fully adjusted IRR of 0.493 (95% CI 0.404–0.601, p<0.001).

Subgroup analysis further showed that patients with cerebrovascular disease experienced a significant decrease in unbearable situations or harms (Model 3 IRR 0.595, 95% CI 0.373–0.949, p=0.029) (Supplementary Table 1 in the onlineonly Data Supplement), whereas those without cerebrovascular disease did not demonstrate any significant improvement in this outcome (Model 3 IRR 0.677, 95% CI 0.297–1.544, p=0.354) (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Although patient harm appeared to increase in the post-implementation group (Model 3 IRR 1.851, 95% CI 0.385–8.904, p=0.443), this was not statistically significant. A closer review revealed that two patients in the pre-PUSH group experienced falls without restraints. In contrast, no falls occurred in the post-PUSH group. Instead, post-PUSH incidents primarily involved minor, self-inflicted injuries or bruises from striking bed rails.

Other individual harm categories, such as removal of therapeutic devices, physical harm to nurses, or refusal of nursing care, showed no statistically significant changes after full adjustment (Model 3).

Effects of the PUSH protocol on healthcare providers

Workload and security management

During hyperactive delirium episodes, staff typically performed clinical assessments, notified physicians, administered sedative medications (Supplementary Table 3 in the online-only Data Supplement), and occasionally called a security team if the situation posed an imminent risk.

The number of hyperactive delirium patients who did not receive sedative medications decreased significantly following PUSH protocol implementation (p<0.001), indicating an overall increased use of sedative medications (Table 3). Post-hoc analyses using Bonferroni correction revealed significant differences among all categories of episodes (0 vs. 1, 0 vs. ≥2, and 1 vs. ≥2 episodes treated).

Additionally, the distribution of the number of sedative medications administered per episode also changed significantly (p<0.001). Post-hoc analyses using Bonferroni correction showed significant differences between patients receiving no medication and those receiving one or more medications. However, there was no significant difference between patients receiving one medication and those receiving two or more. These findings suggest that the PUSH protocol increased the likelihood of administering sedative medications per episode but did not significantly alter the number of medications administered once sedation was initiated.

The median time from initial nursing assessment to sedative administration decreased significantly in the post-implementation group (16 [0–55] min pre-PUSH vs. 0 [0–10] min post-PUSH, p<0.001). Similar improvements were observed for the time from notification to prescription and prescription to administration (Table 3).

Although the incidence of security-team calls rose from 7.1% in the pre-implementation group to 22.4% in the post-implementation group (p<0.001), the median duration of security-team interventions decreased significantly from 20.0 (15.0–20.0) min to 10.0 (10.0–14.5) min (p<0.001) (Table 3). This combination of faster clinical intervention and quicker resolution of delirium helped reduce the overall workload for delirium management.

Staff burnout and satisfaction

A total of 33 medical personnel (25 nurses, 8 physicians; mean age 29.8±5.9 years, 78.8% women) responded to the burnout and satisfaction survey. Among nurses, the mean age was 29.4±6.4 years, and 23 (92.0%) were women; among physicians, the mean age was 30.9±3.9 years, with 5 men (62.5%) and 3 women (37.5%).

After PUSH implementation, total burnout scores significantly decreased (median [interquartile range]: 32.0 [28.0–35.0] pre-PUSH vs. 27.0 [25.5–30.5] post-PUSH, p<0.001), driven primarily by reductions in both exhaustion (19.0 [18.0–22.5] to 18.0 [16.0–19.0], p=0.001) and psychological detachment (13.0 [11.5–15.0] to 11.0 [9.0–12.0], p=0.010) among nurses. In contrast, burnout scores among physicians showed no significant change.

Satisfaction scores also improved significantly overall (3.0 [2.0–4.0] pre-PUSH vs. 4.0 [3.0–4.0] post-PUSH, p=0.009), with significant increases in satisfaction among nurses (p=0.015) but not among physicians. These results suggest that the PUSH protocol improved the well-being and satisfaction with delirium management of nurses, although physician scores remained relatively stable (Table 4).

No significant staffing changes or other notable contextual elements influenced these outcomes during the study period.

DISCUSSION

Our findings demonstrated that the PUSH protocol significantly reduced delirium-related adverse events and improved clinical response efficiency, meeting our primary objectives. otably, these improvements directly translated into decreased staff burnout and enhanced satisfaction. The structured and anticipatory nature of the PUSH protocol enabled faster response times for managing hyperactive delirium, reducing both patient harm and caregiver burden. Furthermore, despite an increased reliance on security-team calls, their shorter response time to address the delirium-related incidents significantly improved the overall safety of the environment.

Rationale for PUSH protocol development and guideline alignment

Before developing the PUSH protocol, delirium management in our neurological ward was inconsistent and reactive, leading to frequent unbearable situations including nursing staff injuries, staff turnover, and caregiver distress. To design a more cohesive system, we reviewed existing guidelines (available in 2021) that emphasized validated delirium screening, risk-factor assessments, and nonpharmacological measures (e.g., patient/family education, mobility, sleep enhancement, geriatric consultation).22233031 Although these foundational principles were not novel, we sought to systematically adapt and operationalize them for the neurological ward setting, where their implementation had been inconsistent and where hyperactive delirium posed specific clinical and safety challenges. A persistently high workload and clinical burnout often led to inconsistent implementation, especially regarding “acting out” behaviors such as verbal harassment or physical aggression. Concerns about sedating neurologically vulnerable patients14151732 further limited the proactive use of PRN antipsychotics. Recognizing that many doctors did not pre-prescribe sedation—particularly during overnight hours—we introduced a clear, explicit “Preparation” component similar to the neuromonitoring stage.31 Nurses used a standardized screening tool (e.g., Nu-DESC2627) and “pushed” physicians to plan medication needs in advance, thereby addressing violent behaviors before they escalated. Although previous guidelines alluded to early preparation,313334 they lacked explicit instructions for how to implement it systematically. The PUSH protocol bridged critical gaps in existing recommendations by making this step mandatory and standardized, ensuring consistent screening and proactive readiness for hyperactive delirium in a busy neurological ward.

Emphasis on real-world consequences of hyperactive delirium

The PUSH protocol’s most distinguishing feature is its explicit focus on the consequences of hyperactive delirium, defined as termed “unbearable situations or harms,” rather than delirium itself. Although standard delirium measures (screening tools, nonpharmacological interventions, and restraint minimization) have already been recognized, they were neither mandatory nor systematically monitored before PUSH. All stakeholders became more motivated and engaged as the PUSH protocol prioritized the prevention of real-world harms such as device removal, nurse assaults, and environmental disruptions. As a result, the protocol significantly reduced workload times (from recognition to intervention) and cut restraint usage in half despite a more frequent need to summon the security team. Notably, the preemptive “Preparation” component allowed physicians to prescribe targeted sedative medications ahead of time, streamlining urgent overnight care.

Unique considerations for neurological patients

Neurological patients present unique challenges for delirium management owing to the need for frequent neurological monitoring, variable sedation requirements, and the complex interplay of underlying conditions (e.g., stroke,16 PD,18 traumatic brain injury, or epilepsy).1317 Existing guidelines recommend individualized approaches that balance nonpharmacological measures (e.g., sleep enhancement, reorientation, and appropriate environmental modifications) with judicious use of medications.2223 However, these recommendations often lack explicit strategies for anticipating hyperactive delirium, particularly for high-risk patients during night shifts. The PUSH protocol addresses these gaps by proactively planning and preparing PRN medications in collaboration with day-shift staff, empowering on-duty clinicians to respond quickly and safely to episodes of delirium-induced violence or aggression. Although originally designed for neurological wards, in which shouting and acting-out behaviors disrupt patients and staff, this approach, emphasizing individualized prescribing and early risk assessment, can be adapted for diverse clinical settings where hyperactive delirium poses significant burdens.

Mitigating burnout through proactive delirium care

Burnout among nurses and physicians, particularly those caring for neurological patients with delirium, is closely linked to suboptimal patient outcomes, including decreased safety, quality of care, and satisfaction. Excess workload, unpredictable delirium behaviors (especially during night shifts), staffing shortages, and insufficient training exacerbate stress and exhaustion, undermining morale and clinical effectiveness. Delirium management demands heightened vigilance, frequent assessment, and rapid intervention, placing additional pressure on strained teams. Structured, anticipatory models such as the PUSH protocol can alleviate these burdens by reducing unpredictability and providing clear guidelines for timely intervention. Although most outcomes aligned with expectations, increased calls to the security team initially appeared counterintuitive; however, this likely reflects improved proactive management rather than problematic escalation. Strategic trade-offs included increased security resource use and initial training requirements, but these investments were offset by overall efficiency gains, improved safety outcomes, and reduced staff burnout.

Importantly, the observed about 4-point reduction in total burnout scores aligns with previously reported clinically meaningful changes. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that burnout interventions that produced reductions as small as 2.65 points in emotional exhaustion were associated with improved physician well-being and clinical outcomes.35 These results suggest that the about 4-point reduction observed in our high-stress neurological ward represents a meaningful improvement in staff well-being, which can potentially translate into better clinical performance, safety, and retention.

Unexpected reduction in delirium incidence and possible explanations

Although our study focused on reducing unbearable situations and harms related to hyperactive delirium, we also observed a decline in the overall incidence of delirium (Nu-DESC≥2) and episodes of hyperactive delirium during the post-implementation period. This finding is somewhat counterintuitive, as the PUSH protocol was designed to enhance early recognition and response rather than to prevent the onset of delirium. A possible explanation is that the structured implementation of standard delirium care inadvertently reinforced preventive practices such as reorientation, sleep hygiene, and family communication, which are core elements of nonpharmacological delirium prevention. The mandatory ward-posted flow chart and consistent application of “Standard Care” during each Nu-DESC screening likely improved the fidelity of these practices in all shifts.

Implementation science consistently shows that passive education, such as guidelines, lectures, or printed materials, rarely changes clinical behavior, while active workflow-integrated interventions, including care bundles, visual decision supports, and team participation are far more effective363738 Programs such as the Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) have reduced the incidence of delirium by about 50% when rigorously applied.39 A recent randomized trial of a tailored, family-involved HELP model (t-HELP) reduced postoperative delirium by 86%,37 and a German cluster trial achieved a reduction of delirium rates from 20.5% to 6.8% with standardized multidisciplinary protocols.40 These examples underscore that systematic reinforcement within clinical routines, and not passive education, is key to improving outcomes.

Together, these observations suggest that the structured nature of the PUSH protocol may have improved the consistency of delirium care, indirectly reducing the occurrence of delirium. However, since our study was not designed to assess the incidence of delirium as a primary outcome and we did not analyze the entire screened cohort, this finding should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating, which warrants further study.

Generalizability and diagnostic-specific effects

Despite its single-center tertiary design, our study suggests that the core components of the PUSH protocol, that is, proactive delirium screening, visual flow charts and team-based escalation, are not inherently restricted to large academic hospitals. These strategies are feasible and can be easily adapted to smaller hospitals, primary stroke centers, or general neurology wards, where similar behavioral crises related to delirium and coordination challenges arise, although the availability of staff and resources may differ. Additionally, our subgroup analyses demonstrated that patients with cerebrovascular disease (or acute stroke) derived the greatest benefit from the PUSH protocol, exhibiting reductions in the incidence of delirium, severe behavioral incidents, and the use of restraints. The increased vulnerability of stroke patients because of acute cognitive fluctuations, motor impairments, and the frequent use of invasive medical devices likely makes them especially receptive to structured interventions such as PUSH.16 Although non-stroke patients showed a significant reduction in restraints, trends for reduced delirium and harm events did not reach statistical significance, possibly owing to lower statistical power. Taken together, these findings suggest that although the PUSH protocol may be broadly beneficial in neurological inpatients, stroke units and other settings with high risk of delirium may see the most pronounced benefits. Future multi-center studies across varied hospital types and care environments are essential to validate its broader generalizability and refine implementation strategies for non-tertiary and lower-resourced settings.

Limitations

Our study faces certain notable limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we did not isolate or formally evaluate the effectiveness of the “Standard Care” component of the PUSH protocol, as our primary focus was on the newly introduced “Preparation” and “Action” components.

Second, the nonrandomized nature of our design, while practical for a busy clinical setting, could introduce selection or confounding biases. In particular, we did not collect baseline clinical characteristics for the entire cohort of 2,457-patients over the two study periods, which limits our ability to determine whether differences in patient populations (e.g., acuity or comorbidity profiles) may have contributed to the observed reduction in incidence of delirium. However, our subgroup analysis of patients who developed delirium did not reveal indications of a healthier post-implementation cohort; in fact, delirious patients in the post-PUSH phase were slightly older and more often male (both risk factors for delirium), making it less likely that population changes explain our findings. Moreover, study periods occurred after the peak COVID-19 pandemic, when hospital operations had largely normalized, reducing the potential for pandemic-related case-mix differences between phases. We adjusted for key demographic confounders (age, sex) and surveyed the same staff before and after implementation to reduce variability, thus minimizing limitations. Nevertheless, the absence of randomization remains a constraint on internal validity and generalizability.

Third, because this was a hospital-wide implementation study, the blinding of nurses and physicians was not feasible. This lack of blinding may have introduced observer bias in outcome reporting, especially for subjective outcomes such as hyperactive behaviors or staff-reported harms. However, to mitigate this risk, we relied on routinely collected clinical documentation (Nu-DESC scores, incident reports, and standardized adverse event logs) that was consistently applied throughout both pre- and post-implementation periods.

Fourth, although the Nu-DESC is a widely used and validated delirium screening tool in general clinical populations, it has not been specifically validated in patients in the neurological ward. Some neurological symptoms, such as aphasia, pre-existing cognitive impairment, or fluctuating consciousness, may mimic or obscure features of delirium, raising the possibility of misclassification. However, in this study, Nu-DESC served primarily as a practical clinical trigger to initiate proactive delirium management, rather than a definitive diagnostic tool. Future research should aim to validate delirium screening instruments specifically for neurological populations to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Fifth, variables such as sleep deprivation and pain were assessed based on nursing documentation without the use of validated measurement tools. As such, these assessments may reflect some degree of inter-rater variability and clinical subjectivity, which could introduce inconsistency in how these factors were recorded across shifts or providers.

Sixth, the statistical power varied between outcomes and subgroups. For our primary outcome (i.e., unbearable situations or harms), the study was well-powered in the total cohort (96.4%) and moderately powered in stroke patients (80.8%), supporting the robustness of these findings. However, statistical power was limited in the non-stroke subgroup (29.4%), and rare secondary outcomes such as patient harm were underpowered, with power estimates generally <20%. These non-significant results should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect limited sample size rather than absence of clinical effect.

Finally, our findings are specific to a single neurological ward and may not fully translate to other patient populations or hospital units. The specialized clinical characteristics, staffing patterns, and resource availability of a tertiary neurological ward, such as the high prevalence of cerebrovascular and seizure disorders, access to neurology-trained staff, and the increased concern for neurological deterioration during sedation, may differ substantially from those of general medical wards, smaller community hospitals, or non-tertiary centers. These differences may affect both the feasibility and impact of the PUSH protocol when applied outside of a tertiary care context. Future studies should validate the effectiveness of the PUSH protocol in broader populations of neurological patients and in diverse clinical settings. Multicenter trials across various types of hospitals and patient populations will be essential to confirm the generalizability and scalability of our findings.

In conclusion, the PUSH protocol markedly lowered delirium-related harms and improved workflow efficiency in our neurological ward, reducing staff burnout and boosting satisfaction. We demonstrated the efficacy of a proactive model that is both feasible and replicable by explicitly preparing approaches to manage hyperactive delirium and standardizing response strategies. Future studies should validate these findings in broader populations to confirm the generalizability of PUSH.
