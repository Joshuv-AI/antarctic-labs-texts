Genome-wide identification, characterization, and expression pattern analysis of the glyoxalase gene family in Phyllostachys pubescens during abiotic stresses
Authors: Anita Kumari, Umesh Bhati, Ravi Shankar, Sudesh Kumar Yadav, Sudhir K Sopory, Rohit Joshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe glyoxalase pathway comprising of three enzymes i.e., glyoxalase I (GLYI), glyoxalase II (GLYII), and glyoxalase III (GLYIII), which play vital role in mitigating abiotic stresses by detoxifying the stress induced cytotoxic metabolite methylglyoxal (MG). Phyllostachys pubescens an ecologically and economically important forest species, plays vital roles in carbon sequestration and climate change mitigation. A genome-wide study was conducted to identify and characterize GLYI, GLYII, and unique DJ-1/GLYIII gene candidates in P. pubescens. The identified members were evaluated based on phylogenetic analysis, gene structure, chromosomal distribution, gene duplication, presence of conserved domain(s) and cis regulatory region.ResultsA total of 19 GLYI, 18 GLYII, and 15 GLYIII members were identified, each featuring characteristic domains: glyoxalase, metallo-β-lactamase, and DJ-1/PfpI, respectively. The presence of different cis-elements in the promoter region of the glyoxalase genes gives insights into their role and regulation under hormonal response, developmental processes and stress adaptation. Besides this, stress responsive transcription factors binding sites also dominated the promoter regions of glyoxalase genes. Expression analysis of various glyoxalase genes demonstrated significant variability under different stress conditions, underscoring their potential roles in stress modulation. Significant upregulation of all of the PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII were observed under cold, drought, heavy metal and salinity stress suggesting their involvement in oxidative stress management, osmotic regulation and remodelling cellular redox homeostasis. Among the glyoxalase genes, PhGLYI-15, PhGLYII-9, and PhGLYIII-3 showed consistent upregulation under various abiotic stresses.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that glyoxalase genes crucially contribute towards the improvement of cellular osmotic potential in moso bamboo under different abiotic stresses. This study enhances our understanding of glyoxalase genes’ evolution and functional roles in plants and opens new avenues for developing stress resilient crop varieties for sustainable agriculture.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07306-y.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Cis-regulatory network, Conversed motif, Expression profiling, Glyoxalase, Moso bamboo, Phylogenetic analysis, QRT-PCR

Background

Climate change is profoundly driving frequent and severe disturbances, decreasing productivity and threatening the capacity of terrestrial carbon sinks [1]. Plants can adapt to fluctuating environmental conditions and accordingly, they adjust their growth and developmental processes [2]. The toxic metabolites methylglyoxal (MG), glyoxal (GO) and 3-deoxyglucosone (3-DG) are produced as by products through various metabolic pathways in plants under stress, which impede their normal growth and development [3]. Among these, MG is the most abundant RCS and is produced as a by-product of non-enzymatic reactions during glycolysis and the Calvin cycle, as well as enzymatic processes related to protein and fatty acid metabolism [4]. Excessive MG levels can be cytotoxic as it is strong electrophile which form complexes with functional group of several macromolecules i.e., nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids to form advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [5, 6]. Despite its toxicity, MG at lower concentrations play a crucial role as a signaling molecule in various biological processes [5], making its homeostasis essential to ensure cell survival leading to proper plant growth and development.

Plants maintain MG homeostasis via regulated detoxification through glyoxalase and non-glyoxalase pathways. The glyoxalase pathway mainly involves three enzymes: glyoxalase I (GLYI, also known as lactoylglutathione lyase), glyoxalase II (GLYII, or hydroxyacylglutathione hydrolase), and glyoxalase III (GLYIII, also called DJ-1) [6]. MG reacts spontaneously with glutathione (GSH) to form hemithioacetal (HTA), which is converted by GLYI into S-D-lactoylglutathione (SLG). Subsequently, GLYII hydrolyzes SLG to yield D-lactate and releases GSH molecule, completing the cycle [5]. The roles of these GSH-dependent glyoxalases have been widely studied in plants, highlighting their functions in MG detoxification, signal transduction, cell division, differentiation, starch synthesis, stress responses, and protection against oxoaldehyde toxicity. These activities make the pathway a “marker for cell growth and division”. Additionally, numerous studies have demonstrated the glyoxalase system’s contribution to stress tolerance in plants. Transgenic plants overexpressing GLYI and/or GLYII exhibit enhanced tolerance to various abiotic stresses such as salinity, drought, and heavy metal toxicity [4, 5]. Consequently, MG and glyoxalases are recognized as potential biomarkers for plant stress tolerance [7]. Phytohormones such as salicylic acid, auxin, cytokinin, brassinosteroids, gibberellic acid, etc. are also involved in regulating the function of glyoxalase enzymes either positively or negatively [8]. GLYIII, a novel member of the DJ-I protein family, catalyzes the direct conversion of MG to non-toxic D-lactate in a GSH-independent manner [9]. Although studies on GLYIII in model plants and crops are limited, existing research has highlighted its involvement in stress responses, fruit ripening, and regulation of ethylene production in tomatoes [2, 10, 11].

Unlike other eukaryotes, plant glyoxalases exist as a multigenic family wherein the encoded enzymes have acquired vital metal dependency for their catalytic activity [12, 13]. The expansion of multiple members, especially in the plant kingdom, probably offers a scope for the GLY family to be involved in diverse roles apart from the classical role of functional GLY genes in stress tolerance [5]. The identification and functional analysis of glyoxalase gene family members has therefore gained significant attention due to their important role in plant development and adaptation. While genome-wide studies have identified GLYI and GLYII genes in model plants and key crops such as Sorghum bicolor [14], Glycine max [15], Capsicum annum [16], and Brassica napus [17], the genome-wide identification of DJ-1 remains scarce [17–19]. Notably, GLYI and GLYII genes in these species are present as multiple members with diverse subcellular localizations [4]. Thus, overall identification and comparative analysis of GLYI, GLYII, and DJ-1 genes is essential for comprehensive understanding of the regulation of plant growth, development, and stress responses.

Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys pubescens), is a perennial woody grass species of tropical and subtropical forests and is one of the most economically, ecologically and socially valuable bamboo species [20, 21]. Despite known for its rapid growth, high silicon and high carbon storage capacity, intensified climate change seriously threatens this habitat-based species [22, 23]. In addition, besides understanding the rapid growth of moso bamboo shoots, the molecular mechanisms governing this growth under abiotic stresses remain largely unexplored [24]. In an earlier study we explored the role of transcription factors in moso bamboo and their regulation under a stressful environment [25]. Our previous studies demonstrated the accumulation of glyoxalase genes in nano-elicited Bambusa balcooa plants [26] and their involvement in biotic stress responses in Pyrus bretschneideri [27].

Advances in bioinformatics for model plants and the availability of a chromosome-level reference genome for moso bamboo [20, 28] provide a reliable database for a genome-wide investigation of the glyoxalase gene family in this species. In this paper, we present an exhaustive genome-wide analysis of GLYI, GLYII, and GLYIII (DJ-1) genes at the whole-genome level, along with in-depth analysis of their chromosome mapping, phylogenetic analysis, synteny analysis, conserved domain prediction, cis-regulatory element prediction, subcellular localization prediction, and gene-specific expression analysis. Furthermore, quantitative real-time PCR was employed to analyse the transcript abundance of glyoxalase gene family members in bamboo growth under stress conditions. This comprehensive analysis of glyoxalase genes in moso bamboo will significantly contribute to further explore the relative role of members of glyoxalase family in MG detoxification and also refine breeding strategies to fetch superior alleles in this vital species.

Methods

Acquisition of data resources for glyoxalase gene analysis

To create a local database, the Moso bamboo genome sequence and the most recent annotation files were acquired from GigaDB (http://gigadb.org/dataset/100498). Additionally, the Ensembl Plants database (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) provided genome-wide data for Arabidopsis, rice, soybean, and Moso bamboo.

Comprehensive identification of Phglyi, Phglyii, and Phdj-1 in moso bamboo

To identify the GLYI, GLYII, and the distinct GLYIII (DJ-1) proteins in Moso bamboo, a methodical approach was used: Step 1. The Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) provided the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles for the glyoxalase domain (PF00903), metallo-beta-lactamase domain (PF00753), and DJ-1/PfpI domain (PF01965), with an E-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 [29]. Step 2: The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) (http://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp), Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.uga.edu/), and Soybean Genome Database (Wm82.a2.v1) (http://www.soybase.org/) were the sources of the amino acid sequences of the GLY proteins of Arabidopsis, rice, and soybean, respectively. Step 3: Using an E-value limit of 1 × 10−5, candidate protein sequences were verified using the BLASTp tool against the NCBI non-redundant database (nr-DB). Step 4: To confirm the presence of glyoxalase, metallo-beta-lactamase, or DJ-1/PfpI domains, putative GLY proteins were further validated using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/), InterProScan [30, 31], and SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [32].

Following previously established nomenclature, confirmed GLY genes were named based on their chromosomal locations in Moso bamboo [15, 33]. The ProtParam software (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to examine physicochemical parameters, such as grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), molecular weight (MW), and isoelectric point (pI) [34]. CELLO v.2.5 (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/) and WoLF PSORT (http://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html) were used to predict subcellular localization. ChloroP (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/ChloroP/) was used to confirm the location of chloroplasts.

Phylogenetic analysis, tertiary structure prediction, and multiple sequence alignment

The evolutionary relationship between the conserved domains of the PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII protein sequences and their known orthologues from various plant species was determined using ClustalW. The alignment was visualized using Jalview 2 with default settings (v2.11.2.0) [35]. WebLogo was used to create conserved motif logos (https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/). The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) provided the tertiary structures of the DJ-1/PfpI, metallo-beta-lactamase, and glyoxalase domains. AlphaFold2 provides per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT) ranging from 0 to 100, with regions below 50 pLDDT potentially representing unstructured regions in isolation.

A phylogenetic tree was built utilizing the protein sequences of PhGLY, AtGLY, OsGLY, GmGLY, and MtGLY to analyze the evolutionary relationships within the GLY gene family. We used MUSCLE/ClustalW to perform multiple sequence alignment [36]. The protein sequence alignment was used to create a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree using the Neighbour-Joining approach in MEGA 5.3 [37] and IQ-TREE 2 [38]. Using 1,000 bootstrap repeats, branch support was evaluated, and pairwise comparisons of genetic distances were used to determine branch lengths. Pairwise deletion was utilized to address gaps. Using iTOL v6.9 (https://www.itol.embl.de/tree), the resulting phylogenetic tree was displayed [39]. Supplementary Table S1, S2, S3 contained the protein sequences used in the phylogenetic analysis investigation.

Chromosomal localization and analysis of gene duplications in Phgly genes

We employed Circos software [40] to map PhGLY genes onto their corresponding chromosomes based on physical location data retrieved from the Moso bamboo genome database. To identify gene duplication events, synteny analysis of orthologous PhGLY genes between Moso bamboo and selected species was performed using the Multiple Collinearity Scan toolkit (MCScanX). The PAL2NAL program ([http://www.bork.embl.de/pal2nal/] (http://www.bork.embl.de/pal2nal/)) [41] was utilized to calculate non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates, as well as Ka/Ks ratios for all duplicated gene pairs. The method T = Ks/(2λ) was used to estimate the divergence time (T in MYA: million years ago) of PhGLY genes, where λ = 6.5 × 10−9 substitutions per site per year.

Analysis of genomic structure and protein architecture of Phgly genes

The Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0) [42] was used to compare genomic and coding DNA sequences (CDS) to investigate the exon-intron architectures of glyoxalase genes [42]. GFF annotation files extracted gene characteristics from the Moso bamboo genome, including untranslated region (UTR), exon, and intron phase sites. The domain locations were determined and the amino acid sequences for each domain were extracted using Pfam and SMART. Schematic representations of domain organization were then generated manually.

Cis-regulatory elements, co-expression and TFs regulatory network analysis

To analyze the TF-binding regions or sites in the promoters of five GLY genes, PlantCARE [43], PTFSpot [44], PTF-Vāc [45], and CTF-BIND [46] scanned the upstream 2-kb sequence. The TF 3D structures were necessary for both PTF-Vāc and PTFSpot. Since there were no experimental 3D structures available, these structures were generated using AlphaFold2 [47] instead. Co-expression analysis was performed with a correlation coefficient threshold of ≥|0.6| between each GLY gene and the TFs identified as having binding sites within its promoter region. The bambooNET database (http://bioinformatics.cau.edu.cn/bamboo/search.php) provided the RNA-seq expression data under four different stress conditions—drought, salinity, cold, and heavy metals—for the co-expression study [48]. A thorough transcription factor regulatory network was built using the co-expression patterns and TF binding sites found in the PhGLY gene promoter sequences. To show the complex interactions between TFs and their target genes, the regulatory network was displayed using R’s igraph package [49]. Seaborn and the Python tool matplotlib were used to visualize the plots [50].

Plant material growth conditions and abiotic stress treatments

Healthy mother plants of P. pubescens were collected from the field of the CSIR–Institute of Himalayan Bioresource Technology, Himachal Pradesh, India. The collection site is situated in the Dhauladhar range of the Western Himalayas (32.1052° N, 76.5564° E) at an elevation of 1296 m above sea level. The region is characterized by a humid subtropical climate, receiving an average annual precipitation of 2596 mm. Aseptic in vitro cultures of P. pubescens were obtained by inoculating single nodal explants in Murashige and Skoog (MS) [51] media supplemented with 2% sucrose, BAP (9µM) and metatopolin (1µM) [52]. Cultures were kept under controlled lab conditions i.e., PPF density of 50 µmol m−2 s−1 using white fluorescent LED tubes (20 W; Bajaj, India), 25–28 °C and photoperiod of 16/8 h light period and 55 to 60% relative humidity. Further, in-vitro regenerated plants of P. pubescens were exposed to different abiotic treatment conditions of cold, salinity, drought, and heavy metal stress. The in vitro shoot cultures were kept at temperature conditions of 0°, 4° and 10° incubation chambers while samples were collected at 7 and 14 days. The micro-propagated plants were treated with different mannitol concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, and 350 mM) in nutrient media to induce drought stress. The micro-propagated plants were inoculated in different NaCl concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, and 350 mM). The heavy metal stress induction we exposed in-vitro regenerated plants to Potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) concentrations (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, and 700µM). Similarly, treatment cultures containing micro-propagated plants were incubated for 14 days under laboratory conditions. The concentration at which P. pubescens exhibited the highest resistance to stress treatment, was selected for molecular analyses.

Shoot samples of cold, salinity, drought, and heavy metal-stressed plants were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for expression profiling. The shoot number and biomass of micro-plantlets were assessed after 14 days under various stress treatment conditions.

RNA extraction and quantitative realtime PCR analysis (qRTPCR)

The procedures for primer design (as per the guidelines available at Primer3Plus), RNA extraction, and qRT-PCR profiling were performed as described by [53]. Primers specific to glyoxalase I, glyoxalase II, and glyoxalase III were designed for P. pubescens (Supplementary Table S4), with PhActin serving as the internal control. The scientific community has extensively validated Actin as a stable internal reference gene in P. pubescens under various abiotic stress conditions [54, 55], and it continues to be one of the most commonly used reference genes for bamboo transcriptome studies. In our experiments, we ensured consistency by sampling tissues at the same developmental stage and under controlled conditions, minimizing variability. Total RNA was extracted in biological triplicates from various in vitro tissues of P. pubescens plants exhibiting the highest resistance to abiotic stresses, using the iRIS method. The Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) measured RNA quantity while a 1.2% denaturing agarose gel confirmed RNA integrity. The Verso cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA from 1.5 µg of RNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ABI StepOne Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) performed qRT-PCR on three biological replicates with two technical replicates for each sample. The reaction mixture (10 µL) contained DyNAmo Color Flash SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) which was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The amplification program started with a 7-minute denaturation at 95 °C before running 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The melting curve was analyzed using default parameters. Relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and log fold change analysis was employed to quantify variations, with PhActin as the reference gene.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was carried out in biological triplicate. The statistical analysis was conducted using Origin2024 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA), which facilitated one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test to determine statistical significance. Error bars were included to visually illustrate the standard error, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. The graphs were made using Microsoft Excel V13.

Results

Glyoxalase Protein Identification in Phyllostachys pubescens

Glyoxalase genes and encoded proteins have been described from a variety of plant and animal species. In plants like Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Cucumis sativus, and Glycine max, multiple glyoxalase (GLYI and GLYII) genes have been reported, many of which are involved in abiotic stress responses such as drought and salinity [15, 56]. In humans, the glyoxalase system—particularly GLO1 and GLO2, is essential for detoxifying methylglyoxal, a cytotoxic byproduct of metabolism, and is implicated in conditions like diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [57]. These findings underscore the conserved and critical role of glyoxalase enzymes across biological kingdoms. Using a profile HMM search, we found 19 PhGLYI genes in Moso bamboo in a genome-wide study. These genes were spread across various Moso bamboo chromosomes (Fig. 1a). Based on the order of their chromosomal location, these proteins were designated as PhGLYI-1 to PhGLYI-19 (Supplementary Table S5). According to previous studies, there are more GLYI genes in Moso bamboo than in A. thaliana (10) and O. sativa (11) [15, 55]. This expanded gene repertoire may be related to the genome size of Moso bamboo (approximately 2.2 Gb), which is substantially larger than that of A. thaliana (~ 135 Mb), suggesting higher genomic complexity [58]. Recent studies by [59] have demonstrated that genome size, complexity, and gene copy number are often closely associated, with larger genomes typically harboring expanded gene families to accommodate more diverse functional requirements.

Similarly, 18 PhGLYII genes that were identified in Moso bamboo exceeding the numbers reported in A. thaliana (5) and O. sativa (3) [15, 55]. These were designated PhGLYII-1 through PhGLYII-18, following the nomenclature used for the PhGLYI proteins (Supplementary Table S6). The substantial difference in gene copy numbers between Moso bamboo and model plants like A. thaliana reflects evolutionary adaptations potentially driven by the complex genomic architecture of bamboo, which has undergone whole-genome duplication events and subsequent gene retention [60].

Following a similar approach, a total of 15 unique PhGLYIII (DJ-1) genes were identified in Moso bamboo. These genes were systematically named PhDJ-1 A to PhDJ-1 F, following the nomenclature established for similar proteins in other species [33] (Supplementary Table S7). Moso Bamboo possesses a greater number of DJ-1 genes compared to A. thaliana and O. sativa, both of which have been reported to contain 6 DJ-1 genes [33]. This expanded repertoire of DJ-1 genes in Moso bamboo underscores its genetic complexity and suggests possible functional adaptations. The greater number of PhGLYIII genes compared to other species might reflect the unique physiological and ecological requirements of Moso bamboo. As highlighted by [59], the expansion of specific gene families often correlates with adaptation to environmental stresses and specialized metabolic functions, which may be particularly relevant for Moso bamboo given its remarkable growth rate and ecological versatility.

Comprehensive Analysis of PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhDJ-1 members in Phyllostachys pubescens

The identified genes encoding PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhDJ-1 proteins in Moso bamboo were analyzed for various attributes, including transcript length, coding DNA sequence (CDS), and polypeptide characteristics included molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI), and predicted subcellular localization. The analysis showed that the CDS length of PhGLYI proteins range from 285 bp (PhGLYI-18) to 1305 bp (PhGLYI-13), averaging 744 bp, with corresponding polypeptides varying from 95 amino acids (PhGLYI-18) to 434 amino acids (PhGLYI-13). Molecular weights span from 10.63 kDa to 46.18 kDa (Supplementary Table S5), reflecting significant structural variation within the family. The average length and predicted molecular weight for PhGLYI proteins were 247 aa and 27.04 kDa, respectively (Supplementary Table S5). The pI values also show a broad range, from 4.44 (PhGLYI-16) to 9.28 (PhGLYI-8), with the majority (14 out of 19 proteins) being acidic, suggesting their functional adaptation to various physiological conditions. Subcellular localization analyses—conducted using WoLF PSORT, DeepLoc 1.0, CELLO v.2.5, LOCALIZER, and TargetP 2.0 predict that the majority of PhGLYI proteins reside in the cytosol, whereas distinct paralogs are directed to chloroplasts, mitochondria, nuclei, or the extracellular matrix (Supplementary Tables S5 and S8). Importantly, chloroplast targeting was consistently identified by both CELLO and WoLF PSORT, underscoring a putative role for these isoforms in photosynthetic processes and associated stress-response pathways.

The CDS length of PhGLYII proteins ranged from 777 bp (PhGLYII-11) to 2616 bp (PhGLYII-10) (Supplementary Table S5). Thus, PhGLYII-10 is the largest member, comprising 871 amino acids and a molecular weight of 96.21 kDa, while PhGLYII-11 is the smallest, with 258 amino acids and a molecular weight of 28.78 kDa. The pI values of PhGLYII proteins vary widely, from acidic (5.22 for PhGLYII-1) to basic (9.04 for PhGLYII-10), with a majority (10 out of 18 proteins) showing basic pI values, reflecting diverse functional adaptations. The subcellular localization analysis showed that most PhGLYII proteins exist in the cytosol, but PhGLYII-16 and PhGLYII-17 are predicted to be chloroplast-localized by multiple validated tools (CELLO, WoLF PSORT, DeepLoc 1.0, CELLO v.2.5, LOCALIZER, and TargetP 2.0), with WoLF PSORT providing high-confidence predictions that support functional hypotheses (Supplementary Table S5 and S8). Subcellular localization analyses revealed that most PhGLYII proteins are cytosolic. The localization of PhGLYII-16 and PhGLYII-17 to chloroplasts is strongly predicted by multiple tools (CELLO, WoLF PSORT, and ChloroP) (Supplementary Table S5 and S8). Several proteins (PhGLYII-2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11) were predicted to localize to mitochondria, while others are predicted to function in the nucleus (PhGLYII-8, 15) or extracellular matrix (PhGLYII-3, 11, and 13), which highlights the diverse roles of PhGLYII proteins in cellular processes mechanisms.

Different physio-chemical properties of distinct glyoxalase III (PhDJ-1) proteins were also examined, the coding sequence (CDS) length of PhDJ-1 proteins exhibited considerable variability, ranging from 285 bp for the smallest member, PhDJ-1B.3, to 2100 bp for the largest member, PhDJ-1 F, with an average CDS length of 1032 bp (Supplementary Table S7). Correspondingly, the polypeptide lengths also varied significantly, with PhDJ-1B.3 consisting of 95 amino acids and a molecular weight of 10.63 kDa, while PhDJ-1 F is 699 amino acid long with a molecular weight of 74.47 kDa. The isoelectric point (pI) of PhDJ-1 proteins ranged from acidic (4.87 for PhDJ-1B.3) to slightly basic (8.55 for PhDJ-1E). Among the 15 identified PhDJ-1 proteins, 12 displayed acidic pI values (< 7.0), while three were basic. Chloroplast localization of PhDJ-1 proteins was consistently predicted and confirmed using three different bioinformatics tools: CELLO, WoLF PSORT, and ChloroP (Supplementary Table S5 and S8). This indicates a primary role of PhDJ-1 proteins in chloroplast-associated processes, possibly related to photosynthesis and the detoxification of reactive carbonyl species.

Chromosomal distribution and synteny analysis of PhGLYs genes

Chromosomal distribution of glyoxalase genes across the Moso bamboo genome showed a random yet biased distribution, with certain chromosomes harboring more genes than others, and a total of 15 out of 24 chromosomes carrying glyoxalase loci (Fig. 1a). To precisely identify PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhDJ-1 genes on several Moso bamboo chromosomes, a comprehensive chromosome map was created. 10 Moso bamboo chromosomes were found to have an unequal distribution of PhGLYI genes. A maximum of three GLYI genes were found on chromosome 18 (Fig. 1a). Likewise, we found that eight distinct chromosomes contained 18 PhGLYII genes (Fig. 1a). A maximum of five GLYII genes were located on chromosome 3, indicating a very unequal gene density per chromosome (Fig. 1a). 11 Moso bamboo chromosomes were found to have an unequal distribution of PhDJ-1 genes. Chromosome 16 has a maximum of three of the 15 PhDJ-1 genes (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, the chromosomal locations of all PhGLY genes were biased towards the terminal regions of the chromosomes (Fig. 1a). This positional preference suggests the potential involvement of inter-chromosomal genetic rearrangements during the evolutionary history of Moso bamboo, particularly during genome duplication events. The uneven distribution and clustering of glyoxalase genes highlight their possible role in adaptive responses [15] and genomic plasticity in Moso bamboo [61].

Gene duplication and divergence are critical drivers of plant evolution, contributing to genomic plasticity and species adaptation. The divergence of two major angiosperm lineages, eudicots and monocots, is estimated to have occurred approximately 125–140 million years ago (Mya) to as far back as 170–235 Mya [62]. These evolutionary processes often result in chromosomal duplications and the gain or loss of specific genes. To investigate glyoxalase gene duplication events across the genomes of Arabidopsis, rice, soybean, Moso bamboo, and Medicago, synteny analysis was conducted using the Plant Genome Duplication Database (http://chibba.agtec.uga.edu/duplication/index/downloads) [62]. This analysis identified 6 PhGLYI, 7 PhGLYII, and 4 PhGLYIII sister-gene pairs in Moso bamboo (Supplementary Table S9; Fig. 1a). The gene pairs are listed in Additional file 3: Table S3, and orthologous PhGLY genes were detected in the genomes of Arabidopsis, soybean, rice, and Medicago, signifying conservation across diverse plant species. (Supplementary Table S9; Fig. 1a).

To explore the evolutionary forces shaping glyoxalase genes, the non-synonymous to synonymous substitution (Ka/Ks) ratio was calculated. This metric serves as an indicator of selective pressure, where Ka/Ks < 1 denotes purifying selection, Ka/Ks = 1 indicates neutral evolution, and Ka/Ks > 1 suggests positive (Darwinian) selection [63–65]. The analysis revealed that in monocotyledonous plants, such as rice, all calculated Ka/Ks ratios for collinear glyoxalase gene pairs were less than 1, indicating strong purifying selection (Supplementary Table S9). Conversely, for dicotyledonous plants like Arabidopsis, Medicago, and soybean, Ka/Ks ratios for most collinear gene pairs could not be determined, likely due to an increased number of synonymous mutations. This observation reflects greater sequence divergence and a larger evolutionary distance among glyoxalase genes in these dicot genomes. These findings emphasize the distinct evolutionary trajectories of glyoxalase genes in monocots and dicots, influenced by differential duplication patterns and selective pressures. Understanding these evolutionary dynamics provides valuable insights into the functional diversification and adaptation of glyoxalase genes in plants.

Analysis of PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII gene structures

The exon-intron structure of the PhGLYI (Fig. 4b-i), PhGLYII (Fig. 4b-ii), and PhGLYIII (Fig. 4b-iii) genes was thoroughly examined, and significant variation was found. All genes in the PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII families contained at least one intron within their open reading frame (ORF), indicating the absence of intronless glyoxalase genes in Moso bamboo. The number of introns in PhGLYI genes ranged from 1 to 9 (Fig. 4b-i). For instance, PhGLYI-8, PhGLYI-10, PhGLYI-16, and PhGLYI-18 contained a single intron, while PhGLYI-2 harboured the highest number (8 introns). On the other hand, PhGLYII-14 contained only two introns, while other PhGLYII genes displayed variable intron numbers. The PhGLYIII family showed a similar range, with intron numbers varying from 1 to 8 in different genes. Notably, PhDJ-1B.1, PhDJ-1B.2, and PhDJ-1 C.1 each contained 8 introns, while PhDJ-1 A.1 and PhDJ-1–23 A had just one intron in their ORFs (Fig. 4b-iii).

Analysis of the PhGLYI protein’s conserved domain structure and active site

The structural and sequence features of 19 predicted PhGLYI proteins were examined to elucidate their functional characteristics and evolutionary relationships. All PhGLYI proteins were found to harbor at least one conserved glyoxalase domain (PF00903), as identified using Pfam analysis. Interestingly, four members (PhGLYI-1, PhGLYI-2, PhGLYI-3, and PhGLYI-4) contained two consecutive GLYI domains, which is a feature previously observed in certain species such as Oryza sativa [13], Saccharomyces cerevisiae [68], Plasmodium falciparum [69], and Glycine max [15]. In addition to the conserved GLYI domain, some PhGLYI proteins possessed additional domains, including the glyoxalase-like domain (PF18029), the hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase N-terminal domain (PF14696), and the Zn-dependent metallo-hydrolase RNA specificity domain (PF07521). While the exact roles of these supplementary domains remain unclear, their presence may suggest specialized or multifunctional roles for these PhGLYI proteins in cellular processes (Fig. 4a-i).

To predict the metal ion dependency of PhGLYI proteins, the GLYI domain sequences were aligned with the Ni2+-dependent OsGLYI-11 and Zn2+-dependent OsGLYI-8 proteins [70]. The dependency of glyoxalase enzymatic activity on metal ions was found to correlate with domain length: a domain length of ~ 120 amino acids indicated Ni2+-dependence, while ~ 142 amino acids suggested Zn2+-dependence. Notably, Zn2+-dependent proteins, such as OsGLYI-8, contained an extra stretch of amino acids absent in Ni2+-dependent proteins like OsGLYI-11 [70]. Based on sequence alignment, PhGLYI-17 was predicted to be Zn2+-dependent, whereas PhGLYI-1, PhGLYI-2, PhGLYI-3, PhGLYI-11, PhGLYI-13, PhGLYI-15, PhGLYI-18, and PhGLYI-19 were likely Ni2+-dependent (Fig. 5). The glyoxalase gene family in P. pubescens exhibits differential expression under abiotic stresses, with Ni²⁺-dependent GlyI genes (PhGLYI-1, PhGLYI-2, PhGLYI-3, PhGLYI-7, PhGLYI-9, PhGLYI-11, PhGLYI-13, PhGLYI-15, and PhGLYI-18) upregulated under cold, drought, and salinity stress, while Zn²⁺-dependent GlyI genes (PhGLYI-5, PhGLYI-8, PhGLYI-17) respond to heavy metal stress (Supplementary Table S11).

The active site of glyoxalase I (GLYI) proteins is characterized by a conserved motif, H/QEH/QE, which remains invariant across proteins regardless of their metal ion dependency [15]. This motif includes four key residues that are critical for catalytic activity, as observed in both OsGLYI-8 and OsGLYI-11, and these residues are marked with red stars in sequence alignments (Fig. 5). Among these residues, the first glutamate position exhibited the highest divergence, particularly in GmGLYI proteins. Interestingly, two PhGLYI proteins, PhGLYI-17 and PhGLYI-19, displayed variations in all four of these conserved active site residues, suggesting possible functional divergence. Proteins retaining all four conserved residues are predicted to exhibit functional GLYI enzymatic activity, as supported by previous research [13, 15]. Among the 19 predicted PhGLYI proteins in Moso bamboo, 13 possess all four highly conserved residues and are therefore expected to have functional glyoxalase I enzyme activity.

These results demonstrate the structural diversity and potential functional specialization of PhGLYI proteins, emphasizing their adaptation to specific metal ion dependencies and the importance of conserved residues in maintaining enzymatic function. The presence of additional domains and variations in domain architecture suggests that PhGLYI proteins may play multifaceted roles in cellular processes beyond glyoxalase activity, potentially contributing to stress responses, detoxification, and metabolic regulation in Moso bamboo. Moreover, the observed differences in conserved active site residues point to potential functional divergence within the PhGLYI protein family.

Analysis of the structure and sequence of PhGLYII proteins for active sites and conserved domains

All PhGLYII proteins contain the conserved Metallo-beta-lactamase domain (PF00753), either by itself or in combination with other domains, according to Pfam analysis (Fig. 4a-ii). Out of the 18 proteins, 13 of the PhGLYII proteins only had the metallo-beta-lactamase domain; the other five had other domains like the POT family (PF00854), the beta-casp domain (PF10996), the Zn-dependent metallo-hydrolase RNA domain (PF07521), and the hydroxyacylglutathione hydrolase C-terminus (PF16123) (Fig. 4a-ii). It is unclear how these extra domains relate to the activity of the GLYII enzyme [71]. reported that GLYII proteins, like GLYI, had two well-conserved motifs: an active site motif (C/GHT) and a metal binding motif (THXHXDH). It is essential for a GLYII enzyme to have both of these motifs. Thus, the sequences of all PhGLYII proteins were compared to those of the GLYII proteins from Arabidopsis and rice (Fig. 4). All PhGLYII sequences have both the conserved metal-binding (THXHXDH) and active site (C/GHT) motifs, according to alignment analysis, which suggests that they may have GLYII enzyme activity. Red stars indicated where each of these motifs was located (Fig. 4).

Analysis of the domain structure and sequence of distinct Glyoxalase III (DJ-1) proteins in Phyllostachys pubescens

Unique glyoxalase III (GLYIII) proteins, characterized by the DJ-1/PfpI domain, have been identified in various microorganisms and plants [15, 68, 69]. Functional GLYIII enzymatic activity has been experimentally confirmed in DJ-1 proteins such as AtDJ-1D from Arabidopsis and OsDJ-1 C from rice [33, 72]. In Moso bamboo, Pfam analysis confirmed the presence of the conserved DJ-1/PfpI domain (PF01965) in all 15 PhDJ-1 proteins, either alone or with additional domains. Among these, 9 proteins contained two consecutive DJ-1/PfpI domains, while others had a single domain accompanied by other functional domains, such as nuclear pore complex assembly (PF13934) and helix-loop-helix DNA-binding (PF00010) domains (Fig. 4a-iii). The DJ-1/PfpI domain typically spans 140–150 amino acids in length across species. Sequence analysis of PhDJ-1 proteins indicated that most members contained two DJ-1/PfpI domains connected by a linker region approximately 40 amino acids long. However, two proteins, PhDJ-1B.3 and PhDJ-1 C.1, exhibited single DJ-1/PfpI domains with lengths of 68 and 80 amino acids, respectively, suggesting that these may represent partial domains.

To evaluate the catalytic potential, the N-terminal DJ-1/PfpI domains of PhDJ-1 proteins were aligned with AtDJ-1D and OsDJ-1 C, which are known to possess robust GLYIII activity (Fig. 5). It has been established that both DJ-1 and Hsp31 proteins feature a conserved Glu-Cys-His catalytic triad [73]. Among these residues, glutamate and cysteine, located at consistent positions within both enzymes, are critical for GLYIII enzymatic activity, while histidine, found at variable sequence and structural sites, plays a less significant role [62, 73]. GLYIII proteins universally possess this catalytic triad, including aspartate/glutamate, cysteine, and tyrosine/histidine residues, which are essential for enzymatic function [33]. These key residues were highlighted with red stars in the alignment (Fig. 5). The central cysteine residue is particularly vital, as it directly participates in the catalytic process [74]. All PhDJ-1 proteins maintained this conserved cysteine residue, indicating their potential to act as functional GLYIII enzymes (Fig. 5). The absence of this cysteine residue, as observed in AtDJ-1 C, results in a complete loss of GLYIII activity. Similarly, the absence of either the aspartate/glutamate or tyrosine/histidine residues leads to partial loss of activity, as shown in AtDJ-1E and AtDJ-1 F [72]. Among the PhDJ-1 proteins, PhDJ-1 F and PhDJ-1E exhibited all three conserved residues, suggesting that they are likely the most efficient GLYIII enzymes in Moso bamboo, followed by PhDJ-1-6E (Fig. 5).

Phylogenetic analysis of PhGLYIs, PhGLYIIs, and PhGLYIIIs

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted to investigate the evolutionary relationships among the identified PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII proteins and previously reported GLYI, GLYII, and GLYIII proteins from various plant species. Using MEGA 5.2, a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was generated based on 73 full-length GLYI protein sequences from Moso bamboo, Medicago truncatula, Glycine max, Oryza sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, and other plant species. The resulting tree resolved into five distinct clades (I–V) (Fig. 5b). The presence of representatives from both dicots (A. thaliana, M. truncatula, and G. max) and monocots (Moso bamboo and O. sativa) within all clades indicates that the diversification of plant GLYI genes predates the monocot-dicot divergence. Clade V contained the largest number of GLYI proteins from various plant species, while Clade III contained the fewest, primarily from A. thaliana and G. max (Fig. 5b). Clade I exclusively comprised GLYI members from the complete genomes of A. thaliana, M. truncatula, and G. max. Clade IV included multiple members from Moso bamboo and O. sativa, and two from A. thaliana. Among the Clade IV members, O. sativa OsGLYI-2, OsGLYI-7, and OsGLYI-11, and A. thaliana AtGLYI-3 and AtGLYI-6, have been previously predicted to function as Ni2+-dependent GLYI enzymes [70], suggesting that the remaining members of this clade are also likely Ni2+-dependent. Similarly, Clade III included O. sativa OsGLYI-8 and A. thaliana AtGLYI-2, which are known Zn2+-dependent GLYI enzymes [70]. Therefore, the remaining members of this clade from other species are expected to require Zn2+ for optimal enzymatic activity (Fig. 5b).

To explore the phylogenetic relationships among GLYII proteins, a separate phylogenetic tree was constructed for all PhGLYII, MtGLYII, GmGLYII, OsGLYII, and AtGLYII proteins (Fig. 4b). Similar to the GLYI phylogenetic tree, this tree was divided into four distinct clades (I to IV). Clade II comprised 11 proteins from Moso bamboo, and one each from O. sativa (OsGLYII-1) and A. thaliana (AtGLYII-3). OsGLYII-1 has been reported to exhibit sulfur dioxygenase (SDO) activity, rather than GLYII activity [7], indicating functional divergence within this clade. Clade III contained six proteins each from Moso bamboo and soybean, four from Medicago, two from rice (OsGLYII-2 and OsGLYII-3), and one from Arabidopsis (AtGLYII-2). Among these, AtGLYII-2 has been identified as a mitochondrial-localized member of the Arabidopsis GLYII family [75]. Clade IV was particularly noteworthy from an evolutionary perspective. It included GLYII proteins exclusively from dicot species such as Arabidopsis, soybean, and Medicago, emphasizing the evolutionary divergence of GLYII proteins following the monocot-dicot split (Fig. 4b).

PhGLYIII proteins were aligned with previously reported DJ-1 protein sequences from Medicago truncatula, Oryza sativa, Glycine max, and Arabidopsis thaliana using ClustalW [33, 76, 77]. Incomplete sequences and proteins with either the N-terminal or C-terminal sections devoid of the highly conserved glutamate and cysteine residues were not included in the analysis. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 5.2 based on the N-terminal and C-terminal DJ-1/PfpI domains of Moso bamboo, A. thaliana, O. sativa, G. max, and M. truncatula proteins (Fig. 5b). The resulting tree resolved into four major clades (I–IV). Clade I contained AtDJ-1 A and AtDJ-1B, which are said to have a stronger specificity for GO but a weaker specificity for MG. Interestingly, oxidative stress response is linked to AtDJ-1 A [78, 79]. This suggests that PhGLYIII-1, PhGLYIII-4, PhGLYIII-6, PhGLYIII-11, and PhGLYIII-15 (Clade I) may similarly display weak glyoxalase activity or have alternative functions. Clade II contained heat-inducible molecular chaperones (Hsp31), such as AtDJ-1D and OsDJ-1 C, previously identified as primary GLYIII enzymes [33, 72, 73]. PhGLYIII-9 is hypothesized to be a prominent GLYIII enzyme within this lineage, warranting further investigation. Clade III, containing proteins like AtDJ-1E and AtDJ-1 F, is characterized by lower levels of MG-specific GLYIII activity and an inability to utilize GO [72], suggesting that PhGLYIII-2, PhGLYIII-4, PhGLYIII-5, PhGLYIII-6, PhGLYIII-8, and PhGLYIII-12, which belong to this clade, similarly do not utilize GO as a substrate (Fig. 5b). Clade IV includes OsDJ-1 C and AtDJ-1D, which exhibit higher GLYIII activity compared to other family members, with the ability to use both MG and GO as substrates, showing greater activity with GO than MG [33, 72, 80]. This indicates that Clade IV consists of catalytically more efficient PhGLYIII proteins, including PhGLYIII-1, PhGLYIII-5, PhGLYIII-7, PhGLYIII-10, PhGLYIII-13, and PhGLYIII-14 (Fig. 5b).

Co-expression and CREs analysis of PhGLYs

According to [28], cis-acting regulatory elements (CAREs) are essential aspects of gene transcriptional control that mediate reactions to internal and external stimuli. Using the PlantCARE database, we discovered 36 different kinds of CAREs in the 2-kb upstream promoter regions of the PhGLY genes (Fig. 6a). These CAREs were categorized into four main groups according to their biological roles: (1) elements connected to the stress response, (2) elements associated to development, (3) elements responsive to light and (4) elements related to the hormone response (Fig. 6b). With 496 elements (33.82%), stress-responsive elements were the most prevalent among them. ARE, CAAT, DRE-core, F-box, LTR, MBS, MYB, W box, AE box, and GC-motif were important stress-related components. These elements were followed by hormone-related (396 elements, 26.99%), light-responsive (327 elements, 22.29%), and development-related elements (248 elements, 16.90%) (Fig. 6b). The abundance of stress-responsive elements indicates that PhGLY genes are involved in a variety of biological processes and plant responses to biotic and abiotic stressors. Figure 6a provides a gene-by-gene breakdown of CARE counts across all PhGLY family members. Each cell reports the number of occurrences of a given element (e.g., ARE, LTR, Box-5) in the promoter region of each gene. Figure 6b aggregates those per-gene counts across the entire family, grouping elements into four functional classes (stress-responsive, hormone-related, light-responsive, and development-related). Each bar in Fig. 6b therefore represents the sum of that CARE across all PhGLYs (with the total number placed atop each bar).

The PlantCARE database provides information on broad cis-acting regulatory elements; however, it does not specify the TFs regulating the GLY genes. To address this limitation, we employed the PTFSpot deep learning tool to identify specific TF interactions with these genes. Subsequent analysis revealed PhGLY genes. The lengths of these cis-elements ranged from 5 to 18 bp, with the most prevalent elements situated within the 2 kb upstream promoter regions. Among the identified TFs were ABF1, ABI5, ATF1, bHLH, bZIP14, CAMTA, FHY3, MYB46, SOS3, and HSP101. Using PTFSpot, a highly accurate deep learning method for detecting TF binding regions, we identified 149 TF binding sites within the 2-kb upstream promoter regions of five PhGLY genes (Fig. 6a, b). PhDJ-1B.1 showed the highest number of binding regions, particularly with bZIP14, MYB46, and HSP101 (4 binding regions each), indicating extensive regulatory involvement with these TFs. PhDJ-1E displayed strong associations with SOS3 (4 regions) and bZIP14 (5 regions). PhGLYII-15 showed moderate binding across multiple TFs, with its strongest interactions observed in bHLH (6 regions) and MYB46 (3 regions). PhGLYII-3 had relatively lower binding, with notable interactions in bHLH and CAMTA (3 regions each). PhGLYI-3 showed moderate binding primarily with ABI5 (4 regions) and ATF1 (2 regions). This suggests differential regulatory roles among the genes, with PhDJ-1B.1 and PhDJ-1E demonstrating the most extensive TF binding, indicative of their potentially pivotal roles in transcriptional regulation.

The expression correlation of TFs with those Gly genes showing their binding regions was further examined (≥|0.6|). In order to conduct co-expression analysis, expression data for four distinct conditions—drought, salinity, cold, and heavy metals—were obtained from the bambooNET database, as was mentioned in the “Methods” section. The association between a TF’s expression levels and its target gene in various samples or experimental settings is known as the expression correlation. A functional link, which may be a sign of regulatory interactions, is suggested when the expression levels of a TF and its target gene tend to changes in a coordinated way under various conditions. A total of 97 TFs-gene combinations were found (Fig. 7a-d), and these TFs-genes interaction were represented in the form of TF regulatory network as shown in Fig. 7a. PhGLYI-3 showed a strong positive correlation with SOS3 (0.96), while demonstrating mixed correlations with other TFs, such as bZIP14 (0.58), and a slight negative correlation with HSP101 (−0.28). PhGLYII-3 had a marginally positive connection with SOS3 (0.60) and a substantial negative correlation with Myb46 (−0.96). PhGLYII-15 showed moderate to high positive correlations with bHLH (0.78) and Myb46 (0.67) (Fig. 7d). PhDJ-1E exhibited strong positive correlations with bHLH (0.93), CAMTA (0.94), and ABI5 (0.89), indicating significant regulatory interactions, though it had a strong negative correlation with ABF1 (−0.88). PhDJ-1B.1 exhibited a moderate positive connection with Myb46 (0.74) but a strong negative correlation with CAMTA (−0.96) and bZIP14 (−0.86). These patterns highlight varying degrees of regulatory influence between these genes and TFs (Fig. 7d).

Plant material growth conditions and abiotic stress treatments

In this study, in-vitro regenerated plants of P. pubescens cultured in BAP (9µM) and metatopolin (1µM) were taken for validating the expression of glyoxalase genes. Morphological parameters

After 7 and 14 days of treatment under various abiotic stress conditions, morphological differences among plants were observed, with prolonged exposure to higher levels of stress resulting in plantlet mortality. Under cold stress, P. pubescens plants demonstrated tolerance at 15 °C and 4 °C. However, at 0 °C, leaves began to turn yellowish brown after 14 days, indicating stress. In the case of heavy metal stress, high dosages were lethal to the plants, whereas lower concentrations (100–200 µM) were tolerated, although chlorosis (leaf yellowing) was observed even at these lower levels (Fig. 9). For drought stress, mannitol concentrations above 150 mM proved highly damaging to in vitro plants; at 100 mM, leaf yellowing occurred, which subsequently progressed to the formation of brown and black spots. Under salinity stress, NaCl concentrations exceeding 200 mM were extremely toxic and ultimately lethal for the plants. The selected treatments for subsequent molecular analyses included cold stress (0 °C for 14 days), heavy metal stress (200 µM chromium ion concentration), drought stress (150 mM), and salinity stress (200 mM). These concentrations were chosen as they represent the threshold at which plants exhibited significant stress responses (Fig. 9).

Temporal-spatial and stress-induced expression pattern analysis

Analysis of tissue and stress specific expression profiles provided valuable insights into the potential functions of genes in plant species. Distinct spatial expression patterns were observed for the glyoxalase by using the publicly available RNA-seq [28, 84] data from 6 different tissues and 4 different abiotic stress (Fig. 10). Within the PhGLYI family, one cluster (PhGLYI-4, −1, −2, −10, −7, −8) is highly expressed in root and bud (FPKM > 4), a second cluster (PhGLYI-12, −9, −19, −11, −13, −5, −14, −15) shows minimal expression (FPKM < 1) across all six tissues, and a third (PhGLYI-17, −16, −18, −6, −3) is moderately expressed in leaf and sheath (FPKM ≈ 1–3). PhGLYII genes split into a shoot/leaf-favored group (e.g. PhGLYII-12, −11, −14; FPKM 2–3.5), a rhizome-enriched subgroup (PhGLYII-1, −3, −16; FPKM ≈ 3), and several low-expression members (FPKM < 1). Among PhGLYIII, a root/bud-preferential set (PhGLYIII-7, −5, −9; FPKM > 4), a broad low-expression cluster (PhGLYIII-12, −13, −10; FPKM < 1), and a moderately expressed group (PhGLYIII-8, −14, −3; FPKM 1–3) were identified (Supplementary Table S12).

In PhGLYII, PhGLYII-3/−7 show the highest salt-induced expression (FPKM > 4), while PhGLYII-1/−9 respond to ABA (FPKM > ~ 2.5). These patterns pinpoint PhGLYI-4, PhGLYII-3, and PhGLYIII-3/−10 as prime candidates for functional studies on bamboo’s abiotic stress tolerance.

The P. pubescens PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII glyoxalase genes expressed differently under cold, heavy metal, drought, and salinity stress conditions. The results show stress-specific responses that help to understand how glyoxalase enzymes function to reduce oxidative damage and maintain cellular homeostasis under stress conditions.

PhGLYI Gene Family

Cold stress triggered strong upregulation of PhGLYI-8 (4.44-fold), PhGLYI-3 (3.97-fold), and PhGLYI- 6 (2.93-fold), indicating these enzymes play a vital role in neutralizing the MG rise and related oxidative stress observed in low temperature conditions. The increase in gene expression indicates higher glycolytic flux and cellular detoxification requirements that protect proteins and membranes from denaturation by cold temperatures. PhGLYI-9 (0.80-fold) and PhGLYI-10 (0.66-fold) expression remained low during cold stress, indicating that not all PhGLYI isoforms are equally important for cold stress tolerance because of differences in promoter responsiveness and post-transcriptional regulation. Heavy metal stress led to the upregulation of PhGLYI-15 (2.98-fold), and PhGLYI-8 (2.24-fold) while PhGLYI-3 and PhGLYI-9 showed significant downregulation. Heavy metals like chromium that produce ROS through oxidative stress preferentially activate detoxification-specific isoforms, which suppress other less efficient or redundant ones. The drought stress regulation of PhGLYI-8 (5.42-fold) and PhGLYI-15 (4.21-fold) indicates their importance in maintaining osmotic stability and protecting against oxidative damage during water scarcity. The transcripts of PhGLYI-10 and PhGLYI-11 show non-significant changes which suggests their reduced significance during dehydration possibly because their functional activity becomes less important under water deficit conditions. Salinity stress resulted in the significant upregulation of PhGLYI-8 (4.49-fold), PhGLYI-5 (3.21-fold), PhGLYI-10 (3.06-fold), and PhGLYI-15 (3.04-fold), indicating these isoforms collectively participate in an extensive defense mechanism to stabilize ionic and osmotic homeostasis. PhGLYI-6 expression decreases by −0.07-fold during salt stress, which demonstrates the modified stress response through selective isoform recruitment (Fig. 11).

PhGLYII Gene Family

The PhGLYII gene family expressed under different stresses which demonstrates its participation in multiple regulatory and detoxification processes. The cold stress response includes PhGLYII-2 at 6.56-fold followed by PhGLYII-18 at 5.23-fold and PhGLYII-10 at 5.19-fold. The high expression levels of glyoxalase II enzymes under cold conditions indicate their crucial role in detoxifying MG while protecting against ROS damage because they convert S-D-lactoylglutathione to D-lactate. The heavy metal stress response shows PhGLYII-10 (5.23-fold) and PhGLYII-12 (5.09-fold) alongside PhGLYII-2 (4.33-fold) as major responders which demonstrates their protective function against metal-generated oxidative stress. The glyoxalase II pathway proves essential for preserving cellular redox stability in toxic metal exposure environments because these genes show continuous strong expression levels. The top PhGLYII-12 (5.94-fold), PhGLYII-15 (5.44-fold), and PhGLYII-4 (5.19-fold) responders during drought stress highlight the need for rapid MG detoxification when water scarcity accelerates metabolic stress and ROS accumulation. PhGLYII-2 (5.92-fold), PhGLYII-3 (5.34-fold) and PhGLYII-18 (4.46-fold) show high regulation during salinity stress which demonstrates their essential role in managing salt-induced ionic disturbances and osmotic imbalance. PhGLYII-2 remains a dominant member of the stress response network in P. pubescens across different stress conditions (Fig. 11).

PhGLYIII/PhDJ-1 Gene Family

The PhGLYIII/PhDJ-1 gene family shows stress-specific expression patterns that match the particular needs of each stress situation. The substantial upregulation of PhGLYIII-6 (5.66-fold), PhGLYIII-4 (5.05-fold), and PhGLYIII-3 (4.88-fold) under cold stress reveals their essential role in stopping protein damage and scavenging ROS and possibly using their chaperone capabilities in addition to their enzymatic activity. PhGLYIII-3 shows exceptional regulation under heavy metal stress at 7.10-fold because it functions as a key enzyme for quick ROS detoxification which protects vital cellular components. The significant increases in PhGLYIII-7 (4.76-fold) and PhGLYIII-6 (4.00-fold) show that the glyoxalase III system works in conjunction with other antioxidant pathways to detoxify MG while reducing ROS accumulation and protecting cellular integrity. PhGLYIII-7 shows the highest upregulation at 6.14-fold under drought stress followed by PhGLYIII-3 at 5.91-fold and PhGLYIII-5 at 4.87-fold. The enzymes function to decrease MG-induced oxidative stress while maintaining osmotic stability and reducing ROS-generated cellular damage under water scarcity conditions.

In contrast, under salinity stress, while PhGLYIII-5 (4.88-fold), PhGLYIII-4 (4.26-fold), and PhGLYIII-2 (4.15-fold) are significantly upregulated, the relatively lower expression of PhGLYIII-7 (1.00-fold) and PhGLYIII-15 (0.52-fold) suggests that only a subset of the PhGLYIII family is involved in countering salt-induced ROS. This indicates a stress-specific regulatory mechanism, where different members of the PhGLYIII family contribute to redox homeostasis based on environmental conditions (Fig. 11).

The expression patterns of PhGLYI, PhGLYII and PhGLYIII/PhDJ-1 genes in P. pubescens show a complex, dynamic and stress-specific interplay that highlights the multifaceted nature of glyoxalase-mediated detoxification. The tested stress conditions did not result in detectable expression or regulation of the isoforms PhGLYI-12, PhGLYI-13, PhGLYI-14, PhGLYI-16, and PhGLYI-19, as well as PhGLYII-13, PhGLYII-14, and PhGLYIII-9. The data show that certain isoforms, notably PhGLYI-8, PhGLYII-2, and PhGLYIII-3/PhGLYIII-6, are consistently upregulated under multiple stresses, indicating their crucial roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis under different environmental challenges. The MG detoxification and ROS-scavenging pathways contain the core components of Glyoxalase family members, which activate universally under different abiotic stresses. The stress tolerance mechanisms of P. pubescens are robust, and the continuous upregulation of most PhGLY genes matches its adaptive response to harsh environments. Brassica napus and Avena sativa have shown similar broad upregulation patterns when exposed to combined stresses [85, 86]. The differential expression across stresses provides compelling evidence of evolutionary specialization, with each family—and even individual isoforms within each family—tailoring their response to specific stress signals such as cold-induced metabolic slowdown, metal-induced oxidative bursts, water deficit-driven osmotic imbalances, or salt-induced ionic disruptions. This detailed comparative analysis not only enhances our understanding of the glyoxalase system’s contribution to stress tolerance in P. pubescens but also identifies promising candidate genes for future research aimed at engineering enhanced stress resilience in plants.

Discussion

The glyoxalase enzyme family functions as essential components in plant biology to manage stress responses, seed germination, senescence, nutrient regulation, signal transduction, starch biosynthesis, and pollen development [5, 7, 87]. The enzymes function to detoxify methylglyoxal (MG) which forms as a toxic compound under abiotic stresses such as salinity, drought, heavy metals, low temperatures that damage plant growth and development [7, 88]. MG forms through various pathways and remains present in stressed plant tissues. Plants use glyoxalases to transform MG into less toxic substances which protects them from oxidative damage. The plant kingdom contains three glyoxalase gene family members known as GLYI, GLYII, and GLYIII which exhibit different cellular locations, stress adaptation functions, and expression patterns [5, 15, 88]. To understand their stress tolerance function, researchers need to conduct genome-wide studies that analyze chromosomal distribution and evolutionary relationships, conserved domains, cis-regulatory elements, and expression patterns to identify their potential for plant resilience enhancement.

The GSH-dependent pathway consists of GLYI and GLYII enzymes which act as the primary system for MG detoxification throughout plant tissues. The extensive research on these gene families has resulted in the discovery of GLYI and GLYII enzymes throughout various plant species. The genome of Arabidopsis thaliana contains 11 GLYI and 5 GLYII genes but Oryza sativa has 11 GLYI and 3 GLYII genes [55]. Researchers have found 24 GLYI and 12 GLYII genes within Glycine max while Sorghum bicolor contains 15 GLYI and 6 GLYII genes [14, 15]. The GSH-independent GLYIII family gained plant research attention only during recent years [15]. has identified some GLYIII genes although systematic characterization remains limited. The Swiss-Prot database search using the AtDJ-1d (AT3g02720) protein sequence revealed 217 GLYIII genes spread across Oryza sativa and other species including, Triticum aestivum, Hordeum vulgare, Zea mays, Sorghum bicolor, Setaria italica, and Brachypodium distachyon [33]. showed that among these 12 O. sativa GLYIII genes became activated when exposed to MG [33]. Studies of 183 GLYIII genes spread across 69 species have revealed important information about their evolutionary background [80]. The scientific investigation of plant GLYIII genes has become a critical research direction. The researchers identified and classified members of the GLYI, GLYII, and GLYIII gene families in P. pubescens through sequence similarity analysis of their domains to establish the groundwork for future studies on their cellular distribution and possible biological functions.

The majority of PhGLYI proteins were predicted to exist in the cytoplasm, yet smaller amounts were present in the nucleus, mitochondria, and chloroplasts (Supplementary Table S5). The PhGLYII and PhGLYIII proteins predominantly predicted to reside in the mitochondria and chloroplasts according to Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Table S3. Chloroplasts and mitochondria as crucial organelles responsible for respiration and photosynthesis generate ROS during MG metabolism [89]. Glyoxal-derived advanced glycation end products (AGEs) frequently occur in stressed plant components of mitochondria and chloroplasts yet excessive MG leads to impaired photosynthesis and mitochondrial dysfunction [90]. The nuclear presence of SbGlyI8/8.1 in sorghum along with OsGLYI8 in rice demonstrates how glyoxalase enzymes in Moso bamboo protect photosynthetic systems and mitochondrial structures when exposed to stress. The observed compartmentalization demonstrates a coordinated protective system in which glyoxalases operate as sentinels throughout cellular compartments to combat MG-induced damage thus requiring further investigation in bamboo and woody perennial species.

The subcellular localization analysis demonstrates that GlyI genes exist in multiple cellular compartments which include cytoplasm (PhGLYI-1, PhGLYI-2, PhGLYI-4, PhGLYI-7, PhGLYI-10, PhGLYI-11), mitochondria (PhGLYI-2, PhGLYI-3, PhGLYI-6) and chloroplasts (PhGLYI-3, PhGLYI-5, PhGLYI-6, PhGLYI-8, PhGLYI-15) and the nucleus (PhGLYI-9) indicating their different roles in MG detoxification. The expression of PhGLYI-3, PhGLYI-6, PhGLYI-8, and PhGLYI-15 is highest in chloroplasts which is evident from our analysis and suggests their function in protecting photosynthesis under abiotic stress. GlyII genes which have THXHXDH and C/GHT motifs in their structure retain glyoxalase function with PhGLYII-1, PhGLYII-2, PhGLYII-4, PhGLYII-7, and PhGLYII-11 showing maximum induction under cold, drought, and salinity conditions. The localization patterns show that GlyII enzymes are mainly located in the cytoplasm (PhGLYII-1, PhGLYII-3, PhGLYII-9), mitochondria (PhGLYII-2, PhGLYII-4, PhGLYII-5, PhGLYII-7) and plastidial (PhGLYII-16, PhGLYII-17) compartments indicating a wide range of functions in various cellular settings. The GlyIII family (DJ-1 homologs), characterized by their multifunctional MG detoxification capabilities, shows different subcellular locations and stress-related gene expression. PhGLYIII-3, PhGLYIII-5, and PhGLYIII-6 exhibit significant upregulation under heavy metal, drought, and salinity stress. Their presence in the cytoplasm (PhGLYIII-6, PhGLYIII-9), mitochondria (PhGLYIII-3, PhGLYIII-7), and plasma membrane (PhGLYIII-2) suggests a role in metal ion homeostasis and oxidative stress response.

In rice, glyoxalase pathway components have been extensively studied, with OsGLYI-11.2 encoding a Ni²⁺-dependent glyoxalase I enzyme that is inducible by MG, suggesting its regulatory role in stress modulation [5]. These findings underscore the importance of glyoxalase-mediated MG detoxification in plant stress responses, necessitating further comparative analysis of glyoxalase regulation in rice and sorghum under different environmental conditions. The functional significance of metal ion dependency in glyoxalase I enzymes is another critical aspect of glyoxalase regulation. Studies in Arabidopsis thaliana have identified both Zn²⁺-dependent and Ni²⁺-dependent GLYI enzymes, with AtGLYI2 (Zn²⁺-dependent) exhibiting a more prominent role in stress tolerance than its Ni²⁺-dependent counterparts [33]. The differential regulation of Zn²⁺- and Ni²⁺-dependent glyoxalases across plant species suggests that metal ion specificity may influence their enzymatic activity under stress conditions. Beyond cytosolic glyoxalase activity, organellar glyoxalases contribute significantly to stress adaptation. A study by [91] provides direct evidence of GLYI activity in highly purified mitochondrial fractions from durum wheat seedlings (Triticum durum). The researchers observed that under hyper-osmotic stress conditions, there was an increase up to 55% in mitochondrial GLYI activity, suggesting its active involvement in MG detoxification within mitochondria during such stress. Understanding how glyoxalases localized in mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes are regulated under different abiotic stresses will be crucial in elucidating their functional significance.

The P. pubescens glyoxalase gene family shows different expression patterns when exposed to various abiotic stresses while maintaining distinct Ni²⁺- and Zn²⁺-dependent GlyI gene expression. The Ni²⁺-dependent members PhGLYI-1, PhGLYI-2, PhGLYI-3, PhGLYI-7, PhGLYI-9, PhGLYI-11, PhGLYI-13, PhGLYI-15, and PhGLYI-18 contain conserved H/QEH/QE motifs in their active sites which indicate their function in methylglyoxal (MG) detoxification during stress conditions. The genes show increased expression mainly during cold, drought and salinity stresses which demonstrates their importance in protecting against oxidative damage from MG accumulation. The Zn²⁺-dependent GlyI genes PhGLYI-5, PhGLYI-8, PhGLYI-17 have different active site residues which differ from the typical H/QEH/QE motif. The upregulation of these Zn²⁺-dependent genes in response to heavy metal stress suggests a potential role in metal chelation and detoxification.

The combined analysis of metal dependency, subcellular localization, and stress-induced expression patterns indicates a compartmentalized and specialized function of the glyoxalase family in P. pubescens. Ni²⁺-dependent GlyI genes predominantly function in MG detoxification under abiotic stress [13], while Zn²⁺-dependent members are potentially involved in metal stress adaptation [92]. The significant upregulation of GlyII and GlyIII genes in response to salinity, drought, and heavy metal stress further suggests their role in cellular redox homeostasis.

Phylogenetic analyses of PhGLYI, PhGLYII, and PhGLYIII proteins from Moso bamboo and other plant species provide significant insights into the evolutionary diversification and functional specialization of the glyoxalase system across monocots and dicots. The glyoxalase pathway, comprising GLYI, GLYII, and GLYIII enzymes, is critical for detoxifying cytotoxic MG and GO, which accumulate under abiotic and biotic stress conditions [7, 33]. Our findings reveal that the evolutionary trajectories of these gene families predate the monocot-dicot divergence, with distinct clades reflecting both conserved and divergent functional roles.

The abundance of stress-responsive cis-acting regulatory elements (CAREs) in the 2-kb upstream regions of PhGLY genes (33.82% of total elements) underscores their role in abiotic stress tolerance. Elements such as ARE, DRE-core, MBS, and MYB are known to mediate responses to drought, salinity, and oxidative stress in plants [93]. The co-expression analysis and transcription factor binding predictions further illuminate how transcriptional regulation fine-tunes the glyoxalase system in Moso bamboo. For instance, the strong positive correlation between PhGLYI-3 and SOS3 (0.96) suggests a salt-stress-specific regulatory axis, consistent with SOS3’s role in ion homeostasis [94]. Similarly, PhDJ-1E’s high correlations with bHLH (0.93), CAMTA (0.94), and ABI5 (0.89) implicate these TFs in integrating calcium, abscisic acid (ABA), and developmental signals—key pathways in bamboo’s rapid growth and stress resilience [60].

The identification of overlapping TF binding sites (e.g., ABI5, SOS3, bZIP14) suggests cooperative or competitive regulation, a mechanism widely observed in grasses with complex genomes [59]. Cooperative binding, such as bZIP14-MYB46 complexes, could enhance transcription under combinatorial stresses [81], while competitive binding between ABI5 and bHLH may enable dynamic switching between stress repression and growth promotion [82]. These regulatory hubs likely evolved to manage MG and GO detoxification during Moso bamboo’s rapid vegetative propagation, a process generating high metabolic flux and ROS [58].

Diversification of PhGLY genes and their transcriptional regulation reflect Moso bamboo’s adaptation to diverse ecological niches and stressors. The Ni²⁺- and Zn²⁺-dependent GLYI isoforms, SDO-active GLYII variants, and catalytically efficient GLYIII enzymes collectively enhance the plant’s capacity to mitigate carbonyl stress, a critical factor in its perennial growth and biomass accumulation [13, 80]. The extensive TF interactions, particularly with stress-responsive elements, position the glyoxalase system as a central player in bamboo’s resilience, warranting functional validation of key candidates like PhGLYIII-9 and PhDJ-1B.1 via gene expression and enzymatic assays. Future studies should also explore the structural basis of metal dependency in GLYI clades and the in vivo roles of overlapping TF binding under specific stress conditions.

Our study revealed that cold stress triggered a major increase in several glyoxalase genes including PhGLYI-8, PhGLYII-2, PhGLYII-9 and PhGLYIII-6. The established function of glyoxalases in detoxifying MG under stress conditions through oxidative stress mitigation matches the findings of this study. The increase of ROS under cold stress causes cellular damage but glyoxalase detoxification systems help protect redox balance. The high expression levels of these genes indicate their active role in protecting against ROS-induced damage during cold stress. These findings are in agreement with previous studies in oats and rice, where glyoxalase genes were found to be upregulated in response to cold stress, enhancing redox homeostasis [95]. Similarly, in Avena sativa, glyoxalase genes such as AsGLYII-5D and AsDJ-1-5D exhibited significant induction under low-temperature conditions, highlighting their conserved role in abiotic stress responses [96]. Studies have shown a direct relationship between glyoxalase gene regulation and ROS homeostasis in Poaceae members. The expression of glyoxalase gene (AsGLYI) in Avena sativa correlated with seed vigor but its decline resulted in increased ROS accumulation during seed aging [97]. The antioxidant system and glyoxalase activities in Oryza sativa increased after silicon treatment which reduced ROS levels and enhanced stress tolerance [98]. The research shows that glyoxalase gene regulation functions as a key mechanism to control ROS levels when plants experience stress. The upregulation of PhGLYI-6, PhGLYII-12 and PhGLYIII-3 indicates their role in metal detoxification through chelation, compartmentalization, and ROS scavenging pathways. PhGLYI-3 and PhGLYI-9 showed downregulation which indicates different regulatory patterns and possible metabolic trade-offs when plants face toxic ion stress. Heavy metals including chromium, cadmium and arsenic produce ROS, which results in oxidative stress [99, 100]. The observed upregulation of glyoxalase genes under heavy metal stress indicates a protective function to decrease cellular toxicity and improve plant survival, as demonstrated in previous studies on rice and other species [95]. The induction of PhGLYII-12 and PhGLYIII-3 in bamboo under heavy metal stress suggests their potential role in enhancing tolerance mechanisms through MG detoxification and antioxidant defences.

The drought stress response led to increased expression of PhGLYI-8, PhGLYII-4 and PhGLYIII-7 genes as indicators of their participation in osmo-protection and ROS detoxification processes. The high levels of PhGLYIII-7 indicate its leading role in protecting against oxidative stress during water scarcity. The glyoxalase system in wheat and other crops enhances drought tolerance through improved MG detoxification and stress-responsive transcriptional regulation [101, 102] [103]. suggests glyoxalase genes perform conserved drought resilience functions in bamboo plants through WRKY-dependent transcriptional regulation [103]. Salinity stress resulted in a moderate increase of PhGLYI-5, PhGLYII-6, and PhGLYIII-5 expression levels along with a slight decrease in PhGLYIII-7 expression. The stress-specific functional divergence of glyoxalases indicates that different members of this enzyme family participate in ion homeostasis and ROS mitigation, while others show reduced activity levels. Plants require effective detoxification mechanisms to survive oxidative stress from salinity, and glyoxalases have been proven to support cellular stability in barley and rice crops [104]. The lesser extent of glyoxalase responses under salinity stress compared to cold and heavy metal stress did not prevent PhGLYI-5 and PhGLYIII-5 from showing their ability to preserve cellular homeostasis and control ion transport.

The glyoxalase genes showed their greatest upregulation under cold and heavy metal stress conditions with PhGLYI-8, PhGLYII-2, PhGLYIII-3, and PhGLYII-9 displaying the most significant increases among all stress conditions. These findings support earlier research which showed glyoxalases function as stress adaptation mechanisms [95]. The study identified PhGLYIII-7 as a promising candidate for drought tolerance, while PhGLYIII-3 showed potential for phytoremediation purposes. The different stress-specific gene expression patterns indicate specialized functions among the glyoxalase family members, which emphasizes the necessity for studying each gene to enhance bamboo stress resistance. Additional research through promoter analysis and functional verification will help understand the specific regulatory pathways that control glyoxalase-dependent stress responses in bamboo plants. The research shows the glyoxalase system plays an essential part in plant responses to abiotic stress by protecting against MG toxicity and maintaining mitochondrial stability. The substantial increase in glyoxalase gene expression indicates their role in improving stress tolerance and plant survival. Thus, in future key glyoxalase genes and their transcription factors can be functionally validated through gene knockout or overexpression analysis, which will provide further insights. Based on our in depth analysis and previous studies on other crops, we hypothesize that ABI5 and SOS3 are major abiotic stress responsive transcription factors modulating glyoxalase pathway. Future research on genetic engineering combined with CRISPR-mediated gene editing will confirm their functional response during abiotic stress in this economically important species.

Conclusions

P. pubescens has evolved a complete defense system to deal with abiotic stress. PhGLYI-8, PhGLYI-15 and PhGLYIII-7 glyoxalase genes demonstrated elevated expression under drought stress, while PhGLYIII-3 and PhGLYII-12 genes showed increased expression under heavy metal stress which indicates their essential function in detoxifying MG to protect cells from AGEs formation and oxidative damage and maintain homeostasis (Fig. 12). The two proteins ABI5 and SOS3 function as primary regulators of abiotic stress by managing osmotic and oxidative stress responses. The bZIP14–ABI5–SOS3 regulatory hub works together with CAMTA- and MYB46-mediated control of GLY genes to integrate calcium and redox signaling pathways for improved abiotic stress tolerance. The glyoxalase system plays a vital role in stress resilience as it helps manage oxidative stress during extreme environmental conditions. The expanded glyoxalase gene families in bamboo resulting from genome duplication create valuable targets for breeding stress-resistant crops. These findings provide essential information for future studies to characterize glyoxalase gene functions, which can guide breeding and biotechnological methods to enhance plant stress tolerance.
