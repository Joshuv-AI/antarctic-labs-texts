Analysis of hybrid CNN models optimized with metaheuristic algorithms for melanoma detection
Authors: Pamela Hermosilla, Ricardo Soto, Eric Monfroy, Emanuel Vega, Cristian Suazo, Lucas Erazo, Broderick Crawford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelanoma is one of the most aggressive forms of skin cancer, with a high mortality rate when not detected early. This public health challenge underscores the need for accurate and efficient diagnostic tools. Convolutional Neural Networks have shown strong performance in medical image analysis. However, their effectiveness relies heavily on optimal architectural and hyperparameter configurations, which are often designed without alignment to the target domain or transferred from unrelated domains, limiting adaptability to specific medical datasets. Existing hybrid CNN-metaheuristic approaches typically optimize only fixed network parameters. They often fail to explore how metaheuristics can adaptively shape the CNN architectures themselves.In this study, a comprehensive hybrid optimization framework is proposed that integrates CNNs with six nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms that mimic biological or physical phenomena to solve complex problems. These include Cuckoo Search, Firefly Algorithm, Whale Optimization Algorithm, Particle Swarm Optimization, Grey Wolf Optimizer, and Crow Search Algorithm. Rather than tuning a predefined architecture, each optimizer searches the architectural and training space to identify high-performing CNN configurations, enabling emergent and data-driven network design. This unified framework allows a systematic cross-algorithm comparison under identical conditions, providing new insights into convergence stability, exploration-exploitation dynamics, and generalization behavior. A robust preprocessing and data augmentation pipeline, including brightness normalization, hair artifact removal, and geometric transformations, is incorporated to improve model generalization and enhance the optimizer’s search landscape. Experiments on the HAM10000 dataset demonstrate that the metaheuristic-optimized CNNs outperform the baseline, achieving accuracies up to 91.25%. These findings confirm that population-based optimization is an efficient and reliable mechanism for guiding CNN architecture design. This approach achieves superior performance compared to traditional manual or other optimization-based strategies.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Over the past decade, the identification and classification of skin conditions, particularly those associated with cancer, have become critical areas of research within the field of machine learning (ML). Among the various types of skin cancer, melanoma accounts for the majority of skin cancer-related deaths due to its tendency to spread rapidly to adjacent tissues and distant organs. Its high metastatic potential makes missed diagnoses (false negatives) particularly dangerous. A missed diagnosis, known as a false negative, has severe clinical consequences. Unlike a false positive, which may lead to an unnecessary biopsy, a false negative delays treatment. This delay allows the cancer to progress to more advanced stages where it can metastasize to other organs, drastically reducing survival rates. Therefore, the sensitivity of a diagnostic system, its ability to minimize false negatives is not just a performance metric but a critical clinical requirement for improving patient prognosis. To address this challenge, a wide range of computational techniques have been proposed1. These include early image comparison strategies2, the application of CNNs for image-based classification3, and more recently, the integration of advanced optimization techniques aimed at fine-tuning CNN architectures4,5. The field of optimization algorithms has evolved significantly over the past decades, giving rise to metaheuristics: problem solving methods inspired by natural or social behaviors. These algorithms are designed to find high-quality solutions in complex problems where traditional optimization techniques often fail. Their main strength lies in balancing the exploration of new possibilities with the refinement of the best solutions found so far, making them particularly effective for tuning deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). For instance, recent studies have explored enhancing physics-inspired optimizers by integrating chaotic maps to improve the identification of complex biomedical systems6. Concurrently, newly introduced algorithms, such as the Gazelle Optimization Algorithm, are being successfully applied to solve similarly challenging parameter estimation problems7. This dynamic landscape underscores the continuous search for more robust and efficient optimization methods. Deep learning models, particularly CNNs, have demonstrated remarkable success in medical imaging by effectively bridging the gap between human and machine perception8. A convolutional neural network model that integrates a novel regularization strategy for melanoma detection achieved strong classification performance9. These findings highlight the importance of combining architectural improvements with optimization techniques tailored to clinical needs, reinforcing the pivotal role of CNNs in advancing diagnostic accuracy, supporting disease monitoring, and enabling personalized treatment strategies across various medical domains10. One emerging direction to further improve CNN performance involves metaheuristic based optimization. Metaheuristic algorithms have been widely applied to complex optimization problems across science, engineering, and industry11. When employed to optimize CNN parameters, they have demonstrated the ability to enhance model accuracy and reduce training time12. The synergy between CNNs and metaheuristic algorithms, defined as optimization strategies inspired by natural phenomena such as swarm intelligence or evolutionary processes, has proven effective across a variety of domains.

Within the medical domain, the synergy between Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and metaheuristic algorithms (MHs) has been validated in several critical tasks. For example, Cuckoo Search (CS) has demonstrated improved accuracy in lung cancer classification and activity recognition tasks13. Firefly Algorithm (FA) has enhanced classification performance in melanoma detection and brain tumor segmentation14. Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) has been applied successfully in the optimization of CNNs for hyperspectral and dermoscopic image classification15. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) has improved precision and recall in skin lesion detection16, while the Crow Search Algorithm (CSA) has delivered strong results in feature selection and hybrid model optimization for image classification in clinical contexts17. These diverse applications emphasize the versatility, generalization capabilities, and strong potential of metaheuristic algorithms to enhance CNN optimization in both general purpose and clinical image analysis scenarios.Unlike traditional grid or random search techniques, metaheuristic algorithms offer a more efficient and scalable means of navigating complex, high-dimensional hyperparameter spaces18.

Research gap

Although numerous studies have applied metaheuristic algorithms to optimize CNN architectures in medical image analysis, the existing literature remains fragmented and inconsistent. Most prior works evaluate a single metaheuristic in isolation, using heterogeneous datasets and varying experimental conditions, which limits reproducibility and prevents fair comparison. Moreover, there is no standardized benchmark assessing how different metaheuristic strategies perform when applied to melanoma detection, a clinically critical task requiring both diagnostic accuracy and computational efficiency. Additionally, the influence of data preprocessing and augmentation techniques is often underestimated, despite their importance for improving model generalization and ensuring that CNN optimization strategies are evaluated under realistic imaging variability.

Despite the breadth of applications in this area, most studies analyze individual metaheuristics separately and under non-standardized conditions, which hinders systematic comparison and reproducibility19. Furthermore, while some research addresses skin cancer classification broadly, very few provide a direct, controlled comparison of multiple metaheuristics specifically for melanoma detection within a unified experimental framework20,21. This lack of a unified and reproducible evaluation protocol represents a critical research gap, limiting the ability to identify the most effective optimization strategy for high-stakes clinical applications. The present study is designed to fill this methodological void by providing a comprehensive and reproducible comparative evaluation of multiple metaheuristic algorithms under consistent experimental conditions.

Although previous works have demonstrated the potential of individual metaheuristic algorithms for CNN optimization, their relative strengths and weaknesses remain unclear due to the absence of controlled, side-by-side comparisons. This study addresses that need by presenting the first large-scale comparative analysis of six metaheuristic algorithms integrated with CNNs for melanoma classification, offering a unified performance benchmark that advances methodological rigor and clinical relevance.

Motivation

This study is motivated by the need to establish a unified and systematic framework for evaluating multiple metaheuristic algorithms under consistent experimental conditions. By focusing on melanoma, a domain where false negatives have severe clinical consequences, the research seeks to identify optimization strategies that not only improve model performance but also enhance clinical reliability. To ensure robustness and mitigate dataset bias, the framework integrates an advanced preprocessing and data augmentation pipeline that improves image quality, variability, and generalization. The study also addresses the practical challenge of balancing accuracy, interpretability, and computational efficiency in AI-assisted diagnostic systems.

Accordingly, this work aims to develop and evaluate a well-tuned CNN architecture optimized through metaheuristic algorithms for reliable and accurate melanoma classification. The methodological pipeline comprises three main stages: (i) data preprocessing and augmentation, (ii) hyperparameter tuning for both CNN and metaheuristics, and (iii) comparative evaluation using standard performance metrics.

This process enabled a rigorous and reproducible assessment of each algorithm’s optimization potential, identifying the most effective configurations for melanoma image classification. Statistical validation was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction to ensure robust inference. The study aims to identify which algorithms, such as Cuckoo Search (CS) or Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), emerge as top-performers.

Novel contribution

The present study introduces a comprehensive hybrid framework that integrates CNNs with six population-based metaheuristic algorithms (CS, FA, WOA, PSO, GWO, CSA) for hyperparameter optimization. Unlike previous works limited to single algorithm evaluations, this approach enables direct comparative analysis within a standardized environment. The framework incorporates robust preprocessing and data augmentation procedures to enhance input quality and model generalization, along with statistical validation (Wilcoxon test with Bonferroni correction) and Grad-CAM visualization. Collectively, these elements establish a reproducible benchmark, identify Cuckoo Search and Grey Wolf Optimizer as the most effective optimizers, and demonstrate a clinically relevant balance between performance, interpretability, and computational efficiency.

Related work

Metaheuristic algorithms constitute a broad family of stochastic optimization methods designed to effectively explore complex search spaces22. According to recent taxonomic studies in the field23,24, these algorithms are frequently organized into four primary categories based on their source of inspiration. The first category encompasses swarm-based algorithms, which model the collective intelligence and social interactions of organisms to solve optimization problems. Representative examples include Sperm Swarm Optimization (SSO), which utilizes a distributed search strategy where agents navigate the solution space guided by global and local best positions25. Similarly, the Artificial Protozoa Optimizer (APO) employs diverse foraging strategies to balance exploration and exploitation, allowing the population to adaptively switch between broad search and local refinement based on performance feedback26. The second group consists of physics-based algorithms, modeled after physical laws or dynamic phenomena. Recent developments in this area include the Chernobyl Disaster Optimizer (CDO), which simulates stochastic particle propagation mechanisms to drive agents from high-instability regions toward optimal stable states23. Additionally, the Bermuda Triangle Optimizer (BTO) leverages probabilistic attraction forces to guide candidate solutions toward the global optimum while maintaining diversity to avoid premature convergence27. A third category comprises evolutionary-based algorithms like the Genetic Algorithm (GA). Inspired by natural selection, GA relies on iterative operators such as mutation and crossover to evolve a population of candidate solutions, making it particularly robust for navigating non differentiable search landscapes28. Finally, man-made or human-based algorithms draw inspiration from human activities, social behaviors, or abstract concepts. A notable example is the Five Phases Algorithm (FPA), rooted in traditional philosophy. FPA models dynamic cycles of generation and overcoming to iteratively update agent positions, thereby enhancing the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima24.

Within this diverse landscape, the synergy between metaheuristics and deep learning models, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has attracted growing interest for medical image analysis. CNNs have emerged as the backbone of many diagnostic systems due to their strong ability to extract complex visual features. However, the high dimensionality and sensitivity of CNNs to their hyperparameters have motivated researchers to explore hybrid approaches, combining CNNs with various metaheuristics to improve accuracy, generalization, and training efficiency29–32.This trend includes not only the application of established algorithms but also the development of novel or modified metaheuristics tailored to specific diagnostic challenges. For instance, hybrid models combining CNNs with classifiers like XGBoost have been successfully tuned by modified versions of the Arithmetic Optimization Algorithm for COVID-19 detection from X-ray images33. Similarly, modified Variable Neighborhood Search and hybridized Elephant Herding Optimization have been employed to optimize CNN architectures for ocular disease diagnosis and glioma brain tumor classification, respectively34,35.Further extending this hybrid paradigm, other metaheuristics like the Genetic Algorithm have been employed to optimize different complex machine learning frameworks, such as Fuzzy Reinforcement Learning, for challenging bio-signal classification tasks. This approach has been effectively applied to classify epileptic seizures from EEG signals36 and to identify forearm movements from EMG data for advanced prosthesis design37.

In the field of medical image classification, evolutionary algorithms such as Cuckoo Search (CS) have proven highly effective. CS has demonstrated high effectiveness in cervical and lung cancer prediction by optimizing gene identification and feature extraction38,39. Furthermore, improved versions of the algorithm have successfully optimized Deep Belief Networks for imbalanced medical data, enhancing overall classification sensitivity and specificity13,40. The Crow Search Algorithm (CSA), inspired by the intelligent foraging behavior of crows41, has garnered attention for its efficacy in optimizing complex problems. CSA has been effectively employed to optimize deep transfer learning frameworks for skin lesion detection and to enhance multi-threshold image segmentation for COVID-19 X-ray analysis17,42. Additionally, its memory-based search mechanism has shown strong convergence capabilities in hybrid models for complex recognition tasks43. The Firefly Algorithm (FA), inspired by the bioluminescent signaling behavior of fireflies, has emerged as a robust metaheuristic technique. FA has proven instrumental in refining hyperparameters for brain tumor classification and melanoma lesion segmentation14,44. Hybrid approaches combining FA with other optimizers have also successfully reduced false positives in respiratory disease screening, demonstrating its utility in evolving robust CNN architectures45,46. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) has been widely utilized to efficiently optimize architectures for breast cancer detection in mammography and to improve precision in chest X-ray classification47,48. Its versatility extends to hybrid frameworks for diabetic retinopathy and respiratory condition analysis, demonstrating robust search capabilities in high-dimensional spaces49,50. The Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) has been effectively applied to automate hyperparameter selection for COVID-19 CT analysis and breast cancer detection51,52. Advanced variants have further enhanced feature selection, helping to mitigate overfitting in hybrid deep learning models for medical imaging53. Finally, the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) has outperformed traditional methods in kidney ultrasound classification and glaucoma detection by balancing exploration and exploitation54,55. Its hierarchical search mechanism has also proven effective in optimizing CNN architectures for general skin cancer tasks, providing a strong foundation for melanoma-specific applications56.

Overall, the reviewed evidence indicates that metaheuristic algorithms have contributed substantially to improving CNN-based medical image analysis across diverse diagnostic domains; however, consistent comparative studies targeting melanoma detection remain scarce. This gap underscores the need for controlled evaluations that assess their optimization capabilities under unified experimental settings. Although several metaheuristic algorithms summarized in Table 1 have been applied to CNN optimization in medical imaging tasks, the literature remains fragmented. Most studies evaluate a single metaheuristic under heterogeneous datasets and conditions, limiting reproducibility and fair comparison19. Moreover, while some research addresses skin cancer classification in general, few focus specifically on melanoma using a unified framework with standardized preprocessing, architectures, and metrics20,21. To address this gap, this study presents the first comprehensive and controlled comparative analysis of six metaheuristic algorithms for CNN optimization in melanoma classification.

Background on metaheuristic algorithms

Metaheuristics have become widely used for tuning CNNs, where traditional optimization methods often fall short12,28. Recent surveys confirm their expanding role in deep learning tasks, including neural architecture search (NAS), feature selection, and medical image classification79,80. These algorithms have gained prominence as flexible, domain-agnostic optimizers capable of navigating highly non-convex and high-dimensional search spaces, making them well suited for guiding CNN configuration in medical imaging applications. This study explores six representative algorithms selected for their diverse search strategies. A brief conceptual description of each is presented below.

Cuckoo search (CS)

Inspired by cuckoos laying eggs in other birds’ nests, CS (Cuckoo Search) explores the solution space using a mix of local and global search strategies. The local search is analogous to a cuckoo laying an egg in a nearby nest, while the global search, simulated using “Lévy flights,” involves longer, random jumps to explore entirely new areas. Solutions (nests) that are of poor quality are abandoned with a set probability, allowing new, potentially better solutions to be established. This combination of broad exploration and local refinement helps prevent the search from getting stuck in suboptimal configurations38,57.

Firefly algorithm (FA)

FA (Firefly Algorithm) simulates the flashing behavior of fireflies, where individuals are attracted to those with a brighter flash. In this algorithm, each solution’s “brightness” corresponds to its quality (e.g., the CNN’s validation accuracy). Less bright fireflies (worse solutions) are drawn towards their brighter neighbors, with the attraction diminishing over distance. This mechanism allows the algorithm to converge around promising areas of the search space, while a random component in their movement still permits exploration81.

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)

PSO (Particle Swarm Optimization) models the social behavior of a flock of birds or a school of fish. Each “particle” represents a solution, which moves through the hyperparameter space. Its movement is guided by two main influences: its own best position found so far (personal best) and the best position found by any particle in the entire swarm (global best). This dual influence allows the swarm to collectively explore and exploit promising regions, balancing individual discovery with group knowledge59.

Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)

WOA (Whale Optimization Algorithm) is inspired by the bubble-net hunting strategy of humpback whales. The algorithm simulates this behavior by alternating between two main phases: “encircling prey,” a local search mechanism where solutions move towards the current best-known solution, and a “spiral update” that mimics the whale’s bubble-net attack, allowing for a different path of local exploitation. This dual-strategy approach allows WOA to thoroughly refine solutions in promising areas of the search space61,82.

Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)

GWO (Grey Wolf Optimizer) emulates the social hierarchy and cooperative hunting behavior of a wolf pack. The three best solutions found so far are designated as the leaders: alpha (), beta (), and delta (). All other solutions (omega wolves) update their position in the search space based on the locations of these three leaders, effectively “encircling” the optimal solution (the prey). This leadership-based hierarchy naturally balances exploration (searching) and exploitation (refining)83.

Crow Search Algorithm (CSA)

CSA (Crow Search Algorithm) is inspired by the intelligent behavior of crows, which hide food and remember their hiding spots, but also follow other crows to steal their hidden food. Each “crow” (solution) has a memory of its best hiding spot (best solution). To find a new solution, a crow randomly decides either to explore a new random spot or to “follow” another randomly chosen crow to check its hiding spot. This strategy of leveraging both individual memory and the memory of others provides a balance between diversifying the search and intensifying it around known good solutions41.

These algorithms were selected for this study due to their diverse search strategies and prior success in deep learning optimization. Their integration with CNNs represents a well-supported strategy for enhancing model accuracy, improving convergence reliability, and strengthening generalization capacity in medical image classification tasks, such as melanoma detection. In particular, this selection encompasses algorithms with complementary exploration–exploitation dynamics, allowing a balanced evaluation of stochastic, social, and memory-based search behaviors. Such diversity not only supports benchmarking across different optimization paradigms but also enhances understanding of how distinct metaheuristic principles influence CNN learning stability and robustness. The selection of the six metaheuristic algorithms (CS, FA, WOA, PSO, GWO, and CSA) was guided by their diversity in search strategies and their proven performance in previous deep learning and medical image analysis studies. These algorithms collectively represent different paradigms of global optimization, including swarm intelligence, attraction–repulsion dynamics, and randomization-based exploration. Such diversity allows for a balanced evaluation of exploration and exploitation capabilities. Moreover, according to the “No Free Lunch Theorem” proposed by Wolpert and Macready, no single optimization algorithm can outperform all others across every problem domain. Consequently, this study adopts a comparative perspective to systematically assess which algorithms yield the most effective configurations for CNN hyperparameter optimization in melanoma classification86.

Hybrid optimization framework

The hybrid optimization strategy proposed in this study aims to improve the classification performance of CNNs by integrating metaheuristic algorithms into the hyperparameter tuning process. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of traditional optimization methods when facing the high dimensional, non-linear, and multimodal search spaces characteristic of deep learning architectures. CNNs are highly sensitive to design parameters such as the number of convolutional layers, kernel sizes, learning rate, and batch size87,88. Manual tuning or grid search techniques are often inefficient and computationally expensive87. In contrast, metaheuristics, bio-inspired algorithms based on natural processes such as swarm intelligence, foraging, or evolutionary dynamics, offer a promising alternative for navigating complex solution spaces with fewer assumptions and better global search capabilities88,89. The conceptual framework is illustrated in Figure 2. It outlines the collaborative interaction between the CNN model and the metaheuristic engine. To clarify this process, the hybrid strategy operates as an iterative cycle:

Iterative operational workflow of the hybrid optimization strategy. The diagram illustrates the feedback loop where metaheuristic agents (CS, FA, WOA, PSO, GWO, CSA) dynamically tune CNN hyperparameters based on validation accuracy to guide the search for optimal architectures.

First, the metaheuristic algorithm generates an initial population of candidate solutions, where each candidate is a complete set of CNN hyperparameters (e.g., number of layers, filter sizes, learning rate). Second, for each candidate solution, a full CNN model is built and trained from scratch on the training dataset. Third, this trained model is then evaluated on the validation dataset, and its classification accuracy is returned to the metaheuristic algorithm as the “fitness” value for that candidate. Based on these fitness values, the metaheuristic engine applies its search strategies to generate a new, improved population of hyperparameter sets for the next iteration. The CNN model is continuously retrained and validated as part of this evaluation loop, enabling a dynamic and guided search of the solution space. This hybrid scheme facilitates a balance between exploration (searching new regions of the solution space) and exploitation (refining known good solutions), ultimately producing a hybrid CNN architecture.

Model interpretability with Grad-CAM++

To enhance model transparency and support human-AI decision making, we adopt Grad-CAM++ as a visual explanation tool. Grad-CAM++ extends the original gradient-weighted class activation mapping approach and can produce class-discriminative saliency maps from any convolutional layer of a CNN102,103.

The method computes a weighted combination of the feature maps in a chosen convolutional layer, where the weights are obtained from the gradients of the target class score with respect to each activation. Compared to Grad-CAM, the “++” variant incorporates higher-order gradient information, yielding sharper and more localized heatmaps useful when multiple findings may overlap or visual differences are subtle.

Discussion

Convergence of the best-performing run per metaheuristic

After analyzing the training behaviors of each metaheuristic and identifying the best-performing configuration for each, a comprehensive comparison of their convergence dynamics was performed. To ensure a fair comparison among the six algorithms, the training epochs were normalized before plotting the convergence curves. Because each algorithm completed training with a different number of epochs (ranging from 20 to 40), the x-axis represents normalized training progress (0–100%), enabling consistent comparison across methods. Figure 16 illustrates the evolution of the validation Accuracy for the best run of each metaheuristic, where the best-performing run was defined as the one achieving the highest validation Accuracy, which served as the objective function during optimization.

This representation highlights both the rate and stability of convergence, allowing examination of how quickly and smoothly each optimizer approaches its optimal accuracy level. A continuous trend was used to capture the underlying learning trajectory rather than discrete epoch fluctuations, emphasizing the overall efficiency of each search strategy. Notably, CS and GWO exhibit steeper initial improvements and earlier stabilization, reflecting an effective balance between exploration and exploitation, whereas FA, PSO, and CSA display slower or more oscillatory patterns before reaching convergence.

An important observation derived from these convergence profiles is the absence of divergence between training and validation performance across all metaheuristics, indicating stable learning dynamics. Even without applying early stopping, the curves did not exhibit signs of drift or instability, supporting that the best configurations, particularly those obtained with CS and GWO, achieve genuine generalization rather than overfitting to the training data.

Finally, it is also relevant to note that the CNN architecture used in this study is not a fixed baseline model but a dynamically defined structure whose hyperparameters (depth, number of filters, normalization layers, learning rate, and batch size) vary according to each metaheuristic’s search trajectory. Because the architecture changes across configurations, a single conventional “baseline CNN” is not well defined for direct comparison. Instead, contextual performance references were taken from recent studies summarized in Table 2, which report typical accuracies for CNNs trained without metaheuristic optimization on the same datasets. The consistently superior stability and convergence exhibited by the metaheuristic-derived configurations, particularly CS and GWO, therefore represent the added value of the proposed approach relative to standard non-metaheuristic CNN training.

The choice between these optimization strategies therefore depends on clinical priorities: CS may be preferable in screening scenarios emphasizing early detection, while GWO may be more suitable for confirmatory diagnostic workflows requiring high precision. These findings highlight the importance of aligning model configuration and decision thresholds with the intended clinical context, balancing patient safety with diagnostic efficiency. While Cuckoo Search (CS) and Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) demonstrated superior and stable performance across evaluation metrics, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Crow Search Algorithm (CSA) yielded comparatively lower results and higher variability. These outcomes can be attributed to their stronger tendency toward premature convergence and reduced exploration capability in high-dimensional search spaces, which may limit their ability to escape local optima during CNN hyperparameter tuning. In addition, both algorithms are highly sensitive to initial parameter settings and population diversity, which can affect convergence stability across runs. Despite these limitations, PSO and CSA remain valuable for less complex optimization tasks, where faster convergence may outweigh the need for extensive exploration. An additional analysis was conducted to address the discrepancy observed between validation loss and predictive performance, particularly for FA and PSO. Although both algorithms achieved relatively low validation loss values, this did not translate into higher Accuracy or AUC. The Discussion now highlights possible contributing factors, including the sensitivity of these algorithms to class imbalance, potential overfitting to loss-driven features, and the presence of noisy or ambiguous labels within dermoscopic datasets. These aspects may cause the optimization process to prioritize local minima that minimize loss without necessarily improving classification performance, underscoring the importance of evaluating multiple complementary metrics when assessing model quality.

In summary, performance gains arise not from isolated hyperparameters but from their joint interactions. The ability of metaheuristics to discover effective combinations, particularly involving batch normalization, network depth, and batch size, highlights their value not only in fine-tuning but also in guiding architectural design. These findings underscore the utility of metaheuristic-driven CNN optimization as a practical strategy for identifying performant and generalizable configurations in complex medical imaging contexts.

Specifically, for the melanoma prediction, the heatmap covers the full breadth of the lesion, suggesting the model is identifying its larger, more irregular structure. For the benign nevus, the focus is tightly centralized on the smaller, more regular shape. This visual evidence suggests that the model learns to base its predictions on clinically relevant morphological features within the lesion, providing a crucial first step towards building trust and validating the model’s reasoning for clinical applications.

Beyond qualitative visualization, the integration of explainability methods such as Grad-CAM++ helps bridge the gap between algorithmic decisions and clinical reasoning. By highlighting morphological patterns consistent with dermatological criteria, the proposed framework enhances model transparency and fosters clinician trust in AI-assisted diagnosis. However, visual interpretation alone may not fully capture the relative contribution of specific image features. Therefore, it is recommended to incorporate quantitative interpretability analyses to complement heatmap-based visualization and enable a more rigorous assessment of feature relevance and decision consistency across patient subgroups.

As shown in the confusion matrix of our best model (CS Scheme 1, Fig. 5), two main types of error were identified: False Negatives (10.75%) and False Positives (9.97%). False Negatives often corresponded to early-stage or morphologically ambiguous melanomas, which are difficult to distinguish from benign lesions. False Positives were mainly attributed to atypical (dysplastic) nevi, which mimic characteristics of melanoma (irregular borders, color variation). These ambiguous cases, along with residual artifacts in the image, represent the greatest challenge for model discernment and clinical adoption.

Metaheuristic and alternative approaches to CNN optimization

The integration of metaheuristic optimization within CNN training offers an effective balance between performance and computational efficiency. Compared with more resource-intensive approaches such as Bayesian Optimization or evolutionary Neural Architecture Search (NAS), metaheuristics provide a flexible and scalable alternative that can adapt to diverse problem domains with lower computational cost. This comparative advantage becomes particularly relevant in medical imaging applications, where access to high-performance hardware may be limited.

From a theoretical perspective, metaheuristics excel at balancing exploration and exploitation through adaptive search mechanisms, enabling efficient traversal of highly non-convex loss landscapes typical of CNN training. In contrast, Bayesian optimization methods may struggle to scale beyond a few hyperparameters, and NAS approaches often demand orders of magnitude more computational resources due to architecture-level search.

To further contextualize the advantages of the proposed hybrid framework, a conceptual comparison with traditional non-metaheuristic optimization methods, such as grid search and the standard Adam optimizer, was also considered. These approaches, while widely used, are constrained by fixed search patterns and gradient-based updates, which limit their ability to explore complex, multimodal loss landscapes. Previous studies98,107 have shown that such methods tend to suffer from local minima sensitivity, limited adaptability, and suboptimal convergence when applied to high-dimensional medical imaging data. In contrast, population-based metaheuristics enable broader and adaptive exploration of the search space, achieving more stable convergence and performance consistency under comparable conditions, thereby offering a flexible and computationally efficient alternative to both gradient-based and Bayesian optimization strategies.

This finding suggests that population-based algorithms represent a viable middle ground between heuristic simplicity and model-driven optimization, offering a pragmatic route toward automated, resource-efficient model tuning. The results obtained in this study confirm that metaheuristic-based optimization can achieve competitive accuracy while maintaining reasonable runtime and convergence stability, reinforcing its practical value for real-world and clinical environments.

Ethical implications and deployment considerations

The practical deployment of automated melanoma detection tools carries significant ethical responsibilities. We have proactively addressed interpretability, a key component for clinical trust, by integrating Grad-CAM++ to provide visual explanations of the model’s decision-making process . The most critical clinical risk remains the false negative, where a melanoma is missed. Our failure analysis highlights that this risk is highest in morphologically ambiguous lesions, reinforcing the ethical mandate that this tool must be used only as a decision-support system to augment dermatological expertise, never to replace it. Finally, while this study utilized the public, anonymized HAM10000 dataset , a transition to a real clinical workflow (as proposed in our future work) introduces major data privacy concerns. Any future deployment should consider privacy-preserving technologies, such as on-premise processing within the hospital’s firewall or federated learning, to protect sensitive patient information.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising findings, several limitations and opportunities for future research remain. First, the current framework was validated exclusively on the HAM10000 dataset. While this benchmark provides high-quality dermoscopic images, it does not fully capture the diversity of skin types, populations, and lesion presentations encountered in clinical practice. This may introduce bias and limit generalizability, as model performance could degrade on underrepresented populations or images acquired under different conditions (e.g., varying dermatoscopes, lighting, or operator expertise). Future work should therefore incorporate external validation using larger and more heterogeneous datasets, including ISIC 2020108, PH2109, and Derm7pt110, which will contribute greater variability in imaging conditions, lesion types, and patient demographics, allowing a more comprehensive assessment of model robustness, fairness, and clinical generalizability. Expanding validation in this manner will strengthen the robustness, external validity, and clinical applicability of the proposed metaheuristic-optimized CNN framework, supporting its translation toward practical and equitable diagnostic use.

As summarized in Table 2, several prior studies have employed CNN-based architectures for melanoma classification using the ISIC 2018 and HAM10000 datasets. Among these, the models most comparable to the proposed configuration, characterized by the use of 3 3 convolutional filters, ReLU activation, MaxPooling 22, and fully connected layers, are ResNet152 (86.47%)67, EfficientNet-B4/B5 (87.90%)66,and DenseNet-201 + SMA (91.70%)74. While these architectures differ substantially in depth, they share similar convolutional and pooling design principles with the developed hybrid CNN, providing a relevant reference for contextual performance comparison.

To provide a referential context for performance interpretation, results from CNN-based studies with comparable architectures were considered. The proposed hybrid CNN achieved an accuracy of 91.25%, corresponding to relative differences of +4.78 pp, +3.35 pp, and −0.45 pp, respectively. Although these values are not directly comparable due to variations in datasets, preprocessing, and evaluation protocols, they serve as a reference benchmark highlighting the competitive performance of the proposed approach. A standardized baseline CNN trained under identical experimental conditions will be included in future work to quantify the relative improvement more precisely and to establish a more rigorous empirical foundation for comparative evaluation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that metaheuristic algorithms play a crucial role in enhancing the performance of CNNs for melanoma detection from dermoscopic images. Among the evaluated strategies, CS and GWO demonstrated highly competitive results, frequently appearing as top performers across all metrics. This highlights their robustness and adaptability to the high-dimensional, non-convex optimization landscapes typical of CNN architectures. One of the most clinically relevant insights emerging from this work is the importance of optimizing for Recall, especially in the context of melanoma diagnosis, where false negatives can lead to critical delays in treatment. A key clinical insight emerging from the results is the prioritization of Recall in high-stakes medical domains such as melanoma diagnosis, where minimizing false negatives is essential to avoid delayed treatments.

A direct comparison with manually designed baseline architectures confirmed the superiority of the proposed hybrid CNN framework. While the best manual configuration reached a performance ceiling of 83.69%, the metaheuristic-optimized models consistently surpassed this barrier, achieving 91.25% with Cuckoo Search. This demonstrates that hybrid optimization automates network design while discovering configurations significantly more robust than those obtainable through conventional expert tuning.

Integrating data preprocessing and augmentation techniques proved essential for improving generalization. This integration enabled metaheuristic algorithms to explore more effective configurations. Furthermore, the diversity in hyperparameter values across different algorithms, including the use of batch normalization and variation in batch sizes, reflects the capacity of these methods to adaptively search heterogeneous architectural landscapes. The convergence patterns observed in this study indicate that metaheuristic search algorithms are capable of uncovering synergistic parameter configurations, suggesting their potential for data driven yet interpretable exploration of complex architectural spaces. These results lay the foundation for new frameworks that can autonomously design optimal CNN structures for medical imaging tasks. Beyond parameter tuning, a promising contribution of this work is highlighting the potential of autonomous search mechanisms. The metaheuristic algorithms demonstrated a capacity to discover high-performing and generalizable CNN configurations without human supervision. They achieved this by learning from performance feedback to guide the search process. This capability positions them as core components in intelligent optimization pipelines, with potential applications in automated model design (AutoML) for medical imaging.

Furthermore, integrating these metaheuristic tuning techniques into clinical workflows presents interesting opportunities. For instance, automated periodic re-optimization of the hybrid CNN models could be implemented as new patient data accumulates within a hospital setting. This would adapt the system to local demographics or changes in imaging equipment, ensuring that the model remains robust and maintains optimal performance as clinical conditions evolve. Ultimately, this research highlights the potential of metaheuristic hybrid CNNs as reliable, interpretable decision support tools that complement dermatological expertise and enhance early melanoma detection. The findings support broader adoption of intelligent optimization in medical AI across diverse diagnostic domains.

Given the large and continuously growing number of proposed metaheuristics in the literature, the authors acknowledge that the field of metaheuristics is exceptionally dynamic, with novel algorithms being proposed continuously. As noted in ’Related Work’ section, this includes new swarm-based algorithms such as Sperm Swarm Optimization (SSO)25 and physics-based methods like the Chernobyl Disaster Optimizer (CDO)23 and the Bermuda Triangle Optimizer (BTO)27. Although a comprehensive comparison, including the algorithms mentioned above, is not addressed within the current experimental setting, these approaches are recognized as interesting and promising directions for future research./<otra opción>These approaches are therefore recognized as interesting and promising directions for future research; however, a comprehensive experimental framework is advisable to enable meaningful and methodologically consistent comparisons.

In summary, our research was designed to establish a critical and comprehensive performance benchmark using six of the most well-regarded and representative algorithms in the literature (CS, FA, WOA, PSO, GWO, and CSA). The findings of this paper, particularly the high performance of CS and GWO, provide a robust baseline that can and should be used in future studies to evaluate the relative efficacy of these emerging optimizers in the context of medical image classification. At the same time this work reinforces the value of metaheuristically hybrid CNNs as reliable, adaptive, and interpretable decision support systems for early melanoma detection. These findings contribute to the broader adoption of intelligent optimization techniques in clinical AI applications and establish a foundation for future research in automated and context-aware deep learning systems.
