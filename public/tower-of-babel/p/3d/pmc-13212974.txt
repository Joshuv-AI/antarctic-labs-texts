Effect of nanoliposomal gamma-oryzanol on cryosurvival, oxidative balance, and fertility of frozen–thawed ram spermatozoa
Authors: Roughayeh Abbasi, Ramin Farhadi, Sara Ghaffarian Varjovi, Abouzar Najafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study was to examine how gamma-oryzanol (GO) and gamma-oryzanol nanoparticles (GO-N) affected the quality of ram sperm after freeze–thaw treatment. Epididymal sperm from Shall rams collected from a slaughterhouse were diluted and divided into seven groups: a control group (0 µM), three concentrations of GO (50, 100, and 200 µM), and three concentrations of GO-N (50, 100, and 200 µM). The results demonstrated that treatment with 100 µM GO-N significantly improved the total motility (70.45%), progressive motility (60.21%), and velocity parameters (VCL, VSL, and VAP) compared to the other groups (p < 0.05). Plasma membrane and acrosomal integrity increased to 75.76% and 77.35%, respectively, while the percentage of morphological abnormalities decreased to 16.67% (p < 0.05). In the same group, flow cytometry revealed a reduction in DNA fragmentation to 17.22%, an increase in mitochondrial membrane potential to 66.11%, a decrease in H₂O₂ concentration to 13.29%, and the highest sperm viability (76.76%). MDA levels dropped to 3.42 nmol/mL, while SOD and GPx activities increased to 138.16 and 74.69 U/mg protein, respectively. Fertility assessments also indicated that the use of 100 µM GO-N in the extender significantly improved the pregnancy rate, lambing rate, and prolificacy compared with the other treatments (p < 0.05). In conclusion, the addition of 100 µM GO-N to the cryopreservation extender appears to be an effective strategy for enhancing the quality and functionality of thawed ram sperm, and may be valuable in breeding programs, reproductive biotechnology, and assisted reproductive technologies.Keywords: Antioxidants, Sperm, Oxidative stress, Gamma oryzanol, NanoliposomeSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Cell biology

Introduction

Cryopreservation and cooling of spermatozoa have emerged as essential methods for achieving this goal. These techniques reduce cellular metabolism through temperature reduction, thereby prolonging sperm viability and enabling long-term storage1. Cryopreservation, while indispensable, exposes sperm cells to osmotic and oxidative stress, which can disrupt cellular integrity and impair functionality. During the freeze–thaw cycle, rapid temperature shifts and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) disturb the delicate balance between antioxidants and pro-oxidants. This imbalance triggers oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation (LPO), and widespread structural and functional damage to sperm2,3. ROS are naturally generated as byproducts of cellular metabolism and play beneficial roles in sperm capacitation, acrosome reactions, and zona pellucida binding at low concentrations. However, their overproduction overwhelms intracellular antioxidant defenses, leading to oxidative damage to the sperm membrane, mitochondria, and nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, ultimately impairing sperm viability and fertilization capacity4.

While liquid-chilled sperm storage results in relatively less damage compared to freezing, it is not suitable for long-term preservation5. Antioxidants are compounds that control, neutralize, stop, or at least counteract the production of free radicals, particularly ROS6,7.

Several in vitro studies have reported that its inhibitory effect on lipid peroxidation may be up to tenfold greater than that of vitamin E; however, such comparisons are highly assay-dependent and influenced by factors including concentration, isomeric composition, and the experimental matrix8. The ability of gamma oryzanol to scavenge free radicals, particularly superoxide, hydroxyl, and hydrogen peroxide radicals, is what gives it its antioxidant qualities. The primary free radical scavenger is the hydroxyl group on the phenolic ring of the ferulic acid moiety. When free radicals receive a hydrogen atom from the hydroxyl group, a phenoxy radical is created in the phenolic ring. This radical is extremely unstable, quickly neutralized, and destroyed9. Gamma-oryzanol also increases the activity of intracellular antioxidant enzymes10.

In recent years, nanoparticle-based delivery systems have emerged as effective strategies to overcome these limitations by enhancing bioavailability, improving pharmacokinetic profiles, and providing more stable and smaller delivery scales11. Among these, nanoliposomes have shown particular promise for the encapsulation and delivery of gamma-oryzanol. This technique can significantly improve the aqueous solubility of a compound, increase its bioavailability, prolong its half-life, enhance cellular uptake, and optimize its metabolic stability. Furthermore, nanoliposomes allow for targeted delivery of gamma-oryzanol molecules to specific cells, including spermatozoa12–14.

The present study was designed to evaluate the antioxidant and cytoprotective effects of gamma-oryzanol on ram spermatozoa subjected to cryopreservation and thawing, with a specific focus on comparing its conventional free form (GO) with a nanoliposome-encapsulated formulation (GO-N) at multiple concentrations in the semen extender. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic investigation to contrast free and nanoliposomal gamma -oryzanol in ram sperm cryopreservation using a comprehensive panel of motility, structural, functional, oxidative, and fertility endpoints. By integrating detailed in vitro assessments with in vivo fertility outcomes, this work aims to introduce GO-N as a novel, nanotechnology-assisted antioxidant strategy to enhance the cryosurvival and reproductive performance of frozen–thawed ram spermatozoa.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

The ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 were followed in this study. All animal care and procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee, Department of Animal Science and Poultry, Faculty of Agricultural Technology, University of Tehran, Tehran, Iran. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations of the University of Tehran.

Preparation of gamma-oryzanol nanoparticles

Gamma-oryzanol powder (> 99.5% purity; TCI Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan; Cat. No. O0172; CAS 11042-64-1) was encapsulated in nanoliposomes (GO-N) using a lecithin-based thin-film hydration approach followed by high-speed homogenization and probe sonication. Briefly, lecithin and gamma-oryzanol were dissolved in an organic solvent system, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Hei-VAP; Germany) to form a thin lipid film. The film was hydrated with an aqueous phase and then processed using a high-speed homogenizer (MTOPS; South Korea) and ultrasonic probe sonication (Hielscher; Germany; performed on ice) to obtain nanoscale liposomes. Nanoparticle formation was confirmed by dynamic light scattering using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (UK). A representative measurement (record no. 3219; 25 °C; dispersant: water) yielded a Z-average hydrodynamic diameter of 72.07 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.228. The GO-N suspension was protected from light and stored at 4 °C until use. In a separate zeta potential analysis (record no. 3214; 24.9 °C; 12 runs; dispersant: water), the mean apparent zeta potential was − 31.5 ± 5.17 mV. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) was determined using a centrifugal filter tube (Amicon® Ultra-15, 100 kDa MWCO; Millipore, USA). Briefly, 1 mL of the formulation was transferred to the filter unit and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to separate free (non-encapsulated) compound from the encapsulated fraction. The filtrate (bottom compartment) was collected for quantification of the free compound using UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Ultrascope 2000®, Pharmacia Biotech, UK) at 276 nm. Total compound concentration was measured in an unfiltered aliquot after complete disruption of the nanocarrier. The average EE of the formulation was 92.6%.

Semen collection

Sperm samples were obtained during the autumn season from 30 adult Shall rams aged three to four years, all verified to possess high fertility, sourced from the Rock slaughterhouse in Karaj, Iran. Epididymal spermatozoa were used to reduce natural variability in semen quality and to ensure comparable sample collection conditions. Immediately after slaughter, testes with attached epididymides were placed in sterile saline, kept in a cooled container (~ 4 °C), and transported to the laboratory within approximately 30 min. All subsequent processing steps were performed after arrival at the laboratory. Spermatozoa were recovered from the cauda epididymis by making multiple incisions and gently flushing/suspending the tissue in 1 × Tris buffer solution. The suspension was filtered to remove tissue debris and centrifuged at 700 × g for 10 min. The sperm pellet was resuspended in Tris solution and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C to allow sperm dispersion before initial quality evaluation. Only samples with motility ≥ 80% and morphological abnormalities ≤ 10% were included. The basic extender was formulated by dissolving 2.71 g of Tris, 1.4 g of citric acid, and 1 g of fructose in 100 mL of distilled water, followed by supplementation with 7% (v/v) glycerol and 20% (v/v) egg yolk. Gamma-oryzanol was added to the extender at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 200 μM in both its free form (GO) and nanoliposomal formulation (GO-N). Each semen sample was subsequently diluted to achieve a final concentration of 100 × 10⁶ spermatozoa per 0.25-mL straw. The concentration range (50–200 μM) was extrapolated from concentrations previously tested for gamma-oryzanol supplementation during semen cryopreservation in other species and experimental systems (e.g., 20–100 μM in poultry and 0.08–0.32 mM in boar), and was applied to both GO and GO-N to enable direct comparison and identification of an optimal dose15,16.

Freezing/thawing

Following a 2 h equilibration period at 4 °C to ensure thermal stabilization, the diluted semen was filled into 0.25 mL French straws (IMV, L'Aigle, France), and the open ends were sealed using polyvinyl alcohol. The straws were subsequently subjected to cryopreservation by positioning them 4 cm above the surface of liquid nitrogen for 7 min, after which they were rapidly immersed directly into liquid nitrogen. After a minimum storage period of four weeks, the frozen straws were thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 s to retrieve spermatozoa for subsequent analyses17.

Sperm motility parameters

Sperm motility was assessed using a computer-assisted sperm analysis system (CASA; VideoTest, Sperm 3.2; St. Petersburg, Russia). The CASA system analyzed several key motion parameters including total motility (TM; %), progressive motility (PM; %), average path velocity (VAP; μm/s), straight-line velocity (VSL; μm/s), curvilinear velocity (VCL; μm/s), amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH; μm), and linearity (LIN; %). At least 200 spermatozoa were analyzed per sample using the CASA system18.

Functional integrity

For this purpose, 10 µL of semen was combined with 100 µL of a hypoosmotic solution (100 mOsm/kg) composed of fructose and sodium citrate (as previously described 16), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, a 5 µL aliquot was mounted on a microscope slide and examined under a light microscope at 400 × magnification across a minimum of ten randomly selected fields. Spermatozoa exhibiting tail swelling or characteristic coiling were classified as possessing intact and physiologically active plasma membranes19.

Sperm morphology

The Hancock staining method was used for sperm morphology evaluation (62.5 mL 37% formalin, 150 mL saline solution, 150 mL buffer solution, 500 mL double-distilled water) by mixing 100 μL of Hancock’s solution with a drop (10 μL) of semen sample and placing 5 μL on each slide under the microscope for evaluation; at least 200 spermatozoa were counted in ten microscopic fields at 400 × magnification (Labomed LX400; Labomed Inc., Culver City, CA, USA), counting abnormalities in acrosome, head attachment, midpiece, and tail to determine percentage of morphologically abnormal sperm20. Morphology was therefore reported as the percentage of total abnormal spermatozoa, without subtype-specific quantification.

SOD and GPx activity

The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) in frozen-thawed ram sperm was quantified after the extraction of proteins using a commercial lysis buffer. The samples were mixed with 300 µL of PRO-PREP™ reagent, vortexed for 30 s, and then incubated for 30 min on ice with gentle mixing every 5 min to ensure complete cell disruption. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was used for enzymatic assays. Total protein content was determined by the Bradford method and normalized enzymatic activity expressed as units per milligram of protein. SOD activity was measured using the RANSOD commercial assay, in which 50 µL of protein extract was mixed with the substrate solution and the reaction was started by the addition of xanthine oxidase. The decrease in absorbance at 505 nm was monitored spectrophotometrically, and enzyme activity was expressed as units per milligram of protein reflecting the ability of SOD to dismutate superoxide radicals. GPx activity was assessed using the RANSEL assay kit which measures GPx-mediated reduction of peroxide substrates coupled to the oxidation of NADPH. In this assay, 10 µL of the sperm lysate (prepared as above for the SOD assay) was mixed with the GPx reagent system and assay buffer, and the reaction was started by the addition of cumene hydroperoxide. The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm, corresponding to NADPH consumption, was recorded over time using a spectrophotometer. Enzyme activity was calculated based on the rate of NADPH oxidation and expressed as units per milligram of protein as an estimate of the antioxidant capacity of GPx in cryopreserved spermatozoa20.

Malondialdehyde evaluation

Lipid peroxidation was quantified by determining malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method. After thawing the semen samples in a 37 °C water bath for 30 s, they were mixed sequentially with EDTA, butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solutions. The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min, after which the clarified supernatant was combined with TBA reagent and incubated in a water bath maintained at 95 °C to facilitate MDA–TBA adduct formation. The absorbance of the resulting chromogenic complex was then measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer (Hermle Technik GmbH, Germany), and MDA concentration was calculated21.

Acrosome membrane integrity

Acrosomal membrane integrity was evaluated using fluorescent staining with Pisum sativum agglutinin (PSA). For this assessment, 250 μL of the sperm suspension was placed into 1.5-mL microtubes and centrifuged at 1200 × g for 10 min. The resulting pellet was then resuspended in 100 μL of 96% ethanol and maintained at 4 °C for 30 min to facilitate fixation. Subsequently, 20 μL of PSA fluorescent dye (50 μg/mL) was added to 10 μL of the ethanol-treated sperm, and the mixture was smeared onto a microscope slide. After a 10 min incubation in the dark at 4 °C, the slides were gently rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and mounted with glycerol. A total of 200 spermatozoa per slide were examined under a fluorescence microscope at 400 × magnification. Cells exhibiting distinct fluorescence were classified as possessing intact acrosomal membranes, whereas non-fluorescent cells were interpreted as having acrosomal damage.

Sperm viability and apoptosis

Apoptotic and viability profiles of frozen–thawed spermatozoa were determined via dual staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide, enabling discrimination among viable, early apoptotic, and membrane-compromised cells. Briefly, 100 µL of semen was washed and resuspended in calcium-containing binding buffer at a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Subsequently, 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL propidium iodide were added, and samples were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in darkness to allow appropriate fluorophore binding. Flow cytometric acquisition was performed on a FACSCalibur system, with 10,000 events collected per sample. Based on fluorescence signatures, sperm were categorized as viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), or necrotic/late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ or Annexin V⁻/PI⁺). This methodology enabled precise quantification of cryo-induced apoptosis and facilitated assessment of treatment-related effects on sperm survival22.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)

The amount of ROS (hydrogen peroxide radicals, H₂O₂) in the samples was measured using the DCFH-DA staining method using a commercial kit from Kiazist Company (Hamadan, Iran)23. The samples were incubated in 250 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37 °C for 20 min, centrifuged at 300 × g for 7 min and the supernatant was discarded; 3 mL PBS was added to the pellet, the mixture was centrifuged for 7 min at the same speed; 10 μL luminol was added to 400 μL of the prepared sample and the ROS level was determined using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Mitochondrial activity

The assessment of mitochondrial function in ram sperm was carried out using Rhodamine 123 staining combined with flow cytometry. In this procedure, a 100-µL portion of the semen sample was incubated with 5 µM Rhodamine 123 at 37 °C for 30 min in darkness to allow selective incorporation of the dye into active mitochondria. After staining, the samples were analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer, with excitation at 488 nm and emission detected at 530 nm. Spermatozoa exhibiting strong Rhodamine 123 fluorescence and negative PI staining were classified as having functional and metabolically active mitochondria. This measurement provided an estimate of the proportion of sperm retaining mitochondrial activity following cryopreservation24.

DNA fragmentation

DNA fragmentation was assessed with the Sperm Chromatin Structure Assay (SCSA), where frozen–thawed semen was diluted in TNE buffer (0.01 M Tris–HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA; pH 7.4) to a final concentration of 2 × 10⁶ sperm/mL, mixed with 400 μL of acid-detergent solution (0.1% Triton X-100, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.08 N HCl; pH 1.2) to induce partial DNA denaturation in damaged chromatin regions, and incubated for 30 s on ice, followed by addition of 1.2 mL of acridine orange (AO) staining solution (6 μg/mL in citrate–phosphate buffer: 37 mM citric acid, 126 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1 mM EDTA, 0.15 M NaCl; pH 6.0). Samples were analyzed within 3 min using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer at 488-nm excitation, with detection of green fluorescence (530 ± 30 nm) for intact double-stranded DNA and red fluorescence (> 630 nm) for denatured or fragmented DNA. A minimum of 10,000 spermatozoa were recorded for each sample, and the DNA Fragmentation Index (DFI) and high DNA stainability (HDS) were calculated using FlowJo software.

CYTb gene copy number

Mitochondrial DNA content in spermatozoa was quantified by real-time PCR analysis targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome b (CYTb) gene, with nuclear β-actin (ACTB) serving as the internal control to normalize variations in genomic DNA input. Total genomic DNA was isolated using a commercial sperm DNA extraction kit (Zist Azma, Tehran, Iran), and its concentration and purity were assessed via NanoDrop spectrophotometry. Gene-specific primers for CYTb and ACTB were designed using Primer3 Plus software and synthesized by Metabion (Germany). Prior to qPCR, primer specificity and the expected amplicon lengths were verified through conventional PCR followed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Quantitative analysis was performed on a LightCycler 96 platform (Roche, Switzerland) employing SYBR Green chemistry as the fluorescence detector. Relative mtDNA abundance was determined using the 2–ΔΔCT method, where ΔCT represents the difference between the CT values of CYTb and ACTB. It is important to note that this assay reflects mitochondrial DNA copy number rather than gene expression or transcriptional activity23.

Fertility evaluation

A total of 140 ewes, whose estrous cycles had been synchronized, were randomly allocated to the experimental groups to avoid allocation bias. Estrus synchronization was achieved using intravaginal progesterone sponges maintained in situ for 12 days, followed by an intramuscular injection of 400 IU equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG; Folligon®). Laparoscopic AI was then performed according to an established protocol using a standard laparoscopic system (Karl Storz®, Germany), enabling direct deposition of semen into the uterine horns. Each insemination dose consisted of 100 × 10⁶ spermatozoa contained in a 0.25 mL straw. Prior to insemination, the ewes were subjected to a fasting period of 24–36 h and water deprivation for 12–18 h. Fertility outcomes were assessed as follows: pregnancy rate was determined as the proportion of ewes diagnosed as pregnant via ultrasonography; birth rate was expressed as the percentage of inseminated ewes delivering live offspring; and lambing and twinning rates were reported as percentages relative to the total number of inseminated animals.

Statistical analysis

After sperm assessment as described above, cryopreservation of pooled semen was performed in seven replicates, and the data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models on the R statistical platform (R Team, 2024). The results are presented as mean ± SEM, and significant differences in means were considered using Tukey’s multiple-comparison corrections method when p < 0.05.

Sperm motility parameters

Total motility (TM; Fig. 1a) and progressive motility (PM; Fig. 1b) increased significantly in nano-supplemented groups, with 100 GO-N exhibiting the highest TM and PM (p < 0.05). Membrane function, morphology, DNA integrity, and mitochondrial activity

Figure 2a demonstrates that plasma membrane integrity increased markedly following nano-supplementation, with 100 GO-N achieving the highest membrane stability (p < 0.05). The 50 GO-N group also exhibited improved membrane integrity relative to the control. According to Fig. 2b, abnormal sperm forms declined significantly only in 100 GO-N, showing the lowest rate of morphological defects. As shown in Fig. 2c, DNA fragmentation decreased substantially after nano-supplementation; the 100 GO-N treatment achieved the lowest fragmentation rate (p < 0.05), and 50 GO-N and 200 GO-N also showed significant reductions versus the control. Figure 2d reveals that mitochondrial activity was highest in 100 GO-N, followed by 50 GO-N and 200 GO-N, all significantly higher than the control.

Oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme response

Lipid peroxidation, represented by MDA (Fig. 3a), declined sharply in nano-treated samples, with 100 GO-N showing the lowest MDA value (p < 0.05). Significant decreases were also observed in 50 GO-N and 100 GO compared with the control. In Fig. 3b, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity rose significantly, peaking in 100 GO-N, followed by 50 GO-N, 100 GO, and 200 GO-N. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity (Fig. 3c) exhibited a similar pattern, with 100 GO-N showing the highest enzyme activity (p < 0.05), and 200 GO-N also significantly elevated over the control. As illustrated in Fig. 3d, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were markedly reduced in 100 GO-N, which had the lowest ROS output among all groups (p < 0.05). The 50 GO-N group also showed a significant reduction compared with the control.

Sperm viability and apoptotic status

Figure 4a shows that post-thaw sperm viability increased in all nanoliposome-supplemented groups compared with the control, with the highest values observed in the 100 GO-N treatment. In contrast, the free GO treatments produced only modest or non-significant improvements in viability relative to the control. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the proportion of early apoptotic spermatozoa was significantly reduced only in the 100 GO-N group, whereas all other GO and GO-N doses remained statistically comparable to the control. Regarding late apoptosis (Fig. 4c), the percentage of late apoptotic spermatozoa decreased significantly in the 50 and 100 GO-N groups compared with the control, while the 200 GO-N and all free GO treatments did not differ significantly from the control. The incidence of necrotic spermatozoa (Fig. 4d) did not vary among treatments, indicating that GO-N supplementation primarily shifted spermatozoa from apoptotic to viable states rather than altering necrotic cell death.

As shown in Fig. 5a, post-thaw acrosome integrity differed significantly among treatments. The 100 μM GO-N group exhibited the highest acrosome integrity, which was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.05). The 50 and 200 μM GO-N groups showed intermediate values and were also higher than the control group (p < 0.05), whereas the free GO treatments did not produce a comparable improvement; in particular, 50 and 200 μM GO remained statistically similar to the control, and 100 μM GO showed a modest increase but did not reach the level observed in the GO-N groups. In Fig. 5b, mitochondrial CYTb copy number (relative mtDNA content) showed a different pattern across treatments. A significant reduction in CYTb copy number was detected only in the 100 μM GO-N group compared with the control and all other groups (p < 0.05). Discussion

Despite the many benefits of sperm cryopreservation for preserving genetic resources and supporting breeding programs, the freeze–thaw process imposes major thermal and osmotic stress and promotes excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), collectively impairing sperm function and fertility25. Therefore, supplementation of semen extenders with antioxidants is a common strategy to mitigate cryo-induced oxidative damage. Gamma-oryzanol (GO) is a phenolic antioxidant; however, its strong lipophilicity can limit its dispersion in aqueous extenders. Nano-encapsulation has been used to improve delivery of lipophilic antioxidants, and gamma-oryzanol nano formulations have been shown to enhance post-thaw sperm quality compared with the conventional form in other models26. Accordingly, this study compared free GO with nanoliposomal GO (GO-N) across multiple concentrations, integrating conventional post-thaw assessments with oxidative markers and in vivo fertility outcomes.

GO-N supplementation particularly at 100 µM produced the most consistent improvements in post-thaw sperm quality, including motility/kinematic parameters, membrane integrity, mitochondrial activity, and viability, while reducing apoptosis and DNA fragmentation. These functional benefits were accompanied by a marked improvement in oxidative balance (lower ROS and MDA levels together with higher SOD and GPx activities), and the same group yielded the highest pregnancy and lambing outcomes. In contrast, the conventional form (GO) at the same nominal concentrations was less effective for several endpoints, supporting the premise that formulation can influence biological efficacy in the cryopreservation context.

A unified mechanistic interpretation of these endpoints is that improved redox homeostasis during freezing and thawing reduces lipid peroxidation of the sperm plasma membrane and acrosomal membranes27, preserves membrane fluidity, and stabilizes mitochondrial function28. Because sperm motility is highly dependent on ATP supply, the higher mitochondrial activity observed in the 100 µM GO-N group is consistent with the concurrent improvements in TM/PM and velocity indices. In parallel, lowering ROS burden may reduce cryo-triggered activation of apoptotic pathways and limit oxidative lesions in sperm DNA29, which aligns with the observed reductions in early/late apoptosis and DNA fragmentation30. GO-N supplementation increased mitochondrial activity, improved viability, decreased early and late apoptosis, and reduced DNA fragmentation. These findings corroborate the observations of Najafi, et al.31, who noted that antioxidant-enriched extenders can mitigate phosphatidylserine translocation, a hallmark of apoptosis, by limiting oxidative stress.

Regarding sperm morphology, abnormalities were assessed across major categories (acrosomal, head, midpiece, and tail defects) to calculate the percentage of total abnormal spermatozoa. The 100 μM GO-N group showed the lowest total abnormality rate, suggesting reduced structural disruption during the freeze–thaw process 30. However, defect subtypes were not quantified separately; therefore, interpretation is limited to total morphological preservation rather than changes in specific defect patterns.

Dose selection (50–200 μM) was chosen to cover a practical low-to-high range while remaining within concentrations reported for gamma-oryzanol supplementation during sperm cryopreservation in other species. For example, gamma-oryzanol and its nano form have been evaluated in poultry semen extenders at 20–100 μM, with optimal responses reported around 60–80 μM for the nano formulation15, whereas in boar semen, effective concentrations have been reported in the 0.08–0.32 mM range (with breed-dependent optima around 0.16–0.24 mM)16. Notably, in the present ram model, increasing GO-N above 100 μM tended to attenuate benefits for several endpoints and fertility. This dose-dependent response may reflect that excessive antioxidant exposure can disrupt redox-dependent signaling required for normal sperm function, and/or that higher concentrations of lipid-based carriers may perturb membrane organization or extender microenvironment32. The weaker response of free GO compared with GO-N may be due to limited dispersion/availability of the conventional form in the aqueous extender, reducing effective interaction with sperm membranes. Importantly, the novelty of the present work lies in evaluating a nanoliposomal GO formulation in a ram cryopreservation system, for which dose–response data have been limited.

Post-thaw acrosome integrity improved predominantly in the GO-N treatments, with the greatest enhancement at 100 μM, and comparable gains at 50 and 200 μM GO-N. Given the vulnerability of the acrosomal and plasma membranes to cryo-induced lipid peroxidation, this response is consistent with the superior oxidative profile of the optimal GO-N dose (lower ROS/MDA and higher SOD/GPx). Notably, CYTb mtDNA content decreased significantly only in the 100 μM GO-N group, which also displayed the most favorable post-thaw phenotype (maximal mitochondrial activity, improved motility/kinematics, higher viability and membrane integrity, reduced apoptosis/DNA fragmentation) and the best fertility outcomes. Taken together, these convergent endpoints suggest that the optimal GO-N dose more effectively preserves mitochondrial status under cryostress, potentially reducing stress-related mitochondrial remodeling.

Nanoparticle characterization in this study was limited to hydrodynamic size distribution (DLS), encapsulation efficiency (EE), and zeta potential; therefore, additional characterization such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was not performed. Although the use of epididymal spermatozoa reduces ejaculate-to-ejaculate variability, epididymal samples may not fully represent ejaculated semen collected under field conditions.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, supplementation of the cryopreservation extender with gamma-oryzanol particularly in the nanoliposomal form resulted in measurable improvements in multiple post-thaw sperm quality endpoints and in vivo fertility outcomes. Within the tested range, 100 µM GO-N produced the most consistent benefits. These results suggest that GO-N is a promising nanotechnology-assisted antioxidant strategy for improving the cryosurvival and reproductive performance of frozen–thawed ram spermatozoa.
