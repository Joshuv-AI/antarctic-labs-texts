Delayed-Onset Benign Action Myoclonus Following General Anesthesia – Two Cases
Authors: Dhanaraj Mathuram, Arul Selvan, Salgunan Nair, Vijay Shankar
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Myoclonus is a movement disorder characterized by brief, rapid, shock-like contractions of muscles that are irregular in rhythm and amplitude, and generally associated with various progressive neurological disorders, and rarely occurs in isolation. Myoclonus following general anesthesia is very rare[1]. It may be linked to factors such as anesthetic agents, altered metabolic states (e.g. hypoxia, electrolyte imbalances), or the presence of systemic infections like sepsis, and medications like opioids or benzodiazepines, which are used for pain management or sedation.[12] The pathophysiology remains multifactorial, potentially involving altered neurotransmitter activity in the central nervous system or impaired metabolic regulation.[3] Prompt recognition and treatment of the underlying causes are crucial for management.[1] We present two cases of delayed-onset benign action myoclonus that occurred following general anesthesia for cardiac surgery.

A 78-year-old woman developed jerky movements 6 days after uneventful coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and aortic valve replacement under propofol and thiopentone. She was fully conscious with no neurological deficits apart from abnormal movements. At rest, mild tremulous myoclonic jerks involved her upper limbs, head, and neck; on arm elevation, negative myoclonus appeared; while drinking, jerks spread to the trunk, head, and neck. Blood work and computed tomography (CT) brain were normal; and electroencephalogram (EEG) showed occasional occipital sharp waves. She improved completely within 4 days on clonazepam (0.25 mg bid) and levetiracetam (500 mg bid), with no relapse on follow-up.

A 59-year-old man developed involuntary trunk and upper limb movements on day 3 after an uneventful CABG under propofol and thiopentone. He remained fully conscious, with intact cranial nerves and reflexes. At rest, fine jerks affected the right upper limb; on arm extension, gross myoclonus involved both arms (right > left) and trunk. Blood work and EEG were normal. He responded completely within 2 days to clonazepam (0.5 mg bid) and valproate (300 mg bid); valproate was stopped and clonazepam tapered before discharge. In both of these patients, a final diagnosis of delayed-onset benign action myoclonus following general anesthesia was made [Video 1].

Myoclonus is a movement disorder characterized by sudden, brief, shock-like jerky movements of body parts. Myoclonus following general anesthesia is relatively uncommon and is attributed to anesthetic agents.[12] In a systematic review of 516 case reports of “seizure-like phenomena following propofol,” the types of presentations were generalized tonic-clonic seizure, focal motor seizure, increased tone with twitching, opisthotonus, and movements like myoclonus.[4] Such movements occurred during induction, emergence from anesthesia, or the immediate postoperative period.[5] In one of the case reports, immediate postoperative myoclonus was followed by recurrence a few days after discharge.[6] In a case report on refractory status epilepticus, thiopentone given as a therapeutic agent has been shown to produce myoclonus.[7] Myoclonus can also be induced by spinal anesthesia, which typically involves the particular spinal segment rather than all groups of muscles.[8]

The unique features observed in the above two patients are: (a) the myoclonus appeared after a normal latent period of 3 and 6 days following general anesthesia; (b) it was mainly action-induced, though mildly present at rest; (c) it was more restricted to the upper limbs and trunk; and (d) it responded well to oral clonazepam and valproate/levetiracetam within a few days, without any relapse. This type of presentation has not been well described in the literature. These movements hindered the process of postoperative rehabilitation of the patients. As it was restricted to a few segments of the body and mainly action-induced, it is presumed to be cortical-reflex myoclonus.[9] However, the EEG was normal in one patient and showed occasional sharp wave discharges in the other. Detailed electrophysiological studies, such as somatosensory evoked potentials (SEP), were not done.

The exact pathogenesis of delayed-onset postoperative myoclonus is not well understood. It is probably due to propofol, which may desensitize the chloride channel and prevent the inhibitory action of gamma-aminobutyric acid, thereby increasing the excitation of neurons, particularly in the thalamocortical circuit.[5610] It may be partly explained by hypoxic damage during cardiac surgery, causing a lack of inhibition in the neuronal circuit. However, the reason for involvement of only certain selected individuals is unknown. The explanation for the delayed onset of myoclonus is also not clear, and it could be due to the metabolites of propofol, which may persist for a longer period, producing neuroexcitation.[10]

Benzodiazepine is used for the management of this type of myoclonus[5]. However, in general, the movements are self-limiting, and even cases resistant to the drug may resolve spontaneously within a period of a few hours to 3 weeks.[10] In the above two patients, a combination of clonazepam and valproate/levetiracetam controlled the myoclonus over a short period, and the drugs could be tapered off without any further relapse.

It is a rare yet important neurological phenomenon in the postoperative period, characterized by its benign nature. It occurs following general anesthesia after a normal latent period of a few days, interferes with the patient’s rehabilitation, and responds well to clonazepam with valproate/levetiracetam without any relapse. Early recognition and prompt treatment result in early rehabilitation and better outcomes for the patient.
