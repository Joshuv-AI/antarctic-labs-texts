Refining the Classification of Steatotic Liver Disease: Beyond Self-Reported Alcohol Intake
Authors: Won-Mook Choi
Published: 2025

The nomenclature and diagnostic framework for fatty liver disease have undergone significant evolution over the past two decades. Initially conceptualized as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, the entity was defined by the exclusion of significant alcohol consumption and other secondary causes of steatosis. However, this “negative definition” was criticized for neglecting the central role of metabolic dysfunction and for the stigmatizing dichotomy between “alcoholic” and “non-alcoholic” forms. In 2020, an expert consensus proposed the term metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, which used positive diagnostic criteria emphasizing metabolic risk factors, while tolerating modest alcohol use. More recently, a multi-society panel has introduced steatotic liver disease (SLD) as an umbrella term, subclassified into metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD), and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), alongside other specific etiologies.1,2 While this revised classification aimed to be more inclusive, clinically relevant, and less stigmatizing, it remains heavily reliant on self-reported alcohol intake for the distinction between MASLD, MetALD, and ALD—a feature that is inherently vulnerable to misclassification.3 Such reliance can influence risk stratification, prognostic assessment, and ultimately patient management.4

In this issue of Gut and Liver, Lee et al.5 report an important nationwide cohort study that directly addresses this vulnerability. Using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, they identified adults with SLD based on a hepatic steatosis index (HSI) >36 and categorized them into MASLD, MetALD, or ALD using self-reported alcohol consumption thresholds. Alcohol use disorder (AUD) was defined by International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes. Among over 3 million participants, they found that 1.1% of MASLD and 4.7% of MetALD patients had concomitant AUD. Importantly, MASLD patients with AUD (i.e., likely misclassified as MASLD based on self-reported intake) had substantially higher risks of liver-related mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 6.53) and all-cause mortality (aHR, 1.97), and MetALD patients with AUD (i.e., likely misclassified as MetALD based on self-reported intake) had corresponding aHRs of 6.98 and 2.12, respectively, compared with MASLD patients without AUD. In addition, both MASLD and MetALD patients with AUD showed significantly elevated risks of extrahepatic cancer-related mortality and cardiovascular disease–related mortality, further highlighting the broad systemic impact of alcohol misuse beyond liver-specific outcomes.

While the study is both timely and provocative, several methodological considerations merit discussion. First, the operational definition of SLD relied on the HSI, with a threshold of >36 to define steatosis. Although this cutoff yields high specificity, it risks missing a substantial proportion of true SLD cases with HSI scores ≤36, thereby limiting generalizability and introducing selection bias.6 Second, the differentiation among MASLD, MetALD, and ALD was based entirely on self-reported alcohol consumption, which is prone to recall bias, systematic underreporting, and failure to capture dynamic fluctuations in drinking patterns. Indeed, in this dataset, approximately 7% of individuals shifted between MASLD and MetALD categories when comparing two survey periods (2009 to 2010 vs 2011 to 2012), illustrating the instability of these classifications, although temporal changes in alcohol intake patterns may also have contributed to this reclassification. Third, AUD is a behavioral diagnosis rather than a quantitative measure of alcohol intake. It reflects patterns of alcohol-related harm and maladaptive use rather than specific thresholds of consumption. As such, AUD diagnosis alone cannot be directly mapped to MASLD, MetALD, or ALD categories, which are defined by absolute daily intake thresholds. Fourth, in the context of a national claims database, AUD identification relied on ICD-10 codes rather than validated screening instruments such as the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test–Consumption (AUDIT-C) or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5)–based assessments, which may lead to under- or overestimation of AUD prevalence.

Despite these limitations, the study’s core message is compelling: the current SLD subclassification system, as operationalized in both clinical and research settings, is vulnerable to misclassification when it depends solely on patient-reported alcohol consumption. This vulnerability has meaningful implications for prognosis estimation and therapeutic decision-making. The problem is further compounded by the inconsistent findings across existing literature—some studies have reported poorer outcomes in MetALD compared to MASLD,7 while others have found no difference, or even better outcomes for MetALD.8,9 Such discrepancies may, in part, reflect the imprecision inherent in self-reported alcohol data, which are typically based on the reported frequency and quantity of drinking over the past year and are susceptible to recall bias, episodic binge drinking, seasonal variations in alcohol consumption, and systematic underreporting.

To advance the field, more accurate tools for quantifying alcohol intake are urgently needed. Further research is warranted to determine whether objective biomarkers, such as phosphatidylethanol and carbohydrate-deficient transferrin,10 can help refine SLD classification by providing more reliable detection and quantification of recent alcohol use. Systematic and comprehensive alcohol history-taking—including longitudinal assessment of lifetime consumption, drinking patterns, and episodic heavy use—should complement biomarker data to capture the complex and fluctuating nature of alcohol exposure. Integrating these measures into future studies may enhance SLD classification, though additional research is needed to clarify their impact on prognostication and personalized management.

In conclusion, while the revised SLD nomenclature represents an important conceptual advance, its reliance on self-reported alcohol consumption remains a critical limitation. The study by Lee et al.5 provides valuable evidence that this approach can misclassify patients in ways that have tangible prognostic consequences. Moving forward, the hepatology community should prioritize the incorporation of objective alcohol use assessment into both research protocols and clinical workflows. Doing so will not only enhance our understanding of MASLD, MetALD, and ALD but will also optimize the care of patients across the SLD spectrum.
