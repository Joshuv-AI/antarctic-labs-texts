Work-family conflict and intimate partner violence among Chinese female nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hongmei Ma, Jie Dong, Yingxia Li, Qian Ning Leong, Xiao Ju Teng, Liping Yuan, Mingfen Tao, Ying Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among Chinese female nurses and examine the association between IPV exposure, independent sociodemographic predictors, and work-family conflict.MethodsThis cross-sectional study surveyed 341 female nurses across five hospitals in China from August 14 to September 16, 2022. Validated instruments included the Chinese version of the Short Form Conflict Tactics Scale-2 (CTS2S) and the Work-Family Conflict Scale. Data were analysed using multivariate logistic regression to identify independent risk factors.ResultsThe overall prevalence of IPV was 42.82% (n = 146). Cold violence was the most frequent type (34.02%), followed by psychological violence (23.75%), injury (5.28%), sexual coercion (4.11%) and physical violence (3.24%). Multivariate logistic regression showed that higher work-to-family conflict, being aged 31 to 40 years, and dissatisfaction with salary were the predictors of IPV exposure (p < 0.05).ConclusionIPV is highly prevalent among Chinese female nurses and is influenced by multiple sociodemographic and work-related factors. Given the significant impact of IPV on both work and family functioning, greater attention and targeted interventions are needed to support nurses affected by IPV and to mitigate its broader occupational and personal consequences.Clinical trial registration numberNot applicable.Keywords: Intimate partner violence, Female nurses, Related factors, Work-family conflict

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is a behaviour within an intimate relationship that causes physical, sexual, or psychological harm, including physical aggression, sexual coercion, psychological abuse and controlling behaviours [58]. It ranges from physical violence like pushing, shoving, assault, and hitting, to controlling behaviours such as isolating victims from loved ones, monitoring their activities, and limiting their access to information and assistance [19]. IPV occurs mainly between current or former intimate partners [33].

IPV has been a global public health priority for more than two decades [43]. It affects people of all ages and genders, but women bear a disproportionate burden [45]. About 27% of ever-partnered women aged between 15 and 49 years have experienced physical and/or sexual IPV [44], with prevalence estimates ranging from 10% to 69% across settings [29]. A recent meta-analysis of 201 studies (250,599 women) reported that lifetime psychological violence was the most prevalent form, with prevalence rates differing significantly between low- and middle-income and high-income settings [57]. Of the many contributing risk factors, gender inequality is the most prominent [59]. Nursing remains a predominantly female profession, and several studies report a high prevalence of IPV among nurses [4, 7, 31]. In China, a recent study found 58.1% of Intensive Care Unit nurses experienced at least one form of IPV [61].

Victims of IPV often experience chronic pain [6], psychological distress [28], and poor social adjustment [8], all of which disrupt daily life and reduce work productivity [27, 40]. Chronic pain alone significantly lowers quality of life and can reduce weekly work performance by 2.4 to 9.8 h [27]. Physical and psychological consequences of IPV frequently reinforce one another, as chronic pain and depression have a reciprocal relationship, increasing the likelihood of co-occurring symptoms [53]. Depression, anxiety, and PTSD-related somatic symptoms, such as sleep disturbances, further impair job performance [3]. Over time, these cumulative effects worsen victims’ overall quality of life and functioning across multiple domains.

These consequences can be explained through the Conservation of Resources theory, which posits that psychological strain occurs when valued resources, such as personal energy, favourable conditions, or self-efficacy, are threatened or lost [22]. The theory asserts that resource loss triggers a “loss spiral,” leaving individuals increasingly vulnerable to future stressors. In this context, IPV and work-family conflict act as simultaneous chronic stressors that continuously drain a nurse’s finite resources. Without adequate buffers, nurses become highly susceptible to chronic pain, distress, and poor social adjustment.

While many studies on IPV against women focus on groups such as pregnant women, disadvantaged populations, and university students [18], there is a clear gap in research specifically addressing IPV among nurses, a profession predominantly occupied by women. Nurses play a critical role in healthcare, providing direct and indirect care to patients and their families, coordinating clinical activities, and exercising organizational leadership [2]. The weight of these duties, combined with a demanding hospital work environment, contributes to high levels of burnout among nurses across countries [54, 63]. For nurses who experience IPV, these challenges are further magnified.

IPV contributes to chronic pain, psychological distress, sleep disruption, and reduced functional capacity, which can impede nurses’ ability to perform clinical tasks effectively. Evidence shows that nurses with chronic pain or depression are more likely to experience declines in work productivity and quality of care, heightening risks for medical errors and reduced patient safety [17, 24, 25, 48]. At the same time, nurses frequently serve as frontline responders for patients facing IPV [10], making their own health and stability especially crucial [42]. Despite their important role, the prevalence of IPV among Chinese nurses remains unclear, and little is known about how IPV contributes to work–family conflict in this group. Given their dual responsibility as caregivers at work and partners at home, IPV not only harms nurses’ well-being but may also erode their capacity to support patients experiencing similar violence. This study aimed to address this gap by estimating the prevalence of IPV among Chinese female nurses and identifying factors associated with IPV among Chinese female nurses.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey study was conducted in five hospitals in Eastern China from 14th August to 16th September 2022. The ethical application was submitted through the Scientific Research Management Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College (Yijishan Hospital of Wannan Medical College) and was granted a review exemption. All participants have given consent and voluntarily participated in this study.

Sampling and study participants

A convenience sampling method was employed. Eligible participants working in the study hospitals were randomly selected and contacted by the research liaison office. The inclusion criteria were female working nurses (1) with a license issued by China’s health authorities, (2) aged 18 or older, and (3) having had a stable partner within the past year. Participants were excluded if they had a history of mental illness or were currently on psychiatric medications.

Data collection

Data were collected through a secure online questionnaire. The study’s purpose and informed consent information were presented on the first page of the survey. Participants would have to agree to participate in the study before proceeding to answer the questions; otherwise, they could exit the survey at any time if they did not wish to participate, and any withdrawn or incomplete responses were discarded. Completion and submission of the questionnaire were considered to indicate informed consent and voluntary participation.

Sample size

Based on the pre-survey results, the estimated prevalence rate is 25%. Using G*Power software (version 3.1) for a two‑sided test with α = 0.05, power = 0.90, and an estimated prevalence of 25%, the calculated minimum sample size was 306 participants. To account for anticipated incomplete or invalid responses, a 10% allowance was added, giving a target of 337 participants. A total of 387 questionnaires were distributed, of which 46 were excluded (male respondents who did not meet the female-nurse inclusion criterion, together with any incomplete or invalid responses), leaving 341 valid responses for analysis. Therefore, the overall response rate of 88.1%.

Measurements

Intimate partner violence (IPV)

IPV was measured using the Chinese version of the Short Form Conflict Tactics Scale-2 (CTS2S) [49], translated and validated by Pan et al. [39]. The CTS2S uses a paired-item format in which each behaviour is assessed by two mirror items, one on the respondent’s own acts (perpetration; e.g., “I threw something at my partner) and one on the partner’s acts (victimisation; e.g., “My partner threw something at me”). Only the victimisation items were analysed. Responses use an 8-point frequency scale (0 = never; 1 = once; 2 = twice; 3 = 3–5; 4 = 6–10; 5 = 11–20; 6 = > 20 times; 7 = not in the past year but happened before). Prior testing of the CTS2S victimisation items reported an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.84 (subscale range 0.64–0.94) [64]. In the current sample, the overall Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.87.

The original CTS2S comprises five subscales, of which the “negotiation” subscale is non-violent and was excluded from prevalence estimation. We assessed the four standard violence subscales: “psychological violence,” “physical violence,” “sexual coercion,” and “injury,” and additionally incorporated a “cold violence” subscale. “Cold violence” is a passive, withdrawal-based form of emotional abuse marked by silence, indifference, and the deliberate withholding of communication, care, and affection [36]; it is conceptually distinct from the overt verbal hostility captured by “psychological violence” and is increasingly recognised as a culturally salient form of IPV in China [36, 52]. Previous Chinese IPV research has similarly supplemented the CTS2 with a separate “cold violence” subscale [30]. We therefore operationalised IPV across the five forms, while noting that “cold violence” is not part of the original CTS2S.

For each form, IPV exposure was coded as a binary variable: a respondent was classified as exposed if she endorsed any non-zero frequency on at least one item of that subscale. Overall, IPV exposure was defined as experiencing at least one act across any of the five violence subscales.

Work-family conflict scale

Participants’ work-family conflict was measured using the Work-Family Conflict Scale originally developed by Netemeyer, Boles, and McMurrian [37]. The scale consists of two separate subscales, each measuring a different direction: Work-to-Family Conflict (WFC) and Family-to-Work Conflict (FWC). The subscale comprises five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Subscale scores were calculated by summing the item scores and dividing by the number of items, with higher scores indicating greater conflict. The Chinese version of the scale has been validated among Chinese parents, demonstrating adequate reliability with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.92 for the WFC subscale and 0.91 for the FWC subscale [26].

Sociodemographic data

Participants’ sociodemographic data were collected using researcher-developed questionnaires that included age, years of work, job title, education, hospital level, department, marital status, fertility, number of evening and night shifts, and salary.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using JAMOVI software. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean, and standard deviation (SD), were used to summarize the participants’ sociodemographic data. Initially, univariate analysis identified potential sociodemographic and other correlated variables associated with IPV. In the logistic regression analysis, the dependent variable was a binary indicator of IPV (yes/no). Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 in the univariate analysis were considered as independent variables (IVs) for subsequent multivariable logistic regression. The parsimonious final model was selected using a backward-step elimination method based on Wald’s statistics. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values exceeding 5 indicating potential multicollinearity. All levels of statistical significance were set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Overview of key findings

This study explored the prevalence of IPV among female nurses in China and its independent predictors. We found a high prevalence of IPV among female nurses, with 42.8% reporting some form of abuse. Nurses who experienced IPV also reported significantly higher levels of work-family conflicts (both WFC and FWC). This study is the first in China to demonstrate this association within the nursing profession. In addition, being aged 31 to 40 years, dissatisfaction with salary, and higher WFC were the significant predictors of IPV exposure. We interpret these associations through the Conservation of Resources theory, which frames IPV and work-family conflict as mutually reinforcing processes of resource depletion rather than a one-directional, causal sequence [22]. These findings are broadly consistent with previous research linking IPV to impaired work performance, reduced concentration, and increased psychological strain [46, 47, 62].

Prevalence and forms of IPV

Globally, IPV remains a major public health concern [38], with an estimated 37.3% of women aged 16 years and above having experienced IPV in their lifetime [57]. Evidence specific to nurses is limited, but a recent meta-analysis reported that 31.3% of healthcare workers had experienced abuse from a family member or partner, most commonly from an intimate partner [12]. Our overall prevalence of 42.8% is consistent with Chinese epidemiological data. A national survey of 31,449 residents across 148 cities reported an overall IPV prevalence of 45.8% [65], and Yan et al. [61] found that 58.1% of ICU nurses in China had experienced at least one form of IPV. Our higher prevalence relative to global estimates may also reflect how IPV was measured. In one meta-analysis, the choice of measurement tool alone accounted for 54% of the variation in lifetime prevalence across studies [56]. Our study assessed five forms of IPV, including cold violence and psychological aggression, whereas global estimates focus mainly on physical and sexual violence [44]. In addition, we counted a behaviour as present if it occurred at least once. Our higher estimate, therefore, reflects a more comprehensive measurement of IPV rather than an unusually violent sample.

Cold violence emerged as the single most prevalent form of IPV in our sample (34.0%), exceeding overt psychological aggression (23.8%). Cold violence, defined as a passive, withdrawal-based form of emotional abuse marked by silence, indifference, and the withholding of communication and affection, is a recognised and measurable construct in China [60], and it has been linked to serious psychological harm [52]. Its prominence fits the cultural context of Chinese intimate relationships, in which Confucian ideals of family harmony and the framing of marital conflict as a private “family affair” discourage open confrontation and help-seeking [15, 50, 55]. In such a context, abuse may more often be enacted through emotional withdrawal rather than the overt verbal aggression commonly reported in the Western literature. In addition, its invisibility can allow perpetrators to maintain a socially acceptable image while evading scrutiny despite legal protections enacted since 2016 [20]. Capturing cold violence may therefore provide a more culturally valid picture of emotional IPV among Chinese women.

Sexual coercion and physical violence were reported least frequently, likely due to substantial underreporting. Sexual coercion is closely linked to shame, self-blame, and identity disruption [21], and long-standing societal narratives can foster internalised shame and victim self-blame [16]. Among nurses, concerns about maintaining a caring, respectable professional image may further discourage disclosure of sexual or physical violence [14].

Predicting factors associated with IPV

Nurses aged 31 to 40 were the most vulnerable group, exhibiting significantly higher odds of experiencing IPV compared with both younger and older nurses. This stage coincides with the mid-career period in Chinese nursing (8–18 years of experience), when many nurses move into middle- or senior-management roles accompanied by heightened occupational stress [32]. Their risk is compounded by “sandwich generation” demands, simultaneously caring for young children and ageing parents, resulting in substantial role strain [1]. From the Conservation of Resources theory perspective, the convergence of escalating work and family demands at this stage may leave mid-career nurses with fewer reserves to regulate emotion and negotiate conflict, which may be associated with greater vulnerability to IPV [9, 22].

Higher WFC were also associated with increased odds of IPV, and the Conservation of Resources theory offers a coherent lens for this association. WFC reflects a drain on finite personal resources, and such depletion can spill over into the home, where it has been shown to manifest as more withdrawn, angry, and less supportive behaviour toward one’s partner [41]. The loss of resources is self-reinforcing, with those who have fewer remaining resources being more susceptible to further loss [22]. Therefore, nurses depleted by IPV may find work-family demands harder to manage, while those strained by WFC may be less able to defuse conflict at home. Since our design is cross-sectional, our findings do not establish causality; rather, they suggest that these processes likely reinforce each other over time.

Salary dissatisfaction was also associated with IPV, but this finding warrants cautious interpretation because it reflects a subjective appraisal of economic adequacy rather than actual income. The relationship between women’s economic position and IPV is complex and not reducible to income alone. Previous studies have shown that perceived economic strain can heighten household tension and raise IPV risk independently of objective resources. This pattern has been reported among married women in China, where both self-perceived financial strain and low family income were associated with higher rates of IPV [34, 66]. This is consistent with the Conservation of Resources theory, in which a perceived threat to valued resources produces strain regardless of objective levels [22]. On the other hand, evidence regarding objective economic resources is mixed: women’s economic empowerment can decrease IPV by enhancing bargaining power and “exit options,” but it might also lead to partner backlash when it threatens traditional provider roles [13]. Since we assessed perceived satisfaction rather than income, our results suggest that feeling economically strained is associated with IPV. However, this does not mean that higher income directly protects against IPV.

Limitations

This is the first study to explore the link between IPV and work-family conflict among female nurses in China, filling a gap in research on IPV in the nursing profession, which is mainly female. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationship between IPV and work-family conflict. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability bias [5], which may lead participants to underreport sensitive experiences such as non-psychological forms of IPV. Third, the online survey format may have introduced self-selection bias, as individuals with stronger views or experiences related to IPV were more likely to participate [11]. Fourth, the study sample was drawn exclusively from hospitals in Eastern China. This regional context may limit the generalizability of our findings to nurses in other parts of China (e.g., central, western, or rural areas), where socioeconomic conditions, healthcare systems, and cultural norms regarding IPV may differ substantially. And fifth, our IPV measure incorporated a cold violence subscale that is not part of the standard CTS2S; although this strengthens cultural relevance, it complicates comparison with studies using the standard instrument.

Clinical implications

The high prevalence of IPV among female nurses has important work implications, as IPV survivors often experience reduced motivation, chronic fatigue, and compromised work performance [47]. Given the established link between nurses’ psychological health and the quality of care [23], IPV should not be overlooked. Workplace support is a critical protective factor for employees experiencing IPV [35]. Interventions such as supervisor training, confidential referral pathways, flexible scheduling, and access to counselling services may mitigate its occupational impact, and employee assistance programs can improve job retention among survivors [51]. By reducing the spillover of IPV-related stress into clinical practice, such strategies may enhance both nurse well-being and patient safety.

Conclusion

This study examined the prevalence of IPV among female nurses in China and its predictive factors. The study found that close to half of the participants reported experiencing IPV, with cold violence emerging as the most prevalent subtype. Independent predictors of IPV exposure include high WFC, being aged 31 to 40 (corresponding to mid-career status), and perceived salary dissatisfaction. These results suggest that IPV may act as a chronic stressor associated with depletion of the emotional and relational resources essential for managing work and family demands.

While causal inferences are not possible, the findings underscore the importance of addressing IPV among healthcare professionals as both a public health and an occupational concern. Future longitudinal and intervention-focused studies are warranted to develop targeted prevention strategies and establish a support system within the clinical environments.
