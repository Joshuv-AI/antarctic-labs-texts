Emerging crops and plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB): a synergistic approach to climate-resilient agriculture
Authors: Francisco Pérez-Montaño, Nieves Aparicio, Francisco Arenas, Jose M. Arjona, María Camacho, Nieves Fernández-García, Paula García-Fraile, Nieves Goicoechea, Sandra Macías-Naranjo, Javier Matías, María del Carmen Montero-Calasanz, Asunción Morte, Enrique Olmos, José J. Pueyo, Miguel A. Quiñones, Luis Rey, María Reguera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This review highlights the benefits of mutualistic plant–microbe interactions in enhancing the resilience of emergent crops and underlies the potential of these crops as valuable resources for exploring novel plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB). Emergent crops such as quinoa, amaranth, millet, lupins, hemp and desert truffles exhibit physiological and ecological traits that make them suitable for stress-prone environments. PGPB offer sustainable solutions to mitigate abiotic stress by improving nutrient availability, modulating phytohormone levels, enhancing root development and inducing systemic resistance. While their benefits have been extensively documented in model crops under controlled conditions, their application in emergent crops remains underexplored. This review examines current knowledge on the individual and combined roles of these crops and microbes, highlighting specific examples where PGPB have improved plant performance, yield and stress tolerance. Microbial inoculants show potential not only to boost productivity but also to reduce agrochemical inputs, contributing to sustainability. The review also discusses the need for tailored microbial formulations and effective field application strategies to bridge the gap between experimental research and real-world agricultural practices. Identifying key knowledge gaps, it emphasizes the importance of further research on strain specificity, crop-microbe interactions and multi-strain consortia for scalable and climate-smart agriculture. Ultimately, harnessing the synergisms between PGPB with emergent crops could convert marginal lands into productive, climate-smart farms, increasing food security in the face of environmental challenges posed by global climate change.

Introduction

Climate change poses an unprecedented challenge to global agriculture, impacting crop productivity and food security through increasing temperatures, erratic precipitation and extreme weather events [1]. These environmental stressors intensify soil degradation, exacerbate water scarcity and threaten conventional crop systems, demanding innovative approaches to sustain agricultural yields. In response, emergent crops and plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) have gained attention as sustainable strategies to enhance crop resilience under changing climatic conditions [2, 3]. Emergent crops such as quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa), amaranth (Amaranthus spp.), lupins (Lupinus spp.), hemp (Cannabis sativa) and desert truffles (Terfezia and Tirmania spp.) are increasingly recognised for their resilience to abiotic stresses, including drought and poor soils, as well as for their rich nutritional profiles. These traits, including drought tolerance, efficient nutrient uptake and adaptability to marginal environments, make them promising alternatives for sustainable agriculture in climate-vulnerable regions such as the Mediterranean and arid zones, where water scarcity and soil degradation are critical challenges [3]. Their cultivation not only diversifies agricultural systems but also promotes food security and economic resilience [4]. Additionally, these crops exhibit outstanding nutritional properties. Quinoa and amaranth are rich in high-quality proteins with well-balanced essential amino acid profiles, particularly lysine, which is often deficient in cereals. Lupins are excellent sources of plant-based protein and dietary fibre, with low levels of antinutritional factors in modern varieties. Hemp seeds provide a balanced ratio of omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, along with bioactive compounds with potential health benefits. They are also an excellent source of high-quality protein due to their favourable amino acid profile and substantial protein content. Desert truffles, known for their high antioxidant content, polysaccharides and bioactive metabolites, have been traditionally consumed in arid regions for their nutritional and medicinal properties [3].

On the other hand, PGPB offer multiple benefits to plants by enhancing nutrient availability, improving water-use efficiency, modulating phytohormone production and inducing systemic resistance against pathogens [5, 6]. Well-documented examples include Azospirillum, Rhizobium, Bacillus and Pseudomonas species, which facilitate nitrogen fixation, phosphate and potassium solubilization, iron acquisition and the production and regulation of phytohormones, thereby improving plant growth under stress conditions [7–9]. Despite extensive research on PGPB in staple crops, their interaction with emerging crops remains largely unexplored. This gap presents a significant opportunity for developing sustainable, climate-resilient agriculture. This review examines the synergistic potential of emergent crops, which can be harnessed in Mediterranean areas, and PGPB to enhance crop resilience under climate change scenarios. It provides an overview of the physiological and ecological traits that make emergent crops suitable for stress-prone environments, discusses the role of PGPB in mitigating abiotic stress and examines existing knowledge on their combined application. Finally, it identifies knowledge gaps and future research directions needed to optimize their integration into climate-smart agriculture.

In summary, this work advocates for the innovative use of diverse microbial probiotics in emerging crops, paving the way for sustainable food production strategies that overcome the limitations of conventional agriculture in the face of global climate change.

Mechanisms Promoting Plant Growth under Changing Climate Scenarios

As mentioned earlier, the escalating effects of climate change pose significant threats to global agriculture, necessitating innovative and sustainable approaches to enhance plant productivity [10, 11]. Plant growth-promoting bacteria have emerged as promising agents in this context, exhibiting diverse mechanisms to support plant health and development, especially under various stress conditions such as drought or salinity [12–14]. Direct Mechanisms of Plant Growth Promotion

Direct mechanisms primarily involve the facilitation of nutrient uptake (biofertilization) and the modulation of plant hormonal status (phytostimulation). Thus, the first key direct mechanism is the enhancement of nutrient availability, particularly nitrogen, phosphorus and iron. Bacteria that promote plant growth by improving plant nutrient uptake are termed biofertilizers.

Nitrogen fixation

Nitrogen fixation, a crucial function of specific rhizobacteria, involves converting atmospheric dinitrogen (N2) into plant-assimilable forms, such as ammonia (NH3). This process is vital in nutrient-poor soils, lessening reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers. Free-living PGPB, such as Azospirillum and Azotobacter, independently fix nitrogen, particularly benefiting cereal grasses, enhancing their resilience to climate change-induced stresses like drought and salinity [15]. Symbiotic biofertilizers, including Rhizobium, Sinorhizobium (Ensifer), Bradyrhizobium and other genera, form mutualistic relationships with legumes, fixing nitrogen within root nodules, an important mechanism for maintaining soil fertility under fluctuating environmental conditions [16].

Phosphorus solubilization

Phosphorus (P), a vital macronutrient, is often found in soils in unavailable, insoluble forms, which limits its uptake by plants. PGPB enhance P availability through the production of organic acids, such as gluconic and 2-ketogluconic acids, and phosphatases, which solubilize inorganic phosphate. Diverse bacteria, including Providencia rettgeri, Advenella incenata, Acinetobacter calcoaceticus, Serratia plymuthica, Bacillus spp. and Pseudomonas spp., effectively solubilize P, a critical function under climate change where P deficiencies are exacerbated. For example, a halotolerant phosphate-solubilizing Bacillus megaterium strain enhances plant growth under salt stress by improving phosphorus availability, which suppresses the adverse effects of salt [17]. Phosphorus biofertilizers produce organic acids that chelate cations bound to phosphate and/or synthesize enzymes (e.g. phosphatases) that hydrolyse organic phosphate compounds. These processes release the phosphate ions (PO₄3⁻) into the soil solution, making them more available for plant uptake [18].

Siderophore production

Iron (Fe) is vital for plant growth, participating in key processes like chlorophyll synthesis and respiration, but its availability is often limited by its low solubility in many soils, especially under alkaline conditions. Rhizobacteria overcome this limitation by synthesizing and releasing siderophores, low-molecular-weight molecules with a high affinity for Fe3+. These siderophores chelate Fe3+, converting it into soluble forms readily available for plant uptake [19]. For example, Pseudomonas fluorescens produces siderophores such as pyoverdine that facilitate iron acquisition, aiding plant growth even under iron-deficient and stressful conditions. Under heavy metal stress, siderophore production is often stimulated by the plant, further enhancing metal uptake by the bacteria [20]. This mechanism is crucial in climate change scenarios, where nutrient deficiencies can be aggravated due to increased soil degradation, altered nutrient cycling and reduced nutrient availability driven by extreme weather events and changes in temperature and precipitation patterns. Furthermore, the ability of siderophores to sequester iron also plays a role in biocontrol, limiting iron availability to pathogenic microorganisms and thus protecting the plant [6].

In addition to nutrient acquisition, some PGPB, named phytostimulators, can directly influence plant growth by producing and regulating plant hormones.

Auxin production

Auxins, such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), are fundamental phytohormones that regulate essential processes such as cell elongation, root architecture and overall plant development and are produced by a wide range of bacteria. Some PGPB synthesize and release IAA, directly stimulating root development. The subsequently enhanced root system improves water and nutrient uptake, thereby contributing to drought stress tolerance and promoting overall plant growth [21]. Furthermore, IAA synthesis by probiotic bacteria has been linked to the alleviation of salinity stress. For instance, the halophilic bacterium Leclercia adecarboxylata strain MO1 produces IAA and improves plant growth under saline stress, making it particularly valuable in climate change scenarios where soil salinity is increasing [22] and several IAA-synthesizing Pseudomonas have been reported to alleviate the salt stress of wheat grown under conditions of soil salinity [23]. Additionally, Neptunomonas spp., isolated from red algae and seagrass, also produce IAA, highlighting the ubiquity of IAA production among diverse bacterial genera and their potential for promoting plant growth and resilience in various environments [24].

Gibberellin production

Gibberellins (GAs) are a class of phytohormones crucial for various aspects of plant development, including stem elongation, seed germination, flowering, fruit ripening and, importantly, stress tolerance, and their production is not exclusive to plants. Several PGPB strains produce diverse GAs, which can enhance plant growth, especially under abiotic stress conditions. For example, Bacillus tequilensis strain SSB07 produces a variety of GAs that increase shoot length and biomass under heat stress conditions, which demonstrates its role in mitigating the effects of extreme temperatures in climate change scenarios [25]. Similarly, Promicromonospora sp. SE188 produces gibberellins and enhances plant growth, further emphasizing the potential of bacterial-produced GAs to improve plant resilience and productivity [26].

Cytokinin production

Cytokinins play a vital role in regulating plant physiology, particularly in cell division, differentiation and responses to environmental stresses. Some phytostimulators can synthesize cytokinins, enhancing plant growth and stress tolerance, crucial for mitigating the adverse effects of climate change on agriculture. Specifically, certain strains of Bacillus aryabhattai, Pseudomonas stutzeri, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia and P. putida significantly promote plant development through cytokinin production, influencing hormone levels, photosynthetic processes and defence mechanisms [27, 28].

Abscisic acid modulation

Abscisic acid (ABA) is fundamental in controlling plant responses to various environmental stresses, particularly those exacerbated by climate change. During stress, ABA levels increase in the roots, resulting in an expansion of the root system. This increases the plant’s water absorption capacity. In the aerial parts, high ABA levels cause stomata to close, which can improve water use efficiency. However, sustained high ABA levels can negatively affect photosynthesis and source-sink relationships, ultimately reducing yields [29]. Some bacteria can alter ABA levels, impacting stress tolerance. For example, Dietzia natronolimnaea strain STR1 influences the ABA-signalling pathway by upregulating ABA-responsive genes, thereby improving salt stress tolerance [30]. Additionally, certain phytostimulators, like P. putida strain MTCC5279, can directly produce ABA, providing plants with enhanced tolerance to salt and drought [31]. Furthermore, some rhizosphere bacteria, such as Bacillus subtilis IB-22 and Pseudomonas mandelii IB-Ki14, regulate ABA under salinity stress by increasing ABA in roots to promote root growth and water uptake, while reducing ABA in leaves to prevent chlorophyll loss. This ultimately boosts plant performance under saline conditions [32]. Overall, bacterial modulation of ABA levels is a vital mechanism that helps plants better endure the increasingly severe conditions associated with climate change, influencing their growth and survival.

Ethylene reduction

Some bacteria play a central role in modulating ethylene levels within plants, particularly under the increasing pressures of climate change, thereby affecting plant growth and stress responses. Ethylene, a gaseous phytohormone exhibiting diverse biological activities, is often induced by both biotic and abiotic stresses, leading to potential growth inhibition and accelerated senescence. However, PGPB can produce the enzyme ACC deaminase, which degrades the ethylene precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC). By reducing the levels of ACC and, subsequently, ethylene, some bacteria alleviate stress-induced limitations on plant growth, promote root development and enhance nutrient uptake, all of which are particularly beneficial in the face of climate-related stresses such as drought and salinity [33, 34]. Notably, ACC deaminase-producing bacteria such as Paenibacillus sp. ANR-ACC3 and Methylophaga sp. AR-ACC3 efficiently relieve submergence stress in rice plants, leading to growth stimulation in seedling vigour, root and shoot length and total chlorophyll concentration [35].

Indirect Mechanisms of Plant Growth Promotion

Indirect mechanisms of plant growth promotion involve the protection of plants against biotic (biocontrol) and abiotic stresses (rhizoremediation and stress control). These mechanisms include competitive exclusion of pathogens, antagonism, induction of systemic resistance and alleviation of several abiotic stresses, including pollutants.

In the context of biocontrol, microorganisms can enhance plant growth by:

Competition with pathogens

By competing for essential resources such as nutrients and space within the rhizosphere, PGPB directly diminish pathogens’ capacity to colonise and infect host plants. This creates a competitive environment where the establishment of pathogenic microorganisms is significantly limited, thereby enhancing overall plant health and resilience [36]. This competitive exclusion strategy is a key aspect of bacterial-mediated biocontrol, contributing to sustainable agriculture in the face of increasing disease pressures due to climate variability. Interestingly, siderophore synthesis, involving the chelation of iron for uptake into microbial cells via specific receptors, also enhances iron availability for plants and functions as a competitive mechanism against other microorganisms lacking the receptors for the chelated iron.

Pathogen antagonism

Some bacteria employ a diverse arsenal of antimicrobial compounds to combat phytopathogens, a crucial function that becomes increasingly important under evolving environmental stressors. This multifaceted approach not only directly suppresses pathogen growth but also contributes to the establishment of a protective rhizosphere that enhances plant resilience against diseases, particularly in fluctuating and stressful environmental conditions. Biocontrol agents synthesize antibiotics, toxins, hydrogen cyanide (HCN), lytic enzymes and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that inhibit pathogen proliferation and activity [37]. Thus, some bacteria produce antibiotics and toxins that directly inhibit pathogen growth, while others synthesize fungal cell wall-lysing enzymes, such as chitinases and glucanases, that degrade pathogen cell walls [38]. Regarding VOCs, certain PGPB, such as species of Bacillus, Paraburkholderia, Streptomyces and Pseudomonas, release volatiles that can suppress pathogen growth or induce systemic resistance in plants, thereby promoting plant health and growth [39].

A well-known example of the role of such microbial communities is found in disease-suppressive soils, where the presence of specific beneficial bacteria naturally inhibits soil-borne pathogens. For instance, soils suppressive to Take-all disease in wheat are enriched in P. fluorescens strains producing 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG), which is critical for pathogen suppression [40, 41]. These disease-suppressive microbiomes demonstrate the ecological potential of beneficial microbes in managing plant diseases under field conditions.

Induced systemic resistance

Climate change can promote the emergence and spread of pests and diseases, against which some plants can activate defence mechanisms such as induced systemic resistance (ISR). This response is associated with protection against biotic stresses, such as pathogen or insect attacks and involves the activation of various plant defence mechanisms, including the production of defence-related proteins and enzymes, which collectively fortify the plant’s ability to withstand infections [42]. For instance, P. aeruginosa 7NSK2 has been shown to induce systemic resistance to Botrytis cinerea in tomatoes, while Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SN13 enhances plant tolerance by activating defence pathways. Furthermore, PGPB influence the expression of defence genes such as RuPR1, RuPR2 (β−1,3-glucanase) and RuPR3 (chitinase), which are crucial for the plant’s immune response, highlighting the complex and multifaceted ways in which PGPB contribute to plant health [43].

Rhizoremediation and stress control can boost plant tolerance to various abiotic stresses, such as drought, salinity and heavy metal toxicity. Thus, these stress controllers are emerging as crucial players in mitigating the adverse effects of climate change on plant health, exhibiting diverse mechanisms to combat abiotic stresses.

Drought

Certain bacterial strains secrete exopolysaccharides (EPS), which are essential for maintaining soil moisture by forming a protective barrier that minimizes water loss and safeguards plants from desiccation. These EPS also enhance soil aggregation, which further improves water retention capabilities within the rhizosphere [44]. Moreover, PGPB facilitate the accumulation of compatible solutes, such as proline and trehalose, within plant cells. These osmolytes play a vital role in maintaining cellular turgor and osmotic balance, which is critical for plant survival under conditions of water scarcity. By enabling plants to retain water, these bacteria minimize the cellular damage associated with dehydration. Furthermore, some bacteria enhance the antioxidant status of plants under drought by increasing the production of antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase, which scavenge reactive oxygen species and reduce oxidative damage [45].

Salinity

Some bacteria improve plant tolerance by regulating ion homeostasis and diminishing the uptake of sodium (Na+). For instance, B. subtilis GB03 has been shown to decrease Na+ accumulation in roots, thereby increasing plant tolerance to this element. The expression of key genes involved in ion homeostasis is modulated by PGPB to alleviate salt stress, allowing the plant to maintain essential potassium-sodium ratios (K+/Na+) [46]. Additionally, some bacteria augment proline content and the expression of antioxidant enzymes under salt stress, helping plants withstand osmotic and oxidative damage [13]. Furthermore, B. halotolerans SCCPVE07, harbouring a wide range of genes important in the osmotic response, such as choline utilization genes and proline synthesis, was found to promote plant growth and nutrient content, including bioactive compounds, under salinity stress conditions [47].

Heavy metals

Some bacteria mitigate the stress by reducing the uptake of heavy metals and augmenting the plant’s detoxification processes. Stress controllers can actively reduce the toxicity of specific heavy metals such as chromium, while others produce enzymes that reduce metal toxicity. The production of phytochelatins by plants, which is induced by some bacteria, aids in the immobilization of heavy metals within plant tissues, thus limiting the cellular damage [48]. These multifaceted mechanisms provide crucial support to plant health and growth under adverse conditions, making them indispensable in strategies to improve plant resilience and agricultural sustainability amidst increasing climate variability.

Use of PGPB on emergent crops: from laboratory to field conditions

Plant growth-promoting bacteria have emerged as a promising strategy to enhance crop productivity and resilience under abiotic stress conditions [5]. These beneficial microbes, including bacteria and fungi, facilitate plant growth by improving nutrient availability, modulating phytohormone levels and inducing systemic resistance [49]. While extensive research has demonstrated their efficacy in model crops under controlled conditions, their application in emergent crops, such as quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa), amaranth (Amaranthus spp.), millet (several genera of Poaceae), lupins (Lupinus spp.), hemp (Cannabis sativa) and desert truffles (Terfezia and Tirmania spp.) remains underexplored. These crops are gaining attention for their nutritional value, adaptability to marginal environments and potential for sustainable agriculture, particularly in Mediterranean regions characterized by increasing drought and soil degradation [3]. Quinoa, amaranth, millet and lupins are recognised for their drought resilience and capacity to grow in nutrient-poor soils, making them suitable alternatives to conventional crops in Mediterranean agroecosystems. Hemp is increasingly considered for fibre and seed production in southern Europe, as delaying the sowing date can decrease crop water requirements due to reduced vegetative growth under unfavourable photoperiodic conditions [50]. Meanwhile, desert truffles, naturally adapted to arid and semi-arid Mediterranean environments, have shown economic and ecological potential through symbiotic associations with host plants able to grow in nutrient-poor soils [51].

Thus, the novelty of these crops, combined with limited agronomic research, means there is a lack of field trials assessing the effectiveness, consistency and long-term impact of microbial inoculants under realistic cultivation scenarios. This gap hinders the development of crop-specific formulations, and the optimization of inoculation strategies adapted to diverse environments. Advancing field-based research is therefore essential to translate experimental findings into viable agricultural practices for these promising crops.

Lupins

Lupins (Lupinus spp.), among pulses, are a rich source of high-protein and nutrients. Lupinus is a diverse genus comprising approximately 200–500 species. Archaeological evidence suggests that lupins were cultivated in Greece and Egypt more than 4000 years ago. Lupinus albus was used in ancient Rome, while the Andean pearl lupin (L. mutabilis) was domesticated around 700 BCE in the Americas. Most wild lupin seeds contain alkaloids, but low-alkaloid (“sweet”) varieties have been developed through traditional breeding. The successful development of these sweet varieties has facilitated their cultivation in Europe and, particularly, in Australia, the world’s largest producer by far. Today, four species are of major agricultural importance: L. albus, L. angustifolius, L. luteus and L. mutabilis.

Despite their early introduction into agriculture, lupins are considered an emerging crop and a promising alternative to soybeans as a plant protein source, particularly in Europe, where climatic and soil conditions are not optimal for soybean cultivation [52]. Lupins have moderate water requirements and can grow in cool climates. Beyond their protein content, lupins provide nondigestible starch and unsaturated fats. Additionally, their unique physical properties, such as emulsification and foaming, allow for a broad range of applications in the food industry, including baked goods and meat alternatives. The health benefits of lupins, such as reducing LDL cholesterol levels and lowering blood pressure, further enhance their value as a functional legume crop [52]. Moreover, recent studies highlight the potential of lupins for heavy metal phytoremediation [53, 54].

As legumes, lupins fix atmospheric nitrogen in association with soil rhizobia, reducing dependence on nitrogen fertilizers. A recent paper reviewed the existing literature on lupin microsymbionts [55], and therefore, this paper does not focus on the interaction between lupins and rhizobia. Beyond the nitrogen-fixing microsymbionts, other PGPB are widely recognised for their ability to enhance lupin growth and improve stress tolerance (Table 1). These bacteria stimulate lupin development by producing phytohormones such as indole-3-acetic acid, gibberellins and cytokinins, which promote root growth and nutrient uptake [49]. While some lupin species, particularly L. albus, develop specialized root structures known as cluster roots, which mobilize otherwise unavailable soil phosphorus [72, 73], certain Bacillus and Pseudomonas strains also solubilize phosphates, making phosphorus more accessible to lupin plants [56, 74]. Moreover, PGPB have been linked to cluster root development, further enhancing phosphorus uptake [75]. Beyond growth promotion, these bacteria positively influence important agronomic traits in lupins. For instance, inoculation with a strain of Paenibacillus has been shown to enhance seed nutritional quality [57]. Additionally, PGPB contribute to stress tolerance. Regarding heavy metal stress, inoculation with specific Pseudomonas strains has been reported to improve growth, increase mercury tolerance, and reduce oxidative stress in lupins [62]. Similarly, Stenotrophomonas and Brevibacterium strains have exhibited PGP activity in lupins grown in nutrient-deficient mining soils [67]. PGPB are also known to trigger induced systemic tolerance, helping plants cope with salt and drought stress [76]. Recent research proposes the use of bacteria-inoculated lupins as a promising strategy for phytoremediating agricultural soils contaminated by petroleum derivatives [77]. Lupin’s PGPB also play a key role in plant disease resistance by producing antimicrobial compounds, competing with pathogens for space and nutrients, and activating systemic resistance. Rhizosphere bacteria can limit Fe availability to pathogenic microorganisms by producing siderophores. Hewedy et al. [65] reported that PGPB from the genera Serratia, Paenibacillus and Pseudomonas protected white lupin from Fusarium solani infection, particularly when co-inoculated with bradyrhizobia. Additionally, some bacteria contribute to soil health by decomposing organic matter and enhancing microbial diversity, indirectly benefiting lupin cultivation [78]. The synergistic relationship between PGPB and lupins highlights their potential as a sustainable agricultural tool. By leveraging these beneficial bacteria, farmers can enhance crop yield, reduce fertilizer inputs and boost resilience to both biotic and abiotic stresses, ultimately making lupin cultivation more sustainable and efficient [79].

Expanding our understanding of lupin–microbe interactions is essential to fully harness the crop’s potential. Recent findings indicate that the beneficial effects of PGPB may be comparable to those achieved through efficient rhizobia inoculation [80]. In parallel, as an emerging crop, lupins offer promising avenues for socio-economic development and environmental sustainability, particularly in the Mediterranean region but also on a global scale. These versatile legumes fit well into crop rotation systems for soil enrichment and hold great promise as a protein source. They also have applications in bioremediation and the extraction of bioactive compounds with potential health benefits. Comparative genomics will provide valuable insights into traits that can improve lupin adaptability to climate change and support future breeding efforts.

Millets

Millets are essential for global food security and nutrition, particularly in the arid and semi-arid regions of Africa and Asia [81]. To recognise and promote the role of millets in advancing climate-resilient agriculture, human nutrition and income generation for smallholder and indigenous farmers, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) declared 2023 the International Year of Millets. Millets belong to the Panicoideae or Chloridoideae subfamily of the Poaceae. As small-seeded C4 plants, they are drought-resistant, disease- and pest-resistant, hardy, and dryland crops that can adapt to various climate conditions, hence known as climate-resilient crops [82]. Millets are also known for their high nutritional value. They are classified into two categories based on grain size: major millets and minor millets. Major millets include sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum) and finger millet (Eleusine coracana). Minor millets comprise little millet (Panicum sumatrense), foxtail millet (Setaria italica), proso millet (Panicum miliaceum subsp. miliaceum), kodo millet (Paspalum scrobiculatum) and barnyard millet (Echinocloa esculenta and Echinocloa colona) [83].

Although millets exhibit resistance to various abiotic stresses induced by climate change, they are particularly cultivated in drylands with minimal water and fertilizer inputs [84]. In this context, beneficial rhizosphere microorganisms, such as PGPB, play a crucial role in enhancing millet resilience to abiotic stresses (Table 2). The composition of bacterial communities associated with different millet species can vary; however, environmental factors may influence such differences, particularly soil properties, rather than plant species alone [89]. Despite this, several PGPB have been reported in association with specific millet species. For instance, B. subtilis, B. cereus and Pantoea stewartii have been isolated from the rhizosphere of sorghum [85]; Azospirillum formosense, B. subtilis, B. pumilus, B. amyloliquefaciens, Shewanella putrefaciens, Cronobacter dublinensis, Pseudomonas spp., Acetobacter spp. and S. maltophilia were found in the rhizosphere of pearl millet; Burkholderia spp. and Azotobacter chroococcum from the rhizosphere of little millet; Bacillus spp., Rhizobium spp., P. fluorescens, P. migulae and Enterobacter hormaechei from foxtail millet and Dyadobacter spp. from finger millet. PGPB associated with millets contribute to nitrogen fixation, phosphorus or zinc solubilization, siderophore production, phytohormone synthesis, drought tolerance and disease resistance [86, 87, 90]. Although several studies support the beneficial effects of rhizosphere microorganisms on millet growth and yield [91, 92], their effectiveness depends on the microbial species [85] and the genetic variability among millet lines [93]. For instance, inoculating foxtail millet seeds with some PGPB improved shoot and root growth and increased yield parameters, with B. subtilis being the most effective microorganism [85]. In controlled greenhouse conditions, Fabbrin et al. [94] found that the mycorrhizal fungus Rhizophagus intraradices significantly benefited pearl millet under climate change conditions, which combined elevated atmospheric CO2 and drought, by inducing proline accumulation and improving plant water content. Field trials confirmed that Funneliformis mosseae co-inoculated with Azospirillum and Azotobacter enhanced S. bicolor drought tolerance by improving grain yield and physiological and biochemical parameters, such as total protein, proline, nitrogen and chlorophyll content, while reducing electrolyte leakage [95]. Additionally, the combination of F. mosseae with Rhizophagus aggregatus proved effective in enhancing P. glaucum vegetative biomass under heat stress [96].

PGPB can also enhance millet drought tolerance. ACC deaminase-producing strains of P. migulae, P. fluorescens, E. hormaechei and B. amyloliquefaciens improved the antioxidant defence system of P. glaucum and S. italica facing drought [86, 88]. Moreover, S. putrefaciens and C. dublinenesis enhanced pearl millet drought resilience by modulating stress-responsive genes and phytohormone levels [90].

Further field studies are crucial to identify the most effective rhizosphere microbial consortia for various millet varieties and environmental conditions. This will help maximize their potential in boosting millet resilience and nutritional quality in future climate scenarios.

Hemp

Industrial hemp (Cannabis sativa L., with legal limits of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) content) has been cultivated for at least 6000 years [97]. Historically, it played a major role as the primary material for sailcloth, ropes, and paper, as well as being valued for its seeds and medicinal properties. However, the rise of synthetic fibres in the early twentieth century and the international ban on cannabis related to intoxicating production (high THC levels) led to a drastic decline in its cultivation [98]. In recent years, there has been increasing interest in reviving industrial hemp cultivation, as it holds potential as a renewable source for materials such as natural fibres, bioplastics, bio-composites and insulating materials. Beyond the use of hemp stems, the high levels of polyunsaturated oils and quality protein in its seeds make hemp grain the primary focus of cultivation [99]. All combined with growing interest in the pharmaceutical properties of cannabinoids, hemp is poised to become a globally valuable crop again.

Early studies have highlighted the crucial role of microbial communities associated with cannabis in influencing plant growth, yield and cannabinoid profiles [100–102]. Additionally, the application of bacterial inoculants is emerging in the cannabis industry as promising elicitors that can significantly enhance these traits [101, 103–105] (Table 3). Inoculants combining Pseudomonas spp. with other plant growth-promoting bacteria such as Comamonas testosteroni, Citrobacter freundii, Enterobacter cloacae, Bacillus spp. and Mucilaginibacter spp. strains, among others, have been highly effective in hydroponic and soilless systems. These formulations have been shown to enhance yields during germination, seedling and bloom stages by stimulating photosynthetic activity, resulting in increased plant height, basal stem area and overall growth during the floral stage [103, 108, 110, 111]. This, in turn, results in a significant increase in flower production, bud yield, and harvest index that may enable growers to achieve a higher number of harvests per year [114].

Biocontrol

Interestingly, microbial inoculants not only promote plant growth but also trigger biochemical pathways in cannabis, leading to the synthesis of secondary metabolites that boost cannabinoid production and modulate cannabinoid profiles [112, 115]. Under greenhouse conditions, applying low concentrations of a bacterial consortium, including Azospirillum brasilense, Gluconobacter diazotrophicus, Herbaspirillum seropedicae and Burkholderia ambifaria, resulted in effects comparable to those induced by standard nitrogen fertilisation. This also led to a more than 10% increase in cannabinoid content, particularly THC, cannabinol (CBN) and cannabidiol (CBD), while enhancing the plant’s antioxidant capacity [105]. A similar trend was observed by Cavallero et al. [109] in studies on hemp sprouts and seed germination. Treatments with the endophytic bacterium Sphingomonas sp. Can_S11 significantly increased phenolic compound accumulation, boosting antioxidant and anti-radical activity. Moreover, environmental stressors appear to amplify the beneficial effects of these inoculations, supporting the shift toward cultivation methods that reduce chemical inputs. For instance, Tanney et al. [113] found that inoculating B. mobilis KJ812449 under hydroponic conditions and a low-nutrient regime increased trichome density on floral organs, resulting in a higher abundance of key cannabinoids. This aligns with the broader understanding that cannabis produces a variety of secondary metabolites in response to biotic and abiotic stressors [116].

Inflorescence pathogens are particularly damaging to cannabis, causing post-harvest flower losses of up to 20%, with Fusarium spp. being one of the most prevalent pathogens [117]. While research on cannabis endophytes for biocontrol is still in its early stages, several studies have highlighted their potential. Strains of Paecilomyces lilacinus, Penicillium spp., Paenibacillus sp., Pseudomonas spp. and P. vagans have been shown to inhibit the growth of Botrytis cinerea, Trichothecium roseum, Fusarium oxysporum and other pathogens in dual confrontation assays [104, 106, 110, 118, 119]. To date, only a few in planta studies evaluating the biocontrol potential of microbial consortia have been reported. Among these, Scott and Punja [107] demonstrated that combinations of beneficial fungi and bacteria (Gliocladium catenulatum, Trichoderma spp. and B. amyloliquefaciens) reduced F. oxysporum severity during the vegetative and flowering stages.

In conclusion, while microbial communities play a key role in enhancing cannabis growth, yield and cannabinoid production, much remains to be explored. Bacterial inoculants offer a promising, sustainable alternative to traditional fertilisation methods and improve resilience to environmental stressors. Microbial consortia also show potential for biocontrol of pathogens like Fusarium spp., although in planta studies remain limited. Further research is needed to better understand their mechanisms and to optimize their use in sustainable cannabis cultivation, especially under the increasingly variable and challenging environmental conditions associated with climate change.

Quinoa

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is an ancient crop native to the Andean region of South America, primarily domesticated around Lake Titicaca. It is valued for its high nutritional content and exceptional resilience to extreme environmental conditions. This crop is mainly characterized by seeds with significant levels of proteins, essential amino acids, lipids and other components such as phenols, minerals, fibre or vitamins, all of which contribute to sustaining nutritious diets with health benefits [120]. More than 6000 accessions of quinoa (distinct samples of quinoa with unique genetic characteristics) are currently registered around the world, growing under different environmental conditions, with extreme climatic conditions and adapted to different environmental stresses [121] such as salinity [122] and drought [123]. These properties make quinoa one of the sustainable foods of the future. Traditional strategies used to solve or mitigate adverse climatic effects on soils are the application of microorganisms, in particular halophiles, as bioremediation agents and plant growth promoters in salt-affected soils [124].

The bacterial species tested on quinoa, both individually and in consortium, belong to different genera, though the genus Bacillus was the most common: Bacillus spp. [125], B. stratosphericus strain MPF-B2a [126], Bacillus sp. MN54 [124], B. licheniformis QA1 [127], B. velezensis QA2 [128], B. amyloliquefaciens 11B91 [129], B. atrophaeus GQJK17 S8 [130], B. inaquosorum ON832055, B. thuringiensis ON832056 and B. proteolyticus ON832055 [131]. Strains from other bacterial taxa tested were Pseudomonas sp. strain M30-35, P. lucknowensis Cq-48, P. flexibilis Cq-32, P. furukawaii Cq-40, individually or in consortium with Bacillus [132–135], Serratia sp. and S. rubidaea ED1 [125, 136], Staphylococcus succinus strain JM40 [126], A. brasilense CNPSo 2084 [137], P. ananatis AM-0261 [138] and Burkholderia phytofirmans PsJN [139].

Considering the integration of microorganisms into crop production as an effective strategy for achieving abiotic stress mitigation [140], PGPB can help quinoa plants improve different stages of plant development, including germination, growth, yield production, seed quality and plant survival under stress factors [125, 141]. In this regard, the bacterial mechanisms of action used on quinoa seem diverse (Table 4). PGPB benefit quinoa through biological nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilisation, phytohormone production and systemic resistance induction mechanisms. They have been described to increase the solubility of phosphate and zinc, thus increasing their availability, as reported for S. rubidaea ED1 [136] or P. lucknowensis Cq-48, P. flexibilis Cq-32 and P. furukawaii Cq-40 [132]. Other mechanisms of action include biocontrol of quinoa phytopathogens by P. ananatis AM-0261, a nitrogen-fixing and phosphate-solubilizing bacterium that also inhibits several quinoa phytopathogenic fungi [138]. Quinoa, a facultative halophyte plant with a high tolerance to salt stress, can grow and complete its biological cycle at high salt concentrations, but grain production can be significantly affected [142]. Likewise, rainfed quinoa cultivation prompts periods of water scarcity that can significantly affect production and grain quality [143]. In line with this, different authors have successfully used PGPB to improve quinoa’s response to different abiotic stresses, mainly drought and salt stress. PGPB, such as Bacillus sp. MN5 can also act by regulating the abiotic stress response in quinoa, proving the osmotic adjustment and increasing nutrient availability under salt stress, with a positive impact on parameters of biomass increase and grain yield per plant [124]. Similarly, B. velezensis QA2 or Pseudomonas sp. M30-35 promote growth and grain yield per plant under saline conditions [128, 135]. Yang et al. [139] also observed that B. phytofirmans PsJN increased salt tolerance in quinoa by improving ionic homeostasis and osmotic adjustment and possibly by increasing antioxidant defences such as catalase and dehydroascorbate reductase enzymes. Aslam et al. [137] observed that inoculation with the bacterium A. brasilense significantly improved vegetative growth and grain yield in quinoa under drought-stress conditions. Gonzalez et al. [125] utilized the ability to synthesize IAA-like compounds as a screening tool for beneficial microorganism strains, as the production of auxin phytohormones by certain PGPB lead to plant growth promotion under certain stressful conditions, such as drought. These bacteria can also contribute to the synthesis of antioxidant compounds and improve seed germination capacity [144].

Moreover, it has been reported that the endophytic bacterial composition in quinoa exhibits tissue-specific stability, particularly in seeds, while root-associated communities show more pronounced shifts influenced by genotype and environmental conditions [145]. These findings align with previous research, which demonstrated that the bacterial composition of the quinoa rhizosphere varies depending on genotype and water availability [146]. Given the extensive number of accessions described to date, this crop exhibits a high potential as a reservoir of PGPB strains [128].

Lastly, it has been shown that different strains of PGPB do not always produce the same effects on quinoa plants. Fanai et al. [147] demonstrated that growth promotion and stress tolerance are distinct traits, and their impact varies depending on the bacterial strain applied. These authors emphasize that the selection of biologically active microorganisms should be tailored to deliver specific benefits to quinoa. Therefore, the development of sustainable PGPB formulations for quinoa requires comprehensive studies and repeated field trials to ensure their efficacy. The successful application of multi-strain formulations in quinoa cultivation holds promise for reducing annual yield losses and contributing to more sustainable agricultural systems. Nevertheless, further field experiments are needed to validate the beneficial effects of these PGPB strains on quinoa under controlled abiotic stress conditions.

Amaranths

Amaranthus is a genus of C₄ dicotyledonous herbaceous plants belonging to the family Amaranthaceae. Most Amaranthus species are native to Central and South America, including the three major cultivated species: A. hypochondriacus, A. cruentus and A. caudatus [148]. These species are widely grown for their edible grains, classified as “pseudocereals” [149]. Due to their ability to thrive under adverse conditions and their exceptional nutritional value, the cultivation of amaranths has expanded into temperate and tropical regions beyond their native range [150]. Amaranth grains are an excellent source of high-quality, gluten-free protein, micronutrients and dietary fibre. Additionally, they contain bioactive compounds and serve multiple purposes, including food, forage, and industrial applications [151].

Despite their growing importance, global and country-specific production data on amaranth remain limited, with China currently leading the global market for amaranth seeds [152]. Given its resilience, nutritional richness and versatility, amaranth is a promising crop to support food security, especially in regions where adverse climatic conditions increasingly threaten traditional staples such as rice, wheat and maize. However, amaranth cultivation faces several agronomic challenges, including nutrient deficiencies in degraded soils, salinity stress, pest infestations and disease outbreaks, all of which can constrain productivity [153].

To address these challenges, the use of PGPB has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance soil fertility, crop health and nutritional quality. PGPB application in amaranth cultivation has demonstrated multiple benefits, including improved growth and yield, enhanced nutrient availability and use efficiency, protection against pathogens, increased tolerance to abiotic stresses and even potential for phytoremediation (Table 5). Beneficial bacterial species and consortia used as PGP microorganisms in amaranth include members of the genera Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Enterobacter, Streptomyces, Rhizobium and Azospirillum, among others. Stress control: Salinity

Numerous studies have documented the positive effects of PGPB on different Amaranthus species. In A. hypochondriacus, bacterial communities from the families Bacillaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonadaceae and Streptomycetaceae, associated with the rhizosheath, a layer of soil particles bound to roots by mucilage and microbial activity, produce extracellular enzymes such as DNases and phosphatases that enhance nutrient availability [160]. Similarly, in Amaranthus tricolor, a microbial consortium composed of Bacillus, Trichoderma and Enterobacter strains, originally isolated from the epiphyte Dendrobium ovatum, significantly increased biomass production, the accumulation of primary metabolites (sugars, amino acids, phenols), and improved photosynthetic performance [158].

In terms of growth and yield, inoculation of A. cruentus and A. hypochondriacus with B. ambifaria Mex-5 and B. caribensis XV led to increased biomass, leaf area and nitrogen content, even under low fertility conditions [161]. In A. caudatus, bacterial endophytes such as Alcaligenes faecalis, Pseudomonas moorei, Bacillus siamensis, B. cereus and Achromobacter deleyi demonstrated strong nitrogen fixation and phosphate solubilization abilities [162]. Likewise, Cellulosimicrobium sp. FRR2 improved A. campestris germination and growth through nitrogen fixation and IAA production, while also exhibiting tolerance to salinity and heavy metal stress [163]. Moreover, consortia that integrate PGPB with mycorrhizal fungi have proven to be highly effective in amplifying these beneficial effects [158].

In the domain of biocontrol, PGPB act as protective agents by producing antibiotics, siderophores and lytic enzymes. For instance, in Amaranthus spp., Bacillus sp. WR1 reduced leaf blight incidence caused by Rhizoctonia solani by 41% [164]. Similarly, Paenibacillus dendritiformis APL3 enhanced growth, increased antioxidant content and suppressed the growth of pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus and Pseudomonas in A. polygonoides [165].

PGPB also play a crucial role in enhancing plant resilience under abiotic stress conditions such as drought, salinity and high temperatures. Strains of Pseudomonas, Kosakonia, Enterobacter and Bacillus have been shown to improve amaranth germination, growth and yield under saline conditions by solubilizing phosphates, producing siderophores and tolerating high salt concentrations [166]. In A. hypochondriacus, treatment with B. pumilus (BS-27, BS-41) and B. subtilis (BS-58) enhanced germination rates, vegetative development and nutrient accumulation, with the combined treatment (BS-27 + BS-58) increasing grain yield by 37% [155, 156]. Likewise, a consortium of B. safensis and B. haynesii isolated from saline soils significantly improved A. viridis growth, photosynthetic efficiency, osmotic adjustment and antioxidant activity [159].

Additionally, PGPB have demonstrated efficacy in phytoremediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils. In A. gangeticus, the KRB bacterial strain alleviated Cr⁶⁺ toxicity and promoted plant development [167]. Furthermore, in A. hypochondriacus and A. mangostanus, Rahnella sp. JN27 served as a growth-promoting and phytoremediating agent by producing IAA, ACC deaminase, siderophores and solubilizing phosphates, enhancing both plant growth and soil recovery in contaminated environments [168].

Overall, the use of PGPB in amaranth cultivation offers substantial potential for enhancing agronomic performance and promoting sustainable agricultural practices. These microorganisms improve the uptake efficiency of essential nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, while reducing reliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides. Moreover, they boost microbial activity, promote organic matter decomposition, improve soil structure and help mitigate soil erosion.

To translate these benefits from laboratory research into practical field applications, it is necessary to develop stable microbial formulations and efficient delivery systems, coupled with extensive field trials across diverse agroecological conditions. The use of PGPB can be cost-effective for farmers by reducing input costs, while contributing to sustainable agricultural systems that enhance microbial biodiversity, increase crop resilience to climate change and ensure environmentally resilient production of amaranth.

Desert truffles

Desert truffles are a group of hypogeous fungi from arid and semiarid ecosystems, mostly located in the Mediterranean region. Among them, Terfezia claveryi Chatin is one of the few edibles and commercially viable mycorrhizal fungi, and it has been the first desert truffle species to be cultivated, becoming itself an agricultural crop in Spain [51]. In addition, this crop could play an important ecological role in Mediterranean ecosystems as a natural resource, preserving cultivated areas from desertification [169]. T. claveryi is associated in mycorrhizal symbiosis with host plants of the genus Helianthemum (Cistaceae family), and its fruiting period is usually in early spring.

The mycorrhizal plant production system, starting from Helianthemum seeds and Terfezia spores, is the most widely used. However, high seedling mortality has been observed during the first 4 weeks after germination in nursery conditions [170], together with a high dependence on fertilization to stimulate plant growth [171]. In addition, the early inoculation with Terfezia does not enhance seedling survival. These facts led to the use of other microorganisms living in the Helianthemum-Terfezia rhizosphere, such as PGPB, which could help in one or more stages of the mycorrhizal plant production system [172]. Although several studies have identified and characterized bacterial communities associated with fungal sporocarps, field mycorrhizal roots or rhizospheric soil [173–176], research specifically addressing their functional impact on plant growth and the survival of mycorrhized plants colonized by edible desert truffles remains very limited. MHBs enhance plant mycorrhization by increasing hyphal growth, spore germination and root colonization [178, 179]. Moderate drought stress has been shown to enhance T. claveryi mycorrhization in H. almeriense plants [180, 181]. In the tripartite interaction between H. almeriense, T. claveryi and P. mandelii strain 29, the presence of this bacterium improved the response to water deficit by avoiding the sharp decrease in root hydraulic conductivity observed in non-bacteria-inoculated mycorrhizal plants [177]. This improvement was associated with an enhanced T. claveryi ectendomycorrhiza formation, which is accompanied by higher efficiency in nutrient uptake and regulation of aquaporin gene expression [177]. Moreover, under drought stress, MHB-inoculated plants decreased the root salicylic acid content and increased ABA levels, compared with the uninoculated plants. ABA is key in the plant response to water deficit since it can increase root aquaporin gene expression [182] and promote arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal colonization [183–186]. The higher ABA accumulation in roots of H. almeriense plants inoculated with MHB during drought conditions could explain the increased expression of several plant aquaporins. Additionally, the lowest root salicylic acid content observed in MHB-inoculated plants under drought stress coincided with the greatest mycorrhization rates [177], since this hormone plays an important role in activating plant defence mechanisms [187, 188].

Taken together, the results obtained by Guarnizo et al. [177] suggest that physiological, molecular and hormonal fine-tuned mechanisms are involved in the complex strategy of H. almeriense and its tripartite interaction to cope with water stress. These data also highlight the potential benefits of applying P. mandelii strain 29 in desert truffle cultivation, not only to improve mycorrhizal plant production in the greenhouse but also to enhance field performance during dry seasons and, even more importantly, in the face of a climate change scenario in which drought is becoming more intense [177].

Under field conditions, desert truffle plants present a typical summer deciduous plant phenology divided into four stages: summer dormancy, autumn bud break, winter photosynthetic activity and spring fruiting [189]. Arenas et al. [190] characterized the functional dynamics of the cultivable PGPB associated with T. claveryi across the different stages of plant phenology. They found that mycorrhizal desert truffle plants harbour phosphate-solubilizing bacteria during truffle fruiting in spring, probably to change the microenvironmental pH around the mycelium and ascocarp primordia to favour their growth and development. Phosphate and other elements solubilization are induced through the release of organic acids, necessary to lower the pH in alkaline soils [191, 192].

All these findings point out that targeted bacterial applications at different stages of nursery plant production may help overcome key bottlenecks in desert truffle cultivation. Potential benefits include increased seedling survival and mycorrhization rates, reduced production time and ultimately, greater plant quality. Furthermore, inoculation with a mix of organic acid-releasing bacteria in late winter might improve desert truffle yields.

Emerging crops as microbiome architects: rhizosphere interactions and domestication effect

Plants are not passive recipients of microbial colonization but actively shape the composition and function of their rhizosphere microbiota through a range of physiological and morphological traits. Root system architecture, root exudation patterns and developmental stage influence the establishment and dynamics of microbial communities in the rhizosphere [193]. Emerging crops, many of which are either partially domesticated or have retained traits from their wild progenitors, may possess unique capabilities for recruiting beneficial microbes compared to modern high-input crops [194, 195]. The process of domestication and breeding for yield and uniformity under high-input systems may have inadvertently reduced the capacity of crops to engage with diverse or functionally beneficial microbial partners. This “domestication bottleneck” could have led to less complex rhizosphere microbiomes in modern crops compared to their wild relatives. In contrast, emerging crops, such as quinoa, amaranth, millet, hemp and desert truffle hosts, may retain a greater ability to interact with native soil microbiota, offering untapped potential for microbiome-based enhancement strategies.

Root exudates, consisting of sugars, amino acids, organic acids, phenolics and secondary metabolites, serve not only as carbon sources but also as chemical signals that modulate microbial behaviour. These exudation profiles can vary between species, genotypes and environmental conditions, thereby influencing microbial recruitment and activity [196]. For example, lupins are known to release flavonoids able to mobilize soil inorganic phosphorus, decreasing the metabolic activity of phosphorus-solubilizing microbes [197]. However, in many emerging crops such as those analyzed in this work, the composition of root exudates and their potential role in shaping the structure and function of the associated microbiota remain largely unexplored. Further research is needed to characterize these chemical profiles under both optimal and stress conditions, and to understand how they influence the recruitment of beneficial microbial communities.

Understanding how these crops shape and are shaped by their microbiome is crucial for the development of sustainable crop management practices. Further studies employing comparative metagenomics, metabolomics of root exudates and synthetic community experiments will be essential to elucidate these interactions and guide breeding programs that favour beneficial plant–microbe partnerships.

Conclusions and perspectives

PGPB represent a promising tool for enhancing the productivity and resilience of emerging crops, yet tailored strategies are still needed to maximize their benefits across environments. These beneficial microbes contribute to plant growth by improving nutrient availability, modulating phytohormone levels and inducing systemic resistance. Their potential to enhance crop resilience under abiotic stresses linked to climate change is particularly noteworthy. However, despite well-documented effects in model crops under controlled conditions, their use in emerging crops, such as quinoa, amaranth, millet, lupins, hemp and desert truffles, remains mostly underexplored. These crops hold substantial promises for sustainable agriculture, particularly in Mediterranean-type climates facing drought and soil degradation.

Further investigation into the interactions between PGPB and emerging crops is crucial to bridge the gap between laboratory research and field applications, allowing for the selection of the most effective microbial inoculants in real-world conditions. For example, in quinoa, bacterial strain-specific effects highlight the need for tailored inoculants and multi-strain formulations to enhance stress tolerance and yield stability. In lupins, PGPB may work alongside or even compete with rhizobial inoculants, supporting soil regeneration, phytoremediation and the production of bioactive compounds. In cannabis, microbial consortia have demonstrated potential to boost growth, cannabinoid biosynthesis and pathogen suppression. Amaranth also benefits from inoculation through improved root development, nutrient efficiency and soil health, thereby reducing reliance on synthetic inputs. Similarly, in desert truffle cultivation, targeted PGPB applications can increase mycorrhization rates, water stress tolerance and seedling establishment.

Importantly, recent advances in microbiome science, including metagenomics, microbiome engineering and the design of synthetic microbial communities, are transforming our ability to utilize these beneficial microbes. Metagenomic methods enable detailed profiling of rhizospheric microbial communities, uncovering new PGPB traits and their ecological roles. This understanding allows the targeted assembly of synthetic communities with tailored functional traits to meet the specific needs of new crops and the environmental stresses they encounter. Microbiome engineering techniques can further improve the stability, colonization success, and resilience of microbial groups, thereby enhancing their performance in field conditions.

Overall, integrating PGPB into the sustainable cultivation of emerging crops, supported by microbiome-based technologies, offers a strong alternative to traditional agricultural inputs. This comprehensive approach can boost crop resilience, lessen reliance on chemicals and support food security and environmental health amid global climate change. Future research should focus on creating reliable microbial formulations, tested through wide-ranging field trials and developing tailored application strategies for each crop system. By harnessing the combined power of PGPB, emerging crops and microbiome innovations, we can move toward more resilient and diverse food systems.
