Genomic Alterations and Their Clinical Implications in Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma in a Large‐Scale Chinese Cohort
Authors: Mingwei Dong, Jie Hua, Zhilong Ma, Rong Tang, Miaoyan Wei, Zonglin Lv, Yuan Liu, Ke Chen, Qiong Du, Wei Wang, Xianjun Yu, Jin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenomic sequencing has made precision oncology of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) feasible and is recommended by numerous clinical guidelines. The clinical significance of genomic alterations in PDAC remains to be revealed. This study was designed to bridge this gap by elucidating the clinical relevance of genomic profiling in a diverse PDAC population. From January 1, 2021 to April 30, 2023, we prospectively collected tumor tissues and peripheral blood from 1135 patients diagnosed with PDAC at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC). Germline and somatic mutations were identified using a 676‐gene panel. PDAC with KRAS/ARID1A co‐mutation was featured with early metastasis and poor prognosis. Patients with BRCA1/2 and PALB2 mutations showed a trend towards improved survival with platinum‐containing regimens, although statistical significance was not reached. In contrast, we observed a statistical preference for platinum‐containing adjuvant chemotherapy in KRAS wild‐type PDAC patients. For the mutational landscape, the current cohort had a lower mutation rate of KRAS, while mutations related to the WNT, Hippo, and PI3K signaling pathways were more prevalent, compared with Caucasian PDAC. Somatic RAD50 p.L719fs, ATR p.I774fs, and germline RAD51D p.K91fs were homologous recombination repair (HRR) gene mutations enriched in this Chinese cohort relative to reported Caucasian series. These results highlighted that genomic sequencing could guide the management of PDAC, including prognosis prediction and chemotherapy regimen selection. Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the predominant form of pancreatic malignancy, with a dismal 5‐year survival rate of 8%–10% [1]. Despite significant advancements in cancer treatment, the complex biology and unique microenvironment of pancreatic tumors contribute to their inherent resistance and limited treatment options. Current treatment strategies for PDAC remain conventional and standardized, lacking the nuances required for a precision medicine approach [2, 3]. Genomic sequencing provided valuable insights into the genomic landscape of PDAC and has revealed potentially targetable genomic alterations [4, 5]. However, the advancement of sequencing technologies is outpacing the development of the infrastructure required to implement precision oncology in routine clinical practice [6]. Also, the low frequencies of actionable mutations make it challenging to determine the clinical benefit of genome‐based treatment strategies in PDAC [7]. Thus, implementing sequencing techniques and precision oncology in clinical pancreatic cancer management is still challenging.

Current studies revealed clinical relevance of genomic mutations in pancreatic cancer, while several questions need to be further investigated: BRCA/PALB2 mutations are established predictors for platinum sensitivity (National Comprehensive Cancer Network [NCCN] guideline [2024.V3]), the clinical utility of other HRR gene alterations (such as ATM, CHEK2, or RAD51) remains controversial and lacks large‐scale validation. The prognostic and predictive value of specific mutational patterns, is not fully understood in the context of adjuvant therapy. Moreover, most existing genomic data are derived from Western populations, and population‐specific genomic profiles and their clinical implications in Asian cohorts require further elucidation.

In this study, we prospectively collected genomic mutation profiles of 1135 patients using a comprehensive next‐generation sequencing panel. We extensively analyzed clinicopathological baseline features, treatment modalities with overall survival outcomes, and genomic alterations. KRAS/ARID1A co‐mutated PDAC was featured with early metastasis and poor prognosis. Patients who had BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, and other HRR gene mutations were likely to benefit from platinum‐containing adjuvant chemotherapy, while no improvement in overall post‐operative survival was observed in patients who had ATR/CHEK1 mutations. Also, KRAS‐wild‐type status could confer platinum‐containing chemotherapy preference.

In summary, our findings highlighted the precision treatment of PDAC with the aid of genomic sequencing, leading to potential improvement in PDAC management.

Materials and Methods

Patient and Tissue Collection

Our study enrolled 1135 patients diagnosed with PDAC at FUSCC between January 1, 2021, and April 30, 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically diagnosed with PDAC; (2) available clinical follow‐up data; and (3) sufficient tissue samples for genomic profiling. Patients were excluded if they: (1) received neoadjuvant therapy prior to sample acquisition; or (2) had incomplete clinical records. Tissues collected using endoscopic ultrasound‐guided‐fine needle aspiration (EUS‐FNA), puncture, or surgical excision were pathologically validated, and special pathologic types were excluded. All samples were approved and authorized by the FUSCC Ethics Committee, and all participants provided informed consent. Platinum‐containing regimen refers to standard regimens containing at least 1 platinum agent (FOLFIRINOX [fluorouracil + leucovorin + irinotecan + oxaliplatin], modified FOLFIRINOX [mFOLFIRINOX, fluorouracil + leucovorin + irinotecan + oxaliplatin], AG + DDP [albumin‐paclitaxel + cis‐platinum], and GEMOX [gemcitabine + oxaliplatin]).

Genomic Sequencing and Analysis

The FUSCC‐PDAC panel consisted of 676 cancer‐related genes, including clinically actionable targets and frequently altered genes in PDAC (Table S1). The FUSCC‐PDAC panel was sequenced as described. Briefly, DNA from tumors and matched blood was fragmented, purified, end‐repaired, poly‐A–tailed, and ligated to construct libraries, which were pooled and sequenced using NovaSeq 6000.

The FUSCC‐PDAC variant calling pipeline was performed as follows: quality‐controlled reads were aligned to GRCh37 using BWA and processed into BAM files, and then somatic SNVs and insertions or deletions (indels) were identified using VarScan2 and GATK Mutect2 and filtered with minimum coverage ≥ 60× and variant allele frequency ≥ 4%. HaplotypeCaller was used to identify germline mutations with the following parameters: minimum coverage ≥ 20×; allelic depth ≥ 5 for the alternative allele; and variant allele frequency ≥ 20%. Samples with tumor purity < 20% were excluded from the analysis. The pathogenicity of germline variants was determined using the Association for Molecular Pathology and American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines [8].

Microsatellite Instability and Mismatch Repair and Tumor Mutation Burden Analysis

Microsatellite instability (MSI) status was assessed by MSIsensor [9]. In brief, the number of reads for each microsatellite locus in tumor and normal samples was compared by the χ 2 test to find somatic MSI loci, the percentage of which was the MSI score. Our study defined an MSI score of > 40% as MSI‐high.

Comparative Analysis With Existing Cohort

To facilitate cross‐ancestry comparisons with existing cohorts, we selected the pancreatic adenocarcinoma cohort from the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC). The genomic and clinical data were accessed via the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org/). The MSKCC cohort is the largest publicly available cohort profiled by targeted sequencing panels, providing comprehensive coverage of key mutations and detailed clinical information.

Co‐Occurrence and Mutual Exclusive Analysis

Co‐occurrence and mutual exclusivity were identified using Fisher's exact test by the “somaticInteractions” function of the R package “maftools” [10]. Events with p < 0.05 were preserved as co‐occurring or mutually exclusive events.

Statistical Analysis

Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to evaluate continuous variables or ordered categorical variables. Fisher's exact test and Pearson's χ 2 test were used to determine the correlation coefficients of nonordered categorical variables. Benjamini–Hochberg's approach for multiple comparisons was used to adapt the p values to the false discovery rate. Cox proportional hazards models and log‐rank tests were used to assess the prognostic significance of individual gene mutations or gene co‐mutations for overall survival. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.2.

Results

Clinical Features and Genomic Characteristics of Somatic Alterations in Chinese Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma

804 primary and 331 metastatic tumors were enrolled in this study. Paired peripheral blood samples and tumor tissues were prospectively collected and sequenced. Blood DNA was obtained to identify germline variants and as a control for all tumor samples to identify somatic mutations. A brief flowchart of this cohort study was shown in Figure 1A. The median overall survival (OS) time for primary and metastatic PDAC was 22.07 months and 12.67 months, respectively (Figure 1B).

We identified 6722 somatic single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) and 1718 somatic indels. The most frequently mutated genes in FUSCC PDAC were KRAS (80%), TP53 (69%), SMAD4 (18%), CDKN2A (17%), and ARID1A (9%) (Figure 1C). Mutations in KDM6A, NCOR1, PALB2, PTEN, FOXL2, and FOXP2, and amplification of MYC and CDK6 had significantly higher prevalence rates in metastatic tumors compared with primary tumors (Figure S1).

To explore ethnicity‐specific SNVs in the FUSCC cohort, we compared our cohort with the mutation data of PDAC in the white ethnicity from the MSKCC cohort [11]. We first compared the mutation frequencies of individual genes in PDAC samples obtained from the primary sites in the MSKCC and FUSCC cohorts to minimize the effect of tumor mutation burden (TMB) [12]. Notably, the mutation rates of KRAS and CDKN2A were significantly lower in FUSCC patients (80% for KRAS and 17% for CDKN2A) than Caucasian patients in MSKCC (93.1% for KRAS and 23.1% for CDKN2A) in both primary and metastatic PDAC, while mutations in KMT2C, KMT2D, NF1, TCF7L2, and ATR were more abundant in Chinese PDAC (Figure 1E, upper left and upper right).

Also, we separately compared the mutation frequencies of genes in FUSCC Chinese PDAC patients to Caucasian patients with KRAS‐mutated samples and KRAS‐wild‐type samples. For KRAS‐mutated PDAC, mutations in KMT2D, KMT2C, ATR, TCF7L2, and NF1 were more abundant in Chinese PDAC (Figure 1E, lower left). For KRAS wild‐type PDAC, mutations in APC, KDM5A, and BRD4 were more abundant in Chinese PDAC (Figure 1E, lower right), suggesting differential ethnic‐specific oncogenic alterations in KRAS‐mutated and KRAS‐wild‐type PDAC in the Chinese population.

ARID1A Mutation in KRAS‐Mutated PDAC Confers Dedifferentiation and Poor Prognosis

A univariant‐Cox regression model was applied to determine the association of somatic single‐gene mutation with OS in primary PDAC. Notably, among the top 20 genes, only ARID1A mutation was found to be significantly related to poor postoperative survival (Figure 2A). Moreover, ARID1A mutation was found related to shortened OS time in KRAS‐mutated PDAC, while it did not have a statistically significant impact on the OS of KRAS wild‐type PDAC (Figure 2B). To profile the underlying interactions in somatic mutational events and their relationship with clinical outcomes, we constructed a heatmap to display the co‐occurrence and mutual exclusivity of mutated genes in both primary and metastatic pancreatic cancer (Figure 2C). Significant co‐mutation and mutually exclusive events were identified as previously described [10] and the odds ratios were represented by square colors. A distinct co‐occurrence of KRAS and ARID1A mutations was found in metastatic pancreatic cancer, while they didn't show co‐occurrence statistically significant in primary pancreatic cancer, and most ARID1A mutations were loss‐of‐function (LOF) mutations (Figure 2D,E). Taken together, these results suggest that loss of ARID1A function in KRAS‐mutated PDAC may lead to a more aggressive malignant phenotype, associated with early metastasis and poor prognosis.

Prior studies linked ARID1A mutations to MSI and potential immunotherapy benefits [13], however, we found no significant difference in MSI levels between ARID1A‐mutated and ARID1A wild‐type PDAC in this cohort (Figure S2). Interestingly, we found that ARID1A mutations frequently co‐occurred with MSI‐related gene (MLH1, MSH2, MSH3, MSH6, POLD1) LOF mutations (Figure S3A,B). For patients who carried MSI‐related gene LOF mutations, those with concurrent ARID1A mutations (n = 5) exhibited significantly higher MSI levels compared with ARID1A‐wild‐type cases (n = 10) (Figure S3C). These results highlighted the potential linkage between ARID1A mutation and immunotherapy benefit in PDAC.

We assessed the tumor burden and the progression‐free interval (PFI) of 31 metastatic PDAC patients who underwent genotyping and received Camrelizumab (PD‐1 inhibitor) in addition to basic AG (Gemcitabine + Albumin‐paclitaxel) chemotherapy. Of these 31 patients, 8 were found carrying ARID1A LOF mutations, and only 1 of these patients was MSI. 7 of 8 ARID1A LOF patients met the criteria of partial response (PR) after medication (Tumor size decrease > 30%), significantly higher than that of ARID1A wild‐type patients (Fisher's Exact test, p = 0.016). ARID1A LOF patients were found to have longer PFI compared with ARID1A wild‐type patients (HR = 0.69, Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test, p = 0.083), indicating that ARID1A status may predict ICI benefits regardless of MSI status in PDAC patients (Figure S5).

Landscape of Somatic and Germline Homologous Recombination Deficiencies

Homologous recombination repair (HRR) deficiencies are biomarkers related to potential responses to platinum‐based chemotherapy and PARPi [14, 15], while the definition of HRR geneset remains controversial [16]. We compiled a comprehensive gene list pertinent to various DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways, drawing upon prior studies (Table S2) [17, 18, 19]. In assessing DDR pathways, mutations in HRR genes displayed the highest incidence of mutations overall and of truncating mutations specifically (Figure 3A). Pathogenic variants were identified in truncating mutations of ATM, BRCA2, RAD50, PALB2, BLM, CHEK2, and RECQL, and most non‐truncating variants were found to be variants of uncertain significance (VUS) (Figure 3B). 225 of 1135 (19.82%) patients were found to carry at least 1 HRR mutation in the current cohort, and the most common somatic HRR gene mutations in the FUSCC cohort were ATM (5%), ATR (4%), BRCA2 (3%), and RAD50 (2%) (Figure 3C).

Pathogenic (P) and likely pathogenic (LP) germline variants were identified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. 5.6% (64 of 1135) of these patients carried at least 1 pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variant. BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2, BRCA1, and RAD51D were the 5 most frequently mutated genes, 18 (1.6%), 9 (0.8%), 6 (0.5%), 5 (0.4%) and 4 (0.4%) patients carried germline variants in these genes, respectively; among these 64 patients, 55 had pathogenic germline HRR gene mutation (Figure 3D). Patients carrying germline variants developed PDAC at a younger age (median age at diagnosis, germline variant carrier vs. patients without germline variants: 60 vs. 63 years, Figure 3E).

Also, we identified 2 somatic (RAD50 p.L719fs and ATR p.I774fs) and 1 germline (RAD51 p.K91fs) HRR gene mutation frequently observed in this cohort, which was not found in Caucasian PDAC cohorts [11, 20] (Figure 3F), conferring the underlying heterogeneity of cancer genomics and related therapeutic opportunities.

Preference of Platinum‐Containing Adjuvant Chemotherapy in PDAC

In this cohort, 101 patients received platinum‐containing adjuvant chemotherapy and 512 received non‐platinum‐containing adjuvant chemotherapy (Table 1). We made comprehensive comparisons to decide how genomic features relate to platinum benefits. Briefly, patients with specified genotypes were assigned to different subgroups; for each subgroup, the postoperative OS rate of platinum‐treated and non‐platinum‐treated patients was compared. We initially focused on the HRR gene mutations. BRCA1/2 and PALB2 were lumped into 1 category due to their high clinical evidence [21, 22]. ATM/CHEK2 and ATR/CHEK1 were lumped into two categories according to their similar functions in DNA damage repair [23]. The rest of the HRR genes were lumped into another category. Notably, patients carrying other HRR gene mutations including RAD50, SLX4, RAD54L, etc. showed prolonged overall postoperative survival time when receiving platinum‐containing adjuvant therapy (Figure 4A,B, Figure S6 and Table S3). Although not statistically significant, patients carrying BRCA1/2 and PALB2 mutations exhibited a numerical improvement in overall survival when treated with platinum‐based therapy. Given the small sample size in these subgroups, this observation should be interpreted as a hypothesis‐generating trend. Also, we subgrouped these patients according to whether they had KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A and SMAD4 mutations, which were known as the four major mutated genes in PDAC. Interestingly, a statistically significant preference for platinum‐containing therapy was observed in both KRAS wild‐types and TP53 wild‐types (Figure 5A,B and Figure S6), especially in KRAS wild‐type patients, with a Hazard Ratio (HR) (95% confidence interval [CI]) of 0.32 (0.14–0.75) by univariant‐COX analysis and 0.45 (0.31–0.66) by multivariant‐COX analysis.

Discussion

Through prospective panel sequencing on an extensive group of Chinese patients with PDAC, this study constructed a large‐scale PDAC cohort with relevant clinicopathological reports and long‐term follow‐up. The genomic landscape of PDAC was analogized to 4 “mountains” (high‐frequency driver genes) involving KRAS, CDKN2A, SMAD4, and TP53 with numerous “hills” (low‐frequency driver genes) [24]. A previous study of targeted‐sequencing profiles of Chinese PDAC suggested a lower prevalence of KRAS mutations in Chinese PDAC patients (83.6%) than in TCGA patients (90.7%), consistent with findings in our FUSCC cohort [25]. Also, we found that instead of commonly mutated genes in PDAC, low‐frequency driver genes such as NF1, APC, and TCF7L2 were more abundant in Chinese PDAC than in Caucasian PDAC. Two previous studies also showed lower KRAS mutation rates in Chinese PDAC cohorts (83.2% and 85% respectively) [25, 26], suggesting that the mutation landscape of Chinese PDAC may be more heterogeneous.

ARID1A mutations were associated with early metastasis and unfavorable post‐surgery overall survival, especially in KRAS‐mutated PDAC [27]. Also, preliminary observation showed that ARID1A mutation is associated with tumor cell dedifferentiation at the histological level (Figure S4). This observation provides a biological explanation for the heterogeneous clinical behavior observed in KRAS‐driven tumors, suggesting that the loss of ARID1A function may define a specific “aberrantly differentiated” subtype characterized by early metastasis. It was highlighted that specific co‐mutation patterns, rather than single drivers, dictate the clinical outcome [27]. Therefore, the KRAS/ARID1A co‐mutation signature may represent a high‐risk biological subgroup requiring more aggressive or alternative therapeutic strategies.

We also compared survival outcomes for patients who received platinum‐containing adjuvant chemotherapy or not in primary PDAC patient subsets. The National NCCN guideline (2024.V3) (www.nccn.org) recommended preference for platinum‐containing chemotherapy regimens for metastatic PDAC patients who had known BRCA1/2 or PALB2 mutations, while there's no such reference for PDAC adjuvant therapy. The HRR process repairs complex DNA damage by utilizing homologous sequences on the same chromosome. ATR and CHEK1 mutations were unrelated to platinum preference, while other HRR gene mutations including RAD50, RAD54L, and RECQL were found to have prolonged postoperative survival time when receiving platinum‐containing therapy. Although it's hard to derive detailed and exact conclusions on how specific HRR gene mutation confers platinum‐containing adjuvant chemotherapy benefits, these results demonstrated that in addition to BRCA1/2 and PALB2 which are recommended, other genes (HRR genes refers to Table S2, except for BRCA1/2, ATM, ATR, CHEK1 and CHEK2) can infer platinum preference, like RAD50 [28]. Previous studies demonstrated that genomic methods can identify homologous recombination deficiency even in the absence of BRCA mutations [28]. This suggests that the biological mechanism of “BRCAness” is broader than previously defined. By validating these associations in a large Chinese cohort, our study reinforces the concept that a comprehensive assessment of the HRR pathway—rather than isolated gene testing—could optimize patient selection for platinum therapy, thereby increasing the fraction of patients who may benefit from this standard‐of‐care treatment.

Another interesting finding is that KRAS or TP53 wild‐type patients could benefit from platinum‐containing adjuvant therapy. Although it's known that KRAS mutation could regulate DNA damage repair, we found it's the first to provide clinical evidence that KRAS wild‐type status is related to platinum sensitiveness in pancreatic cancer. For KRAS mutant patients, it might be effective to apply KRAS inhibitor based on standard platinum‐containing chemotherapy [29], which could be further explored in future research. Also, KRAS wild‐type PDAC was considered more likely to have DNA damage repair gene mutations [30]. Taken together, KRAS wild‐type status could serve as an indicator for platinum‐containing adjuvant therapy.

There were several limitations to our current study. Lack of heterogeneity and limited treatment options for PDAC resulted in limited amounts of patients who carried specific mutations or received specific therapy. Furthermore, although targeted sequencing by a clinical panel provides insights into critical oncogenic mutations and potential therapeutic targets, it may also result in overlooking or misinterpreting tumor genomic status. While our findings generate hypotheses regarding the potential platinum sensitivity of tumors with HRR gene mutations, the small sample sizes in these subgroups preclude definitive conclusions about their clinical utility at this stage. The statistical power to detect significant interactions was limited for rare gene alterations, and the observed associations in these rare subgroups should be reviewed in future larger scale cohorts. As a retrospective analysis, our findings are inherently susceptible to selection bias: patient assignment to adjuvant chemotherapy regimens was not randomized but rather determined by physician discretion and clinical judgment, such as performance status, comorbidities, or socioeconomic factors influencing treatment access—may still have impacted the observed outcomes. An important technical limitation is that panel sequencing might overlook or misinterpret tumor genomic status. This is a single‐center study which may lead to referral bias, and further external validation was needed in future studies.

Overall, our study comprehensively revealed the landscape of the mutations in a large Chinese PDAC cohort and analyzed their underlying associations with therapy outcomes. Our results improved the understanding of the relationship between clinicopathological features and mutational diversity, enabled the identification of potential prognostic and treatment biomarkers, and provided a basis for precision oncology studies.
