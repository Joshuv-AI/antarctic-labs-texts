Safety and efficacy of phrenic nerve stimulation: A case series at the University of Oklahoma and review of the international literature
Authors: Kishore Balasubramanian, Xiaochun Zhao, Abdurrahman Fayez Kharbat, Marianne Kimmell, Maida Hafiz, Houssein Youness, Andrew Jea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: The aim of this study was to evaluate outcomes of phrenic nerve stimulation (PNS) in a patient cohort, review current literature, and propose patient candidacy criteria to ensure that therapeutic effects of PNS are maximized.Methods: We conducted a retrospective chart review at a Level 1 trauma center between January 1, 2020, and October 7, 2024, to assess outcomes of pediatric and adult patients who underwent PNS implantation. To identify relevant studies on PNS, we used PubMed to conduct a literature search.Results: The cohort consisted of 10 patients with 13 PNS implantation procedures. Ages ranged from 12 to 68 years (median, 19 years). Spinal cord injury (SCI) was the most common diagnosis (n = 5, 50%). Pacing was successfully achieved in 80% (n = 8). The two patients whose PNS were not successful shared a history of neurodegenerative disease (NDD). Complications were observed in two patients. Our literature search identified 23 studies. The relevant literature showed that, on average, complete ventilation independence was achieved in 61% of patients who underwent PNS.Conclusion: PNS can be an effective method for achieving ventilator independence in carefully selected patients, particularly those with high cervical SCIs. However, its efficacy in NDDs appears limited. PNS appears to be most effective in a specific subset of individuals, which suggests that proper patient selection is a key element to the success of this procedure. While PNS shows promise, larger controlled studies are needed to establish the long-term efficacy and safety profile of this procedure across various patient populations.

INTRODUCTION

Recent clinical practice guidelines for spinal cord injury (SCI) have primarily emphasized the acute phase of management, with recommendations addressing optimal timing for surgical decompression, hemodynamic parameters, prevention of secondary injury, and initiation of early rehabilitation.[8]

For example, the 2017 joint guidelines from the American Association of Neurological Surgeons and Congress of Neurological Surgeons were developed to standardize care in the acute and immediate rehabilitation phases, but specifically excluded chronic SCI from their scope. Similarly, updated guidelines highlight the need for close monitoring and management of respiratory and cardiovascular function immediately following cervical SCI.[9,20] However, there remains a notable lack of standardized guidance within these guidelines concerning long-term management and rehabilitation of individuals living with chronic SCI, particularly in relation to strategies for chronic respiratory insufficiency or ventilator dependency.

Phrenic nerve stimulation (PNS) is a technique that reanimates the diaphragm in patients with central respiratory insufficiency.[1] Clinicians have used PNS to address a number of conditions, including congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, high cervical SCI, brain stem injury, and severe idiopathic sleep apnea.[1,28,30,34]

Briefly, PNS implantation involves surgical placement of electrical leads around the phrenic nerve and connection of the leads to subcutaneous radio receivers.[1,3] The subcutaneous radio receivers convert radio signals originating from an external radio transmitter into electrical impulses, which then propagate to the motor area of the diaphragm and elicit contractions. An advantage of an external radio transmitter is the adjustability of the frequency and amplitude of the electrical wave [Figure 1].[1] After implantation, patients undergo a conditioning phase to strengthen the diaphragm, with the aim of achieving independence from mechanical ventilation (MV).[1] PNS was first used in clinical practice in the 1970s, with early case reports and series demonstrating its feasibility and potential benefits;[13,33] however, this technique was not widely adopted and was eventually supplanted by diaphragmatic stimulation techniques, which used more direct and reliable stimulation systems. As technological advancements led to the development of sophisticated and consistent wireless stimulation systems, new studies began demonstrating the usability and reliability of PNS technology.[12] Despite this, PNS is still not widely used in current medical practice because it requires specialized expertise and lacks high-quality clinical evidence.[16]

In this study, we aim to evaluate PNS outcomes in a patient cohort from our institution, review current literature, and present our experiences with PNS technology to ensure that the therapeutic effects of PNS are maximized.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort and data collection

We conducted this study using the preferred reporting of case series in surgery 2023 guidelines (http://www. processguideline.com/).[22] A retrospective chart review was conducted on patients who underwent the PNS procedure at a regional Level 1 trauma center between January 1, 2020, and October 7, 2024. The decision to offer PNS required consensus among a team of neurosurgeons, critical care practitioners, and pulmonologists. The PNS procedure was conducted by an experienced board-certified neurosurgeon (blinded for review). We extracted demographic information, indications for PNS, operative data, complications, and outcomes from the medical record. The etiology of the patient’s underlying condition was classified as “Acute/Traumatic” or “Chronic/Syndromic.” Examples of “Acute/Traumatic” included cervical SCI, brainstem stroke, and vasculopathy. Examples of “Chronic/Syndromic” included neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs), such as spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA) or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Diaphragmatic pacing success and complication rates were the primary outcome measures. A telephone interview was conducted with patients who underwent successful PNS, and these patients provided feedback regarding their satisfaction with PNS and how it altered their lives.

Literature review

We conducted a supplemental literature search using the PubMed online database from the inception of the study through October 16, 2024, with the following search terms: (“phrenic nerve stimulation” OR “diaphragm pacing” AND “cervical trauma” OR “spinal trauma” OR “central hypoventilation”). To meet the inclusion criteria for the literature review, studies describing PNS treatments in individuals suffering from cervical CSI were required to be in the form of an original case report or series that was written in English and reported follow-up data. Editorials, letters, review articles, and studies involving nonhuman subjects were excluded from the literature review.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to all institutional guidelines and ethical standards. IRB approval was received from the University of Oklahoma IRB Board on 08/31/2023, IRB#16209.

RESULTS

Surgical procedure: Right PNS

The placement of a right PNS stimulator system (Avery Biomedical Devices, Commack, NY, USA) is briefly described as an example.

Patient positioning

The head was placed on a jelly donut and turned toward the left, and the right lateral neck was exposed. A shoulder roll was placed underneath the right shoulder blade to further expose the posterior lateral neck and prepping and draping were extended through the upper thorax.

Approach to phrenic nerve

A linear skin incision was made approximately 2 fingerbreadths above the clavicle, from the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle to the external jugular vein. The platysma layer was dissected, and the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle was identified. The external jugular vein was skeletonized, and vessel loops were used to retract the vein laterally, exposing the adjacent fat pad. The sternocleidomastoid and fat pad were retracted, exposing the ventral surface of the anterior scalene muscle, which allowed for visualization of the right phrenic nerve. The functionality of the nerve was confirmed with direct nerve stimulation. The phrenic nerve was then circumferentially dissected.

Electrode and receiver placement

The electrode was placed around the phrenic nerve and secured to the ventral surface of the anterior scalene muscle [Figures 2a and b]. An incision was made approximately 2 fingerbreadths below the clavicle, and the pectoralis muscle was dissected down to the level of the fascia, creating a pocket. A shunt passer was tunneled from the chest incision to the neck incision, through which the distal end of the electrode was passed. The distal end of the electrode was connected to the receiver, and the receiver was placed in the pocket. A retention loop was created in the neck and secured to the ventral surface of the anterior scalene muscle. The receiver and generator were then tested. The amplitude required to stimulate adequate tidal volume was identified (500 mA), and the upper range of amplitude (600–900 mA) did not activate the right shoulder or arm. The connection between the generator and receiver was tested once again after closure.

Case series

Our cohort consisted of 10 patients (eight males and two females) aged 12–68 years (median age = 19 years) who collectively underwent 13 procedures involving implantation of a phrenic nerve stimulator [Table 1]. Seven patients were classified as “Acute/Traumatic,” and three patients were classified as “Chronic/Syndromic.” The most common diagnosis was SCI, accounting for 4 of the cases. Other diagnoses included arteriovenous malformation herniation (n = 1), brainstem infarct (n = 1), central sleep apnea/idiopathic hypersomnia (n = 1), severe traumatic brain injury (n = 1), ALS (n = 1), and SCA (n = 1).

Summary of all patients.

At index procedure, PNS was placed on the right side in three patients, on the left side in two patients, and bilaterally in 5. Two patients underwent a second surgery for PNS placement on the contralateral side, and one patient required revision surgery. Pacing was successfully achieved in eight out of ten patients. Notably, the two patients who did not achieve pacing shared a history of NDD (i.e., ALS and SCA). Complications were observed in two patients: the first involved a left phrenic nerve electrode that migrated caudally in the perioperative period, resulting in upper trunk brachial plexus stimulation and shoulder activation with each stimulation; and the second involved postoperative hematomas that had formed in the chest pockets of a patient which obstructed the transmission of the electrical signals from the generator to receivers. While the first patient required a revision surgery to address the migration, the hematomas in the second patient resolved independently within 15 days [Table 1].

There were no neurovascular injuries, deep wound infections, or device malfunctions. The follow-up period ranged from 2 to 38 months (median follow-up time = 17 months).

Unstructured patient-reported outcomes were obtained from five of the eight patients who experienced successful stimulation [Table 1]. One participant stopped using PNS due to adverse effects involving shoulder activation, whereas the four remaining subjects reported significant improvements in quality-of-life after PNS implantation, including reductions in respiratory effort, improvements in autonomy, and restoration of speech function. In one case, PNS was associated with a reduction in pre-existing syncopal episodes and improvements in cognitive function and mood. Two subjects regained the ability to breathe spontaneously with PNS as a bridge.

Illustrative case: Efficacy of PNS in SCI

Patient #8 was a 46-year-old male with a significant history of hepatorenal syndrome who presented with a complete high cervical SCI, secondary to a traumatic fall. His symptoms included quadriplegia, autonomic instability, and loss of sensation below C4. Imaging showed significant cervical spinal cord edema and signs suggestive of infarct. Decompression of C2–T2 and posterior cervical fusion were undertaken. The patient experienced an uncomplicated perioperative course and continued MV after surgery. After several unsuccessful weaning attempts, the patient opted for bilateral PNS to address the loss of central respiratory drive.

Phrenic nerve stimulators were implanted, as described previously. The patient was conditioned with increasing intervals of PNS, beginning with 3 h. By day 3, the patient was tolerating 8 h of PNS pacing and trending toward MV independence. However, on day 10, the phrenic nerve stimulators failed to capture initiating impulses due to surgical site hematomas. PNS was reactivated on day 25 after the resolution of the hematomas. The patient was initiated at 10 h of PNS pacing and achieved MV independence. On day 36, the patient could initiate breathing on his own. The patient was successfully weaned off PNS over the course of 2 weeks.

Illustrative case: NDD

Patient #9 was a 68-year-old male with a history of hypoventilation syndrome and progressive generalized weakness. He reported difficulty breathing after heart surgery and required 24-h continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) support. A diagnostic workup showed possible phrenic nerve injury associated with heart surgery. Phrenic nerve stimulator placement was selected as the treatment modality.

The right phrenic nerve was identified in the patient, but there was no activation of the diaphragm with direct PNS. Tidal volumes also remained unchanged with successive stimulations of increasing amplitude; thus, the phrenic nerve stimulator placement was aborted. In the postoperative period, electromyogram (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS) were performed, given the phrenic nerve dysfunction and progressive quadriparesis. The EMG and NCS studies suggested that the patient was suffering from ALS that had developed recently.

Literature search

We identified 70 studies on diaphragm pacing published between 1972 and 2022, of which 23 (18 cases series and five case studies) met inclusion criteria [Figure 3 and Tables 2-4].[2,4,5,7,10,11,13,14,16-19,21,23-27,29-32,34,36,37]

Eight studies focused exclusively on adults, three on pediatric patients, and 12 on both adults and children, yielding a total study cohort of 390 patients. Among those 390 patients, 259 had a history of spinal cord trauma that led to respiratory insufficiency. The mean follow-up period was 37 months (range of 3–384 months).

Fifteen studies specified the modalities used for pre-implant assessment of diaphragm function, including percutaneous stimulation (eight studies), percutaneous stimulation + fluoroscopy (three studies), EMG (two studies), fluoroscopy + ultrasound (one study), and percutaneous stimulation ultrasound (1 study). In seven studies, the pre-implant assessment method was not reported.

Two hundred eighty-five patients underwent bilateral PNS placement, and 105 underwent unilateral PNS placement. Of the studies that reported the surgical approach (n = 18), 12 used the transcervical approach. Among the 675 total stimulation sites, only 23 instances of immediate stimulation failure were reported. The primary outcome measure was the number of patients who achieved complete independence from MV. The percentage of patients achieving ventilation independence varied widely across studies: 100% of patients achieved independence in five studies, 70–99% in 6, 50–69% in 5, and 50% in 7. Overall, 61% of patients achieved full-time independence from MV. Immediate postoperative complications were reported in two studies, including one case of diaphragmatic nerve injury and two cases of reoperation due to equipment malfunction. Long-term data on reoperation rates were reported for eight studies. The most common reasons for reoperation were mechanical failures or unit upgrades.

DISCUSSION

SCI

PNS can be an effective method for restoring respiratory function in patients with high cervical SCI. Within the past decade, the application and efficacy of PNS in SCI cases have been well-documented. However, large-scale controlled studies have been limited.

In a prospective clinical study lasting more than 20 years and involving 64 patients with SCI, Hirschfeld et al.[16] showed that patients who underwent PNS treatment experienced significantly lower frequencies of respiratory infections than those who underwent MV. In addition, PNS has been shown to improve speech quality and increase the likelihood of employment or education after rehabilitation.[16] A retrospective study involving 126 patients with high cervical SCI showed that patients who underwent phrenic nerve pacing survived slightly longer than those who underwent MV (P = 0.04) and experienced a better quality of life.

Although PNS has been shown to be an effective tool for weaning patients off MV, the outcomes of 2 patients are of note because these patients demonstrated the rare ability to spontaneously initiate breathing. It is unclear how PNS facilitated spontaneous respiration in these patients, but we suspect that PNS could have acted as a temporizing measure that facilitated gradual improvement of phrenic nerve function over time, leading to sufficient partial recovery from SCI. Overall, we showed that patients with traumatic SCI experienced a pacing success rate of 100% and an overall success rate of 85%–rates comparable to those of similar investigations.

NDDs

The role of PNS in the context of NDD is not well known. However, direct diaphragmatic pacing has been studied as an early intervention for NDDs. The RespiStimALS clinical trial included 74 participants suffering from ALS with attendant moderate respiratory involvement who were randomly assigned to receive either active or sham stimulation.[30] Researchers found that early diaphragm pacing did not delay the need for noninvasive ventilation and was associated with a decrease in survival. Indeed, the trial was terminated early due to excess mortality in the treatment group.[15] Since this study, direct diaphragmatic pacing has not been explored in the context of NDDs.

The variable effectiveness of PNS probably reflects the distinct pathological processes associated with SCI and NDD. In the context of SCI, the efficacy of PNS can be attributed to the preservation of neural circuitry below the lesion site, which maintains functional properties when externally stimulated, whereas the efficacy of PNS in NDDs may be limited by the progressive nature of these conditions. The progressive degeneration of neurons and neural pathways compromises the integrity of peripheral nerves, thereby reducing electrochemical signal propagation and limiting PNS’s effectiveness.[6]

Various preoperative methods have been used to assess phrenic nerve viability. Transcutaneous nerve stimulation was initially the most common method, and, though noninvasive, it can be impeded by the physical distance between the cutaneous surface and the phrenic nerve, resulting in low sensitivity.[35] Direct laparoscopic evaluation facilitates real-time assessment of diaphragm contractility but is highly invasive.[35] The fluoroscopic “sniff-test” has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool for assessing phrenic nerve function, with demonstrated accuracy, sensitivity, and reproducibility.[38] However, this procedure requires specialized expertise and further evaluation in large-scale trials. By leveraging the expertise of pulmonologists, clinicians can make more well-informed decisions regarding the most suitable modality for preoperative assessment.

Anatomic considerations

Clinicians must correctly position patients to ensure the safe and optimal placement of the PNS system, but this may be difficult for patients with neck contractures or those who have undergone cervical spine surgery with instrumentation. To overcome this, clinicians can access the phrenic nerve using the video-assisted thoracoscopic surgical (VATS) technique-a less invasive option that allows for direct visualization and manipulation without requiring extensive neck rotation. VATS-based approaches have been used to access and safely displace the phrenic nerve for the implantation of a phrenic nerve stimulator.[27]

Since PNS candidates typically suffer from high cervical SCI, clinicians must also consider the intraoperative management of patients with artificial airways. In these cases, clinicians may need to suture the patient’s tracheostomy to the skin of the neck to remove the tracheostomy collar safely. Clinicians’ understanding and management of these anatomical considerations and potential complications are crucial for optimizing surgical outcomes in patients undergoing PNS treatment.

Limitations

The present study does have limitations. The small cohort size and retrospective design limit the generalizability of our results, and the absence of a control group prevents direct comparisons with those undergoing MV. In addition, the relatively short median follow-up period (17 months) restricts our ability to assess the long-term durability of the device. Similarly, our patient-reported outcomes were collected through unstructured interviews, potentially introducing recall bias and limiting quantitative analysis.

Despite these limitations, this research lays the groundwork for future larger-scale prospective multicenter trials with standardized outcome measures.

CONCLUSION

Our results demonstrate that, in carefully selected patients (particularly those with high cervical SCIs), PNS can effectively achieve ventilator independence. However, as the review of the literature suggests, there is still a clear need for larger controlled studies and randomized controlled trials to establish its long-term efficacy and safety profile across various patient populations.
