Outflow Tract Patency and Ventricular Compression as Determinants of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation Hemodynamics in a Transesophageal Echocardiography–guided Swine Model of Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Felipe Teran, Clark G. Owyang, Manuel Martin-Flores, Robert A. Finkelstein, Joanna Palasz, Caleb Suh, Joshua Satalin, Andrea L. King, Derek Lao, Aaron Landau, Daniel Lopez, Benjamin S. Abella, Joaquin Araos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Perioperative cardiac arrest carries high mortality. Standard landmark-guided chest compressions frequently overlie the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). In the operating room, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can guide compression location in real time. Because LVOT-centered compressions may narrow the outflow tract and reduce direct left ventricle compression, This study hypothesized that continuous intra-arrest TEE guidance to maintain mid–left ventricle compressions would improve hemodynamics versus LVOT-centered compressions.Methods:In a swine model, ventricular fibrillation was induced and followed by 10 min of basic life support. Continuous midesophageal long-axis TEE was used to randomly deliver chest compressions over the mid–left ventricle (CC-LV, n = 8) or the LVOT (CC-LVOT, n = 5). The primary endpoint was coronary perfusion pressure (CPP). Secondary endpoints included end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETco2), left ventricle and right ventricle compression pressures (LVP and RVP), and systolic and diastolic aortic pressures. LVOT collapse was quantified with M-mode. Linear mixed-effects models were used to examine differences in hemodynamic endpoints between the CC-LV and the CC-LVOT groups after adjusting for baseline characteristics.RESULTS:During resuscitation, after accounting for baseline imbalances between groups, CC-LV generated higher CPP than CC-LVOT (difference, 15.6 mmHg; 95% CI, 9.8 to 21.5), as well as higher systolic and diastolic aortic pressures (differences, 32.4 mmHg [95% CI, 12.9 to 51.9] and 11.3 mmHg [95% CI, 3.6 to 19.0], respectively), and higher LVP and RVP (differences, 82.4 mmHg [95% CI, 31.8 to 132.9] and 77.5 mmHg [95% CI, 17.7 to 137.3], respectively). ETco2 was also higher with CC-LV (difference, 11.9 mmHg; 95% CI, 3.0 to 20.7). LVOT collapse was greater with CC-LVOT (82% vs. 10%). LVOT collapse correlated with lower CPP, ETco2, and aortic pressures, while LVP correlated positively with these variables.Conclusions:Continuous TEE-guided, left ventricle–targeted compressions preserved LVOT patency and improved intra-arrest hemodynamics. Correlations between LVOT collapse, left ventricle compression pressures, and perfusion variables suggest that differences between left ventricle and LVOT compressions reflect dynamic outflow tract narrowing and reduced ventricular compression.

Cardiac arrest remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with low survival rates despite advances in emergency cardiovascular care.1 Closed-chest cardiopulmonary resuscitation remains the foundation of cardiac arrest management and restores circulation through a combination of cardiac-pump (direct ventricular compression) and thoracic-pump (intrathoracic pressure–mediated) mechanisms.2

Early intra-arrest transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) studies demonstrated valve motion consistent with direct cardiac compression, with mitral valve closure and aortic valve opening during the compression phase.3,4 These observations informed current guidance recommending chest compressions over the lower half of the sternum.1 However, accumulating evidence suggests that this landmark may not be optimal for all patients.5 Recent work in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest has shown that when compressions are delivered at guideline-mandated sternal landmarks, the area of maximal compression (AMC) frequently overlies the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) or aortic root, producing dynamic narrowing that can impede ejection and that is associated with lower rates of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).6–9 Over the last decade, focused resuscitative TEE has emerged as a practical tool to visualize these phenomena directly, confirm compression location, and guide corrective maneuvers in prehospital, emergency department, and intensive care unit settings.10–13

Recent perioperative consensus recommendations emphasize the unique advantages of the operating room setting, which is well suited for physiology-guided resuscitation because anesthesia teams often have immediate access to invasive arterial pressure monitoring, capnography, and TEE, providing an opportunity to tailor resuscitation to individual cardiac anatomy and physiology in real time.14 Furthermore, contemporary perioperative resuscitation guidelines prioritize rapid etiologic diagnosis, minimizing interruptions, and judicious use of point-of-care ultrasound during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) while avoiding prolonged pauses.14 Preclinical work has suggested that directing compressions over the mid–left ventricle, rather than the LVOT, augments blood pressure, end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETco2), and cerebral blood velocity and increases the likelihood of ROSC.15,16 These studies, however, used transthoracic echocardiography or TEE only to identify the compression site and did not evaluate continuous, real-time guidance to maintain the AMC. Moreover, whether such an approach improves physiology primarily by reducing dynamic LVOT collapse, by enhancing direct ventricular compression, or through the interaction of both mechanisms remains unclear. Using a previously established porcine model of ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest, we designed a randomized study to test whether TEE-guided delivery of compressions at prespecified locations alters intra-arrest hemodynamics.17 We hypothesized that chest compressions centered over the mid–left ventricle (CC-LV) would preserve LVOT patency, generate higher ventricular compression pressures, and thereby improve key basic life support (BLS) hemodynamics. The primary outcome was coronary perfusion pressure (CPP), a well-established predictor of resuscitation success,18,19 and secondary outcomes were aortic pressures (AoP), and ETco2, another well-established surrogate of cardiac output during cardiac arrest.1

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Animal preparation

This was a randomized, controlled experimental study in a swine model of cardiac arrest approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine (Ithaca, New York; protocol No. 2022–0135). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.20 The animals were randomized to one of two experimental groups (CC-LV or CC-LVOT), without a control group, using simple randomization, with allocation performed after instrumentation and baseline data collection. The experimental unit for all analyses was the individual animal. Fourteen healthy juvenile Yorkshire pigs (35 to 55 kg, 7 female) were anesthetized and endotracheally intubated, with eight allocated to the CC-LV group and six to the CC-LVOT group. Baseline mechanical ventilation (Evita V500; Draeger, Germany) was started with a tidal volume of 10 ml · kg−1, a respiratory rate of 15 min−1, and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 cm H2O. Baseline ventilator settings were selected based on previous work from our group demonstrating stable airway driving pressures consistently less than 10 cm H2O and normocapnia under these conditions. Hemodynamic variables were monitored using high-fidelity solid-state micromanometer catheters (5 Fr; Millar Inc., USA), including ascending AoP via a 7 Fr femoral artery introducer and left ventricle and right ventricular pressures via introducers placed in the left carotid artery and right external jugular vein, respectively. Catheters were calibrated before insertion using a two-point electronic reference manometer (Millar TCB-600). ETco2 was continuously recorded using a mainstream carbon dioxide sensor (Capnostat 5; Respironics, USA). All analog signals were sampled at 400 Hz (PowerLab 16/35; ADInstruments, USA) and archived in LabChart (ADInstruments) for offline analysis. Ventricular fibrillation was induced with a transvenous pacing lead (Edwards Lifesciences, USA) advanced through a left external jugular vein introducer.

TEE Imaging and Chest Compression Localization

Thoracic surface landmarks corresponding to the LVOT and mid–left ventricle were identified and marked using transthoracic echocardiography in two orthogonal planes to visualize the aortic valve, papillary muscles, and left ventricle apex. A TEE probe (TEExi/8–3 MHz; Fujifilm SonoSite, USA) was then inserted to obtain a midesophageal long-axis (ME LAX) view for real-time guidance of chest compressions. Throughout CPR, TEE was used to maintain the assigned compression location (mid–left ventricle vs. LVOT) and to prompt minor positional adjustments of the piston as needed to keep the target centered. The experimental protocol is summarized in figure 1. In animals randomized to the CC-LVOT group, the piston was initially positioned between the externally marked mid–left ventricle and aortic valve landmarks. During CPR, real-time TEE with ME-LAX view confirmed that the center of maximal compression was located approximately 2 cm caudal to the aortic valve annulus in the imaging plane.

Cardiac Arrest Induction and Cpr protocol

After instrumentation and baseline data collection, the animals were randomized by block to one of two CPR strategies: CC-LV or CC-LVOT. Each animal received only the assigned compression location throughout resuscitation. Ventricular fibrillation was induced at protocol initiation (i.e., T0) by connecting the right ventricle pacing catheter to two 9-V batteries and was followed by a 5-min no-flow interval. At minute 5 (T5), unsynchronized ventilation resumed (fractional inspired oxygen tension [Fio2], 1.0; tidal volume, 8 ml · kg−1; rate, 10 breaths/min). Mechanical chest compressions were delivered using a piston device (Life-Stat 1008; Michigan Instruments, USA) at 100 compressions/min, approximately 5-cm depth, with a fixed 50:50 compression–release duty cycle according to the manufacturer’s specifications. The piston was centered over the premarked target (mid–left ventricle over the papillary muscles for CC-LV; caudal to the aortic valve for CC-LVOT), and positioning was maintained under live TEE (ME LAX). To link TEE-defined LVOT patency or obstruction with simultaneous hemodynamic performance, the protocol was limited to 10 min of BLS. Advanced cardiac life support interventions (e.g., epinephrine, defibrillation) were withheld to avoid the confounding physiologic effects of vasoactive drugs and shocks.

Data Collection and Study Outcomes

The primary physiologic outcome was CPP, defined as diastolic aortic pressure (DAoP) minus right ventricle end-diastolic pressure.18 Right ventricle end-diastolic pressure was defined as the right ventricle pressure immediately before the beginning of the compression upstroke (fig. 2), which served as a surrogate for central venous pressure as used in classical CPP definitions during cardiac arrest studies.19 Secondary outcomes included systolic aortic pressure (SAoP) and DAoP, left ventricle and right ventricle peak compression pressures, and ETco2.

Physiologic data were collected at baseline (before ventricular fibrillation) and across five BLS epochs (E1 to E5) spaced throughout the 10 min of CPR. Each epoch comprised approximately 30 consecutive compressions (capturing approximately 3 breaths). To minimize respiratory-phase influences, variables at baseline and within each epoch were averaged across the selected beats using the “Scope View” tool in LabChart; figure 3 shows representative waveforms from one analyzed epoch. LVOT patency during CPR was assessed using an anatomic M-mode view at the LVOT annulus obtained from the ME LAX TEE window during an approximately 1-min period early in BLS (within the first 5 min of CPR). For each case, LVOT diameter (D) was measured at compression (comp) and relaxation (relax), and LVOT collapse was calculated as 100 × (D_relaxation − D_compression)/D_relaxation (fig. 4), with larger values indicating greater dynamic closure during compression.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are reported as means ± SD, and categorical variables are reported as counts. Given the small sample size, baseline differences between randomized groups were assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) rather than hypothesis testing, as SMDs are less influenced by sample size and more directly quantify the magnitude of imbalance between groups. SMDs were calculated as the absolute difference in group means divided by the pooled SD. An absolute SMD of less than 0.1 was considered to indicate acceptable balance. Longitudinal CPR data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models fitted separately for each physiologic variable, specifying animal as a random intercept and group, time (baseline and E1 to E5), and their interactions were analyzed as fixed effects. Overall group differences across CPR epochs were estimated from marginal predictions and marginal effects (margins and dydx), where dydx denotes the model-based marginal effect, defined as the average difference in the outcome between compression groups. Because several baseline variables showed imbalance between groups, as reflected by SMDs, the final models were fitted using postbaseline CPR epochs only (E1 to E5) and adjusted for the baseline value of the corresponding physiologic variable. LVOT collapse (%) was compared between groups using an unpaired t test because only a single measurement per animal was obtained. Associations between LVOT collapse (%) and per-animal CPR means of SAoP, DAoP, CPP, and ETco2 were assessed using Pearson’s correlation. Because CC-LV also produced higher left ventricle compression pressures than CC-LVOT, we additionally evaluated correlations between per-animal mean left ventricle compression pressure and these same perfusion variables (SAoP, DAoP, CPP, and ETco2) to examine the relationship between ventricular pressure generation and intra-arrest hemodynamics. Model assumptions were assessed by visual inspection of residual Q–Q plots and residuals versus fitted values, without evidence of major departures from normality or homoscedasticity. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during CPR delivery due to the nature of the intervention. However, data used for outcome assessment and analysis were analyzed blinded to group allocation. The data were analyzed using Stata/SE 19 (StataCorp LLC, USA), and graphs were prepared in Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Sample Size estimation

Inclusion criteria were successful completion of instrumentation and completion of the BLS protocol with analyzable physiologic recordings. No a priori exclusion criteria for animals or data points were specified. The animals that did not reach the CPR phase because of instrumentation-related complications were not included in CPR analyses. The trial was powered for the primary physiologic outcome, a between-group difference in per-animal mean CPP during CPR (CC-LV vs. CC-LVOT), using a two-sample comparison with two-sided α = 0.05 and power greater than or equal to 80%. Previous preclinical models and data from the implementation phase of our experimental preparation informed sample-size estimation. In an asphyxia model, Marshall et al.16 reported an ≈10-mmHg increase in diastolic pressure with left ventricle–centered compressions compared with standard CPR, a physiologic difference closely related to CPP. Based on these data and additional implementation-phase hemodynamics from our laboratory, we anticipated a large CPP separation favoring CC-LV. Assuming a between-animal SD of 8 to 10 mmHg for the per-animal mean CPP, a sample of n = 6 animals per group would provide power greater than or equal to 80% to detect a 13- to 16-mmHg absolute difference (Cohen’s d ≈ 1.6). To accommodate potential losses before analyzable CPR epochs, we planned for 15 experiments targeting 12 or more analyzable animals. Two pilot CC-LV experiments that met all protocol criteria were included in the final cohort, yielding 8-ml left ventricle animals; one CC-LVOT animal was lost during instrumentation, resulting in five analyzable CC-LVOT experiments.

Results

Baseline hemodynamics showed some imbalance between groups, particularly for systolic and diastolic aortic pressure, CPP, right ventricle pressure, and heart rate, whereas left ventricle pressure and ETco2 were more similar between groups (table 1). The largest standardized mean differences were observed for SAoP (0.87), HR (0.79), and DAoP (0.71). Therefore, mixed-effects models were refitted over postbaseline epochs (E1 to E5) with adjustment for the baseline value of the corresponding physiologic variable. Representative traces are shown in figure 5, demonstrating the typical higher pressures and ETco2 seen with CC-LV in one pig. A complete set of baseline-adjusted mixed-effects model outputs is provided in Supplemental Digital Content file 1 (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E577). For each outcome, the corresponding baseline value was included as a covariate in the model, as indicated by the “c.base_[variable]” term in the model specification. As shown in figure 6 (A to F), CC-LV generated uniformly higher perfusion and pressure metrics than CC-LVOT across CPR epochs. CPP was substantially higher during CC-LV (15.0 mmHg, 95% CI, 11.5 to 18.6) than CC-LVOT (−0.6 mmHg, 95% CI, −5.2 to 3.90), yielding an overall difference of 15.6 mmHg (95% CI, 9.8 to 21.5; P < 0.001; fig. 6A). ETco2 also favored CC-LV, with means of 30.9 mmHg (95% CI, 25.5 to 36.4) versus 19.1 mmHg (95% CI, 12.2 to 26.1), and an average difference of 11.9 mmHg (95% CI, 3.0 to 20.7; P = 0.009; fig. 6B). Left ventricle compressive pressure was markedly higher with CC-LV (139.6 mmHg; 95% CI, 108.3 to 170.9) than CC-LVOT (57.2 mmHg; 95% CI, 17.6 − 96.9), with a difference of 82.4 mmHg (95% CI, 31.8 to 132.9; P = 0.001; fig. 6C). Right ventricle compression pressure showed a large separation, with means of 143.3 mmHg (95% CI, 107.3 to 179.4) during CC-LV versus 65.0 mmHg (95% CI, 19.8 to 111.97) during CC-LVOT, and an overall difference of 77.5 mmHg (95% CI, 17.7 to 137.3; P = 0.011; fig. 6D). SAoP similarly favored CC-LV (64.3 vs. 32.9 mmHg; difference, 32.4 mmHg; 95% CI, 12.9 to 51.9; P = 0.001; fig. 6E). DAoP was also higher with CC-LV (20.9 vs. 9.6 mmHg; difference, 11.3 mmHg; 95% CI, 3.6 to 19.0; P = 0.004; fig. 6F).

LVOT diameter measured during CPR showed marked dynamic collapse with CC-LVOT, contrasting with preserved patency during CC-LV. LVOT collapse (%) was 81.7 ± 12.6 for CC-LVOT versus 10.2 ± 9.6 for CC-LV (P = 0.0106; table 2).

Correlations between dynamic LVOT collapse and intra-arrest physiology are shown in Supplemental Digital Content file 2 (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E578). Greater LVOT collapse (%) was associated with lower CPP (R2 = 0.59; P = 0.002), lower ETco2 (R2 = 0.62; P = 0.001), and lower SAoP (R2 = 0.62; P = 0.001) and DAoP (R2 = 0.52; P = 0.006). Left ventricle (R2 = 0.45; P = 0.001) and right ventricle (R2 = 0.52; P = 0.005) compressive pressures also showed an inverse relationship with collapse. Higher left ventricle compressive pressure was positively associated with CPP (R2 = 0.49; P = 0.007), ETco2 (R2 = 0.42; P = 0.017), SAoP (R2 = 0.50; P = 0.007), and DAoP (R2 = 0.41; P = 0.018). These correlation analyses were exploratory and intended to generate mechanistic hypotheses rather than establish causality.

Discussion

Perioperative cardiac arrests occur in monitored settings where ventilation, invasive arterial pressure, capnography, and TEE are often immediately available. Recent perioperative resuscitation guidance highlights the potential value of physiologic feedback, including the use of TEE during arrest, yet the hemodynamic consequences of compression location remain poorly characterized. In this context, our findings support the concept that compression mechanics directly influence intra-arrest physiology.14 In our model, CC-LV generated higher CPP, AoP, and ETco2, whereas CC-LVOT produced substantial dynamic LVOT collapse. Further, CC-LV generated markedly higher left ventricle and right ventricle compression pressures, and higher left ventricle compressive pressure was positively associated with several perfusion variables, suggesting that reduced direct ventricular compression contributes to impaired hemodynamics during LVOT-centered compressions, alongside dynamic narrowing of the outflow tract. These data align with previous work showing that mid–left ventricle compressions preserve LVOT patency and forward flow, whereas LVOT-centered compressions produce suboptimal perfusion.8–17 To our knowledge, this is the first study to combine real-time continuous TEE assessment of LVOT collapse and ventricular compression with quantitative hemodynamics, showing that greater collapse and reduced ventricular compression are associated with worse intra-arrest physiology.

Although CPR has been used for decades, the mechanisms generating forward blood flow during closed-chest CPR remain incompletely understood. Classic cardiac and thoracic pump theories attribute perfusion to either direct cardiac compression or increased intrathoracic pressure.2 Early studies using intra-arrest TEE demonstrated valve motion consistent with a cardiac pump contribution, including mitral valve closure during compression and aortic valve opening during the release phase.3,4 These observations established TEE as a powerful tool for studying CPR physiology. More recently, the esophageal view has enabled real-time identification of maximal compression and ventricular deformation during CPR, allowing individualized, physiology-guided resuscitation. Our findings extend this by showing that left ventricle–centered compressions preserved LVOT patency and generated higher left ventricle and right ventricle compression pressures, indicating more effective ventricular deformation during compression.

Preclinical studies have reinforced the importance of compression location. In a swine model, Marshall et al.16 showed that compressions over the left ventricle produced markedly better hemodynamics and cerebral perfusion compared with standard sternal compressions over the LVOT. Several investigations have demonstrated that standard compression landmarks often overlie the LVOT or aortic root in both out-of-hospital and in-hospital cardiac arrest, affecting forward flow.6–8 Catena et al.8 reported that LVOT patency was the strongest predictor of return of spontaneous circulation, present in all patients who achieved ROSC and in only a small minority of those who did not. Chu et al.9 similarly found higher rates of ROSC and intensive care unit survival among patients without aortic valve compression during CPR. A recent analysis from the Resuscitative TEE Collaborative Registry (NCT04972526) an ongoing international multicenter registry evaluating TEE-guided resuscitation, showed that the AMC was over the left ventricle in only approximately 30% of cardiac arrest cases and that compression location was more frequently modified when CPR was delivered manually.10

A key innovation of this study was the integration of continuous TEE during the delivery of the intervention, CPR, with the chest compressions targeting either the left ventricle or the LVOT. Previous preclinical work, including that by Anderson et al.15 and Marshall et al.,16 used echocardiography to identify compression landmarks but did not maintain imaging during active CPR and therefore could not characterize the degree of LVOT narrowing or the pattern of ventricular deformation during ongoing compressions. Our protocol ensured continuous TEE visualization of the randomized target, enabling real-time assessment of LVOT behavior and ventricular deformation. This allowed simultaneous quantification of dynamic LVOT collapse and left ventricle and right ventricle compression pressures, which has not been previously described. In our model, left ventricle–centered compressions generated higher left ventricle and right ventricle pressures, and greater LVOT collapse and lower left ventricle compression were associated with reduced perfusion variables. Importantly, the lower left ventricle pressures observed during LVOT-centered compressions indicate that reduced chamber deformation alone could plausibly account for impaired forward flow, independent of outflow tract narrowing.

Overall, the data support a cardiac pump-dominant mechanism with left ventricle–centered compressions, in which direct ventricular compression preserved LVOT patency and enabled effective antegrade ejection, yielding higher CPP and arterial pressures. In contrast, LVOT-centered compressions likely obstructed the outflow tract during compression, reducing effective ejection despite similar compression rate and depth. In fact, CC-LV yielded significantly higher left ventricle and right ventricle peak pressures than CC-LVOT, and higher left ventricle peak pressure was positively correlated with CPR hemodynamics, supporting the interpretation that improved perfusion reflects both preserved LVOT patency and greater ventricular deformation during CPR. Although not directly evaluated, compression location may also influence venous return and CPP through effects on right-sided cardiac compression and intrathoracic pressure transmission. Continuous TEE enables visualization of these interacting mechanisms by resolving valve motion, ventricular deformation, and outflow tract geometry on a beat-to-beat basis.

These results have near-term relevance for the clinical implementation of TEE-guided CPR. When TEE is available, it can identify the AMC and guide repositioning to prioritize left ventricle compressions. The consistent improvements in CPP, aortic pressures, and ETco2 and their strong correlation with the degree of LVOT collapse and compressive ventricular pressures suggest that compression location should be viewed as a modifiable variable, similar to rate and depth, rather than a fixed landmark. Of note, abrupt changes in ETco2 are often used in prognostication during CPR, but ETco2 may also change with compression location and mechanics, so trends should be interpreted in context when repositioning is performed. These observations may inform future guideline discussions about individualized compression positioning and device design, for example feedback systems that keep the compression vector away from the LVOT. Future studies should test TEE-guided compression relocation across arrest models and evaluate downstream outcomes, including ROSC, survival, and neurologic function. Pragmatic human trials could assess whether targeting the AMC improves clinical endpoints and supports automated feedback to avoid LVOT obstruction, with advanced TEE enabling standardized anatomic targets for real-time guidance.

This preclinical study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in healthy juvenile swine under general anesthesia, and species-specific thoracic anatomy and our controlled conditions may limit generalizability to human perioperative arrest. Second, the sample size was modest, and despite randomization, residual confounding cannot be excluded. The small and uneven group sizes increase the risk of effect size overestimation and type I error, particularly for correlation analyses, which should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating. Third, hemodynamic comparisons and mixed model inferences were confined to BLS epochs to avoid the confounding impact of epinephrine and defibrillation; therefore, we did not assess the influence of dynamic LVOT collapse on ROSC, postresuscitation survival, or neurologic outcomes, which limits direct clinical inference beyond intra-arrest physiology. Fourth, the LVOT collapse metric relied on diameter rather than cross-sectional area, even though true LVOT narrowing depends on area, which changes with the square of the diameter; as a result, a diameter-based measure likely underestimates the actual degree of collapse. Use of M-mode also required precise alignment with the LVOT minor axis, which is difficult to maintain during CPR because cardiac motion and probe instability introduce measurement variability. The calculation further normalized to the relaxation diameter, yet this reference value may fluctuate from beat to beat under rapidly changing loading conditions, potentially adding noise to the percentage obtained. In addition, this method captured geometric narrowing along a single interrogation line and did not account for the full three-dimensional or hemodynamic complexity of dynamic obstruction, including asymmetric deformation, leaflet intrusion, or flow patterns that may not be reflected in a simple diameter change. Further, LVOT collapse was assessed at an early time point to characterize the immediate geometric effect of compression location; we did not evaluate temporal evolution of LVOT narrowing or its interaction with changing intrathoracic pressure, lung mechanics, or right-sided filling conditions during prolonged CPR, which may further modulate the observed relationships. Despite these limitations, which should be explored with cross-sectional area methods and three-dimensional echocardiography, the collapse index correlated well with perfusion variables. Fifth, chest compressions were delivered mechanically at a fixed rate and depth, which may differ from manual compressions in the operating room. Because LVOT collapse and ventricular compressive pressures were strongly interrelated, this study cannot determine the relative dominance of these mechanisms, and their effects should be considered complementary. One CC-LVOT animal was lost during instrumentation, resulting in a smaller sample size for that group. However, robust hemodynamic separation was observed despite this loss, and no additional experiments were performed to limit animal use. Nevertheless, convergent TEE and hemodynamic data consistently supported a mechanistic link between LVOT obstruction, reduced left ventricle pressure generation, and impaired perfusion-related variables.

In conclusion, in this randomized TEE-guided swine model, compressions centered over the left ventricle yielded higher perfusion pressures and higher ETco2 than compressions over the LVOT. These findings support a mechanistic link between preserved LVOT patency, more effective ventricular compression, and improved forward flow during closed chest CPR. As imaging-assisted resuscitation evolves, TEE-guided optimization of compression location represents a practical and immediately translatable strategy to improve the physiologic quality of CPR, therefore warranting evaluation in clinical care pathways.
