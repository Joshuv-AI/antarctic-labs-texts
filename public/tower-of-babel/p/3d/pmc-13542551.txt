Utility measurement tools and quality of life assessment of cleft care in Sub-Saharan Africa: A systematic review
Authors: Kwame Adu Okyere Boadu, Richard Frank-Torblu, Francisca Egyaim, Gizella Theophilia Opoku, Eva Saeh Afriyie, Rita Oduro, Michael Kwadwo Owusu-Duah, Justine Ti-Baliana Martha Naab, Angelica Boakye-Yiadom, Richard Okyere Boadu, Peter Agyei-Baffour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), there is a heavy burden on the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of both people living with cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) and their families. In these places, there is little to no utility data for cost-effectiveness analysis. The existing condition-specific patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and utility instruments were developed and validated in high-income countries (HIC). Therefore, there is a question on the effectiveness of these tools in SSA. This study systematically maps and appraises the utility measurement tools and HRQoL instruments applied in CL/P care across SSA, characterises their psychometric properties, and identifies gaps to inform instrument adaptation and development.MethodsThe Population-Concept-Context framework was used to conduct a systematic review. Searches were performed across four databases. The inclusion criteria were a peer-reviewed quantitative study conducted in SSA with a CL/P population with a named, validated QoL or utility instrument.ResultsTwelve studies from five SSA countries were included. While fourteen distinct HRQoL instruments were identified, only one study used a direct utility method. There were three condition-specific PROMs but none was formally validated in SSA. After quality appraisal, five studies were rated low, five were moderate and two had a high risk of bias. Across studies, CL/P had a significant association with low HRQoL.ConclusionsThere is a paucity of utility data and SSA-validated HRQoL instruments for CL/P. The existing instruments when applied, are done without formal cultural adaptation. This is likely to lead to ineffective application, and uninformed conclusions and decision making.

1. Introduction

The global prevalence of cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) is 1.4 per 1000 live births, making it one of the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies globally. About 4.6 million people are currently living with CL/P.1,2 In East Africa, the incidence of CL/P is estimated to be 1:700 live births, with Ethiopia alone reported at 1.5:1000. Although birth prevalence is broadly comparable across world regions, and is similar to or higher in several high-income countries (HICs), the burden of CL/P falls disproportionately on sub-Saharan Africa because of limited surgical access, delayed presentation, and scarce specialist care. 3 While 0.96 per 100,000 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) were generated in North America, that of SSA was 13.11 in 2019. 2 Cleft palate surgery is one of the 44 procedures designated essential in the World Bank’s Disease Control Priorities (3rd ed.), 4 and Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia carry the largest share of surgically amenable cleft burden of any world region, at 68%. 5

The effects of CL/P are not limited to just the structural anatomy of individuals. It leads to impaired feeding, speech and hearing difficulties, dentition problems and facial aesthetics concerns throughout the life course of individuals.3,6 CL/P further transcends to a psychological sequalae including anxiety, depression, reduced self-esteem, and social exclusion.3,6 Further, the wellbeing of caregivers, especially mothers are affected both psychologically and financially. 7 All the mentioned repercussions are multifaceted, and a right measurement of these impacts will require validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). With respect to health technology assessment (HTA), cost-effective analysis requires utility values that can anchor health-related quality of life (HRQoL) from a 0 (death) to 1 (full health) scale. 8

There is no shortage of developed and validated PROMs for people living with CL/P together with their families or proxies. The CLEFT-Q is used in the ICHOM Standard Set for cleft care, with its validation studies primarily conducted by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement 9 and Miroshnychenko and colleagues. 10 These studies, which established the tool’s cross-sectional construct validity, were mainly performed in North American and Western European countries. This instrument covers facial appearance and function together with HRQoL across 13 independently functioning scales.

Another instrument for assessing the oral health QoL of children with orofacial anomalies is the Child Oral Health Impact Profile (COHIP). 11 The specific HRQoL impact of velopharyngeal dysfunction is assessed via The Velopharyngeal Insufficiency Effects on Life Outcomes (VELO) questionnaire. Some generic instruments over the course have been applied to CL/P populations. 6 They include the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), 12 the WHO Quality of Life – Brief (WHOQOL-BREF), 13 and the EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D). 14 These encompass the standard set of CL/P outcome measures that have been defined for clinical use. 15

Nonetheless, all the listed instruments supra, were not developed and validated in low-income countries (LICs). It was rather done mostly in the North American and Western European countries. 16 As a result, SSA sensitive characteristics like linguistic heterogeneity and high rates of functional illiteracy impede on effective direct application. The wide-ranging cultures of SSA often posit that CL/P is caused by spiritual factors; socioeconomic characteristics of SSA differ from HICs.17,18 These factors further compromise direct application of the instruments. A noted gap is that prior to 2022, there was no standardised utility value for CL/P derived from patients living with CL/P in SSA. This implies that HTA in SSA on the subject matter are heavily reliant on HIC estimates, leading to an underestimation of the true burden and intervention of cleft in the region. 18

To contribute to addressing these gaps, a systematic review is therefore warranted to map the existing evidence base, characterise instruments in use, appraise their quality in SSA contexts to guide future instrument development. This review addresses two research questions: (1) What utility measurement tools have been applied in CL/P care in SSA? and (2) What HRQoL assessment instruments have been applied in CL/P care in SSA, and what are their measurement properties in this context?

2.3 Search strategy

Four databases were used for the search. They were PubMed, Google Scholar, CINAHL and AJOL. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine three concept blocks corresponding to the PCC framework. The Population block combined cleft terms (“cleft lip” OR “cleft palate” OR “cleft lip and palate” OR “orofacial cleft” OR “oral cleft” OR CL/P). The Concept block combined outcome-measurement terms (“quality of life” OR HRQoL OR “health-related quality of life” OR “patient-reported outcome*” OR PROM* OR utility OR “health utilit*” OR “health state” OR EQ-5D OR “time trade-off” OR “standard gamble” OR “visual analog*”). The Context block combined sub-Saharan Africa terms (“sub-Saharan Africa” OR “West Africa” OR “East Africa” OR “Central Africa” OR “Southern Africa” OR the names of all 48 World Bank-defined SSA countries, e.g. Nigeria OR Ethiopia OR Sudan OR Uganda OR “Democratic Republic of Congo”). Terms within each block were combined with OR, and the three blocks were combined with AND; database-specific syntax (MeSH/CINAHL headings, truncation, and field tags) was adapted for each database. The PRESS checklist 22 was used to peer-review the search strategy before execution. There were no restrictions on publication dates.

2.4 Study selection

At the title/abstract and full-text stages, screening was done by two independent reviewers. All disagreements were resolved by discussion. In some instances, a consensus was not reached via discussion, a third reviewer adjudicated. Cohen’s kappa was used to quantify inter-rater agreement. All full texts were available for screening.

2.5. Data extraction

A standardized excel sheet was used to extract data. Piloting of extraction was done on four papers. The constructs extracted were study identification; design and setting; population characteristics; instrument details (name, type, items, domains, scoring, age range); cultural adaptation and translation; and outcome data. One reviewer performed all the extractions while the second reviewer verified them.

2.6. Quality appraisal

Seven domains from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Analytical Cross-Sectional Study Checklist 23 were used to appraise the methodological quality of the included studies. They were: clarity of inclusion criteria; description of setting and subjects; validity and reliability of exposure measurement; use of objective or standard criteria for the condition; identification of confounders and mitigation strategies; validity and reliability of outcome measurement; and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Domains were either rated Yes, No or Unclear. The Overall risk of bias was classified as Low (≥6 criteria met), Moderate (4–5 criteria met), or High (≤3 criteria met or critical domain failure). While quality appraisal informed interpretation of evidence certainty, it played no role in determining the inclusivity of papers.

2.7. Synthesis

Meta-analysis was not feasible because the included studies were substantially heterogeneous in design (cross-sectional, longitudinal, and utility-elicitation studies), in the instruments applied (14 distinct HRQoL instruments plus three utility-elicitation methods, of which only the CLEFT-Q and COHIP appeared in more than one study), and in the outcome metrics reported (mean domain scores, proportions above impairment thresholds, adjusted odds ratios, and preference-based utility values). No two studies reported a common instrument-and-metric combination that could be pooled without producing a statistically uninterpretable estimate. The SWiM guideline was used to conduct the narrative synthesis of findings. 20 This synthesis was centered around the following themes:i. Instrument characteristics and deploymentii. Cultural adaptation and feasibilityiii. Methodological qualityiv. QoL and utility outcomes within SSA contextual factors.

3. Results

3.1. Search results and study selection

Database searching yielded 144 records after de-duplication. Following title and abstract screening, 40 records were retrieved for full-text review, of which 28 were excluded: 15 were with a wrong population (of which 10 of the 15 were without a named validated instrument, 4 were not peer-reviewed and 1 was a book); 5 used QoL as a secondary endpoint; 4 were either not conducted in SSA or entirely in SSA; and 4 had CL/P as a minor subgroup. Twelve studies met the full eligibility criteria and were included in the review. All were primary quantitative studies. Inter-rater agreement at full-text stage was substantial (Cohen’s κ=0.81). Fourteen distinct HRQoL instruments were identified: three condition-specific PROMs (the CLEFT-Q in three studies; the COHIP in two studies; and the VELO in one study), eleven generic PROMs (PedsQL; WHOQOL-BREF; EUROHIS-QOL 8; IOFS; and psychiatric screening tools: GHQ-28, STAI, SDS, SRQ, BSD, SDQ, HADS were applied as psychosocial QoL proxies). In addition, three utility-elicitation methods were used: the visual analogue scale (VAS), time trade-off (TTO), and standard gamble (SG), all within a single study. No indirect utility instrument was identified. The sole utility study 18 constitutes the only source of CL/P utility data in SSA (Table 1).

3.3. Cultural adaptation, translation, and feasibility

Formal cultural adaptation comprising at minimum forward translation, back-translation, and expert committee review was conducted in two studies. Ali et al. 31 produced an Arabic COHIP with modifications confirmed through test-retest reliability (ICC >0.8; n=15). Chung et al. 18 produced an Amharic adaptation of the utility instruments with third-party back-translation review and a 12-participant pilot, the most methodologically rigorous adaptation in this review. Yakubu et al. 29 used pre-existing Hausa-validated psychiatric instruments and Hausa verbal administration for other scales. Abebe et al. 32 administered an Amharic translation of the COHIP to Ethiopian patients and their parents, reporting strong internal reliability, though back-translation and committee-review procedures were not fully described. The remaining eight studies administered instruments in English without cultural adaptation, despite operating in multilingual, predominantly low-literacy settings.

Completion rates diverged substantially. Onah and Achor 7 reported a 26.7% return rate. This is attributable to passive pre-operative distribution without follow-up rendering the achieved sample of unknown representativeness. Bruneel et al. 17 documented that 50% of Ugandan caregivers were unable to complete the adapted VELO independently, with 31.3% of returned forms containing missing responses; informal qualitative observations identified Likert-format unfamiliarity, vocabulary incongruence, and social desirability bias as the principal barriers. By contrast, Chung et al. 18 achieved 97.1% and 96.3% completion in proxy and societal groups respectively, with 95% reporting complete understanding of VAS and TTO. This affirms that trained bilingual interviewer-administration substantially mitigates SSA feasibility barriers.

Surgery was associated with significant HRQoL improvement in both longitudinal studies. Michael et al. 28 reported statistically significant gains across all CLEFT-Q subscales from preoperative baseline to 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery (all p<0.001 to p=0.04). Yakubu et al. 29 documented a reduction in poor HRQoL from 60% to 20% in children and 68% to 26% in mothers at six months post-surgery (McNemar’s test; p<0.001 for all domains). In the only utility study, Chung et al. 18 found untreated CL/P proxy utilities of 0.57–0.70 to be lower than any published HIC estimates with surgery associated with a VAS gain of +0.17 (95%CI 0.09–0.26; p<.001) and TTO gain of +0.15 (95%CI 0.05–0.25; p=.005).

Across studies employing multivariable analysis, three factors independently and consistently predicted poor HRQoL: combined CLP phenotype, low socioeconomic status (SES), and stigma. Stigma and social exclusion were the single strongest predictor in Yakubu et al. (2025; AOR=3.12; 95%CI 1.88–5.19; p<0.001). Low SES predicted poor HRQoL with AOR=2.55 29 and 3.21 27 in Nigerian samples, and higher income independently predicted better utility in Ethiopia (β=+0.10; p=.01; Chung et al. 18 ). Stigma was documented in nine of the twelve studies; three described supernatural cultural attribution of CL/P causation,17,18,25 with infant abandonment reported in Ethiopia. 18 Delayed surgical presentation, defined here as primary cleft surgery undertaken well beyond the internationally recommended windows (lip surgery by approximately 3–6 months and palate surgery by approximately 12–18 months of age), was documented or implied in seven studies; reported ages at surgery ranged from early childhood to adolescence and adulthood, including a mean palatal-closure age of 14 years in one Ugandan cohort 17 and individual patients operated at 5 and 19 years. 26 Unmet need for psychological support (96.6%) and speech therapy (86.5%) was near-universal, with financial constraint the primary barrier for 84.2% of families. 27 All surgical care in included studies was delivered by non-governmental organisations (Table 3).

4. Discussion

This systematic review mapped and appraised both utility measurements and HRQoL instruments used for CL/P care across SSA. Across SSA, instrument adequacy for CL/P remains limited. Only a single direct utility study was identified, 18 and no indirect utility instrument had been applied to CL/P populations in the region. The condition-specific PROMs that were deployed were most often applied without any formal validation in the region. Feasibility data from this review indicate that the principal barriers to instrument completion are literacy limitations, linguistic mismatch, and the cultural incongruence of HIC-designed response formats; these barriers are structural rather than incidental. Three determinants were consistently and independently associated with poor HRQoL: CLP phenotype severity, socioeconomic deprivation, and stigma. These should be embedded in the construction of future instruments, and in the adaptation of existing ones, for use in SSA.

Health technology assessment (HTA) is not immune from the inherent lack of utility data for CL/P in SSA. The cost-effectiveness analyses of surgical cleft programmes in SSA rely heavily on utility values from HICs. 8 It is the HTA that steers the allocation of resources for cleft care. Chung et al. 18 showed that HIC estimates were substantially higher than those in Ethiopia, understating both the disease burden and the incremental benefit of surgery in low-resource settings. This finding is not in isolation, broader literature shows that utility weights derived from societal elicitation in LMICs differ meaningfully from those generated in Western European or North American populations. 34 The continued use of HIC utility weights in SSA HTA therefore risks systematically undervaluing surgical cleft care, with the consequence that programmes such as those delivered by Smile Train and Operation Smile are allocated fewer resources than their impact warrants.

The psychometric findings from the sole SSA PROM validation study 17 illustrate the inadequacy of deploying HIC-designed instruments without formal cultural adaptation. The VELO’s swallowing problems subscale failed in Uganda (Cronbach’s α=0.097) due to a ceiling effect that directly reflects item content incongruence: most Ugandan participants reported no swallowing difficulty irrespective of their clinical status, likely because item wording presupposes experiential frames like nasal regurgitation and feeding tube history that carries different social salience in this population. The 50% independent completion failure further reflects that self-administered Likert-scale questionnaires assume levels of health literacy and familiarity with psychometric formats that are often missing in SSA settings, necessitating extensive cross-cultural adaptation and localized evaluation. 35 The contrasting experience of Chung et al. 18 who achieved 95–97% completion through trained bilingual interviewers, Amharic translation, and a 12-participant cognitive pilot demonstrates that rigorous adaptation methodology transforms feasibility outcomes.

The dominance of psychiatric screening tools (GHQ-28, STAI, SDS, SRQ, BSD, SDQ, HADS) as de facto HRQoL proxies across four studies reflects the absence of condition-specific, validated alternatives rather than a deliberate methodological choice. These instruments were designed for psychiatric case identification, not for quantifying the multidimensional HRQoL impact of a structural anomaly, and their scores cannot substitute for preference-based utility values or for condition-specific PROM scores in clinical benchmarking or HTA. This pattern is consistent with a well-documented phenomenon in global health outcomes research whereby generic psychiatric screening is conflated with HRQoL assessment when condition-specific instruments are unavailable. 6 A recent South African study 16 illustrates a related response to this gap: rather than applying a pre-existing validated PROM, the authors used a study-specific questionnaire adapted from a prior publication and validated only internally. Because it did not employ a named, previously validated instrument, it did not meet our inclusion criteria and is discussed here rather than included in the synthesis; nonetheless, it reinforces the central finding that investigators in SSA are repeatedly compelled to improvise measurement in the absence of regionally validated tools.

Stigma emerged as the dominant and most contextually distinctive determinant of poor HRQoL in SSA CL/P care. The adjusted odds ratio of 3.12 29 for stigma and social exclusion exceeds those for cleft type and SES. These are findings that align with qualitative evidence from across SSA showing that CL/P is frequently attributed to witchcraft, adultery, or divine punishment, with consequences ranging from social exclusion and infanticide17,18,25 to economic marginalisation. 7 Existing HIC-designed instruments including the ‘perception by others’ VELO subscale, which showed ceiling effects in Uganda may systematically underdetect this stigma burden because their items were developed in social contexts where visible facial difference, while associated with psychosocial difficulty, does not carry the same supernatural or moral attribution. 17 Any SSA-specific instrument must, therefore, incorporate stigma-sensitive content developed through qualitative co-production with patients, caregivers, and communities in target settings.

Although this review was restricted to studies using named, validated quantitative instruments, a substantial and geographically distributed qualitative literature spanning South Africa, Nigeria, Ghana, Uganda, Zimbabwe, and the DRC document the cultural constructs that any SSA-specific measure would need to capture. These studies converge on three recurrent domains: supernatural, religious, and moral attributions of causation; profound stigma with material family and marital consequences; and pluralistic care-seeking that routes many families through traditional and faith healers before biomedical surgery. These are precisely the constructs that high-income-country (HIC)-derived PROMs were never designed to elicit, which is why the quantitative instruments mapped in this review systematically under-represent them.

Aetiological beliefs are near-ubiquitous but ethnically and religiously patterned. In Zimbabwe, Mzezewa and Muchemwa 36 found that among 124 parents, 31% attributed the cleft to witchcraft or ancestral spirits and only 40% to a natural occurrence, with a further group ascribing it to parental infidelity. In Nigeria, Olasoji et al. 37 documented a sharp ethnic contrast: most Yoruba parents invoked supernatural forces (evil spirits, ancestral punishment, curses), whereas most Hausa/Fulani parents framed the cleft fatalistically as the will of God. South African traditional healers similarly attributed clefts to ancestors, spirits, and witchcraft, 38 and Muslim and Hindu healers layered on further explanatory models—God’s will, karma, and a widespread eclipse superstition holding that a pregnant woman who handles a sharp object during an eclipse causes the deformity. 39 Even where biomedical awareness is high, these beliefs persist: in Ghana, roughly a third of clinic-attending mothers still endorsed an evil-spirit aetiology, 40 and in Uganda more than half of mothers attributed the cleft to a supernatural phenomenon. 41

The clinical significance of these beliefs lies in their downstream social toll, which is severe and frequently quantified. Mzezewa and Muchemwa 36 reported that the birth precipitated divorce or maternal abandonment of the child in a substantial minority of families, with affected infants sometimes labelled as evil and concealed from public view. In Uganda, a large proportion of mothers said the child was regarded as an outcast, and the great majority of medical staff reported that children with clefts were not accepted in their communities. 41 In Ghana, stigma manifested as concealment behaviour and public mockery of the child. 40 This maternal blame, concealment, marital breakdown, and community rejection constitute a stigma burden that is largely invisible to generic HRQoL and psychiatric-screening tools, yet it emerged in our quantitative synthesis as the single strongest independent predictor of poor HRQoL (adjusted OR 3.12; Yakubu et al. 29 ).

Belief systems also shape the route and timing of care, with direct implications for access and delayed presentation. In Nigeria, most Yoruba parents consulted a traditional healer to receive herbal preparations, spiritual cleansing, and ritual sacrifice before presenting to hospital, whereas almost no Hausa/Fulani parents did so, tracking the ethnic difference in causal beliefs. 37 Traditional and faith healers therefore function as de facto gatekeepers, and their orientation toward biomedicine varies. Encouragingly, South African healers largely referred onward: all but one of the healers interviewed by Dagher and Ross, 38 and nearly all Muslim healers, 39 referred patients to biomedical practitioners, although referral was unidirectional, with no reciprocal referral from the biomedical system. In other settings the healer’s role obstructs timely surgery: some Zimbabwean healers advised against surgery, claiming it defied ancestral wishes. 36 Where structured referral exists, uptake improves, but surgical access itself remains fragile. Some hospitals lacked their own surgical teams and depended on episodic visiting missions. 41

Only one of these qualitative studies examined lived HRQoL domains directly. Patel and Ross, 42 interviewing South African adults with surgically repaired CL/P, found that participants were greatly affected by negative societal perceptions and prevailing cultural myths and recalled childhood teasing and differential treatment within the family, yet reported broadly satisfactory outcomes in communication, education, and employment, and felt their marital and dating prospects were largely unaffected. This is the closest SSA analogue to the multidimensional constructs that instruments such as CLEFT-Q attempt to measure, and its coexistence of resilience with residual social wounding illustrates why a single generic score cannot represent the SSA cleft experience.

In the continued absence of culturally validated quantitative instruments, this qualitative corpus is the most direct available source of candidate items and domains for an SSA-specific measure. It supplies concrete, empirically grounded content — supernatural and moral causal attributions, maternal blame and self-concealment, community labelling, marital dissolution, and the traditional/faith-healer care pathway — that HIC-derived PROMs do not contain. Any SSA-specific instrument should therefore be co-produced from this evidence through systematic item generation and cognitive interviewing with patients, caregivers, and communities, rather than by translating existing HIC tools whose item content presupposes a secular, individualised model of facial difference.

A parallel priority is comparability. While valid, culture- and language-specific measures are essential for capturing context-bound constructs such as stigma, outcomes in SSA cannot be interpreted in isolation: if SSA studies use one set of instruments and HIC studies another, cross-regional comparison of cleft outcomes becomes impossible. The same problem arises when contextualising cleft populations against general (non-cleft) populations, which requires a shared metric. We therefore recommend a two-tier measurement strategy. A common, internationally used core, anchored on a preference-based generic utility measure (for example the EQ-5D, with a locally valued value set) and a widely adopted condition-specific PROM (such as the CLEFT-Q), should be retained to enable benchmarking across regions and against general-population norms. This core should then be complemented by an SSA-specific module that captures stigma, supernatural attribution, and the structural barriers identified here. Aligning on such a core, while investing in region-specific content, would allow the field to compare like with like without erasing the contextual determinants that make SSA cleft care distinctive.

The coexistence of two findings, that surgery significantly improves HRQoL28,29 while access remains severely restricted to NGO-delivered care in all included studies, encapsulates the fundamental equity problem in SSA cleft care. The near-universal unmet need for psychological support (96.6%) and speech therapy (86.5%; Yakubu et al. 27 ) confirm that surgery is delivered largely in isolation from the comprehensive multidisciplinary model that characterises HIC standard of care. 15 This structural reality constrains achievable HRQoL outcomes in ways that measurement alone cannot address, and highlights that expanding access to comprehensive, multidisciplinary cleft care is as important as improving the instruments used to assess it.

4.2. Limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings, most of which reflect the state of the evidence base rather than the conduct of the review. First, only twelve studies met the eligibility criteria; this small and geographically uneven evidence base, with more than half of the studies originating from Nigeria, limits the regional representativeness and generalisability of the synthesis, and is itself a principal finding of this review. Second, the search was conducted across four databases (PubMed, CINAHL, AJOL, and Google Scholar); although these were selected to maximise coverage of the African and health-sciences literature and the strategy was peer-reviewed using PRESS, additional relevant studies indexed elsewhere may have been missed. Grey literature, which may include programme-level outcome reports, was not incorporated. Third, meta-analysis was not feasible because of substantial heterogeneity in study design, instruments, and outcome metrics; the narrative synthesis was conducted using the SWiM guideline, and quantitative pooling would have produced statistically uninterpretable estimates. Fourth, two studies7,26 were judged to be at high risk of bias and one enrolled only five participants; these were retained in the narrative synthesis for completeness and transparency, but their findings were interpreted with corresponding caution and were not permitted to drive any synthesis conclusion. Several other studies also had modest sample sizes, reflecting the small, NGO-dependent cleft cohorts available in the region. These limitations reinforce, rather than undermine, the central conclusion that the SSA evidence base for CL/P HRQoL and utility measurement is nascent and in need of methodologically rigorous, adequately powered, and culturally grounded primary studies.

5. Conclusions

The evidence base for HRQoL and utility measurement in CL/P care in SSA is nascent, mostly geographically concentrated in Nigeria, dominated by generic and psychiatric screening tools deployed without formal cultural adaptation, and deficient in utility data appropriate for cost-effectiveness analysis. Chung’s study is the only direct utility study in this setting. 18 It shows the feasibility of rigorous in-person elicitation in SSA and the clinical importance of doing so, confirming that HIC-derived utility weights systematically understate the value of cleft surgery in low-resource settings. Any future instrument that is developed should incorporate the context of Stigma, socioeconomic deprivation, delayed surgical presentation, and the near-universal absence of adjunctive care that defines the SSA CL/P HRQoL contexts in its structural underpinnings.

These findings suggest three key recommendations. The CLEFT-Q and VELO are the widely used condition-specific PROMs in this review. It is necessary for them to undergo a formal cultural adaptation and psychometric validation in SSA populations. This process can begin with Amharic, Hausa, Arabic, Luganda, French and Twi, using the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) forward–backward translation framework and cognitive debriefing before any large-scale deployment. To be able to derive sub-regional data for HTA of cleft programmes, there should be at least one SSA-specific direct utility study using VAS, TTO, and SG, modelled on Chung et al. 18 Future studies on this subject should employ dyads of both patients and caregivers or patients/caregivers and practitioners. A longitudinal approach that follows up milestones covered by surgery should be employed. With this approach, stigma should be operationalised and measured directly as a key determinant of HRQoL, rather than treated as a post hoc observation. Future instruments should also draw explicitly on the existing qualitative literature from SSA to ground their content in the lived experience of affected individuals and families.
