Prevalence of pneumococcal carriage and risk factors for pneumonia and carriage among under-5 children in Malaysia: findings from the MY-Pneumo study
Authors: Hoomashini Gunasegaran, Nurul Hanis Ramzi, Andrew Chun Hoong Tan, Nur Alia Johari, Anna Marie Nathan, Norhayati Aida Sulaiman, Cindy Shuan Ju Teh, Mohamad Ikram Ilias, Zakuan Zainy Deris, Siti Nur Haidar Hazlan, Nur Syafiqah Mohamad Nasir, Asrar Abu Bakar, Muhd Alwi Muhd Helmi, Wan Khairunnisa Wan Juhari, Norhidayah Kamarudin, Chun Wie Chong, David W. Cleary, Stuart C. Clarke, Lokman Hakim Sulaiman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaediatric pneumonia remains a significant public health concern globally, and the risk factors for pneumonia and carriage vary across different populations. The accuracy of pneumococcal urinary antigen test (PUAT) remains unclear. We aimed to determine the prevalence of pneumococcal carriage, and the risk factors associated with pneumonia and carriage among children aged under 5 years in Peninsular Malaysia, and to evaluate the performance of the PUAT at detecting pneumococcal carriage.Materials and methodsA total of 500 cases of pneumonia and 500 age-matched controls at three tertiary hospitals across three states in Peninsular Malaysia were recruited from October 2021 to August 2023. Nasopharyngeal swabs (NPS) were taken to test for Streptococcus pneumoniae (Spn) using culture and multiplex-PCR while urine samples were collected for PUAT with BinaxNOW®. Logistic regression analyses were fitted for paediatric pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage risk factors.ResultsThe overall prevalence of nasopharyngeal pneumococcal carriage was 15.4% (n = 154) based on culture and optochin susceptibility, with identical rates observed among children with pneumonia and healthy controls. PUAT positivity was higher among pneumonia cases (11.8%, 59/500) than the controls (8.8%, 44/500) but was not statistically significant (p = 0.119). The carriage prevalence was significantly higher in the east coast (19.5%) compared to the central west coast (9.5%) (p < 0.001). Significant predictors of pneumonia were having siblings aged under 5 years (p = 0.002), pre-existing illness (p < 0.001), exposure to indoor smoking (p = 0.01), overcrowding (p = 0.012), underweight (p = 0.001) and obesity (p = 0.023). The predictors of pneumococcal carriage were recruiting sites and pneumococcal vaccination status. Compared to culture and PCR, the sensitivity and specificity of BinaxNOW® to detect Spn in urine were similar at about 31% and 93%, respectively.ConclusionPneumococcal carriage prevalence was similar in pneumonia cases and healthy controls, and the true burden of pneumococcal pneumonia in Malaysia remains unknown. Our findings identified risk factors for childhood pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage that are consistent with those reported elsewhere. The BinaxNOW® showed low sensitivity and moderate specificity indicating its limited utility as a standalone diagnostic tool for Spn detection in clinical settings. A focus should be made to recognise risk profiles to support early identification, timely intervention, and prevention strategies for respiratory infections in vulnerable paediatric populations. There is a need for continued Spn carriage surveillance and ongoing research to improve diagnostics to guide public health strategies for the prevention of pneumococcal infections.

Introduction

Paediatric pneumonia remains a significant global health challenge across different income-level settings. According to WHO, it is the single largest cause of death in children worldwide [1]. In Malaysia, diseases of the respiratory system were the second largest cause of admission to Ministry of Health (MoH) hospitals in 2022, accounting for 13.4% of the total admission [2]. In 2023, pneumonia superseded ischaemic heart disease as the commonest cause of death for all ages in Malaysia for the first time in two decades (except in 2021 when COVID-19 was the principal cause), whereas for under-5 children, pneumonia (all causes) was the third principal cause of death [3].

A systematic analysis from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 estimated that Streptococcus pneumoniae (Spn) was the most common pathogen responsible for the highest burden and death of childhood lower respiratory infections [4]. The pneumococcus is one of the most common organisms causing paediatric pneumonia in Malaysia and other South-East Asia countries [5]. Despite the introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs), post-implementation surveillance in Malaysia is limited. This gap limits our ability to accurately predict future disease burden, monitor shifts in serotype distribution, and assess the broader effects of herd immunity. Strengthening such surveillance is essential for informing public health strategies and guiding the development of vaccines that also target colonising serotypes.

Diagnosis of pneumococcal pneumonia is challenging. The gold standard is to isolate the bacteria from sterile body fluid, or the lung tissue obtained from lung biopsy or bronchoscopy, but this is not practical and not routinely done in a clinical setting. Sputum Gram stain and culture is usually the first step but obtaining a good quality sputum specimen among children is extremely difficult. Furthermore, the fact that Spn is commensal in the nasopharynx and carriage rate among children can be up to 90% in certain population [6], isolation of the bacteria in sputum needs to be interpreted carefully. Bacterial blood culture is diagnostic but less than 10% of blood cultures are positive [7]. Malaysia lacks literature on the sensitivity and specificity of rapid tests to detect Spn in urine from children. An increased prevalence of Spn colonisers also raises public health concerns including antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Non-invasive diagnostic tools, such as the PUAT offer a potential solution. Although the rapid immunochromatographic test (BinaxNOW®) has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (US-FDA) since 2003 as a bedside test to detect Spn antigen in urine samples, a recent review suggested that it may not be useful and does not alter patient management [8]. While the PUAT has demonstrated good performance in adults, its accuracy in young children has not been well established, particularly due to higher nasopharyngeal carriage rates [9, 10]. Identifying sensitivity and specificity of UAT in detecting pneumococcus leads to possibilities of simple and cost-effective sample collection & rapid diagnosis which facilitates timely and appropriate antibiotic initiation.

Several studies have explored risk factors for paediatric pneumonia in Malaysia, with findings highlighting the influence of socioeconomic factors, breastfeeding practices, antibiotic use, and exposure to parental smoking [11, 12]. Globally, risk factors for pneumonia are poverty, underweight, malnutrition, limited access to healthcare, widespread AMR and incomplete immunisation are among the most significant contributors to the burden and mortality of pneumococcal disease in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) [13, 14].

The 10-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV10) was added to Malaysia’s National Immunisation Programme (NIP) in 2020 using a 2 + 1 schedule (doses at 4, 6, and 15 months) [15]. The PCV acceptance rate for newborns in Malaysia was more than 90%, with most children receiving both the first and second doses from January to September 2021 [16]. In 2023, the MoH Malaysia administered PCV13 under the NIP. According to recent data, the uptake rates for the first, second, and third doses of PCV13 under the NIP in 2024 were 96.42%, 97.81%, and 99.04%, respectively [17].

However, little is known about the actual burden of pneumococcal infection, especially among children. Unlike other childhood vaccine preventable diseases, pneumococcal infection has yet to be made notifiable under Schedule 1 of the Malaysian’s Infectious Disease Act 1988. A comprehensive understanding of the burden of pneumonia, Spn carriage and associated risk factors among children in the post-PCV era in Malaysia is needed. Thus, we conducted a case–control study to understand better regarding pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage among under-5 children in Malaysia (MY-Pneumo) [18]. This study, the largest study of its kind in Malaysia to date, aimed to determine the risk factors associated with pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage rates. In addition, the sensitivity and specificity of BinaxNOW® were also evaluated to detect pneumococcal carriage.

Materials and methods

Based on this, a total of 500 children with confirmed pneumonia and 500 healthy controls were recruited for this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for cases were clinically diagnosed pneumonia of paediatrics five years and below supported by chest x-ray findings, admitted as a hospital inpatient at either sentinel sites, and obtained parental or guardian consent. The exclusion criteria for cases were population with history of antibiotic administration and nasal surgery in a month prior to sampling.

The inclusion criteria for controls were healthy children aged five years and below with parental or guardian consent to participate. The exclusion criteria were children with history of antibiotic administration or nasal surgery a month prior to sampling, significant comorbidities including known case of chronic respiratory diseases (including asthma), cardiac condition and had history of high-grade fever within 72 h pre-selection. All cases and controls are being matched using an age range of ± 6 months.

Study population

Between October 2021 and August 2023, the MY-Pneumo study recruited 1000 children under-5 comprising of 500 cases of pneumonia and 500 healthy age-matched children at Hospital Universiti Sains Malaysia (HUSM), Kota Bharu, Kelantan; Sultan Ahmad Shah Medical Centre International Islamic University Malaysia (IIUM), Kuantan, Pahang; and University Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC), Kuala Lumpur. HUSM and IIUM are on the east coast, and UMMC is on the central west coast of Peninsular Malaysia. Study details and ethics statements have been outlined in detail and published elsewhere [18].

Pneumonia cases were defined as children clinically diagnosed with pneumonia and admitted as inpatients, with radiological confirmation on chest X-ray (CXR) according to WHO guidelines [20]. Controls were healthy, age-matched children (within six months of age) with no signs of respiratory illness, recruited from outpatient departments of the same sentinel hospitals. All participants were tested for Spn via culture sensitivity, PCR and BinaxNOW® UAT.

Primary data on socio-demographic characteristics, environmental, home-related factors, child-related factors and pre-existing illness were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research assistants with the parents of the children. The radiological findings, birth weight, height at birth, gestational term, current weight and height and other clinical risk factors related to pneumonia were retrieved from the medical records. Nutritional status (Body Mass Index, BMI) followed WHO Child Growth Standard 2006: < fifth percentile - underweight; fifth percentile to < 85th percentile - normal weight, ≥ 85th percentile - overweight/obese [21]. Informed consent was obtained prior to the collection of clinical samples, including nasopharyngeal swabs (NPS) and urine samples.

Microbiology and molecular testing

We tested NPS samples for pneumococci using conventional culture and optochin susceptibility, and capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis gene A (cpsA) multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as previously described [18]. Urine samples were subjected to PUAT using BinaxNOW® (Abbott Inc, Portland, Maine). NPS and urine samples were collected within 48 h of admission (prior to antibiotic administration if any) and recruitment among control subjects. Blood cultures were conducted at the discretion of the hospital’s clinical team. Pneumococcal carriage was defined by a positive result on optochin susceptibility testing, a gold-standard and reliable method for Spn identification [22].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 29 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics included means ± standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Pearson’s chi-square test and independent Student t-test were used to test differences in proportion and mean, respectively. Two-sided statistical tests are considered significant at p-values less than or equal to 0.05. Binary logistic regression was used to model the relationship between significant risk factors associated with pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage. For pneumococcal carriage risk factor analysis, data from both pneumonia cases and controls were grouped to increase statistical power and to enable an inclusive assessment of factors associated with nasopharyngeal colonisation in a broader paediatric population, regardless of clinical presentation. Pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage status were used as main outcomes separately in the regression modelling to assess the risk for pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage independently. For each outcome, variables that were significantly associated with the univariate analysis were entered into the logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. The regression models were adjusted for potential confounders (recruiting sites, gender, and ethnicity) to ensure accurate estimation of the independent effects of the predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for significant variables. Sensitivity, specificity, positive (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of BinaxNOW® to detect Spn carriage were calculated using the binomial exact method using optochin susceptibility and PCR as the gold standard methods.

Results

Subject recruitment

A total of 400 subjects were recruited from HUSM and UMMC, respectively, and 200 from IIUM, with an equal number of pneumonia cases and control in each recruiting site. NPS and urine samples were collected from all subjects, while blood cultures (n = 248/500; 49.6%) were obtained exclusively from pneumonia cases. All blood samples tested negative for bacterial growth. No invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) patient was reported at the recruiting sites throughout the duration of the study.

Prevalence and risk factors of Pneumococcal carriage

The overall prevalence of nasopharyngeal pneumococcal carriage by culture and optochin susceptibility in this study was 15.4% (n = 154), with an equal prevalence of children with pneumonia and in controls (n = 77, 15.4% respectively). By PCR, the overall prevalence was slightly lower (n = 151, 15.1%) and was statistically different from culture (χ²=186.06, p < 0.001). Pneumococcal prevalence was higher in the east coast region (HUSM and IIUM) (n = 116, 19.3%) compared to UMMC (n = 38, 9.5%) in the central west coast region (χ²= 17.06, p < 0.001). The pneumococcal prevalence was significantly different between the recruiting sites, with HUSM having the highest prevalence (22.3%) followed by IIUM (13.5%) and UMMC (9.5%) (χ²= 25.65, p < 0.001).

Table 4 shows the risk factors associated with pneumococcal carriage for both pneumonia cases and controls. Subjects with pneumococcal carriage had a significantly higher mean household size (t = 2.34, p = 0.019). Factors that were significantly associated with pneumococcal carriage were living in a landed house (residential properties where the owner has the title to the land beneath and surrounding the house) and having received PCV.

Based on regression analysis adjusted for age and other potential confounders (Table 5), the predictors of positive carriage were PCV status (aOR 1.88, 95% CI 1.26–2.80, p = 0.002) and recruitment sites (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.76, p < 0.001). The sentinel recruitment site of HUSM in Kota Bharu, Kelantan remained as a significant predictor (aOR 2.79, 95% CI: 1.59–4.91, p < 0.001) after adjustment for age, suggesting a persistent and independent effect of geographic disparities on carriage. The influence of PCV on pneumococcal carriage was particularly strong in children who received age-appropriate doses, with the second dose at 6 months (aOR 2.18, 95% CI: 1.36–3.50, p = 0.001) and the third dose at 15 months (aOR 1.92, 95% CI: 1.21–3.07, p = 0.006).

Discussion

The MY-Pneumo project was a multi-centre case-control study investigating the prevalence of pneumococcal carriage and the risk factors for pneumonia and carriage in young children to support future carriage surveillance studies and monitor PCV implementation in Malaysia. Information on Spn carriage among children in Malaysia is scarce [23]. In 1988, Cheong et al. reported a carriage rate of 17% among children with acute respiratory infection [24]. Ten years later, Malik et al. reported a carriage rate of 10% among pre-school children [25]. To our knowledge this is the largest carriage study that has been carried out among under-5 children in Malaysia in recent years. Two recent studies involved a small number of under-5 children among indigenous communities in Terengganu and Sarawak with carriage rates of 57.1% (4/7) and 50% (6/12) respectively [26, 27]. After 26 years, we report a Spn carriage rate of 15.4% among under-5 children in Peninsular Malaysia. Our rate is much lower than that reported in this region, such as 30.4% in China [28], 40.6% in Thailand [29], 33–45% in the Philippines [30] and 30.9–87.6% in Indonesia [31]. However, it was similar to the rate of 14.1% reported in Singapore [32].

The pneumonia mortality rate among under-5 children in Kelantan and Pahang was 4% and 5.2%, respectively [33]. As Kelantan and Pahang are more rural regions than the urbanised Klang Valley which recorded under-5 mortality of 1.6%, there are potential disparities in the level of healthcare access and poor health-seeking behaviours among the caregivers of the children. Environmental factors such as overcrowding, having siblings with age under five, and exposure to tobacco smoke further contribute to the vulnerability of children to pneumonia in this study. Additionally, being overweight and having existing comorbidities are amongst the clinical risk predictors for paediatric pneumonia in our study. This aligns with existing literature that shows children with higher BMI are at increased risk of respiratory infections including pneumonia [34, 35]. Indeed, childhood obesity is a major issue in Malaysia where the prevalence of childhood overweight, obesity, and excess weight in Malaysia doubled over 26 years between 1996 and 2022 [36]. The association between overweight/obesity and pneumonia may be related to underlying dysregulated immune response due to alterations caused by adaptive and innate immunity [37], inflammatory processes [38], and factors influencing the respiratory system [39]. The presence of comorbidities, particularly chronic respiratory conditions such as asthma or bronchopulmonary dysplasia, has been consistently linked to increased pneumonia risk in children [40]. Our findings reinforce the complex interplay of biological and environmental factors in the development of paediatric pneumonia, underscoring the need for comprehensive approaches to prevention and management that address both individual and population-level risk factors.

A higher mean household size was shown to be associated with an increased risk of both pneumonia and pneumococcal carriage among under-5 children in this study. Similar findings have been reported in the South-East Asia region and LMICs, where overcrowding has been identified as a significant risk factor for pneumonia [10], and pneumococcal carriage [41]. We also found that PCV status was a significant predictor of Spn carriage. In the post-PCV era, serotype replacement was observed across various settings, driven by an increase in non-vaccine type carriage rates among vaccinated children [42]. A systematic review identified a significant reduction in hospital admission rates due to all-cause, severe, radiologically confirmed, and bacteraemic pneumococcal pneumonia in children, supporting the ongoing use of PCV10 and PCV13 [43]. A randomised controlled trial in Vietnam demonstrated that a two-dose PCV10 schedule significantly reduced vaccine-type carriage in infants compared to unvaccinated controls [44]. Similarly, long-term follow-up in Brazil found substantial reductions in vaccine-type colonisation among children following the introduction of PCV10, although overall pneumococcal carriage showed slight increases, possibly due to serotype replacement [45]. Even after vaccination with PCV10 or PCV13, children living in a high-risk setting such as Papua New Guinea experience persistent pneumococcal colonisation [46]. These findings highlight the role of PCV not only in preventing invasive disease but also in altering pneumococcal ecology at the population level, particularly in reducing transmission of vaccine-included serotypes. Although pneumococcal serotyping was performed using PCR, serotype-specific data are not presented in this analysis, as they will be reported in a subsequent study upon completion of the dataset. Thus this limits the interpretation of vaccine impact and the potential for serotype replacement.

The dynamics of Spn exhibit significant variation and changes following vaccination targeting a subset of serotypes, influenced by underlying transmission patterns, as well as vaccine efficacy and coverage across host communities [47]. In addition to pathogen-related factors such as virulence, host-related factors like immune status and genetic background and environmental influences, a potential fourth factor of relevance is the interplay between the microbiota and host characteristics [47]. The commensal bacterial community that “hosts” these potential pathogens may play a crucial role in the persistence of Spn colonisation after the introduction of PCVs. Advanced infection models and a substantial amount of data are required to develop this concept further and understand the effect of carriage persistence in the at-risk populations [48].

We also investigated the utility of BinaxNOW® in diagnosing pneumococcal pneumonia and in detecting Spn colonisation. BinaxNOW® positivity was not significantly different between the two groups. Nevertheless, without appropriate diagnostic method to determine the cause of pneumonia to compare, the performance of BinaxNOW® to detect Spn pneumonia was not conclusive. Generally, various studies have shown that BinaxNOW® has inadequate specificity in diagnosing pneumococcal pneumonia in children [7]. However, the sensitivity of BinaxNOW® to detect colonisation was also not so promising, both when comparing to the detection of viable Spn or its DNA. This contrasts with a previous study, which reported a sensitivity and specificity of 88% and 95%, respectively, for NPS samples [49]. In other studies, BinaxNOW® was positive in 44–66.5% of pneumococcal carriage confirmed urine samples [50, 51]. Differences in the performance could be due to differences in study design, sample size and other factors. More comprehensive studies are needed to confirm the utility of BinaxNOW® to detect pneumococcal carriage.

A meta-analysis on paediatric community-acquired pneumonia reported that only 9.89% of blood cultures from hospitalised children with severe pneumonia were positive, with a high false-positive rate and non-existence of validated clinical prediction criteria to define severity of paediatric pneumonia [52]. As the blood samples taken from pneumonia cases from this study were all negative, this aligns with the British Thoracic Society and Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines, which advocate for the selective use of blood cultures in patients with severe or complicated pneumonia [53, 54].

The observational MY-Pneumo study is subject to several potential biases. Data obtained through parent or caregiver reported questionnaires are subject to recall bias that may affect the accuracy of exposure assessment. The causality between identified risk factors and pneumococcal outcomes cannot be established. Additionally, not all possible determinants are included nor controlled for and thus could be liable for residual confounders. Socio-economic categories were unidentified in this study. Comorbidities influencing pneumococcal infection were not explored. Finally, the current study does not assess vaccine-type versus non-vaccine-type carriage or address potential implications of serotype replacement, which may limit interpretation of vaccine impact. Despite these limitations, the multi-centre case-control framework from this study allowed for the identification of distinct risk factors for pneumococcal pneumonia and carriage. It also provides crucial baseline data for evaluating the impact of the recently introduced PCV vaccination program for children and older adults in Malaysia and informing national immunisation strategies. The MoH Malaysia is strongly recommended to include pneumococcal infections (all types) into Schedule 1 of the Infectious Disease Act 1988 as an objective mean to measure the impact of the vaccination programme. A coordinated serotype surveillance network representing all regions of the country will be crucial to determine the most appropriate vaccines to use in the national programme.

Conclusions

In conclusion, through the largest case-control study to date on paediatric pneumococcal pneumonia and carriage in Malaysia, we identified independent risk factors associated with paediatric pneumonia (living with under-five siblings, underlying illnesses, indoor tobacco exposures, overcrowding, and abnormal BMI) and carriage (eastern coast Peninsular region and PCV status). Ongoing surveillance is essential to assess the evolving impact of PCVs on both carriage and disease, ensuring sustained vaccine effectiveness and informing public health vaccine strategies. In our study, PUAT did not show good sensitivity to detect Spn carriage and does not appear useful for diagnosis of pneumococcal pneumonia among children. The study highlights the continuing burden of pneumococcal carriage and pneumonia in children aged under 5 years in the post-PCV era. It underscores the need for enhanced vaccine surveillance, investment in higher-valency pneumococcal vaccines, and the implementation of community-level interventions targeting modifiable risk factors such as household crowding and tobacco smoke exposure.
