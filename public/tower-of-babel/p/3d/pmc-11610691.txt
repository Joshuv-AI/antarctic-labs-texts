Exploring the World of Curcumin: Photophysics, Photochemistry, and Applications in Nanoscience and Biology
Authors: Meghna Ghosh, Nilmoni Sarkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurcumin is a bright yellow naturally occurring polyphenol which is the principal component of turmeric. It is used as herbal supplement, cosmetics ingredient, and food coloring agent. Over the years, the therapeutic properties of the natural product curcumin have gone unexploited but not unnoticed. Curcumin cannot be employed as a drug due to limitations such as low aqueous solubility and limited bioavailability. Many attempts have been made to overcome these limitations by confining the drug in various confined media to enhance its bioavailability. The biomolecule is emissive and undergoes fundamental excited state processes such as solvation dynamics and excited state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT). Curcumin based biomaterials and nanomaterials are also a fast advancing field where curcumin is an intrinsic component necessary for formation of these materials and no longer added as an external free drug. In this review, we will summarize the recent research on the photophysical and photochemical properties of curcumin and its excited state dynamics in various bio‐mimicking systems. At the same time we wish to also incorporate the various applications of curcumin, especially in biology. Lastly due to the growing importance of materials science, we will briefly discuss some recent advances on curcumin based biomaterials and nanomaterials. We believe such a compilation of recent research surrounding curcumin will provide an overall understanding of its potentialities in different areas.

A Brief Introduction of Curcumin: Chemical Properties and Importance

A class of polyphenolic compounds known as curcuminoids may be isolated from turmeric, the highly common spice and food coloring agent. Turmeric is in turn, extracted from the roots of Curcuma longa, a plant belonging to the family zingeberacea. [1] The major curcuminoid (~77 %) extracted from turmeric is curcumin. [2] Demethoxycurcumin (17 %), bisdemethoxycurcumin (6 %), and cyclocurcumin are other curcuminoids also obtained in lesser amounts. [2] The chemical structures of various curcuminoids, including curcumin is given in Figure 1. In 1913, the chemical structure of curcumin was identified as consisting of two feruloyl units linked by methylene, leading to curcumin being also known as diferuloylmethane. [3] According to IUPAC nomenclature, the name given to curcumin is (1E, 6E)‐1 ,7‐bis(4‐hydroxy‐3‐methoxy phenyl)‐1,6‐heptadiene‐3,5‐dione. This scientific name implies that curcumin is a β‐diketone. However, the β‐diketo moiety is involved in tautomerism resulting in three taumeric forms of curcumin i. e. the β‐diketo tautomer and two equivalent asymmetric keto‐enol tautomers. [4] Extensive studies using NMR spectroscopy have established that in solution, curcumin exists almost exclusively as keto‐enol tautomers. [4] Moreover, DFT studies have shown that the keto‐enol tautomer is more stable than the β‐diketone by 7.75 kcal/mol. [5] Since ancient times, the therapeutic properties of curcumin were known and used by people to treat different diseases such as common cold, inflammations, open wounds etc. In the 1970s, the various beneficial properties of curcumin were reported and the importance of curcumin was enhanced significantly by the recognition of its anticancer properties in the 1990s. [3] Today, curcumin is known for its anticancer, antiamyloid, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐Alzheimer's, anti‐cystic fibrosis, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and wound healing properties.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ] The excited state proton transfer process associated with curcumin is reported to be related to its antioxidant properties. Widespread use of curcumin as a potential medicine is limited due to poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability. Furthermore, curcumin degrades rapidly in buffer solutions under neutral‐basic conditions at 37 °C, forming trans‐6‐(4′‐hydroxy‐Y‐methoxyphenyl)‐2,4‐dioxo‐5‐hexenal (major degradation product), vanillin, ferulic acid, and feruloyl methane.[ 12 , 13 ] Enhancing the bioavailability of curcumin is the need of the hour. We have in most cases, focussed on research publications from the year 2010 onwards. This review has been divided into three sections. In the first section we have discussed about the photophysical and photochemical properties of curcumin and different ways to modulate the same. In the next part, we have illustrated the various strategies that have been utilized to stabilize curcumin with the help of bio‐mimicking systems. Lastly, we have discussed some recent studies pertaining to applications of curcumin containing biomaterials and nanomaterials in living systems.

Photophysical and Photochemical Properties of Curcumin and its Modulation

Each of the two aromatic rings of curcumin, possess one phenolic functionality and a methoxy functionality ortho to the phenolic moiety. Moreover, extended conjugation between the two aryl systems through the β‐diketo moiety results in interesting optical properties for curcumin. Curcumin has a broad absorption spectrum with maximum at ~420 nm in polar solvents which has been assigned to the ππ* transition. [14] In non‐polar solvents, keto‐enol tautomerism occurs such that both tautomeric forms are present. However, the observed absorption spectrum of curcumin in polar solvents is red‐shifted, and most likely represents the curcumin keto‐enol tautomer which is predominantly present. The cis β‐diketo tautomer absorption is predicted to be significantly blue‐shifted and occurs in the near ultraviolet region due to the twisted distorted geometry of the molecule due to the repulsion of the oxygen lone pairs. [15] The red‐shifted absorption of cis keto‐enol tautomer may also be justified on the basis of strong intramolecular hydrogen bonding leading to planer geometry resulting in extended π‐conjugation.

The curcumin emission spectrum exhibited a Stokes shift of 2000–6000 cm−1 depending on the nature of the solvent. [14] The emission spectrum of curcumin has a higher solvent dependence compared to absorption spectrum which may be seen from the observed values of Stokes shift of curcumin emission in different solvents. While, the strong ππ* absorption band is at ~410–430 nm for most solvents, the emission maximum varies greatly. The curcumin emission maximum occurs between 494 and 538 nm for aprotic solvents, whereas a larger Stokes’ shift is observed in solvents capable of hydrogen bonding resulting in the curcumin emission maximum to be red‐shifted to 535–560 nm. On the other hand, in the non‐polar solvent cyclohexane, there exists two curcumin emission maxima i. e. 446 and 470 nm while in non‐polar aromatic solvents like toluene, the same occurs at 460–464 nm. It was further reported that the spectral shifts were due to the influence of the proton transfer reactions (both intramolecular and intermolecular) on the curcumin excited state. [14] The temperature and acid effects on curcumin absorbance and fluorescence was investigated by Huppert and co‐workers.[ 16 , 17 ] In the presence of acid in trifluoroethanol solvent, the fluorescence of neutral curcumin (ROH) was quenched and new emission band at 620 nm of protonated curcumin (ROH2 +) was observed. [16] Similarly in the absorption spectrum, a new absorption band at 520 nm due to ROH2 + appeared. [16] The temperature dependence study of curcumin excited state dynamics in the temperature range of 84 K–330 K was carried out by the same group. [17] The temperature dependence studies were done in two glass forming solvent ethanol and 1‐propanol. As in other conventional fluorophores, decrease in temperature reduced the non‐radiative deactivation of the S1 excited state of curcumin. In the steady‐state emission spectra of curcumin, vibronic substructure was present within the spectra for temperatures below 160 K whereas, for temperatures higher than 160 K, the spectra tended to be broad and structureless. Furthermore, the emission maximum was also red‐shifted with increasing temperature. For the time‐resolved emission spectra of curcumin, red‐shift of emission maxima was observed with increasing delay time, such that a net band shift of 1800 cm−1 was observed for the time interval 100 fs to 1 ns.

Palit and co‐workers utilized the fluorescence upconversion and transient absorption techniques to study the excited state dynamics of the S1 state of curcumin in fifteen different solvents. [15] In non‐polar solvents, curcumin predominately existed in the cis keto‐enol form where completion of reversible excited state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT) in the S1 state occurred within a few hundred femtoseconds. The presence of a six membered hydrogen bonded chelate ring was responsible for the low energy barrier, reversible reaction and ultrafast timescale of the process. Consequently, the fluorescence radiative lifetime of curcumin in the non‐polar solvents was short. The excited state dynamics of curcumin was evaluated in the non‐polar solvent 1,4‐dioxane with the help of transient absorption spectroscopy. The transient spectrum recorded at 0.2 ps delay showed one negative absorption band due to stimulated emission at 470–500 nm and one broad positive band related to excited state absorption in the region 500–830 nm. At higher delay time, both the bands were found to exhibit multiexponential decay having 3–4 components. The three average lifetime components of 1.6 ps, 44 ps, and 325 ps were observed where the fast 1.6 ps component was assigned to solvent reorganization. On the other hand, 44 ps and 325 ps components were due to cis keto‐enol and trans β‐diketo curcumin conformers. Moreover the symmetrical nature of the molecule induced intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) character to the S1 state of curcumin where transfer of charge occurred from the 4‐hydroxy‐3‐methoxyphenyl functionality to the dicarbonyl functional group by means of the extended conjugation of the π bonds. [15] Photoexcitation of curcumin led to a large change in dipole moment of Δμ ∼6.1 D. Thus, solvent reorientation and reorganization of the solvent H‐bonding network around the excited molecule highly influenced the excited state dynamics of curcumin. Hence, the ultrafast dynamics of curcumin was associated with different excited state processes such as solvation dynamics, ESIPT and ICT. The timescale of these processes were significantly different. For curcumin, solvation dynamics occurred on the ultrafast timescale, such that after photoexcitation, observed emission of curcumin was from the solvated/relaxed ICT like S1 state. When studying the retinal photoisomerization in Channelrhodopsin‐2 (ChR2), Marazzi and co‐workers predicted partial charge transfer character in the excited states S1 and S2 associated with the hydrogen bonding interactions in protonated retinal Schiff's base (RSBH+). [18] For this the authors considered the model involving hydrogen bonding between RSBH+ and water molecule, where the vertical transition from S0 to S2 resulted in major positive charge transfer from Schiff base functionality to the β‐ionone ring implying S2 to be a charge transfer state. On the other hand, for visual rhodopsin (Rh) and bacteriorhodopsin (bR), the charge transfer state was reported to be S1. Adhikary et al. elucidated the excited state curcumin photophysics in methanol, ethylene glycol and chloroform. [19] The time constants 12 ± 2 ps and 70 ± 10 ps were obtained for the biexponential time‐resolved decay of curcumin in methanol. In deuterated methanol, the slow component showed considerable isotope effect and was enhanced to 120 ± 20 ps. Curcumin was kept in deuterated methanol for 48 h to allow H/D exchange. When such equilibration time was not given, isotope effect was not observed. Therefore the slow component was concluded to be due to ESIPT and the fast component was attributed to solvation dynamics. Moreover, in polar aprotic solvent chloroform where H‐bonding between solvent and curcumin was not possible, single exponential fast decay was observed and the ultrafast solvation was beyond the time resolution of the instrument. The authors also established that the rotational anisotropy of curcumin was heavily dependent on the solvent viscosity. [19] The experimentally obtained value for time‐resolved rotational anisotropy of curcumin in the bulk solvents methanol and ethylene glycol was 200 ps and 5080 ps respectively. Furthermore, the theoretically calculated rotational anisotropy from the Debye‐Stokes‐Einstein equation was 375 ps for methanol, and 10200 ps for ethylene glycol. Although the theoretical values were somewhat overestimated, in both the cases, the same trend was observed. The results were rationalized on the basis of solvent viscosity (η) where slower rotational anisotropy of curcumin was seen in more viscous solvent ethylene glycol (η=16.1 cP at 20 °C) compared to that in methanol (η=0.59 cP at 20 °C).

Saini and Das studied the behavior of curcumin in binary mixtures of toluene and polar solvents (methanol, chloroform, acetonitrile, DMSO, HFP).[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] The ESIPT process in curcumin was associated with non‐radiative decay pathways. Hence, the short radiative lifetime of curcumin was usually attributed to highly efficient ESIPT. Hydrogen bonding nature as well as the polarity of the solvent played crucial roles in modulating the proton transfer dynamics of the chromophore. In polar solvents, intermolecular hydrogen bonding between curcumin and solvent molecules highly perturbed the ESIPT process. Here, toluene was not involved in hydrogen bonding with the molecule. But subsequent addition of increasing amounts of polar solvents with increasing mole fractions resulted in the loss of the vibronic structure in absorption and emission spectra of curcumin. Significant rise times were observed for alcohol mole fractions for decays taken near the red end of the emission spectra. For example, in the methanol fractions of 0.04, 0.07, and 0.57 there existed rise times of none, 170 ps, and 30 ps respectively. [20] The rise times were a function of the solvent mole fractions. The intermolecular hydrogen bonding between a curcumin molecule and several methanol solvent molecules were influenced by presence of toluene. Moreover, the time‐resolved area normalized emission spectra (TRANES) constructed for three different mole fractions, showed two isobestic points implying the presence of three different species. The three species of curcumin were rationalized to be an intramolecular hydrogen bonded structure (I), a structure having intermolecular hydrogen bonds with solvent (II) and a solvated structure evolved from I and II having optimized intermolecular hydrogen bonds with solvent.

Bhatia et al. investigated the effect of temperature and pH on curcumin in 5 % binary mixture of ethanol and phosphate buffer. [23] In acidic and neutral pH, increase in temperature disrupted the intramolecular hydrogen bonding in the cis keto‐enol conformer of curcumin and favored the formation of the β‐diketo conformer. However, since the process was reversible, the equilibrium shifted towards the keto‐enol form on lowering the temperature. In alkaline pH, curcumin underwent degradation.

Two phenolic protons and one enolic proton are present in curcumin such that a total of three acidic protons are there in the molecule. Recently, Trofimov and co‐workers studied the optical properties of curcumin (CurH3) in alkaline dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).[ 24 , 25 ] The different deprotonated states of curcumin (CurH2 −, CurH−2, Cur−3, Figure 2b) were obtained by varying the concentration of the base NaOH. [24] Each deprotonated form had distinct absorption band in the corresponding absorption spectrum and exhibited different color (Figure 2a) in DMSO. The triple deprotonated curcumin (Cur−3) was demonstrated to show significant emission having quantum yield 0.13. In a separate publication, the authors suggested that Cur−3 along with alkali metal (M+) cation formed the metal co‐ordinated complex [CurM]2− in alkaline DMSO to explain the observed results. [25] Hence, when Cur−3 was obtained using a high concentration of the base NaOH, both the reported steady‐state and time‐resolved properties of Cur−3 in alkaline DMSO, invariably was actually that of metal co‐ordinated complex [CurNa]2− as, Na+ ions were present in the medium. However, the authors did demonstrate the effect of different metal cations Li+, Na+, and K+ on spectral properties of Cur−3 in alkaline DMSO. [25] Upon formation of the complex, electron density of Cur−3 was transferred to the positively charged metal ion. Moreover, coordination of metal ion with keto‐enol oxygen atoms of Cur−3 enhanced the rigidity of the molecule resulting in higher quantum yield and lower susceptibility to auto‐oxidation in alkaline DMSO. Ghosh et al. with the help of time‐resolved fluorescence experiments and TD‐DFT studies, attempted to elucidate the excited state dynamics of the Cur−3. [26] This deprotonated state of curcumin was demonstrated to have higher excited state lifetime (Figure 2e), and associated with slower solvation dynamics. Moreover, the excited state properties were due to solvation dynamics and ICT. Furthermore, the favored deprotonation pathway involved deprotonation of the two phenolic protons first, followed by the deprotonation of the enolic proton in alkaline DMSO. This was contrary to previous literature reports where the lowest pKa of curcumin was related to the dissociation of the enolic proton. [5] Ghosh et al. also demonstrated the possibility to modulate the excited state photophysics of Cur−3 using non‐aqueous alkaline binary solvent mixtures.

The curcumin photobase and photoacid properties were studied by Huppert and co‐workers.[ 27 , 28 ] In the presence of weak acids, protonated curcumin (ROH2 +) was formed from curcumin (ROH) within the picosecond timescale, illustrating the photobase properties of curcumin. [27] Of the four bands observed in the transient pump‐probe spectrum, the absorption bands at ∼600 nm and ∼500 nm were assigned to ROH and ROH2 +, respectively, whereas emission bands at ∼540 nm and ∼620 nm were due to ROH and ROH2 +, respectively. A separate work from the same group, demonstrated the curcumin photoacid properties where, ESIPT from acidic group of curcumin (ROH) to acetate was observed in the presence of mild base (sodium acetate) in alcohol leading to formation of monodeprotonated RO−. [28] The authors proposed that curcumin undergoes two different proton transfer processes, in addition to solvation dynamics in the excited state in the presence of mild base. The ultrafast intramolecular proton transfer was the ESIPT whereas the second proton transfer occurred in a solvent cage to acetate anion. The second proton transfer was not only slower, but also deactivated the excited state by non‐radiative pathways resulting in the quenching of curcumin emission, and faster dynamics of curcumin in excited state.

The existence of curcumin triplet excited state and possibility of intersystem crossing (ISC) was demonstrated by Monari and co‐workers. [29] With the help of non‐adiabatic surface hopping dynamics, the authors unravelled the interplay of ESIPT and ISC in the rigid planar keto‐enol tautomer of curcumin. The ISC process of timescale 3.4 ps was the rate determining step to go from S1 (ππ*) singlet state to the T4 triplet state of curcumin. Furthermore, this process competed with the ultrafast curcumin ESIPT which occurred from the S1 singlet state. Likewise, the same group also showed that ISC was possible in other systems such as the biomolecule hypericin, thiophene dimer bithiophene, and thiophene trimer terthiophene.[ 30 , 31 ] Mai et al. observed ISC followed by photoinduced electron transfer when elucidating the ultrafast non‐adiabetic dynamics of rhenium based metal complex coupled to the tryptophan side chain of the protein azurin derived from Pseudomonas aeruginosa. [32] Another curcuminoid isolated from turmeric and having some biological properties is cyclocurcumin. This molecule is frequently studied to further improve our understanding of curcumin. [33] The steady‐state and time‐resolved spectroscopic measurements of Petrich and co‐workers demonstrated the sensitivity of cyclocurcumin excited state dynamics to temperature, solvent, and viscosity. [33] NMR spectroscopy of cyclocurcumin in deuterated solvents methanol‐d4 and acetone‐d6 revealed that in the ground state, the molecule existed in trans form about the C6−C7 bond. The authors also proposed trans‐to‐cis photoisomerization in cyclocurcumin excited state resulting in non‐radiative decay. Moreover, a twisted intermediate was formed during photoisomerization. The low cyclocurcumin average lifetime in methanol was due to the stabilizing effects of low viscosity polar protic solvents like methanol, on the twisted intermediate which promoted cyclocurcumin non‐radiative decay. Molecular modelling techniques established the ability of cyclocurcumin to function as a photoisomerizable switch. [34] The same group further showed that the cyclocurcumin excited states having lowest energy were 1(n,π*) and 1(π,π*) states. In polar solvents, 1(π,π*) was stabilized causing the flattening of the potential energy surface (PES), which in turn enhanced the accessibility of the S1/S0 conical intersection structures that favor photoisomerization. On the other hand, 1(n,π*) state was favored in non‐polar medium resulting in fluorescence as the dominant process.

Photophysics and Photochemistry of Curcumin in Bio‐Mimicking Systems

Despite having many therapeutic properties, curcumin is not widely used as a drug due to low solubility in water and limited bioavailability. Micelles of various kinds have been utilized to enhance its aqueous solubility. Petrich and workers elucidated the excited state dynamics of curcumin in cationic, neutral and anionic surfactant based micelles (Figure 3a–c). [35] The fluorescence decay of curcumin was biexponential where the slow component was attributed to ESIPT with the help of isotope experiments. Encapsulation of curcumin in cationic micelles was found to suppress its degradation by alkaline hydrolysis up to 90 %. Moreover, the same group further established that cationic surfactants were more effective than their anionic counterparts in stabilizing curcumin, especially at pH 9.2 where curcumin existed as monoanionic Cur−1.

Ghatak et al. suggested that the S1 excited state of curcumin possess ICT character in micelles composed of ionic liquid in water. [36] The ionic liquid 1‐butyl‐3‐methylimidazoliumoctyl sulfate (bmim Os) can form micelles in water having critical micelle concentration (CMC) 31–37 mM. The hydrophobic microenvironment within the micelles caused significant blue‐shift in the emission maximum of curcumin with the addition of ionic liquid in water. The time‐resolved studies showed that curcumin excited state lifetime increased from 103 to 158 ps upon addition of ionic liquid. Moreover, addition of the salt NaCl favored the intermolecular H‐bonding between curcumin and anion of ionic liquid which in turn retarded the ESIPT process. Moreover, the steady‐state fluorescence of curcumin was utilized to evaluate the cmc of ionic liquid based micelles in presence and absence of salt. On the other hand, higher temperature facilitated the non‐radiative decay pathways of curcumin resulting in decreased fluorescence intensity and shorter lifetime of curcumin. The enthalpy driven binding between curcumin and ionic liquid was due to not only H‐bonding but also the hydrophobic interaction between curcumin aromatic moieties and alkyl chain of ionic liquid. Both the determined partition coeffiecient (Figure 3d) and binding constant (Figure 3e) were indicative of favorable interaction between curcumin and micelles.

Banerjee et al. explored the effects of addition of room temperature ionic liquids (RTILs) and anionic surfactant sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) on the photophysics of curcumin in zwitterionic micellar aggregates where SB‐16 was the zwitterionic surfactant. [37] Curcumin was present in the palisade layer of the micelles. In low concentrations, RTILs ‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium ethylsulfate (EmimES), 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium butylsulfate (EmimBS), and 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium hexylsulfate (EmimHS) acted as electrolytes and decreased the cmc of the SB‐16 micelles. As a result, steady‐state fluorescence intensity and average fluorescence lifetime of curcumin was enhanced after addition of RTILs. The RTILs increased curcumin solubility in micellar solution as well as perturbed the ESIPT dynamics. However, at high concentrations, the RTILS acted as the solvent and increased the cmc of SB‐16 micelles, thereby destabilizing the system. On the other hand, addition of SDS caused curcumin to move from palisade layer of micelles towards bulk water.

The excited state dynamics of curcumin was significantly affected in n‐heptane/ Aerosol‐OT (AOT)/core solvent reverse micelles when using different core solvents such as water, ethylene glycol, glycerine and DMF. [38] Curcumin photophysics was also altered upon change in the W0 values (W0=[core solvent]/[AOT]) of the reverse micelles. Steady‐state absorption and fluorescence studies indicated that curcumin was present within the interfacial region of reverse micelles. The microenvironment of the reverse micelle interface was highly structured due to the hydrogen bonding interaction between hydrogen bond accepting solvent (water, ethylene glycol, glycerine) and AOT. These interactions were absent for hydrogen bond accepting solvent DMF leading to less structured microenvironment. H‐bonding interaction between curcumin keto‐enol functionality and AOT was also present. The non‐radiative deactivation of curcumin was hindered leading to increment in excited state lifetime of curcumin with addition of the core solvent. Increment was less for reverse micelles containing water because of the lower stability of curcumin in water. On the other hand, the higher curcumin lifetime in n‐heptane/AOT/DMF was attributed to perturbation of ESIPT by dipole‐dipole interactions.

Mandal et al. compared the photophysical properties of curcumin in non‐ionic Tween‐20 micelles and Tween‐20/cholesterol niosomes (Figure 4a). [39] Niosomes are structurally similar to phospholipid vesicles and are capable of solubilizing hydrophobic molecules like curcumin, making them an ideal drug delivery system. Curcumin was present within the rigid bilayer structures of nisomes which were more hydrophobic than micelles due to limited accessibility of water. ESIPT was one of the non‐radiative deactivation pathways of curcumin and responsible for its short fluorescence lifetime. The ESIPT of curcumin is very efficient in water, and limited contact with water caused higher fluorescence intensity (Figure 4b), enhanced fluorescence quantum yield (Figure 4c), and longer lifetime of curcumin in niosomes. Curcumin was involved in H‐bonding interaction with both the oxyethylene functionality of Tween‐20 and the hydroxyl functionality of cholesterol which may also have been responsible for its higher stability in niosomes.

Ghosh et al. studied the modulation of excited state dynamics of curcumin due to micelle‐to‐vesicle transition of pluronic triblock copolymer micelles to vesicular aggregates after addition of 5‐methylsalicylic acid. [40] These micelles consist of hydrophobic core made of poly(ethylene oxide) (PPO) units and hydrophilic corona composed of poly(propylene oxide) (PEO) units. Curcumin photophysics was studied in two different micelles composed of P123 and F127 triblock copolymers respectively, where P123 micelles were more stable due to presence of higher number of PPO units. Partition coefficients of curcumin for both P123 as well as F127 micelles were higher than conventional micelles. Reduced curcumin‐water interactions, and the interactions of curcumin with PPO and PEO units of the triblock copolymers perturbed the ESIPT process of curcumin, thereby stabilizing it. As in the case of niosomes, stability of curcumin was further enhanced when vesicular aggregates were formed after adding 5‐methylsalicylic acid to P123 micellar solution. This was due to more rigid membrane structure of vesicles having higher hydrophobicity.

In another work by the same group, comparisons were drawn between curcumin photophysics in surface active ionic liquid (SAIL) micelles and the SAIL‐nonionic surfactant mixed vesicles. [41] The cationic SAIL 1‐hexadecyl‐3‐methylimidazolium chloride ([C16mim]Cl) and surfactant sorbitan stearate (Span 60) were used in this study. Furthermore, modulation of curcumin excited state dynamics due to addition of a sugar, trehalose in the two systems was also investigated. In the micelle, curcumin interacted with the imidazolium head group of SAIL but in vesicles curcumin interacted with hydroxyl functionality of Span 60 in addition to the interactions with the SAIL. Hence, curcumin was more stable in vesicles as seen from the enhanced values of fluorescence intensity, quantum yield, and lifetime. Increasing concentrations of trehalose in the vesicular aggregates resulted in trehalose replacing water molecules at the interfacial region leading to deactivation of non‐radiative decay of curcumin.

Mishra and co‐workers demonstrated that addition of curcumin to micelles composed of nonionic surfactant tween 80 (T80) led to the formation of niosomes, where the role of curcumin was similar to that of cholesterol in formation of stable niosomes. [42] This micelle‐to‐vesicle transition induced by curcumin occurred when the molar ratio of T80: curcumin was 7 : 1 as observed from curcumin steady‐state anisotropy measurements (Figure 4d) and zeta potential experiments (Figure 4e).

Bile salts are bioactive amphiphilic molecules which spontaneously form aggregates in water.[ 43 , 44 ] At low concentrations, hydrophobic interactions between steroid moieties lead to formation of primary dimeric aggregates. [43] At higher concentrations, hydrogen bonding between carboxyl and hydroxyl functionalities is involved to form large secondary aggregates. [43] Bile salt formation occurs in the liver and they are utilized for digestion of fats and removal of toxins from the body.[ 43 , 44 ] The potential of aggregates of different bile salts sodium cholate (NaCh), sodium deoxycholate (NaDC) and sodium taurocholate (NaTC) to suppress the degradation of curcumin in aqueous medium was demonstrated by Sarkar and co‐workers. [43] Of the three different bile salts, NaTC aggregates were most successful in stabilizing curcumin, where less than 5 % of curcumin degraded even after 50 h. Moreover, curcumin fluorescence efficiency increased in NaTC aggregates whereas, the same decreased in NaCh and NaDC aggregates. These results were rationalized on the basis of intermolecular transfer of curcumin enolic proton to carboxylate functionality of NaCh/NaDC resulting in increased non‐radiative deactivation of the curcumin excited state. Notably, NaTC contains sulfonate functionality in place of the carboxylate functionality. Hence small differences in the chemical structures of the bile salts resulted in profound changes in excited state dynamics of curcumin. On the other hand, another research group utilized curcumin as an embedded probe to study the effect of submicellar concentrations of bile salts NaCh and NaDC on liposomes composed of dipalmitoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DPPC). [44]

A group of naturally occurring cyclic oligosaccharides called cyclodextrines have also been used for encapsulation of curcumin.[ 45 , 46 , 47 ] Formation of 1 : 1 host‐guest complex between curcumin and diamide linked γ‐cyclodextrines (66γCD2su and 66γCD2ur) in water, was reported by Kee and co‐workers.[ 45 , 46 , 47 ] Using transient absorption spectroscopy, the authors observed a band at 530 nm corresponding to excited state absorption (ESA). [47] The dynamics of this ESA band was associated with one growth component and three decay components. The fast decay component showed deuterium isotope effect and was attributed to curcumin ESIPT whereas, solvent reorganization was responsible for the fast growth component. The slower decay components were due to dynamics of curcumin in complexed form, and molecular motions related to the 66γCD2su and 66γCD2ur flexibility.

EPR spin label studies showed that curcumin assumed perpendicular orientation within phospholipid membranes and strongly affected the membrane lipid order, polarity, and phase transition. [48] Moreover, curcumin increased the water penetration in the center of the membrane as well as in the head group region. Another study illustrated that membrane bound curcumin supressed the vesicle‐to‐micelle transition for phospholipid/sodium cholate (NaC) mixed vesicles by increasing the amount of NaC required for phospholipid solubilization. [49] Pasc and co‐workers studied the keto‐enol properties of curcumin within the confined environment of lipid vesicles derived from soy lecithin. [50] While the planar and rigid keto‐enol tautomer assumed an orientation perpendicular to the membrane surface, the twisted and more flexible β‐diketo tautomer aligned itself parallel to the membrane. Moreover, increasing concentration of curcumin was characterized by higher red‐shifted fluorescence intensity implying higher concentration of the keto‐enol tautomer. In other words, higher curcumin concentration led to higher number of curcumin molecules occupying the same volume within the lipid bilayer. Because of the planar geometry and perpendicular orientation of keto‐enol tautomer, it occupied less space and was preferred, resulting in the keto‐enol equilibrium to shift towards the formation of this curcumin tautomer.

For curcumin in hydrogel, the time scale for solvation and ESIPT were 1.18 ps and ~17 ps, respectively. Furthermore, the ESA decay profile unveiled that in addition to solvation and ESIPT, the curcumin non‐radiative relaxation processes also included other slower motions which were the result of dynamic interactions between the octadecyl aggregates of the hydrogel and curcumin.[ 51 , 52 ] Hasan et al. prepared curcumin containing chitosan‐coated liposomes and studied the interactions between curcumin, phospholipid, and chitosan. [53] Kee and co‐workers demonstrated that curcumin binds to hydrophobic pockets of plasma proteins human serum albumin (HSA) and fibrinogen with binding constants of magnitude 104–105 M−1. [54] The stabilization of curcumin within these proteins suppressed degradation of curcumin (suppression yield 95 %) while retaining its therapeutic properties. The interaction of curcumin was also studied with the proteins transferrin and IgG where the binding constant order was 103 M−1.

Applications of Curcumin in Nanoscience and Biology

Applications of curcumin in nanoscience are quite diverse. Curcumin without any modifications may be incorporated into various nanomaterials for the purpose of increasing bioavailability and decreasing the rate of its degradation in water. Incorporation of curcumin within polymeric nanoparticles significantly reduced its degradation rate in aqueous medium. [55] Furthermore, curcumin interacted with keto and enol moieties of polymeric nanoparticles leading to perturbation of curcumin ESIPT dynamics resulting in higher excited state lifetime of curcumin. Similarly, in another work, encapsulation of curcumin in polyester nanoparticles provided the necessary separation between the curcumin molecule and water to prevent its degradation in aqueous medium. [56] This decreased interaction with water was characterized by lower amplitude of decay component related to solvent reorganization of the curcumin excited state dynamics as observed using femtosecond fluorescence upconversion spectroscopy. Interaction between curcumin and nanomaterials may also alter the excited state dynamics of curcumin. Sarkar and co‐workers studied the effect of plasmonic noble metal nanoparticles of gold and silver (AuNPs and AgNPs) on the excited state photophysics of curcumin in a biocompatible microemulsion. [57] It was observed that there was significant difference between interaction of curcumin with AgNPs, and that with AuNPs. The average curcumin lifetime was enhanced in the presence of AgNPs because curcumin‐AgNP nanoconjugates were formed. On the contrary, nanometal surface energy transfer (NSET) occurred from donor curcumin to acceptor AuNP leading to fluorescence quenching and faster dynamics of curcumin. Recently, curcumin was taken in giant vesicles composed of a surface active ionic liquid and sorbitan monoester (span 20 or span 80) for applications in white light generation. [58] Curcumin acted as the intermediate donor dye, along with 1,6‐diphenyl‐1,3,5‐hexatriene (DPH) as donor and anticancer drug doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX) as acceptor, to form a three component fluorescence resonance energy transfer system (Triple‐FRET) leading to generation of white light. In this context, it may be mentioned that the emissive properties of curcumin have also been exploited for imaging of lipid vesicles and tubules. [59] Ghosh et al. with the help of fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM), demonstrated that curcumin was not only a good dye for imaging but also that curcumin was stable within lipid tubules in aqueous medium for at least 48 h. [59] In another work, curcumin was employed as a molecular probe to study the effect of silver nanoparticles on model phospholipid membranes composed of 1,2‐dimyristoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DMPC). [60]

The monoanionic form of curcumin can bind to metal ions to form metal‐curcumin complexes which have applications such as photoinduced anticancer activity, and cellular imaging. [61] The natural alkaloid piperlongumine is known for its anti‐tumor and chemotherapeutic effect against lung cancer. [62] However, the molecule has low efficacy and high toxicity. Curcumin applications are limited due to its low bioavailability. Liu and co‐workers designed and synthesized a novel hybrid molecule 3‐[(E)‐4‐hydroxy‐3‐methoxybenzylidene]‐1‐[(E)‐3‐(3,4,5‐trimethoxyphenyl)acryloyl]piperidin‐2‐one (CP) consisting of both curcumin and piperlongumine (Figure 5a). [62] The hybrid molecule CP overcame the limitations of both the biomolecules and was capable of inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis. Moreover, the apoptosis was partly due to the cell cycle arrest in G2/M phase which was also caused by CP. In a separate work, curcumin was incorporated within the hydrophobic cavity of a water soluble molecular nanobarrel. [63] This composite not only enhanced the aqueous solubility of curcumin, but was also used to transport curcumin to HeLa cells. The complex between curcumin and diamide linked γ‐cyclodextrine had anti‐proliferative effect against human prostate cancer PC‐3 cell line. [46] In fructose containing aqueous solution, curcumin underwent self‐assembly with fructose to form hollow nanoparticles. [64] Dopamine was added to stabilize fructose‐curcumin nanoparticles by forming polydopamine coated nanoparticles. Similarly, in a separate work, molecular dynamics simulations predicted the self‐assembly of curcumin, induced by the natural anti‐diabetic molecule trigonelline. [65]

Curcumin nanoparticles (nanocurcumin) were prepared by spraying a solution of curcumin in dichloromethane, into boiling water under ultrasonic conditions. [66] These particles of size 2–40 nm, easily dispersed in water and were more effective than curcumin against different microorganisms. In another publication, curcumin nanoparticles (CURNPs) were utilized for the development of a fluorescence off‐on sensor for selective detection of Cu2+ and S2− ions. [67] The CURNPs selectively combined with Cu2+ to form CURNPs‐Cu2+ resulting in quenching of CURNP fluorescence (off type sensor) and subsequent addition of S2− to restored the CURNP fluorescence (on type sensor). Curcumin nanoparticles (nanocurcumin) prepared by a sol‐oil method, was found to inhibit the growth of human Hep‐2 cancer cells. [68] Positively charged curcumin nanoparticles having potent antifungal property, were prepared by incorporating curcumin within the polymer poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA), and subsequently modifying the surface charge of the nanoparticles using the cationic surfactant cetyltrimetylammonium bromide (CTAB) (Figure 5b) [69]

Curcumin also behaves as a ligand to form curcumin functionalized metal nanoparticles. Amyloid fibrils of proteins are characteristic feature of neurodegerative disorders such as Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, and Huntington's diseases. [70] Jana and co‐workers demonstrated the antiamyloid properties of curcumin functionalized gold nanoparticles, where these nanoparticles were capable of not only dissolving existing fibrils but also, inhibited fibril formation in the protein hen egg white lysozyme (HEWL) and the peptide Aβ1–40. [70] Another research group reported a curcumin nanoconjugate (PVP−C–AuNP) which was found to suppress the aggregation in the peptide Aβ1–16. [71] This curcumin nanoconjugate also promoted the disintegration of existing aggregates. The catalytic activity of curcumin stabilized silver nanoparticles (c‐AgNPs) was shown by the catalytic reduction of p‐nitrophenol to p‐aminophenol. [72] Huang and co‐workers reported the ability of curcumin modified silver nanoparticles to inactivate the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) before the virus enters the host cell, and thereby preventing the infection of the host cell. [73] Song et al. used curcumin as both reducing agent and capping ligand for preparation of curcumin stabilized silver nanoparticles (cAgNPs) under ultrasonic conditions. [74] The synergistic antibacterial properties of cAgNPs were tested against both Gram positive (Bactrium subtilis) and Gram negative (Escherichia coli) bacteria models. Presence of Ag+ ions and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are factors which are responsible for the antimicrobial properties of silver nanoparticles. [74] Curcumin was found to increase the binding of silver to the bacterial membrane and release of Ag+ ions compared to silver nanoparticles without curcumin, resulting in a temporary high Ag+ concentration in proximity to the bacterium surface. At the same time, curcumin also facilited more ROS to be generated. The net result was damage of bacterial membrane causing the intracellular contents and bacterial lipases to leak, ultimately leading to the death of the bacterium. Radecka and co‐workers prepared curcumin capped silver nanoparticles (cAgNPs) by mixing silver nitrate with a solution containing curcumin‐cyclodextrine inclusion complex. [75] The resultant cAgNPs were incorporated into bacterial cellulose (BC) hydrogels to form cAgNP‐loaded BC hydrogels. The hydrogel was found to have good antimicrobial effect against the microbes Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida auris, common pathogens known for infecting wounds.

Both curcumin and curcumin‐loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) were found to induce cell death in MCF7 (human breast carcinoma) as well as PC3 (human prostatic adenocarcinoma) cancer cell lines. [76] In another study, curcumin containing SLNs (Man‐CUR SLN) were effective against lung cancer A549 cell line and Mycobacterium intracellulare infected macrophages. [77] The surfaces of these SLNs were modified using mannose to target the mannose receptors on cancer cells and bacterial macrophages (Figure 6a). A highly biocompatible composite nanodrug Cur@Fe&TA, consisting of curcumin encapsulated in Fe3+/tannic acid metal‐polyphenol network, was used for treatment of irritable bowel disease (IBD) (Figure 6b). [78] The antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties of curcumin in nanodrug improved gastrointestinal cytopathology related to IBD in the mouse model.

Lipid nanocapsules containing curcumin were able to induce apoptosis in 9 L glioma cells. [79] Curcumin loaded hybrid lipid nanocapsules were effective against both cancer stem cells (CSCs) and non‐CSCs of MCF‐7 breast cancer cell line. [80] A nanocomposite hydrogel consisting of dialdehyde cellulose cross‐linked with chitosan and embedded with zinc oxide nanoparticles, was used as drug delivery vehicle for curcumin. [81] This hydrogel exhibited antimicrobial activity against Trichophyton rubrum and Staphylococcus aureus. Moreover, the curcumin loaded hydrogel enhanced anticancer activity compared to free curcumin as seen from the in vitro cytotoxicity studies performed using murine fibroblast L929 cell lines and human skin carcinoma A431 cell lines. Curcumin loaded lipid vesicles were used for transdermal delivery of curcumin. [82] Zhang and co‐workers prepared a curcumin based ionic liquid, and subsequently used the ionic liquid for preparation of a hydrogel Cur‐Car‐IL@Ilo. [83] This hydrogel was used as topical transdermal drug delivery vehicle for curcumin to effectively treat erythema, scaling, and inflammation, which are pathophysiological features of the autoimmune disorder psoriasis in mouse model.

Conclusion and Perspective for the Future

In this review, we have compiled various aspects related to the photochemistry and photophysics of curcumin. We have discussed various excited state processes associated with the radiative decay of curcumin and the different strategies employed to modulate same. We have also elaborated on the multitude of ways in which various confined media and biomacromolecules may be exploited to enhance curcumin bioavailability. Furthermore, curcumin not only has so many different therapeutic properties, but also diverse applications. The molecule has applications in the free form, in various confined media, and when encapsulated in materials. Moreover, curcumin can also act as reducing agent and ligand for nanomaterial synthesis. In many of these materials, the anticancer and antimicrobial properties of curcumin are enhanced. In biology, curcumin has been employed in vivo on mouse model and in vitro on various cell lines.

Despite, the many recent innovations, the major setback to widespread use of curcumin is still its limited bioavailability and poor aqueous solubility. Hence, most recent research still focuses on eliminating, or at the very least minimising these limitations associated with curcumin. Nanomaterials because of their large surface to volume ratio and high biocompatibility, are slowly emerging as ideal candidates for curcumin drug delivery. Curcumin containing materials while retaining the medicinal properties of curcumin, are also often functionalized to target specific problems. The future of curcumin lies in curcumin based materials which show considerable promise as therapeutic agents in different fields of medicine.
