Spatial self-organization of cancer stem cell niches revealed by live single-cell imaging
Authors: Mathilde Brulé, Anais Horochowska, Emeline Fontaine, Raoul Torero-Ibad, Flavie Woesteland, Marie Denoulet, Jean Pesez, Eric Adriaenssens, Robert-Alain Toillon, Xuefen Le Bourhis, Benjamin Pfeuty, Chann Lagadec, François Anquez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhenotypic plasticity is a major factor in tumor heterogeneity and treatment resistance. In particular, cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a small subpopulation within tumors that possesses self-renewal and tumor-forming capabilities. Understanding reprogramming, maintenance, and lineage properties of CSCs requires dedicated tools to disentangle the respective influences of phenotypic inheritance and cell-cell interactions.MethodsHere, we set up ultra-wide field microscopy to image breast cancer cell lines expressing a stemness fluorescent reporter over several days. The fluorescent reporter distinguishes three phenotypes: CSCs, cancer differentiated cells (CDCs), and intermediate/transiting cancer cells (iCCs).ResultsSpatial statistics indicate significant zonation in which CSCs cluster together and are spatially separated from CDCs, forming patterns resembling niches. Surprisingly, single-cell time series reveal spontaneous reprogramming events from CDC to CSC even in unperturbed populations. We identify that such transitions are prone to arise during the cell cycle. Moreover, lineage analysis shows that the phenotype is partially inherited from ancestor cells. However, such heredity is not sufficient to explain the spatial properties of the cell population, which also depend on cell-cell interactions. Indeed, we find that phenotypic transitions of cancer cells are influenced by the phenotypic state of neighboring cells. Reprogramming into CSCs is respectively promoted and inhibited by the presence of CSCs and CDCs in the neighborhood.ConclusionsAltogether, our results disentangle how phenotypic inheritance and intercellular interactions orchestrate the spatio-temporal self-organization of cancer cell heterogeneity, maintaining a subpopulation of CSCs within niches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04681-z.

Introduction

Tumors consist of multiple sub-populations of cells, each differing in their response to drugs and ability to adapt to environmental changes. This variability between cells significantly influences therapeutic outcomes and affects disease progression [1, 2]. While part of this variability in treatment response can be attributed to the presence of distinct genetic and epigenetic profiles [1, 3–5], the existence of multiple phenotypic states, resembling the hierarchical organization of normal tissues, plays a crucial role in tumorigenesis and treatment resistance [6–8]. At the apex of this hierarchy, Cancer Stem Cells (CSCs) generate cellular diversity by differentiation within the tumor [8, 9]. This process is not strictly unidirectional, as cells can switch between states in response to specific conditions, a phenomenon known as cell plasticity [10–14]. This ability of cells to transition between states enables them to evade selective pressures during tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to treatment [14, 15]. For instance, phenotypic plasticity was shown to be triggered during tumor progression, such as epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [14, 16] or immune escape [17, 18]. Consequently, cellular plasticity, which enables dynamic transitions between CSCs and non-CSCs, may undermine therapeutic strategies aimed solely at eliminating CSCs [9, 13, 19, 20]. A deeper understanding of phenotypic plasticity is essential for developing effective treatments that can target and deplete the CSC population [9].

Under homeostatic conditions, a complex network maintains a balance between stem-like and differentiated cells. This equilibrium is orchestrated by a multitude of pluripotent transcription factors, including OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, KLF4, and MYC [9, 21], and numerous intracellular signaling pathways, including Wnt [22, 23], Notch [24], Sonic Hedgehog [25], NF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}κB, JAK-STAT, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, TGF/SMAD, and PPAR [26]. Moreover, various extracellular factors play a critical role, including vascular niches, hypoxia, tumor-associated macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts, cancer-associated mesenchymal stem cells, and the extracellular matrix [27–31]. Both paracrine and juxtacrine-mediated signaling spatially shape the tumor, leading to the formation of CSC niches. For instance, differentiated cancer cells secrete factors like Interleukin-6 [32], BDNF [33], or NGF [34] that foster the survival and self-renewal of CSCs in lung cancer, breast cancer, and glioblastoma. In contrast, CSCs secrete factors like DKK1 that promote differentiation [35]. Perturbing this equilibrium results in a phenotypic switch. Indeed, phenotypic plasticity of CSCs was first observed upon perturbation of environmental conditions such as hypoxia [36] or anticancer treatments [37]. Following treatment, the cell population is enriched in stem-like cells and then restores the original cell composition [38–40]. The diversity of phenotypes, interaction motifs, and signalling pathways involved make it difficult to understand how CSC niches auto-organize in space and time.

There is a need for ex vivo assays that emulate the spatial arrangement of cell types in tumors, similar to approaches used to recapitulate essential developmental processes such as germ layer and axial patterning [41]. Although the regulation of tumor heterogeneity is complex, arising from a diversity of intracellular and extracellular signaling mechanisms, some general insights could be gained through system-level approaches. Indeed, regardless of the specific molecular pathways at play, the primary sources of spatial self-organization of cell phenotypes are fundamentally influenced by a few cell-fate processes, namely phenotypic switch, division, motility, and death [42–44]. Some fundamental determinants can, in principle, be unveiled by focusing on the initial and spontaneous trends to self-organize in unperturbed environments. We therefore hypothesize that cultured cancer cell lines exhibit rudimentary forms of self-organization characterized by the spatial segregation of CSC niches. Live cell imaging tools provide rich spatio-temporal information, enabling tracking of the main processes involved in spatial self-organization. This wealth of data facilitates a system-level approach to investigate the intricate interplay between niche effects and the homeostatic capacity of tumor models. In this work, we focus on Breast Cancer Stem Cells (BCSCs) characterized by high Aldehyde Dehydrogenase (ALDH) expression [45]. We explore the interplay between the temporal transitions and the spatial distribution of stem-like phenotypes under unperturbed and steady culture conditions.

To this end, we developed an ultra-wide field imaging system capable of tracking thousands of individual cells in space and time over several days. Using mNeptune fluorescent protein expression under the control of the promoter of ALDH1A1 (pALDH1A1:mNeptune) as a stemness reporter for breast cancer cells [38], we conducted a series of analyses to characterize the spatial and temporal features of phenotypic heterogeneity. From the broad distribution of fluorescent marker over the population, statistical analysis identifies two main phenotypes associated with stemness and differentiation traits, as well as an intermediate state. Point pattern analysis uncovered spatial segregation between stem-like and differentiated phenotypes. Notably, we observed phenotypic transitions, including reprogramming events from CDCs to CSC phenotypes, even within unperturbed cancer cell populations. Analysis of cell fate further underscored the significant role of intercellular signaling in phenotypic transitions and CSC reprogramming. Altogether, our findings demonstrate that cancer stem cell niches spontaneously emerge in unconstrained populations of cancer cells through the interplay of phenotypic inheritance across generations and intercellular communications that stabilize the stemness phenotype.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, plasmid transfections, and generation of stable cell lines

Cell culture

The experiments are carried out on two triple-negative breast cancer cell lines, SUM159PT (51083) and MDA-MB-231 (50058). The SUM159PT cell line is obtained from Asterand and cultured in F12 Nut Mix media (Gibco) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (5%, FBS, PAN-Biotech), insulin (5 μg/ml), HEPES (10 nM, 15630080, Gibco), hydrocortisone (1 μg/ml), and Zell Shield. The MDA-MB-231 cell line is obtained from ATCC and cultured in MEM media (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAN-Biotech), 1× non-essential amino acids (11140035, Gibco), and Zell Shield. For both cell lines, Zell Shield is used to prevent bacterial, fungal, and mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained in a 5% CO2/air environment. In routine conditions, cells are split (1/10) twice a week using trypsin-EDTA, and kept in culture for no more than 25 passages.

Plasmid transfections

The pALDH1A1-mNeptune vector is constructed with mNeptune fluorescent protein under the control of the ALDH1A1 promoter, a breast CSC marker, and is obtained as previously studied [38]. Briefly, the high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Q5 DNA polymerase, New England Biolabs) was employed to PCR-amplify the human ALDH1A1 promoter region (−1248 to +52) from genomic DNA of the SUM159PT cell line. The mNeptune-TK fused protein coding sequence, replication origin (ori), and neomycin resistance gene were PCR-amplified using various template vectors. Twenty cycles of PCR were performed, employing specific primers with flanking BsaI sites. The flanking overhangs were selected to enable complementary ligation with overhangs from other PCR fragments. Therefore, the PCR fragments can be assembled in an orderly manner to form a circular vector. The resulting PCR fragments were purified, and closed circular plasmids were assembled via a single restriction-ligation reaction with BsaI enzyme (R3535L, New England Biolabs) and T4 DNA ligase (M0202L, New England Biolabs). The assembled plasmids were transformed into competent cells (C404003, Invitrogen). The plasmids were extracted with QIAGEN kits. Sequencing primers were synthesized by Eurogentech, and the vector was sequenced by GATC (Sanger sequencing).

Thanks to this construction, we can sort the mNeptune-high cells that exhibit stemness characteristics (self-renewal, differentiation, and tumorigenicity) [38].

The cells were transfected with the vectors using nucleofection. Five hundred thousand cells were resuspended in 100 μL of buffer (kit V, VCA-1003, Lonza) with 1 μg of DNA and electroporated using the X-013 protocol of the Nucleofector II Device (Amaxa).

Generation of stable cell lines

Stable cell lines expressing pALDH1A1-mNeptune are obtained as previously explained [38]. The mNeptune fluorescence was examined 24 h post-transfection, and the positive cells were selected with 1 mg/mL of G418 (Invitrogen). Subsequent to selection, cells positive for fluorescence were sorted using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (BD FACS Aria III). Several days after cell sorting, a heterogeneous cell population with mNeptune-positive and mNeptune-negative cells was regenerated as expected [38]. Regularly, cell cultures are checked for a functional stemness reporter. To do so, negative cells are sorted using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (BD FACS Aria III), and we look for the establishment of a heterogeneous cell population with mNeptune-positive and mNeptune-negative cells.

After the establishment of pALDH1A1-mNeptune stable cell lines, cells were transfected with a pCMV-Grx1roGFP2-Hygromycin vector, then the stable cell lines were selected with hygromycin B (the vector was modified from pEIGW-Grx1-roGFP2, 64990, Addgene). The information obtained from Grx1-roGFP2 fluorescence is not utilized in this work.

Image processing, cell segmentation and tracking

Image processing was performed offline using custom-written code in Matlab R2019a. Cell segmentation was performed on images of cell nuclei (Hoechst channel), assuming one nucleus per cell. After image restoration, cell segmentation and tracking were performed simultaneously. Tracking data were indeed used to improve segmentation, as proposed in [47]. The SUM159PT dataset was fully curated by human intervention. Cell lineage and single-cell fluorescence signal were subsequently extracted.

Cell segmentation and tracking

We used tracking information to correct segmentation errors as suggested in [47]. Here, we first describe segmentation, then cell tracking, and finally explain how tracking is used as feedback to further refine cell segmentation.

Shading correction was performed as described above. Then, images of nuclei were blurred with a Gaussian filter of width 6.5 μm to remove tiny details that were unnecessary for nuclear shape segmentation. This procedure resulted in bell-shaped intensity distributions centered on nuclei. We then detected local maxima to assign putative cell centers. Individual masks were initialized to disks of radius 22 μm around putative centers. Nuclear masks were refined by iteratively removing border pixels using Otsu thresholding [50]. This procedure is followed by several morphological operations. First, holes are filled; then we performed erosion followed by dilatation with a kernel of 2 pixels. Each mask was finally automatically screened to detect putative neighbouring cells for which masks were merged. To split joined masks of neighbouring cells, we compared the mask boundary to its convex hull. To do so, for all subsets of boundaries found inside the convex hull, we selected the point closest to the mask center and then computed the ratio between (i) the distance of this point to the center and (ii) the distance of the closest section of the convex hull to the mask center. If at least two points were found with this ratio smaller than 0.75, the mask was split in two parts.

Cell segmentation as described above was performed for each vignette separately. Then, the reconstructed field of view was automatically screened for duplicate cell masks caused by image overlap. Overlapping masks found in two different but neighbouring vignettes were identified as duplicates, and only the largest mask was kept for further analysis. Information on nuclei area and Hoechst fluorescence intensity were then collected for the whole field of view and at each time point. These data were then used to filter false positives and, in particular, small masks with low fluorescence signal. Detected cells from all vignettes were assembled to assign coordinates (X, Y) for tracking. Tracking was performed by connecting all cells detected at a given time point to their nearest neighbour at the next time point. The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α was set to 0.12. Pairings with a cost function higher than 30 μm were not considered, as described in [51].

As proposed in [47], we used tracking as a feedback to enhance cell segmentation. A common error in nuclei segmentation is that two neighbouring cells come in close contact and lead to detection of a single mask for both cells. We call this event “cells collision”. Collision events can easily be identified from tracking data because the trajectory of one cell prematurely ends. Conversely, the mask from a single cell can be correctly detected at one frame but split in the following frame. We named this event “cell over-split”. Over-split events can be confounded with natural cell division. However, during mitosis, the cell transiently becomes brighter in the brightfield channel. To distinguish between mitosis and over-split, we thus used a contrast parameter estimated from brightfield images. We computed a histogram of all background pixels. The contrast parameter was defined as the fraction of mask pixels out of the 99% confidence interval. Cells with a contrast parameter higher than a user-defined threshold (typically 0.2) were considered as mitotic, ruling out over-split. Screening the tracking data for over-split or collision events allowed us to correct the segmentation. Tracking was then run again. This procedure was repeated 10 times, and the number of collision/over-split events was progressively reduced close to zero.

For instance, assuming an average track length of 20 frames, one expects the probability to detect the cell at all time points to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{20}=0.54$$\end{document}s20=0.54. The segmented and tracking data were thus manually corrected using custom-built software in Matlab. In brief, segmentation masks were overlaid with images of Hoechst staining and brightfield. Data were screened by a human to detect segmentation errors, which were classified into three categories: false positives, missed cells, or masks to fuse. Manual correction was saved, and corresponding masks were corrected automatically. Tracking was performed once again. Manual correction of a full time-lapse took 40 h for an untrained user, which reduced to 25 h after one round. The SUM159PT dataset presented here was fully corrected.

Lineage reconstruction

Lineage was reconstructed from tracking data by detecting mitotic events. We note that tracking assigns the same identifier to a mother cell and its closest daughter. Putative divisions were detected by screening the creation of a new trajectory in the vicinity of an existing one (distance lower than 60 μm). Again, we used the contrast parameter estimated from brightfield images to validate mitotic events. If the contrast parameter of either the mother cell or daughter cells was higher than a user-defined threshold (typically 0.2), the event was considered as a mitosis.

After detection of all mitotic events, we screened for cells for which we could detect the beginning and end of the cell cycle, i.e., cells for which birth and subsequent division were captured by the time-lapse and also detected by the algorithm. Only cells with a cell cycle duration greater than 15 h were retained for time-resolved analysis (lineage analysis and time-series clustering). On the other hand, all cells were used for spatial analysis.

Single cell time-series clustering

The aim of time-series clustering was to identify, without a priori knowledge on time scales or shape, families of single-cell temporal patterns of the CSC reporter signal.

All cells drastically change shape during mitosis. They transiently round up, and their apparent area was thus smaller than during the rest of the cell cycle. To avoid clustering on these parts of the time-series, we excluded the first six time points and the last six time points of resampled time-series to search for temporal patterns.

To favor detection of transitioning temporal patterns, clustering was first performed on 6 subsets independently. The first 3 subsets were cells found to be either CDC, iCC, or CSC at the beginning of the cell cycle and which had changed phenotype at the end; the other 3 subsets were cells that did not change phenotype. To do so, we randomly initialized synthetic time-series with signals according to the fitted fluorescence distribution. The noise level was chosen by examining the mean squared deviation of constant experimental traces. The synthetic datasets were then clustered using the same procedure as for the experimental dataset. As expected, for low values of k, W(k) decreases faster for the simulated datasets compared to the experimental data. The optimal number of clusters was chosen as described in the original article [59].

Statistical analysis

Bootstrap resampling

Bootstrap resampling [60] was used to estimate the sensitivity of several quantities without knowledge of the underlying error distribution. To do so, the bootstrap randomly resamples the data with replacement. The procedure is repeated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{bs}$$\end{document}Nbs times to estimate the mean and confidence interval for the quantity of interest. For all uses of bootstrap resampling, we chose \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{bs}$$\end{document}Nbs to ensure that the values obtained with bootstrap coincide with the empirical mean estimated without bootstrap[60].

Phenotype shuffling

Phenotype shuffling was used as a statistical test for bivariate point pattern analysis. Indeed, the confidence interval of the point correlation function (PCF) strongly depends on the number of samples. Moreover, the univariate PCF exhibits a structure indicating that cells display spatial clustering independently of their phenotype.

With phenotype shuffling, we aim at deciding whether correlations and anti-correlations between phenotypes revealed by bivariate PCF could be solely explained by the univariate spatial pattern or whether such correlations characterize specific interactions between phenotypes. To do so, phenotype shuffling computes the phenotype distribution as if phenotypes were randomly distributed across the univariate distribution. At a given time point, we used measured cell positions, but fluorescence intensities were attributed randomly by permuting all cell intensities. The bivariate PCF was then computed as described above with the simulated intensities. The procedure was repeated 1500 times to compute the mean and confidence interval.

p-values

p-values are used in this work to assess the significance of spatial correlations (anti-correlations) between phenotypes compared to (i) phenotype shuffling or (ii) numerical simulations. p-values estimate the probabilities of the null hypothesis.

For Supplementary Figures 6 and 7, the null hypothesis is “the estimated bivariate PCF does not differ from the bivariate PCF obtained from phenotype shuffling”. To estimate p-values, we first used bootstrap resampling to estimate the mean, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}μ, and standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}σ of the bivariate PCF at a given radius. Then, we applied phenotype shuffling and computed the corresponding bivariate PCF 1500 times. The p-value is defined as the fraction of shuffled datasets for which the corresponding bivariate PCF falls within the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\mu -\sigma ;\mu +\sigma ]$$\end{document}[μ-σ;μ+σ] at the desired radius.

Numerical simulations

Numerical simulations are used for statistical hypothesis testing. Cells are assumed to have an internal variable, I, which represents the fluorescence intensity of the CSC reporter. Cells are assumed to have two states, 1 and 2. The first state corresponds to the beginning of the cell cycle, and the second to the end of the cell cycle. Cells can make transitions from state 1 to 2 (which corresponds to cell cycle evolution), and then from 2 to 1 (which corresponds to mitosis).

Memory-less spatial model

Motility and divisions are not simulated. Instead, we used trajectories and lineage extracted from experiments. Thus, for each trajectory, state transitions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \rightarrow 2$$\end{document}1→2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \rightarrow 1$$\end{document}2→1 are defined by experimentally determined lineage. Again, the internal variable, I, is assumed to change only during state transition. Because time evolution of fluorescence of transiting cells was found to be monotonous, we assumed linear time evolution during the cell cycle. The evolution of the internal variable, I, is calculated the same way as for the Memory-less chain model. Doing so, we could simulate intensities at each time point for each cell in the experiment. The simulations were repeated 500 times for each experiment, and the quantities described in the text are computed the same way as for experimental data.

Results

Ultra-wide field imaging characterizes cancer cell phenotypic diversity and its spatial distribution

To distinguish CSCs from non-CSCs and to track the spatio-temporal dynamics of cells, we previously developed a reporter based on mNeptune fluorescent protein expression under the control of the promoter of ALDH1A1 (pALDH1A1:mNeptune) [38]. In 2D cell culture conditions, CSC frequency is rather low (from 1% up to 5%) [45, 61]. Thus, we set up ultra-wide field microscopy to investigate the phenotypic diversity of breast cancer cells with sufficient statistics.

SUM159PT and MDA-MB-231 cells were imaged with a high numerical aperture objective to allow detection of the faint pALDH1A1:mNeptune signal. A 6.8 mm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}× 6.8 mm wide field of view was reconstructed by assembling 60× high-resolution images (Fig. 1A–C). Images reveal a large diversity in single-cell mNeptune fluorescence intensity, with the majority of cells exhibiting no or low fluorescence and a small fraction of cells expressing high fluorescence (Fig. 1C). This observation qualitatively aligns with the low CSC fraction reported in breast cancer cells [61]. At a larger scale (Fig. 1A), reconstructed images indicate high spatial heterogeneity with small regions populated by cells exhibiting high fluorescence.

To further quantify the distribution of SUM159PT sub-populations, in addition to the CSC reporter, we used cell segmentation and image processing (see "Materials and methods" section). At the cellular level, the CSC reporter signal is evenly spread over the cell volume (Fig. 1C). Thus, we used average nuclear mNeptune fluorescence as a proxy for single-cell phenotype. We extracted single-cell fluorescence for up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^4$$\end{document}104 individual cells per reconstructed field of view. We observed that single-cell fluorescence shows a tailed distribution (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Figure 2A), with ~95% of cells having fluorescence below 130 Relative Fluorescence Units (RFU). More precisely, the empirical fluorescence distribution is well described by the sum of three probability density functions (lower panel of Fig. 1D). A normal distribution centered at low fluorescence level (2.6 RFU) likely corresponds to cancer differentiated cells (CDCs), while, on the other side, a gamma distribution with a mean of ~150 RFU corresponds to CSCs. Additionally, a third distribution, also fitted by a gamma distribution (mean ~42 RFU), indicates the presence of intermediate cancer cells (iCCs), with characteristics that presumably lie between CDCs and CSCs. The cell fluorescence distribution is reproducible from one experiment to another, although the weights of each sub-population may slightly vary (Supplementary Figure 2 A, 2B). Similar results are obtained with another cell line, MDA-MB-231 (Supplementary Figure 3 A), for which the fluorescence distribution is also captured by the sum of three distinct probability density functions centered on low, intermediate, and high fluorescence intensity. The intersection between the fitted probability density functions (PDF)s defines thresholds that are further used to approximate the fraction of cells in the respective phenotypic states: CDC, iCC, or CSC.

Finally, our setup allows time-lapse imaging of live cells (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Video 1). Unperturbed SUM159PT cells are tracked for up to 5 days at an acquisition rate of one frame every 45 min. Analyzing cell growth over time, we find that the growth rate is similar under our imaging system compared to cells grown in a standard incubator (Fig. 1F). We also do not observe qualitative morphological differences between cells growing under imaging and in standard conditions. This indicates that imaging with our top-stage incubator does not introduce major side effects on the cancer cell population. By means of cell segmentation and image processing, we recovered the time evolution of the three identified populations (Fig. 1G). After a small drop during the first 30 h, the fraction of CSCs remains constant (~5%) for the entire duration of the experiment. This observation is consistent with previous reports in breast cancer cells with our CSC reporter [38] or using other CSC markers [37]. On the other hand, CDC and iCC sub-populations are much more dynamic during a transient phase that lasts approximately 24 to 36 h, after which their proportions stabilize to ~65% for CDCs and ~30% for iCCs. This behaviour is reproducible from one experiment to another (Supplementary Figure 2B), and we observe a similar trend with the other cell line, MDA-MB-231 (Supplementary Figure 3B).

Interestingly, we do not find a significant difference between the mean division rates (0.02 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.04\ h^{-1}$$\end{document}0.04h-1) of all three populations with SUM159PT (Supplementary Figure 2C ) and MDA-MB-231 (Supplementary Figure 3C) cell lines. Specifically, the division rate of CDCs does not significantly exceed that of iCCs during the transient phase. Point pattern analysis unveils spatial segregation of phenotypes with clusters of CSCs

To quantitatively characterize spatial heterogeneity, we employed functions from point pattern analysis [53], a methodology recently applied to biological systems [62]. Each cell is characterized by both its spatial coordinates (X,Y) and a specific phenotype (CDC, iCC or CSC) (Fig. 2A, left panel). We estimated the empirical Point Correlation Function (PCF), denoted as g(r), which is a function of a distance, r. The value of g(r) is computed by counting events within a thin annulus of radius r with a thickness of 5 μm (see "Materials and methods" section). The PCF measures the increase or decrease in the likelihood of finding an event at a distance r compared to what would be expected under complete spatial randomness (CSR). g(r) greater than 1 indicates a more clustered pattern than CSR, while a value smaller than 1 indicates a more dispersed pattern than CSR (Fig. 2A, right panels).

We first computed the univariate PCF, for which cell phenotypes are not distinguished (Supplementary Figure 4). At very short distances (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r \le 10$$\end{document}r≤10 μm), the univariate PCF remains below 1, indicating that cells do not overlap in our experimental conditions. Subsequently, the function peaks at approximately 1.5 to 2 for radii of around 40 to 60 μm (depending on the experiment), reflecting the close proximity of sister cells and the formation of micro-colonies. At larger scales, the PCF gradually approaches 1, indicating complete spatial randomness.

To investigate the spatial correlation between phenotypes, we computed the bivariate PCF. For each phenotype, we screened the presence of the same or other phenotypes within its surroundings. Because the univariate PCF has a well-defined shape reflecting tissue structure, we needed to disentangle properties due to putative relationships between phenotypes from the univariate spatial organization. To achieve this, any statistical test must account for the sample size to correctly estimate the confidence interval [53]. We thus compared the experimental bivariate PCF with the one obtained from phenotype shuffling, in which cell fluorescence values are randomly permuted (see "Materials and methods" section). Overall, no significant differences between shuffling and bivariate PCF are found for iCC versus iCC or CSC (Fig. 2C, F). In contrast, CSCs (respectively CDCs) are more clustered with CSCs (respectively CDCs) compared with shuffling or complete spatial randomness (CSR) (Fig. 2D, G). This effect is more pronounced with CSCs, for which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{++}(r \approx 15 \mu m)$$\end{document}g++(r≈15 is about 20 times greater than CSR and 10 times greater than shuffling (Fig. 2D). Fitting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{++}(r)$$\end{document}g++(r) to an exponential curve indicated a characteristic length of ~50 μm at the beginning of the experiment, slowly increasing to ~100 μm after 4 days (Supplementary Figure 5). Finally, at short range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r \sim 15$$\end{document}r∼15 μm), both CSC and iCC are excluded from regions with a high density of CDCs (Fig. 2B, E). Again, the effect is more pronounced with CSC versus CDC, for which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{+-}(r \approx 15 \mu m)$$\end{document}g+-(r≈15 is found to be ~10 times smaller than CSR and 15 times smaller than shuffling (Fig. 2B). This clustered pattern is observed at all time points (Supplementary Figure 5) and is reproducible from one experiment to another (Supplementary Figure 6). We also applied point pattern analysis to data collected for the other breast cancer cell line, MDA-MB-231, and similar results are found, though with weaker correlation (Supplementary Figure 7).

In summary, we observe a spatial patterning of cancer cell phenotypes, where cells of a given phenotype (low or high fluorescence intensities) tend to cluster with cells of the same phenotype. Notably, CSCs tend to form clusters of an average diameter of 100 μm (about 10 cell widths). Machine learning applied to single-cell time series identifies phenotypic transitions such as CSC reprogramming events

Because proliferation rate is similar for all phenotypes, the robust establishment of a specific proportion of different phenotypes throughout the population is expected to solely rely on dynamic transitions between stem, intermediate, and differentiated states. It is questionable whether these transitions mainly occur during the initial transient phase to establish a steady proportion, or throughout the entire course of steady population growth. To address these issues, we used unsupervised partitioning analysis of single-cell time series (SCTS) to, without prior knowledge of time scales or specific shapes, identify distinctive temporal patterns of differentiation or reprogramming events. Automated tracking of cell trajectories, detection of division events, and single-cell fluorescence extracted by means of image processing (see "Materials and methods" section) allowed us to reconstruct single-cell time series (SCTS). Then, as depicted in Fig. 3A, cells for which the full cell cycle could be monitored were selected for further analysis. For SUM159PT cells, we collected 19,620 SCTS from 5 different experiments. We used cell-cycle progression (time relative to the cell cycle duration) as a metric for time evolution, and each time series was re-sampled so that all SCTS share the same number of points along cell cycle progression. Euclidean distance is used as a dissimilarity measurement, and k-medoids [57] is used to partition SCTS, while the Gap statistics [59] is applied to determine the relevant number of clusters. This procedure results in 16 clusters. However, the last cluster (#16) contains SCTS from only 2 experiments, while all other clusters cover all experiments (Supplementary Figure 8A and 8B). This last cluster was excluded from further analysis, and we finally considered 15 clusters (Fig. 3) with 99.9% of the SCTS clustered. The resulting clustering did not show significant bias for any experiment (Supplementary Figure 8A and 8B).

Partitioning analysis effectively captures the phenotypic diversity, as the procedure results in SCTS categorized based on their average fluorescence signal (Fig. 3B). More generally, clustered SCTS could be classified into two main categories: approximately 70% of cells exhibiting a stable signal (Supplementary Figure 9B), while approximately 30% of the cells are within a transiting state undergoing an increase toward higher fluorescence (Supplementary Figure 9A). It is important to note that these transitions occur gradually throughout the cell cycle, without any abrupt changes. We obtained similar results for the other cell line, MDA-MB-231 (Supplementary Figure 10), for which we found approximately 50% transitioning SCTS. However, we note that transitioning SCTS clusters of MDA-MB-231 also include SCTS with decreasing fluorescence (Supplementary Figure 10, clusters #14, 7 and 8).

Notably, for SUM159PT and MDA-MB-231, the partitioning analysis unveils clusters (#10 and 11) with ascending signal, and for which cells transition from a fluorescence level close to the CDC threshold to a fluorescence level close to or above the CSC threshold (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Figure 9 A). Clusters #10 and 11 comprise 4.8% of the analyzed time series (Fig. 3B), and are not limited to the transient phase at the beginning of the experiment (Supplementary Figure 8C). This last observation supports the idea that a steady proportion of phenotypes arises from dynamic conversions throughout the experiment time course. More specifically, among cells within ascending clusters, we found representative instances of single cells transitioning from CDC to CSC (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, progenitors from the same mother cell can have very different fates. Indeed, the sister of the reprogramming cell depicted in Fig. 3C does not exhibit a transition nor significant fluorescence variation (Fig. 3C, right panel).

To qualitatively visualize whether phenotype is transmitted to the cell progeny, we plot the clustered SCTS together with the mother SCTS and daughter SCTS when available (Supplementary Figure 11). While the fluorescence level of a mother seems to be preserved for its daughter, cell division nevertheless introduces some variability, which will be studied more systematically in the next section. Remarkably, transiting SCTS are not limited to the transient phase (0 to 24/36 h), during which we observed exchange between iCC and CDC (Fig. 1G). These reprogramming SCTS are also detected during the second phase of the experiment, when steady state is already reached at the population level (Supplementary Figure 8C). Lineage analysis reveals phenotypic inheritance through symmetric division

To investigate how the cell cycle influences phenotypic changes, we examined the fluorescence variations across generations. Figure 4A shows an example of lineage reconstruction with a depth of three generations. From a mother cell, we kept track of its 2 daughters, which gave rise to 4 grand-daughters, for which we could detect 6 out of 8 great grand-daughters.

We first considered the fluorescence correlation between mothers and daughters. To do so, we compared the fluorescence intensity measured at the end of the mother’s cycle with the one measured at the beginning of the daughter’s cycle (see "Materials and methods" section). From the 5 experiments with SUM159PT cells, we could retrieve 37,582 of such relationships. As expected, daughter’s fluorescence strongly correlates with that of its mother (Fig. 4B, center panel). Contribution of phenotypic inheritance to the spatial clustering of CSCs

A mechanistic hypothesis to explain the spatial clustering of CSCs (Fig. 2B–G) relies on the phenotypic inheritance described above (Fig. 4D). Divisions where sister cells display similar fluorescence intensities may lead to the creation of spatial correlation due to the proximity of daughter and mother cells. The patterning role of this mechanism depends on the degree of signal correlation between sister cells, as well as on the characteristics of cell motility. We need a statistical test to estimate the extent to which cell displacement and lineage can solely explain the clustered pattern associated with bivariate PCFs (Fig. 2B–G). To guarantee that the test accurately accounts for observed cell motility, we used cell trajectories reconstructed from experimental data, while fluorescence variation was obtained from Monte Carlo simulations. At the initial time, cells were attributed the fluorescence signal measured from the experiment. Time evolution of fluorescence is assumed to be driven by two independent processes (see "Materials and methods" section): transitions occur (i) upon division (Fig. 4B), and (ii) during the cell cycle (Fig. 4C).

On the one hand, simulations reproduce well the time evolution of each phenotype at the population level (Fig. 5B). After a transient period of ~24 to 36 h, a steady state is reached with proportions of each phenotype comparable to those observed in experimental data. On the other hand, we find a determination coefficient between mother and offsprings of first, second, and third generation that is weaker than the one measured on experimental data (Fig. 5C). Similar results are obtained for MDA-MB-231 (Supplementary Figure 16).

Importantly, simulations of SUM159PT cells fail to reproduce the spatial clustering of CSCs (Fig. 5E). Contribution of intercellular coupling to phenotypic transitions and CSC reprogramming

Motility and division processes are not sufficient to fully explain the spatial patterning of cancer cell phenotypes. Moreover, phenotypic inheritance across several generations is stronger than expected for a memory-less chain (Fig. 4D). We hypothesize that spatially regulated phenotypic transitions must therefore occur and promote a clustered cell distribution. CSCs display the most pronounced spatial correlation. This pattern, reminiscent of a niche-like effect, is likely to involve cell-to-cell communication.

Supplementary Video 2A–B qualitatively shows how a CSC niche is progressively established from slow fluorescence increase and phenotypic inheritance. We note that fluorescence is stabilized in cells surrounded by CSCs (in the middle of Supplementary Video 2B), while cells in the vicinity of CDCs seem to exhibit lower signal (upper right of Supplementary Video 2B). Discussion

Intra-tumor heterogeneity and cancer stem cells (CSCs) have been recognized to play key roles in tumor resistance and invasiveness. Effectively targeting CSCs for treatment requires a comprehensive understanding of their multifaceted and intricate plasticity. Despite efforts to characterize the transcriptomic and metabolic profiles of CSCs, their self-organizing capacity to spontaneously reform stem-like cell niches and spatially heterogeneous tumors remains poorly understood. In this study, live-cell tracking of cultured breast cancer cells marked with a stemness fluorescent reporter enables us to link phenotypic transitions at the single-cell level with the emergence of spatiotemporal organization of phenotypic heterogeneity at the population level. ALDH1A1 expression/activity is commonly used as a CSC marker, where high expression is associated with a hybrid epithelial/mesenchymal CSC phenotype [8, 64]. In our experiments, careful analysis of fluorescence intensity distributions reveals two well-distinct populations characterized by high and low intensities, and therefore classified as CSC-like and CDC-like phenotypes. Interestingly, a population with intermediate reporter levels is also observed, possibly associated with a metastable transiting phenotype or reflecting the presence of diverse non-stem cell types [8].

The phenotypic organization suggests a fluid hierarchical arrangement of differentiated states, exhibiting an asymmetry between differentiation and dedifferentiation events. Indeed, live-cell tracking of fluorescence signals provides insights into the temporal characteristics of both transitions. Temporal resolution of phenotypic transitions is constrained by the image acquisition interval (1 h) and the degradation timescale of the fused fluorescent proteins (~10 h). Given these resolution limits, dedifferentiation events can be clearly identified during the cell cycle over the course of about ten hours. In contrast, the timescale of differentiation events is more difficult to extract from signal fluctuations, especially when using clustering analyses restricted to a single cell cycle. Nevertheless, decreases in signal intensity are consistently observed just after mitosis, which may be due to chromatin condensation and global transcriptional arrest during mitosis [65], or to symmetric divisions accompanied by the accumulation of differentiation factors [66]. This suggests that differentiation occurs more progressively over timescales much longer than a single cell cycle, or via intermediate or primed states [67, 68]. This is consistent with cell sorting data: selecting only CSCs restores the steady-state composition over more than 10 days, whereas selecting only CDCs leads to reestablishment of the steady state after only a few divisions. Interestingly, although CSC reprogramming has been previously observed in response to radiotherapy or chemotherapy [37, 69], the present work reveals a significant number of reprogramming events occurring within an unperturbed population. A balance between reprogramming and differentiation maintains a dynamic equilibrium between heterogeneous phenotypes at the population level. Such a dynamic equilibrium may in itself account for the existence of an intermediate population. Examination of the clustered time series (Supplementary Figure 9A) indicates that most cells with intermediate fluorescence levels are in transition from CDC to CSC. These transient phenotypes can be interpreted within the framework of the intersection between epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and stemness, which supports epithelial to mesenchymal plasticity (EMP) and involves multiple intermediate states along the EMT spectrum ([12, 70]). These intermediate, transitioning phenotypes retain the capacity to adjust their fate in response to ongoing signals from dynamic niches [67, 68].

The ability to track cancer cell phenotypes in space and time allows inference of system-level self-organizing mechanisms responsible for the spatial patterning of cell types. General principles of spatial self-organization involve diverse cellular processes such as signaling feedback, motility, and division, each contributing deterministic and stochastic components [42–44]. Spatio-temporal point pattern analysis of cell phenotypes clearly identifies clusters of stem-like cells with a typical size of 150 to 300 μm. The formation of CSC clusters and their spatial exclusion from CDCs is found to result from the interplay of phenotypic inheritance and signaling cues provided by neighboring cells. These observations were consistent across two distinct breast cancer cell lines, SUM159PT and MDA-MB-231. In both, symmetric division acts as a local positive feedback mechanism that can generate clusters of similar phenotypes, while cell-cell interactions provide population-level feedback that shapes and stabilizes long-range spatial structures. In contrast, cell motility in these cultures does not act as a morphogenetic cell-sorting process [71], but rather as a source of spatial noise that mixes phenotypes. While our data show that both MDA-MB-231 and SUM159PT cell lines exhibit cell-to-cell variability in motility, we did not find any correlation between motility metrics–such as mean squared displacement or velocity persistence [72]–and the ALDH phenotype as measured by our reporter fluorescence. Cell motility together with intracellular noise thus provide multiple sources of stochasticity influencing the level of spatial correlation and the nature of the patterns. Variation in fluorescent reporter expression and associated noise, along with differing motility between cell lines, might contribute to the less pronounced spatial segregation and reduced cell-cell interaction effects observed in MDA-MB-231 compared to SUM159PT.

Can mechanistic signaling insights be inferred from the spatial correlation of phenotypes and phenotypic changes? The clustering of CSC phenotypes is more pronounced than that of other phenotypes, and reprogramming events are promoted by neighboring CSCs–altogether supporting a lateral induction mechanism. Lateral induction is a well-known mode of cell-cell communication that can generate spatial cell-fate patterns with wavelengths on the order of a dozen cells [73, 74]. In the present case, CSC clustering is observed over distances up to 300 μm. Given a typical diffusivity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D \approx 100$$\end{document}D≈100 μm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2\,{\text{s}}^{-1}$$\end{document}2s-1 for a soluble signaling factor, this range is compatible with paracrine signaling involving ligands with lifetimes of 5 to 15 min [75].

In contrast, the mutual exclusion between CSCs and CDCs occurs over a much shorter length scale (25 to 50 μm), suggesting juxtacrine signaling or paracrine signaling with ligands of short lifetime or diffusivity. However, such short lifetimes are unlikely given the low cell density and the geometry of the culture dish, in which cells are plated on a surface in contact with a large reservoir. The spatial pattern characterized by two distinct correlation length scales is therefore proposed to reflect the involvement of both paracrine and juxtacrine signaling mechanisms in intercellular interactions.

Notch-mediated juxtacrine signaling plays a crucial role in maintaining stemness in CSCs [76], and Jagged-Notch interactions have been proposed to contribute to the spatial segregation of hybrid E/M phenotypes within tumors [31]. Alternatively, CSC self-renewal or differentiation has been proposed to be sensitive to various diffusible signaling factors, such as SHH ligands affecting the Hedgehog pathway [77], cytokines influencing the JAK/STAT pathway [78], or DKK1 ligands modulating the WNT pathway [35]. Based on the present work, we can propose new experiments to gain a more precise understanding of the signaling pathways involved in the spatial partitioning of CSCs and CDCs reported here. Notably, both the single-cell fluorescence distribution and the point pattern analysis are robust to segmentation errors. We verified through numerical simulations that introducing up to 10% segmentation errors (such as missed or false-positive cells) does not significantly affect the bivariate PCF. This robustness makes point pattern analysis suitable for its use, with minimal manual curation, to analyse experiments designed to chemically perturb signaling pathways. First, canonical signaling hubs could be targeted with inhibitors or agonists, and the resulting spatial organization assessed using endpoint bivariate PCF. Second, after identifying candidate pathways through such perturbations, time-lapse experiments with cell tracking could be used to monitor single-cell dynamics and reprogramming events. This would provide further insight into the molecular mechanisms driving phenotypic transitions and clarify the functional role of the intermediate population.

A mathematical modeling approach would be valuable to test these diverse signaling hypotheses. In particular, naïve models of spatiotemporal phenotypic dynamics in cancer cell populations [79] could help identify the contributions of various intercellular mechanisms to the observed spatial patterns and the homeostatic establishment of phenotype proportions. Refining our understanding of the feedback mechanisms that enable CSCs to rapidly reestablish intratumor heterogeneity holds promise for advancing cancer therapy.

While the present study is limited to the use of an ALDH1A1 fluorescent reporter, the methodology developed here can readily be applied to investigate the spatial distribution of other stem-like subpopulations. Such characterization could be performed using static imaging and immunofluorescence staining. Dynamic analysis would require the availability or engineering of fluorescent reporter systems or biosensors for pathway activation. A wide range of such fluorescent tools already exists [80–84], providing opportunities for broader application. Finally, we note that the analytical framework presented in this study is not restricted to breast cancer and could be extended to other solid tumor cell lines.

Conclusions

This study advances our understanding of how cancer cell lines exhibit spatial self-organization reminiscent of stem cell niches. Our results emphasize how phenotypic inheritance following cell division and phenotypic transitions controlled by cell-to-cell communication are essential in maintaining the CSC sub-population within tumors, and how CSCs are prone to spontaneously self-organize into niches even in the absence of a tumor microenvironment. Using cultured cells–a common in vitro system–we explore fundamental properties of rudimentary CSC niches, with potential applications for rapidly testing therapeutic strategies targeting tumor heterogeneity and resistance.

While this study benefits from the controllability of 2D and in vitro cell culture, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of such in vitro models. To assess the relevance of our findings in more physiological contexts, an initial step would be to determine whether the spatial zonation between CSCs and CDCs also emerges spontaneously in 3D spheroid cultures. Spheroid and tumoroid systems could then be incrementally complexified–for example, by introducing nutrient gradients, incorporating other cell types such as cancer-associated fibroblasts, or modulating the extracellular matrix–to progressively bridge the present results with more realistic models of tumor architecture and microenvironment.
