Work experiences, burnout, and psychological distress of frontline health professionals during the COVID-19 omicron epidemic: a multicenter cross-sectional study in Southern China
Authors: Xiuyuan Shi, Pei Xie, Zhiming Zhou, Yunpeng Zhao, Kanru Luo, Gang Liu, Xinxin Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has substantially increased burnout and psychological distress among frontline health professionals. Although this issue has been widely recognized, limited research has explored specific work arrangements during the Omicron epidemic and the associations with burnout and psychological distress among frontline healthcare professionals.MethodsWe conducted a citywide, multicenter cross-sectional survey from July 12 to 26, 2022, during the Omicron outbreak in Shenzhen, China. Participants included physicians, nurses, and health technicians from public hospitals and community health centers. Burnout was measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel (MBI-HSS-MP), and psychological distress was assessed using the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21). Multinomial logistic regression models were constructed to examine associations of specific work arrangements with burnout and psychological distress.ResultsAmong the 3158 respondents, 85.5% reported high levels of burnout and 50.4% reported psychological distress. Over one-third were consistently assigned to nucleic acid sampling (33.1%) or worked more than 5 days per week on containment tasks (32.0%), in addition to routine duties. Frequent assignments to sampling and extended working days were significantly associated with high emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, depression, anxiety, and stress. For example, those always assigned to sampling had higher risks of emotional exhaustion (RRR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.20–3.15) and depression (RRR = 2.01, 95% CI 1.16–3.48). Negative emotions during work and dissatisfaction with current work arrangements were also strongly associated with poor mental health outcomes.ConclusionsThis study highlights the burnout and mental health burden among frontline health professionals linked to specific emergency work arrangements during COVID-19 containment. Our findings underscore the need for targeted institutional design in future health emergencies. Future preparedness planning should integrate psychological sustainability into emergency protocols, including rotational deployments, support measures, and workload balancing strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12960-025-01030-2.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic imposed extraordinary psychological and physical demands on health systems worldwide, particularly on frontline health professionals such as physicians, nurses, and allied health staff [1–3]. A growing body of evidence has reported high levels of burnout, emotional exhaustion, and psychological distress among healthcare professionals during successive waves of infection [4–9]. These outcomes have been associated with extended working hours, critical care responsibilities, limited resources, and the dual burden of pandemic-specific duties (e.g., mass testing, isolation protocols) alongside routine clinical work [10–13]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses—including those based on Chinese populations—have consistently documented moderate to severe mental health challenges across healthcare settings [14]. However, most existing studies have concentrated on prevalence estimates, with limited attention to how specific emergency-related work experiences have contributed to these psychological outcomes.

In China, the COVID-19 containment strategy between early 2021 and December 2022 centered on high-frequency nucleic acid screening, including border, hospital, and large-scale community testing in megacities such as Beijing and Shenzhen [15]. These efforts were highly labor-intensive and resource-consuming [16, 17], requiring nearly all health professionals to assume pandemic-related duties in addition to their routine roles. Emerging evidence suggests that long-term stress among healthcare workers was driven less by direct virus exposure and more by institutional factors such as insufficient workplace support, inadequate safety protection, and social stigmatization [18]. These emergency work arrangements likely heightened occupational strain, contributing to burnout and psychological distress. Yet, empirical research remains scarce on how specific containment-related work tasks shaped the mental health of healthcare professionals, limiting the evidence base for improving health system preparedness and workforce sustainability.

While numerous studies have assessed the mental health status of healthcare workers during the pandemic, relatively few have examined the psychological effects of specific frontline work arrangements, especially within centralized, large-scale epidemic response systems like China’s. This study addresses that gap by linking concrete emergency duties (e.g., community mass testing, isolation management, and dual clinical workloads) with outcomes such as burnout and psychological distress. In doing so, it provides timely evidence to support data-informed planning for sustainable emergency workforce deployment. Such evidence is particularly important for informing future preparedness planning and sustainable workforce design.

Guided by the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which emphasizes the dynamic interplay between occupational demands and available resources, this study investigates how pandemic-related job demands during the Omicron surge in 2022 affected the mental well-being of frontline healthcare professionals [19]. We conducted a citywide, multicenter cross-sectional survey in Shenzhen—a megacity of nearly 20 million residents—with 3158 physicians, nurses, and health technicians across hospitals and all community health centers. As the world transitions into a post-COVID era, it is critical to identify which emergency work arrangements were psychologically sustainable and which may undermine workforce resilience. Our findings offer valuable insights to inform institutional responses that safeguard both the effectiveness of epidemic control and the long-term well-being of health professionals.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Southern University of Science and Technology (approval number: 20220257). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Study design, data collection, and participants

Shenzhen is the third largest megacity in China, following Beijing and Shanghai, with a population of nearly 20 million and one of the highest gross domestic product (GDP) levels nationwide. Approximately 68.5% of its residents are internal migrants and their families. Since May 2021, the city implemented mandatory, citywide nucleic acid testing as a core COVID-19 containment strategy. At the time of data collection (July 2022), Shenzhen was undergoing intensive, population-wide nucleic acid screening in response to local Omicron outbreaks.

This multicenter survey study was conducted between July 12 and July 26, 2022, using an anonymous, self-administered electronic questionnaire. Prospective physicians, registered nurses, and other health technicians working in public hospitals and community health centers across Shenzhen were invited to participate. Recruitment was facilitated through an official notice issued by Shenzhen Community Health Association, with information on the study purpose and design, assurance of confidentiality and voluntary participation, and a OR code linking to the online survey. Informed consent was obtained electronically from all respondents using the same link prior to participation. No financial or material incentives were offered.

The self-developed questionnaire comprised four components: (1) participants’ social-demographic and occupational characteristics; (2) detailed work experiences specifically related to the COVID-19 containment and massive nucleic acid screening; (3) burnout measures; and (4) psychological distress measures. Prior to full deployment, the questionnaire was piloted with a small group of frontline physicians and nurses to ensure clarity, relevance, and content validity. Several strategies were implemented to ensure data quality. The survey was distributed anonymously via WeChat to protect privacy and reduce duplicate responses. Response times were monitored to identify potentially fraudulent submissions. Common method bias was addressed by designing the questionnaire with both positively and negatively worded items.

A total of 3158 frontline health professionals completed the survey, including 1354 physicians, 1315 registered nurses, and 489 other health technicians.

Measures for burnout and psychological distress

Burnout was measured using the Chinese version of the Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) for Medical Personnel of the Maslach Burnout Inventory, which have been used extensively and validated with physicians and nurses in prior studies [20–23]. The scale consisted of three dimensions with 22 items, including emotional exhaustion (EE, 9 items), depersonalization (DP, 5 items), and personal accomplishment (PA, 8 items). Each item was measured by a seven-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 6 (every day) according to the frequency of symptoms. For EE and DP dimensions, higher scores indicate more severe burnout, while for the PA dimensions, lower scores indicate more severe burnout. The severity level of burnout under each dimension was classified to three levels, i.e., no/mild, moderate, and high, as used in prior studies [20–23]. The cutoff score for EE was ≤ 18, 19–26, and ≥ 27, for DP was ≤ 5, 6–9, and ≥ 10, and for PA was ≥ 40, 34–39, and ≤ 33. The Cronbach’s alpha for EE, DP, and PA subscales in this study were 0.953, 0.908, and 0.917, respectively.

Psychological distress was measured by the Chinese version of the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21), which has also been used extensively and validated in many studies [24–27]. The scale included 21 items scoring 3 sub-scales: depression (7 items), anxiety (7 items), and stress (7 items). Each item was measured using a four-point Likert scale. The severity level of psychological distress under each scale was classified into three levels, i.e., normal, moderate, and high, as used in prior studies [24–27]. The cutoff score for depression was 0–9, 10–20, and ≥ 21, for anxiety was 0–7, 8–14, and ≥ 15, and for stress was 0–14, 15–25, and ≥ 26. The Cronbach’s alpha for depression, anxiety, and stress subscales in this study were 0.957, 0.944, and 0.943, respectively.

Work experiences related to omicron containment

Work experiences related to Omicron containment included: (1) the type of work related to Omicron containment; (2) whether being assigned to perform nucleic acid sampling, with a response of yes versus no, (if yes, whether occasionally being assigned vs. always being assigned); (3) hours worked per day for Omicron containment (< 8, 8 vs. > 8); (4) days worked per week for Omicron containment (< 3, 3–5 vs. > 5); (5) whether having negative emotions such as worry, fear, and nervousness, with a response of yes versus no; and (6) whether being satisfied with the work arrangement, with a response of yes versus no.

Covariates included participants’ socio-demographic and occupational characteristics: gender (male vs. female), marital status (married vs. unmarried), educational level (associate degree or below, bachelor’s degree vs. master’s or doctoral degree), monthly income in Chinese Yuan after tax (< 4000, 4000–6999, 7000–10,000 vs. > 10,000), occupation (physician, nurse, vs. other health technician), professional rank (none, junior-level, middle-level vs. senior-level), working institution (community health center vs. hospital), and whether holding an administrative position (yes vs. no).

Statistical analysis

We reported the frequency and distribution of work experiences related to Omicron containment, as well as the prevalence of burnout and psychological distress among participating health professionals. Because our outcome measures were nominal variables with more than two categories, we used multinomial logistic regression to examine the associations between specific work experiences related to COVID-19 containment and the likelihood of reporting burnout, categorized as no/mild, moderate, and high, using no/mild as the reference group, or the likelihood of reporting psychological distress levels, categorized as normal, moderate, high, using normal as the reference group. All models controlled for participants’ socio-demographic characteristics as mentioned earlier. Because the model estimated the relative risk of each outcome category compared to the reference category, relative risk ratios (RRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were therefore reported. Variance inflation factor (VIF) test was conducted to assess multicollinearity among all independent variables across models. The mean VIF score was 1.33, indicating no significant multicollinearity among independent variables. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

About 23.9% of participants reported that they worked more than 8 h per day and 32.0% reported that they worked more than 5 days per week for epidemic control in additional to their routine work. A majority of participants reported that they had negative emotions (such as worry, fear, anxiety, and nervousness) after participating in epidemic control (70.2%). Among those who reported having negative emotions, the top-rated reasons were highly intensive working conditions (68.9%), worry about exposing family members to viruses (55.2%), worry about risk spillover due to inappropriate operation (54.4%), and worry about being self-infected (48.1%).

Associations of burnout and psychological distress with work experiences related to omicron containment

The multinominal logistic regression analysis showed that, compared to participants who were not assigned to perform nucleic acid sampling, those who were always being assigned were more likely to report high emotional exhaustion (RRR = 1.95, 95% CI = 1.20, 3.15), high depersonalization (RRR = 1.58, 95% CI = 1.07, 2.35), high depression (RRR = 2.01, 95% CI = 1.16, 3.48), high anxiety (RRR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.06, 2.67), and high stress (RRR = 2.08, 95% CI = 1.09, 3.95) (Tables 4 and 5). Those who were always being assigned were less likely to report moderate personal accomplishment (RRR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.22, 0.99), no or mild personal accomplishment (RRR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.25, 0.87). Discussion

This study provides detailed empirical evidence on the specific work experiences of frontline health professionals during China’s Omicron containment period, and how these experiences were associated with burnout and psychological distress. We found a high prevalence of emotional exhaustion (85.5%) [28–30] and considerable levels of depressive symptoms (45.1%) and anxiety (44.2%) [11, 31–34], comparable to or even exceeding previous reports from the early phase of the pandemic in China. These findings align with international studies suggesting that burnout may persist or worsen during prolonged public health emergencies, particularly when healthcare workers are repeatedly exposed to high-intensity pandemic-related tasks [35, 36].

A key contribution of our study lies in its focus on concrete work arrangements during emergency mobilization, particularly nucleic acid sampling and dual workloads. Nearly all participants reported involvement in nucleic acid testing, with over one-third being continuously assigned. A substantial proportion also reported extended working hours—32.0% worked more than 5 days per week and 23.9% more than 8 h per day for epidemic containment—while continuing routine duties. These findings echo prior research indicating that excessive workloads and prolonged duty cycles are significant drivers of burnout and job dissatisfaction [11]. One-third of respondents reported dissatisfaction with their work arrangements, citing factors such as long hours, unstable schedules, and high-intensity working conditions.

Our results further show that negative emotions, such as worry, fear, and nervousness, were prevalent in over 70% of participants, with infection-related concerns—such as fear of self-infection or transmitting the virus to family—being the most commonly reported causes. These psychological responses have been widely recognized as risk factors for mental health deterioration among healthcare professionals in global studies [37–39]. However, unlike most prior research that relied on broad symptom assessment, our study captured granular data on specific work exposures and responsibilities, enabling a more targeted understanding of which duties were most strongly associated with burnout and psychological distress.

When analyzing associations between specific work experiences and psychological outcomes, we found that those who were repeatedly assigned to nucleic acid testing or worked more than five days per week were significantly more likely to report symptoms of burnout, depression, anxiety, and stress. Dissatisfaction with work arrangements was also a strong predictor. These results suggest that rigid and repetitive deployment patterns may amplify emotional burden and reduce workforce resilience [40, 41]. A rotational mechanism that distributes pandemic-related duties more equitably—both in terms of time and personnel—could help mitigate psychological harm and support sustainable workforce engagement. We also found that, professionals who were continuously involved in nucleic acid sampling were also more likely to report higher levels of personal accomplishment, possibly reflecting a sense of mission and social responsibility [42]. This highlights the importance of recognition, institutional support, and psychological affirmation in reducing the negative impacts of high work demands.

In addition, our findings underscore the importance of emotion monitoring and institutional psychological support. Negative emotional states were significantly associated with all indicators of burnout and psychological distress. This suggests that real-time emotion tracking, early screening, and timely mental health interventions should be incorporated into emergency workforce management [43]. Capacity-building efforts, such as regular professional training for public health emergencies—even outside of pandemic periods—may further strengthen health professionals’ confidence and self-protection awareness, thereby reducing negative emotional reactions and improving resilience [44].

Policy implications

Our findings offer important insights for future pandemic preparedness and health workforce resilience. Emergency work arrangements that disproportionately increase workload and psychological strain can undermine the well-being and stability of frontline health professionals. Future preparedness strategies must therefore embed psychological sustainability into core planning—by ensuring clear communication, equitable and flexible task assignment, rotational deployment, and institutional mechanisms for rest and recovery, alongside accessible mental health support. These measures are essential not only for maintaining individual well-being but also for ensuring continuity and quality of care in the face of prolonged public health emergencies.

This study provides empirical evidence to guide institutional improvements in emergency workforce planning. By linking specific containment-related duties, such as repeated nucleic acid sampling, long working hours, and rigid assignments, with elevated risks of burnout and psychological distress, our findings underscore the need for data-informed personnel deployment. Health institutions should implement flexible rotation systems, avoid assigning the same individuals to prolonged high-intensity tasks, and establish mechanisms for proactive emotional monitoring. Integrated psychological support, such as routine mental health check-ins, peer support networks, and access to counseling, should be incorporated into emergency preparedness and response protocols to protect workforce well-being and ensure operational continuity.

As global health systems prepare for future pandemics, this study highlights the critical importance of placing workforce well-being at the center of emergency response planning. Policies that prioritize flexible deployment, early detection of emotional distress, and institutional psychological support will help reduce staff attrition, maintain care quality, and enhance system resilience. Lessons from China’s Omicron response offer valuable insights for building more sustainable, adaptive, and human-centered health systems worldwide.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, although Shenzhen represents a typical case of China’s intensive COVID-19 containment strategy, the generalizability of our findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, as with all retrospective surveys, this study is subject to recall, selection, response, and social desirability biases, which may have influenced participants’ reporting and affected internal validity. Third, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to infer causal relationships between work experiences and psychological outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the lasting effects of pandemic-related stressors over time. Fourth, although we explored multiple psychological outcomes, we did not apply formal corrections for multiple comparisons. This might raise the potential risk for Type I error; however, we focused on consistent patterns across related outcomes. Lastly, we did not employ structural equation modeling (SEM), which could have enabled a more integrated analysis of latent constructs and mediating mechanisms. Future studies may consider SEM approaches to better elucidate the complex interplay between work-related stressors, emotional responses, and mental health outcomes.

Conclusions

This study offers novel and detailed insights into the specific work experiences of frontline health professionals during the COVID-19 Omicron containment in China and their associations with burnout and psychological distress. By systematically linking concrete emergency duties, such as repeated nucleic acid testing and extended working hours, with adverse mental health outcomes, our findings fill a critical gap in the literature beyond general prevalence estimates. The high levels of burnout and psychological distress observed highlight the urgent need for more sustainable workforce management in prolonged public health crises. This study provides empirical evidence to inform institutional policies that prioritize flexible and equitable workforce deployment, proactive emotional monitoring, and integrated psychological support systems. Such measures are essential to maintain frontline workers’ well-being, reduce burnout, and preserve health system resilience during future emergencies. As the global health community moves into a post-COVID era, these findings offer valuable lessons for designing more human-centered and adaptive health systems capable of withstanding the challenges of future pandemics.
