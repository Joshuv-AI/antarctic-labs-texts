Fast highway abandoned object detection via block-based multi-group foreground extraction
Authors: Dian Liu, Haoxiang Wang, Xiujie Zhang, Xingming Zhang, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbandoned objects on highways pose a significant risk of causing severe traffic accidents. Current abandoned object detection technologies are limited by hardware constraints and real-time processing requirements in complex highway environments. To address these challenges, we propose a Universal foreground extraction framework consisting of Block-based frame selection and Multi-Group foreground Detection, called UBMG, which effectively alleviates false alarms caused by either illumination changes on the highway, traffic targets, or road markings. Specifically, two modules of block preprocessing and adaptive size filtering are first designed, followed by a static target matching module, to extract candidate targets. To accurately distinguish abandoned objects from other entities, a candidate verification strategy is proposed involving traffic target elimination, road noise elimination, and trajectory discrimination. This framework can seamlessly integrate with existing pixel-wise foreground detection algorithms and demonstrate high efficiency in practical applications. In addition, we have established a comprehensive video dataset of highway abandoned objects, named HAO, under various conditions (e.g.,lighting conditions, camera movements, and object occlusions) for thorough evaluation. Extensive experiments on HAO and public ABODA datasets demonstrate that the UBMG framework can perform robust and real-time detection of abandoned objects, especially outperforming the state-of-the-art methods in the HAO dataset and showing good performance on ABODA.Keywords: Abandoned object detection, Highway, Foreground extraction, Trajectories tracking, Real-time processingSubject terms: Engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Discarded objects or debris from passing vehicles on highways pose severe hazards to traffic safety. An intelligent obstacle avoidance and alert system on highways can significantly decrease the incidence of traffic mishaps and enhance the operational efficiency of highways. However, existing abandoned object detection research within video surveillance systems focuses on scenes where pedestrians frequently appear, especially in public and crowded places such as airports, buildings, or railway stations1, with scant attention given to highways involving vehicle traffic. In addition, publicly available datasets of abandoned objects usually contain objects like trash, luggage, and other discarded items in indoor/outdoor areas1, while neglecting specific objects in highway scenarios. Therefore, effective solutions and comprehensive benchmarks of highway abandoned object detection are crucial for ensuring the safety of passengers’ lives and property, especially in the context of the rapid development of autonomous driving.

Current abandoned object detection methods comprise two principal components: foreground detection and object validation. The extraction of foreground objects is primarily achieved through background subtraction, utilizing background modeling algorithms. Predominant techniques include single-background models2 and dual-background models3, which exhibit sensitivity to dynamic background changes, camera jitter, and the appearance of ghost images4. designed a shadow detection framework through foreground detection and cast shadow detection to reduce the negative effects caused by the cast shadows of vehicles in traffic surveillance video analysis. The object validation phase principally considers pedestrian-related factors to identify objects such as garbage, luggage, and pedestrian-abandoned items. A variety of techniques are employed to minimize false detections, focusing on large objects with regular outlines, such as edge clustering5, pedestrian detection6, backtracking7, similarity comparison8–10, and deep neural networks11. Recently, some deep learning research has been conducted on improving the detection performance against challenging outdoor conditions on highways, but they highly rely on GPU acceleration or carefully optimized algorithms12. For example13, utilized YOLO to detect individuals with luggage, while14 proposed a deep learning framework tailored for detecting outdoor hand luggage as a proxy for abandoned objects15. proposed an efficient object localization and classification method for edge devices through seamlessly blending the strengths of semantic segmentation and object detection. However, these deep models usually require extensive training datasets, which are unsuitable for highway contexts due to unpredictable object categories. Additionally, they do not have universality and cannot be seamlessly integrated into existing systems and devices, increasing implementation costs.

In general, there are several issues when deploying abandoned object detection systems on highways. Firstly, methods need to be highly efficient, enabling them to detect multiple video streams simultaneously with limited hardware resources16. Common hardware components on highways are roadside units with elementary computing power, either based on CPUs or GPUs. Meanwhile, since there are thousands of surveillance cameras along the highway, a universal detection framework is required that can flexibly integrate with various detectors and devices. Secondly, highway scenes are susceptible to complicated illumination variations, thereby degrading detection performance4. For example, sudden lighting changes in daytime environments can easily cause background modeling failures, while lighting changes in nighttime or tunnel scenes pose a significant challenge to both object detection models and background models. Finally, small traffic targets or lane markings at the far end of highways are prone to triggering false alarms of littering due to the wide range of monitoring areas17.

In this study, we propose a universal framework for highway abandoned object detection by performing block-based multi-group foreground extraction, named UBMG, which significantly reduces computational burden while preserving the detection accuracy. Following the framework, we revolutionize the detection procedure in terms of block-based frame selection, multi-group foreground detection, static target matching, and candidate verification, enabling robust and fast abandoned object detection in complex highway scenarios. This framework can be used for the foreground detection process in general scenarios. Besides, a comprehensive video dataset of highway abandoned objects (HAO) is established to provide sufficient training data and extensive experimental validation.

Related work

Anomalous object detection

The detection of abandoned objects on highways can be considered a subset of anomalous object detection, particularly crucial within the context of autonomous driving. In recent years, remarkable strides have been achieved in the realm of object detection methodologies, with an array of studies earmarking their application towards the detection of anomalous objects. Leading-edge techniques, including YOLO18, Faster R-CNN19, and SSD20, have demonstrated adeptness in delineating object boundaries via bounding boxes for pre-defined categories. Kraft et al.21 introduced a cost-effective approach leveraging drones to amass a dataset of refuse, thereby crafting a platform for garbage detection. This system, underpinned by a deep neural network, is adept at identifying discarded waste and autonomously formulating collection trajectories. Cortesi et al.22 unveiled an enhancement to object detection paradigms based on the Faster R-CNN framework, integrating a feature pyramid network. This refinement was achieved by amalgamating disparate layers through multiplication, harnessing focal loss to rectify anchor imbalances, and introducing an attention module to augment the entire network’s performance, culminating in a litter detection accuracy surpassing 98%. In a parallel vein, Yang et al.23 put forward a pioneering technique for the automated discernment and extraction of litter from Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) 3D point clouds, highlighting commendable resilience across a spectrum of natural environments, litter types, vegetation densities, and environmental perturbations. Pfeiffer et al.24 embarked on utilizing both YOLOv5 and Faster R-CNN algorithms to pinpoint objects along Malta’s coastline through drone-captured footage. Zhou et al.25 innovated within the tracking domain by integrating a heat map into the CenterTrack model, which processes the thermal signatures of detected objects across sequential frames to ascertain the directional shifts of targets. Peicheng Shi et al.26 proposed a Transformer Feature Fusion Encoder (TFFEn) for joint depth–image modeling, a deformable convolution-based enhancement (FEDC) guided by depth confidence to better handle truncated objects, and a Depth-perception NMS (Dp-NMS) that fuses classification and depth confidences to improve proposal ranking. Xinlong Dong et al.27 introduced a CNN-transformer adaptive feature fusion network that uses a convolutional transformation adaptive fusion kernel to adaptively fuse local CNN and global Transformer features and an encoder-decoder to propagate this fused information across stages. At the same time, research on high performance algorithms for small object detection has made progress12. Amudhan et al.16 proposed a novel hypermetropic Convolutional Neural Network that balances performance and cost to improve small object detection; Haotian Liu et al.28 proposed a knowledge distillation framework with masked scale-aware feature distillation and local attention distillation to enhance small-model performance, particularly for improving the detection of tiny objects with high accuracy and efficiency. However, a critical gap remains in these methodologies’ applicability to highway scenarios, particularly due to the ambiguous nature of abandoned object categories. This limitation underscores the necessity for specialized approaches tailored to highway abandoned object detection, where conventional methods may falter in efficacy.

Detecting objects that are not present in the training dataset, or those belonging to unknown categories, is a significant challenge in the domain of highway abandoned object detection. Lis et al.29 introduced a method utilizing a disparity network to highlight differences between original input frames and re-synthesized images from semantic maps. While disparity-based approaches offer more reliability compared to uncertainty estimation methods, they are also prone to a higher rate of false positives. Building on this, Giancarlo et al.30 proposed a novel anomaly segmentation framework that leverages both the uncertainty inherent in semantic maps and the dissimilarity between original and re-synthesized images. This method achieves notable enhancements in the detection of anomalous objects within autonomous driving scenarios, albeit at the expense of increased computational demand due to the necessity for pixel-wise analysis. Nayak et al.31 contributed a structured and exhaustive review of the most recent advancements in video anomaly detection, with a particular focus on applications of deep learning. They introduced a graphical classification technique tailored for video anomaly detection that utilizes deep learning, marking a significant step forward in the field. Similarly, Vojir et al.32 presented an innovative approach tailored for autonomous driving contexts, which calculates anomaly scores for individual pixels based on discrepancies in road structure similarity, leveraging a combination of a reconstruction module and a semantic segmentation network to identify unknown objects. Alternatively, Bogdoll et al.33 explored the integration of various sophisticated detection models to analyze data from both LiDAR and cameras in anomalous object detection. Nevertheless, such methods are less effective in the specific case of detecting abandoned objects on highways. This reduced efficacy can be attributed to limitations in image quality and the distance from which monitoring devices can operate on highways.

Abandoned object detection

As mentioned above, the detection of abandoned objects in surveillance video streams is a critical task that encompasses two primary phases. In the initial phase, researchers have explored various approaches to segregate foreground entities from the background. St-Charles et al.34 adopted the classical method of single-background subtraction to delineate foreground objects. Lin et al.35 enhanced this approach by incorporating a finite state machine that captures temporal transitions of static foreground pixels. Despite these advancements, methods based on single or dual-background subtraction are prone to errors caused by ghost effects, changes in lighting, camera jitter, and other disturbances. To mitigate these limitations, some studies have shifted towards more sophisticated background modeling techniques. Ilias et al.5 introduced a method that relies on edge background subtraction and edge clustering to extract stable edge masks, which is demonstrated to be resilient to occlusion and lighting variations. Concurrently, the advent of deep learning has spurred innovations in this domain, with Kim et al.14 proposing the CNN-HLDNet, a deep learning framework tailored for the detection of outdoor hand luggage as a proxy for abandoned objects. These methods primarily focus on addressing the challenges posed by illumination variations and occlusions in indoor settings but often fall short in outdoor environments.

The second phase involves the verification of whether an extracted object has indeed been abandoned. Yun et al.36 proposed a new framework to detect dumping action from cameras, in which background subtraction and human joint estimation are utilized to model the distance relationship between pedestrians and objects to determine dumping action. Sarker et al.37 introduced a real-time monitoring system that employs shadow removal and background subtraction in human and object detection, integrating Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) to recognize dumping actions. Santad et al.13 utilized YOLO to detect individuals with luggage and track their movements to identify suspicious activities indicative of abandonment, such as placing luggage down and walking away. Shyam38 trained a detection network with backpacks and handbags as a training dataset to detect abandoned objects. Mohiu et al.39 applied a frame-differential technique to ascertain the historical location of luggage, enabling tracking of moving objects. However, the unique challenges posed by highway environments, such as the extended distances of monitoring scenes (often exceeding one hundred meters), dynamic backgrounds, and frequent camera jitter due to passing vehicles or strong winds, significantly complicate the application of existing methods. These conditions lead to a high rate of false positives, complicating subsequent analysis. Additionally, the diverse range and size of objects typically abandoned on highways, ranging from small bottles and paper pieces to tires and vehicle components, necessitate real-time detection solutions tailored to these specific contexts. The existing methodologies, primarily developed for urban or indoor scenarios, are ill-equipped to address the nuances of highway environments, highlighting the need for innovative approaches in this area.

Fast highway abandoned object detection via UBMG framework

Overview

In this section, we propose a universal foreground extraction framework, involving block-based frame selection and multi-group foreground detection, and specifically refine it for efficient detection of abandoned objects on highways. The complete method includes four integral components, as shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, the block-based frame selection module contains road masking, block-based extraction, and block preprocessing to reduce computational load. Subsequently, blocks among different frames are grouped and are subjected to foreground detection, respectively, in terms of background modelling, foreground mask, morphological operation, and adaptive size filtering for multi-group, to identify suspicious targets. Then, a static target matching strategy is applied to obtain target candidates by accommodating the possibility of multiple matches per target, enhancing the precision of object identification. Finally, non-abandoned objects within target candidates are discarded based on traffic object classification and binary road noise removal, while trajectory discrimination facilitates the distinction of real targets by predicting transition states of objects, allowing for the meticulous detection and analysis of abandoned objects in highway scenarios.

The proposed UBMG framework for highway abandoned object detection.

Block-based frame selection

Foreground detection is a key component to model every pixel of the image and update the state of all pixels iteratively to determine whether they are foreground or background in traditional abandoned object detection algorithms40. According to our preliminary experiments, focusing only on updating pixels with detected changes and disregarding stable pixels significantly reduces computational load while preserving detection accuracy, because comparing pixel changes demands significantly less computational power than modeling background variations. Following this observation, we propose a block-based frame selection module to ensure low computational costs and high efficiency for highway scenarios. The module includes three parts: road masking, block-based extraction, and block preprocessing.

Road masking aims to extract images of road areas, further reducing computational requirements. Considering that highway monitoring equipment is typically installed in fixed locations, the monitored areas remain constant. Thus, we adopt Deeplabv3+41 for extracting road regions from video frames, which processes input frames to yield a binary image delineating the road area, as shown in Fig. 2. This approach significantly decreases the computational load and minimizes the likelihood of false alarms.

Multi-group foreground detection

To handle static foreground objects that persist throughout the sequence, we develop a multi-group foreground detection module. This module constructs a background model and produces a foreground mask with progressively higher detection frequency for stationary targets. Firstly the input sequences from block-based frame selection are evenly divided into nGroup groups ,,. Block-based background modeling is then applied independently to each valid block in each group. Subsequently, morphological operations, as per Formula (2), with the only difference that is 5 × 5 square structure and Ostu43 method for converting the grayscale image into a binary image are performed on the entire image obtained by merging foreground blocks to reduce noise. Instead of using a fixed threshold, an adaptive Gaussian model filters the target by size to further eliminate noise. The final foreground mask images are generated by merging the individual block masks. Figure 8 shows the total flow of multi-group foreground detection with nRow=nCol=5 and nGroup=2.

The universal foreground extraction framework consisting of block-based frame selection and multi-group foreground detection can reduce the computation time while maintaining the effectiveness of foreground detection. Firstly, the block-based frame selection algorithm divides the image into multiple blocks, and then uses a fast block content comparison method to identify the blocks with changes. Only the blocks with changes are subjected to continuous multi-frame foreground analysis. Due to the significant spatiotemporal imbalance of highway traffic, there will always be image blocks with no content changes in the surveillance videos along the highway, thus saving time for foreground detection. Then multiple consecutive blocks will be evenly divided into different groups for foreground detection. Considering that this step is to extract foreground targets in a stationary state while the scenes on highways mainly involve vehicle motion, multi-group foreground detection is more conducive to eliminating foreground error boxes caused by slow vehicle movement and improving our accuracy, which is also reflected in Sect. 4.5. Finally, the adaptive size filtering method can effectively solve the problem of target distortion in monitoring images caused by monitoring angles, avoiding the erroneous filtering of abandoned objects caused by fixed thresholds. In Sect. 4.5, we also conducted a theoretical performance analysis of our framework UBMG, providing theoretical support for its effectiveness. According to our experiments, the overall performance is significantly improved, and the processing time is decreased by 44.3% with parameters set to nRow = nCol = 5 and nGroup = 2, compared to standard foreground detection workflows (detailed in Sect. 4.5).

Static target matching

To identify potential suspicious targets for spills, we first acquire stable prospective targets. The next step processes these detected entities to create stable candidates, ready for further verification phases. This candidate generation process is inspired by the matching strategy of associating detected and predicted targets to create trackable objects. However, our objective is to identify and maintain stable objects that remain in the same location over a specified duration, eliminating the need to predict new track locations. To achieve this, we employ both Intersection over Union (IoU) and feature-based metrics to assess object similarity using a refined static target matching algorithm, referred to as Algorithm 2, which generates stable candidate matches. Our approach integrates two principal similarity assessment techniques: IoUMatch and RepNetMatch. IoUMatch calculates the IoU between objects for similarity. Meanwhile, RepNetMatch uses features extracted from RepNet45 to compute cosine similarity.

Candidate verification

In this subsection, we present a methodology for tracking the trajectories of abandoned objects, identifying their transition from motion to stillness to confirm their validity. This approach effectively reduces interference from irrelevant entities, such as shadows and light reflections, through a three-step process: traffic object removal, binary noise elimination on road surfaces, and motion analysis for process verification.

Traffic object removal

Highways often contain numerous traffic entities, such as vehicles and construction workers, which may trigger false alarms for abandoned objects. These entities are filtered out using object detection techniques. Here, we employ YOLOv8s44 for object detection, leveraging its performance of approximately 170 FPS (at 640 × 640 resolution). The official COCO128-based training model is converted to TensorRT for enhanced inference speed.

Binary road noise removal

To streamline the extraction of object trajectories, we implement a binary classification framework to distinguish objects from road surfaces, mitigating foreground distortions caused by artifacts and watermarks. We define two classes (road vs. non-road) and use ResNet34 as the backbone for feature extraction and classification. Image samples of size 96 × 96 are used to fine-tune the pre-trained model, which processes foreground regions to classify them as either part of the road or an object.

Trajectory discrimination

While the preceding methods help reduce false positives, they are not entirely sufficient. Missed detections may occur due to limitations in object detection, such as distant traffic entities that are difficult to detect. Additionally, lane markings or signs may be misclassified during binary classification. Such errors differ in trajectory from abandoned objects. Stationary objects, like vehicles stopped illegally or lane markings, remain in the same position for extended periods, whereas abandoned objects typically exhibit a trajectory pattern of moving to rest. Significant progress has been made in transformer-based tracking algorithms, including ReTrackVLM46 that integrates multimodal embedding from a visual language model (VLM) with a zero-shot re-identification (ReID) module to enhance tracking accuracy and robustness. To further refine detection, we use a lightweight tracking algorithm based on transformer combined with fast SVM discrimination for trajectory analysis due to hardware limitations and the need for multiple video analyses in highway scenes. We propose a trajectory discrimination module that utilizes target tracking to capture the trajectory, extracts trajectory intersection features, and employs an SVM model for classification to effectively filter out non-abandoned objects, as presented in Fig. 10.

The flow of trajectory discrimination. The video is extracted at 1fps for processing. Trajectory discrimination is performed on the box object in Frame 30. The corresponding block changes in Frame 22, and the object is detected as foreground in Frame 26. Then it is identified as a stationary foreground target by static object matching in Frame 30. After obtaining the trajectory of the target through stark-lighting, positional features are extracted for discrimination by SVM.

To capture the target’s trajectory comprehensively and minimize tracking errors due to occlusion, we select three distinct time periods for trajectory extraction. In the first stage, when a change is detected within a block, the block-based frame extraction algorithm records the first frame where the BlockChange function returns True as the beginning of the block-change phase. We then select m frames in reverse order to construct the tracking image sequence for this phase. In the second stage, the frame initially detected by foreground detection is designated as the start of the object-track phase, from which m frames are again selected in reverse order. In the third stage, the current frame marks the beginning of the object-station phase, and m frames are similarly chosen in reverse order. These three image sequences are combined to create a comprehensive sequence for tracking and are passed into the next stage to derive the target’s trajectory. Examples of the three stages across various scenarios are illustrated in Fig. 11. By sampling images from these time periods, we can obtain a complete trajectory of the abandoned object from its motion to its stationary state. In scenarios a, b, and c, which involve litter objects, the trajectory information from movement to stillness is captured across the three stages. To determine the trajectory of an identified object candidate, we employ the STARK-Lightning tracker47, which is built on an advanced tracking architecture that integrates an encoder-decoder transformer. This tracker achieves performance six times greater than Siam R-CNN, delivering real-time speeds of 200–300 FPS on one RTX TITAN GPU. We designate the target in the current frame as a template and proceed through the image sequence to track its trajectory. The presence of the tracked target is evaluated based on the tracking confidence; if this confidence falls below a specified threshold , the position for that frame is marked as empty.

In the feature extraction step, we integrate GIOU48 and IOU methods to capture features of the target’s trajectory. If box A indicates the final position in the discrimination frame, box B represents the target’s position derived from trajectory tracking, and C is the area where they intersect. Trajectory features are calculated using GIOU(A, B), IOU(A, B), IOU(A, C), and IOU(B, C). If the target position is empty, GIOU is set to -1. Finally, the feature values with a dimension 12*m are obtained as input data for SVM model training and discrimination, where m is the number of frames tracked in each stage.

Discussion

In this part, we analyze the distinctions between the UBMG approach we propose and other methods for abandoned object detection, such as Majchrowska49, Amrutham50, Luna1, Nyarko51, Giancarlo30 and Lis52.

1) difference from Majchrowska49 and Amrutham50: Both the EfficientDet model in49 and the YOLO based approach50 belong to strongly supervised object detection frameworks, which performance heavily relies on large-scale annotated datasets. However, highway abandoned object datasets are extremely difficult to collect, resulting in poor generalization of such methods to unseen object categories. In contrast, UBMG innovatively adopts an unsupervised static object detection logic, which requires no labels to extract candidate objects, and further integrates the motion characteristics of debris to effectively filter and recognize them. The proposed UBMG framework not only overcomes the dependence on annotated samples but also ensures stronger adaptability and practicality when dealing with diverse debris in open highway environments.

2) difference from Luna1 and Nyarko51: Both the candidate region generation algorithm in1 and the dual-background model in51 rely on pixel-level differences across the entire image for foreground segmentation. This full-frame background modeling results in high computational costs and limited adaptability to complex scenarios. In contrast, UBMG introduces several innovations: it reduces the computational burden of foreground detection through a block-level parallel strategy, and it enhances robustness under rapid illumination changes by combining illumination-adaptive preprocessing (i.e., CLAHE) with multi-group foreground modeling, achieving more efficient and adaptive foreground extraction while maintaining reliable detection performance.​.

3) difference from Giancarlo30 and Lis52: The anomaly detection method in30 and the model based on perceptual difference maps in52 both identify anomalies through computationally intensive pixel-level feature modeling. Such approaches are associated with high complexity and encounter limited generalization in highway scenarios, where camera installation heights and angles vary significantly. In contrast, UBMG emphasizes lightweight design and practical applicability through foreground extraction combined with motion-trajectory discrimination, enabling efficient debris detection without complex pixel-level computations. Overall, UBMG not only reduces computational complexity but also enhances robustness and adaptability in diverse highway environments, making it more suitable for real-world deployment.

The HAO dataset and experiments

Video-based highway abandoned object dataset

Currently, as far as we know, no publicly available dataset focuses specifically on abandoned object detection in highway scenes. To address this gap and provide a solid foundation for evaluating our proposed algorithm, we introduce the video-based dataset of highway abandoned objects (HAO). HAO consists of 103 short video sequences captured by highway surveillance cameras, each carefully selected to include at least one abandoned object. The sequences are drawn from an extensive library of surveillance footage, ensuring diversity in object types and environmental conditions.

The videos come in various resolutions, including 720 × 480 and 704 × 576, with a frame rate of 25 fps. The dataset covers a wide range of abandoned object sizes, from as small as 10 × 10 pixels to as large as 50 × 50 pixels, featuring diverse objects such as boxes, tires, traffic cones, bottles, bags, and paper. Recognizing the critical role of environmental conditions in detection accuracy, HAO is categorized into three distinct scenarios: pavement-daytime, pavement-nighttime, and tunnel. This classification reflects the unique challenges of different lighting conditions, such as illumination changes, video jitter, occlusion, and other realistic factors that may affect detection algorithm performance. Detailed descriptions of sample sequences are provided in Fig. 12; Table 1. The details of the dataset used to train the binary noise removal model for non-road objects and roads are described in Table 2; Fig. 13; the data was collected from the Internet and surveillance videos. Training data for the non-road objects category includes boxes, tires, road cones, bottles, bags, paper, etc., mainly from the internet; meanwhile, data for road categories includes lane markings, water streaks, reflective pavement, road shadows, etc., manually cropped from the surveillance videos in real highway scenes.

Implementation details

The experiments were conducted under Python 3.8 and PyTorch 1.12.0 on an Ubuntu 22.04 LTS system, equipped with a Quadro RTX 5000 GPU and an Intel Xeon Silver 4210 CPU (2.20 GHz). All videos are preprocessed to 1fps before conducting experiments. 30% of the data samples containing multiple scenarios are selected for SVM training in trajectory discrimination and the remaining samples are used for testing. The area size range of the targets to be detected is required to be within [10*10,50*50].

In UBMG, nRow and nCol are both chosen as 5 while nGroup is set to 2 and nExtracted is set to 4. KNN-based background model algorithm is implemented in OpenCV-Python library with version 4.8.1. In the phase of static target matching, IoUMatch applies a similarity threshold of 0.3. Meanwhile, RepNetMatch computes cosine similarity with a threshold set at 0.15. In the phase of candidate verification, the confidence level of YOLOv8 detection is 0.3 and the confidence of Stark-Lightning is set to 0.15. The number of frames tracked in each stage is selected 4. SVM training details are described in Sect. 4.6.

Effects of the trajectory discrimination module: For the trajectory discrimination process, we collected target trajectories from 31 training videos in our dataset. This yielded a total of 5,516 trajectory records, including 334 true trajectories of annotated objects. To increase the sample size, we did not remove duplicate trajectories from repeated alarms. We employed the SVC method from the sklearn library to build an SVM model, using the polynomial kernel function and a penalty coefficient of 5. Besides, it can be concluded that the three-stage trajectory analysis effectively eliminates erroneous targets, achieving accuracy and recall rates above 90% for label = 0 across various trajectory features. Incorporating GIoU features further enhances detection performance, with the optimal indicators achieved using a combination of GIoU (A, B), IoU (A, B), IoU (A, C), and IoU (B, C) features. Consequently, these four area-based features are selected for SVM training with an input dimension of 48. In our procedure, we use TRACLUS59 and various strategy methods to replace our trajectory discrimination method for comparative evaluation, as shown in Table 16. The TRACLUS algorithm segments trajectories into line segments, clustering these to identify key sub-trajectories. Sub-trajectories containing three or fewer trajectories are flagged as abandoned objects. Strategy 1 assesses target movement frequency, where IoU < 0.3 indicates movement. If the movement ratio exceeds 0.4, the target is classified as being in motion and considered an abandoned object. Strategy 2 divides each trajectory into two equal segments and applies Strategy 1 to evaluate movement in each. If the first segment is in motion while the second is stationary, the target is identified as a spilled object. Failed cases

Apart from the significant performance improvement, our method may encounter recognition errors in some highly challenging situations, which reflects the limitations of the current model in practical applications. It mainly includes the following aspects:

Inability to detect rolling targets: Since the goal of our algorithm is to detect stationary objects abandoned on the road, continuously rolling targets such as paper blown by the wind will be filtered by our static target matching module, as shown in Fig.19a.

Sensitivity to small or distant objects: Small-scale or far-distance objects often lack salient features and are easily overwhelmed by the background. Additionally, small objects with visual similarity to the road surface may be mistakenly filtered out during binary road noise removal, resulting in false negatives, as shown in Fig. 19b.

Generalization challenges and model limitations

Although UBMG achieves significant performance improvement compared to existing methods, its generalization ability remains challenged by several factors inherent to complex highway environments. These limitations are complementary to the failure cases described in Section X and provide a broader perspective on the model’s applicability:

Cross-scenario generalization: UBMG demonstrates reduced adaptability when deployed across scenarios with markedly different traffic densities, camera perspectives, or road structures. For example, modules optimized for highway traffic may show decreased precision in urban expressways with significantly higher flow density or in rural roads with irregular boundaries. This highlights the difficulty of transferring the model across diverse deployment conditions without adaptation.

Extreme environmental conditions: Under challenging conditions such as backlighting, glare, heavy rain, or snow, foreground extraction and trajectory verification are prone to instability. While UBMG incorporates CLAHE enhancement and multi-group background modeling, these strategies cannot fully mitigate the visual disturbances caused by severe illumination changes or weather artifacts.

Object-specific detection blind spots: Very small debris items and continuously rolling objects remain problematic for detection. The static-object assumption of UBMG leads to the exclusion of rolling debris, while the minimal block-size constraint and road-noise suppression can filter out extremely small objects. These issues point to the need for refined strategies tailored to different debris types.

Conclusion

To address the challenges of abandoned object detection on highways, this study presents a universal foreground detection framework, UBMG, along with a dedicated method to mitigate false alarms caused by highway lighting variations. Additionally, we introduce the HAO dataset, a comprehensive collection of fully annotated video sequences across three distinct highway scenarios. This dataset is designed to facilitate robust evaluation of various detection approaches. The proposed method significantly improves key performance metrics, including precision and recall, outperforming existing methods on the HAO dataset and showing good performance on the public ABODA dataset-even though the ABODA dataset is not designed for scenarios where pedestrians discard their luggage. Future work will focus on addressing other challenging environments, such as scenes with dense pedestrian activity, and researching new hybrid frameworks (e.g., combining deep learning for candidate generation and UBMG for verification).
