Prevalence of Psychotropic Use and Psychotropic Polypharmacy in a Finnish National Cohort of Persons With Intellectual Disabilities
Authors: Ville Virtanen, Leena Saastamoinen, Maria Arvio, Hannu T Vesala, Sonja Miettinen, Antti Teittinen, Anna‐Maija Tolppanen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPsychotropic use and psychotropic polypharmacy are common in people with intellectual disabilities, but representative population‐based studies on this topic are scarce. We evaluated the prevalence of psychotropic use and psychotropic polypharmacy in a Finnish nationwide cohort of people with intellectual disabilities aged 0–97 years.MethodsThe annual prevalence of psychotropic use in 2019 was studied among 37 196 individuals with intellectual disabilities and an age‐ and sex‐matched comparison cohort with no diagnosis of intellectual disabilities. Psychotropics included antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics, hypnotics and sedatives and antiepileptics indicated for bipolar disorder (carbamazepine, valproic acid, lamotrigine, pregabalin and clonazepam). Prevalence of interclass psychotropic polypharmacy (use from ≥ 2 different psychotropic groups) was evaluated in a 4‐month time window at the end of 2019.ResultsPrevalence of psychotropic use was higher in the intellectual disability cohort (42.3%) than comparison cohort (15.0%). Antipsychotics were the most common psychotropic group in the intellectual disability cohort (28%), with lower prevalence in the comparison cohort (3.3%). The prevalence of antidepressant use was 19.4% in the intellectual disability cohort and 9.7% in the comparison cohort. A likely indication was identified for 52.9% of psychotropic users with intellectual disability (major psychiatric comorbidity 38.1%, challenging behaviour 22.1%). Psychotropic polypharmacy was more common in the intellectual disability cohort (18.2%), than in the comparison cohort (3.6%).ConclusionsOur findings highlight concerns about psychotropic polypharmacy and potential overmedication. Using a comprehensive, nationwide cohort, this study emphasises the need for more evidence‐based, person‐centred approaches, including careful diagnostics, non‐pharmacological interventions and regular treatment reviews.

Introduction

Psychotropic medications, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, hypnotics and sedatives, are widely used among people with intellectual disabilities. They are used for psychiatric comorbidities but also off‐label to manage challenging behaviour despite limited evidence (Song et al. 2020). Although people with intellectual disabilities have higher rates of mental disorders and challenging behaviours compared to the general population, they may be prescribed psychotropics in quantities that exceed what the difference in the comorbidity prevalence alone would justify (Doan et al. 2013). Further, psychotropic polypharmacy increases the risk of adverse drug events and drug–drug interactions (McMahon et al. 2020).

A high prevalence of psychotropic use among people with intellectual disabilities has been reported in previous studies, with estimates ranging between 23% and 70% (Doan et al. 2013; Lunsky and Modi 2018; Sheehan et al. 2015; Tsiouris et al. 2013). Variation in prevalence estimates is likely due to differences in study populations and settings: the highest prevalence was observed in a cohort of patients with intellectual disability referred to a specialised psychiatric outpatient clinic (Lunsky and Modi 2018), while lower prevalences were observed in studies of community‐dwelling individuals (Doan et al. 2013). Understanding the prevalence of psychotropic use and psychotropic polypharmacy in people with intellectual disabilities is important to address potential overprescribing or inappropriate prescribing practices, and therefore population‐level studies also including adolescents and children are needed.

Many of the previous studies have been conducted in smaller selected cohorts. The number of large, population‐based studies (Gomes et al. 2019; Hsu et al. 2014; Lunsky and Modi 2018; Sheehan et al. 2015) is limited. Three (Gomes et al. 2019; Lunsky and Modi 2018; Sheehan et al. 2015) of the four previous population‐based studies were restricted to adults, and two (Gomes et al. 2019; Lunsky and Modi 2018) to adults aged < 65 years, limiting generalisability. A wider inclusion of age groups is necessary as, for example, in Finland, over 10% of people with intellectual disabilities were > 65 years old, and 20% < 18‐year‐olds (Nurminen et al. 2023).

Similarly, previous studies which reported the prevalence of psychotropic polypharmacy have been conducted in smaller cohorts, possibly limiting the generalisability (Deb et al. 2015; Lunsky and Modi 2018; McMahon et al. 2020; Odalović et al. 2024). The definitions of psychotropic polypharmacy have varied between these studies in terms of the number of medications (≥ 2 or ≥ 3), included medication groups and whether inter‐ or intra‐class polypharmacy was considered. Still, all studies have consistently reported a high prevalence of psychotropic polypharmacy with estimates ranging between 22% and 45%.

To address the current knowledge gaps on psychotropic use in more representative cohorts, we assessed the prevalence of psychotropic use and interclass psychotropic polypharmacy in a Finnish nationwide cohort of people with intellectual disability aged 0–97 years and compared the prevalence estimates to an age‐ and sex‐matched comparison cohort. We also examined factors associated with psychotropic use and psychotropic polypharmacy.

Methods

Study Participants and Data Sources

The study population and data sources have been described previously in detail by Nurminen et al. (2023) in a study assessing overall medication use and medication costs among people with intellectual disabilities. People with recorded intellectual disability diagnoses in 2017–2019 who were alive on 31 December 2019 were identified from five Finnish national registers: Register of the Disability Allowance and Register of the Disability Pension, which are maintained by the Social Insurance Institution of Finland (Kela) and Care Register for Health Care, Register of Primary Health Care and Care Register for Social Welfare by The Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL). Intellectual disability was defined using ICD‐10 codes F70, F71, F72, F73, F79 and Q90, and ICD‐9 codes 317 and 318 (World Health Organization 1996). The Register of the Disability Allowance and Register of the Disability Pension contain information on the beneficiaries of disability allowance and disability pension, including beneficiaries' diagnoses and the start and end dates of the allowances. The Care Registers for Health and Social Care contain records of inpatient and outpatient healthcare and social care, including admission and discharge dates and diagnosis codes. Personal identification numbers enabled the linkage of individual‐level data from different registers. Finland has a universal tax‐funded healthcare system, and healthcare‐related events, and, e.g., information on eligibility for disability benefits are recorded in statutory registers with nationwide coverage (Laugesen et al. 2021).

An age‐ (+/− 1 year) and sex‐matched comparison cohort without intellectual disability (1:1) was identified from the register of Digital and Population Data Services Agency. Aside from the aforementioned ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes for intellectual disabilities, no other exclusion diagnoses were applied for the comparison cohort. The intellectual disability cohort contains 37 196 individuals, 21 361 (57.4%) of whom were men and 15 835 (42.6%) women. The average age was 43.4 (43.1–43.7). The following age categories were used in this study: early childhood (0–6), childhood (7–12), adolescence (13–17), young adulthood (18–29), middle adulthood (30–45), late adulthood (46–64), retirement (65–74) and old age (75+).

For both cohorts, information on dispensed prescriptions was identified from the Kanta prescription centre, where every prescription dispensed in Finnish community pharmacies, regardless of reimbursement status, is recorded. Additionally, we utilised the Dispensations reimbursable under the National Health Insurance (NHI) Scheme register (previously known as the Prescription Register), which includes information on all reimbursable medicine dispensations in community pharmacies.

The Special Refund Entitlement Register, maintained by Kela, contains information on individuals entitled to higher reimbursement of medications for specific chronic or severe conditions. To be eligible for higher reimbursement, the conditions need to be diagnosed according to specific diagnostic criteria. This register was used to obtain information on chronic comorbidities such as epilepsy, severe psychiatric conditions and challenging behaviour.

Ethics Statement

The Health and Social Data Permit Authority, Findata, granted authorisation for data usage. All data were pseudonymised before submission to the research team. Data preprocessing and analyses were conducted in an audited remote use environment. According to Finnish legislation, ethical review or informed consent was not required since the study exclusively used pseudonymised register data.

Artificial intelligence‐generated content tools were not applied in the preparation of this manuscript.

Definition of Psychotropic Use

Dispensed psychotropic prescriptions in outpatient settings in 2018–2019 were identified from national registers using the following Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system codes (WHO Collaborating Centre for Drug Statistics Methodology 2022): N05A (antipsychotics), N05B (anxiolytics), N05C (hypnotics and sedatives) and N06A (antidepressants). We also included drugs from the antiepileptics group (N03A), which can be used to treat bipolar disorder: N03AF01 (carbamazepine), N03AG01 (valproic acid), N03AX09 (lamotrigine), N03AX16 (pregabalin) and N03AE01 (clonazepam). By using the NHI scheme register, we excluded dispensed prescriptions of carbamazepine, valproic acid, lamotrigine, pregabalin, clonazepam, midazolam and clobazam which had special refund entitlement for epilepsy (exclusion criteria provided in Table S1). Dispensed prescriptions without these refund entitlements were included in the study.

Psychotropic use was considered prevalent if the individual had at least one dispensed psychotropic prescription in 2019. The use of each psychotropic drug group and the most common individual psychotropic drugs from each group were evaluated. Psychotropic use was considered incident if a person had dispensed a prescription in 2019 but not in 2018. Psychotropic polypharmacy was defined at the drug group level as dispensed prescriptions from at least two psychotropic drug groups, thus addressing interclass polypharmacy. A similar cutoff has been used in previous psychotropic polypharmacy studies. (McMahon et al. 2020; O'Dwyer et al. 2017) Psychotropic polypharmacy was categorised as follows: no psychotropic use, one psychotropic (no polypharmacy) and polypharmacy (psychotropics from two or more psychotropic drug groups). The assessment period for psychotropic polypharmacy was the last four months of 2019, as defined previously for the same data sources (Paulamäki et al. 2023) and arising from the Finnish system of reimbursing a maximum of three months' supply at the same time. Therefore, most people purchase their medications as three‐month supplies. To evaluate the robustness of the polypharmacy estimate for the assessment window, we performed sensitivity analyses using an alternative definition of purchases from two different psychotropic classes within 14 days during 2019 and a stricter time window of 7 days.

Other Characteristics

Information on specialised healthcare use for the intellectual disability cohort was obtained from Care register for Health Care. Information on the following comorbidities diagnosed in 2010–2019 was extracted: autism spectrum disorders, schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders, mood disorders, neurotic, stress‐related and somatoform disorders, disorders of adult personality and behaviour, behavioural and emotional disorders, dementia and epilepsy. Mood disorders were further separated into bipolar disorder/mania and depression. Information on epilepsy, severe psychiatric disorders and challenging behaviour in intellectual disability was obtained from the Special Refund Entitlement Register of Medicines. The number of hospital days and individuals with hospital days in 2019 was calculated as a proxy of overall comorbidity. Detailed definitions are provided in Table S2.

Because the dispensing data sources do not directly record the indications, presumed indications for psychotropic use on an individual level were derived based on diagnosis of major psychiatric comorbidity (schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional or mood disorder) in the Care register for Health Care or special refund entitlement for severe psychiatric disorders (major psychiatric comorbidity), or a special refund entitlement for challenging behaviour in intellectual disability (Table S2).

Statistical Analysis

Data are described as mean, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI, normally distributed continuous variables), median, interquartile range (continuous variables with skewed distribution) or n, % (categorical variables). Differences between the psychotropic users and nonusers within the intellectual disability and comparison cohort were compared using t‐test (age) and Chi‐Square test (sex, categorised age). Association between intellectual disability and psychotropic use in different age and sex strata was evaluated with logistic regression. Associations of demographic characteristics, medication use and comorbidities with psychotropic use (yes/no) in intellectual disability were studied with logistic regression, and associations with interclass psychotropic polypharmacy (no psychotropic/psychotropic from one group/psychotropic from ≥ 2 groups) were studied with ordinal logistic regression, except logistic regression (psychotropic from one group/psychotropic from ≥ 2 groups) for medication use. Stata SE16.0 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC) was used for statistical analyses and data management.

Results

Prevalence of Psychotropic Use in Intellectual Disability and Comparison Cohorts

Psychotropic use was significantly more common in the intellectual disability cohort (prevalence 42.3%), than in the comparison cohort (15.0%) (Table 1). Psychotropic users in the intellectual disability cohort were younger than those in the comparison cohort (mean age of users 43.4 years and 47.4 years in the intellectual disability and comparison cohort, respectively). In all age groups, the use of psychotropics was more common in the intellectual disability cohort with age group‐specific prevalence varying between 15.9% and 59.5% in the intellectual disability cohort and 0.9% and 36.4% in the comparison cohort (Table 1). The largest difference in psychotropic use was observed in the youngest age groups: of children and adolescents with disabilities (age 0–17 years), 21.9% used psychotropics, while the proportion in the comparison cohort was only 2.9%. In both cohorts, the proportion of psychotropic users increased with age. The highest prevalence, 59.5% of people with intellectual disabilities and 36.4% of the comparison cohort, was observed in the oldest age group (age 75 years or older).

Psychotropic use was more common among women than men for all drug classes in both cohorts, except for antipsychotics in the intellectual disability cohort (Table 1 and Figure S1). In general, the prevalence differences between sexes were small. The largest difference was observed for antidepressants in the intellectual disability cohort (prevalence 23.5% in women and 16.4% in men). Psychotropic use was twice as common in women with in the intellectual disability cohort compared to women in the comparison cohort. The difference was even higher among men, as psychotropic use among men with intellectual disabilities was nearly fourfold compared to men in the comparison cohort (Table 1).

Use of Different Psychotropic Drug Groups

Antipsychotics were the most used psychotropic group in the intellectual disability cohort (28.2%), followed by antidepressants (19.4%) and anxiolytics (15.1%) (Table 2 and Figure S1). In the comparison cohort, antidepressants (9.7%) were the most commonly used psychotropic group. The largest between‐cohort difference was observed for antipsychotics (prevalence 3.3% in the comparison cohort). Risperidone was the most used antipsychotic in the intellectual disability cohort (11.7% of the cohort, 41.4% of all antipsychotic users).

Use of all other psychotropic groups was also more common in the intellectual disability cohort than in the comparison cohort. Mirtazapine was the most common antidepressant and diazepam the most commonly used anxiolytic in the intellectual disability cohort. Melatonin was the most common hypnotic in both cohorts.

The majority (98.1%) of people with intellectual disabilities who used psychotropics in 2019 had used them in the preceding year. The proportions of prevalent users in different psychotropic drug groups varied from 89.8% (hypnotics and sedatives) to 98.3% (antiepileptics). More granular information on the prevalence of different psychotropic subgroups is provided in Table S3.

Characteristics of Psychotropic Users and Non‐Users With Intellectual Disabilities

Within the intellectual disability cohort, psychotropic users were on average 10 years older than non‐users and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, as demonstrated by a strong association of different comorbidities and psychotropic use (Table 3). The prevalence of any mental or behavioural disorder was 51.7% in psychotropic users and 17.7% in non‐users. The strongest associations with psychotropic use were observed for schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders (OR = 14.7) and having either major psychiatric comorbidity (OR = 15.5), challenging behaviour (OR 29.3) or both (OR = 67.4). Altogether, 38.1% of psychotropic users had a diagnosis of major psychiatric comorbidity (schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional or mood disorder) and 22.1% had refund entitlement for challenging behaviour.

Interclass Psychotropic Polypharmacy

The prevalence of interclass psychotropic polypharmacy in the intellectual disability cohort was 18.2% (43.2% of the psychotropic users), which was significantly higher than in the comparison cohort (prevalence 3.6%, 23.9% of the psychotropic users; Table 4 and Figures S2–S3). Comparable prevalence estimates were obtained with alternative polypharmacy definitions (Table S4). In addition, the age and sex distributions of different psychotropic polypharmacy groups were similar with different polypharmacy definitions.

Psychotropic polypharmacy was more prevalent in the older age groups in both cohorts. There was only a small difference in the prevalence of psychotropic polypharmacy between women (19.7%) and men (17.2%) in people with intellectual disabilities.

The most common drug groups in the people with psychotropic polypharmacy were antipsychotics (88.0% of people with psychotropic polypharmacy in the intellectual disability cohort) and antidepressants (65.9%). Consistently, the most common interclass psychotropic combination was antidepressant‐antipsychotic (n = 2180, 32.1% of psychotropic polypharmacy in the intellectual disability cohort) (Table S5). The most common combination of individual medications in the intellectual disability cohort was risperidone and escitalopram, followed by risperidone and melatonin. Although the majority of the most common combinations of individual medications included risperidone or quetiapine and an antidepressant from the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) class, there was notable variation in the psychotropic combinations as those combinations with at least 10 users constituted 35% of interclass psychotropic polypharmacy in the intellectual disability cohort (Table S6). Similar high variation in the combinations was observed in the comparison cohort (Table S7) and the most common interclass psychotropic polypharmacy combinations were for antidepressants, mainly SSRIs, mirtazapine and venlafaxine.

In the intellectual disability cohort, older age, female sex, hospitalisations and all psychiatric comorbidities were associated with interclass psychotropic polypharmacy. Over half of those in the polypharmacy group had a diagnosis of a major psychiatric comorbidity (schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional or mood disorder), and 24.9% had refund entitlement for challenging behaviour.

Discussion

This national cohort study of persons with intellectual disabilities from all age groups showed that the use of psychotropics and interclass psychotropic polypharmacy is more common in people with intellectual disabilities when compared to a matched cohort of people without intellectual disability diagnoses. Our study makes an important contribution to current literature by including all age groups and demonstrating a large difference in psychotropic use in children and adolescents as the largest difference in psychotropic use was observed in younger age groups. Altogether 21.9% of people with intellectual disabilities aged < 18 used psychotropics compared to 2.9% of the comparison cohort.

Most of the previous literature based on a larger data set has been limited to adults with intellectual disabilities (Henderson et al. 2020; Sheehan et al. 2015; Song et al. 2023; Tsiouris et al. 2013), and all recent previous population‐level studies have been restricted to adults (Gomes et al. 2019; Sheehan et al. 2015; Tsiouris et al. 2013). A Taiwanese study on psychotropic use in 1997–2005 also included people aged < 18 years (Hsu et al. 2014), but significant changes in psychotropic use have occurred since then (Henderson et al. 2020). The larger magnitude of difference in the youngest age group may be partly influenced by the shorter lifespan of individuals with more severe intellectual disabilities (Arvio et al. 2017), which would increase the proportion of psychotropic users in younger age groups because psychotropic use is more common among individuals with more severe intellectual disabilities (Sheehan et al. 2015). A higher prevalence of psychotropics in children and adolescents with intellectual disabilities was also reported in a Scottish study of individuals aged 4–19 and attending primary, secondary or special schools. In that study, the prescription of antipsychotics was 15 times more common and the prescription of antidepressants 1.8 times more common in children with intellectual disabilities compared to children without intellectual disabilities (Henderson et al. 2021). Although the estimates of these two studies are not directly comparable, the prevalence of psychotropic use appears higher in our study, which likely reflects differences in study populations. The Scottish study included children with intellectual disabilities who attended schools, which may skew the severity distribution towards milder forms of intellectual disabilities compared to our study.

The prevalence of psychotropic use in the entire intellectual cohort was within the range of estimates from previous studies reporting prevalences between 23% and 90% (Deb et al. 2015; Hsu et al. 2014), and a meta‐analysis of 19 studies reporting the prevalence of 41% (Song et al. 2023). The highest prevalences (90% and 70%) were observed in studies where the study populations were restricted to people with intellectual disabilities exhibiting challenging behaviours (Deb et al. 2015) or referred to specialised psychiatric outpatient clinics (Lunsky and Modi 2018). Conversely, the lower prevalence rates (23%–58%) were from larger population‐based studies (Henderson et al. 2020; Hsu et al. 2014; Sheehan et al. 2015; Tsiouris et al. 2013). The prevalence of 42% in our study aligns well with these previous population‐based studies, although they were restricted to older populations except for Hsu et al. (2014) and were from earlier time periods.

Interestingly, in our study the use of psychotropics was more common among women than men in both cohorts, but in the intellectual disability cohort the difference in use of psychotropics between women and men was smaller. The difference to the comparison cohort was larger for women than for men. Although previous studies have not made similar comparisons with individuals without intellectual disability, comparable sex differences in people with intellectual disabilities have been reported. Henderson et al. (2021) found a higher prevalence of antipsychotic use among boys and adolescent men with intellectual disabilities, whereas antidepressant use was more common among girls and adolescent women. Similarly, Tsiouris et al. (2013) showed a higher prevalence of antidepressant use among women than men with intellectual disabilities. In our study, the largest sex difference in the intellectual disability cohort was observed for antidepressants, and the differences for other categories were small. Nevertheless, these smaller differences collectively contributed to a higher prevalence of psychotropic polypharmacy among women, consistent with findings by Lunsky and Modi (2018). The observed sex difference in antidepressant use is also in line with patterns seen in the general population, where antidepressant use is more common among women (Ballin et al. 2026). This pattern is often attributed to a higher prevalence of depression and anxiety in women, driven by a complex interplay of affective, biological and cognitive factors, as well as differences in exposure to negative life events and health‐seeking behaviour (Hyde et al. 2008; Thunander Sundbom et al. 2017). A previous study comparing sex differences in psychiatric comorbidity patterns between individuals with and without intellectual disability showed similar sex difference patterns in both groups (Lunsky et al. 2009). Notably, in that study women with intellectual disabilities were more likely to have a diagnosed mood disability disorder or a history of sexual abuse than men.

The prevalence of antipsychotic use in this study (28.2%) is consistent with previous estimates of approximately one‐third of people with intellectual disabilities using antipsychotics (de Kuijper et al. 2010; Lunsky et al. 2018; Song et al. 2023). This is significantly higher than the point prevalence of psychotic disorders among people with intellectual disabilities (Cooper, Smiley, Morrison, Allan, et al. 2007). Although the prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities is higher in intellectual disability, it still does not explain the higher number of antipsychotics used. One reason for this discrepancy may be the mainly off‐label use for challenging behaviours and symptoms such as anxiety and sleep disorders. Risperidone was the most common antipsychotic in the intellectual disability cohort in this study, and it is the only antipsychotic in Finland with an authorised indication for short‐term treatment of challenging behaviours in children and adolescents with intellectual disabilities. For this indication, an assessment of the necessity for pharmacological treatment, based on an evaluation by a medical specialist or examinations conducted in a specialised unit is required. However, these assessments are unlikely to occur routinely in real‐life situations. The evidence for the efficacy of risperidone varies across studies, highlighting the need for more high‐quality research on this topic (Henderson et al. 2021; Matson and Neal 2009; Unwin and Deb 2008).

The prevalence of antidepressant use in previous studies has varied considerably, between 5.35% and 34% (Bowring et al. 2017; Branford and Shankar 2022; Deb et al. 2015; Hsu et al. 2014; Lunsky and Modi 2018; Sheehan et al. 2015; Song et al. 2020; Tsiouris et al. 2013). The lowest prevalence was reported in a Taiwanese study (Hsu et al. 2014), and based on a cross‐sectional study of two time points, the use of antidepressants has increased significantly since then (Henderson et al. 2021). The prevalence in our study (19.4%) is within the range of more recent studies. (Branford and Shankar 2022; Henderson et al. 2021) A study covering 56%–60% of general practices in the United Kingdom reported that 24.6% of adults with intellectual disabilities used antidepressants, compared to 13.0% in the general population in 2021 (Branford and Shankar 2022). Notably, in that study over half of the antidepressant users with intellectual disability did not have a recent depression diagnosis. This may indicate off‐label use or lack of medication reviews.

There is less previous literature on the use of hypnotics and sedatives, anxiolytics and drugs from the antiepileptic drug group. The prevalence of these medications in intellectual disability has ranged from 1% to 7.83%, 4.15% to 16% and 2.64% to 26%, respectively (Bowring et al. 2017; Doan et al. 2013; Hsu et al. 2014; Tsiouris et al. 2013). Our findings for anxiolytics (15.1%) and drugs from the antiepileptic group (2.9%) were in the same range. However, for hypnotics and sedatives, the prevalence in our study was slightly higher, 9.5%. Notably, several previous studies included antiepileptics regardless of their indication and therefore it is challenging to evaluate whether they were used for psychiatric indications or for epilepsy.

We identified a likely indication for 52.9% of psychotropic users with intellectual disabilities (major psychiatric comorbidity 38.1% and challenging behaviour 22.1%). The prevalence of challenging behaviours among psychotropic users is comparable to previous studies with estimates ranging between 18.1% and 22.5% (Bowring et al. 2017; Cooper, Smiley, Morrison, Williamson, and Allan 2007; Sheehan et al. 2015). Although the definition of challenging behaviour has varied in earlier studies from a list of over 200 Read codes (a coding system used in the UK clinical system for symptoms, diagnoses, procedures and social factors) (Sheehan et al. 2015) to specific instruments and proxy‐provided data (Bowring et al. 2017; Cooper, Smiley, Morrison, Williamson, and Allan 2007), the prevalence in previous studies and ours are quite similar. We identified challenging behaviours from special refund entitlements, which require that the diagnosis and assessment of the need for medication are based on an examination by a medical specialist, and that the medical statement describes the appropriate diagnostic examinations and a treatment plan in accordance with good clinical practice. Therefore, our definition mainly captures persons for whom the use was more likely initiated according to the treatment recommendations. However, the true prevalence of psychotropic use for challenging behaviours is likely higher than 22.1% and partially off‐label.

In our register‐based study, half of the psychotropic users among people with intellectual disabilities did not have a record of psychiatric diagnosis or challenging behaviour. This is considerably higher than previous studies in which this proportion ranged between 26% and 33.2% (Doan et al. 2013; Henderson et al. 2020; Sheehan et al. 2015). In previous studies based on chart reviews of a psychiatric outpatient unit (Lunsky and Modi 2018), residential care providers (de Kuijper et al. 2010) and adults receiving drug benefits (Lunsky et al. 2018), 30%–40% of adults with intellectual disabilities had psychotropic medication without a psychiatric diagnosis. On the other hand, in a study of 4069 adults with intellectual disabilities living in developmental disability centres, care institutions, individualised housing solutions, at home or with foster families (Tsiouris et al. 2013), concomitant challenging behaviours and a comorbid psychiatric disorder accounted for 38% of psychotropic use, while 49% of psychotropic users had psychiatric comorbidity and 13% had challenging behaviour. These different prevalence estimates and the varying proportion of psychotropic users with no record of a psychiatric condition or challenging behaviour highlight the challenges in ascertaining the indications from routinely collected healthcare data but also emphasise the necessity of medication reviews.

The discrepancy between the prevalence of psychiatric diagnosis and psychotropic use may also reflect the challenges in diagnosing psychiatric comorbidities in people with intellectual disabilities (Charlot et al. 2022). This can lead to underrecording and underestimation of psychiatric comorbidities in routinely collected healthcare data. On the other hand, limitations in communication skills can lead to misunderstandings and overdiagnosis. Still, we observed a higher prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities in psychotropic users with intellectual disabilities compared with nonusers. However, the higher psychiatric comorbidity burden does not entirely explain the higher prevalence of psychotropic use in people with intellectual disabilities (Doan et al. 2013). Another explanation is the use for managing challenging behaviours, which can be indicated, but also off‐label. It may be easier to resort to psychotropics than to non‐pharmacological treatments which can be considered more difficult to access. However, challenging behaviours may stem from psychosocial factors, in which case the psychotropic medication does not appropriately address the root cause and is therefore unethical and likely ineffective. This underlines the importance of identifying the causes of challenging behaviour and consequently the most appropriate and efficient intervention. Based on a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis, there were no significant differences between pharmacological and non‐pharmacological interventions for challenging behaviours of different topologies (Groves et al. 2023), and care guidelines recommend psychological, social and environmental interventions (Bristow et al. 2022; National Collaborating Centre for Mental Health (UK) 2015).

Both intraclass and interclass psychotropic polypharmacy pose additional challenges, increasing the risk of adverse events (Bowring et al. 2017; Deb et al. 2024). In our study, the prevalence of interclass psychotropic polypharmacy in intellectual disability was 18.2%. As we could not differentiate between add‐on and switching with the data available to us, we intentionally limited this study to interclass polypharmacy when the polypharmacy is less likely to represent switching. Our interclass polypharmacy estimate is in accordance with previous studies that have also included intraclass polypharmacy (Deb et al. 2015; Lunsky and Modi 2018; McMahon et al. 2020; Odalović et al. 2024). In a small study conducted on Jersey Island, the proportion of psychotropic polypharmacy (≥ 2 psychotropics) among people with intellectual disabilities was 23% (McMahon et al. 2020). A very similar prevalence, 22%, was observed in a cohort study of adults enrolled in a specialised psychiatric outpatient clinic with a different definition of psychotropic polypharmacy (concurrent use of at least three psychotropics) (Lunsky and Modi 2018). However, it should be noted that the first study also included anticholinergic agents (ATC code N04A) and psychostimulants (N06B), which were not included in our study. The highest prevalence (45%) was reported in a British study involving 88 people with intellectual disabilities exhibiting aggressive behaviour (Deb et al. 2015). An almost equally high prevalence (42%) was observed in an Irish cohort study of 433 adults aged over 40 years, with a definition of at least two psychotropics (Odalović et al. 2024).

The main strength of our study is its comprehensive register data, which includes all individuals with intellectual disability diagnoses in Finland using healthcare and social security services during 2017–2019. On the other hand, it is possible that some people with milder intellectual disabilities are not captured if they have not been diagnosed with intellectual disability and/or applied for the disability benefits. Indications of individual prescriptions are not directly recorded in the applied data sources, and therefore they were deduced based on the comorbidity and reimbursement information for a specific individual. Although this approach was likely more effective for identifying prescriptions related to psychiatric comorbidities and epilepsy, there remains a residual risk that some of the pregabalin dispensations may have been for treatment of neuropathic pain. Therefore, misclassification of indications is possible, but the proportion of adult psychotropic users without psychiatric comorbidity or challenging behaviour is in line with previous studies. Further, as psychotropic use in people with intellectual disabilities is a complex issue, providing a comprehensive overview in one study is not possible. Further studies should investigate the duration of psychotropic use and different use patterns and associated outcomes in more detail. As our study and the majority of the other psychotropic polypharmacy studies are cross‐sectional, with only one small longitudinal cohort, (Odalović et al. 2024) the temporal changes in intra‐ and inter‐class polypharmacy prevalence, prescription sequences leading to or away from it, and predictive factors of polypharmacy development and continuation are unknown and should be assessed in future studies.

Investigating how commonly psychotropics are used is the initial step towards improving their use. (Song et al. 2023) Persons with intellectual disabilities should receive appropriate treatments, and psychotropic drugs can be a crucial part of this. However, if not appropriately indicated and monitored, their use may predispose to further cognitive and functional decline. Unnecessary use also burdens health services with avoidable costs arising from supplying the drugs, monitoring their use and, in worst cases, care of the adverse events. Reducing antipsychotic use in people with intellectual disabilities has been proposed as one way to reduce the health inequalities in this group, (Sheehan et al. 2015) and inappropriate use of psychotropics has also been viewed as possibly violating the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. (Deb et al. 2024) Improving psychotropic treatment for people with intellectual disabilities requires a shift towards evidence‐based and person‐centred care (Branford and Shankar 2022; Doan et al. 2013; Tsiouris et al. 2013). This means initial use of nonpharmacological interventions, initiating psychotropics only when behaviours are severe or persistent enough to cause harm, and deprescribing when appropriate indication is not present or has resolved, adverse effects occur or there is no clinical response. By striving for more accurate diagnoses, reducing off‐label prescriptions, promoting non‐pharmacological interventions and ensuring regular follow‐up visits, clinicians could better address the complex needs of this vulnerable population. It is important to support the self‐determination of people with intellectual disabilities by adapting to the environment and aligning the actions of those around them to meet their needs. Ensuring that people with intellectual disabilities can engage in activities and routines that are meaningful to them in daily life may have positive effects on challenging behaviours.

In conclusion, our observations on high prevalence of psychotropic use, particularly possible off‐label use in intellectual disabilities across all age groups is concerning. It is important to motivate better adherence to treatment recommendations on psychotropic use in this vulnerable group. Our findings align with previous research and highlight concerns about psychotropic polypharmacy and potential overmedication, emphasising the need for more evidence‐based, person‐centred approaches, including careful diagnostics, non‐pharmacological interventions and regular treatment reviews.
