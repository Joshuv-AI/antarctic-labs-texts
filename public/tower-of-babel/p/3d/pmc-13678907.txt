Validation and prognostic utility of the mSMART 4.0 risk stratification system in Chinese patients with multiple myeloma
Authors: Jian Yin, Rui Liu, Yanping Zhang, Zeshi Liu, Yangyang Gong, Aili He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe 2024 Mayo Clinic mSMART 4.0 risk stratification system features significant revisions, yet its utility in Chinese multiple myeloma (MM) patients remains unvalidated. This study evaluates its prognostic performance in a Chinese cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively collected clinical data from newly diagnosed MM (NDMM) patients and followed them up. Clinical characteristics, therapeutic responses, and survival were compared between high-risk and standard-risk groups defined by mSMART 4.0. Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) analysis was used to compare the predictive efficacy of mSMART 4.0 versus version 3.0.ResultsAmong 202 patients, 69 (34.2%) were high-risk according to mSMART 4.0. The mSMART 4.0 high-risk group demonstrated significantly lower deep response rates, alongside shorter median PFS (P < 0.001) and OS (P < 0.001). The proportion of the high-risk group was lower under mSMART 4.0 (34.2%) compared to mSMART 3.0 (51.9%). C-index revealed that mSMART 4.0 possessed superior prognostic predictive validity (C-index = 0.738, 95% CI: 0.677–0.792) relative to mSMART 3.0 (C-index = 0.572, 95% CI: 0.490–0.649). The increase in the C-index was statistically significant (ΔC-index = 0.167, 95% CI: 0.065–0.269, P = 0.003). Multivariate analysis identified mSMART 4.0 high-risk status as an independent adverse prognostic factor for both PFS (HR = 2.027; 95% CI: 1.364–3.012) and OS (HR = 5.352; 95% CI: 2.794–10.250). Additionally, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was an independent predictor of poor PFS (HR = 1.002; 95% CI: 1.001–1.004).ConclusionIn this Chinese cohort, mSMART 4.0 effectively stratifies multiple myeloma patients and serves as an independent predictor of inferior prognosis, demonstrating superior prognostic value over version 3.0.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16521-1.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) ranks as the second most prevalent hematologic malignancy globally [1], characterized by the aberrant clonal proliferation of bone marrow plasma cells (BMPC) and the excessive secretion of monoclonal immunoglobulins and consequent end-organ damage [2]. The global incidence ranges from 1.5 to 1.8 per 100,000 population, with the disease showing a distinct trend toward an aging demographic [3]. Currently, MM remains an incurable disease with substantial heterogeneity in therapeutic response and prognosis. Real-world studies [4, 5] have indicated that while the OS for transplant-eligible MM patients exceeded 10 years, elderly patients (aged>75years) exhibited a significantly lower median OS of approximately 5 years. Although survival outcomes have improved with the introduction of monoclonal antibodies and novel agents, the clinical risks and disease burden remain significant [6].

Precise prognostic assessment and risk stratification are pivotal for the early identification of high-risk populations and the formulation of personalized treatment strategies. To date, multiple assessment systems have been established internationally. First proposed in 1975, the Durie-Salmon (D-S) staging system [7] relies mainly on multiple biochemical markers, primarily reflecting the tumor burden in patients. In 2005, the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) analyzed data from 10,750 newly diagnosed MM (NDMM) patients to establish the International Staging System (ISS) [8], which centers on serum β2-microglobulin (β2-MG) and albumin (ALB) levels. The 2015 Revised International Staging System (R-ISS) for MM incorporates three high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities: Del 17p, t(4;14), and t(14;16), along with elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels [9]. The Second Revision of the International Staging System (R2-ISS) was introduced in 2022 to formally incorporate Gain/Amp 1q [10].

With deepening insights into the molecular genetics of MM, the prognostic significance of cytogenetic abnormalities has become increasingly prominent. The Mayo Clinic Stratification for Myeloma and Risk-Adapted Therapy (mSMART) classification system was first introduced in 2007 (mSMART 1.0) [11]. In 2009, the mSMART 2.0 incorporated the t(4;14) into the high-risk category [12]. In 2018, mSMART 3.0 further refined the definition of high-risk disease by classifying any single occurrence of t(4;14), t(14;16), t(14;20), Del 17p, TP53 mutation, or Gain 1q as high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities. Additionally, R-ISS stage III, a high plasma cell S-phase fraction, and high-risk gene expression profiling (GEP) signatures were included in the high-risk category. Notably, this version introduced the concepts of ‘Double Hit’ (presence of two high-risk genetic abnormalities) and ‘Triple Hit’ (presence of three or more) [13].

Released in October 2024, the mSMART 4.0 stratification system [14] features substantial revisions in its high-risk cytogenetic criteria compared to the mSMART 3.0 framework. Notably, Bi-allelic del(1p) has been newly incorporated as a high-risk feature. The translocations t(4;14), t(14;16), and t(14;20) are no longer considered high-risk in isolation, but they are defined as high-risk when co-occurring with Gain/Amp(1q) or Del 1p. Similarly, isolated gain(1q) is no longer classified as high-risk unless it presents concurrently with Del(1p). Furthermore, while Del(17p) and/or TP53 mutations remain in the high-risk category, mSMART 4.0 has newly introduced specific clone size thresholds for defining Del(17p) positivity. Clinically, mSMART 4.0 removed R-ISS stage III from its criteria, replacing it with features indicative of aggressive disease biology, including β2-MG (> 5.5 mg/L) with normal renal function, primary plasma cell leukemia, and the presence of extramedullary disease at diagnosis. Consequently, the ‘Triple Hit’ designation was retired, and ‘Double Hit’ MM was redefined strictly as the concurrent presence of two or more of these newly delineated high-risk cytogenetic factors. This updated system integrates contemporary clinical evidence to enhance the precision of risk-adapted therapeutic strategies.

Significant genetic disparities exist between Chinese and Western MM populations, notably reflected in the varying prevalence of 1q21 abnormalities and Del 17p [15]. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no published studies evaluating the clinical characteristics and prognosis implications of the mSMART 4.0 specifically in Chinese patients with MM. Thus, its applicability within Chinese clinical practice warrants urgent validation. Consequently, this study aims to systematically analyze the clinical-biological profiles and outcomes of NDMM patients under this framework, providing a robust evidence-based foundation for its rational implementation in China.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We retrospectively collected clinical data from patients with MM who were initially presented to the Department of Hematology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University between March 2019 and June 2024. Patient information was collected through the electronic medical record system and was cross-verified by two investigators. All cases met the inclusion criteria: (1) NDMM diagnosed in accordance with the IMWG updated criteria for the diagnosis of MM [16]; (2) availability of complete baseline clinical data; (3) receipt of at least two cycles of systemic therapy. Exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) previously diagnosed MM; (2) receipt of any prior systemic chemotherapy or radiotherapy before enrollment; (3) concurrent hematologic malignancies or non-hematologic malignant wasting diseases; (4) absence of fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) results or patients lost to follow-up.

Grouping criteria

Based on the mSMART 4.0 risk stratification criteria [14], patients exhibiting any of the following abnormalities were classified as having high-risk cytogenetics: (1) del(17p) and/or TP53 mutation; (2) Bi-allelic del(1p); (3) t(4;14), t(14;16), or t(14;20) plus either gain/amp(1q) or del(1p); (4) gain/amp(1q) plus del(1p). Patients meeting any of the following criteria were classified into the high-risk clinical features category: (1) β2-MG > 5.5 mg/L with normal renal function; (2) Primary plasma cell leukemia; (3) High plasma cell S-phase; (4) NDMM with extramedullary disease. The mSMART 4.0 high-risk patients were stratified into cytogenetic-only high-risk, clinical-only high-risk, and dual high-risk with both cytogenetic and clinical high-risk features. Direct comparative analysis was limited strictly to the cytogenetic-only high-risk and clinical-only high-risk subgroups.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies or percentages and compared using the Chi-square (χ²) test. P-values from multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Holm method to control the family-wise error rate. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences between groups assessed by the log-rank test. All censored data were processed with the standard right-censoring method: censored patients retained valid survival time from enrollment to the last confirmed follow-up visit, who were coded as event status 0, while patients reaching defined survival endpoints were coded as event status 1 in survival datasets. To identify independent risk factors affecting PFS and OS in NDMM patients, univariate analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazards model with the forced entry method. Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) analyses were used to compare the prognostic discrimination of the mSMART 4.0 and mSMART 3.0 risk stratification systems. A C-index of ≥ 0.80 indicates excellent discrimination, a value between 0.70 and 0.79 denotes acceptable discriminative ability, whereas a value between 0.60 and 0.69 reflects modest discrimination. Non-parametric bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations to statistically test the difference between the two C-indices. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0, GraphPad Prism version 8.0, and R software.

Results

Inclusion and exclusion of study subjects

Between March 2019 and June 2024, a total of 325 patients with NDMM received treatment at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. A total of 123 patients were excluded due to concomitant malignancies (n = 15), missing FISH data (n = 64), receiving < 2 treatment cycles (n = 23), or loss to follow-up (n = 21). Ultimately, 202 patients comprised the final analytical cohort (Fig. 1).

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 202 patients with NDMM were included in the study, comprising 118 males (58.4%) and 84 females (41.6%), with a median age of 62 years (IQR: 56–69). Based on the mSMART 4.0 risk stratification, 133 patients (65.8%) were classified as standard-risk, and 69 patients (34.2%) were classified as high-risk. Among the 69 high-risk patients, 16 (7.9%) were classified as having high-risk cytogenetics only, 47 (23.3%) as having high-risk clinical features only, and 6 (3.0%) presented with both concurrent high-risk cytogenetics and clinical features (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics and survival outcomes based on mSMART 4.0

Comparison between the high-risk and standard-risk groups showed that the proportions of patients with ISS stage II–III (P = 0.007) and R-ISS stage II–III (P = 0.013) were significantly higher in the high-risk group. Significant intergroup differences were also observed for BMPC percentage (P < 0.001), PT (P = 0.008), and APTT (P = 0.011). To guard against type I error inflation due to multiple testing, we applied the Holm-Bonferroni correction to all comparative analyses. After adjustment, the difference in BMPC percentage between the two groups remained statistically significant (adjusted P = 0.002), whereas the differences in PT and APTT no longer reached statistical significance (adjusted P > 0.05) (Table 2). Regarding treatments, there were no statistically significant differences between the high-risk group (n = 69) and the standard-risk group (n = 133) in terms of induction therapy regimen selection or the rate of ASCT (P > 0.05). In terms of therapeutic response, the standard-risk group achieved a significantly higher ORR (adjusted P = 0.001) and deep response rate (adjusted P = 0.040) compared to the high-risk group (Table 3). At the final follow-up, the median follow-up duration was 33 months for the high-risk group and 47 months for the standard-risk group. The progression or relapse rates were 69.5% and 52.6%, respectively. In the high-risk group, 28 (40.5%) patients died, while in the standard-risk group, 18 (13.5%) patients died. The high-risk group demonstrated a significantly shorter median PFS of 19.0 months (95% CI: 13.4–24.6) and a median OS of 39.0 months (95% CI: 34.8–43.2) compared to the standard-risk group, which had a median PFS of 40.0 months (95% CI: 39.7–49.3) and a median OS that was not reached. These differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A and B).

Comparison between mSMART 4.0 and mSMART 3.0 risk stratification systems

To further compare the prognostic performance of mSMART 3.0 and 4.0, patients were stratified according to mSMART 3.0, with 105 cases in the high-risk group and 97 cases in the standard-risk group. Compared with the mSMART 4.0, among the 105 patients classified as high-risk by mSMART 3.0, 47 (44.8%) remained in the high-risk category under mSMART 4.0, while 58 (55.2%) were downgraded to standard-risk. Specifically, among those 58 downgraded patients, 56 patients presented with isolated amp(1q), and 2 patients presented with isolated t(4;14). Among the 97 patients classified as standard-risk by mSMART 3.0, 75 (77.3%) remained standard-risk under mSMART 4.0, whereas 22 (22.7%) were upgraded to high-risk. The reasons for upgrading in these 22 patients included the presence of extramedullary disease at diagnosis (n = 6), primary plasma cell leukemia (n = 3), and β2-MG > 5.5 mg/L with normal renal function (n = 13) (Table 4).

Factors influencing survival in NDMM

Univariate analysis identified gender (HR = 1.519, 95% CI: 1.057–2.181), mSMART 4.0 (HR = 2.169, 95% CI: 1.482–3.172), LDH (HR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.001–1.005), and HB (HR = 0.992, 95% CI: 0.985–0.999) as factors influencing PFS. mSMART 4.0 (HR = 5.675, 95% CI: 3.090–10.425), BMPC percentage (HR = 1.018, 95% CI: 1.005–1.031), and LDH (HR = 1.004, 95% CI: 1.001–1.007) were identified as factors influencing OS (Fig. 6). Variables with a P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox regression model. The results demonstrated that mSMART 4.0 high-risk status was an independent adverse prognostic factor for both PFS and OS in patients with NDMM. Specifically, the risk of progression or relapse in high-risk patients was 2.027 times that of standard-risk patients (HR = 2.027, 95% CI: 1.364–3.012), and the risk of death was 5.352 times that of standard-risk patients (HR = 5.352, 95% CI: 2.794–10.250). Additionally, for every 1 U/L increase in LDH, the risk of disease progression or relapse increased by 0.2% (HR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.004) (Tables 6 and 7).

Discussion

MM is characterized by profound heterogeneity in treatment response and clinical outcomes. Consequently, precise prognostic assessment is of paramount importance for optimizing therapeutic strategies. The Mayo Clinic first introduced the mSMART 1.0 system in 2007, a pioneering framework that emphasized the prognostic value of molecular genetic variations to guide clinical decision-making. The latest iteration, mSMART 4.0, features substantial refinements to both its cytogenetic and clinical criteria. To date, the clinical utility of mSMART 4.0 has not been systematically validated within the Chinese MM population. This study retrospectively analyzed clinical data from patients with NDMM to evaluate their clinical characteristics, therapeutic response, and prognosis based on the mSMART 4.0 criteria, aiming to provide robust evidence-based support for its application in clinical practice.

With respect to ASCT uptake, the 30.2% utilization rate identified in our cohort was largely concordant with contemporary data from the National Institute of Hematology and Clinical Excellence (NICHE) registry. Specifically, a recent NICHE registry analysis reported that 34.9% of patients with MM received frontline ASCT in 2023, with annual utilization proportions fluctuating between 22% and 48% over the 2013–2023 period [19]. Accordingly, the ASCT utilization in the present study reliably reflects current real-world clinical practice in China. Furthermore, our study observed no significant differences between the high-risk and standard-risk groups regarding induction regimens or the rate of ASCT. This suggests a current lack of risk-adapted induction therapy in clinical practice. This disparity may be attributed to these factors. First, the NCCN guidelines [20] do not yet provide specific recommendations for treatment selection based strictly on the mSMART 4.0. Second, drug accessibility has historically constrained the precision selection of therapeutic regimens. However, with the ongoing improvement in drug accessibility, implementing risk-stratified treatment protocols is becoming highly feasible in China. Third, ASCT remains a core treatment for MM in China. Although accumulating clinical evidence has challenged the necessity of upfront ASCT, especially for standard-risk patients in the novel-agent era, ASCT still represents an important and highly cost-effective consolidation option under China’s current real-world clinical landscape. Nevertheless, as the accessibility of novel targeted and immune therapies continues to improve nationwide, this treatment paradigm is expected to evolve substantially in China in the near future.

This study demonstrates that the prognostic performance of mSMART 4.0 for overall survival is superior to that of mSMART 3.0 (C-index: 0.738 vs. 0.572). The superior prognostic performance of mSMART 4.0 over version 3.0 may stem from its refinements. On one hand, mSMART 4.0 corrects previous over-stratification: 55.2% of patients formerly classified as high-risk under version 3.0 were downgraded to standard-risk, predominantly those harboring isolated amp 1q or t(4;14). This aligns with emerging evidence that the prognostic impact of amp 1q may be overestimated, as incorporating amp 1q into the R-ISS fails to enhance its predictive accuracy for high-risk stratification [21]. Furthermore, the high-risk significance of IgH translocations is waning, given that continuous PI-based therapy largely abrogates the adverse outcomes associated with isolated t(4;14) [22]. Furthermore, Del 1p is firmly established as a critical high-risk abnormality [23]. While it independently predicts inferior prognosis [24] (though debate remains whether its impact is confined to PFS [25]), its co-occurrence with gain/amp 1q21 synergistically drives profoundly inferior prognosis compared to either lesion alone, marking a distinct ultra-high-risk subgroup [24]. On the other hand, mSMART 4.0 enhances sensitivity by upgrading 22.7% of previously standard-risk patients to high-risk due to the presence of primary plasma cell leukemia, extramedullary disease, or isolated massive β2-MG > 5.5 mg/L with normal renal function. These features represent highly aggressive tumor biology characterized by bone marrow microenvironment escape [26–29] and massive proliferative activity. By accurately reclassifying these ‘occult’ high-risk and ‘over-diagnosed’ standard-risk patients, mSMART 4.0 markedly refines prognostic precision.

Multivariate analysis confirmed that mSMART 4.0 effectively stratified PFS and showed a preliminary association with OS in Chinese patients with MM. Importantly, the OS data remain relatively immature, with a limited number of death events and a median OS estimate in the high-risk group that exceeds the median follow-up duration. Consequently, the OS hazard ratio exhibited a wide confidence interval. Therefore, these OS results should be regarded as preliminary, and longer follow-up is required to robustly confirm the prognostic value of the mSMART 4.0 system for OS in this population. Concurrently, elevated LDH, a surrogate for accelerated tumor glycolysis and microenvironmental hypoxia [30], was identified as an independent predictor of inferior PFS, consistent with established literature across both conventional [31, 32] and novel-agent eras [33]. However, its predictive value did not extend to OS in our cohort, suggesting that while LDH remains a strong harbinger of early relapse, highly effective subsequent therapies may eventually neutralize its long-term impact on OS.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study conducted at a large university-affiliated tertiary hospital that may receive patients with more complex diseases or higher-risk clinical features. It may introduce referral bias and limit the external generalizability of our findings. Second, our inclusion criteria may introduce selection biases. Requiring at least two treatment cycles systematically excluded early deaths, an outcome disproportionately common among patients with high-risk myeloma, which may overestimate PFS and OS. Furthermore, mandating complete FISH and follow-up data of inclusion criteria may exclude real-world patients with emergent complications or poorer performance statuses who miss comprehensive diagnostic workups. Third, due to limited assay accessibility, TP53 mutations and high plasma cell S-phase fraction of the mSMART 4.0 risk-stratification system were not directly evaluated in this retrospective cohort enrolled from 2019 to 2024. This testing gap is consistent with previous real-world myeloma studies in China, which also reflects the practical challenges in fully implementing the mSMART 4.0 stratification system. Furthermore, it highlights an unmet clinical need for standardized, widespread integration of these assays into routine clinical practice. Fourth, treatment heterogeneity is another limitation. Only 6.9% of patients received monoclonal antibody-based regimens from 2019 to 2024 in this study. Given the small sample size of the mAb subgroup, we could not adjust for the mAb treatment effects in survival analyses, leaving residual confounding from variable treatment access and intensity unaccounted for. Larger cohorts receiving standardized modern mAb quadruplet regimens are needed to thoroughly validate the prognostic performance of mSMART 4.0 in the current therapeutic era.

In summary, our systematic evaluation of the prognostic utility of mSMART 4.0 risk stratification in Chinese single-center patients with MM provides a robust scientific basis for its implementation in clinical practice in China.

Conclusions

In this Chinese cohort, mSMART 4.0 effectively stratifies NDMM patients and serves as an independent predictor of inferior survival, demonstrating significantly superior prognostic accuracy over version 3.0. Consequently, these findings strongly support the routine clinical implementation of mSMART 4.0 to guide more precise, risk-adapted therapeutic strategies for MM management in China.
