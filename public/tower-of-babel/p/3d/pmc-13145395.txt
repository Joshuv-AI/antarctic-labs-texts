Long-term aerobic exercise enhances circulating exosomal miR-214-3p to promote endothelial progenitor cell-mediated repair of endothelial damage induced by obesity
Authors: Junhao Huang, Peilun Li, Bing Shen, Huiwen Gao, Shen Wang, Pingyu Wang, Weiji Deng, Dongdong Gao, Min Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Long-term aerobic exercise enhances the function of endothelial progenitor cells via upregulation of circulating exosomal miR-214-3p, thereby promoting the repair of endothelial dysfunction induced by obesity.•miR-214-3p promotes the function of endothelial progenitor cells directly, by activating COL1A2, and indirectly, through the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway.•The myocardium is an important source of the long-term aerobic exercise-induced increase in circulating exosomal miR-214-3p.

Introduction

Endothelial dysfunction is characterized by reduced vascular regulatory capacity and impaired endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation.1, 2, 3 This impairment is associated with a decrease in the reparative functions of circulating endothelial progenitor cells (EPC) within the bloodstream.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Regular aerobic exercise training not only increases the number of circulating EPC but also enhances their proliferative capacity, which is essential for their reparative role in endothelial damage.9, 10, 11, 12 A previous study demonstrated that the number of EPC was promoted in overweight and obese participants after a 3-month physical activity program.9 These findings indicate that exercise training may counteract the detrimental effects of obesity on endothelial function by enhancing the reparative capabilities of EPC; however, the underlying mechanisms of exercise-induced EPC-mediated endothelial repair are still unclear.

In recent years, as exosome-related research has intensified, a substantial body of evidence has emerged demonstrating that exercise training is associated with alterations in the quantity of circulating exosomes and their molecular constituents.13, 14, 15 Exosomes are vital for long-distance cell-to-cell communication and crosstalk between organs; they are capable of being regulated remotely via the circulatory system and crossing key biological barriers, including the cytoplasmic membrane, blood-brain barrier, and gastrointestinal tract.16 Exosomes have been demonstrated to exert their therapeutic effects on stem cells in the cardiovascular system.17, 18, 19, 20 While EPC are mobilized from the bone marrow into the bloodstream, they could be influenced by circulating exosomes, which may represent a possible avenue through which exercise could regulate EPC function. However, the current evidence is considered insufficient to robustly support this perspective.

Herein, we scrutinized the impact of aerobic exercise on endothelial and EPC functionalities in both humans and rodents with obesity. Our previously reported results detected an upregulation of microRNA-214-3p (miR-214-3p) after exercise training, which appears to be a crucial regulator of EPC function.21 To validate our hypothesis, an 8-week aerobic exercise intervention was conducted in both humans and rats with obesity, utilizing techniques from molecular biology to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Methods

Participants

Based on the preliminary experimental data of our laboratory, the comparison threshold was set at 1% (standard deviation = 1.3%). Using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) with the significance level α = 0.05 and power = 80%, the minimum sample size was calculated. After comprehensive consideration, the sample size of each group was determined to be 16. All participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (a) 18–30 years of age, regardless of sex; (b) obesity status, as assessed by body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2; (c) irregular exercise habits (<150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity in a typical week in the past 6 months); (d) able to swim. Participants with a history of myocardial events, lung disease, limited mobility due to neurological conditions, substance addiction (medications or alcohol), or other medical conditions were excluded. A total of 35 obese adults were recruited and randomly assigned to an exercise group or a non-exercise control group using the random number table method. All human studies were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Review Committee of Guangzhou Sport University (No. 2023LCLL-28). Prior to the study, all participants provided informed written consent and were briefed on the procedures and relevant information.

Participants in the exercise group conducted the swimming training 3 days per week for 8 weeks, totaling 24 training sessions. The water temperature of the constant-temperature swimming pool is maintained between 26°C and 28°C. Participants in the control group were requested to maintain their current daily lifestyle and avoid regular exercise during the study. During these periods, participants were asked to maintain their usual dietary habits and physical activities. Vascular function was measured before and after the training program. Participants were not allowed to consume alcohol and caffeine for 24 h before the measurement and were requested to fast for 12 h before the vascular function test. Participants' peripheral blood was collected 24 h after the last exercise session.

Before each session, the exercise group performed a 5-min warm-up on land, followed by a 10-min specialized aquatic warm-up exercise at an intensity of 50%–55% of participant’s maximum heart rate (HRmax). Each session concluded with a 10-min cool-down. Heart rate monitors (V800; Polar Unite, Kempele, Finland) were applied to continuously monitor heart rate. HRmax was obtained by a formula: 220 – age (year). The exercise session performed uninterrupted swimming (breaststroke) for 20 min in the first week and 30 min in the second week at an intensity of 70%–75% HRmax. The intensity was checked every 5 min during the session.

Animals and the exercise training

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from the Guangdong Laboratory Animal Center. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Guangzhou Sport University and followed the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All rats were housed and trained in the Animal Facility of the Scientific Research Center at Guangzhou Sport University. The rearing environment was maintained as follows: room temperature at 22°C‒24°C, relative humidity at 45%‒55%, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and ad libitum access to food and water. Rats were randomly assigned into 3 groups: the normal diet with sedentary group (NC), the high-fat diet with sedentary group (HC), and the high-fat diet with exercise group (HE). To establish a high-fat diet-induced obesity rat model, 6-week-old male SD rats were fed a high-fat diet (45 kcal% Fat, Research Diet, D12451; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) for 10 weeks. Rats fed a standard maintenance diet served as the control group. Body weight and length were measured weekly, and the Lee's index was calculated to assess the degree of obesity in the rats. The Lee's index is defined as body weight (g) divided by the cube root of the length from the nose to the anus (cm).22 The exercise intervention was carried out using a motor-driven treadmill without inclination at 15 m/min for 1 h, 5 times per week for 8 weeks. Rats were anesthetized and sacrificed for blood sampling from the abdominal aorta 24 h after the last exercise session; peripheral blood was collected simultaneously using EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Subsequently, vascular tension detection was performed on the mesenteric arteries, and tissues (e.g., heart, liver, and skeletal muscle) were collected for subsequent use.

The miR-214-3p knockout (KO) rats were prepared and bred according to the protocol provided by a previous study.23 To identify the role of miR-214-3p in the long-term aerobic exercise-induced protective effect against EPC dysfunction induced by obesity in vivo, we utilized miR-214-3p KO rats to establish a high-fat diet-induced obese rat model. Rats were assigned into 4 groups: the wild-type + normal diet with sedentary group (WT + NC), the wild-type + high-fat diet with sedentary group (WT + HC), the wild-type + high-fat diet with exercise group (WT + HE), and the knockout + high-fat diet with exercise group (KO + HE).

Flow-mediated dilation (FMD)

We assessed endothelial function in obese adults by quantifying brachial artery FMD using UNEX EF device (UNEXEF-38G; UNEX Corp., Nagoya, Japan) and following the protocol used in our previous studies.24, 25, 26 After measuring baseline brachial artery diameter, we compressed the brachial artery (at least 50 mmHg above systolic blood pressure) for 5 min, using a blood pressure cuff placed around the forearm. After compression, the maximum brachial artery diameter was measured after cuff release for 2 min. The UNEX EF device calculated %FMD as ((maximum diameter − baseline diameter) / baseline diameter) × 100%.27 The testing period was consistently scheduled from 8:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m. All participants were instructed to observe a fasting period of 12 h prior to the test, refraining from consuming food, alcohol, coffee, and tea. Additionally, they were advised against using medications or supplements known to affect vascular function. During the examination, participants were required to maintain a supine position and engage in a resting state for a duration of 30 min within an ambient temperature range of 22°C‒25°C. All FMD tests were conducted by the same experienced operator.

In vitro arterial tension measurement

Vessel tension measurement was performed as previously reported.28,29 Briefly, we dissected vessel segments of the rat mesenteric arteries in a petri dish, which was filled with ice-cold Krebs solution (118 mmol/L NaCl, 4.7 mmol/L KCl, 2.5 mmol/L CaCl2, 1.2 mmol/L KH2PO4, 1.2 mmol/L MgSO4 • 7 H2O, 25.2 mmol/L NaHCO3, and 11.1 mmol/L glucose, pH 7.4) that was continuously bubbled with 95% oxygen (O2) and 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) mixed gas. The vessel segments were cut into approximately 2 mm rings and then transferred to a myograph chamber (610 M; Danish Myo Technology, Hinnerup, Denmark). The isometric tension was recorded and analyzed using LabChart 8.0 (AD Instruments, Dunedin, New Zealand). Arterial rings were contracted with phenylephrine (1 μmol/L). Once a steady-state tension was obtained, acetylcholine (ACh) was added to the bath in a cumulative fashion to obtain concentration-response curves.

Isolation of circulating exosomes

Plasma was collected and centrifuged at 400g for 35 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. A secondary spin was performed at 2000g for 20 min at 4°C to remove platelets. The supernatant was then transferred to a 13.2 mL ultracentrifuge tube, diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to two-thirds the tube's capacity and ultracentrifuged at 170,000g for 90 min to pellet exosomes. The obtained exosome samples were observed for morphology by transmission electron microscopy, detected for diameter using a NanoSight NS300 (Malvern, UK) nanosizer, and analyzed for exosomal marker proteins CD63 and TSG101 by Western blotting.

Quantitative analysis of EPC

EPC were identified as CD34+/KDR+ cells in peripheral blood.30 Blood samples were collected in EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes (366643; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and treated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-KDR antibody (359914; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and Allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-CD34 antibody (17-0349-42, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) in accordance with the product instructions. Enumeration of EPC was conducted on a Cytomics FC500 flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) with a sample volume of 40,000 events, using fluorescently labeled iso-matched antibodies as controls. Data analysis was performed using CytoDiff CXP 2.0 (Beckman Coulter).

microRNA (miRNA) sequencing

The exosome samples obtained from both human individuals and rats were used for miRNA sequencing, which was performed by a specialized company using Illumina sequencing method. After the library's construction and preparation were completed, the constructed library was sequenced with HiSeq 2500 Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The bioinformatics analysis of the sequencing results was composed of 3 main steps: quality evaluation of sequencing data, comparative analysis of enriched data, and deep transcriptional analysis.

For functional enrichment analysis, all differentially expressed miRNAs were mapped to terms in the Gene Ontology (GO) database, and then significantly enriched GO terms were searched among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using p < 0.05 as the threshold. GO term analysis was classified into 3 subgroups: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. All differentially expressed miRNAs were mapped to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database and searched for significantly enriched KEGG pathways at p < 0.05 level.

Cell culture and proliferation/migration assays

Human bone marrow-derived EPC were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China) and cultured in EPC-specific medium (CM-H162; Procell). All cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment. Only 1‒2 passages of the cells were used for subsequent experiments. Proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Wuhan, China), and migration was evaluated using a scratch test assay. The plates were incubated at 37°C, and optical density values and microscopic images were recorded using a microplate reader.

Cell transfection

Hsa-miR-214-3p mimics and mimics negative control (mimics NC) were designed and synthesized by Genebio (Shanghai, China). The transfection sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates, and transfection was conducted while cell growth density reached 70%–80%. Briefly, the original medium in each well was discarded, and after a single wash with PBS solution, 900 μL of serum-free M199 medium (PM150612; Procell) was added. In a centrifuge tube, 1 μL of LipofectamineTM 3000 (No. L3000008, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was mixed with 49 μL of Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher). In another tube, 5 μL of miRNA preservation solution was mixed with 45 μL of Opti-MEM. After a 5-min incubation at room temperature, these 2 mixtures were combined in equal proportions. Following a 15-min incubation at room temperature, the mixture was added to the corresponding wells and placed in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C for 6 h. Subsequently, the transfection solution was aspirated, and EPC-specific medium was added.

Western blot analysis

RIPA lysate (89900; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was employed for the extraction of total cellular proteins from the tested cells. The supernatant was collected, and the total protein content was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (G2026-200T; Servicebio). Equal amounts of proteins (40 μg/lane) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels, separated by electrophoresis, and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using a wet transfer system. The PVDF membrane papers were blocked with 5% skim milk ((phosphate buffered saline with tween 20 (PBST) solution consisting of 5% skim milk powder and 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C. The primary antibodies used included PI3K (SC-365290, 1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), Akt1 (SC-5298, 1:1000; Santa Cruz), p-Akt (Ser473) (66444-1-lg, 1:1000; Proteintech Group, Rosemont, IL, USA), phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) (60300-1-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech), tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101) (DF8427, 1:1000; Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA), cluster of differentiation 63 (CD63) (AF5117, 1:1000; Affinity), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (GB15002-100, 1:4000; Servicebio). After washing out, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The target bands were visualized using the Tanon-5200 multi chemiluminescent imaging system (Shanghai, China).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNAs from tissues, cells, and exosomes were extracted using Trizol (R0016; Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. mRNA samples underwent reverse transcription using the Evo M-MLV Kit (AG11705; Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China), while miRNA samples were reversely transcribed using the All-in-One™ miRNA qPCR Kit (QP115; iGene Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China), following the provided instructions. Experimental procedures were conducted using the ABI Quant Studio 6 Flex real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). β-actin was used as an internal control for mRNA, and U6 was used as an internal control for miRNA. The relative transcription levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Dual-luciferase reporter assay

293T cells were routinely cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1.5 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in a 37°C, 5%CO₂ incubator with saturated humidity. The dual-luciferase wild-type and mutant plasmids were designed and synthesized by General Biology (Anhui) (Supplementary Table 2). For transfection, 293T cells at 80%–90% confluence were used. miRNA was dissolved in diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated water (DEPC-H₂O) at 20 μM (125 μL per 1 OD₂₆₀). In a 1.5 mL EP tube, 100 μL serum-free Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM), 2 μL miRNA, and 0.6 μg of the reporter plasmid were mixed. In another 1.5 mL EP tube, 100 μL serum-free DMEM and 2 μL Lipofectamine 2000 were mixed and added to the cells, which were then incubated for 5 h. After removing the transfection mixture, 500 μL DMEM with 10% FBS was added, and cells were cultured for another 24 h. Cells were then collected for dual-luciferase assays using a BioTek (Winooski, VT, USA) Synergy 2 system. Luciferase activity was measured with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the protocol. Results were recorded as firefly luciferase/renilla luciferase ratios and normalized to the empty psiCHECK2 vector for statistical analysis.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS24 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. For the human study, data from pre- and post-measurements were analyzed using two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), with the 2 independent variables being the grouping factor (exercise group vs. control group) and the repeated factor (before intervention vs. after intervention). While a significant interaction effect between the 2 independent variables was observed, simple effects were used for comparison; otherwise, the main effects were examined. The criteria for effect size (partial η²) in analysis of variance were as follows: 0.01 for a small effect size, 0.06 for a medium effect size, and 0.14 or higher for a large effect size. For normally distributed data, comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using the independent Student’s t test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For transcriptome sequencing data, DEGs or miRNAs were identified using a threshold of |log2 fold change| > 1 and a false discovery rate < 0.05. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (Prism 8; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and all statistical tests were two-tailed unless otherwise specified.

Results

Exercise training promoted EPC-mediated repair of endothelial damage in both humans and rats with obesity

The baseline characteristics of participants were detailed in Supplementary Table 3. To account for the potential influence of baseline fat mass and body fat percentage, we conducted a repeated measures ANOVA with these variables included as covariates. The interaction effect between the control group and the exercise group on FMD values was significant (p = 0.033, η² = 0.139, medium effect size), while no main effects were observed (Fig. 1A). Additionally, neither significant interaction effects nor main effects were observed for baseline fat mass and body fat percentage, thus they are considered to have no significant impact on the results. To further investigate the impacts of the experimental intervention on FMD in both groups, t tests were conducted on the differences in FMD values before and after the 8-week intervention for each group. The results revealed a significant difference in the changes of FMD between these groups (Fig. 1B). In summary, the FMD value of obese participants was influenced by the interaction of the experimental intervention method and time. An 8-week regimen of exercise training significantly enhanced FMD in the exercise group. Moreover, a significant increase in the number of EPC was observed in the exercise group compared to the control group (Fig. 1C and 1D). To further substantiate the effects of exercise on endothelial function, a high-fat diet-induced obese rat model was utilized (Fig. 1E). The body weight changes of the rats are depicted in Fig. 1F. From Week 1 to Week 10, a high-fat diet was used to induce an obesity model, during which the body weights of the HC and HE groups increased rapidly and were significantly higher than that of the NC group. From Week 10 to Week 18, rats in the HE group performed exercise training while continuously fed the high-fat diet. The body weights of rats in the HE group tended to stabilize, while those of the HC group continued to increase. To avoid the impact of individual differences in body weight data due to size differences on the modeling effect, we recorded and analyzed the body weight and length data of rats at Week 10 and Week 18 to calculate the Lee’s index (Fig. 1G). Our results showed that, compared to the NC group, the Lee’s index of the HC and HE groups at Week 10 significantly increased. However, at Week 18, the Lee's index of the HE group was significantly lower than that of the HC group. These results indicated that the animal model of obesity and exercise intervention was successfully established. As shown in Fig. 1H, ACh-induced vasorelaxation was markedly attenuated in the HC group compared to that in the NC group; however, this attenuation was significantly reversed in the HE group. Similarly, compared to rats in the NC group, rats in the HC group exhibited higher half maximal effective concentration (EC50) values for ACh, and this enhancement was significantly inhibited in the HE group (Fig. 1I). However, we examined phenylephrine-induced vasoconstriction and found no significant differences among groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A). These results indicated that long-term aerobic exercise protected against endothelial dysfunction in rats with obesity. Furthermore, compared to rats in the NC group, rats in the HC group showed a significant decrease in peripheral blood EPC levels, and this attenuation was significantly reversed in the HE group (Fig. 1J). Taken together, the above-mentioned results indicated that 8 weeks of aerobic exercise training promoted EPC-mediated repair of endothelial damage in both humans and rats with obesity.

Exercise-derived circulating exosomes exerted protective effects against EPC dysfunction in both humans and rats with obesity

We further evaluated circulating exosome levels in obese people before and after an 8-week exercise intervention. Circulating exosomes were extracted, followed by identification and quantification (Fig. 2A and 2B). We observed no significant variation in exosome number following the exercise training program (Fig. 2C). We then examined the effects of exercise training on circulating exosomes in rats. Circulating exosomes were extracted from rat peripheral blood, followed by identification and quantification (Fig. 2G). Our results showed no significant change in the number of circulating exosomes among all 3 groups (Fig. 2H). We then incubated EPC with exosomes from the 3 groups and observed that exosomes derived from the HC group significantly inhibited EPC proliferation and migration compared to those from the NC group. Importantly, exosomes derived from the HE group were able to reverse the decline in EPC proliferation and migration induced by obesity (Fig. 2I–2K). Collectively, these results indicated that exercise-derived circulating exosomes exerted a protective effect against EPC dysfunction induced by obesity, thereby potentially promoting the repair of endothelial damage in both humans and rats with obesity.

miR-214-3p was a key component in exercise-derived circulating exosome-induced protective effects on EPC function in both humans and rats with obesity

To elucidate whether miRNAs are involved in exercise-derived circulating exosome-induced protective effects on EPC function, we further conducted miRNA sequencing analysis to compare the expression of miRNAs within circulating exosomes in obese individuals before and after exercise intervention. Differentially expressed miRNAs are shown in Fig. 3A. Our examination of existing literature, in conjunction with the observed upregulation of miR-214-3p in the present study as well as our previous study,31 points to the potential of miR-214-3p as a key regulator of EPC function. We then examined whether long-term aerobic exercise training increased circulating exosomal miR-214-3p in rats with obesity. Through transcriptome sequencing analysis of miRNAs expression in circulating exosomes from obese rats, we observed that miR-214-3p expression in the HE group was markedly higher compared to that in the HC group (Fig. 3B), which aligns with our human experimental outcomes. The results of our qPCR further confirmed that the expression of miR-214-3p was significantly upregulated in circulating exosomes from obese adults after exercise training (Fig. 3C). Additionally, the qPCR data showed that the expression of miR-214-3p in circulating exosomes from rats was markedly reduced in the HC group compared to the NC group. Our hypothesis was further substantiated by Western blot analysis, which demonstrated significant upregulation of PI3K and phosphorylated Akt (Ser473) protein levels in miR-214-3p-overexpressing EPC, without a significant change in total Akt protein level (Fig. 4C and 4D). This suggested that miR-214-3p may regulate the phosphorylation of Akt, thereby potentially modulating EPC function through the PI3K-Akt pathway. PTEN, a negative regulator of the PI3K pathway, was also found to be associated with the effects of miR-214-3p.33 Furthermore, PTEN has been demonstrated to be a target gene of miR-214-3p.34,35 Our Western blot result showed that the expression of PTEN was significantly decreased in miR-214-3p-overexpressing EPC (Fig. 4E). We further utilized a PI3K inhibitor to investigate the role of the PI3K-Akt pathway in miR-214-3p-induced EPC proliferation (Fig. 4F). Our results demonstrated that the proliferative capacity of miR-214-3p-overexpressing EPC was significantly reduced in the presence of the PI3K inhibitor LY294002, underscoring the functional significance of the PI3K-Akt pathway in mediating the effects of miR-214-3p on EPC proliferation. Taken together, the present findings suggested that miR-214-3p may influence the PI3K-Akt pathway by regulating the expression of PTEN in EPC.

Collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2) is the regulating target in the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway mediated by miR-214-3p

Through an exhaustive analysis of our sequencing data, we identified genes significantly enriched in the PI3K-Akt pathway and found the expression of COL1A2 significantly upregulated in EPC overexpressing miR-214-3p compared to the control (Fig. 4G). COL1A2 has been demonstrated to be a key gene in the regulation of endothelial function in the vasculature.36,37 We further found that overexpression of miR-214-3p significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity of EPC, which was abolished upon silencing of the COL1A2 gene (Fig. 4H). Initially, we hypothesized that COL1A2 is a target gene of miR-214-3p. We designed a luciferase assay and verified that miR-214-3p reduced luciferase activity for wild-type COL1A2 but had no effect when the miR-214-3p binding site was mutated, confirming that miR-214-3p directly targeted COL1A2 (Fig. 4I and 4J). However, upon co-incubation with a PI3K inhibitor and miR-214-3p mimics, we found that expression of COL1A2 was also suppressed while PI3K was inhibited (Fig. 4K). These results suggest that the regulation of COL1A2 by miR-214-3p is dependent on the expression of PI3K. Therefore, the interaction between miR-214-3p and COL1A2 is not limited to a direct targeting relationship but also involves indirect regulation dependent on a PI3K-mediated pathway. It has been demonstrated that the PI3K-Akt pathway can induce COL1A2 expression.38, 39, 40, 41 Therefore, it is plausible that COL1A2 may serve as a regulatory target within the PTEN-PI3K-Akt axis mediated by miR-214-3p. Collectively, these results suggested that COL1A2 plays a crucial role in the miR-214-3p-mediated regulation of EPC function.

Origin of exercise-induced increase in exosomal miR-214-3p

Our study also probed into the tissue origin of exercise-induced exosomal miR-214-3p. Aerobic exercise is known to enhance cardiopulmonary function and boost oxygen consumption in skeletal muscle. Additionally, literature indicates that aerobic exercise prompts the liver to release extracellular vesicles that foster angiogenesis.42 Consequently, we assessed the expression levels of mature miR-214-3p and its precursor (pre-miR-214-3p) in skeletal muscle, myocardium, and liver tissues of rats subjected to high-fat diets with and without aerobic exercise intervention. These tissue samples were obtained from the animal experiments described above, with the grouping details provided in Fig. 1. Our results showed that the expression levels of both mature miR-214-3p and pre-miR-214-3p in the myocardium of the HE group were significantly enhanced compared to those in the HC group (Fig. 5H). However, the expression levels of mature miR-214-3p and pre-miR-214-3p were unchanged in skeletal muscle and liver tissues from obese rats with and without exercise training. These results imply that the myocardium is an important origin of the long-term aerobic exercise-induced increase in circulating exosomal miR-214-3p.

Discussion

Exosomal miR-214-3p is a novel mediator of exercise-induced vascular protection

Our study for the first time demonstrated that long-term aerobic exercise can enhance the expression of miR-214-3p in circulating exosomes, which regulates COL1A2 expression directly as well as indirectly, through the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway in EPC. This mechanism enhanced the proliferation and migration of EPC, thereby promoting the repair of endothelial dysfunction induced by obesity in both humans and animals (Fig. 6). Obesity-related endothelial dysfunction, influenced by inflammatory and metabolic factors, could be significantly improved by aerobic exercise.43 Aerobic exercise plays a crucial role in promoting the mobilization of EPC from bone marrow, which is instrumental in augmenting endothelial function.44 Although we have previously reported the positive effect of aerobic exercise on EPC function in obese individuals,31 the underlying mechanisms of exercise-induced protective effects against EPC dysfunction induced by obesity are still unknown. Exercise-induced release of exosomes from various tissues can exert systemic biological effects through circulation,45 suggesting that the positive effects of aerobic exercise on endothelial function might be mediated by inducing the release of tissue-derived exosomes into circulation, thereby promoting the function of EPC. Ma et al.11 had shown that exercise stimulated the release of EPC-derived exosomes, which enhanced angiogenesis and endothelial repair by modulating miR-126, a key miRNA within these vesicles. Additionally, another study identified that exercise induced the secretion of exosomes from rat aortic endothelial cells into the circulation, contributing to the amelioration of myocardial damage.46 These findings provide novel insights and theoretical support for further exploration of the mechanisms by which aerobic exercise ameliorates cardiovascular dysfunction induced by obesity. In the present study, our quantitative analysis of human and rat circulating exosomes disclosed no changes in the amount of exosomes after 8 weeks of exercise training, which is in line with results from the previous study.46 However, the circulating exosomes isolated from exercised humans and rats exerted a protective effect against obesity-induced EPC dysfunction as evidenced by enhanced EPC proliferation and migration, thereby promoting the repair of endothelial dysfunction. Our transcriptome sequencing demonstrated notable alterations in the expression levels of miRNAs within exercise-derived circulating exosomes, among which miR-214-3p expression was not only upregulated in both humans and rats with obesity after exercise but also had a protective effect against obesity-induced EPC dysfunction both in vitro and in vivo. These results showed that long-term aerobic exercise-derived exosomes enriched with miR-214-3p exerted a protective effect against obesity-induced endothelial damage.

miR-214-3p enhances EPC function via the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway and direct regulation of COL1A2

Emerging evidence underscores the significance of miR-214-3p in vascular homeostasis and neoangiogenesis.47 Yang et al.48 identified a significant association between plasma miR-214 level and endothelial dysfunction in hyperuricemia patients. However, the regulatory mechanisms of miR-214-3p on EPC function are still unclear. Our transcriptome sequencing showed the PI3K-Akt pathway had the highest significance, and Western blot analysis further demonstrated significant upregulation of PI3K and phosphorylated Akt protein levels in miR-214-3p-overexpressing EPC. Furthermore, PI3K inhibitor LY294002 significantly reduced miR-214-3p-induced EPC proliferation. These results suggest that miR-214-3p modulates EPC function through the PI3K-Akt pathway. In addition, numerous studies have employed luciferase assays to confirm the targeting relationship between miR-214-3p and PTEN, establishing that PTEN is a target gene of miR-214-3p.34,35 In the present study, we found that the expression of PTEN was significantly decreased in miR-214-3p-overexpressing EPC. Our result is consistent with a previous study demonstrating miR-214’s role in myocardial fibrosis and cardiac mesenchymal transition in viral myocarditis through the PTEN-PI3K-Akt signaling,33 which reinforces the mechanistic importance of our findings. Based on the collective evidence, we proposed that exosomal miR-214-3p, induced by exercise, enhanced EPC proliferation and migration through the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway. miR-214 has been previously associated with the cellular redox state and Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (Reduced) (NADH) levels,49 suggesting it is responsive to energy status. A previous study showed that the circulating expression level of miR-214-3p was significantly reduced in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D), indicating that the insulin resistance or metabolic abnormalities in patients with T2D may be associated with downregulated miR-214 expression.50 In addition, the PI3K-Akt pathway has been demonstrated to be a key player in insulin signaling and glucose metabolism regulation.51, 52, 53 Given its known interaction with the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway, miR-214-3p may play an important role in exercise-improved insulin resistance in patients with obesity or T2D via the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway.

We also discovered that COL1A2 plays a crucial role in miR-214-3p-mediated EPC function. Our transcriptome sequencing and qPCR data revealed the upregulated expression of COL1A2 in EPC overexpressing miR-214-3p. Moreover, silencing of the COL1A2 gene abolished miR-214-3p-induced EPC proliferation. Meanwhile, the upregulated expression of COL1A2 in EPC overexpressing miR-214-3p could be completely inhibited by the PI3K inhibitor, LY294002. This is further evidence that miR-214-3p directly targets COL1A2. These results suggest that, in addition to indirect regulation of COL1A2 through the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway, miR-214-3p may also directly regulate COL1A2. However, there is insufficient compelling evidence to prove this hypothesis, and the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Especially considering potential interference from the indirect PTEN-mediated pathway, the regulatory mechanisms of miR-214-3p on PTEN and COL1A2 could be related to different seed region backgrounds, mRNA secondary structures, or Akt-induced protein synthesis. Thus, further verification is needed to confirm the direct regulatory effect of miR-214-3p on COL1A2.

COL1A2, a quintessential fibroblast marker, has been identified as a key player in the basement membrane formation of endothelial cells,36 and it plays an integral role in vascular repair and angiogenesis by modulating VEGF to stimulate blood vessel formation.37 Within the PI3K-Akt signaling cascade, COL1A2 is an important protein, with Akt phosphorylation being partially reliant on COL1A2 expression.38 It has been demonstrated that the PI3K-Akt pathway can induce COL1A2 expression, thereby facilitating the synthesis of type I collagen.39, 40, 41 These studies provide strong support for the conclusions drawn in this study, highlighting the significant role of COL1A2 in promoting EPC function.

The present study has some strengths. Firstly, through experiments in both humans and rodents, this study confirms the protective impact of long-term aerobic exercise-induced exosomes against obesity-induced EPC dysfunction, suggesting a novel mechanism underlying exercise-mediated vascular protection. Secondly, this study is the first to reveal the regulatory mechanisms of exercise-induced increases of circulating exosomal miR-214-3p on EPC function using molecular biology and bioinformatics approaches, and the first to validate the in vivo effects of miR-214-3p using a gene KO rat model. However, the study also has some limitations. Firstly, due to the limited number of KO rats, the quantity of circulating exosomes obtained was insufficient, which prevented us from analyzing the number of circulating EPC in KO rats. Secondly, given the potential of land-based exercises to increase the risk of injury such as joint injury in obese individuals, swimming was selected as an alternative exercise modality. However, this choice introduces potential confounding factors. To validate the effects of aerobic exercise and to minimize such confounding influences, the exercise modality was altered in the animal experiments. This approach demonstrates that the improvement in endothelial function attributed to aerobic exercise is relatively independent of the specific modality of exercise training. Nevertheless, this methodological adjustment also has its limitations. Thirdly, we have noted the limitation that cardiopulmonary fitness wasn’t measured at the baseline for participant grouping. Fourthly, in the cell experiment, since the main research purpose was to compare the effects of exosomes from the control group and exercise group on the function of EPC, a non-treated control group was not set up. Finally, although our data imply that the myocardium is an important origin of long-term exercise-induced increase of circulating exosomal miR-214-3p, current evidence is not sufficient to confirm the source of miR-214-3p-carrying circulating exosomes, and more rigorous experiments are needed in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, our research confirmed the beneficial impact of long-term aerobic exercise on the endothelial function and the proliferation and migration of EPC in both humans and rodents with obesity. Aerobic exercise stimulated an increase in miR-214-3p within circulating exosomes, which enhanced EPC function directly, by activating COL1A2, and indirectly, through the PTEN-PI3K-Akt pathway, thereby promoting the repair of endothelial dysfunction induced by obesity. These findings not only help refine our understanding of the mechanisms by which aerobic exercise promotes cardiovascular health but also lay a theoretical foundation for the prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation of cardiovascular diseases.
