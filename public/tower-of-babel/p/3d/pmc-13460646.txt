Avascular Necrosis After a Supracondylar Humerus Fracture Resulting in Capitellar Dissolution and Fishtail Deformity: An 11-Year Follow-Up Case Report
Authors: James G Gamble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A six-year-old boy sustained a displaced and rotated supracondylar humerus fracture (SCHF) after falling from the monkey bars. He underwent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning using two lateral-entry Kirschner wires. He had an uneventful postoperative recovery and returned to full normal activity. A routine follow-up radiograph obtained one year after the injury showed that the capitellum ossific nucleus was absent, presumably due to dissolution caused by avascular necrosis (AVN). He also had AVN of the trochlea. Despite these radiographic findings, he was completely asymptomatic. He was treated with activity restrictions to avoid upper-extremity weight-bearing for two years. Eleven years after the fracture, he remained asymptomatic, with an elbow range of motion of 5°-135°. His radiograph demonstrated a fishtail deformity of the distal humerus resulting from the effects of AVN on the distal humeral growth plate.

Introduction

Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) is a safe and effective procedure for most children who sustain supracondylar humerus fractures (SCHF) [1,2,3]. Complications are few and usually resolve with appropriate treatment [4,5]. This low complication rate is remarkable given the high frequency of these fractures, which account for approximately 15% of all fractures in children and more than half of all pediatric elbow fractures [6].

It is important to document the treatment and outcomes of rare complications of pediatric elbow fractures. Physicians who encounter a similar problem can consult the literature for clinical information to help guide their own treatment recommendations. The purpose of this case report is to present a rare complication of SCHF, namely, avascular necrosis (AVN) of the distal fragment, with dissolution of the capitellum, delayed ossification of the trochlea, and, ultimately, a fishtail deformity of the distal humerus. The patient and family were informed that information and radiographs concerning the case would be submitted for publication, and they provided consent.

Case presentation

A six-year-and-one-month-old boy sustained a displaced right SCHF of his dominant arm after falling from the monkey bars at school (Figure 1).

Elbow radiograph of a six-year-old boy after a fall from the monkey bars.

Preoperative radiograph of the right elbow showing a displaced and rotated type III supracondylar humerus fracture (SCHF).

He underwent CRPP using two lateral pins approximately 10 hours after the injury. Both pins were in acceptable positions after the first pass, and the fracture was stable during passive flexion and extension. The pins were removed three weeks after the operation, and three months later, the elbow range of motion was from 30° to 110°, with full supination and pronation. The radiographs showed satisfactory alignment with a normal-appearing capitellar ossific nucleus (Figures 2A-2B).

(A) Anteroposterior and (B) lateral radiographs showing normal ossific density of the capitellum and early fracture union.

CRPP: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

The boy returned one year after the injury for a scheduled re-evaluation. He was asymptomatic, and his elbow range of motion was from 5° to 135°. Radiographs disclosed the absence of the capitellum ossific nucleus (Figures 3A-3B).

Radiographs obtained one year after CRPP.

(A) Anteroposterior and (B) lateral radiographs obtained when the patient was seven years old. The capitellum ossific nucleus, which was present on the initial postoperative radiographs, is now absent.

CRPP: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

Since he had returned to normal activities and was asymptomatic, it was recommended that he use the arm normally but restrict playground climbing and weight-bearing exercises such as push-ups.

Two years after the injury, his elbow range of motion remained from 5° to 135°, with full supination and pronation, and his carrying angle was normal and symmetrical to that of the uninjured contralateral extremity. Radiographs of the elbow disclosed normal alignment with patchy ossific densities in the epiphyses (Figures 4A-4B).

Radiographs obtained two years after CRPP.

(A) Anteroposterior and (B) lateral radiographs showing normal alignment with small, irregular, patchy ossific densities in the capitellum.

MRI was performed to assess the status of the articular cartilage and physis and to rule out any mechanical block to full extension. The articular surfaces were smooth with normal contours, and no mechanical block to extension was identified. An unexpected finding was that the trochlea showed focal cystic changes indicating the sequelae of AVN (Figures 5A-5B).

MRI obtained two years after CRPP using a 3-Tesla scanner.

(A) Anteroposterior and (B) lateral T1-weighted MRIs (TR, 2500 ms; TE, 33.2 ms) showing cystic changes within the trochlea, absence of the capitellum ossific nucleus, and no abnormal signal within the articular cartilage.

CRPP: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

Based on the MRI findings, we recommended that he continue normal movement while restricting weight-bearing activities.

Three years after the injury, he used the arm normally without discomfort. His elbow range of motion was unchanged at 5° to 135°, with full supination and pronation. The radiographs disclosed normal alignment on the AP view, with an irregularly shaped ossification center of the capitellum. No ossification was apparent in the trochlea (Figures 6A-6B).

Radiographs obtained three years after CRPP.

(A) Anteroposterior and (B) lateral radiographs obtained at nine years of age, showing irregular ossification of the capitellum and delayed appearance of the secondary ossification center of the trochlea, which normally appears around seven to eight years of age.

CRPP: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

When the patient was 12 years old, the radiographs again showed normal coronal-plane alignment, and a small fishtail deformity was apparent at the center of the distal humerus (Figure 7).

Radiograph obtained six years after CRPP.

Anteroposterior radiograph demonstrating ossification of the capitellum and a somewhat irregular radiocapitellar articulation. The trochlea remains dysplastic, and ossification is absent in the region between the capitellum and trochlea.

CRPP: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

The patient was lost to follow-up but returned four years later, when he was 16 years old, because he had started weight training and was experiencing discomfort in the posterolateral aspect of his elbow. His range of motion, measured with a goniometer, was from 5° to 135°, with full supination and pronation, essentially unchanged from that measured previously. The radiographs disclosed a fishtail deformity of the distal humerus (Figure 8).

Radiograph obtained 10 years after CRPP.

Anteroposterior radiograph obtained at 16 years of age, showing a fishtail deformity of the distal humerus.

CRPP: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

His symptoms resolved with rest and anti-inflammatory medication. At the latest clinical follow-up, when he was 17 years old, he was completely asymptomatic, participated fully in activities, and had a range of motion of 5° to 135°, with full supination and pronation.

Discussion

This case involves a six-year-old boy who sustained a displaced SCHF after a fall from the monkey bars at school. He underwent CRPP and had an uneventful postoperative recovery. One year later, the ossific nucleus of the capitellum was absent, presumably having dissolved as a result of AVN. The diagnosis of AVN was made retrospectively by noting the absence of radiographic density in the capitellum where it had previously been present (Figures 2-3).

AVN of the capitellum causing dissolution (disappearance) of the ossific nucleus is rare. The Danish radiologist Panner HJ (1871-1930) reported three cases of the condition in 1927, all without an apparent cause [7]. All three children eventually had restoration of normal ossification, but in Panner HJ’s first case, it took more than three years until, as he noted, “the osseous center again assumes a more or less normal aspect.” Since then, it has been observed that most cases of Panner disease occur in boys and are unilateral [8]. Most cases eventually become “more or less normal,” as Panner HJ noted. Treatment recommendations for the condition include a brief period of immobilization, rest, anti-inflammatory medication, and avoidance of upper-extremity impact activities. The available evidence indicates that Panner disease is self-limiting and that resolution can take 18 to 24 months [8].

Only a few cases of AVN of the capitellum following an SCHF have been reported in the English-language literature. Kim MB et al. presented the case of a three-year-old boy who developed what they called “transient osteochondrosis” of the capitellum after CRPP [9]. In their patient, the ossific nucleus reappeared two years later. Ruiz PD et al. reported a similar case in a five-year-old boy after a nondisplaced SCHF [10]. Another case was discussed only in an abstract [11]. AVN of the capitellum is a known complication of lateral condyle fractures [12,13], as is posttraumatic AVN of the trochlea [14,15]. However, AVN after SCHF is extremely rare.

Our patient had complete dissolution of the capitellar ossific nucleus, and the radiographic appearance never completely returned to normal (Figure 8). He also had AVN of the trochlea, as demonstrated by the cystic changes noted on MRI (Figure 5) and delayed ossification. The patient did not develop cubitus varus or valgus, but he did lose 5° of elbow extension, and the central aspect of the physis did not develop normally, resulting in a fishtail deformity of the distal humerus. A fishtail deformity of the elbow is a concave abnormality in the shape of the articular surface at the center of the distal humerus between the capitellum and trochlea [15-18]. AVN in this area is most likely due to the tenuous nature and longitudinal orientation of the vascular supply in the region between the capitellum and trochlea [19].

Little is known about the long-term outcomes of a fishtail deformity of the elbow. Ishii and coworkers reported a case of fishtail deformity after a nondisplaced supracondylar fracture in a seven-year-and-eight-month-old boy. The growth deformity became apparent three years after the injury, and at the 10-year follow-up, he had lost 20° of extension [20]. Sawyer E et al. [21] reported a case of fishtail deformity after a type II supracondylar humeral fracture. Their patient developed pain and early arthritis; however, the patient had a loose body in the elbow that most likely caused the damage. Joseph EG and Gardner WE 2nd [22] reported the case of an intra-articular distal humerus fracture in a 28-year-old man who had a fishtail deformity before sustaining the fracture. The authors were unable to use standard distal lag-screw fixation of the articular fragments because of the deformity. However, they used more proximally placed screws, with a satisfactory outcome. Lehnert SJ et al. reported seven adolescents with fishtail deformity [23]. Six patients were symptomatic, with a high incidence of radiocapitellar joint abnormalities, including osteochondritis dissecans.

Almost a quarter of a million children are treated annually in US EDs for playground equipment-related injuries [24]. More than half of all serious playground fractures are caused by falls from jungle gyms and monkey bars [24]. Given the frequency of these fractures, one might assume that orthopedic surgeons had invented these structures. However, that would be incorrect. Both the jungle gym and monkey bars were invented in 1920 by Sebastian Hinton, an attorney from Winnetka, Illinois. His first structure was a 3D cube of interconnected pipes called the jungle gym. To this, he added a horizontal ladder called the monkey bars [25].

Conclusions

The case presented here is that of a six-year-old boy who fell from the monkey bars at school and sustained a displaced type III SCHF that resulted in AVN after CRPP. The AVN involved both the capitellum and trochlea, representing a very rare complication. The displacement and rotation of the distal fragment most likely compromised the vascular supply at the time of injury. One important aspect of this case is that the patient was essentially asymptomatic and that the AVN was discovered incidentally on a routine follow-up radiograph. Furthermore, revascularization and remodeling took years, and the radiographic appearance never returned to normal. Our treatment for AVN consisted of restricting weight-bearing and avoiding impact activities to prevent collapse of the joint surface. Otherwise, we permitted the patient to move and use his arm normally. Ultimately, he had only a 5° loss of elbow extension. At his latest evaluation, when he was 17 years old (11 years after the fracture), he remained asymptomatic and had normal daily use of the extremity. However, he had developed a fishtail deformity of the distal humerus, causing incongruity of the joint surface, and this abnormality may eventually result in symptoms and arthritic changes.
