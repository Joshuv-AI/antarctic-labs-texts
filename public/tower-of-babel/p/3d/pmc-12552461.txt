ABCD2 improves the diagnostic accuracy of carotid artery stenosis when combined with CT angiography
Authors: ZiWei Hou, Chen Chen, Hong Liu, TaoMing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To prospectively evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of combining head and neck computed tomography angiography (CTA) with the ABCD2 score (Age, Blood pressure, Clinical features, Duration, Diabetes score) for detecting carotid artery stenosis in patients with transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) over an extended follow-up period. In this single-center prospective cohort study, 416 TIA patients (January 2020–January 2025) were stratified into high-risk (ABCD2 ≥4, n = 200, 48.08%) and low-risk (ABCD2 <4, n = 216, 51.92%) groups. High-risk patients underwent head-neck CTA within 2 hours of symptom onset, followed by digital subtraction angiography (DSA) within 48 hours if CTA revealed ≥50% stenosis. Low-risk patients received CTA within 24 hours, with DSA escalation for clinical deterioration. Diagnostic confirmation required combined ABCD2 ≥4 and CTA ≥50% stenosis in high-risk patients. DSA served as the reference standard. Diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were analyzed using chi-square tests. Among 416 patients, 231 underwent both CTA and DSA. CTA detected cerebrovascular stenosis/occlusion in 169 cases (70.13%), showing significant discordance with DSA (χ2 = 14.785, P = 0.005). ABCD2 scores ≥4 were observed in 157/231 patients (67.97%), significantly correlating with cerebrovascular disease risk (χ2 = 14.351, P < 0.001). The combined approach of CTA and ABCD2 score demonstrated a diagnostic accuracy of 88.74%, sensitivity of 91.15%, and specificity of 76.92% for post-TIA cerebrovascular stenosis. These values showed statistically significant improvements compared to either method alone (all P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed versus the reference standard DSA (χ2 = 0.914, P = 0.339). Head and neck CTA combined with the ABCD2 score offers significant advantages in the early diagnosis of carotid artery stenosis in TIA patients.

Introduction

Transient ischemic attack (TIA) is defined as a transient, reversible, localized disturbance in blood flow to the brain that may recur, with each episode typically lasting from a few minutes to an hour. Symptoms and signs usually disappear completely within 24 hours1. Typical symptoms of a TIA include speech disturbances, visual impairments, and unilateral numbness or weakness in the face, arm, or leg muscles2. Research indicates that patients who have experienced a TIA exhibit a significantly elevated risk of subsequent stroke, with approximately 30% to 40% of stroke cases being preceded by a TIA history. Although episodes of TIA typically resolve within minutes, in some instances, symptoms may persist for up to 24 hours, thereby increasing the risk of cerebrovascular stenosis or even occlusion in affected patients3. Thus, early detection of cerebrovascular stenosis in TIA patients and the implementation of preventive strategies are crucial for patient safety and long-term health.

Currently, neuroimaging techniques are commonly employed to diagnose TIA and assess the presence of cerebrovascular stenosis. Primary neuroimaging techniques encompass digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and computed tomography angiography (CTA), offering detailed visualization of vascular routes and wall configurations. Although DSA remains the gold standard for diagnosing vascular stenosis, its invasive nature, high cost, and risk of complications limit its routine use for initial TIA evaluation4. Conversely, CTA is a less-invasive and highly sensitive technique method that provides excellent temporal and spatial resolution, accurately depicting ischemic cores and delineating lesions from surrounding normal tissue5. In emergency TIA patients, CTA can quickly and accurately assess cerebrovascular stenosis and occlusion, whereas DSA is less suitable for acute initial diagnosis due to its preparation and procedure time6. Patient variability, procedural techniques and image post-processing methods may also influence the diagnostic results of these imaging modalities7. In particular, the use of low-dose CT scanning technology has significantly reduced patient radiation exposure while maintaining image quality, making CTA a more non-invasive, rapid and accurate diagnostic techniques8. Accurate identification of carotid stenosis in TIA patients remains challenging. While CTA is widely used for vascular assessment, its diagnostic performance may benefit from integration with clinical risk stratification tools.

Dependence solely on imaging for clinical decision-making lacks a holistic perspective. The ABCD2 score(Age, Blood pressure, Clinical features, Duration, Diabetes score) employed alongside neuroimaging to gauge the risk of TIA advancement to stroke9. This score assesses the risk of stroke in patients based on factors such as age, blood pressure, clinical symptoms and duration of symptoms10. By utilizing this scoring metric, healthcare providers can enhance diagnostic precision, formulate tailored treatment and preventive plans, and heighten patient awareness regarding disease severity, consequently fostering adherence to preventive measures11. In general, higher scores correlate with an increased risk of recurrence. However, no scoring system can provide absolute predictive accuracy. Some patients with a low risk score may still have symptomatic carotid stenosis or atrial fibrillation, necessitating prompt intervention12. Previous studies have shown that while the ABCD2 score is a reliable predictor of cerebral infarction risk following a TIA, it does not account for intracranial and extracranial vascular factors, which can be addressed by CTA to complement the ABCD2 score1. This study aims to evaluate the diagnostic value of combining CTA with ABCD2 scoring for identifying carotid artery stenosis in TIA patients, with the goal of providing scientific evidence to improve early stroke risk stratification and support clinical diagnosis.

Material and methods

Participants

This prospective cohort study enrolled 462 TIA patients admitted to the Department of Neurology at The Affiliated Cardiovascular Hospital of Shanxi Medical University between January 2020 and January 2025. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Cardiovascular Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (Approval No.XXGYY2020017) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants prior to the enrollment of this study.

Inclusion Criteria: Patients included in the study met the diagnostic criteria for TIA as stipulated in the "2014 Chinese guidelines for secondary prevention of ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack13" and their diagnoses were corroborated based on clinical symptoms, signs, and imaging investigations.

Exclusion Criteria: Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: hospitalization duration <1 week; presence of partial seizures, altered consciousness, migraine with aura, metabolic disorders, or glaucoma; history of atrial fibrillation, cardioembolic ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic cerebrovascular diseases, intracranial aneurysms, or cerebral dysfunction attributable to non-vascular etiologies; diagnosed hematologic disorders; absence of focal neurological deficits; or incomplete medical records precluding comprehensive clinical analysis. Patients with renal insufficiency were excluded due to the contraindication of iodinated contrast agents. Non-vascular causes of neurological symptoms were systematically excluded based on a comprehensive evaluation. This included assessment of clinical symptoms and signs, detailed medical history, neurological specialist examination, brain MRI (to rule out tumors, demyelinating diseases, or other structural lesions), and electroencephalography (EEG) when seizure-related disorders were suspected. Only patients with confirmed vascular etiology were retained for analysis.

Imaging and clinical assessments

This prospective study collected data from all patients who underwent head-and-neck CTA, ABCD2 scoring, and DSA within 48 hours of admission.

CT angiography

The instrument is 64 rows produced by GE Revolution Spiral CT machine. The patients were positioned supine, and iodinated contrast medium (Iopromide, 370 mg I/mL) was administered via an antecubital vein at a dose of 30-40 mL, with a flow rate of 4-5 mL/s. The scanning range covered from the base of the skull to the aortic arch, with the scanning direction from feet to head. The scanning parameters were as follows: dual tube voltages of Sn150 kV and 90 kV; tube currents ranged from 100-180 mAs and 130-330 mAs; tube rotation time of 0.28 s per rotation; reconstruction thickness of 0.75 mm and interslice distance of 0.5 mm.

Digital subtraction angiography

A Siemens DSA machine (Germany) was used for the procedure. The patient was positioned supine, and standard disinfection of the bilateral groin and perineal skin was performed. Local anesthesia with 1% lidocaine was administered through infiltration. Employing the Seldinger technique, successful puncture of the right femoral artery was accomplished, followed by placement of a 5 F arterial sheath. Systemic heparinization was achieved with 3000 units of heparin. Catheterization was carried out utilizing a 5 F pig-tail catheter and a single-curve catheter under pressure injection for aortic arch and comprehensive cerebral angiography, encompassing images of the aortic arch and common vertebral arteries. Measurements were taken of the diameters of the internal carotid artery and the common carotid artery.

Cerebrovascular stenosis grading

Stenosis severity was quantified using the NASCET criteria (≥50% luminal narrowing defined as significant stenosis). According to the degree of vascular stenosis, it is divided into mild stenosis: the stenosis rate is less than 49%, moderate stenosis: the stenosis rate is 50% ~ 69%, and severe stenosis: the stenosis rate is 70% ~ 99%. Two independent neuroradiologists (with >5 years of cerebrovascular imaging experience) analyzed CTA and DSA images. Both assessors were blinded to ABCD2 scores and clinical outcomes.

ABCD2 score

The ABCD2 score comprises five items with a total score of 7 points. Scoring 3 or lower indicates low risk, 4-5 points indicate moderate risk, and a score of 6 or higher signifies high risk. Integrated ABCD2-CTA algorithm

High-risk group (ABCD2 ≥ 4 score) : For these patients, head and neck CTA examination was performed within 2 hours of symptom onset. If the CTA results show ≥50% vascular stenosis, a further DSA examination is performed within 48 hours to confirm the extent and nature of the vascular stenosis.

Low-risk group (ABCD2 < 4) : For these patients, head and neck CTA examination was completed within 24 hours. If symptoms worsen or other clinical changes occur, DSA tests are upgraded to further confirm stenosis or rule out other lesions.

ABCD2 ≥ 4 and CTA stenosis ≥ 50% were used as joint positive criteria to confirm the diagnosis of cerebrovascular stenosis. This criterion combines the ability of the ABCD2 score to predict 7-day stroke risk in TIA patients with the accuracy of the CTA in localizing vascular stenosis.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and compared using t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as n (%), and comparisons were made using χ2 tests. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The performance of the diagnostic test was evaluated using several metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), all presented as n (%).

The ABCD2 scores ranged from 1 to 6, with a mean score of 3.1 ± 1.2. Distribution of cerebrovascular stenosis

Among 416 patients, 231 underwent both CTA and DSA. DSA identified a total of 192 positive and 39 negative cases of carotid artery stenosis. CTA identified 184 positive and 47 negative cases. The accuracy of head and neck CTA was 79.22% (183/231), with a sensitivity of 79.69% (153/192) and a specificity of 76.92% (30/39). The accuracy of the ABCD2 score was 66.67% (154/231), with a sensitivity of 70.83% (136/192) and a specificity of 46.15% (18/39). The combined CTA and ABCD2 approach achieved an accuracy of 88.74% (205/231), a sensitivity of 91.15% (175/192), and a specificity of 76.92% (30/39). In conclusion, the CTA test demonstrated robust reliability in assessing the accuracy of cerebrovascular stenosis, while the ABCD2 score, with its simplicity and accuracy, provided effective support for patient risk assessment. The combination of these two methods can significantly improve the safety and efficiency of diagnosis in clinical practice, thereby maximizing the diagnostic utility for carotid artery stenosis in individuals experiencing TIA.

Discussion

The high short-term stroke risk following a TIA necessitates prompt evaluation and risk stratification to improve outcomes12,14,15. This prospective study demonstrates that the integration of head and CTA with the ABCD2 clinical risk stratification tool significantly enhances the diagnostic performance for detecting carotid artery stenosis in TIA patients compared to either method alone. This synergistic approach achieved an accuracy of 88.74%, sensitivity of 91.15%, and specificity of 76.92%, outperforming standalone CTA (accuracy 79.22%) and ABCD2 scoring (accuracy 66.67%). These findings underscore the complementary value of combining anatomic vascular imaging with clinical risk assessment to optimize the evaluation of TIA patients16. CTA alone detected fewer stenoses compared with DSA (70.13% vs. 77.92%, P=0.005), likely reflecting limitations in visualizing distal vessels or differentiating near-occlusion from complete occlusion17. However, the combined CTA–ABCD2 strategy mitigated these limitations and achieved non-inferiority to DSA (P=0.109), supporting its reliability for clinical decision-making18,19.

Several clinical implications emerge from these findings. First, the combination of CTA and ABCD2 provides a pragmatic protocol for rapid risk stratification in TIA patients, enabling targeted secondary prevention while avoiding unnecessary DSA-related risks in over 70% of cases. Second, the predictive value of the combined approach could safely exclude significant stenosis in low-to-moderate risk patients, optimizing resource allocation. Third, the improved specificity may reduce overtreatment with antithrombotics or interventions in patients with false-positive imaging findings.

This study highlights the potential clinical value of combining head and neck CTA with the ABCD2 score to identify transient ischemic attack (TIA) patients at risk of carotid artery stenosis and subsequent ischemic stroke. The integration of anatomical imaging and clinical risk stratification may facilitate early interventions, such as initiating plaque-stabilizing therapies or considering endovascular procedures, which could reduce stroke incidence. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-center design and moderate sample size may limit the generalizability of our results, particularly for rare outcomes like complete arterial occlusion. In addition, although carotid duplex ultrasound is widely used as a first-line non-invasive screening tool, its diagnostic performance is operator-dependent and variable. CTA, by comparison, provides more consistent and reproducible assessments, which is why it was selected as the primary imaging modality in this study. Nonetheless, carotid ultrasound remains a valuable routine test, and future studies should explore multimodal strategies incorporating ultrasound, CTA, and DSA.

Another limitation is the lack of plaque characterization through vascular wall imaging. While vascular wall MRI is not typically used for stenosis grading, it plays a crucial role in assessing plaque vulnerability and detecting high-risk non-stenotic lesions. Future research should include such evaluations to further refine risk stratification in TIA patients.

The increased likelihood of CTA/DSA-detected lesions in high-risk patients (ABCD2 ≥4) may reflect a selection bias inherent in using clinical scores for group allocation. To further validate the long-term predictive value of this combined approach, future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods (e.g., 90 and 180 days post-TIA) are warranted. Longitudinal tracking of stroke incidence and stenosis progression in such cohorts will be crucial to clarify the role of this strategy in reducing diagnostic omissions and improving clinical outcomes. Addressing these evidence gaps is essential for the integration of CTA and clinical scores into standardized TIA management protocols.

Finally, although our study focused on the combination of CTA and ABCD2 scoring, this should not suggest that patients with low ABCD2 scores be exempt from vascular imaging. In clinical practice, a stepwise diagnostic approach—beginning with non-invasive techniques (e.g., ultrasound, MRI, or CTA) and escalating to DSA when necessary—is recommended for all TIA patients. Our findings highlight that CTA combined with the ABCD2 score represents a practical and accurate strategy for early detection of vascular stenosis, particularly in high-risk individuals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the integration of head and neck CTA with the ABCD2 score presents notable benefits for the prompt diagnosis of TIA patients. The integration of these methods significantly improves diagnostic accuracy. Clinicians should consider using this combined diagnostic approach on an individual patient basis to guide subsequent treatment decisions.
