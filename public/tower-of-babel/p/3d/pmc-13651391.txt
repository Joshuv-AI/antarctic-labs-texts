Targeting the gut microbiome: an integrated probiotic and prebiotic strategy for polycystic ovary syndrome management
Authors: Muchu Ni, Qin Wang, Tao Ye, Hongyan Lei, Yongzhou Wang, Yuan Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine-metabolic disorder in which gut dysbiosis acts as a key environmental driver. This review synthesizes how probiotics and prebiotics remodel the gut ecosystem and ameliorate PCOS through multi-pathway mechanisms:restoring intestinal barrier function, modulating microbial metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids(SCFAs), bile acids(BAs)), attenuating chronic inflammation, and regulating androgen metabolism. We further propose a novel “integrated microbiome‑centric management” framework, demonstrating how microbiota-targeted interventions synergize with dietary, pharmacological, and behavioral strategies to enable personalized, multi-modal PCOS care. This work provides a transformative perspective for translating gut microbiome science into clinical practice across disciplinary boundaries.Graphical Abstract At the core of this integrated management strategy for PCOS lies the targeted use of probiotics and prebiotics. These interventions directly modulate the gut microbiome, leading to improved barrier function, beneficial shifts in microbial metabolites (e.g., SCFAs, BAs), and reduced systemic inflammation. This restored gut ecosystem serves as a foundational platform that powerfully synergizes with three key clinical pillars: evidence-based dietary patterns, conventional pharmacology (e.g., metformin, GLP-1 RAs), and behavioral modifications (e.g., exercise, stress reduction). Together, this multi-targeted approach addresses the interconnected metabolic, endocrine, reproductive, and psychological features of PCOS, paving the way for more holistic and personalized patient care. Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine-metabolic disorders in reproductive-age women, clinically characterized by ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian changes. It is a major cause of infertility in this population. Epidemiological studies indicate a prevalence of 6%–20% among women of reproductive age [1].This disorder involves not only reproductive endocrine abnormalities but is also frequently associated with multiple systemic manifestations, including abdominal obesity, insulin resistance(IR), metabolic syndrome, and increased cardiovascular disease risk [2–5]. Patients exhibit diverse clinical features, encompassing anovulation and virilizing signs (such as weight gain, abdominal obesity, hirsutism, alopecia, and acne), which severely impact reproductive health and quality of life [3].In recent years, with the rapid advancement of microbiome research, gut dysbiosis has been observed to be associated with the pathogenesis and progression of PCOS, although causal evidence in humans remains limited [6].The gut microbiota—a complex ecosystem comprising bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi—extensively participates in host metabolism, immune regulation, and endocrine control, earning it the designation as the human “second genome” or “invisible organ“ [7].Growing evidence suggests that gut microbes may contribute to core PCOS pathophysiology, potentially via pathways involving metabolite-host interactions, immune modulation, and neuroendocrine signaling [8]. Against this backdrop, probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) and prebiotics (e.g., fructooligosaccharides, inulin) have garnered significant attention as intervention strategies capable of actively reshaping the gut microbiome.These agents not only help restore microbial structure and diversity but also modulate the “microbiota-gut-axis” function, enabling multi-targeted intervention in key PCOS-related pathways involving metabolism, inflammation, and hormonal imbalance [9]. This review systematically examines the potential mechanisms and clinical research progress of probiotics and prebiotics in PCOS treatment, aiming to provide theoretical references and new strategic perspectives for comprehensive PCOS management based on microbiome regulation.

PCOS and gut microbiota dysbiosis (pathological basis)

Dysbiosis, or disruption of gut microbiome homeostasis, represents a significant environmental factor in the pathophysiology of PCOS.Extensive research confirms that PCOS patients commonly exhibit significant alterations in gut microbial composition, characterized by overall gut microbiome degradation: on one hand, this manifests as simplified community structure, such as reduced α-diversity and abnormal β-diversity; on the other hand, it reflects imbalances in specific microbial communities, including alterations in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and other systemic disturbances [10].Despite some heterogeneity across studies, a recurring pattern of dysbiosis is emerging.For instance, Pan et al. observed significant depletion of metabolically protective Lachnospiraceae and Prevotella in the intestines of PCOS patients, alongside marked enrichment of inflammation-associated genera like Bacteroides and Escherichia/Shigella [11].Deeper mechanistic investigations indicate that specific bacterial alterations directly correlate with host metabolic dysregulation. QI et al.‘s research demonstrated that overgrowth of Bacteroides vulgatus, accompanied by altered levels of bile acid(BA) metabolites (such as glycocholic acid(GDCA) and tauroursodeoxycholic acid(TUDCA)), provides direct evidence for dysregulation in the microbiota-host co-metabolism [12].However, it is crucial to recognize that the relationship between gut microbiota and PCOS is bidirectional [13, 14]. While the above evidence supports a causative role of dysbiosis in driving PCOS-related metabolic and reproductive abnormalities, microbiota alterations may also arise secondarily from PCOS phenotypes—including IR, chronic low-grade inflammation, obesity, and hyperandrogenism—which can reshape the intestinal environment and select for distinct microbial communities [15]. For example, IR and hyperinsulinemia may alter intestinal transit time, BA pool, and mucosal immune responses, thereby promoting the overgrowth of certain pro-inflammatory bacteria. Conversely, once established, such dysbiosis can exacerbate systemic inflammation and IR, creating a vicious cycle [16]. Mendelian randomization studies incorporating reverse causal analysis have further supported this bidirectional interplay [17]. Thus, the observed compositional changes likely reflect a complex interplay of cause and effect, rather than a unidirectional driver–consequence relationship.In summary, PCOS-associated gut dysbiosis fundamentally involves an ecological imbalance characterized by loss of diversity, reduction in beneficial microbes, and proliferation of potential pathogens [18, 19]. This imbalance is not merely a passive consequence of disease but also an active contributor that can perpetuate the pathological state. This raises the possibility that gut microbiome “disorder” could remotely influence ovarian function and systemic metabolism, potentially by disrupting systemic immune and endocrine homeostasis via altered metabolic output and compromised intestinal barrier integrity. The following sections will focus on dissecting these core mechanisms.

Multipathway improvement of IR and metabolic health (core mechanisms)

IR is the core pathological driver of metabolic and reproductive abnormalities in PCOS, affecting approximately 70% of patients and prevalent in both obese and non-obese phenotypes [20, 21].IR not only disrupts glucose metabolism but also exacerbates hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction by promoting excessive androgen production in the ovaries and adrenal glands, creating a vicious cycle [22]. Recent studies have implicated gut microbiota dysbiosis as a potential environmental factor in this cycle.PCOS patients exhibit specific microbial profiles (e.g., reduced Prevotella, increased Bacteroides) significantly correlated with IR severity [23]. Foundational evidence for this link emerged from a 2004 study: transplanting conventional mouse microbiota into germ-free mice induced obesity and IR, establishing gut microbes as causal drivers of IR for the first time [24].Based on this, the intervention value of probiotics and prebiotics becomes evident: they aim to regulate PCOS-associated dysbiosis and improve IR through multiple targets via the following core mechanisms.

Repairing the intestinal barrier to block inflammatory sources

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a core feature of PCOS, manifested not only in elevated systemic circulating levels of C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α), and interleukin-6(IL-6) in systemic circulation, but also confirmed in the local ovarian microenvironment of PCOS patients—where pro-inflammatory factors like interleukin-1β(IL-1β) and interleukin-18(IL-18) are significantly elevated in follicular fluid [25–28]. This persistent inflammatory state is closely linked to impaired intestinal barrier function.The intestinal barrier serves as a critical defense against pathogen and toxin invasion, with its integrity highly dependent on a healthy gut microbiota. Dysbiosis leads to increased intestinal permeability, facilitating increased translocation of lipopolysaccharide(LPS)—a cell wall component of Gram-negative bacteria—into the circulatory system, resulting in low-grade “metabolic endotoxemia” [29, 30]. LPS entering the bloodstream activates the Toll-like receptor 4(TLR4) signaling pathway, triggering systemic chronic low-grade inflammation. This, in turn, disrupts insulin signaling, becoming a major contributor to IR and metabolic dysfunction in PCOS [29, 31, 32].Therefore, repairing the intestinal barrier and blocking LPS translocation represent key mechanisms for probiotic and prebiotic interventions in PCOS inflammation. Studies indicate that specific probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) promote mucin secretion and enhance tight junction protein expression between epithelial cells, physically reinforcing the intestinal barrier [33–36].Simultaneously, prebiotics (e.g., inulin) fermented by gut microbiota produce abundant Short-chain fatty acids(SCFAs). Butyrate, serving as the primary energy source for colonic epithelial cells, significantly promotes barrier repair and regeneration [6, 37, 38]. Through these synergistic actions, probiotics and prebiotics effectively reduce LPS translocation and systemic inflammation levels, thereby curbing inflammation-induced IR and its progression at the source [39, 40] (Fig. 1).

Regulation of metabolic signaling molecules: core roles of SCFAs and BAs

SCFAs

The gut microbiota ferments indigestible dietary fiber to produce SCFAs, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate [10, 41].These metabolites serve not only as direct energy sources for colonic epithelial cells but also as key signaling molecules regulating intestinal and systemic homeostasis. SCFAs synergistically maintain gut health and metabolic balance through multiple mechanisms: they suppress pathogenic bacterial growth by lowering intestinal pH and stimulating antimicrobial peptide synthesis, thereby optimizing the microenvironment [42]; directly activate genes encoding tight junction proteins to enhance the physical barrier function of the epithelium [43]; and bidirectionally regulate immune responses through G protein-coupled receptor(GPR41/43) signaling and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition, thereby shaping an anti-inflammatory environment [42, 44].At the metabolic level, SCFAs (particularly butyrate and propionate) directly stimulate colonic L cells to secrete glucagon-like peptide-1(GLP-1), promoting insulin secretion and suppressing appetite; simultaneously activate GPR41/43 to promote white fat browning, enhance skeletal muscle glucose uptake, and suppress hepatic gluconeogenesis, thereby comprehensively improving glucose metabolism and alleviating PCOS [45, 46].Notably, some studies have observed reduced SCFA levels in PCOS patients, which may be associated with depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria [47, 48]. However, findings are not entirely consistent across studies, and the hypothesized downstream cascade requires further validation in humans [16, 17]. As key substrates for SCFA production, prebiotics occupy a central role in regulating this metabolic pathway.Inulin, a typical prebiotic linked by β-2,1 bonds, specifically promotes the proliferation of SCFA-producing beneficial bacteria (e.g., Faecalibacterium prausnitzii) [49]. Animal studies have shown that inulin supplementation can increase the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria and elevate SCFA levels, accompanied by improvement in PCOS-related metabolic disorders and ovarian dysfunction [6].The underlying mechanism may involve the beneficial metabolic and anti‑inflammatory microenvironment shaped by SCFAs, which could act on the ovaries through the circulatory system, downregulate local expression of inflammatory factors such as IL‑1β and TNF‑α, and thereby improve granulosa cell function and follicular development [50, 51]. However, direct human evidence confirming this causal pathway is currently lacking. Nonetheless, increasing endogenous SCFA levels by supplementing prebiotics or SCFA‑producing probiotics remains a potential microecological intervention strategy targeting gut barrier function, systemic immunity, and glucose homeostasis in PCOS patients, and provides a theoretical basis for developing individualized therapies based on the “microbiota‑metabolite‑organ axis.”Notably, SCFAs and BAs converge on common downstream receptors to synergistically regulate GLP‑1 secretion and inflammation. BAs, through farnesoid X receptor(FXR) and Takeda G protein‑coupled receptor 5(TGR5), similarly promote GLP‑1 release and inhibit pro‑inflammatory pathways, whereas SCFAs operate via GPR41/43 and HDAC inhibition [52, 53]. This mechanistic convergence, as illustrated by the cross‑talk arrows in Fig. 2, underscores a central host‑microbe interaction axis for metabolic regulation [54].

BAs

PCOS patients exhibit characteristic BA metabolism disorders, which are not only consequences of dysbiosis but also key signaling links driving metabolic and reproductive abnormalities. Compared to healthy women, PCOS patients show significantly reduced serum and fecal levels of GDCA and TUDCA [12].This altered BA profile correlates directly with abnormal enrichment of Bacteroides vulgatus in the gut: this bacterium excessively degrades conjugated BAs via highly expressed bile salt hydrolase (BSH), depleting metabolic precursors and thereby reducing GDCA and TUDCA production [12, 55].Notably, this microbiota-driven alteration in BA pool composition impairs the physiological activation of metabolic nuclear receptor FXR and membrane receptor TGR5. These receptors are central to maintaining metabolic homeostasis: FXR activation suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis and regulates lipid metabolism [56, 57]; TGR5 activation stimulates GLP-1 secretion from intestinal L cells, thereby enhancing insulin sensitivity and promoting energy expenditure [58, 59]. Thus, the deficiency of GDCA/TUDCA in PCOS may lead to insufficient FXR/TGR5 signaling activity, directly exacerbating hyperinsulinemia, IR, and impaired energy metabolism efficiency. This provides a clear molecular pathway for how gut microbiota dysbiosis influences the metabolic phenotype of PCOS. Based on this mechanism, targeting BA metabolism regulation emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy. Studies confirm that specific probiotics can reshape the gut BA pool, such as by increasing the proportion of beneficial BAs with FXR/TGR5 agonist activity, including ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) [60, 61].This microbial intervention holds promise for partially restoring normal BA signaling.Consequently, it may improve glucose and lipid metabolism while indirectly alleviating ovarian hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction exacerbated by hyperinsulinemia (Fig. 2). As depicted by the dashed arrows in Fig. 2, these reproductive benefits are primarily mediated through the reduction of IR and systemic inflammation (metabolic outcomes) [12, 13]. Currently, there is no direct evidence to suggest that BAs exert a direct action on the ovaries to improve these outcomes. Therefore, the improvement is considered indirect, acting via the metabolic pathways described above.

Direct anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory

Effects The beneficial effects of probiotics and prebiotics on PCOS partly stem from their capacity to directly regulate immune homeostasis and suppress chronic low-grade inflammation. PCOS patients exhibit significant immune dysregulation, manifested by systemic and local (e.g., endometrial) pro-inflammatory states characterized by elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, alongside impaired regulatory T cell(Treg) function and reduced levels of barrier-protective cytokines like IL-22(which has been shown to improve insulin response and protect mice from PCOS [12, 62–65]. These abnormalities are closely associated with IR and ovarian dysfunction [66].Probiotics and prebiotics correct these immune dysregulations through multiple interrelated mechanisms. A core pathway involves promoting fermentation of dietary fiber by beneficial gut bacteria to produce SCFAs, particularly butyrate.SCFAs not only lower colonic pH to selectively promote proliferation of beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [67, 68], but also act as key signaling molecules directly regulating immunity: by inhibiting HDAC and nuclear factor-κB(NF-κB) pathways, SCFAs promote Treg differentiation and suppress excessive inflammation [65, 66]; simultaneously, by regulating transcription factors like the aryl hydrocarbon receptor(AhR), they restore immune cells’ ability to produce IL-22, thereby strengthening the mucosal barrier and alleviating tissue inflammation [69–71].Furthermore, specific probiotics and their metabolites can directly modulate immune cell function. For instance, fermentation products of Bifidobacterium C50 induce dendritic cell(DC) maturation and increase production of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 [72]. Other prebiotic and synbiotic supplements have also been shown to regulate immune cell differentiation, promote anti-inflammatory cytokine profiles, and thereby reduce systemic inflammation [73].Clinical and meta-analytic evidence supports these mechanisms. Supplementation with prebiotics (e.g., inulin and Fructooligosaccharides(FOS)) and synbiotics producing SCFAs correlates with improved subjective satiety, reduced postprandial glucose and insulin levels [74, 75], and significantly lowers systemic inflammatory markers including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α [76]. Thus, probiotics and prebiotics directly remodel the dysregulated immune microenvironment in PCOS patients through both SCFA-dependent and independent pathways, providing a key immunological explanation for their effects in reducing chronic inflammation, improving insulin sensitivity, and addressing reproductive dysfunction.

Comprehensive benefits for reproductive and neuroendocrine function

Regulation of androgen metabolism

The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of host metabolism and endocrine function. A substantial body of evidence supports its role in modulating insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis [77]. In PCOS patients and animal models, excessive androgen exposure induces significant and persistent alterations in gut microbiota composition. This early-programmed dysbiosis further exacerbates host metabolic disorders and gonadal dysfunction [78]. It is characterized by the proliferation of pro-inflammatory bacteria (e.g., certain Bacteroides species) and a marked reduction in metabolically protective genera (e.g., Akkermansia, Alistipes, and Faecalibacterium, which are positively correlated with insulin sensitivity) [79–82]. Such imbalances, particularly Akkermansia depletion, are closely associated with intestinal mucosal barrier damage [80, 83]. Targeting this pathological basis, microbiome interventions centered on probiotics and prebiotics demonstrate potential for regulating PCOS endocrine disorders. Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials have shown that supplementation with synbiotics or probiotics is associated with improvements in sex hormone-binding globulin(SHBG) levels and free androgen index(FAI) in women with PCOS [84, 85].Their mechanism of action involves multi-pathway synergism: First, by increasing beneficial bacteria and their metabolites (e.g., SCFAs), they systematically improve IR, thereby reducing the stimulation of ovarian theca cells by hyperinsulinemia and decreasing androgen synthesis at its source [86–88]. Second, the improved metabolic environment promotes hepatic synthesis of SHBG, thereby lowering concentrations of biologically active free testosterone [89, 90].In contrast, evidence for direct regulation of androgens by the gut microbiota remains preliminary, and currently suggests that at least two distinct mechanisms may exist: Mechanism 1: β-glucuronidase-mediated androgen reactivation (deconjugation). This enzyme, primarily expressed by intestinal bacteria such as Bacteroides, may theoretically hydrolyze conjugated androgens into free forms, promoting their enterohepatic recirculation and potentially contributing to hyperandrogenism [91–93]. However, direct measurements of this enzyme activity in PCOS patients are limited, and findings across studies are not entirely consistent; some studies have reported associations with metabolic parameters rather than with androgens per se [94, 95]. Notably, specific probiotic strains (e.g., Lactobacillus plantarum CCFM8610) have been reported to reduce gut β-glucuronidase activity in animal studies [96]. Nevertheless, whether this finding translates to PCOS patients requires further investigation. Given that high β-glucuronidase activity has been shown to drive elevated circulating free testosterone in experimental settings [92], regulating this enzyme activity through probiotic intervention represents a potential microbiome therapeutic target for alleviating PCOS hyperandrogenism, independent of insulin-sensitizing effects. However, its clinical significance and efficacy remain to be validated in future studies; Mechanism 2: Bacterial androgen catabolism (androgen degradation). In contrast to the reactivation pathway described above, certain gut bacteria can utilize androgens as a carbon source for catabolism, thereby potentially reducing circulating androgen levels. Hsiao et al. demonstrated in male mice that specific gut bacteria (e.g., Thauera sp.) possess androgen-catabolizing capacity and can regulate host circulating androgen levels through this mechanism [94]. Other studies have also identified bacteria such as Mycobacterium neoaurum and Pseudomonas nitroreducens capable of degrading testosterone via distinct enzymatic pathways [97, 98]. However, direct evidence for this pathway in PCOS patients is currently lacking, and its role in PCOS pathophysiology remains to be explored.(Fig. 3).

Modulating the gut-brain axis and mood

Beyond metabolic and endocrine dysregulation, PCOS patients frequently experience significant psychological comorbidities such as depression and anxiety, with markedly elevated risk [96]. This neuropsychiatric dimension exhibits profound pathological interplay with the disease’s core pathologies: IR and neuroendocrine dysfunction. IR and its associated hyperinsulinemia not only directly drive ovarian androgen synthesis but also substantially contribute to hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis dysfunction and central neurotransmitter imbalance by promoting central inflammatory responses, altering brain energy metabolism, and modulating neuronal excitability [99, 100]. Studies indicate that PCOS patients exhibit enhanced signaling of excitatory neurotransmitters (e.g., glutamate) in the hypothalamus, while inhibitory neurotransmitters including serotonin(5-HT), dopamine, and gamma-aminobutyric acid(GABA) show relatively diminished function. This combination leads to hyperactivity of the Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone(GnRH) pulse generator and elevated Luteinizing Hormone(LH) secretion [101].The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in this pathological network via the “gut-brain axis.” Over 90% of 5-HT is synthesized in the gut by enterochromaffin cells, and the metabolic pathway of its precursor, tryptophan, is finely regulated by the microbiota. Concurrently, specific gut bacteria can directly produce or modulate neuroactive substances like GABA and dopamine [102]. In PCOS patients, gut bacteria producing GABA (e.g., Bacteroides fragilis and Bacteroides pseudobacteroides) show increased abundance, positively correlated with serum LH levels and the LH/FSH ratio [103].Increased bacterially derived GABA may act on GABA receptors in hypothalamic GnRH neurons via circulatory or vagal pathways, directly stimulating LH secretion. Furthermore, GABA locally participates in regulating steroid production and follicular development in the ovary, a pathway potentially impaired in PCOS [104, 105]. Concurrently, the 5-HT system is deeply involved in regulation: 5-HT receptors are widely distributed in the hypothalamus and ovaries, influencing GnRH pulsatility and ovarian hormone synthesis/follicular maturation, respectively [106, 107]. Thus, gut microbiota may synergistically mediate PCOS neuroendocrine abnormalities and emotional symptoms by simultaneously regulating multiple neurotransmitter systems including GABA and 5-HT. A vicious cycle may form between IR and gut dysbiosis: IR induces gut environment changes, promoting dysbiosis; the dysbiotic microbiota further exacerbates peripheral and central IR and inflammation by producing neuroactive metabolites (e.g., GABA), disrupting 5-HT metabolism, and releasing inflammatory signals, thereby persistently disrupting hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian(HPO) axis function and emotional regulation circuits [103, 108]. Against this backdrop, targeted probiotic and prebiotic interventions demonstrate multi-target regulatory potential. By improving IR, reducing low-grade inflammation, and directly regulating microbial composition and function, they promote the restoration of neurotransmitter metabolic balance. This approach not only enhances glucose metabolism but also helps stabilize HPO axis function and alleviate associated psychosomatic symptoms [109, 110]. This provides a robust pathophysiological framework for understanding and intervening in PCOS from an integrated “microbiome-gut-brain-metabolism axis” perspective (Fig. 4).

Synergistic effects of the mediterranean and DASH diets

As a “natural synbiotic” strategy, the core mechanism by which the Mediterranean Diet(MD) improves PCOS metabolic and reproductive phenotypes lies in its multidimensional, synergistic reshaping of the gut microbiota. Similarly, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension(DASH) diet follows comparable principles of high fiber and plant-based foods, positively influencing gut microbiota and metabolic health by regulating mineral intake and reducing dietary inflammation indices [119]. First, its high dietary fiber intake forms the cornerstone of metabolic regulation. This pattern is rich in “microbiologically accessible carbohydrates“(MACs) from whole grains, legumes, and fruits/vegetables. These host-indigestible complex carbohydrates selectively nourish colonic symbionts, significantly promoting SCFA synthesis—particularly butyrate [113, 114, 120, 121].Butyrate serves not only as a critical energy source for colonic epithelial cells—directly strengthening tight junctions and reinforcing the intestinal barrier to reduce LPS translocation—but also functions as a key signaling molecule. By activating G protein-coupled receptors (e.g., GPR41/43) and inhibiting HDAC activity, exerting systemic anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects [115, 116, 121]. This pathway directly targets the core PCOS pathologies of IR and chronic low-grade inflammation. Secondly, abundant polyphenolic compounds provide critical immune and microbial regulation. Polyphenols in extra virgin olive oil, nuts, red wine, and dark fruits/vegetables are predominantly metabolized by gut microbiota in the colon. Their metabolites not only selectively promote proliferation of beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium while inhibiting pathogens, but also significantly reduce circulating inflammatory markers such as TNF-α and IL-6 by downregulating key pro-inflammatory pathways like Nuclear Factor Kappa-Light-Chain-Enhancer of Activated B Cells(NF-κB) [121–123]. This creates conditions for alleviating systemic and local ovarian inflammatory microenvironments associated with PCOS. Finally, the unique fatty acid profile synergistically optimizes metabolic homeostasis. A fat composition dominated by monounsaturated fatty acids (primarily from olive oil) and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (primarily from fish) not only helps improve lipid profiles and insulin signaling. Research confirms that omega-3 fatty acids enhance intestinal barrier integrity by inducing intestinal alkaline phosphatase, thereby reducing LPS translocation. This approach mitigates metabolic endotoxemia and systemic inflammation at its source [117, 118].

Low Glycemic Index Diet (LGI)

LGI aims to stabilize postprandial glucose and insulin levels by selecting carbohydrates that digest slowly and produce a gradual glycemic response, thereby directly improving IR—a core pathological component of PCOS. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs with Type 2 Diabetes(T2DM) have shown that LGI diets are associated with greater improvements in glycated hemoglobin, fasting blood glucose, and insulin sensitivity compared with high-glycemic-index diets, with effects comparable to some pharmacological interventions [124, 125]. However, direct evidence in PCOS patients remains limited. Its metabolic benefits extend beyond glycemic control.Studies confirm that the LGI significantly improves lipid metabolism, reduces body weight and waist circumference, and helps alleviate chronic inflammation [126, 127]. More importantly, this dietary pattern exerts deeper metabolic regulation by modulating the gut microbiome.For instance, animal studies indicate that LGI interventions significantly increase the abundance of beneficial gut bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacteria, Faecalibacterium) while reducing potentially pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Shigella), thereby optimizing microbial composition [128]. This shift is closely associated with elevated levels of gut SCFAs, creating a more favorable ecological niche for probiotic colonization. Thus, LGI and probiotic/prebiotic interventions share inherent synergistic logic: this diet provides abundant prebiotic substrates through complex carbohydrates (e.g., whole grains, legumes) while avoiding hyperglycemic fluctuations that disrupt the gut environment. This establishes a stable metabolic and microbiological foundation for the colonization and functional expression of exogenous probiotics.

Phased ketogenic or low-carbohydrate diets

Characterized by high fat, extremely low carbohydrates, and moderate protein, ketogenic diets force metabolic shifts from glucose to fat breakdown for ketone production by drastically reducing carbohydrate intake, inducing physiological ketosis [129]. This metabolic shift demonstrates clear therapeutic value for specific neurological disorders (e.g., refractory epilepsy, Glucose Transporter Type 1(GLUT1) deficiency) [130, 131], while its metabolic profile—similar to calorie restriction—has also been explored for improving metabolic health.In the context of metabolic disorders, the ketogenic diet significantly improves glycemic control in T2DM patients, promotes weight loss, and benefits lipid profile regulation by rapidly reducing glycemic load and insulin requirements. For PCOS patients, this dietary pattern may rapidly improve IR in the short term and help reduce circulating inflammatory markers by modulating the balance between leptin and adiponectin, thereby alleviating chronic low-grade inflammation [132].Notably, the ketogenic diet profoundly impacts gut microbiota composition. Studies indicate it may reduce the Firmicutes-Bacteroidetes ratio and, when combined with adequate dietary fiber intake (primarily from low-carb vegetables), promote proliferation of beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia, thereby increasing intestinal SCFAs levels [133].These changes help reinforce intestinal barrier function and alleviate systemic inflammation. However, extreme dietary restructuring may also carry the risk of reduced microbial diversity. Therefore, concurrent supplementation with probiotics and prebiotics during ketogenic diets holds significant strategic importance: prebiotics (such as specific fibers) nourish surviving beneficial bacteria, while probiotics directly replenish and sustain key microbial populations [134, 135]. This synergistic intervention facilitates rapid metabolic remodeling while preserving gut microbiome diversity and stability, preventing dysbiosis triggered by dietary upheaval. It thus offers PCOS patients a short-term intensive management strategy balancing metabolic improvement with microbiome health.

Synergy with conventional medications

Metformin

As a first-line drug for T2DM, metformin is traditionally understood to act primarily by inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhancing peripheral tissue insulin sensitivity. However, animal studies have indicated that the gut is an important target organ for metformin’s hypoglycemic effects, with intravenous administration failing to produce equivalent efficacy, suggesting dependence on gut-mediated pathways [136, 137]. Both animal and human studies suggest that metformin may reshape the composition and function of the gut microbiota. For example, a mouse study reported that metformin treatment was associated with an increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila [138]. In rodent models, metformin treatment has been reported to promote the proliferation of SCFA-producing bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus, Ruminococcus, Butyrivibrio), which was associated with elevated intestinal SCFA levels [139–141]. Increased SCFAs may contribute to enhanced intestinal barrier integrity, modulation of local and systemic immunity, and improved insulin sensitivity. Metformin has also been shown to regulate BA metabolism in animal studies [142]. Based on this proposed “drug-microbiota-host” interaction mechanism, combining specific probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) with metformin has been hypothesized to produce synergistic therapeutic effects. However, direct clinical evidence for this combination in PCOS or T2DM patients remains limited. A cross-sectional study in humans suggested that gut microbiota composition is associated with host health [143], and a recent review summarized the potential mechanisms by which metformin regulates the gut microbiota [144].Thus, combining metformin with probiotic/prebiotic interventions represents a promising area for future research, but current conclusions are based primarily on mechanistic and preclinical data.

Oral Contraceptives (OCs)

Oral contraceptives are commonly used in PCOS patients to regulate menstrual cycles and reduce androgen levels. However, animal studies have suggested that long-term use of synthetic estrogens may exert complex effects on metabolic health, including hyperinsulinemia and increased hepatic oxidative stress in mouse models [145, 146]. Observational studies in humans have indicated that these adverse effects may be partly associated with OC-induced alterations in the gut microbiota, as gut bacteria and their metabolites are thought to play important roles in maintaining metabolic homeostasis [147–149]. Given the proposed role of gut microbiota in metabolic regulation, adjunctive probiotic/prebiotic interventions have been hypothesized as a potential approach to mitigate the metabolic side effects of OCs. In mouse models, specific probiotic combinations (e.g., formulations containing Enterococcus) were reported to alleviate diet-induced intestinal inflammation, improve intestinal barrier function, and mitigate metabolic abnormalities such as impaired glucose tolerance [150]. Based on these preclinical findings, it has been suggested that co-administering probiotics/prebiotics during OC therapy might counteract potential adverse effects on glucose metabolism and inflammatory markers by modulating gut microbiota composition, enhancing barrier integrity, and balancing immune responses. However, direct clinical evidence in PCOS patients receiving OC therapy is currently lacking, and further research is needed to determine whether such benefits translate to humans.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RAs)

GLP-1 RAs represent novel drugs widely adopted in recent years for T2DM and obesity management. Their core mechanisms include glucose-dependent stimulation of insulin secretion, inhibition of glucagon release, delayed gastric emptying, and central regulation of appetite [151, 152]. Notably, these metabolic benefits are closely intertwined with significant alterations in the gut microbiota. Studies demonstrate that treatment with GLP-1 RAs (such as liraglutide and dulaglutide) induces characteristic changes in the gut microbial composition, characterized by a marked increase in the abundance of beneficial bacterial genera including Lactobacillus, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Prevotella [153, 154]. More recent evidence indicates that the GLP-1/GIP dual receptor agonist tirzepatide, while effectively promoting weight loss and improving IR, also increases beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia. It exerts additional metabolic protective effects by regulating BA metabolism (e.g., increasing farnesol X receptor antagonism) [155]. These microbial shifts may partly stem from drugs’ direct effects on gut motility, pH, and mucus secretion, while also indirectly correlating with systemic metabolic improvements (e.g., obesity and inflammation reduction). A key synergistic mechanism lies in the gut microbiota’s role as a major regulator of endogenous GLP-1 secretion. Probiotics and prebiotics have been demonstrated to effectively stimulate GLP-1 secretion from intestinal L cells by increasing metabolites like SCFAs [156, 157]. This indicates that probiotic/prebiotic interventions can enhance endogenous GLP-1 signaling “upstream,” complementing the “downstream” receptor activation by GLP-1 RAs. Therefore, the combination of GLP-1 RAs with probiotics/prebiotics holds clear synergistic potential: by modulating the microbiota and increasing SCFAs, probiotics/prebiotics may enhance baseline endogenous GLP-1 secretion, thereby producing additive or synergistic metabolic improvement effects with exogenous GLP-1 RAs, particularly in glycemic control and weight management [158]. Furthermore, this combined strategy may help maintain long-term microbiota health by optimizing the intestinal environment, offering a multi-tiered, enhanced metabolic management approach for PCOS patients, especially those with significant obesity and IR (Table 2).

Integration of behavioral and psychological interventions

Stress management

Chronic psychological stress is a common trigger for PCOS, profoundly affecting metabolism and endocrine function via the gut-brain axis. Stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal(HPA) axis, releasing the key mediator corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). Locally in the gut, CRH activates mast cells, disrupting tight junctions in the intestinal epithelium and increasing permeability, leading to a “leaky gut” state [159–163]. This facilitates endotoxin translocation and systemic low-grade inflammation, exacerbating IR and ovarian dysfunction. Concurrently, stress-related alterations in intestinal permeability and sustained HPA axis activation also disrupt gut microbiota homeostasis.Microbiota dysbiosis, in turn, influences the central nervous system through multiple pathways: First, gut microbes regulate tryptophan metabolism, affecting central serotonin synthesis and emotional function [109]. Second, SCFAs produced by microbial fermentation modulate neuroendocrine-immune communication via pathways such as G protein-coupled receptors (GPR41/43) [164].Third, certain bacteria directly produce or influence neuroactive substances like GABA and dopamine, thereby regulating HPA axis reactivity and emotional behavior [165, 166]. This creates a vicious cycle: “stress→intestinal permeability/dysbiosis→neuroendocrine disruption→exacerbated metabolic and reproductive symptoms.” Consequently, bidirectional intervention targeting the gut-brain axis is critically important. Probiotic interventions, particularly “psychobiotics” with neuro-modulatory potential, can mitigate stress-induced gut and central nervous system dysregulation through mechanisms including enhanced intestinal barrier function, inhibition of mast cell activation, increased SCFAs production, and regulation of neurotransmitter precursor metabolism [167, 168]. Clinically, combining such probiotic/prebiotic supplementation with psychological interventions like cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness-based stress reduction may synergistically target both ends of the “microbiome-gut-brain” axis. This approach not only alleviates anxiety and depression comorbidities in PCOS patients but may also indirectly improve IR and HPO axis function by stabilizing HPA axis activity and reducing systemic inflammation, offering a novel mind-body integrated strategy for PCOS management.

Exercise prescription

Regular physical activity serves as the cornerstone of PCOS lifestyle management. Its benefits stem not only from energy expenditure and metabolic improvements but also from its positive regulatory effects on the gut microbiota. Research indicates exercise functions as an effective environmental factor that independently shapes gut microbial composition.Regular exercisers often exhibit higher microbial α-diversity and enrichment of specific beneficial genera (e.g., Akkermansia), correlating with improved metabolic phenotypes [169–172]. Exercise-induced microbiota modulation is intensity- and mode-dependent. Moderate-intensity aerobic exercise significantly alters gut microbiota composition while increasing SCFAs levels [173]. High-intensity training specifically increases the abundance of beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia and Lactobacillus. Animal studies confirm that its metabolic benefits (e.g., improved insulin sensitivity) disappear after microbiota clearance and can be restored through fecal microbiota transplantation, directly demonstrating microbiota-mediated effects [174, 175].The underlying mechanism likely involves exercise-induced alterations in gastrointestinal secretion and immune status, creating an optimal colonization environment for beneficial bacteria. These microbial shifts subsequently improve PCOS pathology through multiple pathways. Increased SCFAs, such as butyrate, not only directly strengthen the intestinal barrier and reduce endotoxin translocation but also act as signaling molecules regulating systemic energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity [176–178]. Furthermore, exercise-induced microbiota alterations may positively influence anxiety and depression symptoms associated with PCOS by regulating neurotransmitter metabolism via the gut-brain axis. Given this bidirectional interaction between exercise and microbiota, combining personalized exercise regimens with probiotic/prebiotic supplementation holds clear synergistic potential. Exercise provides an optimized intestinal environment for probiotic colonization and functional expression; targeted supplementation with probiotics/prebiotics can further consolidate and enhance the microbial benefits derived from exercise. Research indicates probiotic interventions can improve biomarkers related to physical function and fatigue recovery in a dose-dependent manner [179, 180]. Therefore, this integrated strategy of “exercise as a foundation, microbiome enhancement” holds promise for producing synergistic effects in weight reduction, improving IR, and regulating mood, thereby establishing a multi-layered, sustainable non-pharmacological management plan for PCOS patients.

Future outlook

Although gut microbiome modulation offers a compelling new dimension for PCOS treatment, translating it into widespread clinical practice remains a significant challenge. Future research must transcend current correlational descriptions and advance toward deeper integration of mechanism elucidation, personalized application, and clinical evidence generation. While most existing evidence demonstrates correlations between gut microbiota composition and PCOS phenotypes, elucidating specific causal molecular mechanisms remains paramount. This requires direct validation using germ-free animal models and strain colonization experiments to confirm the direct regulatory effects of specific probiotic strains (e.g., Lactobacillus plantarum CCFM8610) or microbial metabolites (e.g., butyrate) on ovarian function, insulin signaling, and androgen metabolism [181]. Concurrently, multi-omics integration (metagenomics, metabolomics, single-cell transcriptomics) should map “microbiota-metabolite-host tissue” interaction networks to systematically elucidate how key molecules like SCFAs and BAs remotely regulate ovarian microenvironments and hypothalamic function [182]. To provide high-level evidence, large-scale, long-term, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trials must be conducted, focusing on evaluating the effects of probiotics/prebiotics on reproductive outcomes (e.g., ovulation rates, live birth rates) and long-term metabolic health [183]. Research should strive to identify predictive biomarkers—such as baseline gut microbiota composition, SCFA levels, or host genetic variants—to screen patient subgroups most likely to benefit from specific interventions, enabling precision stratification [184]. Cutting-edge technologies also present new opportunities for microbiome interventions. Synthetic biology and engineered bacteria technologies have demonstrated potential. For example, engineered bacteria developed by Kurtz et al. can target therapeutic molecules or respond to pathological signals, offering novel treatment strategies for metabolic diseases [185].Similar approaches hold promise for PCOS, enabling the design of “next-generation living therapeutics” for more precise microbiome modulation. Ultimately, translating basic research into clinical benefits requires accumulating robust clinical evidence to update international guidelines and integrate evidence-based microbiome interventions into standard PCOS management pathways.
