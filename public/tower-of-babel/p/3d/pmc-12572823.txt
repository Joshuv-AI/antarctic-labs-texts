The Predictive Value of Quantitative Analysis of SPECT V/Q Imaging for Postoperative Persistent/Recurrent Pulmonary Hypertension in Patients With Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension After Pulmonary Endarterectomy
Authors: Pingping Han, Rongzheng Ma, Wanmu Xie, Huan Li, Zhu Zhang, Zhenguo Zhai, Liping Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) is a highly effective treatment for chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). However, persistent or recurrent pulmonary hypertension (persistent/recurrent PH) following surgery can adversely impact patients’ outcomes. This study investigated the predictive value of preoperative SPECT ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) imaging for post‐PEA persistent/recurrent PH. Between 2016 and 2022, CTEPH patients at our hospital underwent PEA after right heart catheterization (RHC) and SPECT V/Q imaging and were followed for 2 years. Postoperative mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mmHg indicates persistent/recurrent PH. Correlations were explored between the occurrence of postoperative persistent/recurrent PH and preoperative parameters, including systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP), mPAP, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), and parameters of the SPECT V/Q scan. Seventy‐four patients were enrolled, including 52 males (71.6%). Seventeen patients (23%) developed persistent/recurrent PH within 2–80 weeks after surgery. The persistent/recurrent PH and non‐persistent/recurrent PH groups exhibited statistically significant preoperative differences in preoperative mPAP, sPAP, PVR, Begic's score, and V/Q mismatched volume percentage (p < 0.05 for all). ROC curve analysis identified the optimal cut‐off values: mPAP—44.5 mmHg, sPAP—79.5 mmHg, PVR—944 dyn × s × cm−5, Begic's score—16.5, and V/Q mismatched percentage—35.58%. Cox regression analysis showed that preoperative mPAP and V/Q mismatched percentage were significant predictors of persistent/recurrent PH‐free survival time, with relative hazards of 3.29 (95% CI: 1.08–10.01) (p = 0.036) and 3.94 (95% CI: 1.25–12.42) (p = 0.019), respectively. These findings indicate that metrics derived from SPECT V/Q scans can effectively stratify post‐PEA patients by the risk of persistent/recurrent PH. Quantitative parameters could provide complementary information that enhances predictive accuracy of postoperative persistent/recurrent PH at the individual level. This may support the optimization of clinical management strategies, particularly by guiding patient‐specific therapeutic interventions, such as balloon pulmonary angioplasty for patients with persistent/recurrent PH after PEA.

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), classified as Group IV of pulmonary hypertension (PH) according to the ESC/ERS 2022 guidelines [1], is defined as symptomatic PH accompanied by persistent pulmonary perfusion defects that remain despite 3–6 months of receiving adequate anticoagulation treatment. The true incidence of CTEPH is unknown but it is estimated to be approximately 3% following acute pulmonary embolism (PE) [2, 3]. Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) can effectively improve pulmonary hemodynamics and right ventricular (RV) function, leading to long‐term functional and symptomatic improvement in the vast majority of patients [3, 4, 5, 6]. Consequently, PEA remains the most effective treatment for CTEPH and, therefore, should be offered to all eligible patients.

However, while most patients can benefit from PEA, persistent/recurrent pulmonary hypertension (persistent/recurrent PH) occurs in 16%–51% of post‐PEA patients, which adversely affects long‐term survival following PEA [7, 8, 9]. In‐hospital mortality and poor long‐term survival were both related to persistent/recurrent PH with mPAP ≥ 25 mmHg according to a multicenter registry study from Europe and Canada [6, 10]. Therefore, predicting persistent/recurrent PH may be crucial for determining the need for patients to undergo PEA or receive additional treatment afterward [11]. Several previous studies have examined the use of computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) in relation to persistent/recurrent PH and have suggested various scoring systems [12, 13, 14]. However, no research on V/Q scan has been reported to date. In this study, we sought to comprehensively characterize the preoperative single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan in patients with postoperative persistent/recurrent PH, and to identify an easy, non‐invasive method to predict persistent/recurrent PH following PEA, so that high‐risk patients could receive appropriate attention and, if necessary, immediate treatment before or right after surgery.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

We reviewed consecutive patients treated for CTEPH between 2016 and 2022 at our center. Patients were considered eligible for PEA at our center following a feasibility evaluation based on the distribution and characteristics of comorbidities associated with thromboembolic lesions, and other factors correlated with increased surgical risk. Patients with other pulmonary conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), vasculitis, and lung masses, were excluded. Right heart catheterization (RHC) and SPECT V/Q scans were performed at baseline within 3 months before surgery. All patients were followed up at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years after surgery. RHC was routinely performed at 1‐ and 2‐year follow‐ups. At 3‐ and 6‐month time points, RHC was performed only to confirm the diagnosis when patients exhibited symptoms of PH or when echocardiography indicated the presence of persistent/recurrent PH. Patients with mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 300 dyn × s × cm−5 were diagnosed with persistent/recurrent PH [15]. Two patients passed away during the perioperative period. One of them succumbed to a multi‐organ failure following postoperative rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury. The other, who had a COPD, developed a high fever after surgery and was diagnosed with infections caused by Aspergillus fumigatus, Enterococcus faecalis, and Enterococcus avium. The patient was discharged from hospital and later died at home. These two cases were excluded from further analysis, along with eight others who were lost to follow‐up. Clinical Information

The preoperative clinical information, including functional class, as determined by the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, the 6‐min walk distance (6MWD), and N‐terminal brain natriuretic peptide precursor (NT‐proBNP) were obtained for each patient. Hemodynamic parameters measured via an RHC examination, including systolic pulmonary arterial pressure (sPAP), mPAP, and PVR, were recorded. Patients exhibiting postoperative mPAP ≥ 25 mmHg during follow‐up were considered to have persistent/recurrent PH.

SPECT/CT V/Q Scanning Protocol and Image Reconstruction

SPECT V/Q scanning was conducted over 1 day, according to the guidelines of the European Association of Nuclear Medicine [16]. Ventilation images were acquired using radioactive 99mTechnegas (99mTc) produced by a Technegas generator. Perfusion images were obtained after an intravenous injection of 99mTc‐labeled macroaggregated albumin (99mTc‐MAA).

SPECT/CT images were obtained using a Symbia T2 system (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany). The 99mTc window was centered at 140 keV photopeak with a total width of 15% (range: 129.5–150.5 keV). The SPECT data were acquired in a step‐and‐shoot sequence with a noncircular orbit (dual detectors arranged in a 180° configuration with low‐energy, high‐resolution collimators). After completion of SPECT imaging, attenuation correction/localization non‐contrast CT was acquired [effective milliampere‐second (mAs): 30, kV: 130, slice thickness: 5.0 mm, Acq: 6 mm collimation, reconstruction interval: 2.0 mm, rotation time: 1.0 s, Scan: Craniocaudal, Pitch 1.0].

SPECT images were reconstructed on the scanner console using iterative reconstruction. For SPECT/CT, both non‐attenuation‐corrected (NAC) and CT attenuation‐corrected (AC) images were reconstructed using FLASH3D software (6 iterations, 10 subsets, and an 8.40 Gaussian filter).

Image Analysis

The CT attenuation‐corrected SPECT V/Q images were evaluated by both visual inspection and quantitative analysis. The images were reviewed blindly by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians, with 6 and 10 years of experience, respectively. If the experts' interpretations differed, a consensus was reached with a third reader with more than 20 years of experience in nuclear medicine. Perfusion images, along with ventilation images, were subsequently assessed. Each lung segment was graded independently. A segmental mismatched defect was recorded when perfusion was disproportionately reduced compared to ventilation, whereas a segmental matched defect was noted when both perfusion and ventilation were similarly affected [17]. The segment was graded as normal if the ventilation and perfusion were uniformly preserved. The number of mismatched segmental defects was recorded. Semi‐quantitative analysis was performed using Begic's scoring [18, 19]: a normal segment was attributed 0 points; a segmental reduction or a subsegmental total loss of function was attributed 1 point; and a segmental total loss of function was attributed 2 points. Both lungs were assessed across 18 segments, corresponding to a theoretical total functional losss of 36 points.

Quantitative Analysis

Quantification was performed using the HERMES Hybrid 3D‐Lung Lobar Quantification software package (Software version 2.3, Hermes Medical Solutions AB, Stockholm, Sweden). Reconstructed perfusion and ventilation SPECT and low‐dose CT images were imported into the Hybrid 3D software system. The right and left lung volumes were computed based on CT images using an automatic segmentation algorithm [20]. Ten percent of the maximum counts from perfusion and ventilation imaging counts were set as the pulmonary boundary threshold for CT attenuation‐corrected SPECT data. The radioactivity counts of perfusion and ventilation images were normalized and corrected to obtain the appropriate counts and the corresponding ratio. To determine the volume of interest, the software automatically outlined the boundaries of the left and right lungs, as well as the whole lung on the lung CT image. Areas of reverse mismatch on V/Q scintigraphy were manually removed. The software then automatically calculated the anatomic volume of the left and right lung based on CT images, and the perfusion and ventilation volumes of the left and right lungs obtained from their respective images, and the volume of V/Q mismatch [21]. Finally, the volume percentage of V/Q mismatched defects (VPMD) in relation to the total volume of both lungs were automatically calculated.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were described as means with standard deviations or medians with range, according to the normality of distribution assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were provided and reported as counts and corresponding percentages. Comparisons between patients with and without persistent/recurrent PH were performed using the t‐test for normally distributed continuous variables, the rank‐sum test for non‐normal‐continuous variables, and Fisher's exact test or the Chi‐square tests for categorical variables. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to calculate the area under the curves (AUC), and the cut‐off values for the preoperative variables against the persistent/recurrent PH group were analyzed. The continuous variables were categorized into two subgroups based on the cut‐off values obtained from the ROC curves. Survival analysis was performed to identify differences between subgroups. The event of interest was persistent/recurrent PH, with days to developing persistent/recurrent PH from PEA measured for all patients. Patients who did not develop postoperative persistent/recurrent PH during the 2‐year follow‐up period were right‐censored. The Kaplan−Meier method was used to estimate cumulative survival free of persistent/recurrent PH, and the log‐rank test was used to compare patients in different subgroups. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was utilized to identify independent predictors of postoperative persistent/recurrent PH within the 2‐year period following enrollment. All computations were performed using SPSS 24.0 software.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Seventy‐four patients were enrolled, including 52 males (71.6%). Patient ages ranged from 20 to 74 years, with a median age of 50.2 years. All patients experienced a reduction in mPAP by 1‐year RHC after surgery, with an average reduction of (16.71 ± 9.23) mmHg. Twenty‐six of them also underwent RHC at the 2‐year mark. None of the patients failed to undergo RHC postoperatively. During the 2‐year follow‐up period after surgery with median observational time of 456.7 days (65 weeks), 17 patients (23.0%) showed persistent/recurrent PH, occurring between 14 and 564 days (2–80 weeks) after surgery. Twelve (70.6%) patients underwent balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), with 7 requiring multiple procedures, and all received targeted pharmacotherapy under medical supervision (e.g., riociguat). Patients with persistent/recurrent PH had higher mPAP, sPAP, and PVR, as well as greater Begic's score, and VPMD compared to those without persistent/recurrent PH. Gender, age, and clinical status did not significantly differ between groups. Patients’ baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Both groups showed a significant mPAP reduction post‐surgery: persistent/recurrent PH group (14.41 ± 8.20 mmHg) versus non‐persistent/recurrent PH group (17.39 ± 9.84 mmHg), with no significant difference (p = 0.29).

Kaplan–Meier analysis and log‐rank test showed that preoperative hemodynamic parameters of PEA may influence surgical outcomes. Similarly, the semi‐quantitative and quantitative parameters from the V/Q scan can also impact surgical outcomes. There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of survival curves for persistent/recurrent PH between the subgroups of Begic's score above 16.5 and below (log‐rank test p = 0.007) (Figure 2d), and the subgroups of VPMD above 35.58% and below (log‐rank test p = 0.001) (Figure 2e).

The hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the five parameters are as follows: mPAP, 3.66, 95% CI: 1.26–10.6; sPAP, 3.04, 95% CI: 1.11–8.38; PVR, 3.62, 95% CI: 1.16–11.27; Begic's score, 5.45, 95% CI: 1.24–23.99; and VPMD, 5.40, 95% CI: 1.76–16.58.

Cox Proportional Hazards Regression Model

With postoperative persistent/recurrent PH as the endpoint event, the 5 statistically significant variables of RHC and the SPECT V/Q scan were included in the Cox hazards regression analysis. At last, the VPMD calculated using the Hermes software (with cut‐off value of 35.58%) and mPAP (with cut‐off value of 44.5 mmHg) were significant predictors of postoperative persistent/recurrent PH in PEA with RR of 3.94 (95% CI: 1.25–12.42) (p = 0.019) and 3.29 (95% CI: 1.08–10.01) (p = 0.036), respectively. Other factors, including PVR, sPAP, and Begic's score were not significant predictors (p‐values > 0.05) (Table 3). The survival curve for persistent/recurrent PH is shown in Figure 3.

Discussion

This single‐center, retrospective study is the first to use SPECT V/Q scanning in the prognostication of persistent/recurrent PH before PEA surgery. The findings indicate that both semi‐quantitative and quantitative metrics derived from SPECT V/Q scanning could predict postoperative persistent/recurrent PH, and this prediction correlated with the parameters obtained from RHC. Multivariable analysis identified the mPAP determined by RHC and the VPMD detected by the V/Q scan as the most accurate preoperative indicators of the potential development of persistent/recurrent PH.

V/Q scanning is a crucial initial diagnostic test for excluding the diagnosis of CTEPH, serving as a standard preoperative examination for almost all patients diagnosed with CTEPH [1]. However, V/Q imaging for predicting persistent/recurrent PH has not been studied. In addition to providing a visual confirmation of the presence of CTEPH, SPECT/CT V/Q scan also enables a detailed quantitative analysis. Therefore, parameters derived from this imaging method can be applied beyond diagnosing CTEPH. Our study leveraged both semi‐quantitative and quantitative parameters from the V/Q scan to further identify and categorize patients at high risk for developing postoperative persistent/recurrent PH. This approach enables clinicians to monitor these patients more closely, thus enabling more timely and supportive treatments, such as targeted medications and BPA therapy.

We found that univariable analysis identified the semi‐quantitative and quantitative data from SPECT V/Q imaging. This identification resembled the way in which the pulmonary hemodynamic parameters (determined by RHC), such as mPAP, sPAP, and PVR, predict persistent/recurrent PH. Interestingly, in our study, SPECT V/Q parameters, including Begic's scores and VPMD (calculated by HERMES software), were strongly associated with postoperative persistent/recurrent PH. However, this correlation was not observed in the number of affected segments. This may be because these analyses offer a more precise assessment of the volume of perfusion defects, particularly the VPMD, including subsegmental or even smaller defects that are often overlooked by traditional visual analysis. Visual assessment, limited by the resolution of human eye and subjectivity, may miss subtle or non‐occlusive blood flow redistribution at the subsegmental level. In contrast, quantitative parameters, such as VPMD, can accurately characterize subtle hemodynamic changes by quantifying the volumes of pulmonary perfusion defect using algorithms. This extended assessment improves performance in detecting pathophysiological changes associated with persistent/recurrent PH. Notably, several studies have indicated that microvascular disease may significantly contribute to persistent/recurrent PH following PEA [7, 22]. Furthermore, patients presenting with segmental and distal manifestations of the disease process have higher postoperative PVR than those with proximal pulmonary artery lesions [23]. Although visual assessment frequently show normal perfusion of these lesions, quantitative analysis can actually reveal a reduction in perfusion efficiency.

Proximal organized thrombi can be surgically removed, resulting in complete restoration of blood flow. However, diffuse microvascular alterations, such as small artery muscularization and plexiform lesions, may lead to redistribution of peripheral blood flow, potentially resulting in postoperative persistent/recurrent PH [24]. Currently, SPECT V/Q imaging lacks the precision required to accurately detect these microvascular changes. Although the pattern of perfusion defects often provides certain diagnostic insights, the absence of clear definitions makes it challenging to delineate these abnormalities accurately. However, our results revealed that quantitative assessment of perfusion defects, while not displaying distal or microvascular lesions visually, provides a more precise assessment of overall blood flow impairment than qualitative analysis. Specifically, the quantitative analysis indirectly identified blood flow abnormalities resulting from distal or microvascular lesions, which continue to exhibit high resistance even after PEA. This amplifies the resistance gradient and promotes the “steal phenomenon.” [25] In this phenomenon, blood flow preferentially redistributes to re‐perfused areas characterized by lower resistance. This leads to a reduced perfusion in untreated segments. Due to chronic high‐flow exposure, patients with higher preoperative VPMD may have the vascular reserve exhausted in non‐embolized areas, which impairs their endothelium‐dependent vasodilation.

When central obstructions are surgically removed, these areas cannot compensate for the “steal phenomenon” through vasodilation, leading to a rebound in mPAP. Thus, assessing VPMD preoperatively helps identify patients at “double high‐risk”—those with pre‐existing distal microvascular changes and a higher risk of postoperative hemodynamic disturbances.

In the absence of established guidelines for the risk assessment in patients with persistent/recurrent PH after PEA, robust data have been obtained from previous research. This data has demonstrated that sPAP, mPAP, and PVR are predictors of persistent/recurrent PH and death resulting from CTEPH [7, 8]. The results of our study highlight that preoperative mPAP is a particularly strong predictor of persistent/recurrent PH, which is consistent with the conclusions of earlier studies. Moreover, the quantitative parameter derived from a SPECT V/Q scan—VPMD, as determined using the HERMES software and based on the calculated cut‐off values—emerged as another strong predictor of postoperative persistent/recurrent PH. Importantly, the volume percentage of mismatched defects provids additional risk stratification in patients with higher mPAP. However, in patients with lower mPAP, this measure did not enhance risk stratification. This suggests that the quantitative V/Q scan parameter, when used in conjunction with mPAP stratification, can effectively identify high‐risk patients. Persistent/recurrent PH occurred less frequently in patients exhibiting lower values for both parameters. Integrating hemodynamic parameters with non‐invasive imaging parameters from the V/Q scan allows for a more nuanced approach to the risk assessment for CTEPH patients before surgery. This comprehensive strategy can improve preoperative planning and patient outcomes. On the other hand, postoperative V/Q scan parameters, especially quantitative parameter‐VPMD, may be an even better stronger predictor of prPH, and this is currently under ongoing investigation in our center. However, because the current sample is limited, we are expanding the cohort through a multicenter collaboration to validate these preliminary findings.

It should be noted that predicting the possibility of persistent/recurrent PH at the individual level should not be a contraindication to surgery, for clinical and pulmonary hemodynamic parameters of most patients improve greatly following PEA. While the number of treatment options has increased, encompassing targeted medical therapies and BPA [9, 26], the prevailing research continues to recommend surgical intervention to rapidly alleviate symptoms and improve hemodynamic status, even among patients with persistent/recurrent PH [27]. This study also observed a significant decrease in postoperative mPAP following PEA, irrespective of the occurrence of persistent/recurrent PH during the follow‐up period. Moreover, the reduction was not significantly different between the persistent/recurrent PH and non‐persistent/recurrent PH groups (Table 1). Nevertheless, predictive tools can provide additional evidence aiding clinicians to differentiate between patients who are at a higher risk and may require more invasive interventions, such as BPA or other effective medical treatments. In addition, these tools can help identify individuals at a lower risk, who could benefit from a more conservative pharmacological approach. Moreover, while the quantitative data yielded by the HERMES software has demonstrated robust predictive power for the presence of persistent/recurrent PH, Begic's score presents an accessible, straightforward, and comparatively dependable method for persistent/recurrent PH prediction. This makes it an excellent alternative for nuclear medicine departments with no access to the HERMES software, ensuring that predictive capabilities remain available regardless of resource limitations.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size from a single center may have led to statistical biases. CTEPH is a rare disease, and strict criteria for surgical eligibility limited the number of patients who underwent both pre‐ and postoperative imaging and evaluations. Second, the retrospective nature of the data collection may have introduced biases in the multivariate regression model results. Future research should include larger, multicenter studies and prospective data to validate the predictive value of V/Q scan quantitative analysis for post‐PEA persistent/recurrent PH stratification. Nevertheless, we sought to minimized heterogeneity bias by using strict inclusion criteria and conducting a 2‐year follow‐up for all patients. We enhanced the robustness of our findings using time‐to‐event analysis (Cox model). We believe that larger sample sizes and multicenter studies will advance future research in this field.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that SPECT V/Q quantification effectively stratifies the risk of post‐PEA persistent/recurrent PH. Moreover, it offers critical insights that enable more accurate prognostication and have the potential to refine clinical strategies in the management of pulmonary disorders. This is particularly relevant in guiding tailored therapeutic interventions, such as BPA for patients with persistent/recurrent PH after PEA. By integrating these advanced imaging biomarkers into clinical practice, healthcare providers can optimize patient outcomes through precision‐based approaches, aligning with contemporary advancements in the management of CTEPH.
