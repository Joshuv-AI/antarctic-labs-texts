Ventilatory inefficiency limits 6-min walked distance in systemic sclerosis-associated interstitial lung disease
Authors: Marco Vicenzi, Gaël Deboeck, Chiara Bellocchi, Lorenzo Beretta, Eleonora Tobaldini, Stefano Carugo, Nicola Montano, Gabriel Dias Rodrigues
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic sclerosis-associated interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD) impacts cardiopulmonary systems and exercise capacity serves as a marker of disease severity. While the six-minute walking test (6MWT) is widely used to assess physical performance, its limitations to assess the cardiopulmonary function remain unresolved in SSc-ILD patients. This study aimed to investigate cardiorespiratory adaptations during the 6MWT, hypothesizing that ventilatory inefficiency is related to exercise capacity in SSc-ILD patients. We recruited 23 female SSc-ILD patients and 13 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC). Inclusion criteria included SSc diagnosis (limited or diffuse cutaneous subset) with mild-to-moderate associated ILD. Participants performed 6MWT with combined cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) assessment using a wearable metabolic system to measure ventilatory and gas exchange parameters, including minute ventilation (VE), respiratory frequency (Rf), tidal volume (Vt), and ventilatory equivalent for CO2 (VE/VCO2). SSc-ILD patients exhibited increased Rf and VE during the initial minutes of exercise and during recovery compared to HC. Despite similar walking distances, SSc-ILD demonstrated higher VE/VCO2 during the 6MWT. VE/VCO2 was negatively correlated with 6MWT distance (6MWD) in SSc-ILD but not in HC. Patients walking more than 474 m (median) presented higher VE/VCO2 than HC with same 6MWD. SSc-ILD patients showed a general impaired ventilatory efficiency during 6MWT. The main result suggests that exercise capacity is not only linked to global cardiovascular adaptation but also to ventilatory performance. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating CPET metrics to cardiorespiratory assessments to improve clinical understanding and assessment of SSc-ILD.Keywords: Ventilatory equivalents, Cardiopulmonary exercise testing, Gas exchange, Systemic sclerosis, Interstitial lung diseaseSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Systemic Sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune disease characterized by systemic inflammation, vascular damage, autoantibody production, and fibrosis of the skin and internal organs1. The heart and lungs are frequently affected in SSc patients with variable manifestations, which could be related to cardiopulmonary dysfunctions2,3.

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary hypertension (PH) are among the most prevalent pulmonary complications in SSc patients3. Moreover, progressive tissue stiffness (skin, muscles, and pulmonary interstitium) limiting chest wall excursions is one of the main reasons for augmented respiratory impedance4. This restriction may cause a ventilatory impairment, resulting in reduced pulmonary gases exchange ability for carbon dioxide (CO2) and high ventilatory to VCO2 ratio (VE/VCO2). On the other hand, a gradual dysregulation in central and peripheral chemosensitivity results in abnormally low arterial partial CO2 pressure (PCO2) and dyspnoea during exercise5,6. Thus, the limitation of physical capacity serves as a valid marker of disease severity, highlighting the need of a reliable test to optimize the clinical work-up and the follow-up.

The cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) is the gold standard for a comprehensive assessment of cardiopulmonary capacity. CPET has been demonstrated to be a safe and useful tool in SSc patients, once symptoms and suspicion of cardiopulmonary disease are present7,8. CPET can discriminate the causes of exercise limitation and, of consequence, the origin of symptoms: a respiratory or cardiac or mixed limitation can be defined according to the interpretation of CPET8,9. Among the main parameters, the ventilatory efficiency assessed through the ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2) has the most powerful diagnostic and prognostic value in SSc disease10,11, pointing out a discussion of potential mechanisms involving increased physiologic dead space, chemosensitivity12 and/or cardiorespiratory autonomic dysfunction13. However, due to its limited applicability in daily clinical practice (i.e., owing to factors such as cost, availability, and facility requirements), physical capacity is usually assessed through the six-minute walking test (6MWT). 6MWT offers more basic evaluation including heart rate response, exercise oxygen saturation and self-reported fatigue and dyspnoea but ultimately simply rely on the distance covered in 6 min of walking. In SSc patients with associated form of pulmonary arterial hypertension (SSc-PAH), the six-minute walking distance (6MWD) is a strong predictor of mortality and correlates with hemodynamic pulmonary pressures14. However, in SSc-associated interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD) the 6MWD shows weak correlations with dyspnoea and pulmonary functional test (i.e. forced vital capacity, and diffusing lung capacity of carbon monoxide - DLCO). Even though, some studies have discussed the limitations15,16 and the lack of associations17 between 6MWT and pulmonary involvement in SSc-ILD or SSc-PH. A recent study suggested that 6MWT should be abandoned as an outcome measure in SSc-ILD because of its low specificity in predicting cardiopulmonary involvement in this population15.

Building on this background, our study aims to unravel and characterize the cardiorespiratory adaptations during 6MWT combined with CPET in a cohort of patients with SSc-ILD. Furthermore, this study was undertaken to test the hypothesis that ventilatory inefficiency plays a potential role in determining exercise tolerance during 6MWT, possibly unmasking the limitations of using 6MWT distance alone as a parameter of physical performance in SSc-ILD.

Methods

Sample

We recruited SSc-ILD patients (more than 18 year old) from the Scleroderma Unit, Immunology and Allergology Department (Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda, Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico, Milan, Italy). Patients already had a diagnosis of SSc according to the 2013 ACR/EULAR classification criteria1 presenting with concurrent mild-to-moderate ILD.SSc patients with (1) other overlapping systemic autoimmune diseases (secondary Sjögren syndrome excluded), (2) diffusion lung capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) below 45% and/or forced vital capacity (FVC) below 70%, (3) previous diagnosis of PH, (4) severe disability that impairs walking ability, and (5) ongoing pregnancy have not been included in the study. Patients were classified according to the antibody profile, and into diffuse (dcSSc) or limited (lcSSc) cutaneous subsets. Finally, all enrolled patients were on a stable therapeutic regimen for at least 3 months prior to inclusion, with a mean prednisone dose of 5 mg/day, which is unlikely to have had a major influence on muscle metabolism.

Following the inclusion criteria, 23 SSc-ILD patients and 13 healthy sex- and age-matched controls, recruit in the community, participated in the current study. To normalize the analysis for the same “amount of exercise”, and then for the same “functional capacity”, the groups were also matched by 6MWT distance (SSc-ILD 482 ±71 m vs. HC 482 ±63 m, p = 0.96).

The protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Comitato Etico Milano Area 2) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants signed informed written consent before participation in the study.

Clinical features

We collected data from the digital medical records from which clinical history, SSc classification, autoantibody phenotype (ANA, ACA and aSCL70 positivity), disease duration, organ involvement, and ongoing pharmacological therapy were extracted. The report of baseline echocardiography was considered if performed within the last 3 months. Concerning cardiac assessment, biplane left ventricular volumes and ejection fraction (LVEF) computed using the modified Simpson’s formula, were considered18,19. Right heart function was considered in terms of contractility and hemodynamic, the former expressed by the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE, normal value when > 16 mm), the latter by the ventriculo-atrial systolic pressure gradient through the Doppler sampling of tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV, m/s). This value has been derived from the simplified Bernoulli’s Eq. (4 x TRV2). Moreover, right atrial pressure (RAP) was estimated from the dimension and inspiratory collapse of the inferior vena cava, according to the current recommendations18,19. Finally, non-invasive sPAP was obtained adding RAP to the right ventriculus-atrial systolic pressure gradient. The efficiency of the cardiopulmonary unit was expressed through the TAPSE/sPAP (mm/mmHg) as surrogate of right ventricular-arterial coupling.

PFTs were performed according to the standard recommendations and the value the forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and expiratory peak flow (PEF) were recorded. The diffusion lung capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) was performed only in the SSc-ILD group, as good clinical practice. FEV1/FVC and maximal voluntary ventilation were calculated according to recommendations20.

6MWT and CPET assessment

The 6MWT with CPET was performed, as described elsewhere21,22 Participants were instructed to walk as fast as possible in 6 min. The distance was calculated by the number of laps, each of 30 m plus the remaining meters. The total distance was used as an index of physical performance22. Gas exchange was recorded breath by breath through a wearable metabolic system (K5, Omnia v1.6, Cosmed, ITA) performing gas and flow calibration prior to each test. Minute ventilation (VE), respiratory frequency (Rf), tidal volume (Vt), oxygen uptake (VO2), carbon dioxide (CO2) production (VCO2) were recorded at rest, during exercise and immediately post-exercise. The respiratory quotient (RQ) and the ventilatory equivalent of carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2) were derived. Rf, Vt, and VE/VCO2 were calculated as an average of the second 30 s of every minute during exercise.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the samples was evaluated by the Shapiro–Wilk test. The two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare groups (HC and SSc-ILD) across the timeframes (baseline, 6MWT, and recovery) with Sidak’s post hoc. One-ANOVA with Turkey’s post-hoc was employed for comparisons between groups. Unpaired t-test was used to compare clinical features between groups. The Person’s correlation was employed to test the association between 6MWD and VE/VCO2. Depending on the data distribution, we plan to divide the SSc-ILD participants into two groups based on the median value of the 6MWD. P-value < 0.05 was interpreted as statistically significant. The software used was SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States) and GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, United States).

Results

Clinical features

Among SSc-ILD patients, the lcSSc subset was predominant (91%), with a prevalence of anti Scl-70 autoantibodies. The mean disease duration for SSc-ILD patients was 13 ± 8 years. All patients presented with a DLCO > 60% and were classified as NYHA functional class I, meeting the criteria to clinically exclude PAH in this population23. No differences were found in body mass and height between SSc-ILD and HC. PFTs showed lower values of FVC, and FEV1 in SS-ILD than HC, while PEF and FEV1/FVC were similar between groups. Cardiopulmonary responses to 6MWT

At the last minute of 6MWT, although we observed a greater maximal RQ in HC than in SSc-ILD (SSc-ILD 0.9 ± 0.1 vs. HC 1.1 ± 0.1, p = 0.01), VO2 at the end of exercise was not significantly different between the two groups (SSc-ILD 14.0 ± 4.1 vs. HC 14.7 ± 2 mL/kg∙min-1, p = 0.60). Consequently, VCO2 resulted higher in HC (SSc-ILD 746 ± 235 vs. HC 950 ± 177 mL/min, p = 0.02).

During the entire duration of the 6MWT, SSc-ILD patients showed higher Rf, excepted by the first minute of recovery compared to HC. Rf leaded greater VE during the first-two minute of exercise and the recovery phase, with isolated significant difference in Vt only at the first minute of walk (see Fig. 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first detailed description of cardiorespiratory adaptations during the 6MWT in SSc-ILD patients. The main findings are as follows: (1) SSc-ILD patients demonstrated a higher VE in the first minute of exercise compared to healthy controls (HC); (2) SSc-ILD patients exhibited reduced ventilatory efficiency, reflected by an increased VE/VCO2, both along the entire duration of 6MWT; and (3) within the SSc-ILD group, VE/VCO2 was inversely correlated with the 6MWD, in contrast to the HC group.

Elevated VE/VCO2 ratio is classically related to ventilatory inefficiency corresponding to an excessive VE response to CO2 production24,25. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain ventilatory inefficiency among the SSc-ILD, such as (1) exercise-induced hypoxemia, (2) concomitant pulmonary vasculopathy, (3) augmented physiological dead space, (4) ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, (5) elevated chemoreflex or ergoreflex sensitivity, (6) sympathetic overstimulation, (7) and exacerbated hyperventilation26.

Higher and exaggerated VE response during 6MWT and recovery in SSc-ILD than HC was mainly driven by respiratory frequency (Fig. 1). This might be a consequence of sympathetic overactivity and elevated ventilatory neural drive that has already been reported in the context of mild-to-moderate ILD27.

During exercise, respiratory muscles activity increases to meet the increased oxygen demand and clear CO2. This process typically begins with an increase in Vt and is followed by an increase in Rf as lung compliance decreases at higher tidal volumes. This strategy ensures the most energy-efficient way to increase ventilation during exercise27,28. In ILD patients, the pulmonary system is less efficient due to structural impairments that reduce lung compliance, even at lower exercise intensities28. As a result, SSc-ILD patients exhibited increased Rf to elevate VE during exercise. However, even if VE/VCO2 remained higher in SSc-ILD, VE increased in similar proportion in both the SSc-ILD and HC groups during the main part of the exercise (Fig. 1). Higher Rf might be the source of high VE/VCO2 because of higher anatomical dead space ventilation during exercise. This mechanical limitation, which results in reduced ventilatory efficiency and increased ventilation, cumulates moreover to the overactivation of other afferent feedback mechanisms, such as the group III/IV afferents from locomotor muscles. These afferents excessively stimulate the cardiorespiratory centres, leading to abnormal increases in both Vt and Rf29. Interestingly, Rf and VE remained elevated during the recovery phase, potentially reflecting a residual effect of redundant neural mechanisms (including ergoreflex, baroreflex, chemoreflex, and central command) that continue to enhance ventilation after exercise6,30,31.

Similar O2 consumption during the 6MWT was observed in both SSc-ILD and HC groups, consistent with their comparable walking distance. However, SSc-ILD patients exhibited lower VCO2 and RQ during the final stage of the 6MWT compared to HC, a finding aligned with prior observations in chronic heart failure (CHF) patients21. This suggests that SSc-ILD patients exercised at lower metabolic level ((farther from their ventilatory threshold (VT)). This findings is a pattern typically seen in aerobically trained individuals32.

While we did not directly assess maximal exercise capacity or VT in our cohort, it is unlikely that SSc-ILD patients had superior fitness to HC. Notably, in CHF, VT occurs later relative to peak exercise capacity33, which may explain the lower RQ in SSc-ILD patients at matched walking speeds despite their reduced maximal capacity32. Additionally, we speculate that (1) CO2 retention due to impaired gas exchange and/or (2) reduced CO2 reserve from chronic hyperventilation5,6 could have contributed to the measured VCO2 reduction. These mechanisms might also underlie the elevated ventilation seen during exercise and recovery in SSc-ILD patients.

Unexpectedly, although both groups walked at near-maximal speeds, HC achieved an RQ ~ 1 (approaching VT intensity), whereas SSc-ILD patients maintained an RQ significantly below 1. This discrepancy parallels findings in our prior studies of CHF, pulmonary hypertension (PH), and healthy subjects13,33. In those studies, both patients and HC reached a VE/VCO2 ratio during the 6MWT comparable to that at VT (i.e., the nadir during CPET), suggesting that participants self-regulated intensity to optimize ventilatory efficiency for sustained exercise. This aligns with evidence that VE/VO2 at nadir best predicts prolonged exercise intensity34 and that 6MWT ventilatory parameters approximate those at critical walking speed35.

This study has some limitations. First, by excluding patients with severe ventilatory impairment (DLCO < 45% or FVC < 70%), our findings may not generalize to advanced SSc-ILD populations. Second, while we included steroid-treated patients, skeletal muscle function was not systematically assessed; thus, potential steroid-related effects on exercise metabolism remain unexplored. These gaps highlight the need for future studies encompassing broader disease severity and detailed evaluations of peripheral muscle contributions.

Lastly, the 6MWT is a valuable tool for assessing exercise capacity and functional limitations across diseases21,33,35,36 but its reliance on distance alone may not fully capture the complex mechanisms influencing exercise tolerance in SSc-ILD patients 14–16,37. We show indeed that while SSc-ILD patients may achieve walking distances comparable to healthy individuals, their ventilatory responses remain distinct, with persistently higher VE/VCO₂ ratios even in high performers. Clinically, patients covering more than 474 m during the 6MWT exhibit better (though not fully normalized) ventilatory efficiency (VE/VCO₂ ~34 vs. typical healthy values ≤ 30). This aligns with prior findings linking VE/VCO₂ and a 500-meter threshold to survival in SSc-ILD36–38. However, these values still exceed healthy norms, suggesting residual disease-related ventilatory impairment. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal follow-ups for SSc-ILD patients, particularly those walking less than 474 m, to refine prognostic markers. Integrating CPET could provide deeper insights into ventilatory efficiency and exercise capacity, enabling more targeted therapeutic interventions.

Conclusions

The CPET assessment during 6MWT reveals an inverse and negative correlation between 6MWD and VE/VCO2. Patients achieving greater distances (> 450–500 m) showed VE/VCO₂ values approaching, though still slightly elevated above, normal reference ranges (typically ≤ 30), suggesting relatively preserved but not completely normalized ventilatory function.
