Is the association between financial scarcity and health behaviours moderated by cultural capital? The GLOBE study
Authors: Sigrid L Oosterwegel, Nienke W Boderie, Frank J van Lenthe
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExperiencing financial scarcity taxes cognitive bandwidth. This leaves less capacity to withhold temptations and makes relying on easiest default options more likely. Whether this default option is (un)healthy may depend on the amount of cultural capital acquired during life course. This study examined whether the association between financial scarcity and health behaviours is moderated by cultural capital.MethodsSelf-reported data were used from Dutch adults of the 2014-survey of the GLOBE study (N = 2466). Using linear regression analysis, financial strain (no, some, great) and cultural capital (institutionalized, objectivized, incorporated) were related to body mass index (BMI), alcohol intake, sports participation, cycling and walking, fruit intake and vegetable consumption. The interaction between financial strain and cultural capital was used to assess moderation.ResultsExperiencing some financial strain was associated with a higher BMI (0.7 kg/m2) and less sport participation (−31.8 min/week). Great financial strain was associated with less sport participation (−41.4 min/week). Being in the lowest tertile of cultural capital was associated with a higher BMI (1.3 kg/m2), drinking less alcohol (−10.0 units/week), less sport participation (−31.5 min/week) and consuming less fruit (−2.9 pieces/week). Cultural capital had no significant moderating effect on the relationship between financial strain and these health behaviours.ConclusionFinancial strain and cultural capital seem associated with different health behaviours. Cultural capital had no moderating effect on the relationship between financial strain and different health behaviours. While financial strain and cultural capital could both be entry points for interventions to improve health behaviour, underlying mechanisms require further attention.

Introduction

Reducing socioeconomic health inequalities remains a major societal challenge. In the Netherlands, the highly educated live about 7 years longer than the lower educated and the difference in healthy life years is as high as 18 years.1 To reduce these inequalities, it is important to understand the underlying mechanisms. A large part can be explained by more unhealthy behaviours among those in lower socioeconomic groups,2 but what determines inequalities in health behaviour is less clear.

Two theories increasingly used for understanding socioeconomic inequalities in health behaviour are Mullainathan and Shafir’s3 theory of scarcity and Bourdieus’ theory of cultural capital.4,5 Scarcity means ‘having less than you feel you need’. Scarcity is a broad concept that can relate financial resources, social isolation or lack of time. The experience of scarcity is thought to capture the mind, may change ways of thinking, and make people less forward-thinking and less self-regulated. The theory implies that scarcity, for example financially, captures a great part of cognitive bandwidth. This then cannot be used for executive functions, such as problem solving and withholding temptations, and may make unhealthy food consumption in widely available obesogenic environments more likely. By scarcity induced less forward-thinking may also imply less engagement in other health behaviours, with health consequents later in life (such as physical activity in leisure time). This explains why previous studies suggest that financial scarcity is associated with unhealthy behaviour.6,7

Cultural capital is a concept conceived by the French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu to explain the unequal scholastic achievement of children originating from different social classes by relating academic success.5 Cultural capital can be divided in three forms: (i) incorporated cultural capital contains knowledge, skills, habits and preferences obtained through socialization processes. The transmission and accumulation of incorporated cultural capital begins early and more in families with much incorporated cultural capital; (ii) objectivized cultural capital is about material objects and media, such as books, paintings and musical instruments that require cultural abilities to appreciate this and (iii) institutionalized cultural capital is the objectification of cultural capital in the form of academic qualifications and thereby a neutral representation of competences. Since educational attainment is one of the three components of cultural capital, high education does not equal high cultural capital. Evidence suggests that cultural capital is related to healthier behaviour.8–12 This is because cultural capital comes with differences in lifestyle, not only in cultural taste, but also in eating habits, sport preferences and other habits related to health.13 The important function in society of this differentiation in lifestyle is showing to which social environment you belong. Hereby health behaviour can be a way to distinguish itself from other social groups. Social advantage cumulating in such groups can help keeping body mass index (BMI) lower in obesogenic environments.14

Following the theory of scarcity, it is expected that when people face difficulties in making ends meet, they fall back on the default option, the easiest way, because this option taxes bandwidth the least.3,6,15 Applied to health behaviour, the default option may often be unhealthy behaviour, including little physical activity, eating easily available unhealthy diets and alcohol consumption. However, the default option possibly depends on the amount of cultural capital. Long lasting dispositions internalized over the life course play an important role in the establishment of health behaviours; as such, cultural capital may provide the resources needed to resist temptations for an unhealthy lifestyle.4 Thus, to what extent financial scarcity causes unhealthy behaviour may depend on the amount of cultural capital. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between financial scarcity and different health behaviours and whether cultural capital moderates this association.

Methods

Study population

Self-reported data were used from the Dutch population-based longitudinal GLOBE (Dutch acronym for ‘Health and Living Conditions of the Population of Eindhoven and surroundings’) study, a prospective cohort study. The main aim of the GLOBE study is to quantitatively assess mechanisms and factors explaining socioeconomic inequalities in health. The GLOBE study was initiated in 1991 at the Department of Public Health of Erasmus Medical Centre Rotterdam, in cooperation with the municipal health services in the region of Eindhoven, where the study was conducted among residents aged between 15 and 75 years. The study was conducted in Eindhoven and 15 surrounding villages, given its reasonably representative population for the Netherlands in those years. For representativeness of the study sample, a new random sample was included in 2004 and 2014. The GLOBE study contains (postal) surveys and oral interviews with questions about a wide range of indicators of socioeconomic position, material and social deprivation, cultural factors, health outcomes and health-related behaviours. Surveys were executed six times so far between 1991 and 2021. Interviews were held eight times between 1991 and 2012. For this study, we used cross-sectional survey data from the latest (2014) wave of data collection (N = 2466, after 45.5% response). These participants were representative of the source population in terms of health-related behaviours.16 More detailed information on the objectives, study design and data collection of the Dutch GLOBE study can be found elsewhere.16

Financial scarcity

Financial scarcity was assessed by asking: (i) whether respondents could make ends meet considering their monthly household income (fairly easy, easy, with some, or great difficulty) and (ii) whether respondents had experienced financial difficulties in paying bills for food, rent, electricity and so forth during the preceding year (no, some or great). Then financial scarcity was distributed into having no, some or great financial strain: ‘no financial strain’ if respondents could make ends meet fairly easy or easy, or if they experienced no financial difficulties in the preceding year; ‘some financial strain’ if respondents could make ends meet with some difficulty or if they experienced some financial difficulties in the preceding year; and ‘great financial strain’ if respondents had great difficulty making ends meet or if they experienced great financial difficulties in the preceding year.17

Cultural capital

In line with tremendous previous research,15 incorporated cultural capital questions asked about cultural participation, e.g. visiting historical museums, art museums, opera or ballet, classical concerts and architecture [never (0 points), once per year (1 point) or more than once per year (2 points), leading to a total score]. For objectivized cultural capital, the number of books in possession was used [0–10 books (1 point), 11–25 books (2 points), 26–100 (3 points), 101–200 (4 points) and >200 books (5 points)]. For institutionalized cultural capital the educational level of the respondent and, if available, the educational level of the respondent’s father, mother and partner were asked to capture both institutionalized and family institutionalized cultural capital. More points were given to a higher educational level [no education (1 point), primary education (2 points), up to 8 points for bachelor’s till doctoral]. Available answers were summed after which the mean educational level was calculated (table 1).

Measurements and possible answers of the variables for cultural capital

The scores for each of the three forms of cultural capital were divided into quintiles. Subsequently, the quintiles of the different forms of cultural capital were summed. This total score was used to divide the score of cultural capital in tertiles, with tertile 1 including those with the most cultural capital.

Body weight and health behaviour

Body mass index (BMI in kg/m2) was calculated by self-reported weight (kg) and height (cm).6 While BMI is not a health behaviour, we also refer to BMI in our term ‘health behaviours’ for readability purposes.

Alcohol intake was measured combining questions about the amount (number of glasses) of alcohol consumed per occasion, as well as the frequency of consumption (drinking days/week) to calculate the average units of alcohol consumed per week.

Sports participation was measured with the standardized and validated Short Questionnaire to Assess Health enhancing physical activity, SQUASH,18 by asking which sports were practiced, up to four, on a weekly basis. For each type of sport, respondents reported the frequency (days per week) and duration (hours and minutes a week), which was reported per thirty minutes activity a week (a score of 1 equals 30 min).

Walking and cycling were also measured by using the SQUASH questionnaire.18 For walking and cycling in both leisure time and active commute, self-reported frequency (days per week) and duration (hours and minutes per day) were assessed. When hours and/or minutes were registered but not the number of days, it was assumed that respondents had been active for at least one day.19 The total time spent walking and cycling per thirty minutes a week was calculated by multiplying the amount of days with the amount of time spent a day on walking and cycling.

Fruit and vegetable intake were examined with a food frequency questionnaire. Respondents reported frequency (days per week) of both fruit and vegetable consumption over the past month and indicated the portion size [i.e. number of pieces of fruit (=100 g), and number of serving spoons (=50 g) of vegetables] on an ordinary day. Total weekly intake of fruit and vegetables was calculated by using the two questions above.6

Confounders

Potential confounders included were age (in years), sex (women/men), employment status (nine categories collapsed in two groups: employed vs. unemployed, retired, student, other) and income (five categories, ranging from 0 to 1200 euros per month to more than 4000 euros per month).20–23

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the characteristics of the participants in this study and the missing data. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between financial scarcity and cultural capital, as well as between income and financial scarcity was calculated, to test for possible collinearity. Assuming that missing data were missing at random, missing data were handled via multiple imputation (m = 50).24 To perform multiple imputation, the chained questions function (with predictive mean matching for continuous variables, ordered logistic regression for ordered categorical variables and logistic regression for dichotomous variables) was used. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 4.0.325 with the packages ‘tidyverse’26 and ‘lattice’.27

Four different models were used for the analysis. Linear regression analysis was used to assess the association between (i) independent variable financial scarcity and the health behaviours as dependent variables, (ii) cultural capital and the health behaviours, (iii) both financial scarcity and cultural capital and the health behaviours. To assess moderation, model 4, we tested for an interaction of cultural capital on the association between financial scarcity and the health behaviours. All analysis were adjusted for the confounders mentioned above. For statistical significance, 95% confidence intervals were used.

Ethics approval

No formal approval of the medical ethics committee of the Erasmus University Medical Center was required for the study. The use of personal data in the GLOBE study is in compliance with the Dutch Personal Data Protection Act and the Municipal Database Act, and has been registered with the Dutch Data Protection Authority (number 1248943). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Data was missing between 0.5% for educational level and 16.9% for education of the mother (table 2). The mean age of respondents was 63.1 (SD = 13.1) and 55.6% of the respondents was female. Mean BMI was 26.3 kg/m2 (SD = 4.9), 11.7% was a daily smoker, mean alcohol intake was 19.7 units per week (SD = 19.1). Among respondents with the most cultural capital, 1.7% experienced great financial strain, among those with the least cultural capital this was 7.8%. Two-third (66.7%) of the respondents experiencing great financial strain was in the tertile with the least cultural capital. Among respondents experiencing no financial strain, this was 33.4% (table 3). The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between cultural capital and financial scarcity was 0.24. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between financial scarcity and income was −0.40.

In the matter of cultural capital, less cultural capital (middle and lowest tertile) was associated with a higher BMI, drinking less alcohol, less sport participation and less fruit intake. For those in the middle tertile of cultural capital, this was 3.8 less units of alcohol a week (95% CI −5.8:−1.9) and 1.7 less pieces of fruit a week (95% CI −2.8:−0.7). In the tertile of respondents with the least cultural capital, a 1.3 kg/m2 higher BMI (95% CI 0.79:1.83), 10.0 less units of alcohol a week (95% CI −11.9:−8.0), 31.5 min less sports participation per week (−1.1, 95% CI −1.8:−0.3) and consuming 2.9 pieces of fruits less a week (95% CI −3.9:−1.8) were observed compared to the tertile of respondents with the most cultural capital. Other associations in model 2 were not significant.

Model 3 included both financial scarcity and cultural capital. In terms of financial strain, some as compared to no financial strain was associated with a 0.6 kg/m2 higher BMI (95% CI 0.1:1.1) and 29.1 min less sports participation per week (−1.0, 95% CI −1.7:−0.3) (model 3). Those in the middle tertile of cultural capital consumed 3.8 less units of alcohol a week (95% CI −5.8:−1.9) and 1.7 less pieces of fruit a week (95% CI −2.8:−0.7) as compared to those in the highest tertile of cultural capital. For those in the tertile with the least cultural capital, a 1.3 kg/m2 higher BMI (95% CI 0.7:1.8), 10 less units of alcohol (95% CI −11.9:−8.0), 28.2 min less sports participation a week (−0.9, 95% CI −1.7:−0.2) and 2.8 less pieces of fruit consumed per week (95% CI −3.9:−1.8) were observed compared to those in the tertile with the most cultural capital. Other associations in model 3 were not significant.

Regarding the moderation analysis, cultural capital had no significant moderating effect on the relationship between financial strain and different health behaviours (model 4).

Discussion

Using data from the Dutch GLOBE study, we investigated the moderation effect of cultural capital on the association between financial scarcity and health behaviours. Some financial strain was associated with a higher BMI and less sport participation and experiencing great financial strain was associated with less sport participation. The theory of scarcity hypothesizes that more financial strain is associated with more unhealthy behaviour, which has only been confirmed for a few health behaviours (BMI and sport participation).3 Cultural capital, however, was significantly associated with many health behaviours (BMI, alcohol and fruit intake, sports participation). Our hypothesis that cultural capital moderates the association between financial strain and health behaviour could not be endorsed by these results, possibly due to limited power as the number of respondents with great financial strain per tertile of cultural capital was low.

The association between financial strain and a higher BMI is in line with the findings in other studies. Prentice et al.28 found that prolonged financial stress was associated with subsequent obesity, and Siahpush et al.7 and Beenackers et al.6 also showed that financial stress was associated with obesity. An association between financial strain and fruit and vegetable consumption was not affirmed in this study, unlike the study of Beenackers et al.6 in which financial strain was associated with the combined consumption of less fruit and vegetables. Our study analysed fruit and vegetable consumption separately, which may explain the difference in results. Similar to previous studies, we also found a positive association between cultural capital and sports participation,8,9 a higher self-perceived general health status,12 the consumption of more healthy foods10,29 and a lower BMI.11 Whether cultural capital has a moderating effect on the relationship between financial strain and health behaviour was not studied before.

Notable was that lower alcohol intake was reported among respondents with less cultural capital. Research of Wang et al.30 shows lower frequency of alcohol drinking when reporting more cultural participation. This study looked only at incorporated cultural capital, which could explain the difference in outcome. That is, when taking all parts of cultural capital into account, higher education contributes to more cultural capital. And since people with a higher level of education drink more alcohol than people with a lower level of education this could lead to a higher alcohol intake among respondents with more cultural capital.31

Respondents experiencing financial strain did not walk and cycle more than respondents who did not experience financial strain. On one hand, experiencing financial strain could increase walking and cycling behaviour as they are cheaper and more accessible options compared to for example riding a car. However, less mental bandwidth to think about healthier options may also make car riding or inactive transportation a more convenient and quicker option, thus cancelling out the effects of scarcity on walking and cycling behaviour.

The correlation between cultural capital and financial scarcity was low. Financial scarcity therefore does not necessarily lead to less participation in cultural activities, which is in line with the theory of cultural capital and earlier research.14 The correlation between financial scarcity and income was moderate. Higher income does help to prevent financial scarcity, but does not necessarily avoid financial scarcity, as shown by Beenackers et al.6: having great compared to no financial scarcity increases risk of different unhealthy health behaviours independent of income.

This was the first study to investigate the potential moderating role of cultural capital on the association between financial scarcity and different health behaviours. Although the use of questionnaires will have excluded participants with low literacy, the sample seems representative of the source population in terms of health behaviours.16 Further, the amount of respondents experiencing some or great financial strain (28.4%) is reasonably in accordance with the numbers of the whole Dutch population (32.9% according to the National Institute for Budget Information).32 The possibility that participants with financial strain were less likely to fill in the questionnaire, probably because of less cognitive bandwidth available, does not seem to be the case.

Several limitations, however, should be noted. Firstly, our results are less generalizable to groups with a migration background; they are underrepresented in the GLOBE study.16 Secondly, self-reported data were used which could lead to socially desirable answers and recall bias. This is particularly problematic as such bias differs by levels of financial strain or cultural capital; most likely, this will have resulted in an underestimation of associations. The use of valid questionnaires for sport participation, walking and cycling, fruit and vegetable intake, alcohol intake, and cultural capital may have mitigated some of the bias, but it cannot be excluded to have influenced our findings.18,33–35

Studying cultural capital can contribute to a better understanding of socioeconomic inequality and what resources people (unconsciously) use to determine their health behaviour. Future research could focus on potential differences between specific elements of cultural capital. Policy actions to improve health behaviour should take into account the effects of financial scarcity and the possible preventive role of cultural capital. For instance, for effective improvement of health behaviour, financial stress needs to be reduced first. To improve cultural capital low-threshold access, for example through a city discount pass for affordable prices for museums, theatre, etc. and free access to the library as well as a wide range of cultural activities can help. In addition, more (financial) opportunities for further education can be of added value.

In conclusion, financial strain and cultural capital were both associated with health behaviours, but general cultural capital had no moderating effect on the relationship between financial strain and different health behaviours.

Author contributions

S.L.O. drafted the manuscript, N.W.B. supervised the analysis and F.J.v.L. supervised the research project. All authors contributed to the manuscript and critically revised and approved the final version.

Funding

The GLOBE study is conducted by the Department of Public Health of the Erasmus University Medical Centre in Rotterdam, The Netherlands, in collaboration with the Municipal Public Health Service in the study region (GGD Brabant–Zuidoost). The study has been and is supported by grants from The Netherlands Ministry of Public Health, Welfare and Sport, the Sick Fund Council, the Netherlands Organisation for Advancement of Research, Erasmus University and the Health Research and Development Council.
