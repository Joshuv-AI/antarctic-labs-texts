Constructing a disease management journey map from the acute decompensated heart failure journey perspective: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Cai Wenqing, Liu Jie, Zhang Chen, Li Qingyin, Zhang Baolin, Zhu Keping, Su Yajing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to identify patients' multidimensional needs at different phases of heart failure (HF) management from the acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) journey perspective, providing a basis for developing full-cycle and timely disease management strategies.MethodA descriptive qualitative study was conducted at a cardiovascular specialist hospital in mainland China from January to March 2025. Purposive sampling was used to select participants. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore their disease management needs during the journey. Inductive content analysis was employed to analyze the interview texts and create the disease management journey map.Results18 eligible ADHF patients participated. A journey map framework was constructed based on a literature review, with its horizontal axis divided into the ADHF phase, the vulnerable phase, and the CHF phase. Through interview content analysis, 23 subcategories were extracted, which were categorized into needs, pain points, and emotions. Additionally, relevant personnel and their participation time domains were identified, thus forming the HF disease management journey map.ConclusionsMedical staff should comprehensively assess patients' needs at different phases, identify intervention targets, and gradually establish a full-cycle and timely intervention protocol to optimize patients' experience and quality of life.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a serious clinical consequence and terminal phase of various cardiovascular diseases. It is categorized into acute heart failure (AHF) and chronic heart failure (CHF) according to disease course characteristics (Heidenreich et al., 2022; McDonagh et al., 2023). Among them, acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF), the main type of AHF, refers to the acute exacerbation of existing HF symptoms or signs in patients with a previous HF history (Lala et al., 2024; McDonagh et al., 2023). During the ADHF phase, patients experience the pre-hospital exacerbation stage, the outpatient or emergency department visit stage, the hospitalization stage, and the discharge preparation stage. After discharge from the hospital following treatment, patients will go through a high-risk period of 3 months, referred to as the HF-vulnerable phase, which consists of the discharge phase and intensive treatment phase (Greene et al., 2015). If ADHF does not recur during this period, patients can transition to the stable phase of CHF (Latif et al., 2022). However, patients in the vulnerable phase or stable CHF phase remain highly susceptible to recurrent ADHF, thus falling into a vicious cycle of repeated hospitalizations and disease progression (Abdin et al., 2021). The data indicate that the 1-year readmission rate among these patients ranges from 10% to 20%, with 30-day, 1-year, and 3-year post-discharge mortality rates of 2–8%, 10–30%, and 30–50%, respectively (Savarese et al., 2023; Tromp et al., 2020).

Given the high recurrence rate and mortality of ADHF, implementing full-cycle and timely disease management strategies tailored to the needs of HF patients at different stages is crucial. However, current studies on HF often lack dynamic needs assessment mechanisms (Kiuchi et al., 2025; Mishra et al., 2018; Xiong et al., 2025). Furthermore, medical staff often rely on clinical experience or structured checklists when assessing patients' needs, making it difficult to capture the dynamic changes in patients' needs across various disease stages in real time (Cotie et al., 2024; Jankowska et al., 2023; Liljeroos et al., 2023; Puthumana et al., 2025). This leads to a disconnect between the management guidance provided and patients' understanding and implementation, increasing the risk of ADHF recurrence and adverse outcomes (Kamath et al., 2020).

With the rise of the patient-centred concept, patient journey mapping has gradually been applied in the field of nursing. It has demonstrated significant efficacy in optimizing care for diseases such as diabetes (Solh et al., 2024) and breast cancer (Yao et al., 2024) and is expected to provide an innovative perspective for the full-cycle disease management of HF patients, assisting medical staff in refining HF management processes and optimizing patient experience (Davies et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2020). Patient journey mapping aligns with the holistic perspective of HF management by integrating all phases of the disease process. Based on patient needs, it dynamically presents the interaction between patients and various links of the healthcare system through timelines and graphical methods (Bulto et al., 2024; Fraser et al., 2022; Holt et al., 2024; Saragosa et al., 2023; Van Schalkwijk et al., 2023). Through patient journey mapping, medical staff can deliver precise support at appropriate times from the patient's perspective, addressing the shortcomings of traditional assessment mechanisms in capturing dynamism and personalization (Voorheis et al., 2025).

This study aims to use a descriptive qualitative approach to construct a disease management journey map from the perspective of the ADHF journey, systematically sort out and analyze the entire process of patients from acute ADHF onset and hospitalization to the HF vulnerable phase and the CHF stable phase, comprehensively reveal the multidimensional needs of patients at various stages of disease management, identify intervention targets, and provide a reference for constructing a full-cycle and timely HF management intervention protocol.

Methods

Study design and participants

A descriptive qualitative study approach was used to explore patients' disease management needs at various stages during the ADHF journey. The study was conducted at a cardiovascular specialist hospital in mainland China from January to March 2025. Purposive sampling was adopted to select participants from patients hospitalized for ADHF who had experienced the ADHF phase, the vulnerable phase, and the stable CHF stage to ensure the relevance and depth of the findings (Campbell et al., 2020). The sample size was determined by the principle of data saturation, and interviews were terminated when no new information emerged from participants after three consecutive interviews (Malterud et al., 2016).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following inclusion and exclusion criteria were established to ensure the representativeness of the study sample.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with ADHF, with a history of HF for at least 6 months; (2) New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification of II to IV; (3) aged 18 years or older; (4) able to understand and communicate; and (5) informed and willing to participate.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with unstable conditions preventing their prolonged communication; (2) patients with malignant tumours or other serious physical conditions; and (3) patients in the terminal phase of the illness; and (4) patients with psychological or psychiatric disorders.

Data collection

First, through the literature review, the key nodes experienced by patients during HF disease management were identified, and a preliminary journey map framework was constructed. Purposive sampling was employed to select participants (Campbell et al., 2020). During the selection process, the general sociodemographic information of the participants was collected using the general information questionnaire. In alignment with the principle of maximum variation sampling, factors such as age, gender, educational level, marital status, economic status, duration of HF, frequency of repeated hospitalizations, and cardiac function were comprehensively considered. This approach aimed to ensure diversity in sociodemographic characteristics and provide a broad perspective for constructing the disease management journey map of HF.

Second, the researchers informed eligible participants of the study's objectives and clinical significance, ensuring anonymity and their privacy was strictly protected. After the participants provided their consent, the informed consent forms were signed.

Finally, to visualize the disease management needs of patients during the ADHF phase, HF vulnerability phase, and CHF phase, data were collected through semi-structured face-to-face in-depth interviews with patients on the day before their discharge. Meanwhile, patients' past disease management needs during the HF vulnerability phase and CHF phase were collected through retrospective interviews. The interviews were held in private and quiet environments such as empty wards or demonstration classrooms to ensure that the process was uninterrupted and patients' privacy was fully protected. Each interview was conducted by two principal researchers and lasted 30–60 min. With the participants' consent, the interview contents were audio-recorded and verbatim transcribed within 24 h. Data collection continued until information saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new key information emerged (Rahimi and Khatooni, 2024). When researchers identified issues or events highly relevant to the study topic that had not been adequately discussed in previous interviews, they conducted follow-up interviews to obtain more detailed and accurate information.

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the study objectives and relevant literature (Freedland et al., 2024; Pu et al., 2024) to explore the symptom management needs of participants in detail and the effectiveness of data collection. The guide was piloted with two participants who met the study's inclusion criteria and were selected by purposive sampling method. Following their feedback, the guide was revised and refined. Additionally, the interview data from these two pre-interview participants were fully incorporated into the final analysis of the study results. The formal interview guide was as follows: (1) What discomfort led you to hospitalize this time? (2) What symptoms did you experience during different phases of your illness? How do these symptoms affect your daily life or work? (3) What disease management measures did you take during different phases of your illness? What challenges did you face in the management process, and how did you address them? (4) How did you feel throughout the different phases of your illness? How did your emotions change specifically? (5) Who supported you during different phases of your illness? In what specific ways did they offer support? and (6) What additional support do you need?

Research team

The research team comprised experienced nursing experts, consisting of three principal researchers and two research assistants. The three principal researchers were responsible for the study design, interview implementation, and data analysis. Specifically, two principal researchers collaboratively conducted all the interviews, with one leading the interview process and the other providing assistance and recording the interview notes. The two research assistants were responsible for organizing the interview content and processing the data. Additionally, the third principal researcher supervised and resolved potential ambiguities during the analysis process to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data analysis. All team members were trained in qualitative research methods and possessed extensive clinical experience, laying a solid foundation for the rigor and scientific validity of the study.

Data analysis

Within 24 h after the interview, two research assistants repeatedly listened to the audio recordings and transcribed them verbatim into texts, and sorted out the data in combination with on-site notes. The data were analyzed using inductive content analysis, and Nvivo12.0 software was used for data coding (Lindgren et al., 2020). The specific steps were as follows: (1) The interview texts were repeatedly read to gain familiarity with the overall content. (2) A bottom-up logic was adopted to analyze the texts line-by-line. The meaningful statements were labelled, semantic units were extracted from them, and open coding was performed. (3) Based on the attributes of the codes and the degree of association between them, codes with commonalities were categorized to form subcategories. (4) Subcategories with commonalities were integrated to form categories. Subsequently, the previously constructed journey map framework was integrated with relevant data and information obtained from the interviews to further identify changes in patients' disease management needs across different stages of HF. The journey map was then used to visually present the results. Finally, the researchers and patients jointly reviewed the research findings. Through discussions organized based on thematic data categories, the content of the journey map was refined to ensure its comprehensiveness and consistency.

Rigour

This study complied with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research(SRQR) (O'Brien et al., 2014). To minimize potential bias, the researchers ensured that there was no prior relationship between themselves and the participants before the interviews. Between the two principal researchers, one led the formal face-to-face interviews while the other focused on observing and documenting participants' nonverbal behaviours. To encourage the expression of genuine feelings, researchers actively listened, adjusted questioning styles as needed, avoided leading or suggestive language, and promptly paraphrased and clarified ambiguous content, minimizing social desirability bias. After each interview, interview notes were written collaboratively by the two researchers to capture the most insightful and vivid perspectives. The research team maintained neutrality throughout data collection and analysis by implementing reflexive practices to enhance the trustworthiness and credibility of the findings (McNair et al., 2008). Triangulation methods were employed, integrating participant validation, researcher analysis, and expert evaluation, to enhance the reliability of the study (Bekhet & Zauszniewski, 2012). Additionally, procedures such as the interview guide, data transcription, and analysis steps were documented, ensuring that all records were securely stored to facilitate reproducibility.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases (NO.2025-2570). Each participant signed an informed consent form before participating in the interviews. Throughout the study, participants' privacy was strictly protected. All the recordings and texts were used solely for research and were not disclosed or used for any other reason without participants' consent. In reporting the results, participant information was anonymized to ensure complete privacy protection.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 18 eligible participants were included in this study. To ensure privacy protection, participants' names were anonymized and sequentially labelled as P1–P18. A total of 21 interviews were conducted, with participants P10, P11, and P16 each receiving two interviews to ensure data completeness. The gender distribution included 10 males and 8 females. The participants' ages ranged from 21 to 78 years, with a mean age of 57. For the NYHA cardiac function classification, 66.7% of the participants had NYHA class III. The disease duration ranged from 6 months to 21 years, with a mean duration of 5.4 years. Additionally, 55.5% of the participants were rehospitalized for ADHF during their CHF phase (Table I).

Disease management journey map from the perspective of ADHF journey

The patient journey map consists of a horizontal axis (time axis) and a vertical axis (task axis) (Yao et al., 2024). Based on the preliminarily constructed journey map framework, the horizontal axis was divided into the ADHF phase (including the pre-hospital exacerbation stage, outpatient or emergency department visit stage, hospitalization stage, and discharge preparation stage), the HF vulnerable phase (including the discharge stage and early intensive treatment stage), and the CHF phase (stable control stage) in accordance with relevant guidelines (Heidenreich et al., 2022; McDonagh et al., 2023). Through in-depth analysis of the interview content, the vertical axis was categorized into three dimensions: needs, pain points, and emotions. A total of 23 themes were extracted, which reconstructed the task challenges and emotional changes faced by patients at different stages of the HF disease management journey and identified the relevant personnel involved in disease management at each stage along with their respective time domains of participation. Ultimately, a disease management journey map from the ADHF journey perspective was developed (Figure 1).

ADHF phase

Needs

Subcategory 1: Early hospitalization

ADHF often leads to systemic congestion due to progressive fluid retention. When patients experience acute exacerbation of HF symptoms and signs, early hospitalization or emergency medical care is required to stabilize haemodynamics, correct hypoxemia, maintain organ perfusion and function, reduce organ damage, and improve long-term prognosis. Most patients indicated that early hospitalization was crucial for preventing further symptom deterioration.

P9: “Whenever I feel short of breath or notice swelling at home, I go to the hospital immediately.”

P17: “Once I notice symptoms or feel unwell, I hurry to the hospital without delay, as delay may worsen my condition.”

Category 2: Active engagement in treatment and shared decision-making

Most ADHF patients actively cooperated with various examinations and treatments during hospitalization, took the initiative to participate in discussions on treatment plans and formulation of follow-up plans, and made joint decisions with medical staff, showing a high level of treatment compliance, and a desire to be discharged as soon as possible. However, due to insufficient understanding of HF, some patients still face certain difficulties in choosing treatment options or understanding disease management strategies.

P3: “I try my best to cooperate with the treatment and examinations, controlling the fluid intake. I'm eager to go home quickly.”

P7: “Now, I hope to work with my doctor. I tell him my needs and concerns, and he analyzes the pros and cons of treatment plans. We evaluate together to determine an appropriate treatment plan for me.”

P4: “I know ADHF patients need to limit fluid intake (she picks up a 500 ml carton of milk and drinks it all in one go).”

Pain points

Category 3: Coexistence of physiological symptoms and rapid decline in physical function

ADHF is characterized mainly by multiple physiological symptoms, such as fatigue, oedema, dyspnoea, insufficient sleep, and loss of appetite. These symptoms occur frequently and are difficult to control, leading to a sharp decline in physical function and seriously affecting the ability to perform daily activities.

P17: “When ADHF flares up, I can't sit up straight or lie down. It feels like a string is pulling at me, and I'm too uncomfortable to fall asleep at all.”

Category 4: Comorbidities increase symptom recognition difficulty and treatment complexity

Non-cardiac diseases such as diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease often coexist with HF. These symptoms, which are similar to those of HF, may mask HF symptoms, increase the difficulty of early identification of HF, and even lead to delayed medical consultation. In addition, the presence of HF can also increase the complexity of treating other diseases, prolong treatment and hospitalization time, and affect the overall prognosis.

P2: “I had a cough for a while. At first, I thought it was just a common cold, so I didn't go to the hospital. But it never got better. When I finally went for a checkup, they told me the HF symptoms had worsened.”

Category 5: Inconvenience in seeking medical treatment across regions and noisy environments

Due to the annual increase in the incidence of HF, the lack of unified standards in the diagnosis and treatment processes of primary medical institutions, and the underdevelopment of the family doctor system, most patients prefer to seek diagnosis and treatment in tertiary hospitals. However, seeking medical treatment across regions involves many inconveniences, which not only prolongs patients' waiting time but also increases their economic burden. In addition, some patients have to visit the emergency department because of sudden and critical conditions. Nevertheless, the emergency room is crowded and noisy, which is not conducive to patients' recovery.

P5: “The hospitals in our area aren't good enough. I still trust the large hospital, but it's too hard to get treated and admitted. I waited a full ten days.”

P15: “The emergency room is too noisy and overcrowded. I haven't caught a wink of sleep in two days.”

Category 6: Limited communication time and insufficient health education

Due to the rapid turnover of hospital beds and the heavy workload of medical staff, patients with ADHF generally feel that their communication time with medical staff is limited. Medical staff fail to provide sufficient discharge guidance in an appropriate manner, resulting in inadequate discharge preparation, which leads to symptom deterioration, repeated hospitalizations, and other issues.

P16: “The doctors are too busy. They just gave me a quick explanation about post-discharge care before I was discharged, but I still don’t know the specifics of how to do it.”

Emotions

Category 7: Anxiety and fear

Most ADHF patients tend to experience negative emotions such as anxiety and fear in the early stage of hospitalization due to insufficient understanding or misunderstanding of HF. Additionally, the decreased cardiac pumping function fails to supply sufficient blood to the brain, which reduces the stress regulation ability of the cerebral cortex and further exacerbates the occurrence of anxiety.

P6: “When I got hospitalized, the doctor said I needed surgery, but then they changed their mind. I don't know if my condition was too serious for surgery. This not knowing scares me so much, like I might not have much time left (with tears).”

P8: “I'm usually pretty calm, but when my heart failure acts up, I get so short of breath that I can't sit still. That's when the anxiety starts.”

Category 8: Concealment

Some ADHF patients pretend to be relaxed and are reluctant to reveal their condition to their families, even concealing their medical visits, so as not to cause worry to their families.

P11: “I didn't tell anyone I was in the hospital, especially my parents. Telling them would just make them worry.”

Category 9: Gratitude

Most ADHF patients feel happy when discharged from the hospital and are grateful for the support provided by medical staff during their hospitalization.

P12: “Even though they were busy, the nurses still accompanied me to every check-up personally. I'm truly grateful to them.”

HF vulnerable phase

Needs

Category 10: Exploring diverse disease management strategies

Patients in the vulnerable phase of HF gradually grasp patterns of disease progression by continuously accumulating disease management experience. By observing specific symptoms or signs, they can identify the exacerbation of HF in a timely manner. Meanwhile, patients in this phase actively adapt to the disease and begin to explore diverse disease coping strategies. Most patients reported that active participation in early disease management not only helps improve symptoms but also enhances confidence in adopting a healthy lifestyle, contributing to the prevention of ADHF.

P13: “When I urinate less during the vulnerable phase, can't catch my breath, or get swollen, I just adjust my diuretic dosage on my own. It works pretty well.”

Category 11: Establishing care support systems

During the vulnerable phase of HF, effective disease management relies not only on patients' efforts but also on support from families and society. Establishing an effective care support system is crucial for promoting patient recovery. Most patients reported that companionship, guidance, and supervision from spouses or family members improved disease management adherence and quality of life. Additionally, social support helped alleviate psychological and financial burdens, enabling them to effectively cope with challenges in disease management.

P4: “My husband has been supporting me fully. He won't let me do housework, doesn't make me angry, and stays with me every day. I couldn't get through the vulnerable phase without him.”

P9: “During the vulnerable phase, my boss took care of me by paying my base salary and letting me rest at home. That eased my financial burden a lot.”

Pain points

Category 12: Inadequate medical resources

Primary medical institutions play an important role in reducing the readmission rate of patients in the vulnerable phase of HF and improving their quality of life. However, due to the relative imbalance in economic development, there are significant differences in the diagnostic and treatment capabilities of HF among medical institutions at all levels. Due to the lack of advanced medical equipment and experienced medical staff, primary medical institutions often recommend that patients be transferred to higher-level hospitals. Although the treatment process in primary medical institutions is relatively simple, some patients still have resistance to primary hospitals. They believe that their condition is relatively severe and primary medical institutions are unable to provide appropriate diagnosis and treatment services, so they prefer to seek treatment in medical institutions with better medical standards and higher levels.

P5: “I just feel that the local hospital is incompetent. I don't know if it's because the medication dosage is too low, there's a lack of medicines, or what.”

P16: “During my follow-up appointment in the vulnerable phase at the local hospital, the doctor appeared uncertain (chuckling) and referred me to a tertiary hospital for further evaluation.”

Category 13: Lack of knowledge in disease management

The lack of systematic disease management knowledge is one of the key factors for repeated hospitalizations among patients in the vulnerable phase of HF. Although most patients recognize the importance of mastering disease management knowledge, they struggle to obtain continuous and systematic guidance due to limited access to effective information, resulting in a limited understanding of disease-related knowledge and even misunderstandings. Some patients mainly accumulate disease management experience by observing disease changes, but such experience often lacks scientific basis and accuracy, with the possibility of deviations or errors, which increases the risk of ADHF occurrence.

P10: “I have coronary heart disease, so I should drink more water. But with HF, I shouldn't drink too much. I'm so confused about what to do. During the vulnerable phase, there's no doctor around to guide me whenever I need it.”

Category 14: Reduced participation of families and society

Patients in the vulnerable phase of HF experience decreased physical strength due to the physiological burden caused by the disease and are forced to adjust their original living habits to alleviate physical discomfort. Most patients feel fatigued in daily life and struggle to balance disease management with family responsibilities, resulting in a limited ability to perform daily activities. In addition, some patients experience a sharp drop in work efficiency, leading to inability to fulfill job responsibilities or even resignation, which in turn reduces income and exacerbates medical financial strain. Furthermore, patients both fear being a burden to others and struggle to afford social activities due to financial constraints from healthcare expenses, which results in reduced social participation.

P1: “I used to be able to pick up and drop off my granddaughter, but during the vulnerable phase I can't even walk to the bus stop (sighs).”

P3: “Because of the illness, it takes me longer to handle work during the vulnerable phase. My efficiency is way down, and that means my income's taken a hit too.”

P18: “I no longer go to parties during the vulnerable phase. I'm worried about being a burden to others. Moreover, with so much money spent on treatment, I can't afford that kind of thing.”

Emotions

Category 15: Irritability

Due to the incurable nature of heart failure, frequent acute complications, repeated hospitalizations, and poor prognosis, some patients experience irritable moods.

P10: “I keep getting hospitalized during the vulnerable phase, and this is my sixth hospitalization for HF this year. The condition keeps flaring up and getting better, and I feel so irritable all the time (got into arguments during treatment).”

Category 16: Insecurity and helplessness

The sudden onset of some heart failure symptoms makes it difficult for patients to take timely intervention measures, which enhances their sense of insecurity. Meanwhile, in the face of challenges in disease management, some patients often feel helpless due to a lack of confidence in their own disease management capabilities.

P2: “During the vulnerable phase, I wasn't used to the lack of doctor guidance. I worried I couldn't manage well on my own. It made me feel so insecure.”

Category 17: Despair

Some patients develop a mindset of despair and reluctance to face difficulties due to worries about their illness, frustration with the challenges of symptom management, and the heavy medical burden.

P11: “During the vulnerable phase, living feels like a waste of air, while dying would be a waste of land. Every day in this state feels like a slow drowning, and both my life and work are steeped in utter despair.”

CHF phase

Needs

Category 18: Effectively managing trigger factors

Through long-term observation of the disease course, patients in the stable phase of CHF have found that the occurrence of ADHF may be closely related to specific trigger factors. Actively controlling triggers such as volume overload, elevated blood pressure, infection, and poor treatment adherence is crucial for breaking the vicious cycle of repeated hospitalizations and disease deterioration.

P8: “I've found out that with this illness, I can't overwork myself, can't catch a cold, and there's just so much I can't do during the CHF phase.”

P18: “I weighed myself and checked my blood pressure every day during the CHF phase, and I tried my best to avoid getting sick. Maybe that could reduce the risk of getting worse."

Category 19: Gradually resuming work and life

During long-term coexistence with the disease, patients in the stable phase of CHF gradually resume work and daily life, reasonably arrange various plans, and try to set new life goals. Additionally, patients recognize the importance of self-independence, striving to unleash personal potential for daily life.

P5: “When my heart failure was stable, I did everything on my own. I didn't rely on others. It was important to me that I can still be useful.”

Pain points

Category 20: Decreasing attention to disease management over time

Over time, patients in the stable phase of CHF gradually neglect symptom monitoring and reduce their attention to heart failure-related knowledge. Since these symptoms do not immediately affect daily life, patients gradually become less vigilant about their disease. Patients reported that the symptoms of HF are not obvious and that they have subjective cognitive biases towards the disease symptoms, which may lead to delayed medical consultation.

P5: “I only felt a little nauseous. Nothing else bothered me. I didn't think it was a big deal”

P14: “Five months after my last discharge, I started feeling short of breath again and called the ambulance. But once I got to the hospital, I had no symptoms. I said never mind, then I went home.”

Category 21: Poor symptom coping strategies

Patients in the stable phase of CHF often show varying degrees of non-adherence behaviour due to factors such as symptom relief, medication side effects, difficulty in altering unhealthy lifestyles, conflicts between physiological demands and fluid restriction, and environmental constraints.

P1: “I couldn't control my fluid intake at all when my heart failure was stable. At home, when I was thirsty, I just drank until I wasn't thirsty anymore.”

P13: “Western medicines upset my stomach, so I stopped taking them all if my heart failure was stable.”

Emotions

Category 22: Happiness

Some patients maintained an optimistic attitude during the stable phase of CHF, actively adapting to symptoms and learning to coexist with the disease, which enhanced their quality of life and sense of happiness

P9: “I've had HF for 21 years. I think I find happiness in managing my disease when my symptoms are stable.”

Category 23: Hope

Some patients stated that family support positively influenced their ability to rebuild future goals. When facing challenges, they also sought peer support, encouraged each other, shared experiences, and aspirated to realize their self-worth.

P5: “During my last hospitalization, I said I didn't want treatment. I thought there was nothing left to worry about. But my daughter cried, 'Without you, I have no home. From then on, all I want is that when my symptoms are stable, I can spend more time with her.”

P9: “I used to be hopeless about life, but my fellow patients kept cheering me up. When my symptoms are stable, I feel like it's not bad to be alive.”

Discussion

The disease management journey map based on the perspective of ADHF provides a basis for optimizing service processes and resource allocation

Based on the patient-centred concept, this study explored HF management in detail from the patients' perspective, identifying needs, pain points, and emotions across different stages, and constructed a disease management journey map from the ADHF journey perspective. It breaks through the limitations of previous studies, which focused only on specific stages of HF and lacked an overall perspective, laying a theoretical foundation for the transformation of HF management towards a full disease cycle. Meanwhile, it emphasizes the importance of providing timely, full-cycle disease management strategies according to patients' actual needs. In addition, this study identifies the relevant personnel involved in each stage of HF management and their participation time domains, providing support for optimizing the disease management process and the rational allocation of medical resources. Studies (Jaarsma et al., 2021; Jankowska et al., 2023) have shown that providing precise and personalized disease management strategies for HF patients helps improve their self-reported outcomes and enhance adherence, which can serve as a reference for subsequent research.

Recognizing signs of disease exacerbation is crucial for patients in the ADHF phase

This study reveals that disease exacerbation is the main cause of readmission among HF patients. Correctly identifying disease exacerbation and seeking hospitalization early are important needs for patients in the ADHF phase. However, due to individual differences, there are significant variations in patients' ability to recognize symptoms. The overlapping symptoms between comorbidities make it difficult for patients to accurately identify the pathogenic causes, increasing the risk of ADHF episodes. Over 75% of elderly HF patients have three or more comorbidities, and they have low sensitivity and alertness toward HF symptoms, making them prone to ignoring abnormal signals from the body (Gomez et al., 2024). They often take a wait-and-see attitude in the early stage of disease exacerbation and fail to seek medical treatment in a timely manner, which significantly increases the risk of adverse events and mortality (Hoevelmann et al., 2024). In contrast, younger HF patients often perceive themselves as young and dismiss their symptoms as insignificant, thus delaying medical consultation. They only seek hospital admission for treatment when their symptoms become severe, which not only prolongs their hospital stay but also increases the complexity of treatment (Qiu et al., 2020). In addition, some patients present with occult symptoms that are difficult to attract attention, and the condition is only detected when it further deteriorates, which also prolongs the hospital stay and increases the difficulty of disease management. In the guidelines released by the American Heart Association in 2022, it is clearly stated that HF patients should undergo risk assessment for readmission and death (Heidenreich et al., 2022). A study (Salah et al., 2014) developed the ELAN-HF score based on factors such as patient age, percentage decrease in N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) during hospitalization, presence of oedema, systolic blood pressure, serum sodium level, serum urea level, NT-proBNP value at discharge, and heart function classification. This score enables risk stratification for ADHF patients without ventricular assist device support before discharge, predicts the risks of decompensation and death, and optimizes the subsequent HF management strategy (Vinck et al., 2022). Effective risk assessment combined with standardized follow-up helps identify high-risk factors for HF exacerbation, provides patients with personalized disease management guidance at an early stage, and reduces the risks of ADHF occurrence and death. However, most current risk assessment tools for HF patient readmission and death use data from European and US populations, and their prediction results may not apply to the Chinese population. Therefore, medical staff should focus on the comorbidity issues of elderly HF patients, help them clarify potential or confusing symptoms, and guide them to seek medical treatment early when they experience unexplained fatigue, decreased exercise tolerance, increased heart rate, or significant weight gain in a short period (Paleckiene et al., 2024). For young HF patients and those with occult symptoms, medical staff should emphasize the importance of symptom monitoring and regular follow-up, help them identify and manage subtle symptom changes early, reduce delays in seeking medical care, and lower the risks of readmission and death (Luo et al., 2020). In the future, risk assessment tools for readmission and death should be developed based on the characteristics of the Chinese population to achieve rapid response and early warning for disease exacerbation.

Family and social needs as well as emotional management run through the HF vulnerable phase

This study reveals that with the increasing incidence of HF and the increasing turnover of hospital beds, ADHF patients often enter the vulnerable phase after acute treatment but before full symptom resolution. Patients in the HF vulnerability phase have low cardiac output and insufficient hemoperfusion to organs and tissues, accompanied by compensatory tachycardia, which results in multiple and complex physiological symptoms such as dyspnoea, fatigue, oedema, anorexia, and insufficient sleep. Persistent physical discomfort severely limits these patients' ability to perform daily activities and work during this phase, further increasing their economic burden. A study (Yu et al., 2024) indicated that the aggravation of economic burden is closely associated with disease progression. Compared with patients with moderate or mild economic burden, those with excessive economic burdens have a significantly higher incidence of subjective distress. In addition, high medical expenses and over-reliance on family make this group of patients prone to feelings of self-blame. Some even conceal their uncomfortable symptoms or deliberately delay seeking medical care to reduce the family's burden. Therefore, medical staff should identify the specific needs of patients in this phase at an early stage, conduct dynamic and multi-dimensional assessments based on factors such as family income, type of medical insurance, selection of treatment plans, and disease progression, and provide them with personalized information as well as diverse social support resources. Meanwhile, patients should be encouraged to actively participate in appropriate outdoor activities and social interactions to promote social integration and emotional improvement. Additionally, family members of patients should be encouraged to actively engage in overall disease management and guide patients with a positive attitude to increase their motivation and confidence in disease management, thereby reducing the risk of ADHF recurrence (Kitzman et al., 2021; Thapa et al., 2025).

Strengthen health education for patients in the CHF phase to improve their cooperation in disease management

This study reveals that patients with HF at different stages exhibit varying needs for acquiring disease management knowledge. Unlike patients in the ADHF phase or the HF vulnerability stage, who proactively obtain knowledge related to HF disease management, patients with stable CHF often gradually neglect symptom monitoring or rely on their own disease management experience for self-management as their condition improves, daily life and work resume, and quality of life increases, which increases the risk of ADHF. Although patients receive disease management guidance during the discharge preparation phase and follow-up visits, they struggle to digest a large amount of information in a short time. Moreover, facing a large patient population and constrained by time, space, and human resources, medical staff are unable to meet the personalized health education needs of each patient. This leads to patients' insufficient comprehensive understanding of disease management guidance related to HF and inadequate awareness of the severity of the disease, which in turn reduces their ability and willingness to proactively adopt positive disease management strategies. In recent years, the emergence of mobile health technology has transformed traditional diagnosis, treatment, and nursing models. Mobile health channels such as mHealth platforms and WeChat groups optimize and improve the health service process for HF patients through functions including real-time vital sign monitoring, disease progression tracking, high-risk factor identification, early warning alert setting, medication reminders, and personalized disease management guidance. These functions help standardize HF patients' self-management behaviours, enhance disease management efficiency, and improve patient outcomes (Zhao et al., 2024). However, due to significant individual differences in the disease progression of HF patients, unscreened universal health education information struggles to meet patients' personalized needs. Furthermore, some negative information may exacerbate patients' anxiety and fear and even lead to misjudgement of their condition and delayed treatment. Therefore, medical staff should strengthen health education for patients in the CHF phase and optimize educational methods. Advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence, remote monitoring, and virtual digital humans can be leveraged to deliver health education content in an accurate and intuitive manner through intelligent early warning and personalized management strategies. This approach addresses the challenges of information access and verification, assists patients in conducting scientific disease management and symptom monitoring, and enhances their initiative and compliance in disease management (Allida et al., 2020; Cui et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2025).

The need for convenient access to medical resources covers the entire journey of disease management

This study reveals that patients have a high demand for continuous medical and nursing services throughout the entire journey of HF management and they expect convenient access to medical resources and professional guidance on disease management. Primary medical institutions, with their simple consultation procedures, significantly shorten patients' waiting time and show great potential in providing long-term and effective disease management guidance for HF patients. However, factors such as uneven development levels of primary medical institutions, insufficient resource allocation, and low patient trust have restricted the full exertion of their potential (Huang et al., 2025; Teng & Li, 2022). Therefore, in the future, efforts should be made to actively promote the two-way linkage mechanism between primary medical institutions and tertiary hospitals, clarify the responsibilities and divisions of labour of medical institutions at all levels, and rationally optimize the allocation of regional medical resources (Wang et al., 2024). In addition, training for cardiovascular specialists and nurses in primary medical institutions should be strengthened to increase patients' trust in primary medical institutions, thereby improving the service quality and efficiency of the overall medical service system (Yuan et al., 2022).

Limitations and future directions

This study focused only on interviews with HF patients and did not incorporate perspectives from relevant individuals, such as medical staff and caregivers. This may result in insufficient integration of interactions and contradictions among multi-dimensional subjects, failing to fully present a complete picture of HF disease management. Therefore, future studies should expand the scope of participating subjects, take into account multi-dimensional perspectives, including those of patients, medical staff, and caregivers, strengthen the acquisition of multi-source data, and analyze patients' disease management needs and role collaboration mechanisms across different stages and the entire journey of HF from diverse perspectives. This will further enrich the content of the disease management journey map from the perspective of the ADHF journey.

Meanwhile, the current disease management journey map presents the common needs, pain points, and emotional changes of patients at different stages of HF disease management. Building on the existing work, future studies should further explore the individual differences among patients with different characteristics and construct a targeted disease management journey map from the ADHF journey perspective based on portrait classification. This will the applicability and accuracy of research findings in clinical guidance and health education.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively sorted out the entire process of HF disease management through in-depth interviews. Based on the perspective of the ADHF journey, it constructed a disease management journey map, revealing the needs, pain points, and emotional changes of patients during the ADHF phase, HF-vulnerable phase, and CHF phase. This provides a basis for exploring a patient-centred active disease management model and offers references for the construction of a timely full-cycle support structure and intervention procedures for HF disease management.
