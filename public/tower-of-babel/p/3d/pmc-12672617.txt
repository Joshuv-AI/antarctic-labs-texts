The global, regional, and national burden of Non-Hodgkin lymphoma in 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational locations, 1990–2021: an update from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Tong Li, Xiaojie Liang, Bingyu Lin, Baiwei Luo, Dan Liu, Weixiang Lu, Shengyu Tian, Jia Guo, Xinyu Zhou, Zhihao Jin, Yuquan Huang, Shipeng Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) constitutes a significant portion of the global cancer burden and associated mortality. However, a comprehensive understanding of NHL's scale and trends remains limited, underscoring the need for evidence-based epidemiological research to inform healthcare decisions and planning effectively. Incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) estimates, along with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), were derived from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021. This study delineates NHL epidemiology by sex and age categories globally, regionally, and nationally. It examines NHL burden trends from 1990 to 2021 across various dimensions, analyzes burden breakdowns by population size, age structure, and epidemiologic changes, assesses cross-country inequalities using WHO-endorsed health equity methodologies, and projects NHL burden changes over the next 30 years. Notably, it explores how social development levels influence NHL epidemiological patterns and utilizes frontier analysis to evaluate health potential across different countries and regions. In 2021, 604,554 individuals (95% UI: 558,229–648,746) were diagnosed with NHL, and NHL-related deaths totaled 267,061 (95% UI: 246,095–288,696). From 1990 to 2021, the total number of newly diagnosed cases rose from 255,668 (95% UI: 242,749–272,801) to 604,554 (95% UI: 558,229–648,746); deaths grew from 146,657 (95% UI: 136,931–160,542) to 267,061 (95% UI: 246,095–288,696); and DALYs surged from 5,199,945 (95% UI: 4,797,150–5,770,129) to 7,766,063 (95% UI: 7,130,942–8,486,078). At the regional level, Andean Latin America had the highest ASIR, with 20.2 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI 16.13–25.26). At the national level, Peru recorded the highest age-standardized incidence rate (24.00 [95% UI 17.61–31.1] per 100,000). High-SDI regions exhibited sharp declines in age-standardized DALYs rates. Cross-country inequality increased from 22.68 DALYs per 100,000 in 1990, to 66.26 in 2021. Population growth appeared to have the most significant influence on incidence rates. Frontier analysis reveals that middle and upper-middle SDI countries have greater potential for health improvements. It is projected that the age-standardized rates (ASR) for mortality and DALYs will continue to decline up to 2051. Over the past three decades, NHL burden has intensified, necessitating increased health resources to address challenges associated with aging populations. Currently, high-SDI countries experience the highest NHL incidence and mortality rates, while developing nations with moderate to low-middle SDI levels must enhance efforts to manage the rising NHL burden effectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06559-9.

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is a prevalent malignant hematological neoplasm originating from the lymph nodes and extranodal lymphoid tissues. NHL is categorized into over 60 distinct types of lymphomas based on the involved cell types, exhibiting substantial diversity in clinical features, histopathology, phenotypic profiles, and genetic aberrations. Factors influencing the risk of developing NHL include immunological disorders, pharmaceuticals, infections, lifestyle factors, genetics, ethnicity, family history, and occupational exposures [1–3]. Consequently, the prevention, early detection, diagnosis, and management of NHL rely on a high standard of medical care. There is geographical variation in the incidence of NHL, with B-cell lymphomas being more prevalent, and the incidence of low-grade B-cell lymphomas is higher in high-income regions compared to middle and low-income regions. Globally, NHL ranks as the 11th most common cancer diagnosis and the 11th leading cause of cancer-related mortality [4, 5]. In 2020, there were 544,352 new cases of NHL and 259,793 deaths reported worldwide [6]. Due to factors such as aging populations, AIDS infection, immunosuppressive drugs, occupational exposures, pesticide exposure, and smoking, a significant increase in new cases is anticipated in many countries over the coming years [7, 8]. NHL imposes a substantial economic and healthcare burden on society; therefore, understanding the current epidemiological patterns of NHL worldwide is crucial for improving the allocation of public health resources and clinical practices targeted at NHL.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) project provides the most accurate and comparable assessments of health conditions, injuries, and risk factors through global collaboration [9]. This project provides detailed epidemiological estimates on incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALYs for diseases and injuries, categorized by country, region, gender, age group, and year. The GBD study also performs annual updates to incorporate new data and develop techniques to reduce representativeness bias in the data component [10]. The most recent GBD 2021 study is invaluable for updating our understanding of the epidemiological patterns of NHL and for projecting the cancer burden in regions with limited data, particularly in areas where cancer surveillance is inadequate [9]. It helps policymakers use region-specific data to evaluate cancer control efforts, measure progress, and allocate resources effectively within healthcare systems.

Although there have been numerous regional and national studies on NHL morbidity and mortality, a comprehensive global assessment is still lacking [7]. This study aimed to evaluate regional variations in NHL incidence and mortality by examining age-standardized rates (ASRs). We analyzed temporal trends using average annual percent changes (AAPCs) to gain a detailed understanding of the global NHL burden. Additionally, we investigated related DALYs, conducted a decomposition analysis informed by demographic and epidemiological factors, performed a cross-country inequality analysis using WHO-endorsed health equity methods, and provided demographic forecasts for the next 30 years. For the first time, we conducted a frontier analysis of NHL, assessing each country or region's untapped health potential relative to its developmental stage and its correlation with the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) and baseline ASR.

Our study, through a comprehensive assessment of the global epidemiological patterns of NHL, provides policymakers, funding agencies, researchers, and clinicians with additional information to develop more targeted strategies. This enables the prevention, early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of NHL in high-risk areas, determines the allocation of scarce health resources, and guides the direction of future NHL research.

Methods and materials

Data source and processing

Based on GBD 2021, we collected data on NHL from the Global Health Data Exchange query tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), including figures, rates, and ASR for incidence, mortality, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021, categorized by sex, age, and geographic location. The data, covering 204 countries and territories, were grouped into 5 SDI-based regions and 21 GBD geographical regions. Estimates are available for all age groups, categorized by sex and 5-year age intervals (< 5, 5–9, 10–14, 15–29, … ≥ 95 years), at global and regional levels from 1990 to 2021. Sociodemographic data, including SDI, were also obtained from the Global Health Data Exchange query tool. Rates are reported per 100,000 individuals annually, with age-standardized rates adjusted according to the GBD global population standard. All estimates include 55% uncertainty intervals (UIs), derived from the 25th and 975th percentiles of 1,000 simulations and applied at each estimation stage.

Socio-demographic index

The SDI is a composite measure of developmental progress linked to health outcomes. Calculated as the geometric mean of indices ranging from 0 to 1, it includes lag-adjusted income per capita, average years of education for individuals aged 15 and above, and the total fertility rate for those under 25 [11, 12]. An SDI score of 0 indicates the lowest development status, while a score of 1 signifies the highest theoretical development level. Additional information on the 2021 SDI values for the 21 GBD regions and 204 countries and territories is available through the GBD Exchange query tool.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to shifts in NHL DALYs from 1990 to 2021, we performed decomposition analyses based on population size, age distribution, and epidemiological trends. We assessed the impact of population growth, aging, and epidemiological shifts on changes in NHL epidemiology over the past three decades, a decomposition analysis of DALYs based on population size, age distribution, and population- and age-adjusted DALY rates (referred to here as epidemiological changes) was conducted. Cross-country inequality analysis

Monitoring health inequality informs data-driven health strategies and enhances policies and interventions aimed at reducing health disparities. This study utilized two conventional measures of absolute and relative inequality: the slope index of inequality and the concentration index, to assess NHL burden distribution among nations [14]. The slope index of inequality was calculated by regressing national DALYs rates across all age groups onto a relative position scale associated with sociodemographic development [15]. The concentration index was determined by computing the area beneath the Lorenz concentration curve, modeled with the cumulative DALYs fraction and cumulative relative population distribution ordered by SDI [16].

The Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, which demonstrated superior accuracy and coverage compared to the APC model, was used to forecast the global NHL burden up to 2036. Frontier analysis

Frontier analysis is a quantitative method used to evaluate and enhance health system performance, employing techniques such as data envelopment analysis (DEA) and local polynomial regression (LOESS). It provides benefits in examining the relationship between disease burden and socio-demographic development. Frontier analysis identifies the lowest achievable age-standardized disease burden at various SDI levels by defining"frontier"or optimal practice boundaries. It also assesses the untapped health potential of each country or region relative to its developmental stage by computing the gap between the observed DALY rate and the frontier value (i.e., the effective discrepancy) [18]. The effective difference refers to the absolute deviation from the frontier. For countries or regions below the frontier (where the age-standardized DALY is less than the frontier), the effective difference is assigned a value of zero.

Statistical analysis

To analyze trends in NHL incidence, mortality, and DALYs across regions, we employed ASR and AAPC. Standardization is essential for comparing populations with different age distributions or assessing the same population over time due to age profile shifts. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and death rate (ASDR) per 100,000 individuals were calculated by summing the products of age-specific rates, where αk represents the reference standard population distribution in the k age groups, and βk denotes the corresponding age-specific rate [19]. A weighted average of rates was derived according to the age distribution, with the corresponding formulae provided: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ASR=\frac{{\sum }_{k=1}^{n}{\alpha }_{k}{\beta }_{k}}{{\sum }_{k=1}^{n}{\beta }_{k}}\times 1\text{00,000}$$\end{document}. To assess the temporal trends of incidence, mortality, and DALYs for NHL, we employed Joinpoint regression analysis. Joinpoint regression is a segmented linear regression model that can identify trend change points in time series data and calculate the annual percentage change for each trend segment. If the lower bound exceeds zero, it indicates an increasing trend, while an upper bound below zero suggests a decreasing trend [20].

Results

Global burden of NHL

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the global burden of NHL, we utilized the latest GBD data to evaluate the epidemiological patterns of NHL in 2021 and over the past three decades.

In 2021, an estimated 604,554 individuals (95% UI: 558,229–648,746) were diagnosed with NHL, and NHL-related deaths totaled 267,061 (95% UI: 246,095–288,696). The ASIR was 7.14 per 100,000 individuals (95% UI: 6.58–7.66), while the age-adjusted mortality rate was 3.19 per 100,000 individuals (95% UI: 2.93–3.44). NHL accounted for 7,766,063 DALYs (95% UI: 7,130,942–8,486,078), with an age-standardized rate (ASR) of 93.11 per 100,000 people (95% UI: 85.37–101.95) (Tables S1-S3).

The rates of NHL incidence, mortality, and DALYs in 2021 exhibited age and sex variations. The incidence rate began rising at age 25, with the mortality rate following at 35, both surging most noticeably in the 80–89 age bracket. In contrast, the DALYs rate initially declined in individuals younger than 20, then rose steadily, peaking between ages 90 and 94. Additionally, incidence, mortality, and DALYs rates were consistently higher in males across all age groups. The incidence, mortality, and DALYs followed a similar trend to the rates across age and sex categories. Both incidence and mortality peaked between ages 70 and 74, whereas DALYs shifted from decreasing to increasing and then back to decreasing. Males generally had more cases than females, with the disparity widening as age advanced but narrowing at ages 70–74. Notably, in those over 90, females slightly surpassed males in number (Fig. 1 A-C). From 1990 to 2021, the AAPC in the global ASIR rose by 0.55% (95% UI 0.42–0.69), while the mortality rate saw a decline of 0.41% (95% UI 0.33–0.48), and DALYs dropped by 0.60% (95% UI 0.53–0.66; Tables S1-S3). In conclusion, the rise in the global age-standardized incidence rate of NHL, coupled with significant increases in the absolute numbers of new cases, deaths, and DALYs, highlights the escalating global burden of NHL.

NHL burden in different countries

In order to more effectively discern regional disparities in the burden of NHL, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the epidemiological patterns of NHL at the national level.

At the national level, China reported the largest number of new cases (110,924 [95% UI 86,934–135,200]), fatalities (42,857 [95% UI 33,553–51,712]), and DALYs (1,277,097 [95% UI 997,263–1,551,800]) in 2021, accounting for about 16% of global totals in each category. The highest age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALYs rates at the national level in 2021 were found in select countries across Andean Latin America (e.g., Bolivia, Ecuador, Peru), Australasia (e.g., Australia, New Zealand), the Caribbean (e.g., Grenada), Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (e.g., Malawi, Uganda), and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (e.g., Eswatini, Zimbabwe; Fig. 3; Tables S1-S3). Peru recorded the highest age-standardized incidence rate (24.00 [95% UI 17.61–31.1] per 100,000), closely followed by Iceland (23.81 [95% UI 20.33–28.43] per 100,000). In 2021, the disparity between the highest and lowest age-standardized incidence rates was 29.3, with Kiribati reporting the lowest rate (0.82 [95% UI 0.47–1.11] per 100,000). Trends in age-standardized mortality and DALYs rates mirrored those of incidence rates, underscoring the poor survival outcomes for NHL patients. In line with global and regional trends, from 1990 to 2021, the majority of countries and regions saw an increase in age-standardized incidence alongside a decline in age-standardized mortality and DALYs rates related to NHL (Tables S1-S3). For instance, age-standardized incidence rates rose in Mauritius (AAPC 4.44% [95% UI 3.40–5.49]; Table S1), Lithuania (AAPC 3.57% [95% UI 2.76–4.38]; Table S1), and New Zealand (AAPC 1.34% [95% UI 0.97–1.70]; Table S1).

Decomposition analysis on the incidence and mortality of NHL

To discern the factors influencing the incidence and mortality of NHL, we performed a decomposition analysis. Over the past three decades, there has been a significant rise in the global incidence of NHL, with the greatest surge occurring in the high SDI quintile (Fig. 5C). Aging, population growth, and epidemiological shifts contributed 40.11%, 39.52%, and 20.37%, respectively, to the increase in incidence within the high SDI quintile. The most notable impacts of aging, population growth, and epidemiological transitions were seen in the high SDI quintile (40.11%), low SDI quintile (90.28%), and middle SDI quintile (43.16%), respectively (Table S4). Population growth appeared to have the most significant influence on incidence rates across most SDI quintiles and globally, whereas aging had the strongest effect in the high SDI quintile. Notably, the negative impact of aging on incidence was exclusively observed and most prominent in the low SDI quintile. Mortality trends over the past three decades mirrored those of incidence (Fig. 5D, Table S5).

Frontier analysis of NHL

As societal development progressed, the ASIR rose in most countries and regions with increasing SDI levels. Countries exhibiting significant effective differences were primarily found in the middle and upper-middle SDI categories (Fig. 6A, B), indicating greater potential for health improvements in these regions. Interestingly, in certain high-SDI countries, despite a continuing rise in ASIR, the effective differences remained substantial. Overall, considering the development levels of advanced countries or regions, their unrealized health gains are substantial, and NHL burden could be significantly diminished through improved policy and resource management. For countries and regions with similar economic levels, the successful practices of Peru, Slovenia, and Iceland can serve as models for developing effective disease control and prevention strategies. From 1990 to 2021, the ratio of decreases to increases in the age-standardized rates of mortality and DALYs was approximately 1:1. Discussion

This study offers the latest data on NHL incidence, mortality, and DALYs at global, regional, and national scales from 1990 to 2021, along with an in-depth assessment incorporating trend analysis, decomposition, inequality, and forecasting. Despite regional variations in NHL incidence, mortality, and DALYs, the global NHL burden rose from 1990 to 2021, with new cases growing continuously since 1990, reaching 604,554 (95% UI 558,229–648,746) in 2021. Decomposition analysis identified population growth and aging as key drivers of the shifts in NHL burden. Analysis of cross-country disparities showed that high SDI countries bore a disproportionate NHL burden, with SDI-related inequalities worsening over time. Notably, while the ASR of incidence, mortality, and DALYs is projected to decline from 2022 to 2036, the number of cases for these metrics is expected to continue rising, indicating substantial challenges for NHL management and control in the coming decades.

In 2021, NHL was responsible for 604,554 new cases, 267,061 deaths, and 7,766,063 DALYs globally, with the highest number of cases reported in East Asia and Western Europe, and the highest age-standardized rates observed in Andean Latin America and High-income North America. It is crucial to address not only regions currently experiencing the highest burden but also those with the most significant increases over recent decades. Consequently, Andean Latin America, showing the most substantial rise in age-standardized rates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021, also warrants significant attention. National-level disparities in NHL burden and its temporal trends indicate the need for adaptable health policies tailored to the specific conditions of different countries and regions. Peru reported the highest age-standardized rate of incidence, while China had the highest total number of cases in 2021. HIV infection also plays a significant role in the etiology of certain types of NHL, and the burden of NHL tends to be higher in areas with higher HIV prevalence. Our findings substantiate this notion, demonstrating that in developing countries and regions with substantial HIV-positive populations, such as Peru and sub-Saharan Africa, the high ASIR of NHL can be partially attributed to this factor [21–23]. By actively implementing HIV prevention and control measures, enhancing immune function in patients, and establishing standard chemotherapy regimens that are tolerable for HIV-positive lymphoma patients in combination with antiretroviral therapy, it appears that a reliable strategy for NHL prevention and treatment can be developed for these countries and regions [24, 25]. China, as a representative of highly populous middle-SDI countries, reported the highest number of NHL cases, which may be due to its large population.

Age, period, and birth cohort effects represent three dimensions through which individuals and their societies evolve over time [26]. Investigating the temporal trends in NHL, with particular emphasis on their links to these factors, can enhance our comprehension of disease epidemiology. Incidence rates were found to rise with age, peaking in the 70–74 years age bracket, and then declining in subsequent age groups. The epidemiological variations of NHL across different age groups suggest that future diagnostics and therapeutics for NHL may necessitate the development of more precise age stratification, informed by epidemiological theory. This implies that distinct treatment strategies should be employed for NHL patients in various age groups. Specifically, the ASIR for individuals aged 70 and older was higher compared to other age groups, suggesting that individuals in this age bracket should receive more focused preventive, management, and treatment strategies.

NHL predominantly affects males globally, with higher numbers of cases and elevated ASR of incidence, mortality, and DALYs [27]. Gender disparities in NHL incidence and outcomes varied with age. The male-to-female ratio of NHL initially rose, then declined from age 70 onward, eventually resulting in a predominance of females. This pattern may be due to multiple factors, such as hormonal and genetic differences between sexes, along with variations in environmental exposures and lifestyle factors that might elevate the risk of malignancies in males compared to females. Aligning with existing literature, gender disparities in NHL incidence and mortality are evident globally, with a notably higher number of males diagnosed in developing countries compared to developed nations [28]. This suggests that it may be necessary to consider gender-specific NHL prevention, diagnostic, and treatment strategies in certain populations, depending on the specific circumstances and other influencing factors.

Nonetheless, the NHL burden and trends differed across regions. High-income regions or countries typically exhibited elevated ASR for NHL incidence and DALYs. Despite their high ASR, high-income regions such as Western Europe, High-income North America, High-income Asia Pacific, and Australasia have experienced a marked decline in age-standardized incidence and DALY rates. Conversely, low SDI regions, such as Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, exhibited relatively low ASIR but high ASR for mortality and DALYs. The lower incidence of NHL in low SDI regions may be attributed to a lack of diagnostic resources compared to high SDI regions. Furthermore, in middle SDI regions, the reduction in ASR for incidence, mortality, and DALYs has been gradual. Analyzing cross-country inequalities in NHL burden across the SDI spectrum can elucidate burden distribution patterns and pinpoint countries where NHL prevention and control require enhancement. It is generally presumed that individuals in high-SDI countries have better access to and more effective healthcare systems, potentially resulting in a lower disease burden. Nonetheless, a disproportionate NHL burden was found in countries with high sociodemographic development, consistent with our previous research indicating that nations with elevated sociodemographic levels bear a greater NHL burden. Decomposition analysis reveals that shifts in aging, population growth, and epidemiological factors have significantly influenced NHL incidence and mortality. Analysis of NHL incidence decomposition indicated that increases in burden were chiefly driven by demographic factors, such as population growth and aging, across all development levels. Overall, population growth and aging had more pronounced effects in regions with lower and higher SDI. Our findings indicate that disparities in disease burden among regions and nations are closely associated with the SDI, and a significant increase in these inequalities has been observed over time. Furthermore, the variations in disease burden between countries are primarily driven by population growth and aging. To address these disparities, targeted policies and appropriate allocation of medical resources should be implemented for different nations. For regions with low SDI, the pattern of disease burden is likely primarily attributed to population growth. These areas typically have higher fertility rates; however, slow economic development due to various political, social (such as education, infrastructure), and historical reasons ultimately leads to inadequate medical standards to meet the health needs of a growing population. Global cooperation, especially through the efforts of the World Health Organization and the United Nations to increase medical aid and develop medical guidelines for these countries, could play a significant role in improving the disease burden situation in low SDI regions and contribute to the development of more effective strategies to address these challenges. In middle SDI regions, the variable disease burden patterns may stem from imbalances in socio-economic development, particularly the uneven investment and distribution of medical and health education resources in space and time. These regions need to accelerate socio-economic development on one hand, and on the other, enhance population quality and optimize the allocation of public health resources. For high SDI regions, the disproportionate burden of NHL is mainly due to aging. High SDI countries inevitably have an aging population, which, on one hand, makes age a significant risk factor for NHL, and on the other hand, leads to an increase in chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and diabetes. These conditions require long-term management and costly treatments, putting pressure on healthcare systems. Therefore, high SDI countries should emphasize evidence-based early diagnostic criteria and treatment strategies, focus on multidisciplinary involvement in diagnosis and treatment, and formulate appropriate policies to actively address the challenges brought by their aging population.

Mortality and DALY ASR for NHL are projected to decline annually until 2051. In contrast, the ASR of incidence is expected to decrease annually until 2025, slightly rise between 2025 and 2030, and then either stabilize or decrease marginally post-2030. However, it is important to note that these projections are subject to uncertainties. The actual trends in NHL incidence, mortality, and DALYs in the future could be influenced by a variety of factors, including advancements in medical technology, implementation of public health policies, and changes in population structure. Nonetheless, considering the ongoing global population growth and our decomposition analysis, which indicated that the rise in global NHL cases over the past thirty years is largely due to population growth and aging, the disease burden remains substantial, presenting ongoing challenges for NHL control and management. Consequently, nations worldwide should enhance their healthcare systems to effectively address this demographic shift, particularly focusing on the aging population and optimizing medical resource allocation. In conjunction with our NHL analyses, countries and regions at similar economic levels can learn from the successful experiences of Peru, Slovenia, and Iceland to develop effective disease control and prevention strategies. Clearly, countries with significant potential for health gains and advancements predominantly fall into the middle or high SDI categories. This is because, due to the severe NHL epidemic, advanced medical technology, and adequate research funding in these countries or territories, there is a stronger impetus for conducting fundamental research and enhancing clinical practices for NHL. Such efforts not only benefit these countries but also offer valuable insights for many developing nations, particularly those with less favorable conditions.

As with other conditions analyzed in the GBD study, the precision of NHL models is heavily reliant on the quality and quantity of the input data [29]. Furthermore, underreporting and diagnostic failures can introduce biases in NHL reporting, especially in less developed nations with limited clinical hematology resources, potentially leading to an underestimation of NHL. Last but not least, our study does not cover the ethnic and racial disparities evaluation because of the absence of this information in the GBD dataset. Despite these limitations, this research represents the inaugural comprehensive evaluation of global NHL trends over three decades at the global, regional, and national levels, as informed by GBD 2021. The findings offer a basis for future research in this domain and assist in pinpointing resources and strategies to enhance the quality and comparability of global NHL data.

This research offers insights into trend analysis and forecasting the global burden of NHL. By employing decomposition, cross-country inequality assessment, and frontier analysis, this study aims to inform public health policy and optimize medical resource allocation. Global health policymakers should consider implementing targeted interventions and adaptable strategies to enhance personalized healthcare systems and address the diverse medical needs of each country.
