Complementary and alternative medicine use by patients in pediatric gastroenterology clinics
Authors: Joshua D. Prozialeck, Lauren M. Potthoff, Bridget Whitehead, John E. Fortunato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUse of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) therapies among children seen in pediatric gastroenterology (GI) clinics is high. Multiple CAM therapies are effective as primary and adjuvant therapies for GI disorders and symptoms. Our study aimed to define the historical use of CAM and interest in CAM therapies in a specialized integrative medicine (IM) clinic among patients within an academic pediatric GI practice in the United States.MethodsWe conducted a survey of pediatric patients in outpatient GI clinics. Collected information included patient and caregiver demographics, GI symptoms, GI diagnoses, historical CAM use, interest in learning more about CAM therapies, and interest in an IM clinic. Associations between CAM use or interest and patient or caregiver demographics were tested using chi‐square.ResultsA total of 392 pediatric patients participated (47.2% female). The most common symptoms were abdominal pain (43.4%), constipation (34.7%), and reflux (25.8%). The most common GI disorders were eosinophilic esophagitis (11%), either irritable bowel syndrome or functional abdominal pain (4.3%), and inflammatory bowel disease (3.8%). Prior CAM use was reported by 35.7%. Respondents were most interested in learning more about supplements (36.7%), probiotics (30.6%), and yoga (18.9%). Approximately 86% of patients expressed interest in an IM clinic.ConclusionsCAM use in our study is high. A large majority of respondents are interested in a clinic for complementary care. Our study highlights the support for ongoing education for physicians and patients, deeper investigation into CAM use for pediatric GI patients, and the creation of integrative clinical programs.

INTRODUCTION

According to the National Institutes of Health, complementary approaches to care are defined as non‐mainstream practices used alongside conventional medicine, whereas alternative therapies are used in place of conventional treatment. 1 Many of these therapies are not generally considered part of traditional Western medical care. Some have origins in traditional healing systems, such as Ayurvedic medicine or Traditional Chinese Medicine. Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) therapies include nutritional, physical, and psychological approaches, such as herbal and nutritional supplements, probiotics, acupuncture, yoga, meditation, and chiropractic manipulation. 1 Integrative medicine (IM) refers to a care approach combining conventional and complementary therapies in a coordinated, evidence‐based holistic manner, supported by the collaborative relationship among the patient, family, and health care provider. 2 , 3

Use of CAM among children in the United States (US) ranges from 12% to 50%, 4 , 5 with use being higher in those with chronic illnesses. 6 Much of what is known about CAM use in pediatric gastroenterology (GI) clinics comes from studies of populations outside of the US with a broad range of GI disorders, with up to 80% of these children reporting use of CAM therapies. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Population‐based assessments in the US have examined the use of CAM within specific groups of pediatric GI patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and functional abdominal pain disorders (FAPD). 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 In these studies, reported usage was 36%–84% among patients with IBD and 96% among those with FAPD.

Individuals with chronic GI disorders seek CAM therapies for various reasons, including desire for more natural treatments, improved symptom relief, addressing the perceived root cause of disease, side effects of conventional medications, dissatisfaction with standard medical care, and desire for more personal control of one's health. 8 , 15 , 16 This is particularly significant for patients with IBD, as conventional treatments can involve significant risks, prompting some individuals to seek CAM options. Patients with disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI), historically referred to as functional GI disorders, which are often challenging to manage, may also explore complementary care. This is especially relevant for those patients with chronic pain conditions such as functional abdominal pain (FAP). 17 Given the complex biopsychosocial nature of abdominal pain‐related DGBI (AP‐DGBI), it is not surprising that there is limited success using pharmacologic therapies alone. 18

The objectives of this study were to assess (1) the frequency of CAM use in pediatric patients within an urban US city presenting for outpatient care with a GI physician, (2) caregivers' interest in including CAM in treatment plans for GI symptoms and disorders, and (3) caregivers' interest in an IM clinic to learn more about CAM.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was reviewed by the Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago Institutional Review Board and was determined to be exempt from further oversight.

Participants

This study was cross‐sectional and survey‐based in nature. Between November 2021 and June 2022, English‐speaking caregivers (parents and legal guardians) of new and return patients seen in GI clinics at a tertiary children's hospital (Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago, Chicago, Illinois) were invited to participate in this study. Clinic sites included the main hospital campus located in downtown Chicago and satellite locations in Chicago and surrounding suburbs. Patients were seen in general GI clinics as well as in subspecialty programs including the IBD, intestinal rehabilitation, eosinophilic disease, and motility programs. Participants were excluded if they could not speak, read, or write in English. Study participation was voluntary. Anonymous surveys were distributed by hospital staff and completed prior to the clinical visit with the physician.

Survey

The survey used in this study was adapted from that of Trifa et al. 19 It was modified specifically for our patient population and, as such, has not been formally validated. The survey included 11 items. We collected caregiver demographics, relationship to the patient, and patient age and gender. Additional information collected included new or return visit type, caregiver‐reported symptoms and GI diagnoses, and prior use of CAM therapies. Caregiver interest in CAM therapies and in a specialized IM clinic were also recorded. Given the anonymous nature of the survey, we did not perform review of the medical records to confirm caregiver‐reported diagnoses. Only surveys that included patient age, patient gender, visit type, and interest in an IM clinic were included in data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Patients and caregivers were categorized by age in years (Patient: 0–5, 6–12, 13–18; caregiver: 18–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60), gender (male, female, or other), and visit type (new or return). Frequency of caregiver‐reported symptoms and GI diagnoses were calculated, as well as frequency of prior use of CAM therapy, future interest in CAM therapy, or interest in an IM clinic. Historical CAM use or future interest was defined as an affirmative response to using at least one of a list of treatments: hypnotherapy (HT), guided imagery, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), acupuncture, herbal supplements, nutritional supplements, aromatherapy, meditation, biofeedback, naturopathy, chiropractic, yoga, mindfulness, traditional Chinese medicine, and probiotics. We calculated frequency of prior use and future interest in psychological interventions (defined as a positive response to CBT, guided imagery, HT, or biofeedback), supplements (defined as a positive response to probiotic, herbal supplement, or nutritional supplement) and herbal or nutrition supplements (defined as a positive response to herbal or nutritional supplements). Interest in attending an IM clinic to learn more about CAM therapies was defined as a response of “yes” or “maybe.” We tested for associations of patient and caregiver demographics and visit type with prior CAM use or clinic interest using chi‐square with p values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. In this analysis, patient gender was analyzed as a dichotomous variable (female, male) due to too few observations in the “other” category, and surveys with missing parent age or gender were excluded. Statistical analysis was run using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28.0.1.1. 14

RESULTS

We received a total of 451 surveys; only 392 were included in data analysis due to incomplete information. The total number of surveys distributed is unknown. Demographic data are summarized in Table 1. Most caregivers identified as female (86.9%) and were between the ages of 31 and 50 years (79.7%). Patients were evenly distributed among age groups 0–5 years (30.4%), 6–12 years (34.4%), and 13–18 years (35.2%), with a slight male predominance (52.3%). The most common symptom reported was abdominal pain (43.4%), and the most common diagnosis was eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE, 11%) (Table 2).

Over one‐third of caregivers (35.7%) endorsed prior CAM use by patients. Use of probiotics (23.2%) was the most common modality reported (Table 3). When examining subgroups of patients with various GI diseases and disorders, historical CAM usage ranged from 12.5% to 100%, with the highest frequency in patients with motility disorders, short bowel syndrome, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Patients with FAP were the only subgroup with less than one‐third of patients reporting CAM usage. Caregivers were most interested in learning about herbal and nutritional supplements (36.7%) (Table 3). The majority of caregivers (86%) expressed interest in an IM clinic, with the following responses: “yes” 163 (41.6%), “maybe” 175 (44.6%), and “no” 54 (13.8%).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated frequency of CAM use and interest in patients seeking GI care in a large, pediatric academic medical center in the Chicagoland area. Our specific objectives were to examine (1) historic use of CAM modalities in pediatric GI patients, (2) caregivers' interest in CAM modalities to treat GI symptoms and disorders, and (3) caregivers' interest in an IM clinic to learn more about CAM therapies. More than one‐third of patients in this study have used CAM. This is consistent with prior studies of CAM use in pediatric general GI clinics in the Netherlands and Australia, 8 , 9 though less than reported use in a Canadian study (68.7%). 7 To our knowledge, this is the first report of CAM usage and IM clinic interest in a broad cohort of pediatric GI patients within the US.

It is established that CAM use is higher in those with chronic disorders. 4 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 In all but one GI disorder subgroup, at least one‐third of patients reported prior CAM usage, and at least a third of all subgroups expressed future interest. In the FAP subgroup, historical CAM use was reported by 12.5% of patients. This low value is likely secondary to the small number of patients within the group. When combining the IBS and FAP subgroups, usage increased to 47%, which is similar to our entire cohort, though lower than the reported usage rate (96%) in a larger US population‐based study including a broader range of FAPD diagnoses and CAM modalities. 13 CAM use among subgroups with parent‐reported diagnoses of IBD (40%) and EoE (37.2%) was also similar to overall use within the entire cohort (35.7%). In the literature, reported use of CAM in pediatric IBD patients varies widely from 22% to 84%. 11 , 16 , 24 , 25 , 26 One study described the use of CAM by children with EoE in Australia, 27 which was high; 60.2% used CAM products, 23.3% used CAM therapies, and 40.3% met with a CAM practitioner.

In studies of both pediatric IBD and EoE patients, there is an overlap in trends of CAM and factors influencing CAM use, including concerns related to conventional therapies and adverse effects of treatments. 16 , 28 In a study by Heuschkel et al., 16 pediatric IBD patients identified several reasons for using CAM, which are similar to those reported in a study of pediatric patients with a variety of GI disorders. 8 As potential drivers of CAM overlap across different disease states, motivations for CAM use may be independent of a specific disease or patient condition when considering complementary care.

In our study, probiotics had the highest usage and the second highest future interest of any CAM therapy, with historical use similar to prior reports of pediatric GI patients. 7 , 9 Despite this high frequency, indications for their use based on recent pediatric and adult societal guidelines are limited. 29 , 30 In both guidelines, use of individual strains was mentioned for specific diagnoses such as prevention of nosocomial diarrhea and colic in preterm infants. However, pediatric‐specific guidelines also included consideration of Limosilactobacillus reuteri DSM 17938 for FAPD and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosis GG for IBS. It should be noted that for each of these, the certainty of evidence was low to moderate, resulting in weak grades of recommendation. A strong grade of recommendation was given for the use of probiotics to prevent antibiotic‐associated diarrhea. No recommendations could be made for those with IBD, functional constipation, celiac disease, small bowel bacterial overgrowth, or pancreatitis due to insufficient or lack of evidence.

We did not collect information regarding reasons for probiotic use, which is an opportunity for future studies. Drivers for use may be similar to those of other CAM therapies, as described previously, with additional reasons including general health, wellness support, and disease prevention. 31 Regulation of probiotics is less rigorous than pharmaceuticals, without the need for controlled trials supporting efficacy. 32 In addition, awareness of and access to probiotics is likely influenced by their robust marketing to the general public. 31 , 33

Our study assessed usage and interest in multiple psychological interventions, including HT, guided imagery, biofeedback, and CBT. Though reported historical usage of any psychological therapy was low (7.3%), interest was higher at 22.2%. Considered a mainstream treatment modality, CBT was included in this study due to (1) its proven benefits, (2) IM's focus on mind‐body health, and (3) IM programs frequently including a psychologist for mental health support. 3 CBT was the third most used therapy, though seventh in caregiver interest (13.8%). There is evidence supporting CBT in the treatment of children with DGBI and IBD, 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 with studies demonstrating improvement in GI symptoms associated with FAPD, anxiety, depression, and quality of life, with benefits lasting up to 1 year post‐treatment. 39

In a study by Lalouni et al., therapist‐guided, internet‐delivered CBT was noted to be cost effective compared to standard treatment, with a calculated cost savings of $1050 for every child who received the intervention. 36 In 2025, the European and North American Societies for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (ESPGHAN/NASPGHAN) published consensus guidelines for the treatment of pediatric AP‐DGBI notable for a strong recommendation for the use of CBT based on extensive research support and demonstrated benefits over standard care. 42

Caregivers reported the lowest historical use (0.3%) and the lowest interest (7.7%) in HT, which is a therapy becoming more utilized, especially within pediatric GI. Effective for pediatric patients with AP‐DGBI, 43 , 44 children who have undergone HT can experience sustained reductions in pain frequency and intensity 5–6 years post‐intervention. 45 , 46 In addition, HT is accessible via remote platforms as well as with in‐person providers, 44 with both modalities associated with long‐lasting pain improvement. 46 The ESPGHAN/NASPGHAN guidelines for AP‐DGBI treatment support a strong recommendation for the use of HT based on the degree of evidence and effect size. 42 Many patients and caregivers may not be aware of the potential health benefits of HT and may also have negative impressions associated with myths and depictions of hypnosis for entertainment. 47 Therefore, in practice it is important to fully describe HT and its efficacy to enhance patient acceptance.

In this study, patient and caregiver age, gender, and visit type were not associated with prior CAM use (Table 4). However, younger patient and caregiver age, female caregiver gender, and new visit type were all associated with interest in learning more about CAM in an IM clinic. Females use CAM at higher rates than males, which may account for female caregivers expressing higher interest in CAM for their children. 15 , 48 , 49 , 50 Further studies are warranted to better understand the relationships between younger patient and caregiver age with CAM interest.

There was considerable interest (86%) in scheduling a visit in an IM clinic to learn more about CAM therapies, which supports consideration for the development of multidisciplinary IM programs specifically designed to address GI symptoms and disorders. Programs of this nature are generally comprised of physicians and other providers with advanced training in IM, nutrition, psychology, and specific treatment modalities. 3 They also serve as educational resources for students, trainees, and faculty and as centers to coordinate and advance IM research.

The authors acknowledge this study has several limitations. The survey instrument used was not validated. Descriptions of CAM terms and modalities were not provided (e.g., an explanation of nutritional supplements and herbal supplements and the distinction between HT and guided imagery), which may affect reporting. There may be a selection bias towards a population that has interest in CAM and IM, and surveys were completed retrospectively, which is subject to recall bias and may not completely reflect prior CAM use or future interest by the patient. As review of the medical record was not performed, we were unable to verify caregiver‐reported diagnoses or symptoms, and it could not be determined if prior CAM use was self‐guided or recommended by a physician.

Furthermore, the total number of distributed surveys is unknown; thus, a decline rate cannot be calculated. Broad survey distribution by point‐of‐service staff at multiple clinical sites and the anonymous nature of the project contribute to the unknown number. Surveys were distributed in both downtown and satellite locations to best capture a heterogeneous study population; however, demographic information yielding additional analyses regarding race/ethnicity or socioeconomic background was not collected. In addition, survey completion was limited to English‐speaking caregivers, which further limits population‐based assessment. Subgroup analysis was limited by low numbers of patients in each group. Despite these limitations, our cross‐sectional study contributes to understanding CAM use and helps generate several hypotheses towards future prospective studies.

CONCLUSION

CAM use is high within this broad population of pediatric GI patients in our US‐based, urban academic medical center. There is considerable interest in CAM therapies to treat GI symptoms and disorders. Interest in specific therapies, however, is variable, and potential inverse relationships between level of interest in certain modalities and their established benefits may exist. This study provides a foundation for future research initiatives investigating available CAM therapies, including specific types of supplements, patterns and motivations for CAM usage, condition‐based interest in CAM and specific therapies, and patients' and caregivers' expectations regarding outcomes and perceptions of efficacy. There is substantial interest in IM clinics, which further emphasizes support for ongoing research, educational opportunities for caregivers and healthcare providers, and clinical programmatic development.
