Causal relationship of inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites in cerebral small vessel disease: a two‐step Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Zidong Li, Lu Miao, Tianyi Zhang, Aline M. Thomas, Shen Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeThe aim was to investigate the causal relationships of inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites in cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD).MethodsBidirectional Mendelian randomization was first conducted to screen inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites that were associated with imaging features of CSVD, including white matter hyperintensities, recent small subcortical infarcts, cortical cerebral microinfarcts, cerebral microbleeds, lacunes and enlarged perivascular spaces. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness and pleiotropy of these results. Subsequently, inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites that were associated with CSVD were subjected to functional enrichment. Finally, mediation analysis was employed to investigate whether inflammatory cytokines or serum metabolites acted as an intermediary for the other in their causal relationship with CSVD.ResultsOf the inflammatory cytokines, five were risk factors (e.g., tumour‐necrosis‐factor‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand) and five (e.g., fibroblast growth factor 19) were protective factors for CSVD. Eleven serum metabolites that increased CSVD risk and 13 metabolites that decreased CSVD risk were also identified. The majority of these markers of CSVD susceptibility were lipid metabolites. Natural killer cell receptor sub‐type 2B4 was determined to act as a mediating factor of an unidentified metabolite for the enlargement of perivascular spaces.ConclusionSeveral inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites had causal relationships with imaging features of CSVD. A natural killer cell receptor mediated in part the promotional effect of a metabolite on perivascular space enlargement.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) refers to a series of clinical, imaging and pathological syndromes resulting from structural and functional abnormalities of small vessels in the brain. It is highly prevalent and a common cause of stroke, cognitive decline, dyskinesia and mood disorder [1]. The most established imaging features of CSVD are the presence of white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), recent small subcortical infarcts (RSSIs), cortical cerebral microinfarcts (CMIs), lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds and brain atrophy [2].

The pathogenesis and treatment of CSVD remains elusive [3]. The prevalence of CSVD increases with age and is associated with a complex mix of genetic and vascular risk factors [3, 4]. Accumulating evidence suggests that inflammation and metabolic disorders are associated with the development and severity of CSVD [5, 6]. C‐reactive protein, fibrinogen and cytokines (interleukin 1, interleukin 6), tumour necrosis factor α (TNF‐α) and vascular endothelial growth factor correspond to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of CSVD [5, 7, 8, 9]. In a certain population of early‐onset CSVD patients, the higher presence of fatty acid metabolites and glycoprotein acetyls was associated with WMH [10, 11]. Multiple lipids, cyclic RNAs and peptides participate in intracranial atherosclerosis, microcirculatory endothelial damage, inflammation and other processes attributed to CSVD [12]. Still, since measuring individual metabolites and cytokines in large populations is time‐consuming and expensive, their association with CSVD remains unclear due to the scarcity of studies investigating their causal relationship.

In this study, Mendelian randomization (MR) was utilized to investigate the causal relationship of inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites in the presentation of imaging features of CSVD including WMH, lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds, RSSIs and CMIs. Infarcts (RSSIs and CMIs) were analysed together as ischaemic stroke due to small vessel occlusion (small vessel ischaemic stroke).

METHODS

Study design

The MR used in this study consisted of two steps: the first evaluated the bidirectional association of established inflammatory cytokines [13] and serum metabolites [14] (step 1) with five CSVD imaging features, while the second evaluated the potential that cytokines and metabolites participated in the other's effect on CSVD (mediation; step 2) (Figure 1).

Diagram of the two‐step Mendelian randomization process. (a) Bidirectional association analysis (step 1) between inflammatory cytokines or serum metabolites and CSVD. (b) Mediation analysis (step 2) between serum metabolites, inflammatory cytokines and CSVD.

Data source

Genetic data for inflammatory cytokines were accessed from a prior genome‐wide association study (GWAS) and encompassed 91 plasma inflammatory cytokines across a cohort of 14,824 participants [13]. Genetic data for the 1400 serum metabolites (consisting of 1091 serum metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios, e.g., uridine/pseudouridine) were obtained from the latest GWAS summary data, which included genome‐wide genotype data from 8096 European individuals [14].

Summary data for neuroimaging features in MRI of WMH included WMH in T2‐weighted MRI sequences (WMH‐T2) [15], fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) [16]. WMH‐T2 was consistently used as a radiological marker to identify CSVD, whilst FA and MD were used to measure white matter microstructural integrity to identify abnormalities associated with WMHs [17].

These manifestations of CSVD (outcome of MR) were extracted from the latest version of the GWAS, including the presence of WMHs, lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds and/or small vessel ischaemic stroke. Patients allocated to the small vessel ischaemic stroke group exhibited acute clinical symptoms and imaging features. This group included patients with either RSSIs or CMIs. Details are in Table 1.

Instrumental variable selection

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were determined to be instrumental variables for MR based on the following assumptions: they were (1) closely linked to risk factors; (2) devoid of associations with confounding factors; and (3) exerted influence on the CSVD‐associated imaging features indirectly, that is, solely through their impact on the risk factor (Figure 1b).

First, SNPs that were strongly associated (p < 5 × 10−8) with inflammatory cytokines, serum metabolites and CSVD (WMHs, lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces and small vessel ischaemic stroke in GWAS) in bidirectional causality analyses were selected [23]. If the number of SNPs satisfying the above p value did not reach three, a p value of 5 × 10−6 was used instead (FA, MD, cerebral microbleeds and small vessel ischaemic stroke in GIGASTROKE study). Secondly, low linkage disequilibrium, which reflects the degree of genetic association of each SNP with other SNPs, was performed using the criterion r 2 < 0.001 and distance >10,000 kb to select SNPs that were independently associated with the cytokines and metabolites. Additionally, all palindromic SNPs were removed. Thirdly, the explained variance (R 2) and F‐statistic parameters were calculated to assess SNP association with risk factors.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Primary analysis

A two‐sample, bidirectional MR was conducted to investigate the causal effects of inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites on CSVD. Cytokines or metabolites with fewer than three instrumental variables were excluded from further analysis. MR results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was utilized for MR analysis, which is inversely proportional to the Egger method. A two‐sided p value of 0.00055 (0.05/91) or 0.00003571 (0.05/1400) after Bonferroni correction for inflammatory cytokines or serum metabolites, respectively, was defined as statistically significant.

Mediation analysis

In the two‐sample MR analysis, inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites with significant associations with CSVD were incorporated into the mediation analysis. Whether serum metabolites were significantly associated with cytokines was investigated. If such a relationship existed, a mediation MR analysis was conducted to explore which caused CSVD and which functioned as the intermediary.

Sensitivity analysis

Cochrane's Q test was conducted to evaluate the heterogeneity of each SNP and scatter plots were generated to describe the stability of the causal relationship between inflammatory cytokines/serum metabolites and CSVD imaging features. Moreover, each SNP was sequentially omitted, and the IVW method was applied to the remaining SNPs to assess the potential impact of the omitted variant on the estimates. Additionally, PRESSO and Egger regressions were performed to evaluate the potential for horizontal pleiotropy. MR‐PRESSO identified significant SNP outliers, which were subsequently removed. Additionally, the Steiger test was conducted to ascertain whether our results supported our hypothesis. Positive IVW results that p ≥ 0.05 for all sensitivity analyses were considered causal relationships. All MR analyses were performed using R (4.3.1).

Functional enrichment analysis

The R package “ClusterProfiler” was employed to perform Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of obtained inflammatory cytokines. Serum metabolites causally related to CSVD were functionally enriched using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 website platform [24]. Significantly enriched pathways were determined using a threshold of p and q values <0.05.

RESULTS

Instrumental variable selection

For WMHs

Twelve SNPs were identified as instrumental variables linked to FA‐ or MD‐related inflammatory cytokines at a level of p < 5 × 10−6 and three SNPs with WMH‐T2 at p < 5 × 10−8. Also 37 SNPs linked to FA‐ or MD‐related serum metabolites at a level of p < 5 × 10−8 were identified and 11 SNPs with WMH‐T2 at a level of p < 5 × 10−8. p < 5 × 10−6 was selected as the thresholding criterion for SNPs in reverse causality analyses and mediation analyses. All SNPs and MR analyses are described in Tables [Link], [Link].

For lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds and small vessel ischaemic stroke

In all, 44 SNPs were identified as instrumental variables for CSVD‐related inflammatory cytokines, including three SNPs associated with lacunes, six with enlarged perivascular spaces, four with cerebral microbleeds, and 31 with small vessel ischaemic stroke at p < 5 × 10−8. Also, 66 SNPs were identified as instrumental variables for CSVD‐related serum metabolites, including 11 SNPs associated with lacunes, 22 with enlarged perivascular spaces, three with cerebral microbleeds, 14 with small vessel ischaemic stroke (GWAS) and 16 with small vessel ischaemic stroke (GIGASTROKE) at a level of p < 5 × 10−8. p < 5 × 10−8 was selected as the thresholding criterion for SNPs in reverse causality analyses and mediation analyses. All SNPs and MR analyses are described in Tables [Link], [Link].

White matter hyperintensities

MR (IVW) indicated that the genetic prediction of a specific inflammatory cytokine (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E–binding protein 1) was associated with an increased risk of FA and MD (Figures 2 and S1–S4; Table S6). Three metabolites were associated with FA or MD: N‐acetylarginine, oleoyl ethanolamide and 12,13‐DiHOME. Lower N‐acetylarginine was associated with a decreased risk of FA, whilst decreased oleoyl ethanolamide and 12,13‐DiHOME corresponded to a lower incidence of MD (Figures 3 and S5–S8; Table S7).

Inflammatory cytokine fibroblast growth factor 5 increased the risk of WMH‐T2 (Figures 2 and S1–S4; Table S6). Serum metabolite 1‐palmitoleoyl‐2‐linolenoyl‐GPC was associated with an increased risk of WMH‐T2, whilst 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐GPI and X‐16935 were associated with a decreased risk for WMH‐T2. Higher 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐GPI (16:0/20:4) also significantly decreased the risk of WMH‐T2 (Figures 3 and S5–S8; Table S7).

Lacunes

Inflammatory cytokine fibroblast growth factor 19 (Figures 2 and S1–S4; Table S6) and metabolite 1‐(1‐enyl‐palmitoyl)‐2‐oleoyl‐GPC corresponded to a lower incidence of lacunes. On the other hand, metabolite 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐oleoyl‐GPE significantly increased the risk of lacunes (Figures 3 and S5–S8; Table S7).

Enlarged perivascular spaces

Inflammatory cytokine natural killer (NK) cell receptor 2B4 significantly decreased the risk of enlarged perivascular spaces (Figures 2 and S1–S4; Table S6). Three serum metabolites (ratios) were associated with an increased risk of enlarged perivascular spaces: uridine/pseudouridine, X‐21829 and 1‐stearoyl‐2‐linoleoyl‐GPE. Three serum metabolites were associated with a decreased risk of enlarged perivascular spaces: X‐11308, X‐23639 and levulinoylcarnitine (Figures 3 and S5–S8; Table S7).

Cerebral microbleeds

Cytokine cystatin D was associated with an increased risk of cerebral microbleeds (Figures 2 and S1–S4; Table S6). Metabolite 2R,3R‐dihydroxybutyrate significantly increased the risk of cerebral microbleeds (Figures 3 and S5–S8; Table S7).

Small vessel ischaemic stroke

MR analysis indicated that three inflammatory cytokines were associated with an increased risk of small vessel ischaemic stroke: C–C motif chemokine 25, TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand and TNF ligand superfamily member 12, whilst TNF ligand superfamily member 14 was associated with a lower risk (Figures 2 and S1–S4; Table S6). Four serum metabolites (ratios) were associated with a decreased risk of small vessel ischaemic stroke: 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐docosahexaenoyl‐GPC (16:0/22:6), N‐stearoyl‐sphingosine (d18:1 to 18:0)/N‐palmitoyl‐sphinganine (d18:0 to 16:0), 1‐methylxanthine levels and lithocholate sulfate 1. Five serum metabolites were associated with an increased risk of small vessel ischaemic stroke (GIGASTROKE): carnitine C5:1, gamma‐glutamyltryptophan, glycosyl‐N‐(2‐hydroxynervonoyl)‐sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)), dihomo‐linolenoylcarnitine (C20:3n3 or 6), 3‐hydroxyisobutyrate (Figures 3 and S5–S8; Table S7).

Reverse causal effects of inflammatory cytokines, serum metabolites and CSVD

Reverse causality of CSVD to the inflammatory cytokines or serum metabolites obtained from our two‐sample MR analyses (listed in Figures 2 and 3) was not found. Reverse causality in the rest of the cytokines and serum metabolites is indicated in Table S8.

Functional enrichment analysis

In the case of inflammatory cytokines, KEGG‐enriched pathways were primarily involving their interaction with each other (Figure 4a). On the other hand, in the case of serum metabolites, enriched pathways were those for oligodendrocyte development and lipid metabolism (suggesting roles in myelin repair) and, to a lesser extent, immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid and non‐lymphoid cells (suggesting roles in neuroinflammation) (Figure 4b).

Mediation analysis

Mediation MR analyses (Figure 5) revealed that an increase of an unidentified metabolite (X‐21829) decreased the NK cell receptor 2B4 levels (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.79–099), and this process promoted perivascular space enlargement (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–099). Removal of the mediator's effects revealed that this metabolite did not have a direct effect on perivascular space enlargement (mediation effect ratio 10.35%). Still, another metabolite associated with enlarged perivascular spaces was found to be similarly causally related to NK cell receptor 2B4. These results suggest that NK cell receptor 2B4 may be a protective factor of enlarged perivascular spaces (Figure S9; Table S5).

The causal relationship between serum metabolites (X‐21829, GWAS‐ID GCST90200585) and CSVD (enlarged perivascular spaces) is mediated by inflammatory cytokine NK cell receptor 2B4 (GWAS‐ID GCST90274771). Sensitivity analyses

Tested by the Egger regression intercept method, genetic pleiotropy did not introduce bias into the findings. PRESSO and Cochran's tests indicated the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity (p > 0.05, Table S9). The ‘leave‐one‐out’ analysis demonstrated the reliability of the MR analysis, validating this method. Scatter plots depicted the collective impact of serum metabolites and cytokines on CSVD (Figures S1, S2, S4, S5, S6, S8; Table S9).

DISCUSSION

This genetic susceptibility study identified causal relationships of inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites with CSVD‐associated imaging features (Figure 6). The majority of identified metabolites are involved with lipid metabolism, particularly those that contribute to the myelin formation. Previous investigations into the relationship between lipid metabolism and CSVD had focused on major metabolites such as total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglycerides. Still, there remained a lack of consensus amongst researchers on their interpretations and conclusions [25]. Studies of other metabolites were even more scarce. Interactions between inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites had been demonstrated in Alzheimer's disease but was inconclusive for CSVD with dementia [26, 27]. In turn, the number of serum metabolites analysed (both defined and incompletely defined) was expanded and their associations with inflammatory cytokines in CSVD were evaluated.

Potential roles of serum metabolites and inflammatory cytokines associated with CSVD. Both serum metabolites and inflammatory cytokines influence the progression of CSVD, whilst inflammatory cytokines could act as mediating factors of serum metabolites for CSVD.

The association between triglycerides and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol on WMHs and lacunar infarcts in previous studies have been inconsistent [28, 29, 30, 31]. Still lipid‐lowering drugs (e.g., statin) offered protection against cognitive decline and the recurrence of acute cerebrovascular events from an intervention standpoint, without increasing the risk of microbleeds [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. This benefit may be the result of not only the management of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglyceride levels but also that of other metabolites. In addition to ischaemia, WMH occurs in demyelinating diseases and degenerative gliosis, which differ in metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms. Due to the significant heterogeneity amongst patients with CSVD, it is crucial to incorporate varied aetiologies in CSVD studies [38]. Therefore, expanding the number of metabolites studied for their relationships with CSVD may reveal more insight on this disease.

The 12,13‐DiHOME/12(13)‐EpOME was associated with small vessel ischaemic stroke [39]. It was reported to impact cognitive function in patients with CSVD [40]. Similarly, our MR study found that it decreased MD and increased the risk of CSVD. In addition, choline plasmalogen (16:0/18:1) was reported to be associated with increased WMH in patients with CSVD, whilst the association between glycerophosphorylcholine and WMH remains to be clarified by further studies [41]. Trimethylamine N‐oxide, a metabolite of carnitine, had been shown to be associated with WMH volume and lacunar infarction [42]. However, this was obtained from a single‐centre, small sample size observational study, wherein many factors could not be controlled. Our MR study suggests that the carnitine metabolic pathway plays a role in the pathophysiology of acute small vessel ischaemic stroke. Overall, our findings align with previous research on lipid metabolism in CSVD and identify additional factors of CSVD susceptibility.

Mediation analysis revealed that unidentified metabolites increased the risk of enlarged perivascular spaces in CSVD through the NK cell receptors. Early studies suggested that NK cells were enriched in the cerebrovascular lumen of a hypertensive rat model that exhibits similar neuropathological features to CSVD, including spontaneous brain atrophy and white matter loss, as well as blood–brain barrier leakage [43]. In addition, WMH can be reversed in part by administering inhibitors of granzyme H expressed by NK cells [44]. Still, the specific mechanisms and roles of NK cell receptors in CSVD have yet to be elucidated.

A recent study found that proteins linked to leukocyte migration and inflammation were positively associated with enlarged perivascular spaces, whereas those involved in lipid metabolism were negatively associated with the disease [45]. Multiple proteins identified in our study have been previously linked to CSVD or neuroinflammation. Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E–binding protein 1 has been implicated in delayed neuronal death and ischaemia–reperfusion injury following cerebral ischaemia in animal models [46, 47], and its abnormal expression has also been observed in human vascular smooth muscle cells of patients with atherosclerosis [48, 49]. Higher fibroblast growth factor 23 levels in RSSI patients with renal dysfunction have been associated with new microangiopathic lesions during follow‐up whilst handgrip exercise and remote ischaemic preconditioning was shown to reduce the plasma fibroblast growth factor 21 levels in CSVD patients [50, 51]. TNF‐α is higher in lacunar stroke patients compared to non‐stroke patients [52, 53], whereas low serum TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand levels correlate with acute ischaemic stroke severity [54]. Qiao et al. found that TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand is a potential risk factor for CSVD [55]. Here, different inflammatory cytokines were examined than in their group (Table S1) and the relationship between metabolites and inflammatory factors that had a causal association with CSVD was evaluated. Overall, our findings support prior studies in demonstrating that the TNF superfamily plays a significant role in neuroinflammation and CSVD.

This study has several limitations. First, the UK Biobank and GWAS databases primarily provided summary data of population demographics; thus whilst attempts were made to control for potential variables as much as possible in order to ensure a homogeneous and comparable population for our analyses, the issue at hand could not be entirely resolved. Secondly, separate data on RSSIs and CMIs and on brain size (atrophy) were absent. Thirdly, there was a shortage of large‐sample, multi‐centre clinical studies or basic studies to validate our findings. Additionally, cerebrospinal fluid composition, which may better reflect molecular interactions in the central nervous system as the blood–brain barrier may have precluded CSVD biomarkers from entering the circulation, was not investigated. Last, it is recognized that, in using SNPs as the instrumental variable, inter‐ and multi‐genetic causes of CSVD in our MR analyses may not have been captured.

CONCLUSION

Several inflammatory cytokines and serum metabolites had causal relationships with imaging features of CSVD. NK cell receptor sub‐types mediated the promotional effect of an unidentified metabolite on enlarged perivascular spaces.
