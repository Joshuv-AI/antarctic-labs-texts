Culturally Adapted Cognitive Behavioural Therapy and First Nations Population: A Role for Nurses?
Authors: Robert Batterbee, Aunty Kerrie Doyle, Katherine Te Arii, Catherine Hungerford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis perspective paper examines the inconsistent integration of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) into the mental health care of First Nations people and considers the implications for mental health nursing practice. Although CBT is widely endorsed as an evidence‐based treatment for depression, anxiety, post‐traumatic stress disorder and chronic health conditions, its application in First Nations contexts remains uneven, with limited evidence of nurses delivering the intervention. Drawing on national and international literature, the paper explores whether this inconsistency reflects cultural misalignment, structural barriers or workforce limitations, and considers the role of nurses, who are well placed to address these challenges within communities. The discussion highlights tensions between the individualised, cognition‐focused assumptions underpinning CBT and Indigenous worldviews grounded in relational, community‐oriented and holistic conceptions of social and emotional wellbeing. Without meaningful cultural adaptation and partnership, standardised CBT risks reinforcing mistrust shaped by colonisation, systemic racism and historical trauma. Conversely, its relative absence may restrict access to evidence‐based care. Systemic factors are examined, including limited culturally responsive training and the under‐recognised role of nurses in delivering structured therapies. Evidence indicates nurse‐led CBT is feasible and effective, positioning nurses as a bridge between Western interventions and culturally safe practice. The paper concludes that meaningful progress requires more than surface adaptation and calls for First Nations nursing leadership in co‐designing culturally grounded CBT implementation.Keywords: adaptation, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), evidence‐based interventions, First Nations peoples, indigenous populations, nurses

Aims

Globalisation and increased cultural awareness have prompted the adaptation of previously standardised therapeutic practices, including highly structured approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). Internationally, CBT is endorsed as a primary treatment for depression, anxiety, post‐traumatic stress disorder and chronic health‐related conditions, supported by substantial empirical evidence. However, despite its widespread uptake, there remains limited evidence of effective and culturally grounded adaptation for First Nations populations, with integration into healthcare for Indigenous peoples remaining inconsistent.

This perspective paper advances a position on the inconsistent integration of CBT into the mental health care of First Nations people. Focusing on Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people as a case example, the paper analyses structural, cultural and epistemological factors that limit effective adaptation of CBT. It advances the position that nurses, as a culturally diverse frontline workforce operating across primary, secondary and tertiary settings, are uniquely positioned to bridge Western psychological interventions with culturally responsive practice. The paper proposes a pragmatic positioning of mental health nursing as a conduit for culturally adapted CBT that is clinically rigorous, relationally grounded and contextually responsive.

Background

There are numerous reports of cultural adaptation of CBT in the research literature. For example, Danhauer et al. (2024) ‘transcreated’ (p. 558) a manualised CBT intervention to address depression and anxiety among Hispanic cancer survivors. Outcomes included improved psychosocial care for this population group and increased reach of evidence‐based psychological care (Danhauer et al. 2024). Silva et al. (2024) describe the cultural adaptation of a computer‐based training for a CBT programme to teach monolingual Spanish‐speaking adults how to recognise triggers, avoid triggering situations and cope more effectively with the consequences of substance use. Positive outcomes included high levels of satisfaction with the content. Conversely, in a scoping review of cultural adaptations of CBT in Arab culture, Nasif et al. (2024) found no high‐quality comprehensive meta‐analyses or systematic reviews on culturally adapted CBT. They identified evidence that culturally adapted interventions are effective; however, there is little agreement on which elements work (Nasif et al. 2024). Similarly, in a systematic review of the literature on the effectiveness and cultural adaptation of evidence‐based interventions addressing the mental health of Afghan refugees resettled in Western countries since 2000, Hosseini et al. (2024) found no high‐quality studies. Moreover, most studies only incorporated peripheral changes to interventions in the service of cultural adaptation, and only one intervention included core changes.

Castro et al. (2012) outlined four conditions in which cultural adaptations to interventions are generally acceptable: ineffective clinical engagement; unique risk or resilience factors; unique symptoms of a common disorder and an intervention's efficacy for a particular sub‐cultural group that is not significant. Castro et al. (2004) recommend a planned and systematic approach to adaptation, to maximise both the fidelity of implementation and effectiveness. Richardson et al. (2022) note the use of surface‐structure cultural adaptations, such as replacing images or examples for greater cultural relevance; deep‐structure cultural adaptations, such as replacing curriculum for greater cultural relevance; and culturally grounded interventions, including those developed by the community in partnership with researchers. This approach has important implications for intervention design, evaluation and ethical practice, as it highlights that the depth of cultural integration, from superficial modification to genuine community co‐development, shapes not only relevance and engagement, but also power dynamics, epistemic legitimacy and the likelihood of sustained impact.

For individual clinicians seeking to adapt an intervention, Doyle and Hungerford (2014, 742–743) recommend a structured approach. This begins with conducting a needs assessment specific to the context in which the intervention will be used. Clinicians should then seek guidance from a leader in the area with cultural proficiency and experience in adapting evidence‐based interventions. They note the importance of identifying the intervention's core content, pedagogical approach and key elements responsible for its effectiveness, and carefully consider the potential impact of altering these components. Only those elements that can be modified without compromising outcomes should be adapted. The adapted intervention must then be implemented and the outcomes systematically evaluated. For some cultural groups, such as First Nations peoples, this would involve working with community members (McKinn et al. 2025; Richardson et al. 2022).

Design

This paper is presented as a scholarly perspective in accordance with contemporary guidance on discursive and positional scholarship in mental health nursing (Cleary et al. 2025). It advances a critical position regarding the integration of CBT within healthcare for Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Rather than reporting primary empirical findings, the paper synthesises conceptual, clinical and policy considerations to examine tensions in implementation and to articulate implications for mental health nursing practice.

Methods

The arguments presented in this paper are informed by engagement with contemporary peer‐reviewed literature addressing CBT, cultural adaptation, Indigenous health and nursing practice. Literature was examined to identify recurring conceptual, structural and epistemological themes relevant to CBT implementation for First Nations populations.

A critical interpretive approach was used to interrogate these themes and to develop a nursing‐focused position that integrates empirical evidence with practice‐based considerations. The purpose of this approach was not to provide exhaustive coverage of the field, but to support reasoned argumentation and conceptual clarification consistent with a perspective paper.

The paper was developed collaboratively by Indigenous and non‐Indigenous mental health nurses, academics and cognitive behavioural therapists. The authors bring experience across clinical practice, education and research in both mental health nursing and First Nations health contexts. In recognition that perspectives on cultural adaptation and therapeutic practice are shaped by lived, professional and cultural perspectives, reflexive dialogue informed the development of the arguments presented, thus strengthening the paper's commitment to culturally grounded, practice‐relevant analysis.

The Cultural Adaptation of CBT

CBT continues to occupy a dominant position in contemporary mental health care. Across the United Kingdom, North America and much of Europe, CBT is endorsed as a primary treatment for depression and related conditions, reinforced by clinical guidelines and a substantial body of empirical support (Barkham et al. 2021; Cuijpers et al. 2016; David et al. 2018). Globally, the structured and individualised approach to CBT has been adapted for diverse populations, including First Nations communities (Bennett‐Levy et al. 2015; Jameel et al. 2022; Naeem et al. 2015; Shea et al. 2012) with good effect (Sorenson and Harrell 2021). Unfortunately, the application of CBT without necessary adaptations has exacerbated significant mistrust and disengagement from mental health services by First Nations community members due to historical injustices and systemic racism (Rankin et al. 2022; Taylor et al. 2018). This is explained in more detail in the next section.

The integration of interventions like CBT requires careful navigation that respects Indigenous knowledge systems and self‐determination (Lee et al. 2022; Naeem et al. 2023). The necessity for culturally adapted interventions becomes especially salient when recognising that, without such adaptations, intervention efficacy declines, as communities may disengage with services perceived as incompatible with their worldview (Day et al. 2023; Naeem et al. 2021). Other studies (Armstrong et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2015) report that efforts to integrate CBT into First Nations healthcare without adequate cultural modifications risk reproducing the cycle of marginalisation experienced by these communities. Some headway has been made by psychologists in Australia, with O'Shea et al. (2024) reporting an initiative involving Australian Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander Peoples and psychologists working together to decolonise Western psychological approaches. The authors note that First Nations approaches share commonalities that can support the social and emotional wellbeing of all Australians (O'Shea et al. 2024). However, frameworks that fail to incorporate local cultural contexts can conflict with the holistic, community‐oriented conceptualisations of wellbeing and suffering shared by many First Nations people (McBride‐Henry et al. 2022), leading to poor health outcomes and reduced engagement with mental health services (Chowdhary et al. 2014; Peiris et al. 2012). The inconsistency in these integrations often reflects broader systemic challenges, which require comprehensive strategies prioritising community engagement, cultural safety and respect for First Nations healing traditions (Qinaʻau et al. 2025; Vukic et al. 2011).

Reasons for this inconsistency are debatable. One possible explanation is the limited utility of CBT in some First Nations contexts, with perceptions that it is grounded in Western individualism, emphasising personal cognition, rational restructuring and individual agency (Rathod et al. 2019). CBT is often viewed as being in deep tension between Western frameworks and Indigenous ways of knowing and healing (Razack et al. 2025). Also, First Nations populations experience health disparities rooted in historical trauma, colonisation and systemic inequities, which challenge the relevance of standardised interventions (Kisa and Kisa 2025). Inconsistencies in the adoption of CBT in First Nations populations are further reinforced by systemic barriers, including the geographical remoteness of some communities, inconsistencies in the training of health professionals, and professional hierarchies that create tensions over who is authorised to deliver what is considered psychological care (Durey et al. 2017; Jongen et al. 2018; Qinaʻau et al. 2025). These structural issues further reinforce the cultural and epistemological barriers to adapting evidence‐based interventions to culturally meaningful contexts (Kisa and Kisa 2025).

The Nursing Context

Internationally, there is growing evidence supporting the delivery of CBT by nurses. A brief search yields over 70 randomised controlled trials involving nurse‐led cognitive‐behavioural interventions across nursing specialities, all reporting positive outcomes. Specifically, a recent meta‐analysis of 46 trials involving perinatal mental health interventions that included CBT found that in high‐income countries (including Australia, the UK and the US), about two‐thirds of interventions were delivered by nurses and midwives, with associated reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms (Singla et al. 2021). Studies of non‐specialist‐delivered CBT in low‐ and middle‐income countries similarly report acceptable, feasible and appropriate implementation across common mental disorders and substance‐use problems (Verhey et al. 2020). Recent evidence also supports nurse‐led CBT in a variety of non‐mental health contexts, such as primary‐care nurses delivering telehealth CBT interventions to significantly reduce pain‐related disability and improve outcomes in chronic back pain, and palliative care nurses using cognitive behavioural techniques to support advanced cancer patients (Cort et al. 2009). Such evidence reinforces an important point: nurses can successfully deliver CBT with measurable benefit.

Importantly, the role of nurses as providers of cognitive and behavioural therapies has a substantial, though often overlooked, history. In the United Kingdom, the development of nurse therapists can be traced to the late 1960s and early 1970s, when psychiatrists such as Isaac Marks established training programmes to equip mental health nurses to deliver behavioural psychotherapy in response to workforce shortages. Early evaluations demonstrated that nurse therapists achieved outcomes comparable to psychologists and psychiatrists in the treatment of neurotic disorders (Marks et al. 1975). This tradition was subsequently consolidated and extensively documented by Gournay (2000), whose work traced nearly three decades of nurse‐delivered behavioural therapy, later evolving to incorporate cognitive approaches as CBT developed. Follow‐up studies confirmed that these practitioners formed one of the most rigorously evaluated groups within mental health services, with evidence supporting their clinical effectiveness and cost‐efficiency (Gournay et al. 2000).

This historical trajectory directly informs contemporary service models such as the United Kingdom's Improving Access to Psychological Therapies (IAPT) programme, established following the Layard Report (Layard 2006), which called for large‐scale expansion of access to evidence‐based psychological therapies, particularly CBT. Since its implementation in 2008, IAPT (now NHS Talking Therapies) has become the largest publicly funded programme of its kind globally, delivering over 1 million courses of therapy annually (NHS England 2025). Within this stepped‐care model, low‐intensity CBT interventions are typically delivered by Psychological Wellbeing Practitioners, whereas high‐intensity CBT is delivered by accredited therapists drawn from a range of professional backgrounds, including mental health nursing (NHS England 2024). Mental health nurses represent a major component of this high‐intensity workforce and have played a central role in the large‐scale implementation of CBT within routine care settings. Indeed, taken together with their historical role in nurse therapy programmes and their substantial representation within NHS services, mental health nurses can reasonably be considered one of—if not the dominant—professional groups involved in the delivery of high‐intensity CBT in publicly funded systems (Gournay et al. 2000; NHS England 2024).

Indeed, nurses are well placed to do this work because their practice is grounded in relational engagement, holistic care and sustained contact across the continuum of health services (Ambushe et al. 2023; Karam et al. 2021). Unlike discipline‐specific, episodic models of therapy, nursing practice routinely integrates physical, social, emotional and cultural dimensions of wellbeing, aligning closely with First Nations' conceptions of health as interconnected and community‐embedded (McBride‐Henry et al. 2022). Moreover, nurses often work in primary care, community, rural and remote settings where trust, continuity and accessibility are central to care, and where culturally responsive adaptation is essential rather than optional (Robinson et al. 2024). Their scope of practice supports psychoeducation, collaborative goal setting and strengths‐based intervention (Uzun et al. 2025), while their professional emphasis on cultural safety, advocacy and reflexive practice positions them to adapt CBT approaches that privilege narrative, connection, family and community context (Batterbee et al. 2025). When appropriately trained and supported, nurses can therefore bridge evidence‐based psychological strategies with culturally grounded practice, enhancing both accessibility and acceptability of care for First Nations peoples.

Specifically, nursing provides an important pathway for First Nations peoples to access health care. In Canada, nearly 10 000 First Nations peoples, including Métis and Inuit, are registered nurses, making up around 3.0% of the national nursing workforce (University of Saskatchewan and Canadian Indigenous Nurses Association 2018). Despite underrepresentation relative to the population, this demonstrates the viability of nursing as a career for First Nations peoples. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Māori nurses currently comprise about 7.4% of the nursing workforce, and although Māori make up roughly 18% of the population, there are active strategies to significantly expand Māori participation in nursing to support culturally safe care (New Zealand Nurses Organisation 2024). In Australia, between 2013 and 2021, the number of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander nurses and midwives more than doubled, from 2434 to 5135, reflecting a growing trend to enter the profession (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2021). Given that nurses work across urban, regional, rural and remote areas, increasing the First Nations workforce has considerable potential to improve culturally safe, accessible care in communities.

The presence of First Nations nurses with a deep understanding of the cultural contexts in which they operate can lead to better health outcomes for First Nations peoples (Pipi et al. 2021). Tremblay et al. (2023) emphasise that culturally safe practices are essential for addressing disparities in healthcare delivery and that interventions involving First Nations health workers and community Elders can improve trust between health services and community members. In the context of high rates of chronic illness and mental health issues within First Nations populations, the need for an increase in the First Nations nursing workforce becomes even more salient. Taylor et al. (2025) point out that First Nations health professionals are in a unique position to offer culturally appropriate care, which is essential for addressing the impacts of colonisation and systemic inequities that have historically marginalised First Nations communities. Thus, strengthening and utilising the First Nations nursing workforce as a conduit for cultural adaptation could also play a critical role in the broader effort to decolonise health systems and empower First Nations peoples.

Australia: A Case Example

In Australia, despite the prominence of CBT as a widely accepted standard treatment for mental health issues, its integration into routine care is notably inconsistent, particularly in remote and marginalised communities where First Nations and non‐Indigenous healthcare professionals work together (Bennett‐Levy et al. 2014; Toombs et al. 2020). Moreover, in rural and remote areas, where Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples are disproportionately represented, there are severe shortages of trained psychological therapy providers (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2020). Telehealth has expanded access but remains limited by infrastructure gaps, digital literacy and concerns about cultural appropriateness (Fraser et al. 2017). Mental health services in these settings frequently prioritise crisis intervention and pharmacological treatment, with psychological therapies underutilised due to system and workforce constraints (Fennell et al. 2018). This reflects a broader systemic prioritisation of acute rather than preventive care, despite long‐standing evidence that early intervention CBT can reduce demand on secondary care services (Hollon et al. 2005).

Cultural Mismatches Between CBT and First Nations Worldviews

Another major barrier to the integration of CBT into healthcare provided to Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders lies in the cultural assumptions embedded within Western therapeutic models. The high suicide rates among Australian First Nations peoples are shaped by sociocultural, historical and relational factors (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2022; Dickson et al. 2019; Pollock et al. 2018). Research suggests that suicidal behaviour may function less as a reflection of personal desire to die and more as a communicative act, arising from intergenerational trauma, embedded in local social contexts (Tait et al. 2018). This highlights a fundamental misalignment with CBT, which relies on Western concepts of cognition, emotion and individual agency. Standard suicide prevention models, centred on cognitive behavioural mechanisms, therefore risk overlooking the relational and communal dimensions of the suicidal behaviours in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. As Larney et al. (2012) note, suicidal ideation in Australian Aboriginal populations correlates strongly with social marginalisation, trauma and distress, far more than with mental health and illness.

Emerging research suggests potential avenues for bridging these gaps. In a systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomised clinical trials involving suicide attempts and CBT with and without narrative assessment, Janssen et al. (2025) found that incorporating a structured narrative assessment led to reductions in suicide attempts, whereas CBT without such components did not. While these findings may seem consistent with First Nations narrative traditions, the narrative used in such trials reflects Western clinical formulations rather than relational storytelling embedded in community life. Nevertheless, this evidence reinforces the idea that a hybrid narrative‐enhanced CBT may hold promise for culturally responsive adaptation.

Discrimination and systemic racism compound the challenges for First Nations peoples, directly contributing to depressive symptoms and suicidal thoughts (Haregu et al. 2021). The psychological consequences of colonialism and systemic disenfranchisement also remain inadequately addressed in Western models (Parker 2010). Although narrative therapy has strong cultural resonance, privileging it by default may inadvertently limit exploration of culturally grounded CBT adaptations. As Bennett‐Levy et al. (2014, 2015) argue, CBT remains adaptable, but rigid application risks reinforcing perceptions of cultural irrelevance. Nelson et al. (2014) similarly caution that excluding CBT from culturally adapted care reduces access to evidence‐based interventions and may inadvertently exacerbate existing disparities.

Systemic Disparities and Sociocultural Barriers

The prevalence of mental illness among Aboriginal Australians remains alarmingly higher than in the broader Australian population (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2022). Reasons include systemic issues such as endemic violence, systemic racism, a lack of access to healthcare, the intergenerational impacts of marginalisation and systemic racism as contributory factors to such high rates. Dickson et al. (2019) highlight how systemic challenges such as discrimination, poverty and health service inequalities contribute to heightened suicide and self‐harm rates. Milroy et al. (2022) situate Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander mental health inequities within the ongoing impacts of colonisation and structural disadvantage, emphasising the importance of Indigenous‐led co‐design and cultural safety in service development. Others point towards poor access to healthcare, low life expectancies and social dislocation as contributory factors (Tait et al. 2018). In this context, while it is essential to recognise that increasing collaboration with First Nations communities and adapting healthcare models are not a standalone solution, it is equally important to acknowledge that the mistrust created by these issues presents a significant barrier to collaboration on both sides of the debate.

Educational Limitations and Standards of Practice

Australian clinical psychology training programmes increasingly embed CBT competencies within broader integrative frameworks, reflecting a pragmatic orientation towards client complexity and the use of evidence‐based techniques across multiple therapeutic models (Kazantzis et al. 2018). This orientation is further shaped by personal preferences and service cultures, resulting in routine practice in which CBT is frequently delivered in ways that diverge from empirically supported protocols (Shafran et al. 2019). Evidence from implementation and training research indicates that CBT outcomes are influenced by therapist training and treatment fidelity, with variability in real‐world delivery linked to service and workforce factors (Hogue et al. 2018; Westbrook et al. 2011). In the Australian context, system‐level evaluations of stepped‐care programmes highlight variability in service delivery, but there is limited formalised national infrastructure for auditing psychotherapy fidelity or standardising post‐qualification CBT training pathways (Pirkis et al. 2011). Unlike countries where CBT is delivered across multiple professional disciplines within structured post‐qualification training frameworks, Australian pathways for advanced CBT training remain largely concentrated within psychology professions (Meadows et al. 2020). Workforce studies indicate that non‐psychologist mental health professionals often report lower confidence and variable competence in delivering CBT interventions (Harris et al. 2016). Within this context, the role of nurses in delivering structured psychological therapies remains under‐recognised and inconsistently supported across service settings (Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation 2022; Kavanagh et al. 2023). In Australia's rural and remote areas, where access to specialist mental health services is limited, nurses and primary care providers often represent the first (and sometimes only) point of contact for people experiencing mental health issues (Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists 2025; Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025). The lack of accessible CBT training pathways for these nurses represents a significant, largely unaddressed gap in Australia's mental health workforce strategy.

Discussion

Various therapeutic approaches have been developed or adapted to support the mental health and well‐being of First Nations peoples. Those with cross‐cultural applications include narrative therapy, acceptance‐based therapies and multisystem therapies, with CBT the most commonly reported (Ponturo and Kilcullen 2021). The use of adapted forms of CBT to support First Nations people requires closer examination, including the delivery of this intervention by nurses.

This paper has explored the inconsistent integration of CBT into mental health care for First Nations people, using nurses supporting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in Australia as a case study. The discussion highlighted that the issue is not whether CBT ‘works’ in general terms as it is one of the most empirically supported psychological interventions globally. Rather, it is important to consider how and under what conditions nurses can meaningfully and ethically implement a culturally adapted form of the therapeutic intervention within First Nations contexts.

One of the strongest arguments in favour of CBT is its extensive empirical base. Across depression, anxiety, trauma‐related conditions and chronic illness, CBT demonstrates consistent effect sizes and cost‐effectiveness. In a context where First Nations peoples experience disproportionately high rates of psychological distress and suicide, withholding or marginalising such an intervention may inadvertently perpetuate inequity. Excluding CBT from First Nations health services risks reinforcing a two‐tiered system in which evidence‐based therapies are more accessible to non‐Indigenous populations.

CBT is also structured and teachable. This makes it suitable for dissemination beyond specialist psychologists to nurses and other providers working in primary care and isolated communities. In line with the scope of practice of nurses, nurse‐led adapted CBT can produce meaningful improvements in mental health and chronic disease outcomes. Nurses, therefore, represent a practical and scalable pathway for increasing access to structured psychological interventions.

Importantly, CBT is adaptable. International literature supports both surface and deep‐structure adaptations, and emerging models demonstrate that CBT can incorporate narrative elements, culturally relevant metaphors, community involvement and collective formulations of distress. When adaptation is systematic, collaborative and fidelity‐informed, CBT need not be culturally rigid. Instead, it can become a flexible framework that integrates local meaning systems.

Finally, integrating CBT within First Nations health services may contribute to broader system reform. If implemented through co‐design and First Nations leadership, culturally responsive CBT could sit alongside narrative and culturally grounded healing approaches, broadening rather than narrowing therapeutic choice.

Despite these strengths, however, significant concerns remain. CBT is grounded in Western epistemology, privileging individual cognition, rational analysis and personal agency. First Nations conceptualisations of social and emotional wellbeing are relational, collective and inseparable from Country, kinship, history and spirituality. Without careful adaptation, CBT risks reducing complex sociohistorical distress to intrapersonal cognitive distortions.

There is also the risk of epistemological dominance. The historical imposition of Western health models has contributed to mistrust and disengagement. Introducing CBT without genuine partnership may reproduce colonial dynamics, positioning Western science as superior to Indigenous knowledge systems. Cultural adaptation that is limited to imagery, language changes or token narrative components may inadvertently reinforce perceptions of cultural insensitivity.

Structural barriers further complicate implementation. Rural and remote communities face workforce shortages, limited training pathways, high staff turnover and competing clinical demands. Even if CBT is theoretically appropriate, inadequate training and poor treatment fidelity can undermine effectiveness. Upskilling nurses provides an important way forward.

There is also a danger in framing adaptation as a technical exercise rather than a relational process. Cultural safety is not achieved solely through protocol adaptation or modification. It requires truth‐telling, acknowledgement of colonisation and sustained community governance in decision‐making about care models. Without these elements, adaptation risks becoming procedural rather than transformative.

A Balanced Position

The debate, therefore, should not centre on whether CBT should replace culturally grounded approaches, nor whether it should be excluded from First Nations contexts. The more productive question is how empirical interventions and Indigenous knowledge systems can be brought into respectful dialogue. A binary framing, such as CBT versus narrative or culturally specific healing approaches, oversimplifies the complexity of mental health care. Narrative traditions resonate strongly within Aboriginal communities, yet emerging evidence suggests that structured narrative‐enhanced CBT may offer one pathway towards integration. Hybrid models, co‐designed with Elders, First Nations health workers and communities, may allow CBT principles to be reframed within culturally meaningful contexts.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

The nursing workforce occupies a pivotal position in this dialogue. Nurses often represent the first point of contact in rural and remote communities. Expanding culturally responsive CBT training for nurses, particularly First Nations nurses, could improve accessibility while embedding interventions within trusted relationships. However, such expansion must be accompanied by governance reform, investment in training infrastructure and First Nations leadership in local modification, curriculum design and implementation.

Ultimately, the integration of CBT into the mental health care of First Nations peoples is not simply a technical matter of modifying therapeutic components; it is fundamentally a structural and relational challenge. Meaningful progress depends on First Nations leadership in defining what evidence‐based care should look like in practice, alongside systematic and fidelity‐informed adaptation processes rather than ad hoc modification. Expanding accessible CBT training pathways for nurses and primary care providers is also critical, particularly in rural and remote contexts where they are often the first point of contact. At the same time, evaluation frameworks must give equal weight to clinical outcomes and cultural safety.

Broader policy reform addressing workforce development, funding inequities, service delivery structures and governance will be essential. This includes investment in training infrastructure and the meaningful leadership of First Nations peoples in local modification, curriculum design and implementation. CBT should neither be imposed as a dominant model nor excluded on the basis of presumed cultural incompatibility. Its role in First Nations mental health care depends on whether it can be embedded within decolonising frameworks that uphold self‐determination, relational worldviews and community authority. Under these conditions, CBT may be re‐positioned from a potentially culturally discordant approach to one component of a broader, ethically grounded and culturally responsive system of care.

Conclusion

CBT is one of the most empirically supported psychological interventions, yet its impact is constrained by structural, professional and cultural barriers. This is particularly evident in Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where psychological distress and suicide rates remain disproportionately high. Although narrative and storytelling approaches hold strong cultural resonance, excluding CBT risks limiting access to evidence‐based care. The task is not to privilege one paradigm over another, but to bring cultural and clinical frameworks into constructive dialogue. Achieving this requires systemic reform, workforce development, research investment and sustained leadership from First Nations communities in shaping culturally grounded mental health care. Nurses can play a key role in supporting this action.
