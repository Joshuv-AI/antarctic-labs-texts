INTRODUCTION

Acute liver failure (ALF) is a syndrome that begins with liver dysfunction, jaundice, coagulopathy (INR >1.5) uncorrectable with vitamin K. It can get complicated by hepatic encephalopathy in less than 8 weeks. 1 , 2 This disorder occurs in patients who have no evidence of previous liver disease. 2 Early symptoms of hepatic encephalopathy are difficult to detect in children, so the presence of encephalopathy symptoms is not conclusive for the diagnosis of ALF. 3 , 4 According to the age, the etiology of ALF in children can vary and include metabolic disorders, autoimmune, infectious diseases, and toxins. 1 In infants, the most important causes are inborn errors of metabolism (IEM) and congenital infections. 2 , 3 In different studies, the frequency of IEM as a cause of ALF has varied between 10% and 43%. 3 Therefore, IEM should always be considered in the differential diagnosis of childhood ALF. 5 , 6 Unfortunately, in half of the children, the cause is not diagnosed. 1 Many undiagnosed cases of ALF may be due to unknown metabolic disorders or atypical manifestations of known disorders. 7 Relapsing acute liver failure (RALF) in infants is described as repeated attacks of severe liver dysfunction with remission. Various hereditary causes can lead to RALF, including: mitochondrial disorders, Wolcott‐Rallison syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis, Bi‐allelic pathogenic variations in dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase, Leucyl‐tRNA synthetase, and Bi‐allelic variants in neuroblastoma‐amplified sequence. 8 Although various genes have recently been implicated in RALF during infancy, the cause of some cases is still unknown. 9 Infantile liver failure syndromes begin in infancy with acute liver failure caused by a febrile illness. It can present as recurrent attacks of ALF and is one of the unknown disorders of RALF. 8 , 10 , 11 We present a 2.5‐year‐old boy with clinical manifestation of acute liver failure. In past, he had two similar attacks.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 2.5‐year‐old boy was referred to the emergency department of Bahrami Children's Hospital due to high fever, frequent vomiting, and decreased level of consciousness. Fever and vomiting had started the day before. The decrease in his level of consciousness happened 2 h before the consultation, which was associated with the child's lack of attention to the surroundings and impaired vision. He was the first child of consanguineous parents who was born full‐term with a birth weight of 3000 g by normal vaginal delivery. There have been no findings of developmental delay or disability since birth. This child was the only child in the family and had no other siblings. There is no history of a similar case in the family. He had a history of two episodes of fever, vomiting, and decreased level of consciousness, which occurred at the ages of 10 and 21 months. According to the description of the parents, in the first episode, the patient was treated with phenobarbital for the diagnosis of febrile seizure, and in the second episode, the diagnosis of meningitis was treated with antibiotics. On general appearance, he was restless and mildly dehydrated. The patient was drowsy, but he opened his eyes with pain stimulation, he did not respond verbally, and the limbs were flexed with pain stimulation (Modified GCS: 6). In physical examination, the patient was febrile (T: 38.8°C) and his pulse rate was 125 bpm. Other vital signs were within the normal range. Heart and lung examinations were normal. There was slight hepatomegaly with a liver span of about 7 cm and no splenomegaly. The neurological examinations were normal, including normal head shape and circumference, closed fontanelles, normal size pupils with normal response to light, normal neurodevelopment compared to age, normal tendon reflexes, and muscle tone. The patient's weight was 12 kg which was normal for his age (percentile: 50).

At first, the patient's blood sugar was low (BS: 16 mg/dL) and after receiving 10% glucose, the blood glucose level was corrected, but there was no change in the patient's level of consciousness. In the results of the initial tests, the serum ammonia level was very high (650 mcg/dL), so treatment with sodium benzoate (250 mg/kg bolus IV, then 500 mg/kg/day IV, Veriton Amozate, Veriton Pharma, Australia) was started. In the evaluation of the laboratory tests, in addition to elevated liver transaminase, a decrease in serum albumin and coagulopathy was also observed (ALT: 429 U/L, AST: 65 U/L, Albumin: 2.8 g/dL, PT:20.7, INR: 1.65). Besides, mild metabolic acidosis with a normal anion gap was evident, which resolved with hydration. Urine toxicology was negative. Analysis of CSF was normal (glucose 79 mg/dL, protein 18 mg/dL, WBC 0/mm3, RBC 5/mm3). Cultures of CSF, blood, and urine were negative as well. The brain CT scan reported bilateral symmetric low focal attenuation in thalami and suspicious foci attenuation in posterior parietal and occipital white matter (Figure 1). Based on these findings, acute necrotizing encephalopathy of childhood (ANEC) could have been suspected. Therefore, intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (20 mg/kg/dose for 5 days) was started. Electroencephalography (EEG) did not depict epileptic disorders.

Initial differential diagnoses based on history, clinical examination, and imaging findings included genetic metabolic disorders, ANEC, and Reye's syndrome. The child was hospitalized for 20 days. During the first few days of hospitalization, his level of consciousness was fluctuated (Modified GCS: 5–7) but his gradually general condition improved and his level of consciousness became normal. Also, the abnormalities in the blood tests gradually normalized (Table 1). He was discharged with levetiracetam, L‐carnitine, biotin, vitamin B12, multivitamin, ferrous sulfate, folic acid, zinc sulfate, and cholecalciferol.

To definitively diagnose the disease, whole exome sequencing (genetic test) was performed. The report showed variants with possible relevance to the patient's phenotype including NBAS (NM_015909.3, sequencing; homozygous variant c.1725+1G>T). The genetic diagnosis of infantile liver failure syndrome type 2 was then confirmed.

After discharge, the patient was visited at the endocrine and metabolic clinic periodically. At the age of 3 years, the patient was readmitted with similar symptoms, which were less severe and quickly recovered. Following the health and protective recommendations, no serious problems happened to the patient. The last visit of the patient was done at the age of 8 years, in which the growth and development of the patient was in a normal state.

DISCUSSION

We report a case of acute liver failure in a patient with infantile liver failure syndrome type 2 (ILFS2). The importance of reporting this case is because this syndrome should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any child with symptoms of recurrent hepatic encephalopathy as it can occur as repeated attacks of encephalopathy following febrile infections. Between attacks, the patient has completely normal liver function. The onset of symptoms is vomiting, loss of consciousness, and liver dysfunction. Treatment, like other cases of ALF, is maintenance and supportive care, with gradual improvement. For the definitive diagnosis, genetic evaluation and mutations are needed. There are several important aspects to this case. First, this condition is a rare genetic disorder. Second, this patient was hospitalized with different diagnoses and underwent various tests and treatments. Therefore, in patients who have this sign, considering this disease and examining genetics can be effective in determining the patient's condition and not using unnecessary or even harmful treatments. Third, compared to similar cases, this patient has had a long‐term follow‐up and shows the importance of being aware of the disease and observing protective and health measures in preventing the severity future crises. In general, infantile liver failure syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder and has 3 types. Its type 2, infantile liver failure syndrome type 2 (ILFS2), occurs due to mutations in the NBAS gene (MIM: 616483) (NM_015909) on Chromosome 24p2. This syndrome was first identified by Haack et al. 10 The initial manifestations often appear in the first 2 years of life or in early childhood. A life‐threatening disorder characterized by frequent attacks of acute liver failure during febrile illness. Full recovery is achieved by supportive treatment. 10 Between attacks, liver function is completely normal. Attacks are associated with vomiting, lethargy, increased liver enzymes, jaundice, and coagulation disorders. Some patients develop secondary hyperammonemia, hypoglycemia or encephalopathy. Symptoms of cardiomyopathy (heart muscle disease) and seizures may occur in 1–4% of cases. 10 Further investigations showed that mutations of the NBAS gene have a wide range of phenotypes and include brain involvement, seizures, connective tissue other than bone, immune system, cardiomyopathy, autoimmune disorders of the digestive tract and liver, interstitial lung disease, and neuroblastoma. 10 , 12

Type 1 infantile liver failure syndrome (ILFS1) is caused by mutations in the LARS gene (MIM: 615438). It is characterized by precocious growth failure, recurrent liver dysfunction, hypoalbuminemia, increased lactate, microcytic anemia, seizures, and abnormal brain MRI similar to mitochondrial disorders. Developmental delay occurs in 90% and encephalopathy attacks following febrile illnesses in 80% of cases. 11 Type 3 infantile liver failure syndrome (ILFS3) occurs due to mutation in the RINT1 gene (MIM: 610089) on chromosome 22q7. It is characterized by recurrent attacks of acute liver failure during a febrile illness. These children have short stature and skeletal abnormalities in the vertebrae and femur head. All patients have a normal developmental and usually have a complete remission with supportive treatment. 8

Mitochondrial hepatopathies are on the list of differential diagnoses of this disease, which can occur following exposure to drugs, toxins, hepatotoxic metals, and endogenous metabolites. The most common of them is Reye's syndrome. 13 Disorders of fatty acid oxidation and fatty acid transfer are also other differential diagnoses. 13 The clinical manifestations of Rey's and Rey's like syndrome are quite similar to ILFS2. This syndrome is also associated with non‐inflammatory encephalopathy and an unexplained increase of serum transaminases more than 3 times, usually following a viral infection. The simultaneous use of some drugs such as acetylsalicylic acid, phenothiazines and antiemetics and the absence of jaundice help to diagnose it. 13 , 14 There is an increase in blood ammonia level, as well as in ILFS2. 13 , 14 For final diagnosis of these syndromes, known causes of liver failure and mitochondrial disease must be excluded using biochemical, metabolic, and genetic tests. Especially in children with recurrent acute liver failure, hereditary metabolic diseases should be considered and whole‐exome sequencing (WES) should be used to confirm the diagnosis. 8

CONCLUSION

An important conclusion from this study is that infantile liver failure syndrome‐2 should be included in the differential diagnosis of children with frequent and acute liver failure accompanied by hyperammonemia.
