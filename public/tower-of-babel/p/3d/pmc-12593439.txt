Human Albumin infusion in liver Cirrhosis and overt Hepatic Encephalopathy (HACHE): protocol of an investigator-initiated, open-label, multicentre, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Qianqian Li, Huan Tong, Xuemei Liu, Fei Liao, Han Ma, Zhihang Zhou, Fei Liu, Haitao Shi, Zhongfeng Wang, Yang Sun, Qunyan Yao, Yan Feng, Ximei Li, Jing Feng, Zhoutao He, Ya Peng, Zhaohui Bai, Rong Zhu, Du Cheng, Chengfu Xu, Song He, Huihong Yu, Chen Xie, Jia Yuan, Xinrui Wang, Hongju Yang, Yanting Zou, Shuyu Li, Ting Li, Shengjuan Hu, Chaochao Chen, Zhiyuan Chen, Marten A Lantinga, Mauro Bernardi, Xingshun Qi, Chengwei Tang, On the behalf of Young Member Group of Chinese Gastroenterology Society

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHepatic encephalopathy (HE), a severe complication of decompensated cirrhosis, is characterised as neurocognitive dysfunction. Emerging evidence suggests the potential role of human albumin infusion for the treatment of HE, but its optimal dosage remains undefined. Therefore, we planned a randomised controlled trial (RCT) to compare the efficacy of human albumin infusion at different dosages in patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE.Methods and analysisThis RCT will be conducted in the Departments of Gastroenterology or Hepatology of 16 tertiary hospitals in China. Overall, 174 patients with a diagnosis of liver cirrhosis, overt HE and a serum albumin level of 23–30 g/L will be enrolled. They will be stratified according to the severity of overt HE and randomly assigned at a ratio of 1:1 into the groups of human albumin infusion at a modified dosage and a routine dosage. The primary end point is the improvement of overt HE within 3–5 days after treatment. The secondary end points include recurrence of overt HE, survival and adverse events. We expect that this RCT will provide high-quality evidence on the optimal dosage of human albumin infused, which can achieve more clinical benefits to patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command (ethical approval number Y2024-148). The study findings will be published in academic journals.Trial registration numberNCT06483737.Keywords: hepatology, survival, randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a neuropsychiatric complication secondary to advanced liver diseases and/or portosystemic shunts.1 When patients exhibit abnormalities in personality, behaviour and mental state, and even coma, they are diagnosed as overt HE.2 The prevalence of overt HE ranges from 30% to 40% in patients with liver cirrhosis, and 1-year and 3-year survival rates are <50% and 25%, respectively.3 4 HE indicates poor outcome of liver cirrhosis and brings heavy financial burden to the patients and medical system.5 6

The pathogenesis of HE has not been sufficiently elucidated. Ammonia toxicity is considered the culprit of HE, where ammonia cannot be effectively detoxified due to poor liver function, or it directly enters into systemic circulation due to portosystemic shunt. It crosses the blood-brain barrier and then causes cerebral oedema by promoting astrocytes to synthesise glutamine.7 Besides, ammonia can affect γ-aminobutyric acid neurotransmitter system, increasing inhibitory neurotransmission.8 In recent years, it has been increasingly recognised that inflammation and oxidative stress may play an important role in the development and progression of HE.9 10 Circulating inflammatory cytokines are significantly elevated in patients with acute and chronic liver diseases due to systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation,11 and they can disrupt the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, promote the uptake of ammonia and have a synergistic effect with ammonia in causing brain oedema.12,14 Meanwhile, oxidative stress is closely related to astrocyte swelling, thereby developing an ‘auto-amplifying loop’.15

Albumin, an important plasma protein synthesised in the liver, has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions. It can primarily decrease endotoxin activity, inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines, scavenge reactive oxygen species and bind metals.16,18 In advanced cirrhosis, decreased albumin synthesis by the liver and post-transcriptional molecular abnormalities induced by the systemic inflammation and oxidative stress lead to a substantial reduction in effective albumin concentration,19 which likely favour the occurrence or aggravation of HE. Our previous study showed that serum albumin level was an independent risk factor of overt HE during hospitalisation, and the best cut-off value of serum albumin level was 31.6 g/L for predicting the development of overt HE in patients with liver cirrhosis.20 Besides, a lower serum albumin level might also be closely related to a higher risk of in-hospital mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE, and the best cut-off value was 22.8 g/L.

Theoretically, based on its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, human albumin infusion should be used to treat overt HE in patients with liver cirrhosis, because there are significant decreases in pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress indexes after treatment.21 Its clinical benefits have been demonstrated in some previous studies22 23 and meta-analyses.24 25 However, the cost of human albumin greatly compromises its comprehensive use in clinical practice, partly because of its limited approval by the medical insurance policies in many regions, particularly in underdeveloped countries and low- and middle-income countries.26 27 Nowadays, hypoalbuminaemia with a serum albumin level of <30 g/L is a threshold for obtaining medicare reimbursement of human albumin in mainland China.27 Thus, in the mainland China, the clinicians often prescribe human albumin at a relatively small dosage of 10–20 g/day, an amount significantly lower than that employed in several recent large-scale trials.28 29 Notably, recent evidence suggests that high-dosage human albumin is more beneficial for improving inflammation and cirrhosis-related complications than low-dosage human albumin.30,33 In this setting, we decided to explore whether the outcome of patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE can be further improved by modifying the dosage of human albumin.

Hypothesis and objectives

Hypothesis

Different dosages of human albumin may have an impact on its clinical benefits in patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE.

Objectives

The primary objective is to identify the optimal dosage of human albumin for the improvement of overt HE in patients with liver cirrhosis. The secondary objective is to observe the impact of different dosages of human albumin infused on the clinical outcomes during the 3-month follow-up period.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This multicentre trial will be performed in the Departments of Gastroenterology or Hepatology of tertiary hospitals in China. Hospitalised patients with a definite diagnosis of liver cirrhosis, overt HE and a reduced serum albumin level (23–30 g/L) will be screened. After obtaining the patients’ informed consents (online supplemental material 1), eligible participants will be assigned at a ratio of 1:1 into two groups receiving human albumin infusion either at a ‘routine’ dosage (10–20 g/day) or a ‘modified’ dosage (30–60 g/day). Clinical and laboratory data at admission and during hospitalisation and follow-up periods will be collected. Inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress indices before and after human albumin infusion will be measured (figure 1). This study follows the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the medical ethical committee of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command (ethical approval number Y2024-148). It has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06483737).

Eligibility criteria

Participants will be included if they meet the following criteria: (1) a definite diagnosis of liver cirrhosis and overt HE, regardless of a history of HE; (2) a serum albumin level of 23–30 g/L; (3) age ≥18 years and (4) signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) contraindications to human albumin infusion; (2) a history of transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS); (3) a diagnosis of acute liver failure; (4) severe heart and/or lung diseases; (5) psychiatric or nervous diseases and (6) pregnant or lactating.

Randomisation and blinding

Eligible participants will be stratified according to the severity of overt HE (see below) at admission, and randomly assigned at a ratio of 1:1 to the groups of human albumin infusion at a modified dosage and a routine dosage. A random code table is generated by a computer system. The trial group assignments are open-label to the participants and clinicians, but are blind to the investigators who are responsible for data collection and follow-up visits.

Treatment

All eligible participants will receive standard treatment of overt HE according to the current practice guideline,2 including: (1) 15–30 mL of lactulose orally, 2–3 times daily to achieve a goal of two to three soft stools daily; (2) 800–1200 mg of rifaximin orally daily in a split-dose regimen. Besides, other drugs that have been recommended for the treatment of HE in mainland China will also be considered, including: (1) 10–40 g of ornithine aspartate intravenously or 3–9 g of ornithine aspartate orally daily; (2) 5 g of arginine hydrochloride intravenously daily and/or (3) 250 mL of branched-chain amino acids intravenously daily. Selection of one or more drugs mentioned above is based on the availability of drugs, patients’ disease conditions and physicians’ considerations among participating centres.

Participants assigned to the modified dosage group will receive intravenous infusion of human albumin at different dosages according to the serum albumin level (table 1). The infusion rate of human albumin is 100 mL/hour, and 100 mL of normal saline solutions are used to flush the tube before and after human albumin infusion. If they reach a serum albumin level of >30 g/L or are discharged, human albumin infusion will be stopped.

Study end points

The primary study end point is the improvement of overt HE within 3–5 days after treatment. The secondary study end points include the recurrence of overt HE, survival status during hospitalisation and 3-month follow-up periods and adverse events. The exploratory end point is the changes in inflammatory factors and oxidative stress indices after treatment.

Sample size calculation

Sample size is estimated by using significant difference test in the proportion between two groups on the PASS software V.15.0.5. Notably, until now, there are no data regarding the difference in the outcome of HE between high-dosage and low-dosage human albumin groups. Thus, the data regarding the difference in the outcome of HE between patients who received and did not receive human albumin were reviewed. According to the data from our previous meta-analysis,24 the rate of improvement of overt HE is estimated as 74.7% and 53.7% in patients treated with and without human albumin infusion, respectively. An alpha value of 0.05, power of 80%, a dropout rate of 10% and a ratio of 1:1 for the two groups are used. In total, 87 participants per group will be required.

Definitions

The diagnosis of HE depends on the history, symptoms, laboratory tests, number of connection test and CT/MRI imaging, if necessary. The severity of HE is defined according to the West Haven criteria,2 including: (1) grade I: presence of mild clinical signs, such as mild cognitive impairment, decreased attention, sleep disorders (insomnia and sleep inversion), euphoria or depression; (2) grade II: overt behavioural and personality changes, somnolence or apathy, mild temporal or spatial disorientation, impaired numeracy, dyskinesia, slurred speech; (3) grade III: marked temporal or spatial disorientation, abnormal behaviour, semi-coma to coma but can respond; (4) grade IV: coma, no response to speech and external stimuli. Overt HE refers to grade II–IV HE. The improvement of HE is defined as a decrease of at least one grade of overt HE after treatment. The reversal of HE is defined as the disappearance of overt HE after treatment. Before study enrolment, all investigators will be invited to review the West-Haeven criteria and trained to judge how to diagnose with HE and classify its grade. This is to minimise the observer bias from subjective judgement of HE.

Data collection

The following data will be collected: (1) demographic data, including age and gender; (2) underlying aetiology of liver cirrhosis, including viral hepatitis, alcohol abuse, autoimmune liver disease and other diseases; (3) predisposing factors of overt HE, including infection, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, electrolyte and acid-base balance disorder, large volume paracentesis, diuretics-induced side effects, high-protein diets and sedative drugs; (4) vital signs, including body temperature, heart rate, blood pressure and respiratory rate; (5) other clinical symptoms related to decompensated liver cirrhosis at admission, including gastro-oesophageal variceal bleeding, ascites, bacterial and fungal infections and hepatorenal syndrome; (6) conventional laboratory data at admission and during follow-up periods, including transaminase, bilirubin, serum albumin, red blood cells, haemoglobin, white blood cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes, prothrombin time, international normalised ratio, sodium, potassium, creatinine, urea nitrogen and ammonia; (7) inflammatory cytokines at baseline and 3–5 days from the start of treatment, including tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-10; (8) oxidative stress indexes before and after treatment, including malondialdehyde, superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase and nitric oxide; (9) Child-Pugh and model for end-stage of liver disease scores; (10) grade of overt HE at admission; (11) cumulated dosage of human albumin infused during hospitalisation; (12) changes of overt HE during hospitalisation and follow-up periods; (13) adverse events of human albumin infusion during hospitalisation and follow-up periods and (14) survival status. All original data will be retained as the pictures in the online database for verification. This is carried out by the investigators who work at the lead centre to ensure the accuracy of data.

Follow-up

All participants will be followed and reminded by telephone to undergo examinations on time. Follow-up visits will be scheduled on the first and third month after treatment. Clinical symptoms, especially state of consciousness, physical examinations and laboratory tests will be collected. Adverse events will be monitored. Survival status will be recorded, including the major cause and date of death.

Treatment termination

Treatment termination is defined as the assigned treatment is discontinued without withdrawal from the trial due to the following reasons: (1) participants are not appropriate to continue their assigned treatments, because their conditions worsen; (2) participants’ adherence is poor, which will affect the assessment of efficacy and safety; (3) adverse events occur, even though they may not be related to the assigned treatments; (4); participants request to terminate their assigned treatment, but agree to continue follow-up and (5) pregnancy.

Withdrawal

Participants can withdraw from the trial at any time due to the following reasons: (1) participants withdraw their informed consents; (2) study termination or centre closure; (3) loss to follow-up and (4) death.

Adverse events

Adverse events related to human albumin infusion mainly include allergy, heart failure, pulmonary oedema, acute haemolysis, renal dysfunction and neuropsychiatric abnormalities. They will be closely monitored during treatment and recorded.

Data analysis

The intention-to-treat set comprises all participants randomised. The per-protocol set is a subset consisting of participants who complete assigned treatments without significant protocol violations or develop the primary end point events. The safety set is a subset consisting of participants who receive at least one dose of human albumin infusion and undergo at least one safety evaluation. Continuous variables are described as mean±SD and median (range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are described as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. Multiple imputation will be performed to address missing data, if necessary. The heterogeneity among centres will be evaluated. A two-sided p value of <0.05 is considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses are performed using IBM SPSS V.26.0 software.

Discussion

According to the current guidelines by the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver,34 American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases35 and European Association for the Study of the Liver,36 human albumin infusion has been strongly recommended for the treatment of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and hepatorenal syndrome, and the prevention of paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction in patients with advanced liver diseases. Based on the recent evidence, it seems that the occurrence of overt HE was significantly reduced by human albumin infusion.37 38 Additionally, patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE might also benefit from human albumin infusion (OR 0.43, p=0.0004).25

Until now, only two RCTs explored the efficacy of human albumin infusion for the treatment of overt HE. In a single-centre RCT conducted in India by Sharma et al,22 patients with liver cirrhosis significantly benefited from human albumin infusion combined with lactulose, showing greater improvement in overt HE and lower mortality compared with lactulose alone. Levels of ammonia, IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly decreased after treatment. In another Spanish multicentre RCT by Simón-Talero et al,39 the survival on the 90th day was significantly better in patients with liver cirrhosis treated with human albumin infusion. Both RCTs had some limitations. First, the improvement of overt HE was defined as complete reversal of HE alone, but did not consider a reduction in the grade of HE. Second, only 10-day outcome data, rather than follow-up outcome, were collected in the study by Sharma et al. Third, the study by Simón-Talero et al had a relatively small sample size, which may have limited its statistical power to detect significant differences. Besides, a non-randomised comparative study by Jalan and Kapoor23 suggested the possible role of human albumin infusion for the treatment of overt HE. It should be noted that all patients enrolled in the study by Jalan et al had alcoholic liver cirrhosis, and all overt HE events were induced by excessive diuresis.23 In a previous retrospective study by our group, patients with covert HE (grade I HE) were also included.24

It is worth noting that none of the available studies explored the optimal dosage of human albumin infusion in patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE. Thus, for the first time, we decided to explore this issue. In this setting, we cannot calculate the sample size based on the existing data regarding the outcomes of patients with HE receiving different dosages of human albumin. Theoretically, patients assigned to the high-dosage human albumin infusion group can achieve and maintain the targeted serum albumin level more rapidly, probably improving inflammation conditions and outcomes more rapidly. In addition, in the current RCT, we also try to address the limitations of previous RCTs. First, the sample size is calculated by pooling the data from multiple studies, which were also included in a previous meta-analysis.24 Second, according to the report of the 11th World Congresses of Gastroenterology in 1998,40 only patients with type C HE without a history of TIPS are considered as our study population. Indeed, the mechanisms of HE after TIPS are primarily attributed to excessive portosystemic shunting; by contrast, HE is related to liver failure in most patients with liver cirrhosis who had not undergone TIPS. Third, in our study, the enrolment of patients is restricted to those with a reduced serum albumin level. Thus, the patient population who will most likely benefit from human albumin infusion might be selected. Fourth, the central effect cannot be completely avoided in this multicentre trial.

Ideally, we should have designed a control group where human albumin infusion will not be given in patients with liver cirrhosis and HE. It would be more scientific to confirm the efficacy of human albumin infusion for HE. However, such a protocol cannot be approved by the medical ethical board, because patients with liver cirrhosis and hypoalbuminaemia will present with a series of complications, and those with a serum albumin level of <30 g/L can receive a reimbursement for their use of human albumin in mainland China.

In conclusion, we hope that this study will provide high-quality evidence to identify the optimal dosage of human albumin infusion providing benefits to patients with liver cirrhosis and overt HE.
