PDGFRA in paediatric high-grade glioma – target or distraction?
Authors: Natalie Le, Jingwei Chen, Paul G Ekert, Lauren M Brown, Neevika Manoharan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaediatric high-grade gliomas (pHGGs) are aggressive and molecularly heterogenous paediatric brain tumours with extremely poor survival outcomes. Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are recurrently altered in a significant proportion of pHGGs and can be potentially targeted with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). PDGFRA is the most frequently altered RTK in pHGG and as such, represents an attractive therapeutic target, yet patients harbouring PDGFRA aberrations have largely failed to respond to TKIs. This raises the question as to whether PDGFRA is the only oncogenic dependency in all cases of pHGG, or alternatively, if there are unrecognised mechanisms conferring TKI resistance. Here we explore the mechanisms by which specific PDGFRA alterations drive oncogenesis and potentially mediate therapeutic resistance, to ascertain whether PDGFRA is a clinically useful target or merely a distraction.Keywords: Paediatric high-grade glioma, Receptor tyrosine kinases, Tyrosine kinase inhibitors

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) tumours are the leading cause of cancer-associated mortality in paediatric populations[1]. Paediatric high-grade gliomas (pHGGs) are a particularly devastating subtype, constituting approximately 10 % of paediatric CNS tumours but accounting for over 40 % of deaths[1]. pHGGs harbour genetic and epigenetic alterations that are largely distinct from those identified in adult high-grade gliomas (aHGGs)[2]. Alongside the conventional treatment modalities of chemotherapy and irradiation, these driver alterations have been harnessed in precision medicine programs to inform the selection of targeted therapies that aim to improve responses and minimise toxicity profiles[3].

While the identification of specific molecular biomarkers has improved overall cancer survival rates by enhancing diagnosis, risk-stratification, monitoring, and therapy, pHGGs still have worse survival outcomes compared to other cancer subtypes[4]. This may in part be due to the limitations of current CNS therapies, which are hindered by inadequate blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, poor pharmacokinetics, drug toxicities and tumour heterogeneity[5]. The comparative rarity of pHGG further exacerbates existing challenges to therapeutic development in paediatric cancers, including economic barriers to drug development for paediatric cancer indications, limited patient accrual for clinical trials, the paucity of druggable targets and increased safety concerns. Additionally, extrapolating data from adult high-grade glioma to pHGG makes little sense because of their distinct mutation profiles.

Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) have garnered significant clinical interest as therapeutic biomarkers due to their recurrent alteration in paediatric cancers and the availability and efficacy of RTK inhibitors (or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs))[6]. RTKs have a primary functional role in oncogenic cell signalling pathways that drive cancer cell proliferation, survival and progression[6]. RTK alterations are prevalent in malignancies across all age groups, with pan-cancer studies reporting RTK-signalling pathway dysregulation in approximately 50 % of adult[7] and 39 % of paediatric cancers[8]. Consequently, RTKs have become popular targets for novel therapeutics, promoting the discovery and development of drugs that attenuate or inhibit their function[6].

Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptor A (PDGFRA) is the most frequently altered RTK in pHGG and can be targeted using existing TKIs. However, the utility of targeting PDGFRA in pHGG remains controversial, as PDGFRA-altered patients have largely failed to respond to TKIs[9]. This raises the question as to whether PDGFRA is the only driver in these cases or if there are under-appreciated mechanisms mediating therapeutic resistance. In either case, to truly understand the potential clinical utility of PDGFRA-targeting therapies, it is critical to understand the mechanisms by which PDGFRA alterations drive oncogenesis.

The genomic landscape of pHGGs

The World Health Organisation (WHO) categorises gliomas from Grade I to IV by their genetic, molecular and morphological characteristics[10]. High-grade gliomas (HGGs) comprise WHO Grades III and IV and are characterised by their rapidly proliferative and aggressive nature. Within the paediatric subclass, the 2021 fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours of the CNS (WHO CNS 5) further delineates pHGGs into four subtypes: 1) diffuse midline gliomas (H3 K27-altered), 2) diffuse hemispheric gliomas (H3 G34-mutant), 3) diffuse paediatric-type high-grade gliomas (H3-wildtype and IDH-wildtype) and 4) infant-type hemispheric gliomas.

Histone gene variants and RTKs

Histone modifications in pHGGs are associated with distinct anatomical locations and co-occurring aberrations (Fig. 1). Midline and brainstem pHGGs near-exclusively express Lysine(K)-to-Methionine(M) mutations at position 27 in H3F3A (H3.3K27M)/HIST1HB (H3.1K27M), while hemispheric pHGGs frequently harbour glycine(G)-to-arginine(R) or Glycine(G)-to-Valine(V) alterations at position 34 in H3F3A (H3.3G34R/V)[11]. There are associations between these histone mutations and other molecular aberrations. Notably, H3.3K27M is more commonly associated with PDGFRA amplifications than other histone mutations, and may also harbour co-occurring aberrations in TP53, TOP3A, FGFR1 and CCND2. H3.1K27M tumours more frequently harbour mutations in PIK3CA/R1, BCOR and ACVR1[11,12] whilst H3.3G34R/V tumours are more frequently associated with TP53, ATRX and DAXX mutations[13]. H3-wildtype (H3-WT) pHGGs frequently harbour CDKN2A and TP53 mutations but can be further sub-classified using common molecular alterations that include RTK alterations. WT-A are characterised by BRAF V600E, NF1 mutations and RTK gene fusions, WT-B by EGFR, CDK6 and MYCN amplifications and WT-C by PDGFRA and MET amplifications[11]. Thus, RTKs, including PDGFRA, are of particular interest in pHGGs.

RTK variants in childhood cancer

RTKs are transmembrane receptors that when activated by cognate ligands, mediate the key cellular processes of growth, proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, migration and survival via downstream signalling pathways. Their function may be altered through genomic events, such as somatic mutations, amplifications or structural variations, including gene fusions and intragenic deletions[6]. Large-scale genomic studies have demonstrated that RTKs are recurrently altered in pHGG. DNA analysis of 326 pHGG samples demonstrated that 62 % of pHGGs have aberrations in RTK-signalling pathway genes, with the most frequently amplified RTKs being PDGFRA, KIT, KDR, EGFR and MET respectively and the most frequently mutated RTKs being PDGFRA, EGFR and FGFR1[11]. Similarly, another study identified high rates of RTK aberrations, including PDGFRA (21 %), MET (6 %), NTRK (7 %) and EGFR (5 %), in 127 Grade III/IV diffuse pontine glioma and non-brainstem HGG tumour samples[14] (Fig. 1). Pertinently, the frequency of alteration to specific RTK genes is distinct from aHGGs, which most commonly have EGFR variants (45-57 %), whilst PDGFRA and MET variants are identified in 13 % and 2-4 % of cases, respectively[15,16]. Their disparate mutation profiles may explain the inefficacy of aHGG therapies in pHGG, emphasising the significance of pHGG-specific genomic profiling and corresponding treatment approaches.

Genomic studies have also identified associations between specific RTK alterations in pHGG and other clinical features, including age and anatomical location. NTRK1/2/3, ALK, ROS or MET gene fusions are more frequent in infant high-grade gliomas (IHG), with one study identifying such fusions in 50 % of patients less than four years of age[17]. EGFR exon 7 and 20 mutations and gene amplifications are prevalent in bilateral thalamic gliomas, a molecularly unique subset of pHGGs[18]. PDGFRA has earned especial scrutiny, due to its prevalence. It is frequently amplified, mutated or overexpressed (high RNA expression in the absence of genomic alteration) in pHGG, with frequencies varying from 14 to 21 %[14,19,20]. PDGFRA amplifications (7-13 %) are generally more prevalent than mutations (5-9 %), although a subset of tumours harbour both (2 %)[11,14,19,20]. PDGFRA mutations without copy number amplification are found in older pHGG patients (mean age = 14.5 years) compared to non-mutated variants (mean = 9.4 years). Concurrent mutation/amplification also manifest in older patients (mean = 13.1 years) relative to the wildtype group (mean = 9.3 years). PDGFRA amplifications are more prevalent in brainstem tumours, compared to the more hemispheric localisation of PDGFRA-mutated tumours[19] (Fig. 1).

The clinical landscape of pHGGs

The current standard of care

The current standard of care in pHGG is multimodal therapy, entailing maximal surgical resection, local irradiation and adjuvant chemotherapy with temozolomide (TMZ). Maximal resection confers the greatest survival advantage, with reports of a 45 % increase in one-year survival in pHGG patients undergoing gross total resection (GTR) compared to subtotal resection (STR) or biopsies[21]. However, resection is often unfeasible for tumours in anatomically eloquent areas, such as midline structures, due to substantial morbidity and mortality risk[21]. For inoperable tumours, conventional treatment modalities offer modest or no OS improvements. Chemotherapy with TMZ is ineffective and is associated with haematological toxicity[22], while fractionated radiation therapy can offer a modest survival benefit depending on the subtype of pHGG[23]. This highlights the need for new targeted models of care that account for the heterogeneity of pHGG and minimise adverse sequelae in all paediatric age groups. Consequently, the use of novel targeted therapies, such as small molecule TKIs, have started gaining traction in pHGG.

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)

Over recent years, TKI treatments have demonstrated variable benefit in paediatric cancers. Larotrectinib epitomises their success, as it has demonstrated long-term clinical efficacy in treating NTRK fusion-positive malignancies[24], which include a large subset of IHGs. In fact, IHGs, which are frequently driven by RTK gene fusions, including NTRK, MET, ALK and ROS fusions, have also shown remarkable clinical responses to other TKIs beyond larotrectinib. Entrectinib, a TRK/ROS1/ALK inhibitor, achieved objective response rates of 56 % in NTRK- and ROS1-fusion positive HGGs (n = 9)[25]. Case reports have also reported the efficacy of TKIs against specific molecular indications, including the use of lorlatinib in ALK-fusion positive IHG[26]. However, while TKIs have yielded favourable survival outcomes in fusion-driven malignancies, other RTK-altered HGGs have been less amenable to therapy. Following extrapolation from aHGG and preclinical models, EGFR-inhibitors erlotinib[27,28] and gefitinib[29] have been trialled in pHGGs, but while they were well-tolerated, they conferred minimal survival benefit. As these were tested based on previous evidence of high frequency of EGFR RNA overexpression in pHGGs[30,31], their failure suggests that overexpression alone is not a reliable indicator of EGFR activation and rationale for therapeutic targeting. Furthermore, there is no clear correlation between EGFR alteration status and response to EGFR-inhibitors[32,33] in aHGGs, suggesting that there may be more complex determinants of drug response. Similarly, PDGFRA-altered HGGs have demonstrated variable clinical responses to PDGFRA-inhibitors, alluding to the presence of extrinsic factors that may contribute to drug efficacy or resistance, which will be explored in this review.

PDGFRA signalling and its dysregulation in cancer

Normal PDGFRA signalling

Platelet-derived growth factor receptors, PDGFR-α (or PDGFRA) and PDGFR-β (or PDGFRB) are class III RTKs, which also include KIT, FMS and FLT3[34]. These RTKs have five extracellular Ig domains, a transmembrane domain and an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) that is divided into two components, TKDI and TKDII[34]. In normal cellular contexts, PDGFRA/B each bind to unique homo- or heterodimeric combinations of the five PDGF ligand isoforms (AA, BB, CC, DD, AB), initiating RTK dimerization, activation and downstream signalling cascades[35]. PDFGRA is implicated in organogenesis and neuro-connective tissue development, while PDGFRB predominantly regulates vascular formation and function[34]. Studies in murine knockout models demonstrate a variety of craniofacial, skeletal, gonadal, visceral and neural tissue (oligodendrocyte and astrocyte) defects in PDGFRA-deleted mice and vascular homeostatic perturbation secondary to pericyte and smooth muscle dysregulation in the PDGFRB-knockout mice[35]. PDGFRA is expressed in the CNS in cortical and hippocampal neurons, oligodendrocyte precursors and subventricular neural stem cells during postnatal development[36]. Importantly, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) have been identified as the cellular origins of gliomas[37], and the expression of PDGFRA in OPCs decreases significantly in adulthood[38]. This may explain the enrichment of PDGFRA alterations in pHGG compared to aHGG.

Under normal conditions, PDGFRA signalling is regulated by typical mechanisms of RTK activation (Fig. 2). Initially, PDGF isoforms bind to D2/3 of the extracellular five-immunoglobulin-like domains (D1-5) to induce homo- or heterodimerisation of PDGFRA, which is then stabilised by receptor-receptor interactions at D4/5[35]. Following trans-autophosphorylation, intracellular tyrosine residues become available for selective binding by SH2-containing signal transduction proteins. These can initiate activation of the Ras-Raf-MAPK, JAK-STAT, PIK-AKT and PLC-γ pathways[34], though their individual cellular responses are not clearly delineated due to widespread cross-talk between the signalling pathways[35].

1)Gene Mutations

Somatic mutations in PDGFRA occur across all functional domains[13,14,19,39] and generally result in constitutive activation of the receptor and intracellular signalling pathways. The mechanisms that mediate their functional impacts are dictated by their respective locations on the protein. The PDGFRA Y288C extracellular mutation in Ig-like D3 results in ligand-independent PDGFRA activation. This may be a result of diminished mutant protein degradation, following aberrant glycosylation of PDGFRA Y288C and retention of the mutant receptor in the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum. Furthermore, phosphorylation within these subcellular compartments upregulates STAT3, AKT and ERK1/2 signalling[40]. The transmembrane V536E mutation also mediates ligand-independent PDGFRA activation[41]. PDGFRA V536E does not appear to increase the likelihood of receptor dimer formation, and both the WT and V536E mutant PDGFRA can spontaneously dimerise in cells. Instead, the mutant appears to trap any dimers that do form in their active state[41]. The juxta membrane domain mutation PDGFRA V561D, which has been identified in a patient-derived pHGG cell line[20], is thought to destabilise hydrophobic interactions between the juxtamembrane region and kinase folds, subsequently releasing the auto-inhibitory loop of the receptor[42]. Of the mutations arising within the kinase domain, PDGFRA D842V is the most well studied. Crystallographic studies have identified the presence of an Asp-Phe-Gly (DFG) sequence in the activation loop of PDGFRA, which regulates activation by transitioning between "DFG-in” and “DFG-out” conformations. D842V disrupts polar interactions, destabilising the “DFG-out” state, facilitating ATP-binding and constitutively activating the kinase[42]. Importantly, the active DFG-in state inhibits the binding of type II TKIs such as imatinib, causing drug resistance. In addition to conventional PDGFRA signalling, PDGFRA V561D, D842V and D842Y appear to mediate significant upregulation of STAT1/3/5 signalling activation, in comparison to WT PDGFRA[43]. This suggests the location of the PDGFRA mutation impacts cell signalling and may predict its sensitivity to different types of TKIs, which vary in their affinity with the active or inactive conformations of PDGFRA and the functional domains they target.

2)Structural Variants

Oncogenic PDGFRA gene fusions generally result from chromosomal translocations, but may also arise via other genomic mechanisms, including deletions and inversions[39,44]. PDGFRA has numerous known fusion partners, most of which have been identified in haematological malignancies[45] and rarely in CNS tumours. In pHGG, genes on chromosome 4, including KDR[39], LRPAP1[46] and LNX1[47], have been shown to fuse to PDGFRA. Previously described in aHGG[48], KDR::PDGFRA was the first PDGFRA fusion identified in pHGG[39], and the only such structural variant to be functionally characterised. It is the result of an intrachromosomal inversion, fusing KDR exon 13 to PDGFRA exon 10 with an intermediary cryptic exon, and encodes a chimeric protein consisting of a KDR Ig-like region and intact PDGFRA transmembrane, juxtamembrane and kinase domains. The KDR::PDGFRA fusion is constitutively activated and drives MAPK and PI3K/AKT signalling in model cell lines. In addition to fusion homodimerization, KDR::PDGFRA fusions are capable of heterodimerisation with both WT PDGFRA and KDR. This has been demonstrated by the reduction in canonical surface localisation of WT PDGFR and WT KDR and increase in their perinuclear, cytoplasmic colocalization with KDR::PDGFRA when these receptors are concurrently expressed[48]. These characteristics support the mechanism by which KDR::PDGFRA drives oncogenesis, both through aberrant chimeric protein function and disruption of normal KDR and PDGFRA signalling. Other gene fusions identified in pHGG include PDGFRA::LNX1 and LRPAP1::PDGFRA, both of which were associated with increased PDGFRA expression[46,47]. The details of the activation mechanisms have not been described, but likely involve fusion homodimerization.

Intragenic deletions are more frequent than gene fusions in HGG. The most common variant is PDGFRA Δ8, 9, a 243-base-pair deletion of exons 8 and 9, found in 40 % of PDGFRA-amplified aHGG[15,48] and estimated to occur in 16 % of adult glioblastomas[49]. This variant is rare in the paediatric population, with only two cases reported in the ZERO Childhood Cancer cohort[50] and none in other studies[20,39,51]. Functionally, PDGFRA Δ8, 9 promotes ligand-independent activation, is localised predominantly in the cytoplasm and exhibits potent transforming activity in Rat1 cells in vitro and in vivo[48,52]. While the exact mechanism for its constitutive activation is unknown, the deletion likely promotes dimer formation in the absence of ligands, as the truncated fragment partially encodes the receptor’s fourth and fifth Ig-like domains (D4/5), which mediate dimerisation.

3)Gene Amplification and Overexpression

Gene amplification, meaning gene copy number gain, and overexpression, meaning elevated mRNA levels, are considered here as equivalent potential oncogenic mechanisms, as both may lead to elevated expression of PDGFRA protein. Dysfunctional negative regulatory mechanisms are another pathway to abnormally high protein levels[53]. It remains a conundrum as to how elevated PDGFRA protein levels, in the absence of any activating variants, results in constitutive activation. One possibility is that PDGFRA activation in the presence of a gene amplification event still requires ligand engagement with the receptor. For instance, in Tp53 null primary mouse astrocyte (PMA) cultures, overexpressed WT PDGFRA may be abnormally phosphorylated to the same extent as mutant PDGFRA only after stimulation by PDGF-AA[39]. In transplantation experiments, WT PDGFRA did not enhance the tumorigenicity of these same cells, whereas activating mutations greatly shortened the tumour-onset latency, indicating overexpression of WT PDGFRA was not oncogenically equivalent to activating mutations[39]. Additionally, transgenic PDGFRA expression in mice, whether on a WT or Tp53 null background, did not drive tumour development[54]. However, WT PDGFRA overexpression has been shown to synergise with H3.3K27M and ATRX/Tp53 loss in murine neural progenitor cells to decrease tumour latency and promote invasion[55]. Such lines of evidence indicate that WT PDGFRA activation requires other factors to drive a malignant phenotype, such as ligand availability, loss of negative regulation and/or concurrent mutations.

Yet, PDGFRA amplification is common in pHGGs2, is associated with a poorer prognosis[19] and often correlates with tumour progression and treatment resistance[56], making it a clinically significant alteration. Moreover, the recurrent occurrence and maintenance of the gene amplification event strongly implies positive selection. Importantly, PDGFRA is frequently co-amplified with other genes, particularly KDR and KIT, as part of a focal amplification on chromosome 4 (4q12amp)[1,20]. This amplification event is significantly enriched in gliomas, and is associated with a higher mortality risk when concurrently expressed[56]. In a cohort of 342 aHGG samples, PDGFRA protein expression significantly correlated with gene amplification, with 88.5 % of PDGFRA amplifications associated with receptor protein overexpression. Increased KIT and VEGFR (encoded by KDR) expression were observed in the presence of this amplicon, but overexpression of these proteins occurred less frequently when only PDGFRA was amplified[57]. Interestingly, a separate evaluation reported PDGFRA amplification was not significantly associated with increased PDGFRA protein expression in a set containing both aHGGs and pHGGs[58]. These studies suggest that PDGFRA may not be the only oncogenic driver in the chr4q12 amplification.

4)Ligand Dysregulation

Generally, ligand agonism is influenced by both ligand-receptor properties (availability, specificity, affinity and structural variation) and extrinsic factors (allosteric modulation, subcellular trafficking and co-receptor association)[59]. Disturbances to the ligand-receptor- equilibrium can result in signalling dysfunction.

PDGFRA may be activated via autocrine or paracrine signalling. Constitutive autocrine signalling leads to unregulated cellular proliferation and invasion, and can synergise with other autocrine pathways to drive tumorigenesis[53]. Ectopic PDGF-B overexpression is sufficient to drive proliferation and block differentiation of murine glial precursors ex vivo, and drive the development of oligodendrogliomas in vivo[60,61]. PDGF-B overexpression has also been shown to cooperate with H3.3K27M to drive malignant transformation of low-grade lesions and promote proliferation in both Tp53-wildtype and -deficient murine brainstem gliomas[62]. Strong evidence of PDGFRA and PDGF-A/B/C concomitant expression and upregulation in malignant glioma tissue supports the hypothesis that autocrine activation loops drive oncogenesis with aberrant PDGF/PDGFRA interactions[60,63]. It is also pertinent to consider that the dysregulation of non-PDGF ligands in PDGFRA-altered pHGGs may activate alternate signalling pathways that confer resistance to therapeutic inhibition of the PDGF/PDGFRA axis.

Targeting PDGFRA in pHGG

The presence of PDGFRA genomic and transcriptomic alterations in pHGG has prompted the trial of PDGFRA-inhibitors in preclinical pHGG models and subsequently in clinical trials (Table 1). All trials included below enrolled paediatric patients, either newly diagnosed or recurrent/refractory/progressive HGG. The majority did not employ PDGFRA molecular stratification, though some studies endeavoured to delineate responses in PDGFRA-altered patients post hoc.

The results of PDGFRA-targeting therapeutics in pHGGs have been disappointing even when preclinical efficacy has been demonstrated in HGG models. Dasatinib exhibits more potent inhibition of PDGFR and better BBB penetrance than imatinib[66] yet monotherapy with both inhibitors induced limited tumour responses[71,72]. Subsequent trials combined dasatinib with other TKIs, such as the c-MET-inhibitor crizotinib[64] and the VEGFR-inhibitor vandetanib[66], to target additional RTKs commonly altered in pHGGs. Vandetanib only offered slight improvements in 1-year OS in paediatric patients with newly diagnosed non-metastatic DIPG, while crizotinib combination treatment induced significant toxicity with no objective responses in patients. The limited CNS bioavailability of crizotinib and dasatinib, and vandetanib-induced inhibition of gastrointestinal dasatinib absorption, suggest responses are constrained by insufficient drug delivery. Another trial sought to improve the CNS retention of dasatinib by combining treatment with everolimus in a small paediatric cohort of PDGFRA-altered gliomas[65] with some modest prolongation of OS. This study does suggest potential for PDGFRA inhibition in PDGFRA-altered tumours if the drug can be delivered, safely, in sufficient concentration to the tumour. Other PDGFR-targeting drugs, such as crenolanib and sunitinib, failed to result in any significant clinical response in pHGGs or demonstrate specificity for PDGFRA alterations[69,70].

While earlier generation PDGFRA-targeting TKIs had failed to improve pHGG outcomes, the recent success of the dual PDGFRA/KIT inhibitor avapritinib in PDGFRA-altered pHGG marks critical turning point for PDGFRA-specific therapeutics. Approved for use in gastrointestinal intestinal stromal tumours (GISTs) harbouring PDGFRA exon-18 mutations (including D842V) and mutant KIT-driven systemic mastocytosis, avapritinib has demonstrated highly selective and potent activity against cells expressing these aberrations[73,74]. In a landmark study[20], preclinical modelling demonstrated that avapritinib had efficient CNS penetrance, limited off-target toxicity and increased PDGFRA/KIT-targeting potency compared to dasatinib, crenolanib and axitinib. Avapritinib was shown to inhibit downstream PDGFRA signal transduction in PDGFRA-altered models. Subsequently, in a small cohort of paediatric and young adult patients, avapritinib induced both radiographical (42 %) and clinical responses (37.5 %) as well as improvements to PFS (median=2.8 to 5.2 months) and OS (median=6.1 to 12 months). Notably, PDGFRA amplified patients demonstrated better responses. Ultimately, responders succumbed to metastatic lesions that were avapritinib resistant. Some of these metastatic lesions had upregulated EGFR expression and phosphorylation, suggesting that PDGFRA inhibitor resistance can be mediated by reactivation of other RTKs. In spite of these promising results, enthusiasm for avapritinib must be tempered by the limited cohort size (n = 8). On the other hand, the molecularly heterogenous nature of the cohort, and their advanced disease stage suggests that molecular stratification based on a thorough appreciation of the role of PDGFRA and co-amplified kinases in pHGG, and treatment well before progression, may produce significantly better results.

Mechanisms of PDGFRA inhibitor resistance

One explanation for the generally disappointing responses to PDGFRA-inhibitors in pHGG is innate or acquired drug resistance mechanisms. Diverse mechanisms conferring resistance to RTK-targeting therapies have been identified across a range of cancers, including mutation of the RTK active site or drug-binding site, target gene amplification or protein overexpression, and alternate signalling pathway activation[75]. In PDGFRA-altered pHGGs, AKT/ERK-mediated rescue of downstream signalling was observed following sustained inhibition of PDGFRA by avapritinib[20]. Additionally, findings of spontaneous overexpression and downstream phosphorylation of EGFR pathways in avapritinib-resistant, PDGFRA-altered metastatic lesions suggest alternative RTK signalling pathways can be activated to bypass drug inhibition[20].

While there is limited literature on resistance in PDGFRA-altered CNS tumours, extrapolations from other cancers expressing PDGFRA aberrations can provide valuable insight into contributing mechanisms. Notably, drug resistance against TKIs is well-characterised in GISTs, which predominantly feature KIT (70-85 %)and PDGFRA (5-15 %) mutations[76]. In imatinib-treated GIST, selective inhibition of RTKs has been shown to promote adaptive activation of alternative RTK signalling pathways and consequently reduce sensitivity to treatment[77]. FGF2 and FGFR1 are highly expressed but dormant in GIST tumours, yet FGFR-signalling is activated following inhibition of the main oncogenic driver, KIT. Cotreatment with an FGFR-inhibitor suppresses this adaptive signalling and restores the tumours’ pharmacological responsiveness, suggesting resistance can be circumvented by dual targeting of rescue pathways. These observations, while in other tumour types, prompt further investigation into potential rescue mechanisms mediating PDGFRA inhibitor resistance in PDGFRA-altered CNS cancers, which will in turn identify rational therapeutic combinations that target these pathways. Furthermore, PDGFRA D842V mutations in GISTs are notoriously resistant to imatinib[76], but responsive to avapritinib, highlighting a common therapeutic denominator between a subset of pHGGs and GISTs. Crystallography analysis suggests that avapritinib binds to the active conformation of PDGFRA, which is favoured by activation-loop D842V mutations, while imatinib preferentially inhibits the inactive state, explaining its inefficacy. This concurs with other studies demonstrating the potency of avapritinib against activation-loop mutations in PDGFRA, including PDGFRA D842V and in-frame exon 18 deletions, and structurally homologous mutations in KIT[78,79]. A novel G-alpha pocket in PDGFRA D842V was also found to have substantial affinity for the avapritnib fluorobenzene substituent[80], suggesting further structural analysis may reveal pharmacological features that confer favourable drug interactions with purportedly resistant PDGFRA alterations. Interestingly, secondary PDGFRA mutations that confer resistance to avapritinib have been identified in GIST patients with primary exon 18 mutations, following avapritinib treatment and tumour progression[79]. These novel point mutations in exons 12-15 of PDGFRA were found to substantially increase avapritinib resistance in Ba/F3 cells harbouring PDGFRA D842V mutations and PDGFRA I843-D846 deletions. PDGFRA V658A and G680R were recurrently observed in avapritinib-resistant patients and shown to mediate drug resistance by sterically interfering with avapritinib binding. Furthermore, a triple-mutant PDGFRA (D842V/V658A/G652E) GIST cell line in this study was responsive to the HSP90 inhibitor tanespimycin and MEK inhibitor trametinib, despite resistance to multiple PDGFRA-targeting inhibitors including avapritinib, imatinib and crenolanib[79]. This suggests that inhibition of PDGFRA signalling by targeting downstream pathways or critical molecular chaperones may be a more effective approach in malignancies with resistant alterations.

A potential, unexplored mechanism of drug resistance in PDGFRA-altered CNS tumours is extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA). ecDNA-mediated gene amplification can result in high copy number amplifications of oncogenes with significant intratumoral heterogeneity and drug resistance, resulting in poorer prognoses across multiple cancer types[81]. Fluorescence in situ hybridisation of murine-derived PDGFRA amplified cell lines has elucidated the presence of extrachromosomal PDGFRA signals with variable copy numbers[82] and the loss of PDGFRA signals in culture compared to the primary GBM tumour[83], suggesting PDGFRA amplicons can also exist extrachromosomally in gliomas. Given the tendency for PDGFRA to be amplified in ecDNA, such localisation may confer drug resistance in PDGFRA-altered pHGG. Resistance has been demonstrated in EGFRvIII-mutant adult GBM[84], in which levels of EGFRvIII ecDNA and EGFRvIII-expressing cells reduced with erlotinib treatment but increased when the TKI was withdrawn. This modulation of altered proteins in the presence and absence of inhibitors enables tumour cells to regenerate and continue driving tumour growth after treatment. Investigating such ecDNA-mediated mechanisms in the context of PDGFRA and pHGG could inform therapeutic efforts to target or circumvent this resistance mechanism.

Conclusion

Targeting PDGFRA continues to be an area of major contention in the clinical management of pHGG. Currently, it is known that PDGFRA is prevalently altered in pHGG by multiple mutation types, the most common aberration being amplification. Gene mutations and structural variants have been shown to constitutively activate PDGFRA signalling while the contexts in which PDGFRA amplification leads to oncogenic activation are ambiguous, despite being associated with increased protein expression. Most PDGFRA-targeting therapeutics investigated in clinical trials have been multi-kinase inhibitors, with minimal differences in response between PDGFRA-altered and wildtype cohorts. The propensity for PDGFRA to be co-altered with other genes, particularly KIT and KDR, further complicates the interpretation of current clinical data in determining the efficacy of PDGFRA-targeting therapies that also target these co-mutations, such as avapritinib. Hence, there is a critical gap in current understanding of the situations and mechanisms by which PDGFRA alterations result in oncogenesis – this uncertainty occludes whether PDGFRA alterations are true gene dependencies and targetable oncogenic drivers in pHGG or if resources should be diverted into identifying and treating other driver mutations following repeated failures to effectively target PDGFRA. In the possibility that PDGFRA is a key oncogenic driver, resistance mechanisms, such as alternative RTK reactivation, are likely responsible for the subpar clinical outcomes seen previously; it is therefore necessary to elucidate these in order to devise therapeutic strategies that can bypass these mechanisms.
