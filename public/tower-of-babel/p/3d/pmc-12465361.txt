Severe Japanese encephalitis of a pregnant woman in the first trimester of pregnancy: a case report
Authors: Yafang Deng, Yan Tao, Ming Zhang, Mengyu Li, Yiwen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJapanese encephalitis (JE) is a natural zoonotic epidemic disease, mostly prevalent from July to September every year in China. JE is increasingly affecting adults in China, but severe JE patients who are pregnant are rarely reported in this country and in other countries. We report a case of pregnant Chinese woman who was diagnosed with JE, to highlight treatment points in the gestational state.Patient presentationA 27-year-old pregnant woman at 7 + weeks of gestation was admitted to the hospital due to a 7-day history of fever and progressive confusion, accompanied by urinary incontinence and febrile convulsions lasting for 3 days. The patient was comatose, required mechanical ventilation at admission, and had a history of splenectomy. JE virus sequences and IgG antibodies were detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A head CT scan of the patient revealed no obvious abnormalities, whereas brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed progressively marked high signal intensities in the bilateral thalami. Therefore, she was diagnosed with JE, whose manifestations included fever, intracranial hypertension, coma and respiratory failure. We treated her symptomatically with anti-infection, antivirals, anti-epileptics, and mannitol, to lower intracranial pressure (ICP). Nutritional conditions and coagulation function were monitored. The patient’s respiratory function was restored on day 14. When the condition became stable and the patient had no serious infection, curettage was performed on day 22. After curettage, we comprehensively enhanced the anti-inflammatory effects, nutritional support, anti-epilepsy, acid‒base balance and other treatments, and the patient’s condition was relatively stable. The adverse reactions to treatment included transaminitis and continued hyponatremia.ConclusionPregnancy may have contributed to the pathogenesis of severe JE infection in this patient. The administration of the vaccines in high-risk women considering fertility may prolong protective cell-mediated immunity and maintain IgM response to protect from severe JE virus infection. The treatment of pregnant JE patients was symptomatic, and attention was given to the patient’s nutritional status, acid balance and coagulation function. The incidence of secondary anti-NMDAR encephalitis in JE pregnant patients is also worthy of inquiry.Keywords: Case report, Japanese encephalitis, Pregnant woman

Introduction

Japanese encephalitis (JE) is caused by Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), which is the Main cause of viral encephalitis in Many countries of Asia, with an estimated 100,000 clinical cases every year [5, 21]. However, fewer than 1% of affected people develop clinical disease, and severe disease affects approximately 1 case per 250 JEV infections [2]. JEV is a flavivirus, transmitted to humans by the bite of Culex mosquitoes that have contracted it from infected pigs or wading birds such as herons and egrets [21], which may be prevalent in different seasons in different countries, while the annual peak occurs in July–September July to September every year in China [7, 14, 18]. Although symptomatic JE is rare, the case-fatality rate among those with encephalitis can be as high as 30%. Permanent neurologic, cognitive and behavioural sequelae occur in 30–50% of those with encephalitis [21]. The symptoms of JE could be fever, disturbance of consciousness and headache [5]. Pulmonary infection is the most common acute complication, and the thalamus is the most affected part of the brain [1, 4]. JE vaccination has greatly reduced the incidence of JE, which is still relatively high in children and elderly individuals. China accounts for 50% of the reported JE cases worldwide and the proportion of JE-infected adults is increasing in China [18, 20], while severe JE patients who are pregnant have rarely been reported in this country and in other countries. This paper reports a pregnant woman who was diagnosed with severe JE.

Patient information

A 27-year-old pregnant woman at 7 + weeks of gestation was admitted to the Neurological intensive Care Unit (NICU) department of West China Hospital, Chengdu, on October 23, 2024. The patient presented to the emergency department with a 7-day history of fever and progressive confusion, accompanied by urinary incontinence and febrile convulsions lasting for 3 days. Seven days prior to admission, the patient developed vertigo, fever, nausea, and vomiting of gastric contents accompanied by hypogastric pain and diarrhea following hotpot consumption. Five days before admission, the patient received intravenous fluids, antipyretics, and empiric antimicrobial therapy at an outside hospital (specific treatment details unavailable). Subsequently, her mental status progressively deteriorated to obtundation, followed by non-responsiveness, urinary incontinence, and febrile seizures. Three days before admission, her parents asked for a referral to another hospital, and the chest and head computed tomography (CT) results revealed little inflammation in both lower lungs and small pleural effusion on both sides and that the head CT scan of had no obvious abnormalities. This case was reported as ‘intracranial infection, pulmonary infection’. Due to persistent symptoms despite prior therapeutic interventions, the patient was transferred to our emergency department for further evaluation and management. The parents informed that the patient had a prior history of splenectomy due to rupture of the spleen by trauma. There was no other significant medical, family, or genetic history.

Clinical findings

Physical examination revealed that the patient was comatose, a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 2-T-1, and required mechanical ventilation. Both pupils were equally enlarged and round, 3 mm in diameter, and sensitive to light reflection. Her body temperature was 37.8 °C, her heart rate was 112 beats/min, and her blood pressure was 99/59 mmHg. On day 7 of her illness (day 1 of admission), Next-Generation Sequencing (tNGS) of CSF collected by lumbar puncture detected one Japanese encephalitis (JE) viral sequence, alongside pleocytosis (80 × 10^6/L lymphocytes). Notably, CSF and serum IgM antibodies against JE virus were negative, likely due to testing beyond the optimal diagnostic window (acute phase: ≤ 7 days post-onset). However, markedly elevated CSF immunoglobulin (0.21 g/L) and albumin (0.824 g/L) levels—both ≥ fourfold higher than normal on day 8 of illness—suggested significant blood–brain barrier disruption or intrathecal synthesis. Differential diagnoses initially considered autoimmune encephalitis (e.g., anti-NMDAR encephalitis) and tuberculous meningitis (TBM). These were subsequently excluded based on negative autoimmune antibody panels and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) TBA testing, despite a positive serum TBA result. On day 12 of illness, IgG antibodies and pleocytosis (50 × 10^6/L) were detected in the CSF collected by lumbar puncture. The chest and head CT scan demonstrated bilateral pulmonary scattered nodular opacities, linear densities, and consolidations suggestive of inflammatory etiology, while no significant intracranial abnormalities were identified. Abdominal color ultrasound revealed obvious abdominal distension at 7 + weeks of pregnancy, indicating a single live intrauterine fetus. On day 13 from illness, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed progressively marked high signal intensities in the bilateral thalami, midbrain, posterior pontine, bilateral hippocampus and temporal pole cortex (Fig. 1). These MRI findings may represent characteristic features of JE [5]. The bilateral thalamus and hippocampus showed inflammatory changes. Other diagnoses included intracranial hypertension, pulmonary infection, respiratory failure, electrolyte metabolism disorders and a gestational state. On day 2 from admission, the patient still had recurrent fever. A series of antipyretic measures were implemented, including physical cooling with ice blankets and lukewarm baths, and the temperature became normal. Additionally, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted from ceftriaxone to piperacillin-sulbactam (piperacillin sodium and tazobactam sodium, 4.5 g IV Q8H) to broaden antimicrobial coverage. The prone position was used to reduce atelectasis and improve oxygenation. On hospital day 4, the patient developed emesis requiring gastrointestinal management. Following gastroenterology consultation, the following interventions were implemented: (1) nasogastric decompression, (2) temporary cessation of enteral feeding, and (3) maintenance of parenteral nutrition support. Proton pump inhibitor therapy (Ilaprazole enteric-coated tablets) and sucralfate oral suspension were administered via nasogastric tube. Additionally, triple-lumen nasojejunal tube was placed to facilitate subsequent enteral nutrition support. On hospital day 6, lumbar puncture revealed persistently elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) measuring 220 mm H2O. Consequently, osmotic therapy was escalated to 20% mannitol (125 mL intravenously every 6 h) to enhance intracranial hypertension Management. Tracheotomy was performed at the bedside on day 13, and high oxygen flow was administered on day 14. The patient experienced occasional convulsions, and we added dexmedetomidine; lacosamide was used to prevent epilepsy. The patient had a fever again on hospital day 15 due to serious urinary tract infections (urinalysis demonstrating > 104 CFU/mL bacterial growth) and pulmonary infections, and her left lung affected more. Therapeutic interventions included: (1) urinary catheter replacement with sterile technique, (2) urine alkalization (target pH 7.0–7.5), and (3) escalation of antimicrobial therapy guided by subsequent culture results and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. On hospital day 18, the patient still had paroxysmal convulsions, so phenobarbital was also added as an antiepileptic. During hospitalization in our department, the patient's fluid balance (input–output volume) and arterial blood gas (ABG) parameters were closely monitored throughout the clinical course. Other symptomatic treatments included anticoagulation, acid‒base balance correction, parenteral nutrition and so on.

JEV have marked impacts on the developing fetus [6] and the patient had possible intake of teratogenic drugs such as mannitol, which were highly risky to the fetus and were not conducive to healthy development. After communicating with the patient's family, the patient agreed to terminate the pregnancy. The infection was controlled, as evidenced by a decline in leukocyte count from 12.43 × 10⁹/L on September 23 to 11.47 × 10⁹/L on September 8. Following stabilization of the patient's condition, therapeutic uterine curettage was performed on hospital day 22. Subsequently, oxytocin was administered for uterine contraction augmentation over a three-day period, during which no significant lochia discharge was observed.

On hospital day 28, the patient remained comatose with a GCS score of 2-T-1 and persistent low-grade fever. Hemodynamic stability was maintained through continuous low-dose M-hydroxylamine infusion. Mechanical ventilation with high-flow oxygen was delivered via tracheostomy. Pupillary examination revealed bilateral isocoria (3 mm diameter) with preserved light reflexes. It was stressed that the patient should be turned over frequently to promote the expulsion of lochia, after which she was transferred to the neurology department from the NICU.

Follow-up and outcomes

During neurology department, the patient’s vital signs were stable. She demonstrated spontaneous eye opening but exhibited no verbal response to auditory stimuli and failed to follow commands (including tongue protrusion, eye closure, or other voluntary movements). Physical Examination revealed myoclonic jerks elicited by percussion of the left upper extremity, and generalized hypertonia with absence of purposeful limb movement. The left lower extremity demonstrated sustained ankle clonus (grade 3 +), while deep tendon reflexes were hyperactive throughout all extremities. An equivocal Babinski sign was noted on the right lower limb. Upon admission to the Neurology Department, the patient was administrated continually with antiepileptic therapy with lacosamide (100 mg IV BID) for seizure control, intracranial pressure Management with 20% mannitol (125 mL IV Q6H), anti-infection with piperacillin-sulbactam (4.5 g IV Q8H). Concurrently, comprehensive management was instituted to address the patient's critical condition, including antimicrobial therapy, nutritional supplementation, thrombolytic treatment, and electrolyte stabilization. However, the patient still had severe lung infection during the hospital stay. We have adjusted antimicrobial regimen for several times by consulting with infectious disease department according to Sputum culture result including cefoperazone sulbactam (4 g IV Q12H) combined with tigecycline (50 mg IV Bid), or Meropenem (1000 mg IV Q8H), or Colistin sulfate (750000u IV Q12H) combined with Tigecyline (100 mg IV Bid). On hospital day 33, the patient experienced neutropenia (0.04 × 109/L), whose adult normal range is (1.8–6.3) × 109/L), was treated symptomatically with colony stimulating factor. After 3 days, the Segmented Neutrophil number became 21.97 × 109/L. On hospital day 49, the patient developed a rash on both lower limbs. We considered that the patient May have had allergic dermatitis, so ebastine and cetirizine were used, and the rash subsequently disappeared. On hospital day 58, CSF analysis obtained via lumbar puncture revealed anti-NMDAR-IgG positivity, supporting a diagnosis of secondary anti-NMDAR encephalitis. On hospital day 63, the patient’s condition and vital signs were stable, but she still had a low fever between 37.3 °C and 38.0 °C and was in a minimally conscious state. Therefore, she was discharged to the rehabilitation hospital. The complications during all the treatments included malnutrition, acid‒base imbalance (such as hyponatremia) and thrombus. The adverse drug reactions included transaminitis. All the adverse reactions improved after drug use.

Discussion

JE virus infection is very common in endemic areas (many countries in Asia), and the highly epidemic provinces of JE in China include Henan, Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Yunnan [20]. Severe cases are rare, but can be devastating, among the 30,000–50,000 global cases per year, approximately 20–30% of patients die, and 30–50% of survivors develop significant neurological sequelae [2]. JEV is one of the main flaviviruses of concern during pregnancy, other viruses include Dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV), West Nile virus (WNV), and yellow fever virus (YFV). Many of these viruses including are neurotropic and/or have marked impacts on the developing fetus [6]. The treatment of pregnant women with severe JE is trickier, but such cases have rarely been reported. Weng et al. 19 reported a case of severe JE in the third trimester in 2015. A persistent and latent JE virus infection study in pregnant and nonpregnant mice reported that the JE virus can remain latent for at least 1 year [10]. The body can produce T lymphocytes and IgM antibodies to protect against JE virus [17]. While immunosuppression may be a potentiating factor for the establishment of JEV persistence in animal hosts [13]. Mathur et al. [10] found that following intraperitoneal inoculation into pregnant mice JEV persisted for 16 weeks in contrast to 4 weeks in non-pregnant mice and the infectious JEV persists longer in the thymus than in other organs. Due to immunosuppression of pregnant mice, JEV persists longer and May outbreak more easily than non-pregnant mice. Pregnancy May have contributed to the pathogenesis of severe JE infection in this patient. To prevent an outbreak of JEV during pregnancy, we believe that women who consider having a baby in high-risk areas should be screened and protected from latent infection. Protection against JEV is provided by T lymphocytes and by IgM antibodies, which are present only until approximately 2 weeks after infection [11]. Application of the vaccine in high-risk women considering fertility may prolong protective cell-mediated immunity and maintain IgM response to against JE virus reactivation, while the administration time is not sure. For example, the administration of vaccines that have been developed mainly against JEV genotype III with live attenuated or formalin-inactivated virus (e.g., IXARIO) could induce long-lasting immunity [3].

The diagnosis is commonly based on an increasing titer of different antibodies against JE virus in acute and convalescent sera [12]. On day 7 of her illness, JE virus sequences and IgG antibodies were detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of this patient. A head CT scan of the patient revealed no obvious abnormalities, whereas brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed progressively marked high signal intensities in the bilateral thalami [8] reported that bilateral thalamic involvement, especially hemorrhagic involvement, May be considered characteristic of JE, especially in endemic areas. Therefore, we diagnosed pregnant woman with JE, whose Main clinical manifestations were fever, increased intracranial pressure measuring 220 mm H2O and disturbance of consciousness. The presence of anti-NMDAR IgG in the recovery period confirmed that the patient also had the secondary anti-NMDAR autoimmune encephalitis. Ma et al. [9] reported that JE does not typically trigger anti-NMDAR IgG synthesis. The incidence of JE-induced autoimmune encephalitis in pediatric patients with JE is 7.9%, and the incidence of JE-induced anti-NMDAR encephalitis is 4.7% [9]. The incidence of anti-NMDAR encephalitis in JE pregnant patients is also worthy of inquiry.

The main characteristic of therapeutic intervention for JE is symptomatic treatment, including anti-infection, anti-epileptic, and lowering ICP [15, 17]. In this case, the patient’s condition was severe, with early respiratory failure, fever, severe lung infection, elevated intracranial pressure and convulsion. Therefore, we enhanced the respiratory support and therapy, anti-infection, dehydration and antiepileptic effects. For pregnant JE patients, we periodically reviewed fetal B-ultrasounds and paid attention to vaginal bleeding. Pregnancy and long-term bed rest may lead to a hypercoagulable state and malnutrition in the patient’s blood, so the patient’s coagulation function and nutritional status also need to be considered. After induced abortion curettage, we comprehensively enhance the anti-inflammatory effects, nutritional support, anti-epilepsy, acid‒base balance and other treatments, and the patient’s condition was relatively stable.

In 2008, Mainland China included the Japanese encephalitis (JE) vaccine in the Expanded Program on Immunization to control the JE epidemic [16]. The incidence of JE in China continues to decrease from 2019 to 2022 [18]. It is still necessary to maintain a high coverage rate of the JE vaccine among school-aged children and strengthen JE prevention and control among adults or high-risk groups. For pregnant women diagnosed with JE, in addition to symptomatic treatment, timely termination of pregnancy is crucial when the patient’s condition becomes stable, and infection is controlled.

Our study also has some limitations. First, we lack a detailed history of treatment since the onset of the disease in other hospital so that some experimental data are not available from the onset of the disease. Second, because of the marital status of the patient and the wishes of the parents, we did not consider the safety of the fetus more during the treatment process.

Conclusion

Pregnancy may have been one of the contributing factors for severe disease in this patient. The administration of vaccines in high-risk women considering fertility may prolong protective cell-mediated immunity and maintain IgM response to against JE virus reactivation. Severe JE with pregnancy is rare and tricky due to unstable body conditions. In this pregnant female, the treatment of JE was symptomatic, and attention was given to the patient’s nutritional status, acid balance and coagulation function. The incidence of JE-induced anti-NMDAR encephalitis in pregnant patients is also worthy of inquiry.
