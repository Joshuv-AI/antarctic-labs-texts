Background

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has emerged as a safe and effective alternative to conventional right ventricular pacing, promoting more physiological activation of the cardiac conduction system [1,2]. It has also been adopted in select cases for cardiac resynchronization therapy [1,2]. The technique for LBBAP lead implantation is well-described in two consensus documents that guide physicians in adopting this pacing modality [1,2]. The clinical benefit of obtaining definitive conduction system capture has been highlighted in large-scale data, such as the MELOS RELOADED study, which emphasizes the superiority of successful capture over myocardial-only pacing [3]. Various methods are available to confirm LBBAP capture; one such method is programmed stimulation, including extra-stimuli, which can be diagnostic when standard threshold testing yields inconclusive results [1,4,5]. The use of non invasive programmed stimulation (NIPS) to diagnose conduction system capture was first proposed by Jastrzębski et al., who demonstrated its utility both for HIS and LBBA capture, using an electrophysiology system [6]. While typically employed at the time of implantation, this technique can also be valuable during follow-up, enabling physicians to confirm capture when other methods are unreliable or unfeasible. In this case report, we illustrates the practical application of programmed stimulation principles in an outpatient setting to confirm LBBAP capture in a patient whose QRS complex morphology was not suitable for evaluating the V6 R-wave peak time (V6RWPT) and the V1–V6 inter-peak interval.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old patient with symptomatic paroxysmal 2:1 atrioventricular (AV) block and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 48% received a dual-chamber pacemaker (Zenex DR, Abbott, Sylmar, CA, USA) for left bundle branch area pacing. One month post-implantation, the patient presented for an initial assessment of device functionality and to commence outpatient follow-up. The electrical parameters of the ventricular lead were: sensing amplitude of 10 mV, pacing threshold of 0.5 V at 0.4 ms, and impedance of 480 Ohms.

Surface electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed a sequential pacemaker rhythm (atrial pacing–ventricular pacing, AP–VP) with unipolar ventricular output and an “rS” QRS complex morphology in both V1 and V6. During ventricular threshold testing in VVI mode (from DDD mode), QRS morphology transitioned from “rS” to " rSr’ " in V1 and to “qs” in V6 (Fig. 1). These patterns were not suitable for assessing V6RWPT or V1–V6 inter-peak interval to confirm left conduction system capture. Furthermore, no significant QRS morphology changes were observed during threshold testing at decreasing outputs, starting from 7.5 V, and varying pulse durations. As no baseline ECG was available for comparison, an echocardiogram was performed, which ruled out lead dislodgment despite suboptimal imaging windows. We proceeded to confirm left bundle branch (LBB) capture using the burst pacing method with the Merlin Programmer (Abbott, Sylmar, CA, USA), following informed consent from the patient (Fig. 2). Non-invasive programmed stimulation (NIPS) was performed on the ventricular lead with a unipolar output at 1 V and a 0.4 ms pulse width. The initial drive (S1) consisted of 8 paced beats delivered at a cycle length of 600 ms, followed by an extra stimulus (S2) delivered at progressively shorter coupling intervals, starting at 400 ms and decreasing in 10 ms increments, until either capture was lost or a QRS morphology transition was observed [4].

Discussion and conclusions

The burst pacing method using NIPS represents a valuable strategy to confirm capture in patients undergoing Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing (LBBAP), particularly in cases where the standard criteria recommended by the EHRA consensus on CSP [1] are not met or cannot be applied. As is known, during implantation, the QRS transition is considered the gold standard method to confirm left conduction system capture during threshold tests or programmed stimulation; if inconclusive, other parameters such as V6RWPT and V1-V6 interpeak interval are considered. However, during follow-up, it is more challenging to confirm conduction system capture because the QRS transition is visible in only 30% of cases, and the other parameters can only be evaluated in cases of QRS morphology with a dominant R wave in V5 and V61. In our case, the threshold test proved inconclusive, and the QRS morphology was unsuitable for the evaluation of other parameters confirming capture.

The use of NIPS in this patient allowed the identification of changes in the V1RWPT interval and the QRS morphology transition, thereby confirming conduction system capture. This approach was safe, as no arrhythmias occurred, and feasible using the Merlin programmer. In cases like ours, characterized by an rS morphology in lead V6, to overcome this limitation, Kiełbasa et al. recently proposed the Lead I RWPT as a more reliable marker, establishing a diagnostic cutoff of 81.5 ms for patients with a healthy conduction system. In our patient, the S3 Lead I RWPT measured 82 ms on a standard 50 mm/sec ECG. While the 50 mm/sec sweep speed has inherent resolution limits for millimetric differences, this value, being extremely close to the proposed threshold, provided supportive evidence of LBB capture [7].

These findings extend the utility of NIPS, allowing physicians to use the same programmer for both electrical tests and confirmation of conduction system capture during follow-up, employing one of the two gold standard methods: programmed stimulation. Therefore, the application of NIPS in outpatient follow-up offers several clinical and practical advantages.

This case suggests that non-invasive programmed ventricular pacing via a programmer may represent a useful diagnostic option in the monitoring of patients with LBBAP. Further studies are needed to define the precise role of this method in clinical practice; however, our result is a first step to support the integration of NIPS as a confirmatory tool in outpatient follow-ups.
