Delineating multiobjective ecological management zones and revealing scale effects of thresholds for factors influencing ecosystem services in Jiangxi Province
Authors: Xuelong Yang, Jun Zhang, Haisheng Cai, Xiaomin Zhao, Han Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElucidating the connection between the total supply and demand of ecosystem services (ESTSD) can provide comprehensive decision support for regional eco-management. Previous studies have focused on the spatial and temporal evolution of ESTSDs in the horizontal dimension but studies in the vertical dimension are lacking; too much attention has been given to single dimensions for ecological management zoning while omitting multilevel and multiobjective considerations; and there are limitations in knowledge about the threshold scale effect of ecosystem service (ES) influencing factors. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the ESTSD in Jiangxi Province; to reveal the spatial and temporal evolution of the matching of total supply and total demand, the coupling and coordination, and the vertical spatial gradient effect of the ESTSD; to propose a multiobjective zoning framework that integrates ‘supply–demand–coordination–function’ and ‘strategic guidance–zoning control–functional guidance’; and to delineate three levels of integrated ecological management zones. Constraint lines were used to explore and complement the threshold scale effect. The results show that the ESTSD values are low overall. The high supply‒low demand region is large, and the degree of coupling coordination shows an overall imbalance. The matching and coupling coordination of the ESTSD shows significant vertical spatial gradient effects. Four strategic guidance zones at the primary level, 5 secondary regulation zones, and 5 advantageous function guidelines are delineated. As the scale increases, the optimal thresholds for the digital elevation model, slope, and precipitation all decrease, whereas those for the temperature increase. It is proposed that this study will further refine the management of regional ecosystems, regulate the contradiction within the ESTSD, realize a multiobjective ecological zoning control, and promote regional coordination and sustainable development. This research provides favorable support for ecological management and can serve as a reference for similar regions.Keywords: Ecosystem services supply and demand, Gradient effect, Integrated ecological management zones, Threshold scale effect, Jiangxi provinceSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Ecosystem services (ESs) are a medium for connecting ecosystems and social systems1, reflecting the process of material competition and the shift in the movement of goods and services from supply to demand. The balance of supply and demand, both coupled and coordinated, is strongly correlated with the ecological security and sustainability of a region2,3. With the rapid progression of urban development, the supply‒demand conflict has gradually come to the forefront as ecosystems are caught between maintaining and supporting the orderly operation of the social system and the ever-increasing need for human society to satisfy its own needs for survival and development4. Human activities and demands have resulted in a reduction in ecological land, changed the function and structure of ESs, and triggered regional ecosystem imbalances and degradations5–7. Therefore, research into the supply and demand of ecosystem services (ESSD) is highly important for maintaining environmental quality and security and promoting the rational allocation and utilization of limited resources.

How to accurately quantify ESSD has become a popular research topic in academia, and scholars have used multivariate data to quantify ESSD through model estimations1,8, data overlays9, and ecological process simulations10, among other methods, although these tend to target individual ES rather than considering a region’s total supply and demand of ecosystem services (ESTSD). In response to supply‒demand matching through supply‒demand ratios11, four quadrants12, and the coupling coordination degree (CCD)13, spatial autocorrelation14 and hotspot analysis15 reveal differentiation patterns in two-dimensional supply‒demand planes, but there are limitations in the study of the vertical spatial gradient effect (VSGE). Therefore, quantifying the ESTSD and revealing vertical spatial gradient effects are necessary.

As one of the comprehensive means of ecological management, ecological management zones (EMZs) can provide support for the implementation of differentiated and precise control, the enhancement of ecological governance effectiveness, and the promotion of a harmonious relationship between the ecological environment and economic construction. Nevertheless, previous studies have delineated EMZs through single evaluations or single dimensions14,16,17. For example, Teng et al. delineated ecological management zones in the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area by identifying static and dynamic matching characteristics of supply and demand of ESs16; and Zhang et al. divided the Feng River Basin into 10 land use management zones according to their spatial attributes of ecosystem trade-off types17. EMZs are slightly rough, which leads to zoning control limitations in the actual management process, with unclear strategic objectives and functional guidance, and fails to reflect regional differences. Therefore, the construction of integrated ecological management zones (IEMZs) under a multiobjective system to assist in regional fine-scale ecological management is a priority that requires urgent attention.

In factor threshold studies, methods mainly include segmented linear regression and constraint line methods18. The latter is a method for analyzing nonlinear relationships between variables and determining thresholds. Because ESs are complex and their relationships with influencing factors are often nonlinear, the constraint line method can accurately elucidate intricate constraining relationships between variables and their intrinsic mechanisms19; thus, the constraint line approach is a popular tool for analyzing influencing factor thresholds. Scale effects exist not only for influencing factors but also for the thresholds of influencing factors20. However, a substantial portion of recent investigations into thresholds have focused on the single scale21–24. For example, Berdugo et al. identified thresholds for different drought-driven ecosystem attributes at a global scale21; and Ma et al. identified thresholds for quantifying ecosystem service influences at the ecological reserve scale for the Tibetan Plateau23. Optimal thresholds at different scales are subject to strong uncertainties. Therefore, there is an urgent need to give attention to the scaling effect of thresholds.

The above studies offer insightful perspectives for ESs, yet several shortcomings remain: (1) More attention has been given to supply–demand from individual ES perspectives, and the ESTSD has not been considered. (2) There has been more research on differentiation patterns in the two-dimensional supply‒demand plane and less research on the gradient effect in vertical three-dimensional space. (3) There are limitations in the study of EMZs, which do not reflect the hierarchical logic of goal, regulation, and function, and this is not conducive to differentiated management and policy formulation. (4) Most of the research on thresholds has focused on single scales, and there are limitations in the research on the scale effect of thresholds.

As one of the national-level ecological civilization pilot demonstration zones, Jiangxi Province attaches particular importance to improving the overall function and quality of the ecosystem. Recent ES research in the province has focused primarily on ecosystem service bundles (ESBs)25, drivers26, synergies and trade-offs27,28, ecological security patterns29, and value volumes30, among other factors. In summary, the objectives of this study are as follows: (1) Quantify the ESTSD from 1990 to 2020 via models such as InVEST. (2) Reveal the characteristics of the ESTSD and their evolution patterns and analyze the gradient effect in vertical space. (3) Delineate multilevel objective IEMZs on the basis of the four-quadrant, CCD and self-organizing map (SOM) methods in accordance with the ‘supply–demand–coordination–function’ and the multiobjective ‘strategic guidance–zoning control–functional guidance’ frameworks. (4) Reveal the optimal thresholds for ES influencing factors and their scale differences to facilitate the regulation of ESTSD balance. It is proposed that this study will further refine the management of regional ecosystems, regulate the contradiction within the ESTSD, realize a multiobjective ecological zoning control, and promote regional coordination and sustainable development.

Materials and methods

Study area

Jiangxi Province (“Gan”), which is located in East China, has abundant natural resources29. The landforms are diverse; the northern and central regions are plains, and the remaining regions are mountainous and hilly (78%)28. The water system is well developed and includes Poyang Lake and five major water systems. The number of nature reserves is among the most in China. In recent years, the urbanization level has steadily increased from 20.35% in 1990 to 60.44% in 2020. In addition to a rapid increase in the urbanization level, the areas of forests, water, and meadows have decreased by 1,070.84 km2, and developed land has increased by 2,769.98 km2, resulting in a fundamental reconfiguration of the landscape31. As important ecological barriers in the middle reaches of the Yangtze River, urban expansion and ecological space compression have exacerbated the local ecosystem imbalance, and ecosystem function and supply capacity have continued to decline, with the contradiction between ES supply and demand becoming increasingly acute. Therefore, research on the ESTSD in the region will help identify the shortcomings of regional supply and hotspots of regional demand, which will establish an empirical foundation for the formulation of differentiated ecological management policies and zonal regulations, a highly important goal for promoting coordinated, sustainable development and ensuring ecological security in the area. Methods

The problems to be solved in this study included assessing the ESTSD using models such as InVEST, revealing the supply–demand matching and coupling coordination evolution characteristics of the ESTSD by using four-quadrant and CCD models, and analyzing the gradient effect of the ESTSD in the vertical space using topographic position indices. On the basis of a multiobjective zoning framework that integrated ‘supply–demand–coordination–function’ and ‘strategic guidance–zoning control–functional guidance’, we delineated multilevel objective IEMZs in which the supply‒demand matching relationship was used to delineate the first level of zoning and clarify the overall strategic guiding objectives of each region; the coupling coordination status of the ESTSD was used to delineate the second level of zoning and realize zonal differentiation; and the main ES functions of each region were synthesized for use as the basis of tertiary zoning delineations to guide the dominant ES. The constraint line approach was employed to delineate the thresholds of ES-influencing factors and their scale effects. The technical framework diagram for this research is shown in Fig. 2.

Methods for assessing the total demand for ecosystem services

Following the principles of systemicity, dynamism, data accessibility and quantifiability, and spatial relevance, in conjunction with a study on indicators related to the demand for human activities42, we used POP, GDP, NL and the land use intensity index (LUI) to reflect the spatial demand of human society for ESs. POP responds to the base demand for ESs from population distribution and size, and population growth directly increases the pressure on ESs; thus, using POP as a demand assessment is an effective and commonly used methodology43,44. Moreover, GDP reflects the expansionary pressures on ESs from economic scale and activity, and NL reflects the spatial distribution and intensity of human activity, with GDP and NL having a negative impact on ESs45, all of which can respond to spatial demand for ESs46. Finally, LUI is a direct response to the intensity of anthropogenic interventions on land use, and research has investigated how land use change affects ESs via LUI47,48. Four-quadrant method

The four-quadrant method50 can respond to the spatial matching connections within the ESTSD. A standardized ESTSD was placed on the x- and y-axis, and the average value was used as the 0-point, dividing the data into four quadrants. In counterclockwise order, we defined quadrant I as high supply–high demand (H–H), quadrant II as low supply–high demand (L–H), quadrant III as low supply–low demand (L–L), and quadrant IV as high supply–low demand (H–L).

Self-organizing map

The SOM method54,55 is an unsupervised deep learning algorithm capable of downgrading and clustering to strengthen the sensitivity of the original information and avoid information loss, such as homogenization, during adaptive generation. We first mapped the ESs with similar characteristics onto the neuron grid via the SOM approach and then used K-means to cluster the ESs quadratically. When selecting the most appropriate number of clusters, we judged the quality of the clusters by the average profile coefficients. The above analysis steps were implemented in RStudio software.

Constraint line

The constraint line method refers to finding the optimal value of an objective function while satisfying some specific constraints18. We constructed constraint lines for ES-influencing factors via quantile partitioning: First, we created a 1 × 1 km grid and obtained the values of all the ESs and their influencing factors; second, we divided the data into 100 groups, and 99.9% of the quantiles in each group were taken as thresholds; third, we used linear, quadratic, cubic, logarithmic, exponential, and power functions to fit the thresholds, and then we compared the R2 values and selected the most appropriate function to plot the constraint line; fourth, we derived the optimal threshold value from the fitted function. The above steps were performed via Python software.

Results

Analysis of total supply and total demand of ESs

As shown in Fig. 3a, the ESTSD distributions are clustered, with the total supply tending to be normally distributed and the total demand tending to be skewed. The average values of total supply for 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020 are 0.3451, 0.3387, 0.3276, and 0.3266, respectively, showing a decreasing trend, and the values of total demand are 0.0715, 0.0678, 0.0777, and 0.0811, respectively, with a rising trend in demand space. This demonstrates that in the past three decades, as high-intensity anthropogenic activities have intensified and development and construction boundaries have continued to expand, the demand space has continuously squeezed the supply space, resulting in a decline in ES level and function.

Spatial distribution of ESTSD (a: statistical analysis of ESTSD; b: spatial distribution of the total supply of ESs; c: spatial distribution of the total demand of ESs).

The spatial distributions of the ESTSDs after normalization are shown in Fig. 3b and c. The total supply generally shows a pattern where values are high all around and low in the middle. The high-supply areas are predominantly located along the mountain ranges of Jiuling Mountain in northwestern Gan, Wugong Mountain–Jinggang Mountain in western Gan, Huaiyu Mountain in northeastern Gan, and Wuyishan Mountain in eastern Gan; the area is dominated by mountains, with relatively high degrees of vegetation cover and high levels of CS, SC, and HQ. High-demand regions are predominantly located in central cities and counties around important transport routes, with stronger interference from anthropogenic activities and greater spatial extrusion of ESs.

Analysis of the spatial and temporal evolution of the ESTSD

Analysis of the supply–demand matching of the ESTSD

Spatial differences are significant in terms of matching supply and demand. As shown in Fig. 4a, the H–L region has the largest area, situated chiefly in the northwest, west, south, east and northeast, with a decrease of 12,039.01 km2 in 2020 compared with that in 1990. This is followed by the L–L region, which is situated chiefly in the central and northern regions, with an increase of 6,915.09 km2 in 2020 compared with that in 1990. The smallest areas are the H–H regions, which are predominantly located in the built-up central Nanchang, Jiujiang, Shangrao, and Yingtan urban areas surrounding the region.

Spatial distribution of supply–demand matching and coupling coordination degree of ESTSD (a: supply–demand matching of ESTSD; b: coupling coordination degree of ESTSD).

Analysis of the degree of coupling coordination of the ESTSD

There are obvious spatial differences in the regional CCD. As shown in Fig. 4b, the CCD of the ESTSD shows an overall imbalance as compared to the ideal state, chiefly in the northwestern, western, southern, eastern and northeastern regions, which are more similar to H–L distribution areas; the MI area is the largest, with values of 61507.58 km2, 54,924.33 km2, 58,089.23 km2, and 53169.49 km2 for the years 1990–2020, respectively. The HC and MC areas are more similar to the areas with high ES provisions, and of these, the HC areas are the smallest, with values of 457.26 km2, 514.48 km2, 2,361.78 km2, and 3,150.5 km2, respectively, showing an annually increasing trend. Compared with that in 1990, the changes in area in 2020 were greater in the MI and SI areas, with decreases of 8338.09 km2 and increases of 7076.19 km2, respectively.

Analysis of the gradient effect of the ESTSD

As shown in Fig. 5, the vertical gradient effect of supply‒demand matching is significant and shows similar gradient effects for the slope, DEM, and topographic indices. In terms of the area distribution, H–H, L–L, and L–H are predominantly gradient level I. H–L varies greatly across the three vertical gradients. We find that as the gradient level increases, the topographic gradient distribution index of the H–H and L–H regions decrease sharply, clearly indicating that there is a high demand for ESs on smaller slopes and at lower elevations. The topographic gradient distribution index of the H–L region increases, with the largest index (2.52) occurring in 2020, which shows that H–L is advantageous in regions with large slopes and high elevations. In contrast, the topographic gradient distribution index of the L–L region increases but then decreases, and it is highest at gradient level II, which indicates that L–L is advantageous in regions with a greater slope and higher elevation.

The vertical gradient effect of the CCD is significant, and similar gradient effects are also exhibited for the slope, DEM and topographic indices. In terms of area distribution, the HC, MC, and BC areas are predominantly confined along gradient I; MI is more different on the three vertical gradients, and SI is predominantly located on gradients Ⅰ and Ⅱ. With increasing gradient level, the topographic gradient distribution index of the HC and MC regions decreases sharply, which can be seen in the regions with smaller slopes and lower elevations, and the HC and MC regions are advantageously distributed. The topographic gradient distribution index of the BC regions decreases first but then increases, and the topographic gradient distribution index of the level Ⅴ gradient is the greatest, which can be seen in the areas with large slopes and high elevations in which BC areas are advantageously distributed. The topographic gradient distribution index of the MI and SI regions first increases but then decreases, and the topographic gradient distribution index reaches the highest values at the Ⅳ and Ⅱ levels, respectively, clearly indicating that the supply–demand imbalance in regions with large slopes and high elevations is dominant.

Delineation of integrated ecological management zones for multilevel objectives

Delineation of strategic guidance zones

From the perspectives of ecosystem conservation, health and sustainability, combined with the analysis of ESTSD matching, four strategic guidance zones have been delineated. The H–L area shows ecological redundancy, with numerous nature reserves distributed in the area and irreplaceable biodiversity and ecosystem functions, and it is classified as an ecologically important conservation zone (EICZ) so that it can be utilized at the lowest cost to achieve core habitat protection. In the L–L area, the supply capacity is low, but the demand is also limited, the regional ecosystem is more sensitive, there are potential ecological risks, and disturbances such as anthropogenic activities may lead to severe ecological degradation; thus, it is classified as an ecologically vulnerable conservation zone (EVCZ). H–H reflects the state of supply‒demand tension, with high potential for ES supply but with the challenge of high-intensity human activities and the need to find a balance and sustainability between maintaining ecosystem integrity and meeting human well-being; thus, H–H is classified as an ecologically sustainable utilization zone (ESUZ). The L–H area presents a state of ecological deficit, with an obviously insufficient supply of ESs and degradation of ecological function structure, and these regions urgently need to implement ecological restoration projects to enhance the resilience of the ecosystems; thus, these are classified as ecological restoration and optimization zone (EROZ). Overall, from the perspective of the ecological governance logic revealed by the differences in the ESTSDs, the four first-level strategic guidance zones follow the gradient governance paradigm of protection–prevention–control–restoration; i.e., the EICZ emphasizes protection to enhance ecological quality, the EVCZ emphasizes risk prevention, the ESUZ emphasizes regulation to maintain the dynamic balance between protection and utilization, and the EROZ emphasizes restoration to compensate for ecological shortcomings.

Delineation of regulatory zones

The coupled coordination of the ESTSD is considered an indicator to quantify the steady state or critical state of the system, after which the zonal regulation strategy is usually clarified. Each strategic guiding zone was thus subdivided into 5 secondary zones on the basis of the CCD: High coordination (HC) indicates that the regional ESTSD is in a steady state, has the highest stability, and is classified as a stability zone (SZ). Medium coordination (MC) indicates that the regional supply or demand is slightly high and has the potential to achieve a high degree of coordination by optimizing supply or demand; thus, this zone is classified as an optimization zone (OZ). Basic coordination (BC) indicates that the supply–demand gap is approaching the critical threshold at the edge of the steady state, and a small amount of perturbation may lead to destabilization; thus, monitoring and early warning are necessary, and this zone is classified as a monitoring zone (MZ). Moderate imbalance (MI) indicates that there is a gap in regional supply, which has caused localized degradation of ecological functions, and it is necessary to implement buffer measures to prevent further aggravation of degradation; thus, this zone is classified as a buffer zone (BZ). A severe imbalance (SI) indicates that the supply is significantly lower than the demand, and ecological restoration and demand management need to be strengthened to restore the system to a safe and stable state; thus, the zone is classified as a restoration zone (RZ). Overall, these five secondary control zones follow the spatial governance and control paradigm of stabilization–optimization–monitoring–buffering–recovery, reflecting the five stages of ecosystem homeostasis: maintenance, approach, criticality, transition and destabilization.

Identification of the dominant functions of ESs

In accordance with the results of the SOM clustering identification, the region was separated into 5 ESBs characterizing the dominant functions of the ESs, as shown in Fig. 6. ESB1 is defined as demonstrating overall function optimization (including water conservation, carbon sink, SC, HQ, etc.), where ESs are more integrated, emphasizing multifunctional synergistic optimization. ESB2 is defined as carbon sink and water conservation, ESB3 is defined as water conservation and eco-agriculture demonstration, ESB4 is defined as eco-agriculture demonstration and water ecosystem maintenance, and ESB5 is defined as water conservation and water ecosystem maintenance. Synergistic optimization occurs through the combination of two or more ecosystem service dominant functions. ESB1 has the largest area and is predominantly located in the northwestern, western, southern, eastern and northeastern parts of the region and are more similar to the distribution areas of H–L. In 2020, ESB1 had the largest area, 82,328 km2, which increased by 4925.36 km2 compared with that in 1990. ESB2 and ESB3 show large decreases in areas (4824.4 km2 and 2007.29 km2, respectively), and the area of ESB4 is the smallest (2,072.11 km2 in 2010).

Thresholds and scale effects of the factors influencing ESs

Optimal thresholds on the basis of constraint lines

As shown in Fig. 8, the overall DEM, slope, PRE, EVP, SSD, RHU, TEM, and NDVI reveal humped patterns. After the optimal thresholds are reached, the ESs decrease as the factor values increase. For the optimal thresholds from 1990 to 2020, the DEM increases and then decreases (1441.01 m, 1451.47 m, 1420.41 m, and 1402.49 m); the slope increases and then decreases (24.26°, 24.70°, 24.23°, and 23.60°); the PRE increases and then decreases (2206.02 mm, 2307.70 mm, 3022.50 mm, and 2244.02 mm); and the EVP decreases annually (1087.51 mm, 1039.00 mm, 906.43 mm, and 785.36 mm). Furthermore, the SSD shows a W-shaped fluctuation trend (1667.03 h, 1522.64 h, 1739.36 h, and 1519.18 h); the RHU also shows a W-shaped fluctuation trend (80.64%, 83.05%, 82.87%, and 84.48%); the TEM first decreases and then increases (11.24 °C, 10.55 °C, 8.82 °C, and 12.20 °C); the NDVI has the largest R2 values (all above 0.95), and the thresholds first increase and then decrease (0.54, 0.61, 0.60, and 0.56). The constraint lines of the GDP and POP show negative convexity; i.e., ESs decrease with increasing values; thus, there is no threshold value.

Scaling effects of thresholds

According to the results of the threshold comparison shown in Fig. 9, the optimal thresholds of the DEM, slope, and PRE gradually decrease with increasing spatial scale. The optimal thresholds of the EVP, SSD, RHU, and NDVI factors increase but then decrease, with no obvious change rule. The optimal thresholds of the TEM gradually increase. Combined with the results of the constraint line fitting comparisons, the R2 values overall gradually decrease as the scale increases, which shows that influence factor thresholds are more significantly affected by the scale effect. However, in individual-scale comparisons, a few R2 values also increase.

Discussion

Significant differences in ESTSD

A comprehensive clarification of ESs and their spatial and temporal distributions is beneficial for the management of ecological health56. The quantification of the total supply of ESs through FP, WY, CS, WP, SC, and HQ is representative and typical1. POP, GDP, NL and LUI reflect the demand for ESs by socioeconomic systems in terms of population distribution and size, economic scale and activity, and disturbance intensity, respectively, and can be used as effective indicators for spatially quantifying the total demand for ESs43–48. The ESTSD values in the region are low overall, indicating a normal distribution. Owing to the impact of variations in the individual evaluation results of each ES type and the uncertainty of rainfall amount and distribution57, the total supply is generally low. The average value of total demand does not reach 0.1, indicating a skewed distribution with large differences in total demand. As the high-demand regions are predominantly concentrated in cities, developed towns and densely populated regions and the spatial demand in most areas is low, the total demand is relatively low, but the difference is large. The spatial pattern of the ESTSD can reflect the regional supply‒demand orientation.

Spatial heterogeneity in ecosystem service supply and demand characteristics

The four-quadrant method50 and coupling coordination degree model51,58 reflect the spatial matching between the ESTSD and the level of development of coupling coordination. The spatial heterogeneity of supply–demand matching and coupling coordination was significant. The H–L area was the largest, followed by the L–L area. The CCD of the ESTSD shows an overall imbalance as compared with the ideal state and is more similar to the H–L distribution area. The HC and MC areas are more similar to areas with high supplies of ESs.

The gradient effect is a phenomenon prevalent in the natural and social sciences, and it is important for understanding the properties and behavior of a system59. Supply and demand matching and the CCD in the vertical space gradient characteristics are significant and have a certain degree of harmony. The regions with high ES demand are predominantly located within low-level vertical gradients, i.e., small slopes and low elevations, which is usually because the areas with high demand are strongly related to anthropogenic activities. Therefore, the high-demand regions are distributed in an advantageous way. According to the conclusions of existing studies, the NDVI plays a primary role60 among influencing factors; the vegetation cover of the forest is relatively high and is predominantly located in relatively high terrain and relatively steep slopes, so the regions with low supply are predominantly located on relatively gradual slopes and low terrain. Coordinated regions are predominantly located on slopes and low terrains, in which the advantages of the HC and MC are more significantly distributed, primarily because the relative stability and ecological processes in these areas are relatively complete and because the supply and demand can be coordinated to improve the CCD. Imbalanced areas are predominantly located on steep slopes and high terrain, with MI areas having more significant distributions of advantages in these areas, primarily because the total supply is high in these areas, while the total demand is low, so the contradiction is more prominent, and the CCD still has room for improvement.

Multiobjective integrated ecological management zones are conducive to fine-tuned ecological protection and management

The use of EMZs is key for optimizing territorial space and promoting sustainable development61. The ESTSD reflects the coordinated development of natural and socioeconomic conditions62,63, and it is reasonable and effective to use the ESTSD relationship as a foundation for the delineation of ecological management zones64. In this study, the IEMZs are delineated in accordance with the ‘supply–demand–coordination–function’ and the multiobjective ‘strategic guidance–zoning control–functional guidance’ frameworks. The four-quadrant method was used to delineate the first-level zones to define the direction of strategic guidance. The CCD model has been employed to integrate the surplus and deficit conditions of the ESTSD65 and thus delineate secondary zoning to specify the direction of regulation in each region. The SOM method was used to pinpoint the primary functions of the ESs. This will help us understand regional functional strengths and weaknesses and thus strengthen regional ES function guidance. Overall, in terms of strategic guidance for the first level of zoning, we followed the gradient management paradigm of protection–prevention–regulation–restoration. At the second level, we implemented a zoning control program following the spatial control paradigm of stabilization–optimization–monitoring–buffering–restoration. This zoning framework is essential for defining the strategic objectives of ecological protection, optimizing resource allocation, perfecting ES functions, and implementing ecological protection strategies in accordance with local conditions, which is conducive to regional fine-tuning of ecological protection and management.

Strategic guidance must be strengthened. Ecosystems within the EICZs are generally relatively healthy and stable, with little human activity and high supply redundancy, and existing ecosystem structures and functions should be strictly protected to avoid sudden human interference and destruction while ES reserve areas cope with unforeseen future demands. The ecosystems in the EVCZs are vulnerable to external interference, and supply energy levels, ecosystem integrity, and stability should be enhanced through various measures, such as afforestation and SC. The ecosystems in the ESUZs are relatively healthy, but they are facing relative greater pressure from anthropogenic activities; thus, it is imperative to weigh ecological protection against social progress and to develop ecologically friendly economic activities, such as ecotourism, ecoagriculture, and ecofisheries, to increase the added value of ESs. The contradiction of the ESTSD is more prominent in EROZs, where ecological restoration projects are used to increase supply capacity, optimize efficiency of ES utilization, and strictly limit destructive exploitation of ecosystems.

Zoning control and functional guidelines can aid in fine-tuning management. The ecosystems in the SZ are relatively stable and need to be maintained through various measures; the OZ has some room to improve its coordination and requires optimization and enhancement; the MZ requires the establishment of a dynamic monitoring system and regular monitoring to ensure stability; the BZ has a more prominent contradiction with the ESTSD and requires buffering to reduce pressure; and the RZ has an enormous contradiction between supply and demand and needs to restore ecosystem function through restoration measures. Advantageous functions, such as regional water conservation, carbon sinks, soil conservation, habitat quality, ecological agriculture, water ecosystems and other functional advantages, need to be identified, strengthened and maintained; functional shortcomings need to be combined with the regional realities of local conditions to formulate complementary policies based on the dominant functions of each region.

Differences in thresholds and scale effects of influential factors

According to the impact factor constraint line study, the DEM, slope, PRE, EVP, SSD, RHU, TEM, and NDVI revealed humped patterns, which is consistent with the conclusions drawn in earlier studies23,66. Appropriate conditions of topography, vegetation, and climate are favorable for promoting vegetation growth and CS, SC, and HQ; however, after a certain threshold was exceeded, the total supply tended to decrease after reaching a peak, which may be due to higher elevation and greater slope increasing surface runoff and exacerbating soil erosion, leading to a decrease in SC. Furthermore, excessive PRE, RHU, and vegetation may result in a decrease in soil oxygen content, vegetation decay, and insufficient essential nutrients, leading to impeded vegetation growth, which in turn weakens CS, SC, and HQ. In addition to a high SSD, TEM and EVP can affect vegetation growth, which in turn impedes supply. Anthropogenic factors (GDP and POP) reveal negative convexity, which aligns with the findings of Zhang et al.20. Human activities and expansion lead to a reduction in ecological land and vegetation, resulting in landscape fragmentation and reduced biodiversity, which in turn weakens carbon stocks, food production, soil and water conservation, and habitat quality.

Several studies67,68 have concluded that the importance of ES influences is affected by scale. However, studies on scale differences in optimal thresholds are still limited. It has been reported that there is also a significant scale effect on the thresholds of influencing factors20. As the spatial scale increases, the constraint line fitting R2 decrease, which aligns with the findings of earlier studies69,70. In a large-scale space, the structure of ESs changes, the scale unit contains more service types and more complex ecological processes, and the ecosystem can withstand greater temperature changes but can still maintain relatively stable service functions; thus, the optimal threshold of the TEM increases. The PRE is more sensitive to small, localized areas, but the ESs are maintained through water balance regulation at larger scales; thus, the optimal threshold of the PRE decreases. As the scale increases, the topography and geomorphology become more complex, and ESs realize functional complementarity at different elevations and slopes, which makes ESs more adaptable to changes in topography; thus, the optimal thresholds of the DEM and slope factors decrease.

Research limitations and prospects

The limitations of this study are as follows: First, several typical ES types, FP, WY, CS, WP, SC, and HQ, were considered, which are crucial for human and socioeconomic development. However, several limitations exist in quantifying the above ESs using the InVEST model36,38–40, and the parameter settings refer to existing empirical values with some uncertainties, which should be integrated with the collection of field soil samples, water samples, etc., and experimental observations to verify ES values. In future work, the parameters of the model should be localized by combining experimental observation data, and ecological indicators need to be refined to increase model accuracy71. Second, owing to the limitation of the availability of basic data for the study, the spatial demand of the human society for ESs is affected by the POP, GDP, NL, and LUI, and there are several limitations in this quantification of the demand method, and we only take a national-level ecological civilization pilot demonstration zone in Jiangxi Province as an example. In future studies, the demand measurement of different ES types should be considered1,11,72, and all the ecological civilization demonstration aeras should be considered as the study area. Third, in the process of delineating the integrated ecological management zones, this study considered the factors of ES surplus, deficit, and destabilization to delineate the restoration zones, and these restoration zones should be verified by combining data from the implementation of ecological restoration projects and from the process of the actual work and plan development. Fourth, in the scale effect analysis process, the same set of data acquisition criteria was used; i.e., on the basis of the grid data, the values of larger scales were each extrapolated through the average value, but the data judging criteria and accuracy requirements may differ at different scales. Therefore, in future work, priority should be given to ESTSD assessment methods and spatial management of ecological elements, extend the study area to all ecological civilization demonstration areas, while the richness, completeness and accuracy of the data will provide a scientific basis for ecosystem management.

Conclusion

This study revealed the ESTSD pattern evolution and the gradient effect of the ESTSD on the vertical spatial distribution using the topographic gradient distribution index, and it proposes a multiobjective zoning framework that integrates ‘supply–demand–coordination–function’ and ‘strategic guidance–zoning control–functional guidance’, which achieves integrated management of multilevel objectives. The optimal thresholds of the factors influencing ecosystem services were analyzed via constraint lines, and the threshold scale effect was further explored and enriched. The main conclusions are as follows: The ESTSD values were low overall, and both distributions were more concentrated and had obvious distribution characteristics; the total supply tended to be normally distributed, and the total demand tended to be skewed. The total supply was high all around and low in the middle, and the total demand was high in the middle. The area of H–L was large; the CCD of supply–demand was generally out of balance, and ESB1 had the largest area and was similar to the H–L distribution area. The matching and coupling coordination of the ESTSD showed significant vertical spatial gradient effects. Four strategic guidance zones at the primary level, 5 secondary regulation zones, and 5 advantageous function guidelines were delineated, and measures related to the IEMZs were proposed. The constraint lines of the DEM, slope, PRE, EVP, SSD, RHU, TEM and NDVI revealed a humped pattern, and the constraint lines of GDP and POP showed negative convexity. Spatial scale had a significant effect on the optimal thresholds of the influencing factors. It is proposed that this study will further refine management of regional ecosystems, regulate the contradiction within the ESTSD, realize a multiobjective ecological zoning control, and promote regional coordination and sustainable development.
