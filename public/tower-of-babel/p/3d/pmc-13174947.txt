The effects of black raspberry lozenges on 4-hydroxy-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone-releasing DNA adducts in buccal cells and urinary levels of N′-nitrosonornicotine and its glucuronide in active smokers
Authors: Kun-Ming Chen, Nicolle M Krebs, Yuan-Wan Sun, Dongxiao Sun, Jiangang Liao, Lisa Reinhart, Susan R Mallery, Karam El-Bayoumy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tobacco constituent, N′-nitrosonornicotine (NNN) is a potential etiological agent in the development of oral cancer. NNN is metabolically activated to form DNA adducts that release 4-hydroxy-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (HPB) which was detected at higher levels in the oral cavity of smokers than non-smokers. Black raspberry (BRB) has been proposed as a preventive agent of oral cancer. In a phase 0 clinical trial, 40 active smokers (16 males, 24 females) consumed 5 g of BRB lozenges daily for 8 weeks. Each participant served as his/her own control. Buccal cells and urine were collected at baseline, during BRB administration, and washout period. In all smokers, BRB reduced, but not significantly, the levels of HPB-releasing DNA adducts (Baseline: 0.97 ± 0.51 and End-BRB: 0.61 ± 0.23 HPB/106 Guanine). A sex-specific response to BRB was observed: BRB had no effects in men, but in 16 of 24 women, BRB significantly reduced the levels of HPB. We further tested the hypothesis that detoxification of NNN via N-glucuronidation by BRB may, in part, account for the inhibition of HPB levels. The ratio of NNN-N-glucuronide to free NNN significantly increased (P = 0.028) from 1.29 ± 0.40 (baseline) to 2.77 ± 0.76 (End-BRB) in all 40 women as well as in female responders from 1.24 ± 0.60 (baseline) to 2.87 ± 1.00, but not in non-responders. The metabolic changes of NNN observed in our clinical trial indicate that BRB is a promising candidate for cancer interception and prevention of oral cancer in some, but not in all active smokers.

Introduction

Tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, oncogenic human papilloma virus infection, and betel quid are the causative agents in the development of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [1-6]. The most predominant type of HNSCC is oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [1, 6]. Tobacco smoke is a complex mixture and contains multiple tumor initiators, co-carcinogens, and tumor promotors [7]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear which agents in tobacco smoke contribute to the development OSCC [8]. However, literature data indicate that human oral epithelium possesses the enzymes associated with metabolic activation of selected classes of chemical carcinogens including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and tobacco-specific nitrosamines (TSNAs) leading to the formation of active intermediates that can damage DNA [3, 8-11]. Tobacco smoke is also a rich source of free radicals that can lead to oxidative damage of DNA, proteins, and lipids, which contribute to the initiation and post-initiation phases of carcinogenesis [3].

PAHs are detected in cigarette smoke as well as in numerous environmental sources [12]. We and others have shown that benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P) and dibenzo[a,l]pyrene (DB[a,l]P), found in tobacco smoke, induce OSCC in mice [13, 14]. In fact, we were the first to detect significantly higher levels of DNA adducts derived from B[a]P and DB[a,l]P in buccal cells of active smokers than those found in non-smokers [15] suggesting the contribution of these carcinogens in the development of human HNSCC.

N′-nitrosonornicotine (NNN) (Fig. 1) belongs to the class of TSNA and is present in all tobacco products [16]. NNN has been classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as Group 1 carcinogen to humans [17]. NNN has been shown to induce cancers in multiple organs including esophagus, lung, trachea, nasal, and oral cavity in preclinical models [16, 18]. Metabolic activation of NNN via α-hydroxylation (2’– and 5’– hydroxylation) is primarily catalyzed by P450 2A6 and 3A4. Further, because of its potential to generate active metabolites that can form DNA adducts, 2′– and 5 –hydroxylations are considered to be the major phase I metabolic pathway [16, 19]. Hydroxylation at the 2′-position is catalyzed primary by CYP3A4 and to some extent by CYPs 2A4, 2A5, 2A13. Hydroxylation at the 5′-position is mainly catalyzed by CYP2A6 and to a lesser extent by CYP2E1 and CYP2D6. 2′-Hydroxylation of NNN yields a pyridyloxobutylating intermediate which reacts with DNA to produce a variety of DNA adducts with deoxyguanosine, deoxythymidine, and deoxycytidine. Acid hydrolysis of several pyridyloxobutyl- DNA adducts releases 4-hydroxy-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (HPB, Fig. 1). Thus HPB-releasing DNA adducts are a measure of pyridyloxobutylation of DNA by NNN as well as possibly other TSNA such as 4-(methylnitrosamino)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (NNK) [20]. This assay has been used successfully to provide evidence of tobacco-specific pyridyloxobutylation of DNA in the oral cavity of smokers with and without oral cancer [21-23].

Chemical structures of NNN, NNN-N-Gluc, and HPB.

Human exposure to total NNN can be assessed via quantification of the parent NNN plus its detoxification product NNN-pyridine-N-glucuronide (NNN-N-Gluc) in urine [24]. In fact, a previous prospective case–control study demonstrated that levels of NNN in urine samples collected years before esophageal cancer diagnosis were significantly associated with the risk of this disease in smokers; this study also showed a strong inverse association between the percentage of the detoxification metabolite of NNN-N-Gluc and risk of esophageal cancer [25, 26].

Early detection and prevention are desirable approaches to control this disease since successful treatment of HNSCC at advanced stages remains a significant challenge [3, 9]. A number of promising chemopreventive agents have been reported but their clinical benefits require further investigations [27]. Consumption of diets rich in fruits and vegetables may lower the risk of developing HNSCC and dietary constituents that can inhibit the initiation and progression of the multistep carcinogenesis process are promising candidates for cancer prevention. Several sources of phytochemicals have been proposed, including black raspberry (BRB), as a whole food-based approach, for cancer prevention. BRB has been shown to inhibit DNA damage and OSCC-induced by the tobacco carcinogen, DB[a,l]P in the mouse oral cavity [28]. Furthermore, we recently reported that BRB inhibited covalent DNA damage-induced by B[a]P and oxidative DNA damage-induced by ROS in buccal cells of active smokers [29, 30]. Besides PAHs and ROS, tobacco smoke also contains TSNA, an important class of carcinogens which includes NNN, an established oral carcinogen in the rat. Thus, in this Phase 0 clinical trial, we examined the effects of BRB on the levels HPB-releasing DNA adducts in target tissue buccal cells and determined its effects on urinary NNN and its detoxification metabolite NNN-N-Gluc in active smokers.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Protease K, RNase A, used to digest oral tissues together with other chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Internal Standards including [Pyridine-D4]HPB, [13C2,15N]-guanine and 2,2’,3,4,5,6-13C6-NNN were obtained from Toronto Research Chemicals (Ontario, Canada).

Composition of BRB lozenges

All lozenges were prepared under GMP from a single harvest year of BRB (Rubus occidentalis of the Munger cultivar), which was obtained by BerriHealth, Corvallis, OR, from farms located in Corbett and Sandy, OR. Inert ingredients and binders in each lozenge, include 150 mg organic honey crystal, 150 mg organic rice extract, cellulose, and inorganic elements (copper, magnesium). Lozenge is round (7.43 mm × 22.79 mm) and possesses a dark reddish-purple color with a total mass of 1.3 g each. The composition of constituents, including vitamins, minerals, anthocyanosides, free anthocyanidins, ellagic acid, and quercetin dihydrate, was analyzed by Eurofins Food Chemistry Testing (Madison, WI). The analysis revealed that the total free anthocyanidins and anthocyanosides in our formulation is 41.8 mg/serving size; more details have been recently reported by us [30].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We recruited 40 smokers (16 males, 24 females; 21–75 years old; Supplementary Table S1) from a tobacco user research registry which was maintained at the Penn State College of Medicine in Hershey, Pennsylvania. This Phase 0 clinical trial is designed in a way that every participant serves as his (or her) own internal control and assists by using an internal control for those who, by virtue of their medical conditions, need to take medications such as anti-inflammatory agents on a daily basis. The possible effects of daily meloxicam and low-dose ASA for these participants are already recorded on their baseline levels. Since we are looking at changes to the baseline levels, those participants who take daily medications should not be a problem.

Study design

The Penn State University Institutional Review Board (IRB#13621) approved the design (Fig. 2) of this trial, and it is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04372914). As reported by us [29, 30], each smoker took 1 lozenge, five times/day and was instructed not to eat or drink 30 min following lozenge use. Each lozenge contains 1 g BRB freeze dried powder. The dose of BRB was identical to that used in our previous trials and comparable to that used previously in the form of troches [29-31]. Our clinical coordinator instructed all participants to consume 5 BRB lozenges per day for 8 weeks and not to swallow or crush them. Smokers’ urine samples were collected at baseline 1 week after being enrolled (week 1), followed by mid-BRB (week 5), end-BRB (week 9), and a washout period (week 13). The volume of urine collected at each time point (12 ml) was sufficient for NNN, NNN-N-Gluc, cotinine, and creatinine analysis. For the HPB-releasing adduct analysis, we conducted two sample collections (1 week apart) for each buccal cell sample to ensure an adequate amount of DNA (5 µg) as follows: baseline (weeks 0, 1), the middle of BRB administration (weeks 4, 5), the end of BRB administration (weeks 8, 9), and wash out period after BRB cessation (weeks 12, 13). Following collections, all samples were aliquoted and kept in a −80°C freezer.

The timepoints for the analysis of HPB-releasing DNA adducts in buccal cells and NNN, NNN-N-Gluc, cotinine, and creatinine in urine of smokers.

Buccal cell collection and DNA isolation

The procedure for buccal cell collection was recently reported by us [29, 30]. In brief, participants rinsed their mouths twice with water at the beginning of the trial. Then, they brush the inside of the mouth up and down on each side of the cheek for a duration of 1 min using a soft bristle toothbrush. Approximately 20 ml of saline was used for rinsing (SALJET; Winchester Laboratories LLC; St. Charles, IL) for 2 min before collection using a 50 ml centrifuge tube. A phenol-chloroform extraction method was used for DNA isolation from oral buccal cells [32]. Buccal cells were first suspended in 200 μl alkaline lysis buffer followed by the addition of proteinase K. These buccal cells were kept in an incubator for 3–4 h under 58°C; then 300 μl of Phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol mix (25:24:1, pH 6.7) was added to the solution. After centrifugation for 5 min at 13 000 rpm, the top aqueous layer was collected, followed by adding sodium acetate buffer (to a final concentration of 0.3 M, pH 5.2) and ethanol to precipitate DNA. The resulting pellet was further washed with 70% ethanol before being stored at −20°C.

Analysis of HPB—releasing DNA adducts

DNA isolation, hydrolysis, and SPE purification

The analysis of HPB in human oral buccal cells was conducted by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) based on a published procedure [22]. The DNA was subjected to acid hydrolysis by the addition of 5 N HCl for 3–4 h under 80°C to release HPB after the addition of 200 pg of [Pyridine-D4] HPB and 15 ng of [13C2, 15N] guanine as internal standards. The samples were then neutralized with the addition of 1N NaOH, followed by SPE purification using a Hypersep Hypercarb cartridges (25 mg, Thermo Scientific, Rockwood, TN). The SPE column was activated with 2 ml of methanol, followed with 2 ml of D-H2O before loading samples. The fraction containing HPB was eluted with 65% methanol, and the fraction containing guanine was eluted with 1% TFA in methanol.

LC-MS/MS analysis of HPB

HPB was analyzed using a Sciex 6500 Q Trap mass spectrometry coupled with a Sciex EXion HPLC separation system (Mass Spectrometry and Proteomics Core Facility; RRID:SCR_017831). A 1.7 μm Acquity UPLC BEH C18 analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, Ireland) was used for the analysis of HPB. The gradient elution was conducted using a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min with the following conditions: Initiate in 2% mobile phase B (acetonitrile) and 98% solvent A (5 MM ammonium formate), a linear gradient to 30% mobile phase B in 4 min, and keep the 30% mobile phase B for 0.5 min, then gradient to 70% mobile phase B in 0.5 min, and keep for 0.5 min before returning to the initial conditions. The Sciex 6500 Q Trap mass spectrometer was equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) probe operated in positive mode. The decluster potential (DP) was 60 V, the entrance potential (EP) was 10 V, the collision energy (CE) was 27 V, and the collision cell exit potential (CXP) was 12 V, while the curtain gas (CUR) was 35 l/h, the collision gas (CAD) was set at medium. The ionspray voltage was 5500 V, the temperature was 400°C, the ion source gas 1 was 40 l/h, and the ion source gas 2 was 40 l/h. The multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used to analyze and quantify HPB and internal standard, with the transitions of m/z 166 >106 for HPB, 170 >110 for internal standard. All peaks were integrated and quantified by Sciex OS 3.0 software.

LC-MS/MS analysis of guanine

The analysis of guanine was conducted on an API 3200™ LC-MS/MS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer which is interfaced with an Agilent 1200 series HPLC using an Agilent extend-C18 (5 um, 4.6 × 150 mm). ESI was performed in the positive mode. The MS parameters used are as follows: electrospray source temperature was 400°C and voltage used was 5 kV; parameters such as the declustering potential (DP), CE, EP, and cell exit potential (CXP) were 41, 31, 12, and 4 eV, respectively; the collision activated dissociation (CAD) gas was 5 psi, and CUR was 20 psi. The elution program was 200 μl/min gradient using solvent A (methanol containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (water containing 0.1% formic acid). The gradient was maintained at 10% A for 5 min, and from 10% A to 70% A in 5 min, followed by 70% A for another 5 min, continued from 70% A to 100% A in 5 min, and 100% A was held for another 10 min. Guanine was monitored in MRM mode. The MS/MS transitions of m/z 152→ m/z 135 and m/z 155→ m/z 138 were monitored for guanine and internal standard, respectively.

LC-MS/MS analysis of urinary NNN and NNN-N-Gluc

Analyses of NNN and its detoxification product, NNN-N-Gluc, in urine samples were conducted by LC-MS/MS based on published methods with modification [33, 34]. This typically involves the extraction of 1.5 ml of urine samples with 4 ml of ethyl acetate (2×) after adding 2 ng of 2,2′,3,4,5,6-13C6-NNN as internal standard. The organic layer, containing free NNN, was concentrated under a mild stream of N2 and purified by solid phase extraction (SPE) using an Oasis HLB column (1 cm3, 30 mg, Waters Ltd.). For the aqueous layer, which contains NNN-N-Gluc, another 2 ng of 2,2’,3,4,5,6-13C6-NNN was added, and the mixture was treated with 0.35 ml of 10 N NaOH at 80°C for 30 min to convert NNN-N-Gluc to NNN before neutralization. Subsequently, the mixture was neutralized with 1 ml 10× PBS and 0.7 ml of 5 N HCl followed by SPE purification as in the above. The purified samples were then analyzed using LC-MS/MS with MRM method.

Specifically, NNN analysis was carried out by a Sciex 6500 Q Trap mass spectrometry which is coupled with a Sciex EXion HPLC separation system. Generally, a 1.7 μm Acquity UPLC BEH C18 analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, Ireland) was used for the separation. The gradient elution was conducted using a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min with the following conditions: Initiate in 1% mobile phase B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) and 99% solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and keep it for 1.5 min, then a linear gradient to 95% mobile phase B in 1.5 min, and keep the 95% mobile phase B for 2 min before going back to the initial conditions.

The Sciex 6500 Q Trap mass spectrometer was equipped with an ESI probe operated in positive mode. DP was 35 V, EP was 8 V, CE was 14.4 V, and CXP was 14 V, while CUR was 35 L/h, CAD was low. The ionspray voltage was 5500 V, the temperature was 400°C, the ion source gas 1 was 20 L/h, and the ion source gas 2 was 20 L/h. NNN and internal standard 13C6-NNN were quantified, with the transitions of m/z 178 >148 for NNN, 184 >154 for 13C6-NNN. All peaks were integrated and quantified by Sciex OS 3.0 software. The ratio of levels of NNN-N-Gluc to free NNN in the urine was calculated as an indication of glucuronidation of NNN in the urine.

Creatinine and cotinine analysis

Using an established assay [35], creatinine levels in the urine were analyzed spectrophotometrically following a reaction between creatinine and alkaline picrate. A volume of 50 μl of each sample was mixed with 200 μl of 0.12% of picric acid in 0.15 N sodium hydroxide in a 96 well plate. Samples were incubated at room temperature for a period of 30 min, followed by the measurement with a microplate reader (Biotek Synergy HTX) at the absorbance at 490 nm. Creatinine concentration in each sample was determined using a creatinine standard curve (0.01–0.1 mg/ml).

The cotinine levels in the urine were determined using a commercial competitive enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) kit (Calbiotech, El Cajon, CA) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Cotinine is typically measured by LC-MS/MS method which is more sensitive than ELISA. Our study included only smokers and thus sensitivity is not a limiting factor. Therefore, ELISA was deemed sufficient in the present study. Briefly, samples were added in duplicate to wells coated with an antibody to cotinine. Enzyme conjugate was added, and the plates were incubated for an hour at room temperature. All wells were washed. After the addition of a substrate reagent, the plate was further incubated for 20 min. Subsequently, we added the stop solution and measured the absorbance at 450 nm. The concentration of cotinine was then calculated based on the standard curve generated from the manufacturer provided standards.

Statistical analysis

In this design, each participant is measured at 4 time points: Baseline (weeks 0 and 1), during the middle of BRB administration (weeks 4 and 5), at the end of BRB administration (weeks 8 and 9), and wash out period after BRB cessation (weeks 12 and 13). In this cohort design, every participant’s baseline values serve as their personal internal control from which to compare subsequent changes. This design helps account for the extensive inter-individual heterogeneity inherent to humans and enhances the statistical power. Collectively, for all endpoints, their distribution at baseline, mid-BRB, end-BRB, and washout periods was first inspected using boxplots; cube root, square root or log transformation was then applied to each endpoint so that its distribution is more symmetric and closer to a normal distribution. The change of these endpoints at each later period, respectively, from their corresponding value at the baseline was calculated in the transformed scale. The t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to test whether each change deviates from 0 in mean or median.

Results

Compliance

As described in the literature [36], the rate of compliance to BRB usage was determined based on the following equation and the participants are deemed compliant if their rates were ≥80% [36]: Rate of compliance = (number of lozenges provided—number of lozenges returned)/(number of lozenges instructed to take per day × number of days until return to the study). Based on this approach, the compliance rate was more than 97%.

Analysis of HPB-releasing DNA adducts

Representative chromatograms of the LC-MS/MS analysis of HPB-releasing DNA adducts and guanine (as an internal standard) are shown in Fig. 3a, and b, respectively. The HPB analysis is linear from 0–40 pg based on our calibration curve. Our lower limit of quantitation (LLOQs) for HPB is about 20 fg.

Representative chromatograms of HPB-releasing DNA adducts (a) and guanine (b) obtained from stable isotope dilution HPLC-MS/MS analysis of DNA adducts isolated from human oral buccal cells.

The baseline level of HPB-releasing DNA adducts (mean ± SE) in buccal cell of all 40 participants (males = 16, females = 24) before the administration of BRB was determined to be 0.97 ± 0.51 HPB/106 Guanine. At the middle and end of BRB consumption, the levels of HPB-releasing adducts were 0.57 ± 0.10, and 0.61 ± 0.23 HPB/106 guanine, respectively. The corresponding value at the washout period was 1.66 ± 0.95 HPB/106 guanine. These results demonstrate that during BRB consumption, the levels of HPB-releasing DNA adducts were reduced but not significant in buccal cells of 40 active smokers (Fig. 4a).

The effects of BRB on HPB-releasing adducts in smokers: men and women combined (a), men (b), and women (c).

The effects of BRB on HPB-releasing adducts as a function of sex difference

Men have a higher incidence of oral cancer than women [6]. Therefore, in this study, we analyzed the effect of BRB on HPB-releasing adducts in men and women separately. In men as compared to baseline, BRB exerted a significant increase of HPB-releasing adduct at 4 weeks (P = 0.041) but at 8 weeks and the washout periods, the levels were not significantly different from those at baseline (Fig. 4b). The baseline level of HPB-releasing DNA adducts in buccal cell of all male participants before BRB administration was determined to be 0.36 ± 0.08 HPB/106 guanine. At the middle, and end of BRB consumption, and at the washout period, the levels were 0.55 ± 0.13, 0.41 ± 0.10, and 0.47± 0.15, respectively. However, in women during the BRB administration, the levels of HPB-releasing adducts were reduced but did not reach significance (Fig. 4c). The baseline level of HPB-releasing DNA adducts in buccal cell of all female participants before BRB administration was determined to be 1.39 ± 0.84. At the middle, and end of BRB consumption, and at the washout period, the levels were 0.58 ± 0.11, 0.74 ± 0.29, and 2.45± 1.21, respectively. The effect of BRB on HPB-releasing adducts in each individual women (Fig. 5, spaghetti plot) suggested to us that it is possible that among the 24 women, some responded to BRB while others did not. In fact, after 8 weeks of BRB administration, we observed that 16 women responded to BRB as a significant inhibitor of HPB-reducing DNA adducts (Fig. 5b).

The effect of BRB on HPB-releasing adducts on individual women (24 female participants) (a) and on 16 responders (b).

The effects of BRB on urinary NNN and NNN-N-Gluc

Strong scientific evidence indicates sex-specific differences between males and females in the incidence of cancers including HNSCC [6]. These variations may be attributed, in part, to differences in the expression and/or activities of Phase I and Phase II enzymes responsible for metabolic activation and detoxification of tobacco carcinogens, including NNN [37-46]. Thus, we hypothesize that, compared to non-responders, the 16 women who responded to BRB with reduced HPB-releasing adducts may reflect enhanced NNN detoxification, resulting in higher levels of urinary NNN-N-Glu compared to those in non-responders. Therefore, we tested this hypothesis by comparing the ratios of urinary NNN-N-Gluc to free NNN in female smokers in both responders vs non-responders. The analyses were performed using urine collected at baseline and at end of BRB administration. The NNN analysis is linear from 0–200 pg based on our calibration curve. Our LLOQs for NNN is about 200 fg.

We found that among the 24 female subjects, free NNN was detected in the urine of 23 of them, 15 of them (responders) had lower levels of HPB-releasing adduct at the end of BRB administration compared with their own baseline level, while the other 8 are considered as non-responders. Representative chromatograms of the LC-MS/MS analysis of NNN are shown in Fig. 6a. We found that in responders, the ratio of urinary NNN-N-Gluc to free NNN at baseline is 1.24 ± 0.60 (mean ± SE), and the corresponding value at the end-BRB administration is significantly increased to 2.87 ± 1.00 (P = 0.022). This ratio in the non-responders is increased from 1.39 ± 0.34 to 2.59 ± 1.18, but not significant (P = 0.74) (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, in all 23 female smokers, BRB administration resulted in a significant (P = 0.028) increase in the ratio from 1.29 ± 0.40 (baseline) to 2.77 ± 0.76 (end-BRB).

Representative chromatograms of NNN obtained from stable isotope dilution HPLC-MS/MS analysis of urine from participants (a). The effect of BRB on urinary N-glucuronidation in responders, non-responders, and all women participants (b).

Levels of cotinine and creatinine in Smokers’ urine

The levels of cotinine were expressed as ng/mg creatinine. Throughout the duration of this trial, we found that BRB had no significant effect on urinary cotinine levels of our participants (Supplementary Figure S1). In addition, cotinine levels (mean ± SD) at baseline and after BRB administration for 8 weeks were not significantly different between responders and non-responders.

Levels of cotinine are negatively correlated with reduced levels of HPB by BRB in responders

We ran a series of correlation tests on the combined HPB DNA adduct-cotinine level datasets. We also evaluated whether or not BRB treatment affected this possible correlation. We tried multiple iterations that included (i) all baseline levels—does smoking increase HPB adducts, (ii) group analysis of all the matched data pairs, (iii) mid treatment to determine if BRB modulates the possible correlation, (iv) end of treatment, and (5) wash out period. However, in every assessment run, the rho values were extremely low and the P values were not significant because of the large inter-individual differences in values of these biomarkers. Therefore, further analysis was conducted in both responders and non-responders by expressing the data as the percentage change (from baseline to 8 weeks) that allows every subject to serve as their own internal control. All datasets were initially evaluated for normality via a Shapiro Wilks test. If a normal distribution was determined, we employed a Pearson r correlation, while the comparable non-parametric Spearman rho correlation was used for data that were not normally distributed. Results of these correlation analyses between the percent change in cotinine levels and the percent change in levels of HPB-releasing adducts in responders who demonstrated an increase in cotinine levels with concurrent reduction in HPB DNA adducts at the conclusion of the 8 week study period demonstrated a strong negative correlation i.e. increased cotinine-reduced HPB-releasing adducts (Pearson r −0.895, two-tail test, P = 0.003).

Discussion

We recently showed that BRB lozenges significantly inhibited the formation of both B[a]P-induced covalent DNA adduct and oxidative DNA damage measured as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine in buccal cells of active smokers. However, the effect of BRB on DNA damage-induced by a representative example of TSNA in smokers’ oral cavity has never been examined. Thus, in this study, we used the LC-MS/MS method for accurate quantification of HPB-releasing DNA adducts in buccal cells of smokers before (baseline), during BRB administration (4 and 8 weeks of BRB administration) and after BRB cessation (washout period, 4 weeks post BRB administration).

It has been shown that the oral cavity and esophagus are more susceptible to the carcinogenic effect of NNN in the rat [18, 47]. Furthermore, in a prospective Shanghai cohort study, Yuan et al. showed that levels of NNN and NNN-N-Gluc are strongly associated with esophageal cancer risk in smokers [25]. Pyridyloxobutylation of DNA by NNN and by other TSNA (e.g. NNK) can be accurately measured by HPB-releasing DNA adducts in human exfoliated oral mucosa cells and such levels were significantly higher in smokers with HNSCC than in smokers without the disease [48]. The target oral epithelial cells can be collected noninvasively to assess the extent of DNA damage in smokers; DNA damage is a reliable biomarker for both carcinogen exposure as well as cancer risk and can be quantitatively assessed by LC-MS/MS methods.

As shown in Fig. 4a and 5a, the level of HPB/106 guanine at baseline was 0.97 ± 0.51 (mean ± SE) which clearly demonstrates the interparticipant variability. Therefore, the design of this Phase 0 trial was aimed at comparing the effect of BRB using each participant as his (her) internal control. The results show that using 5 g of BRB per day for 8 weeks reduced, but not significantly, the levels of HPB-releasing DNA adducts in buccal cells of 40 smokers.

Given the higher incidence and mortality of HNSCC in men than in women, we next analyzed the effect of BRB on HPB-releasing adducts separately in the 24 women and 16 men participating in the trial. In men, HPB-releasing adduct levels increased after 4 weeks of BRB administration. However, by the end of the 8-week administration period and following the 4-week washout period, such levels were comparable to those observed at baseline. In women, HPB-releasing adduct levels were consistently lower than baseline during the 4 and 8 weeks of BRB administration, although this reduction was not statistically significant. Upon close examination, despite the high interparticipant variation in the levels of HPB-releasing adducts, the results suggested that only a subset of women responded to BRB treatment. However, in our previous report [29, 30, 48], we showed that BRB lozenges at the same dose used in the present study, significantly reduced DNA adduct levels derived from B[a]P in male but not in female smokers. In fact, Mallery and colleagues showed that topical application of the oral cavity with 10% BRB bioadhesive gel, despite large interpatient variation, reduced the prevalence of loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in a subset of patients with oral intraepithelial neoplasia lesions with 41% demonstrating a favorable response. While these data may reflect differences in individual patient gene expression profiles, the prospect that BRB induced programmed cell death in dysplastic keratinocytes was also entertained [49-52]. Furthermore, a previous study demonstrated that short-term administration of BRB troches, at a comparable dose used in our study, given to current OSCC patients prior to surgery resulted in a significant reduction of several markers of inflammation involved in disease progression [31]. There were patient-level variations in these markers that can assist in the identification of responders vs. non-responders; such information can be highly useful in the design of future interception/prevention trials using BRB. In fact, upon further statistical analysis, our result demonstrated that BRB remains to be an effective inhibitor of HPB-releasing adducts even at higher levels of cigarette consumption measures as cotinine levels among responders.

Clearly, the present study and those reported in the literature support that not all subjects will respond to the protective role of BRB equally which may, in part, be due to genetic predisposition. Other factors could include variations in keratinocyte BRB uptake and the capacity of the target cells to retain BRB through enteric recycling [53].

Cancer-preventive activity of BRB can be attributed to its combined effects including inhibiting Phase I enzymes, inducing Phase II enzymes, and enhancing DNA repair [9, 54-58]. As previously described, α-hydroxylation of NNN is considered the major metabolic activation of this carcinogen [16, 19]. However, N-glucuronidation is considered a detoxification pathway of NNN and is catalyzed by UGT2B10 [43]. Literature data provide convincing evidence which indicates sex-specific differences in males and females regarding Phase I and II enzymes. For example, Phase I enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2A6) involved in the metabolic activation of NNN are more active in females than males. However, most of the Phase II enzymes (UGT) have higher activities in men than women [44]. Clearly, the balance between Phase I and Phase II metabolism and likely the DNA repair activity in men vs. women are contributing factors on the extend of DNA damage induced by NNN in the oral cavity of smokers. While sex differences in DNA repair have been reported, the findings remain inconsistent [59]. BRB and some of its constituents are known to inhibit Phase I enzymes and to induce Phase II enzymes as well as enhance DNA repair capacity [56]. Nevertheless, our results suggest that BRB administration enhances the detoxification of NNN in women through the formation of NNN-N-Gluc, a mechanism that may lead to cancer interception. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that BRB enhances NNN-N-glucuronidation which we showed that in some women can lead to the reduction of HPB-releasing adduct. However, we acknowledge the limitations of our study; we did not examine the role of BRB on genetic polymorphisms in the uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferase gene including UGT2B10 known to be involved in the glucuronidation of NNN [43]. We also did not examine the effect of BRB on Phase I and DNA repair enzymes which are involved in the formation of DNA damages induced by TSNA. Nevertheless, the results of this study under the conditions of our Phase 0 clinical trial indicate that BRB is a promising candidate for cancer interception and prevention of OSCC in some but not all active smokers.
