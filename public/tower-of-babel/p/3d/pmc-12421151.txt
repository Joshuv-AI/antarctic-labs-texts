Superior vena cava isolation added to pulmonary vein isolation enhances outcomes in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation: a meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis
Authors: Wenchi Guan, Jun Liu, Xiaofeng Li, Keping Chen, Yan Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of adding Superior Vena Cava Isolation (SVCI) to Pulmonary Vein Isolation (PVI) in patients with drug-refractory paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF).DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, supplemented with Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) to assess evidence sufficiency.Data sourcesWe searched PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) and Web of Science for relevant studies published up to 13 July 2025.Eligibility criteriaWe included prospective RCTs comparing SVCI+PVI versus PVI alone in adults with drug-refractory PAF, with at least 3 months of follow-up and reporting on atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence and procedural complications. Case reports, reviews, observational studies, editorials, expert opinions and non-RCT studies were excluded.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers used standardised methods to search, screen and code included studies. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration and Evidence Project tools. A meta-analysis was conducted using random effects models, and TSA was conducted to evaluate the conclusiveness of evidence. Findings were summarised in GRADE evidence profiles and synthesised qualitatively.ResultsThree RCTs involving 332 patients were included. The addition of SVCI to PVI significantly reduced AF recurrence compared with PVI alone (Risk Ratio 0.54, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.91, p=0.02; I² = 0%, P_heterogeneity=0.79) at a mean 12-month follow-up. TSA confirmed that the current evidence is sufficient. There were no significant differences in procedural complications between groups (Risk Difference 0.00, 95% CI –0.04 to 0.03, p=0.99; I² = 0%, P_heterogeneity=0.96).ConclusionsAdding SVCI to PVI for PAF ablation significantly reduces AF recurrence without increasing procedural risks. TSA confirms the robustness of the findings, supporting the potential integration of SVCI into routine clinical practice for PAF.

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) stands as the cornerstone of radiofrequency catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF),1 2 yet it fails to achieve satisfactory success rates alone. Recent evidence has suggested that the superior vena cava (SVC) serves as a significant source of AF triggers,35 suggesting that adding superior vena cava isolation (SVCI) to the standard PVI procedure could improve ablation outcomes. Moreover, SVC triggers are known to be more prevalent in Asian populations compared with those in Europe.6 Given that Asia accounts for nearly 60% of the global population, the clinical relevance of SVCI could be significant. However, the existing literature revealed conflicting results;720 therefore, current guidelines do not recommend routine SCVI combined with PVI.

However, previous studies vary widely in methodology and study population—some used empiric SVCI, while others employed as-needed SVCI; some studies focused solely on paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF),1113 while others included a mix of paroxysmal, persistent and permanent AF.10 This heterogeneity may explain the conflicting results observed across different studies.

Given these discrepancies, we conducted this meta-analysis to focus specifically on PAF and empiric SVCI. Our aim is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of adding SVCI to PVI for PAF, resolve conflicting findings, and provide guidance for future clinical practices in AF ablation.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) and Web of Science databases for randomised clinical trials (RCTs) comparing SVCI+PVI to PVI alone in PAF patients. No language restriction was applied; for non-English full-text articles, we planned to use Google Translate for screening if necessary. The search was conducted up to 13 July 2025. Our main search terms included ‘Vena Cava, Superior’, ‘Pulmonary Vein’, ‘Radiofrequency Ablation’ and ‘Atrial Fibrillation’. The full search strategy, including Boolean operators and specific search terms, is detailed in the online supplemental materials. Additionally, we manually reviewed the reference lists of all eligible studies to identify further relevant trials.

Eligibility criteria

Studies with the following characteristics were considered eligible: (1) Prospective RCTs with at least 3 months of follow-up, (2) Included human participants with drug-refractory PAF and (3) Reported on clinical outcomes related to AF recurrence and procedural safety. Case reports, reviews, observational studies, non-RCT studies, editorials and expert opinions were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (WG and JL) independently extracted relevant data from each included study using a standardised form, including study design, patient demographics, intervention details, follow-up duration and outcomes related to AF recurrence and procedural complications. They also independently assessed the methodological quality of each study using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool,21 covering domains such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data and selective reporting. After initial extraction and quality assessment, all data and judgments were cross-checked for consistency. Any discrepancies—either in data extraction or in domain-level bias assessments—were resolved through discussion or, if needed, in consultation with a third reviewer (XL).

Patient and public involvement

None.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was AF recurrence at 12 months post-ablation, defined as episodes of AF, atrial flutter or atrial tachycardia lasting more than 30 s, detected by Holter Electrocardiograph. The second outcome was safety endpoints, which included procedural complications such as pericardial effusion, thromboembolism and phrenic nerve injury, etc.

Statistical analysis

The relative risk (RR) was used as the summary statistic and reported with a 95% CI. Meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models to account for the anticipated clinical and methodological diversity between studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values over 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity; subgroup analyses and meta-regression were pre-specified to explore potential sources of heterogeneity if more than 10 studies were available. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, and Egger’s test was planned when ≥10 studies were included. Sensitivity analyses were planned to evaluate the robustness of the results using leave-one-out analyses. Statistical analysis was conducted with Review Manager V.5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, Software Update, Oxford, UK). All p values were two-sided, and values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The certainty of evidence was assessed and summarised in GRADE Evidence Profile tables using GRADEpro.22

Although traditional meta-analysis is highly regarded in the evidence hierarchy, it may produce misleading results if too few studies or participants are included. To address this, we introduced trial sequential analysis (TSA), which helps determine whether enough evidence has been collected to draw definitive conclusions. TSA was used to control for Type I and Type II errors more effectively and was performed using the TSA programme (V.0.9.5.10 Beta). It is important to note that the results of TSA are sensitive to the choice of the intervention effect. We used a random-effects model with CIs of 95%, an information axis with sample size, a Type I error of one-sided boundary type of 5%, and a power of 80%.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 298 studies were identified through the search. Six relevant RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were reviewed in full. Of these, two were excluded: one due to the use of a second-time procedure and cross-over interventions, and the other because it focuses on isolation from the SVC to the right atrial septum. Another study was excluded because it included both paroxysmal and persistent AF patients but did not report separate outcomes for the paroxysmal subgroup. The final analysis included three trials, as shown in the flow diagram in figure 1. Specifically, Corrado and colleagues enrolled patients with AF exhibiting paroxysmal, persistent and permanent forms. However, for our analysis, we focused only on those with PAF.

Primary outcome by traditional meta-analysis

To assess the effect over time of the SVCI+PVI ablation strategy, we performed traditional meta-analysis (figure 4). Among all patients, 54 (16.3%) experienced AF events during follow-up, with 18 (11.2%) in the SVCI+PVI group and 36 (21.1%) in the PVI alone group. In the pooled analysis, the SVCI+PVI group exhibited a decrease in AF occurrence compared with the PVI alone group (RR 0.54, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.91, p=0.02; I2=0%, Pheterogeneity=0.79), with high-certainty evidence assessed by GRADE.

Primary outcome by trial sequential analysis

TSA was also performed by adding studies sequentially by publication date and conducting meta-analysis after each addition, as shown in figure 5. For those unfamiliar with TSA, a brief explanation of figure 5 is necessary. The cumulative Z-curve (blue line) shows the combined effect sizes of the included studies over time. The conventional boundary (orange line) represents where a conclusion of benefit would be reached in a traditional meta-analysis, without considering whether the sample size is sufficient. In contrast, the sequential boundary (green line) uses an adequate sample size to evaluate if the current data are sufficient to make a conclusive statement about the benefit. The TSA sample size line (green line perpendicular to the X-axis) indicates the required sample size for a reliable conclusion. The trajectory of the cumulative Z-curve (whether it crosses the boundary) shows if the cumulative effect size is statistically significant.

Effect of SVCI+PVI versus PVI alone on atrial fibrillation recurrence using the Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA). The figure shows a TSA plot created using TSA software. The cumulative Z-curve (blue line) represents the combined effect estimate as more studies are added. This curve has crossed the TSA boundary (green line that slopes diagonally), indicating that SVCI+PVI is significantly better than PVI alone, as confirmed by TSA. The total information size of the studies included in the meta-analysis is 332, while the TSA software calculated a required information size of 316, shown as the vertical green line. Since the cumulative Z-curve has crossed both the TSA boundary and the conventional boundary (orange line), it confirms that the effect of SVCI+PVI over PVI alone is statistically significant according to both traditional meta-analysis and TSA. PVI, Pulmonary Vein Isolation; SVCI, Superior Vena Cava Isolation.

Based on previous studies,8 we assumed an AF recurrence rate of 16.7% in the SVCI+PVI group and 28.4% in the PVI alone group, with 80% power and a 5% one-sided Type I error. Our calculations suggested that a sample size of 316 patients was needed to reliably detect a plausible treatment effect. This optimal sample size was used to construct the sequential monitoring boundary. The cumulative Z-curve (blue line) crossed both the traditional boundary (orange line) and the sequential boundary (green line), indicating that the cumulative evidence is reliable and conclusive.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression were pre-specified to explore potential sources of heterogeneity if more than 10 studies were available. However, due to the limited number of included studies (n=3), these analyses were not conducted. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach, which showed that exclusion of the Corrado et al10 study led to a non-significant result (RR=0.62, 95% CI 0.32 to 1.21, p=0.16), whereas removal of either of the other two studies did not substantially alter the overall estimate.

Safety outcome

Regarding the analysis for procedural complications, we excluded the study by Corrado and colleagues as they reported complications collectively without providing a breakdown for the PAF subgroup. The analysis was then conducted with the remaining two RCTs. Notably, no significant difference emerged between the SVCI+PVI and PVI alone groups (Risk difference 0.00, 95% CI −0.04−0.03, p=0.99; I2=0%, Pheterogeneity=0.96)(figure 6), the evidence quality was assessed as low according to GRADE due to small sample size.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of adding empiric SVCI to PVI for patients with PAF, using both traditional meta-analysis and TSA. Our findings demonstrate that the addition of SVCI significantly reduces AF recurrence, without increasing procedural complications. Moreover, TSA confirms the robustness of this effect, with the cumulative Z-curve crossing the trial sequential monitoring boundary, indicating sufficient evidence.

Compared with previous meta-analyses and clinical studies, our study incorporates two important methodological strengths. First, we employed rigorous inclusion criteria, selecting only RCTs with standardised protocols and focusing exclusively on patients with PAF. This approach contrasts with earlier reviews that combined RCTs and observational studies or studies that included mixed populations with persistent or permanent AF.714 For example, Corrado et al10 included a mix of AF types, which may have masked subtype-specific treatment effects—PAF is often triggered by factors like PV sleeve with normal atrial. Tissue tends to involve fewer structural changes,23 while persistent and permanent AF are associated with more complex atrial remodelling.24 Additionally, we excluded studies with substantial crossover or protocol violations, such as the trial by Wang et al,13 in which most recurrent patients in the PVI group subsequently received a second ablation of SVCI, thereby compromising the interpretability of the treatment effect. By strictly controlling for study quality and patient selection, we enhanced the internal validity of our pooled estimates.

Second, we integrated TSA into our analysis to overcome the limitations of traditional meta-analysis, such as uncertainty about adequate sample size and the risk of Type I and Type II errors. Traditional meta-analyses can sometimes prematurely suggest benefits or harms based on insufficient data, leading to potentially misleading conclusions, or otherwise lead to repeated studies, wasting both time and resources.25 By using TSA, we can determine whether the accumulated evidence has reached the required information size, which adjusts for random error and provides a more reliable estimate of treatment effects.26 Our TSA results confirm that the evidence is sufficient to support the efficacy of SVCI, advocating its potential use in clinical practice. Furthermore, the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for efficacy as high, providing additional confirmation of the value of SVCI.

Our findings have important clinical implications. The addition of empiric SVCI to PVI may offer improved rhythm control for patients with PAF, without compromising procedural safety. This is particularly relevant in regions where SVC triggers are more frequently observed. Previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of SVC ectopy in Asian populations compared with Western cohorts.6 Given that Asia accounts for nearly 60% of the global population and faces a growing AF burden, SVCI may represent a contextually appropriate adjunctive strategy in these settings.

Importantly, the safety profile of empiric SVCI appears acceptable. Our analysis revealed no significant increase in procedural complications. However, this conclusion is limited by the small number of studies and the lack of TSA validation. Consistently, the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for complications as low, mainly due to the same reason. Given the anatomical proximity of the right phrenic nerve to the SVC, the risk of collateral damage remains a concern—especially when high-power ablation or balloon-based techniques are used. Future RCTs with larger sample sizes, standardised reporting of adverse events and TSA-powered safety analyses are needed to establish the definitive risk–benefit profile of empiric SVCI.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the assessment of study bias was impeded by the absence of comprehensive details regarding the randomisation process, intention-to-treat analysis and missing outcome data in some included studies. This limitation could have introduced selection bias and affected the internal validity of the included studies. Second, it is important to note that TSA results depend on the definition of adequate information size, which is based on the estimated treatment effect. As TSA relies on these estimates, variability in the recurrence rates of AF ablation across studies could lead to an inaccurate estimate of the required sample size, potentially resulting in inaccurate conclusions about the sufficiency of the evidence. Third, one additional RCT identified in an updated search included both paroxysmal and non-long-standing persistent AF patients, but did not report separate results for the paroxysmal subgroup. We contacted the corresponding author but received no response, so the study could not be included in the final meta-analysis.

Conclusions

Our meta-analysis demonstrates that adding SVCI to PVI reduces AF recurrence for PAF without increasing procedural risks. The finding is further confirmed by TSA, which supports its potential integration into routine clinical practice to improve patient outcomes. According to TSA, no additional studies are needed to establish this benefit. However, variations in study designs, enrolled patients and ablation techniques were not fully addressed; future research is warranted to further confirm these findings.
