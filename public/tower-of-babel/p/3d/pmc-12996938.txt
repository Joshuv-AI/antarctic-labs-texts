Iron overload disrupts lipid desaturation through SKN-1 signaling in C. elegans: implications for ferroptosis-based immunomodulatory biomaterials
Authors: Zhaoyang Dong, Yuxin Liao, Hao Shi, Jia Zhang, Jiajie Lin, Qiong Liu, Shiqing Zhang, Xiuqin Fan, Bin Liu, Yunqing Wang, Yantao Zheng, Lei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The field of biomaterials is shifting from bioinert designs toward bioactive and responsive systems, as exemplified by ferroptosis-inducible immunomodulatory adjuvants. Although these platforms enable programmed immune responses, their metallic components introduce distinct risks. Degradation or wear releases free metal ions (e.g., irons) that disrupt local and systemic metal homeostasis, particularly iron metabolism. This unintended disturbance of the immune-iron axis may act as a “secondary hit”, promoting a pathological microenvironment that compromises therapeutic efficacy and accelerates disease progression. Using Caenorhabditis elegans as a simplified yet physiologically informative model, we show that iron overload, mimicking long-term biomaterial exposure, leads to chronic toxicity, metabolic dysregulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, we identify a regulatory interplay between the fatty acid desaturases fat-5/fat-7 and the cytoprotective transcription factor skn-1 (the Nrf2 ortholog) under high-iron conditions. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating long-term metabolic consequences in biomaterial safety assessments and suggest potential lipid-centric strategies to mitigate iron-associated chronic toxicity and iron-overload disorders.

Introduction

Biomaterials science is shifting from bioinertness to bioactivity and bioresponsiveness, especially in immune engineering, where next-generation immunomodulatory adjuvants (NIAs) are now emerging [1]. These NIAs function not only as micro- or nano-scale antigen delivery carriers but also as active platforms capable of precisely programming targeted immune responses [2]. However, when composed of metals or alloys, their degradation or wear can release free metal ions at levels exceeding local tissue buffering capacity, thereby disrupting ionic metabolic homeostasis and exacerbating oxidative stress and immune polarization [3]. These adverse effects extend beyond the intended immune enhancement and cannot be ignored.

Iron, while essential for life, maintains tightly regulated homeostasis that is vulnerable to disruption by exogenous materials [4]. With the increasing use of metal-based biomaterials, interactions between these materials and endogenous metal ions, particularly iron have become a critical yet historically overlooked issue [5]. Accumulating evidence indicates that conventional iron-based biomaterials, such as corrosion byproducts from vascular stents or wear particles from orthopedic implants, interfere with iron metabolism via two distinct pathways. The first is sustained ion release from material degradation directly overwhelms local physiological buffering systems [6]. The second is chronic inflammation from the material alters hormones like hepcidin, leading to systemic iron misdistribution [[7], [8], [9]]. This unintended perturbation of the immune–iron metabolism axis represents a potential “secondary hit”. A “secondary hit” caused by unintended metabolic or ionic dysregulation, is a pathological insult that follows and worsens the primary immune challenge triggered by the biomaterial(see Fig. 1). In this study, iron overload is the “primary hit”, causing cellular stress, metabolic dysregulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. The resulting disruption of the immune-metabolic axis marked by dysregulated lipid metabolism, altered stress signaling, and impaired physiological function, is the “secondary hit”. It amplifies the initial damage and drives systemic pathology. This adapts the established “two-hit” model from hepatology and neurobiology, in which a predisposing factor is followed by a second insult that triggers disease [[10], [11], [12]]. While capable of eliciting immune responses such as ferroptosis to treat tumor tissues, non-specifically targeted ferroptosis may not only undermine the intended protective immune activation by NIAs but also induce a pathological microenvironment marked by oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis within affected tissues [13]. This adverse milieu could accelerate disease progression or precipitate secondary complications. The prevalence and severity of this effect have been clearly demonstrated in the well-defined, simplified system of the model organism Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans).Fig. 1Metal-laden or immunomodulatory biomaterials can induce off-target iron-mediated ferroptosis, potentially undermining their intended immunomodulatory benefits.Fig. 1

Research on C. elegans has long served as a classical model for studying metal toxicity and metabolic disorders [14,15]. Although it lacks an adaptive immune system, its innate immune pathways demonstrate significant functional conservation with mammalian stress and inflammatory signaling, notably the PMK-1/p38 MAPK cascade. The nematode possesses highly conserved core metabolic pathways and lacks a circulatory system, enabling direct exposure of intestinal cells to environmental substances. In C. elegans, iron is acquired solely via intestinal absorption and subsequently sequestered within enterocyte organelles. These features render the route of exposure to accumulated degradation products from iron-based biomaterials in this model strikingly analogous to localized iron accumulation scenarios in mammalian tissues [16]. Experiments have demonstrated that SPIONs induce iron accumulation in C. elegans, cause acute toxicity, impair growth and development, and accelerate aging [17]. Furthermore, studies have shown that iron oxide/silver hybrid nanoparticles lead to reproductive toxicity in nematodes and are associated with oxidative stress and cholinergic neurodegeneration in muscle tissues [18]. More importantly, these findings reveal that the detrimental effects of iron overload stem not only from the metal ions themselves, but also from their systemic disruption of intracellular redox homeostasis and mitochondrial function. The C. elegans model clearly indicates that persistent disturbances in iron homeostasis induced by exogenous agents can profoundly disrupt host physiological homeostasis through cumulative effects, even at low, non-acute levels.

Therefore, at a time when research on NIAs is rapidly advancing with a primary focus on enhancing targeting efficiency and immunogenicity, it is imperative to broaden the scope of biomaterial biocompatibility assessment beyond cytotoxicity and acute inflammatory responses to include long-term, systemic effects on metabolic networks. This study leverages the high-iron environment in C. elegans to model in vivo iron overload and systematically examines its chronic effects, metabolic consequences, and impacts on mitochondrial structure and function. We further elucidate the reciprocal regulatory interplay between the fatty acid desaturases fat-5 and fat-7 and the transcription factor skn-1 (the C. elegans ortholog of mammalian Nrf2) under conditions of iron overload. Our findings reveal the mechanisms underlying the regulation of lipid desaturases, providing a promising therapeutic avenue for mitigating iron-associated chronic toxicity and ameliorating disorders linked to iron overload.

Discussion

This study does not aim to undermine the considerable potential of ferroptosis-inducing biomaterials in applications such as cancer immunotherapy. Rather, the central objective of our work is to integrate a critical systems toxicology-based perspective for early warning and assessment into the development of this field. We sought to investigate whether biomaterials designed to locally and precisely induce ferroptosis and immune activation might, upon introduction into the complex in vivo environment, lead to off-target systemic disruptions in iron homeostasis and associated toxicities due to intrinsic material properties such as sustained metal ion release and persistent immune stimulation. To evaluate this potential risk, we employed toxicological studies using C. elegans, with high-iron exposure serving as a key entry point.

In C. elegans, the intestine integrates nutrient absorption, metabolic storage, and innate immunity. Lacking the hepcidin-ferroportin axis, worms sequester iron directly in enterocytes, creating a precise model of parenchymal iron overload that mirrors early labile iron accumulation in mammalian hepatocytes and splenic macrophages. Iron-driven Fenton reactions activate SKN-1/Nrf2 and IIS signaling, inducing cytoprotective transcription and metabolic reprogramming [25,26]. Intestinal iron overload triggers systemic decline via ROS-mediated lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, spreading deterioration through endocrine and paracrine signals. This trajectory parallels mammalian hepatic iron toxicity where ferroptosis priming drives low-grade inflammation, metabolic syndrome, and accelerated aging. Thus, the C. elegans intestine provides a genetically tractable model to dissect cellular and molecular events preceding systemic iron redistribution in higher organisms.

In our experiments, ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) was incorporated into the standard nematode growth medium (NGM) to mimic the pathological accumulation of iron resulting from in vivo biomaterial interaction. When nematodes were exposed to the FAC-containing environment and fed OP50 bacterial lawns grown under the same high-iron conditions, their uptake route closely recapitulated the real-world scenario in which continuous release or induced accumulation of iron ions occurs due to biomaterial degradation in vivo. At toxicologically relevant concentrations, FAC significantly shortened lifespan, elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), induced mitochondrial dysfunction, and promoted ectopic lipid accumulation. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that FAC suppressed the expression of fatty acid desaturase genes fat-5, fat-6 and fat-7, and impaired activation of the skn-1/daf-16 antioxidant signaling pathway, thereby establishing a direct mechanistic link between chronic iron overload and accelerated aging.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by phospholipid peroxidation, preferentially utilizes polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) as substrates [27,28]. The stearoyl-CoA desaturases FAT-5, FAT-6 and FAT-7 catalyze the introduction of a Δ9 double bond into palmitic acid (C16:0) and stearic acid (C18:0), producing monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), such as palmitoleic acid (C16:1 n-7) and oleic acid (C18:1 n-9) [29], a process potentially critical in modulating oxidative stress and lipid metabolic disruption following FAC exposure. Notably, FAC exposure dose-dependently downregulated transcription of all three desaturases, representing an adaptive response that limits PUFA biosynthesis and reduces susceptibility to ferroptosis under iron-overload conditions. Under normal physiological conditions, unsaturated fatty acids are essential for cell membrane integrity, signal transduction, and energy storage.

Consequently, fat-7 mutants, fat-5/6 double mutants, and fat-5/7 double mutants exhibit shorter lifespans compared to wild-type (WT) worms. However, under iron overload conditions, these mutants did not display further lifespan shortening, suggesting that the downregulation of desaturase activity may serve as a protective buffering mechanism against iron-induced stress. Notably, fat-7, fat-5/6 and fat-5/7 mutants showed significant resistance to FAC-induced declines in locomotor capacity and lifespan reduction. Desaturases modulate mitochondrial responses to iron stress through two distinct mechanisms: first, by governing membrane phospholipid unsaturation levels, thereby determining susceptibility to iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation [30]; and second, by establishing baseline mitochondrial bioenergetic states that dictate adaptive capacity under oxidative challenge [31]. The fat-5/7 double mutant exhibits resistance to FAC-induced mitochondrial depolarization, as evidenced by stable RH123 fluorescence, indicating that abrogation of desaturase activity diminishes ferroptotic priming. This protective phenotype, however, is accompanied by impaired membrane fluidity and compromised organismal fitness, manifesting as reduced lifespan under normal physiological conditions. Moreover, FAC-triggered ROS accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction were found to depend on the presence of fat-5 and fat-7, indicating that these enzymes act as critical molecular links between environmental iron exposure and organelle damage.

To further investigate the interplay between oxidative stress and lipid desaturases, we repeated the FAC exposure experiment in the fat-5/7 double knockout background. In these mutants, the expression of the oxidative stress regulator skn-1 remained unchanged upon FAC treatment. Conversely, in the skn-1 mutant, the downregulation of fat-7 transcription was abolished, demonstrating that the ROS signaling cascade induced by iron overload is intricately coupled with fatty acid metabolism, and that lipid remodeling exerts a feedback effect to mitigate ferroptosis-like damage. Meanwhile, the antioxidant response is coordinately regulated through the SKN-1 pathway. Thus, chronic low-dose iron intake may promote cellular and organ ageing by simultaneously disrupting iron homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and redox balance. Small-molecule interventions targeting fatty acid desaturases or the SKN-1 pathway hold potential for development into public health strategies aimed at mitigating health deterioration associated with iron overload.

The core design of NIAs is to exploit the ferroptosis vulnerability of diseased cells, such as tumor cells [32]. However, the in vivo fate of these materials is complex and not fully predictable. A subset of nanoparticles may undergo nonspecific clearance and exhibit long-term retention within the reticuloendothelial system, particularly in the liver and spleen [33]. Evidence from C. elegans models indicates that such localized accumulation in metabolically critical organs can establish a microenvironment of chronic iron overload, which may compromise cellular function in vivo, induce unintended ferroptosis or cellular dysfunction, and ultimately disrupt immune surveillance homeostasis. Notably, lifespan assays in C. elegans provide a sensitive readout of the physiological consequences of prolonged exposure. For biomaterials intended for the management of chronic diseases or those inherently prone to extended retention in the body short-term safety evaluations may overlook gradually accumulating disruptions in iron homeostasis and the associated degenerative pathologies they may provoke.

While the FAC exposure model used here provides a controlled, high-throughput system for studying labile iron overload, it simplifies the complex, low-dose, sustained iron release kinetics of degrading biomaterials in vivo. Nevertheless, this foundational study establishes proof-of-concept that iron overload disrupts systemic metabolism and activates evolutionarily conserved stress pathways. Key distinctions remain among soluble Fe3+ complexes, nanoparticulate iron, and protein-corona-coated materials, all of which merit rigorous future investigation. Physical form critically determines cellular uptake mechanisms, subcellular trafficking, local toxicity thresholds, and downstream metabolic and ferroptotic responses. For example, citrate-coated SPIONs undergo rapid lysosomal dissolution and ion burst, whereas albumin-coated SPIONs remain particulate and membrane-impermeable, producing divergent lipidomic and transcriptomic signatures despite equivalent total iron burdens [17]. Future work using microfluidic worm-on-chip platforms with tunable perfusion will enable direct, side-by-side comparison of FAC, ion-releasing nanoparticles, and intact alloy fragments, thereby bridging our simplified model with the heterogeneous corrosion microenvironments encountered by metallic immunomodulatory adjuvants in clinical practice.

Methods

Culture and maintenance

C. elegans strains were cultured on NGM plates seeded with OP50. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were conducted at 20 °C. To prepare a stock solution of FAC at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, ultra-pure water was used, and the solution was subsequently filtered and sterilized using a bacterial filter. The FAC stock solution was then mixed with molten NGM to prepare plates with final concentrations of 10 mg/mL (high concentration group), 5 mg/mL (medium concentration group), and 2.5 mg/mL (low concentration group). This concentration gradient spans the full range of reported optimal ferroptosis-inducing concentrations. It enables simulation of iron-based material degradation in vivo from sustained early release to high-load release, and systematic evaluation of the therapeutic-toxicity balance. It is also compatible with the loading capacities of common biomaterials like alginate and chitosan [[34], [35], [36]]. The bacterial suspension was spread onto these NGM or FAC plates. Control plates were also spread with OP50 bacterial solution.

Synchronization of C. elegans

The nematodes were age-synchronized by bleaching of adult worms with lysis solution. The eggs incubated at 20 °C for 24 h to obtain synchronized L1 larvae. The larvae were transferred to NGM medium with OP50 and cultured for 24 h to L4 for subsequent experiments.

Life span measurement

After synchronization, fifty L4 worms were inoculated onto control plates and plates treated with different concentrations of FAC and cultured in a 20 °C incubator. Worms were counted daily under a microscope, and the numbers of living and dead worms were recorded until all worms had died. The criterion for death was defined as no response to a gentle touch with a sterile needle. The experiment was performed with three independent technical replicates to analyze the effects of FAC on the mean and maximum lifespan of C. elegans.

Following reverse transcription, the expression of target mRNA was detected by RT-qPCR (Hieff ® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix, YEASEN, China), with β-actin used as the internal reference gene. Fluorescence intensity quantification assays

Transgenic worms’ strains EU31, JH3199, CL2166, TJ356, SJ4103, SJ4143, VC2654, LIU104, and ZXW618 were subjected to FAC interventions at concentrations of 2.5, 5 and 10 mg/mL (n = 30 worms per group) for 3 days anesthetized in 0.2% tricaine and placed on a piece of microscope slide. The images were captured using a NIKON microscope and quantified using ImageJ 13.5a. All experiments were performed with three independent technical replicates.

Fluorescence staining

Worms were collected and washed three times with M9 buffer. Subsequently, fluorescence staining was performed as previously described [[37], [38], [39]], using PI (100 μM), RH123 (100 μM), DHE (100 μM), and NDA (100 μM) probes. These probes were used to assess cell viability, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), superoxide (O2−), and glutathione levels in C. elegans (n = 30 worms per group). During incubation with the probes, worms were maintained at 20 °C for 1 h. Afterward, worms were washed with M9 buffer three additional times and then imaged using a fluorescence microscope (NIKON, Japan). All experiments were performed with three independent technical replicates.

Statistics and reproducibility

All measurements were independently repeated at least three times to ensure the authenticity of the results. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 24 software and GraphPad Prism 9 software. The significance of differences in lifespan measurements was calculated using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Comparisons between two groups were made using Student's t-test, while comparisons between three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. In the figures, ns(p > 0.05), ∗(p < 0.05), ∗∗(p < 0.01), ∗∗∗(p < 0.001), and ∗∗∗∗(p < 0.0001) indicate statistically significant differences.
