The current situation and influencing factors of nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care in primary medical institutions: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kai Qian, Hongfei Jia, Haiyan Yang, Ling Zhu, Qianping Li, Tianyue Shi, Ye Xu, Yaping Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDementia has become an escalating problem in China, where people with dementia have high rates of hospital admissions and long lengths of stay. Primary medical institutions play a vital role in providing healthcare for people with dementia. Therefore, nurses’ sense of competence in these settings is critical to ensuring high-quality care. This study aims to assess the current situation of nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care in primary medical institutions and to analyze the relevant influencing factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study obtained data using a socio-demographic questionnaire along with the Chinese versions of the Dementia Knowledge Assessment Scale and the Sense of Competence in Dementia Care Staff Scale. The total SCIDS-C score was used as the dependent variable to assess the nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care. One-way ANOVA was conducted for univariate analysis. The statistically significant variables were included in the multiple linear regression model to analyze the influencing factors.Results359 nurses in primary medical institutions completed the survey. Of all the nurses, 96.4% were female, 49.6% were aged 20–30 years, 53.8% were working in a township hospital, 34.0% held the title of nurse practitioner, 44.6% had worked for more than 10 years and 73% had no caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders. The mean SCIDS-C score among nurses in primary medical institutions was 43.93 out of 68. Degree of attention paid to dementia, degree of confidence in caring for people with dementia, caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders, ability to care for people with dementia and attended education and/or training in care of the person with dementia or related disorders, were significantly associated with the nurses’ sense of competence (p < 0.05). The final model explained 20% of score variance (R² = 0.20).ConclusionThis study found there is room for improvement in their sense of competence in dementia care and is influenced by multiple factors. To improve nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care and provision of better care for dementia people, primary medical institutions should invest in dementia training of nurses, and nurses should make efforts to enhance their dementia care knowledge and skills.

Introduction

Dementia is a disease of cognitive decline that affects independent daily functions [1]. The number of people living with dementia worldwide is approximately 50 million and growing by 10 million per year [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the predicted number of people living with dementia will reach 82 million in 2030, and 152 million in 2050 [3]. In China, the overall prevalence of dementia is 6.0%; it is estimated that 15.7 million Chinese are living with dementia [4]. Chinese Alzheimer’s disease report 2024 suggests that the burden of Alzheimer’s disease in China is higher than the international average, and the incidence and prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease is continuing to increase [5].

Due to the debilitating symptoms and the progressive nature of dementia, people with dementia often require a considerable level of care [6]. Compared with other adults, people living with dementia have higher admission rates and longer hospital stays [7]. Research has shown that nurses are key providers of hospital and other care services for people with dementia [8]. In China, primary medical institutions, which include community health service centers, township hospitals and old-age care institutions, are often the preferred providers of healthcare for people with dementia [9]. While comprehensive hospitals tend to have higher admission rates for patients with dementia because of their advanced diagnostic and treatment capacities, primary care institutions play a crucial role in early screening, routine management, and community-based dementia support [10, 11]. As the healthcare system in China’s primary care sector continues to improve, more people with dementia are likely to remain in these settings once their condition stabilizes [12, 13]. Therefore, nurses play an important role in the care of persons with dementia in primary medical institutions in China.

To ensure that people with dementia are provided with quality healthcare, nurses’ competence in dementia care is crucial [14]. Having a sense of competence in the caring role refers to self-evaluation of level of performance in caregiving [15]. The sense of competence in dementia care refers to one’s self-perception of confidence in meeting the care needs of people with dementia [16]. Multiple factors potentially impact nurses’ sense of competence in caring for people with dementia in primary medical institutions, including the nurses’ level of dementia knowledge and training in dementia care, and their caregiving experiences [17]. In China, many nurses lack education, training, and experience in dementia care, and are therefore under-prepared for this role [18–21]. As well, medical staff are often unaware of the nursing knowledge and skills required to care for the person with dementia [22, 23], and many healthcare staff have low sense of competence in dementia care [24, 25].

The literature suggests that current research on the sense of competence in dementia care focuses on informal caregivers and nurses in comprehensive medical institutions. However, there is a lack of empirical evidence on how nurses in primary medical institutions perceive and develop their sense of competence in dementia care [26, 27]. Nurses in these settings often lack targeted training and support, which further underscores the need to investigate their sense of competence in providing dementia care [28]. Bandura’s social cognitive theory emphasizes that behavior is shaped through the dynamic interaction of personal factors, behavior itself, and environmental influences [29]. In the context of dementia care, the development of competence depends not only on the acquisition of knowledge or skills, but also on observational learning, contextual support, and feedback from practice [30]. In primary care settings—often characterized by limited resources and complex caregiving environments—enhancing nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care requires a combination of structured learning opportunities and a supportive institutional culture to continuously foster professional growth. The study aimed to evaluate the current situation of nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care in primary medical institutions and analyze the relevant influencing factors.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional research design to collect data from nurses in primary medical institutions. This design allows for the examination of the current situation and influencing factors affecting nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care without implying causality.

Sampling and inclusion criteria

Ten primary medical institutions (community health service centers, township hospitals, and old-age care institutions) in Changzhou, China, were randomly selected, and a convenience sample of nurses was included in the survey. The inclusion criteria were people who (1) held a registered nurse certificate and worked in a primary medical institution, (2) able and willing to provide informed consent to study participation and (3) able to complete the self-report questionnaire. The exclusion criteria were being a (1) student nurse, (2) refresher nurse or specialist nurse. These criteria were established to ensure the representativeness of the sample. Student nurses and refresher or specialist nurses were excluded because their roles and experience differ from those of full-time registered nurses, thereby reducing potential bias. Based on Kendall’s [31] sample size estimation method, a minimum of 300 participants was determined, including a 20% allowance for potential attrition. To enhance the credibility of the study results, 450 participants were recruited.

Data collection instruments

The survey study was conducted from December 2023 to January 2024. Data were obtained via through a set of online self-report questionnaires, as follows.

Socio-demographic questionnaire

Based on a review of the literature [26, 32], participant characteristic survey questions were developed by the authors and included: socio-demographic characteristics including: age, gender, education level, job site, title, working years; and professional related characteristics, including the following items: ‘do you often have contact with dementia people at work’, ‘degree of attention paid to dementia’, ‘degree of confidence in caring for people with dementia’, ‘does a family member has dementia or a related disorder’, ‘caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders’, ‘ability to care for people with dementia’, and ‘training in knowledge of dementia or related disorders’(Table 1).

Chinese version of the dementia knowledge assessment scale

The Dementia Knowledge Assessment Scale (DKAS) was developed by Annear [33] in 2015 at the Centre for Dementia Research and Education, University of Tasmania, Australia and the Chinese version of the scale was translated and tested for reliability by Zhang et al. [34] among dementia caregivers. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.840. It consists of 4 subscales on causes and characteristics of dementia, communication and behavior, care considerations, risks and health promotion, with 25 items and 5 options for each item, including‘correct, probably correct, incorrect, probably incorrect, don’t know’. The lowest and highest scores on each item can be zero and two respectively. For correct knowledge items, select ‘correct’ to score 2 points, select ‘possible correct’ to score 1 point, select ‘error’, ‘possible error’ and ‘don’t know’ to score 0 points. For incorrect knowledge items, select ‘error’ to score 2 points, select ‘possible error’ to score 1 point, select ‘correct’, ‘possible correct’ and ‘don’t know’ to score 0 points. The maximum score is 50, with higher scores indicating a higher level of relevant knowledge on the part of the carer. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.783. For this study, the total score of DKAS was regarded as an independent variable.

Chinese version of the sense of competence in dementia care staff scale

The Sense of Competence in Dementia Care Staff scale (SCIDS) was developed by Schepers et al. [16] in 2012. The Chinese version of the Sense of Competence in Dementia Care Staff scale (SCIDS-C) was translated and culturally adapted by Zhao et al. [26] in 2022, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.87, with high internal consistency. The scale is designed to assess the sense of competence in dementia care, especially for caregivers of people with dementia. It contains 17 items divided into four subscales: ‘Professionalism’ (five items), ‘Building Relationships’ (four items), ‘Care challenges’ (four items), ‘Sustaining Personhood’ (four items). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale: 1= ‘not at all’, 2= ‘a little bit’, 3= ‘quite a lot’ and 4= ‘very much’, The total score is the sum of all item scores, ranging from 17 to 68. Higher scores indicate a higher level of sense of competence in dementia care. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.89. In the study, the SCIDS-C total score was regarded as a dependent variable.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data (p > 0.05). Independent samples t-tests were conducted for comparisons between two groups when the data were normally distributed. The SCIDS-C score was used as the outcome variable. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for comparisons involving more than two groups, and Kruskal-Wallis H-test was used for those that did not conform to normal distribution. Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analyzes were entered into a multiple linear regression model for multivariable analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

450 questionnaires were collected, with 359 valid questionnaires and an effective recovery rate of 80%. Participants were drawn from 5 community health service centers, 2 township hospitals, and 3 old-age care institutions.

Participant characteristics are reported in Table 2. Of 359 participants, 96.4% were female, 49.6% were in the 20–30 age group, 66.3% had an undergraduate or above education level, 53.8% worked in a township hospital, 34.0% were nurse practitioners and 44.6% had worked more than 10 years.

Additionally, 63.5% of participants had frequent contact with people with dementia in their work, 72.1% expressed a general concern about dementia, 69.6% reported mediocre confidence in caring for people with dementia, 88.9% reported that they did not have a family member with dementia or a related disorder, 26.7% indicated that they had caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders, 59.9%, considered they had the ability to care for people with dementia and 26.2% had attended education and/or training on dementia or related disorders.

Multiple regression analysis results

As shown in Table 2, the one-way ANOVA analysis results indicated that the degree of attention paid to dementia, degree of confidence in caring for people with dementia, caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders, ability to care for people with dementia, and attended education and/or training in care of the person with dementia or related disorders were significantly correlated with the sense of competence of nurses in care of the person with dementia (p < 0.05). These statistically significant correlated variables and the total DKAS score were included as independent variables in the multiple linear regression analyzes.

Similarly, multiple regression analysis results (Table 4) showed that the degree of attention paid to dementia, degree of confidence in caring for people with dementia, degree of knowledge about dementia, caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders, ability to care for people with dementia, and attended education and/or training in care of the person with dementia or related disorders were significantly associated with sense of competence of nurses in care of the person with dementia (p < 0.05). Specifically, nurses who were very concerned with dementia (β = 0.362, p = 0.000) and nurses who were generally concerned with dementia (β = 0.168, p = 0.027) had higher SCIDS-C scores than nurses who were not concerned with dementia, indicating that higher levels of attention may reflect greater intrinsic motivation and awareness, which are conducive to developing sense of competence. Nurses with strong confidence in caring for people with dementia (β = 0.362, p = 0.000) and nurses with mediocre confidence in caring for people with dementia (β = 0.136, p = 0.045) had higher SCIDS-C scores than nurses with no confidence, suggesting a graded effect of self-perceived efficacy on sense of competence. Nurses who had caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders (β = 0.237, p = 0.000) scored higher than nurses who had not, reflecting the importance of practical exposure in shaping their professional competence. Nurses with ability to care for people with dementia (β = 0.370, p = 0.000) had higher SCIDS-C scores than nurses without ability in caring for people with dementia. Nurses who had attended education and/or training in care of the person with dementia or related disorders (β = 0.231, p = 0.000) scored higher than nurses who had not attended education/training, demonstrating the critical role of targeted education and skill-building in improving sense of competence. Nurses with higher knowledge of dementia, i.e., higher DKAS scores (β = 0.231, p = 0.000) had higher SCIDS-C scores, emphasizing the importance of cognitive reserve in supporting sense of competence. The final regression model accounted for 20% of the total variance in nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care. Multicollinearity was examined using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and no severe multicollinearity was detected, as all VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5.

Discussion

This study focuses on nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care, as measured by the SCIDS-C scale. Unlike most other studies in China [35–37], the survey respondents were nurses in primary medical institutions. In this study, it was found that the total SCIDS-C score of nurses in primary medical institutions in Changzhou was 43.93 ± 7.84. This score was lower than 46.6 ± 7.3 in Zhao et al.’s study [26] and 48.2 ± 9.95 in Zhang et al.’s [22] study. Compared to the healthcare providers (doctors, nurses, physiotherapists, etc.) included in the Zhao et al.’s [26] study and the family caregivers included in Zhang et al.’s study [22], the participants in this study were more homogeneous, their level of dementia care knowledge acquisition was not as rich, they undertook more complex nursing care compared to family caregivers and did not provide as much care for people with dementia. These differences in participant characteristics and care experiences may explain the participants’ lower SCIDS scores [38]. It is important to note that SCIDS-C assesses self-reported competence in dementia care and does not necessarily reflect their actual performance or objective capability.

Multiple factors influence nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care in primary medical institutions. In this study, we identified six relevant influencing factors: degree of attention paid to dementia, degree of confidence in caring for people with dementia, caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders, ability to care for people with dementia, attended dementia education and/or training, and degree of dementia knowledge. Below, we discuss each factor.

Nurses who expressed greater concern about dementia demonstrated significantly higher SCIDS-C scores. Similarly, a study by Wang et al. [39] found that a higher level of concern was associated with greater acquisition of dementia-related knowledge. The acquisition of good dementia-related knowledge can enhance the nurses’ sense of competence in dementia care in primary medical institutions [40]. However, the degree of attention may reflect not only intrinsic motivation to learn but also the influence of prior training or experience, which may confound the observed associations. Surr et al. [41] found that higher dementia awareness was associated with greater engagement in learning activities, suggesting ‘attention’ may be both a prerequisite and a consequence of knowledge building. In China, dementia is often stigmatized and regarded as a family responsibility, which may reduce the level of attention nurses pay to dementia [42]. Therefore, it is essential for organizations to not only promote dementia awareness among nurses but also evaluate whether this awareness effectively translates into improved sense of competence in dementia care.

Confidence in caring for people with dementia was strongly associated with the sense of competence scores. A study by Chen et al. [43] found that confidence enables caregivers to better understand dementia and deliver improved care. This finding aligns with Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, which posits that confidence is a key determinant of behavioral capability [29]. However, self-confidence without underlying knowledge or skills may lead to an overestimation of ability. Therefore, educational programs must be carefully designed to strike a balance between skill building and self-reflection. Primary medical institutions should organize dementia-related seminars to keep nurses up to date with the latest treatments and research advances. These activities can help enhance their confidence in caring for people with dementia.

Nurses with dementia care experience scored higher on the SCIDS-C than those without. However, the results of this study were not very optimistic, with 73% of the participants lacking dementia care experience and only 27% of the nurses having relevant care experience. A study by Robin Digby et al. [44] found that caring for people with dementia placed greater demands on nurses, leading to increased workload and time consumption compared to the care of individuals without dementia. Moreover, lack of care experience may cause patients or their families to question the professionalism of nurses, potentially reducing treatment compliance [45, 46]. Therefore, dementia care is particularly stressful for inexperienced nurses, whereas experienced nurses tend to communicate more effectively with individuals living with dementia. It is noteworthy that Chinese culture has traditionally placed great emphasis on the concept of “family.” For older adults, especially those with dementia, home-based care is often preferred [47]. This reduces opportunities for nurses to gain practical experience in institutional settings, which partly explains why most participants lack experience in dementia care. In conclusion, nurses in primary medical institutions with dementia care experience demonstrate a higher sense of competence in this domain. Primary medical institutions should actively implement a mentoring system to encourage experienced senior nurses to pass on their experience to inexperienced nurses, and give inexperienced nurses more opportunities in dementia care in order to rapidly accumulate care experience and improve sense of competence in dementia care. In addition, we recommend that dementia care training be made available to all nurses in primary care settings to establish foundational competence, while targeted and practical skill-based training should be prioritized for the subset of nurses who are or may become direct caregivers of people with dementia.

Nurses who perceived themselves as having the ability to care for people with dementia reported significantly higher SCIDS-C scores compared to those who did not. This finding underscores the central role of self-assessed practical capability in shaping sense of competence in dementia care. According to a study by Zhon et al. [48], caregivers with higher levels of dementia care ability also demonstrated more optimistic attitudes, stronger psychological resilience, and a greater sense of competence, highlighting the multifaceted nature of caregiving preparedness. This suggests that dementia care ability is not only a matter of technical skills but also involves emotional and psychological readiness. In the context of primary medical institutions—where resources may be limited and specialized support less accessible—the association between care ability and sense of competence becomes even more critical. Nurses with greater functional ability are better positioned to respond to the complex and evolving needs of people with dementia. These competencies contribute directly to improved care quality and patient outcomes. Primary medical institutions should organize regular dementia workshops to improve nurses’ ability to care for people with dementia, and should also motivate nurses themselves to actively learn and improve their own caring ability.

Training attendance was a significant contributor to competence. In a study by Zhang [22] et al., it was found that nursing staff who had attended dementia-related knowledge training had significantly higher dementia care competency scores than those who had not attended dementia-related knowledge training, which is consistent with the results of this study. Theoretically, this finding is supported by Bandura’s social cognitive theory, which suggests that competence development relies not only on the acquisition of skills but also on the establishment of self-efficacy [49]. However, this relationship may be bidirectional. On the one hand, training can directly enhance professional skills, promote reflective practice, and reduce uncertainty when dealing with dementia-related behaviors. On the other hand, nurses with higher initial motivation or awareness may be more inclined to participate in training, leading to potential self-selection bias. To achieve greater impact, primary medical institutions should establish continuous, modular, and multi-format dementia education programs. Incorporating such training into continuing education systems can help ensure sustained improvement in sense of competence, ultimately translating into high-quality clinical care.

Dementia knowledge was positively associated with sense of competence. Studies have shown that the degree of dementia knowledge is important for caregivers to carry out the care of people with dementia, and higher knowledge levels are associated with stronger sense of competence in dementia care [40, 50]. Supporting this, Kuske et al. demonstrated that a structured 3-month dementia care training program conducted in German nursing homes significantly enhanced staff knowledge and overall care competence [51]. Therefore, it is particularly important to strengthen dementia-related knowledge training for nurses in primary medical institutions. Nurses should take the initiative to learn and enrich their dementia-related knowledge and provide higher quality care to patients.

Limitation

There are some limitations to this study. Firstly, the study used a cross-sectional survey and therefore we were unable to draw any causal conclusions from the study. Secondly, a convenience sampling method was employed in a single city, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to nurses working in other primary care settings. Future studies could benefit from multi-center, large-scale investigations using stratified random sampling to enhance representativeness and external validity. Thirdly, all data—including both predictor and outcome variables—were collected through self-reported measures, which may introduce common method bias.

In addition, while six significant variables were included in the multiple linear regression analysis, we did not further explore potential interactions or complex interdependencies among these predictors. Although this may limit a more nuanced understanding of how these factors collectively influence sense of competence in dementia care, our primary aim was to examine the independent effects of each variable. Furthermore, the final regression model explained 20% of the variance in nurses’ sense of competence, suggesting that other unmeasured individual, institutional, or contextual factors may also contribute to their perceived competence and warrant further exploration in future research.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that the sense of competence in dementia care of nurses working in primary medical institutions is influenced by several factors. These include degree of confidence in caring for people with dementia, degree of dementia knowledge, caregiving experience for people with dementia or related disorders, ability to care for people with dementia, and education and/or training in care of people with dementia or related disorders. As the number of people with dementia continues to grow, necessitating an increased level of formal care support, nurses working in primary medical institutions require the knowledge, skills and confidence in dementia care to address this need [38]. Dementia education and training programs should, therefore, be strengthened. Importantly, tailored education and training strategies should be adopted for different influencing factors. For example, workshops and public awareness campaigns may be effective in increasing nurses’ attention to dementia; confidence can be enhanced through structured simulation-based learning or case discussions; a mentoring system could be established to enable experienced nurses to guide those with less exposure; and practical, hands-on dementia care experiences should be provided to improve real-world competence. By implementing such multi-faceted and targeted interventions, primary medical institutions can better equip their nursing workforce to deliver high-quality, person-centered care for people with dementia.
