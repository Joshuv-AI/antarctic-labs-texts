An Ecological Impact Assessment Framework and Integrated Management Pathway for Large-Scale Chemical Mosquito Control in Urban Areas
Authors: Meng-Hua Chen, Yu-Xi Feng, Hai-Ya Zhang, Lei Xiang, Chao He, Jing-Yu Liu, Bin Zou, Yong Xu, Yu-Juan Lin, Yan-Hong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global warming and rapid urbanization are intensifying the threat of mosquito-borne diseases, prompting many cities to default to large-scale chemical spraying as an emergency response. However, this quick-fix approach may conceal profound ecological collateral damage that has long been overlooked. Here, we systematically map the impacts of mass larviciding and adulticiding on urban ecosystems and present a novel ecological risk indicator framework that tracks acute nontarget effects, the environmental fate and transport of active ingredients, impairment of ecosystem functions, and human health exposure. Building on this diagnostic toolkit, we propose a tiered, climate-smart, and city-tailored governance model that integrates environmental management, biological regulation, intelligent sensing, and civic engagement. By shifting mosquito control from reactive chemical dependence to an ecologically informed paradigm, this approach offers both a conceptual architecture and a practical roadmap for ensuring public health safety without compromising urban ecological sustainability.

Introduction

Mosquito-borne diseases rank among the foremost threats to global public health and security. Accelerated by climate warming and rapid urbanization, the geographic range of vector mosquitoes such as Aedes spp. continues to expand, with their active season markedly prolonged. − More critically, elevated ambient temperatures hasten both mosquito reproduction and viral replication within vectors, substantially increasing transmission risks for dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus diseases. , Dengue fever typically manifests with high fever, severe headache, and arthralgia, whereas chikungunya can cause debilitating joint pain persisting for months. These diseases not only strain healthcare systems but also incite widespread public anxiety. Although environmental management, including the elimination of mosquito breeding sites, solid waste management, drainage system improvements, and community-based cleanup campaigns, as the primary approach, supplemented by chemical control, constitutes a widely accepted principle, many cities still default to large-scale chemical spraying as their first-line emergency response. , While this measure can rapidly suppress adult mosquito populations in the short term, the underlying ecological risks remain scientifically and systematically unquantified.

Urban ecosystems are inherently vulnerable and are characterized by highly artificial land cover and simplified ecological structure. Approximately 70% impervious surface coverage enables stormwater runoff to deliver chemical agents to landscape water bodies within 15 min; fragmented green patches (average spacing <200 m) deprive nontarget organisms of effective refuge habitats; and the urban heat island effect further accelerates volatilization and transport of these compounds. These attributes collectively amplify the negative ecological impacts of chemical mosquito control, rendering urban ecosystems more sensitive than their natural counterparts and ultimately trapping control efforts in a vicious cycle of increasing application frequency, yet decreasing effectiveness.

Current risk assessment models for mosquito chemical control predominantly rely on acute lethal dose testing of a single species (e.g., Apis mellifera). These models fail to capture interspecific sensitivity variations, inadequately reflect the long-term chronic impacts of insecticides on complex ecosystems, and overlook interactive effects among multiple stressors. Furthermore, they insufficiently account for urban-specific environmental characteristics. The fragmented evaluation dimensions neglect long-term cascading effects such as ecosystem service degradation and mosquito resistance evolution but lack quantitative linkages between environmental concentrations and socioeconomic costs. , This unidimensional assessment paradigm is inadequate for supporting urban ecological sustainability goals, necessitating the urgent development of an integrated evaluation system that balances emergency vector control with long-term ecosystem conservation.

Herein, we systematically delineate the impacts of large-scale chemical mosquito control on urban ecosystems and innovatively develop an assessment framework that integrates acute nontarget organism effects, environmental pollution and transport dynamics, functional impairment of urban ecosystems, and human health risk evaluation. Notably, the large-scale chemical mosquito control defined in this study refers to the application of chemical agents at doses and frequencies substantially exceeding routine levels during specific periods, such as disease outbreaks, and the use of technologies such as ultralow-volume (ULV) spraying to achieve area-wide coverage. This diagnostic toolkit enables the comprehensive quantification of ecological consequences. Furthermore, we propose an ecologically rational mosquito management system that synthesizes geographic and climate adaptation, city-specific stratification, and collaborative governance. Our objective is to provide the scientific foundation necessary to drive the transformation of mosquito vector control strategies toward environmental sustainability.

Potential Impacts of Large-Scale Chemical Mosquito Control on Urban Ecosystems

In urban ecosystems, when chemical mosquito control shifts from targeted spot treatments to large-scale, excess-dosage area-wide applications, its ecological impacts can escalate from localized issues to systemic risks. These potential impacts are manifested primarily in four aspects (Figure ).

Potential impacts of large-scale chemical mosquito control on urban ecosystems.

Direct Mortality of Nontarget Organisms and Biodiversity Loss

The prevalence of impervious surfaces (e.g., roads, plazas) and dense stormwater drainage networks in cities exacerbates the threat of pyrethroid-based insecticides to aquatic ecosystems. − Postapplication residues readily wash into urban water bodiessuch as ornamental lakes and riversvia stormwater runoff. These compounds exhibit extreme toxicity to aquatic life. For example, deltamethrin has a 96-h median lethal concentration (LC50) of only 23.4 ng/mL for juvenile Scapharca subcrenata and 709.5 ng/mL for adults. This high toxicity indicates that even trace concentrations of pyrethroids can have lethal effects on aquatic organisms. Aquatic insect larvaeincluding mayflies and caddisfliesare directly poisoned, and as these taxa constitute critical prey for fish and waterfowl, their decimation destabilizes the structural integrity of entire aquatic food webs. , The Lake Shinji incident in Japan starkly illustrates such cascading impacts: following neonicotinoid application, zooplankton biomass plummeted by 83%, precipitating a sharp decline in catches of icefish and eel that feed upon them; icefish harvests, for instance, crashed from 240 tons to a mere 22 tons. Moreover, urban water bodies typically possess limited self-purification capacity, enabling pesticides to accumulate in sediments and pose persistent threats to benthic communities.

In urban terrestrial ecosystems, the broad-spectrum toxicity of pyrethroid insecticides presents equally severe challenges. Spray deposits on vegetation directly inflict collateral mortality on vital pollinators such as bees and butterflies, − impairing plant reproductive success and fruit yields in urban green spaces. A big-data study encompassing nearly 888,000 case records demonstrated that even recommended application rates significantly inhibited growth, reproductive capacity, and behavioral activity in nontarget animals. More critically, natural mosquito predatorsincluding spiders, geckos, dragonflies, and birdshave experienced population declines due to prey scarcity or secondary poisoning. , Furthermore, chronic, intensive applications drastically reduce populations of frogs, fish, and birds, , directly eroding the inherent biological control capacity of urban ecosystems.

On the basis of systematic literature searches spanning the past decade (2015–2025), commonly used mosquito control agents exhibit well-documented ecotoxicological effects on nontarget organisms (Table S1). For example, pyrethroids demonstrate 96 h median lethal concentrations (LC50) in the ng/mL range for aquatic invertebrates, whereas chronic neonicotinoid toxicity can precipitate significant population declines in pollinating insects.

Environmental Media Contamination, Transport, and Long-Term Residual Risks

The environmental fate of chemical mosquito control agents in urban ecosystems is highly complex and persistent, as amplified by the highly artificial nature of urban land cover. Postapplication pesticide particulates disperse via urban airflow and settle on building surfaces, roads, green spaces, and water bodies under the influence of street-canyon effects. Pyrethroids, for example, typically exhibit octanol–water partition coefficients (Kow) of approximately 5.00, indicating strong hydrophobicity and high affinity for particulate matter. Critically, extensive impervious surfaces accelerate stormwater runoff convergence, rapidly transporting pesticide-laden particulates through drainage networks into urban water bodiesornamental lakes and riverswhere they accumulate in sediments, forming long-term pollution sources that can impact downstream urban watersheds. − Additionally, urban sediment organic matter content is often elevated, potentially extending pyrethroid half-lives to weeks or months and posing continuous threats to benthic biota. ,,

Moreover, urban aquatic systemssuch as ornamental water features and engineered channelsexhibit hydrological and ecological characteristics distinct from those of natural systems. The bioavailability of pyrethroids in water is influenced by urban-specific suspended particulates (e.g., construction dust, traffic emissions) and dissolved organic matter. , While these substances may mitigate acute toxicity to some degree, the structurally simplified and limited self-regulatory capacity of urban aquatic ecosystems renders them more vulnerable to such pollutants. Research indicates that pyrethroid 24-h LC50 values for aquatic insects range from 0.02 to 13 μg/L, and common urban taxa such as chironomid larvae fall within this sensitivity rangeimplying that even low environmental concentrations can significantly disrupt aquatic ecosystems.

In soils, pyrethroids degrade relatively rapidly but strongly adsorb to soil particles; in urban green spaces, compaction and surficial coverage further constrain natural degradation processes. , Consequently, pyrethroid contamination in urban waters originates less from soil leaching than from surface runoff or atmospheric deposition of pesticide-laden soil particles mobilized by heavy rains or wind erosion. Furthermore, complex co-occurring xenobiotics in urban environmentssuch as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and heavy metalsmay interfere with degradation pathways, generating transformation products rarely observed in natural settings. The environmental behavior and ecological risks of such novel contaminants require a systematic evaluation.

Induction of Mosquito Resistance and Exacerbation of Human Health Risks

Frequent chemical mosquito control in urban ecosystems has induced resistance and compounded contamination issues. , Surveillance data revealed that Aedes albopictus populations in cities such as Guangzhou and Kunming presented resistance ratios to lambda-cyhalothrin that reached hundreds of offspring. Primary resistance mechanisms include target-site mutations in the mosquito nervous system, rendering insecticides ineffective, − and overactivation of P450 metabolic enzymes, enabling rapid pesticide detoxification. − This evolutionary arms race compels control agencies to escalate dosage and application frequency. More alarmingly, the incremental load of pyrethroids disperses through urban air currents, stormwater networks, and surface runoff, contaminating park soils, ornamental waters, and even indoor dust in densely populated areas and creating a persistent, low-concentration multimedia exposure environment. Chronic urban resident exposure via inhalation and hand–mouth contact may trigger neurobehavioral abnormalities or endocrine disruption. , The synergistic crisis of vector-resistance-driven control failure and ambient pesticide pollution not only undermines the emergency response capacity for disease outbreaks but also constitutes a public health emergency.

Impairment of Ecosystem Services

The degradation of urban ecosystem services resulting from large-scale chemical mosquito control can be analyzed by explicit and implicit methods. Explicit impacts include the following: first, severe weakening of biological mosquito controlnatural predators such as spiders, dragonflies, and frogs suffer collateral damage, reducing ecosystem self-regulation capacity; second, a marked decline in pollination servicesreduced bee and butterfly populations directly impair plant reproduction and fruit yields in urban green spaces and community gardens; and third, disruption of water purificationdestruction of aquatic decomposer communities diminishes self-purification capacity, exacerbating eutrophication risks in urban water features.

The implicit impacts are more profound and are potentially irreversible. First, the rupture of food web basal linksthe elimination of nontarget aquatic insectsdeprives higher trophic levels (e.g., birds and bats) of food resources, and such trophic cascades progressively restructure entire ecological communities. Second, soil ecological function degradationpesticide residues suppress soil microbial and microfaunal activity, impeding nutrient cycling and potentially increasing reliance on artificial fertilization in urban greenspaces. Most critically, socioecological resilience is erodedfrequent chemical applications reinforce public dependency on pesticidesdistressing community motivation for proactive source reduction (e.g., eliminating standing water) and undermining the social foundations of sustainable mosquito management. Although less conspicuous, this implicit damage renders urban ecosystems more vulnerable to future environmental stressors.

Urban Ecological Impact Assessment Theory and Basic Principles

Unlike the stringent pesticide registration and environmental risk assessment systems in Europe, North America, and other regions, China’s decision-making for chemical vector control has traditionally prioritized public health emergency response efficiency over prospective ecological risk assessment. Although the regulations on pesticide administration and environmental protection laws contain general provisions on pesticide use, specialized ecological regulatory standards and mandatory environmental impact assessments for large-scale urban chemical mosquito control are lacking. This regulatory gap implies that economic costs and short-term vector suppression efficacy often dominate actual decision-making, whereas ecological risk considerations rely on voluntary engagement by local environmental protection agencies, which lack institutionalized constraints.

This assessment framework aims to overcome the limitations of conventional decision-making, which focuses solely on direct economic costs and the short-term vector control efficacy of chemical mosquito control. It systematically incorporates ecological degradation and loss of ecosystem services into the evaluation scope, shifting the assessment perspective from singular cost accounting to comprehensive value trade-offs. Grounded in the pressure–state–response (PSR) framework and supported by multidisciplinary theories, this study follows clearly defined operational design principles to construct a scientific, systematic, and implementable urban ecological impact assessment system (Figure ), thereby providing theoretical and methodological foundations for ecological risk assessment of large-scale chemical mosquito control.

Core theories underpinning this framework.

Core Framework Model

The PSR assessment model was originally developed by Canadian statisticians Tony Friend and David Rapport, with its core purpose being to analyze the interactions among environmental pressures, environmental states, and societal responses. In the 1970s, the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) subsequently adopted and applied this model in its environmental reports to examine environmental issues, after which it gained widespread application in ecosystem assessment, sustainable development evaluation, and related fields. This study adapts the PSR framework to the urban vector management context, establishing the structural foundation of the assessment framework through the logical chain of chemical mosquito control pressureecosystem state changemanagement response adjustment. Specifically, large-scale chemical mosquito control operates as an external “pressure”, which, through environmental medium migration and transformation, induces changes in the “state” of urban ecosystems (including nontarget organism mortality, biodiversity decline, and ecosystem functional impairment), thereby necessitating “response” measures from society and managers (such as adjusting control strategies, initiating ecological restoration, and optimizing governance decisions).

Supporting Theoretical Framework

This framework integrates the following core theories to establish the foundational logic and value orientation of the assessment system: (1) The PSR theory provides the structural foundation of the assessment framework. (2) The theory of ecosystem integrity and connectivity prompts us to examine how chemical stress propagates through food webs and ecological niches to trigger cascading effects, ultimately impairing urban ecosystem structure and function, such as biodiversity decline, altered community composition, and disruptions to energy flow and nutrient cycling, thereby providing a theoretical basis for comprehensively identifying the ecological impacts of chemical mosquito control. (3) Ecosystem service valuation theory in environmental economics, which provides a methodology for making hidden costs explicit by monetizing or quantifying losses in ecosystem services such as clean air, water self-purification, and natural mosquito predation, thereby revealing the true socioeconomic costs overlooked in conventional market transactionsi.e., hidden ecological costs. This theory bridges the gap from ecological impact identification to value quantification.

Operational Design Principles

On the basis of these theories, the assessment framework adheres to five core principles: (1) Systematic principles, emphasizing that the complete causal chain from source pressure, medium transport, and ecological impacts to socioeconomic effects must be reflected in the framework. (2) Hierarchical and integrative principles require assessment to sequentially traverse four progressive levels: effects, functions, services, and values. This means starting from acute toxicological effects on individual organisms, advancing to functional indicator changes at the population and community levels, then evaluating alterations in ecosystem service flows, and finally attempting to quantify their socioeconomic values. (3) Dynamic and predictive principles, stressing that the framework should not be limited to ex post evaluation but must incorporate modeling tools such as system dynamics to simulate accumulation pathways and potential tipping points of ecological damage under various application scenarios, enabling early warning of ecological risks. (4) Operability and standardization principles ensure that each assessment component corresponds to measurable, obtainable core indicators that effectively interface with existing environmental monitoring systems, thereby guaranteeing the practicality and standardization of the evaluation process. (5) Decision-service orientation principle, which requires all assessment activities to ultimately be transformed into structured information that directly supports management decision-making. By integrating multidisciplinary theories and adhering to explicit design principles, this framework endeavors to construct a comprehensive assessment system that can both scientifically reveal the hidden ecological costs of large-scale chemical mosquito control and prospectively guide public health strategies. Its fundamental logic lies in converting fragmented, implicit ecological damage into concentrated, explicit decision-making parameters.

Urban Ecological Impact Assessment Indicators and Sampling Strategies

Indicator Framework Development

In response to the potential impacts of large-scale chemical mosquito control on urban ecosystems, this framework is an indicator system, as shown in Figure , encompassing four assessment dimensions: acute nontarget organism effects, environmental media contamination and transport, functional impairment of urban ecosystems, and human health risks. Each dimension features two tiers of indicatorscore and advanced. Core indicators prioritize uniformity and operability, focusing on key parameters obtainable through conventional ecological surveys and chemical analyses to ensure comparability across regions and time periods. Advanced indicators incorporate cutting-edge techniques such as environmental DNA (eDNA), biomarker analysis, and multimedia fugacity models to increase scientific depth and forward-looking capacity, revealing more cryptic or complex ecological effects on the molecular, community, and landscape scales. All indicators explicitly define their specific connotations, measurement methods, and pathways for economic loss conversion, thereby quantifying fragmented, implicit ecological damage into comparable economic parameters (Table S2). Through this systematic indicator design, the assessment system not only more accurately reflects the comprehensive ecological costs of chemical mosquito control but also provides robust scientific evidence for urban managers to weigh alternative control strategies, thereby driving the transformation of vector management toward environmentally sustainable practices.

Assessment indicator system for urban ecological and environmental impacts.

Sampling Strategy Design

To achieve economical, efficient, and objective assessment of large-scale chemical mosquito control risks, this study’s sampling strategy adheres to three core principles: risk hotspot orientation, spatiotemporal dynamic coverage, and synchronized response across environmental media. For site selection, the approach eschews geographic uniformity in favor of precision targeting of critical zones where pollutants are generated, transported, and accumulated and where ecological effects manifest. Specifically, the sampling network covers four typical areas: the application core zone (serving as the source point of ecological pressure and the area with the highest exposure risk), transport pathway zones (e.g., stormwater outfalls, to identify dispersion patterns via urban runoff), ecologically sensitive and accumulation sink zones (including urban water features and park green spacessensitive areas for assessing nontarget biological effects and functional impairment), and nonapplication control zones.

With respect to sampling timing, single postevent sampling cannot capture the dynamic evolution of ecological damage or establish causal relationships. Therefore, this study employs a longitudinal time series design to track four complete ecological response stages systematically: pre-exposure baseline, peak exposure, short-term recovery, and long-term trends. Specific sampling methods include 1–2 weeks before application to establish environmental baseline values and biological community baselines, 24–72 h after application to capture acute toxicological effects and contamination peaks, and follow-up sampling at 1–2 weeks and 1–3 months postapplication to evaluate short-term recovery capacity and potential long-term residual effects.

In developing specific sampling protocols, the objective is to optimize information acquisition efficiency under economically feasible conditions, prioritizing environmental media and taxa with a high information density and controllable costs. The recommended scheme comprehensively considers sample representativeness, collection economy, and measurement standardization for measurement. For example, sediments are considered core environmental media because of their strong sorptive enrichment of pyrethroids, effectively amplifying the contamination signals. However, given the highly dynamic nature of urban ecosystems where sediment–water systems rarely achieve thermodynamic equilibrium, this study employs apparent distribution ratios in lieu of traditional equilibrium partition coefficients to directly reflect actual partitioning under urban pulse pollution conditions. Aquatic insects (e.g., chironomid larvae) and pollinators are designated core bioindicator groups because of their high sensitivity and low sampling costs. Additionally, indoor dust collection combined with structured community questionnaires enables a cost-effective preliminary assessment of human exposure levels and potential health risks. The specific sampling types, methods, and minimum sample size recommendations are detailed in Supporting Information Table S3.

In emergency response scenarios, public health interventions often require immediate initiation, precluding the 1–2 week preexposure baseline surveys typically conducted under normal circumstances. To address such urgent situations, this study proposes three alternative baseline establishment strategies: (1) reference site approach: selecting neighboring cities or regions with comparable geographical and ecological conditions where no spraying is currently conducted as surrogate baselines; (2) historical data approach: utilizing monitoring data from the same city during the equivalent season in previous years or drawing from public databases; and (3) retrospective reconstruction approach: conducting intensive sampling at 24 h postapplication and comparing it against 72-h recovery monitoring data to derive relative impact magnitudes. Although these methods are suboptimal, they nonetheless preserve the framework integrity when a rapid response is imperative.

Assessment Methodology

The ecological risk posed by chemical mosquito control is identified through sampling-based monitoring of ecosystem state changes, with the risk quotient (RQ) , quantifying the exposure risk for different receptors (e.g., aquatic insects, bees, and humans). Additionally, while target resistance does not directly alter the environmental fate, it significantly increases the spraying frequency and total environmental loading, thereby indirectly amplifying ecological risk. Consequently, a resistance correction factor (RCF) is incorporated into the calculations. The calculation formula is as follows: RQ=(EC/PNEC)×RCF where EC is the actual environmental concentration of the active ingredient and PNEC is the predicted no-effect concentration (safety threshold) for the receptor. PNEC values reference the Guidance on Environmental Risk Assessment of Pesticide Registration (NY/T 2882-2016) and are set hierarchically by receptor type. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) resistance intensity classification criteria: low intensity (1× < mortality <5×, RCF = 1.0), moderate intensity (5× < mortality <10×, RCF = 1.5), and high intensity (mortality >10×, RCF = 2.0).

Drawing upon China’s NY/T 2882-2016, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the classification of the risk quotient was based on previously existing studies: − RQ < 0.1 (low risk), 0.1 ≤ RQ < 1 (medium risk), and RQ ≥ 1 (high risk), providing a clear risk classification basis for subsequent economic quantification and decision-triggering mechanisms.

Common approaches for assessing ecological risk include the RQ method, probabilistic risk assessment (PRA), and species sensitivity distribution (SSD). This study selects the RQ method on the basis of three primary considerations: (1) operational feasibilityRQ requires only the ratio of environmental concentration to PNEC, with moderate data demands suitable for rapid screening in resource-constrained cities; (2) decision-making alignmentthe three-tier RQ classification directly corresponds to this framework’s tiered management strategies (routine monitoring/intensified control/emergency protocols), facilitating implementation by managers; and (3) international standardizationRQ is adopted by the EFSA, EPA, and China’s Guidelines for Environmental Risk Assessment of Pesticide Registration (NY/T 2882.1-2016), ensuring result comparability. When conditions permit, the PRA or SSD may be applied for further validation in high-risk areas, forming a two-tier “RQ screeningadvanced verification” system.

Urban Ecological Impact Assessment Process

To enable risk assessment of large-scale chemical mosquito control in urban ecosystems, this study designs a phased assessment process that spans the entire decision-making cyclepreapplication, during application, and postapplicationforming a closed-loop management system (Figure ). The process aims to avert risks at the source, precisely diagnose damage, implement timely restoration interventions, and achieve the continuous optimization of governance strategies.

Urban ecological impact assessment process.

Planning and Avoidance Stage

The core objective is to prioritize ecological protection in decision-making, thereby avoiding and mitigating ecological risk from the outset. First, ecological sensitivity zoning is conducted by integrating spatial data on urban water systems, park green spaces, nature reserves, residential areas, apiaries, and drinking water source protection zones via geographic information systems (GISs). An urban ecological sensitivity classification map is generated on the basis of regional ecological functional importance and nontarget organism distribution characteristics. This map stratifies the assessment area into nonspray zones (e.g., water source core areas and rare species habitats), restricted-spray zones (e.g., densely populated parks and community gardens), and conventional-spray zones. Second, differentiated contingency plans for each zone should be developed, specifying a negative list of chemicals, maximum application dosages, appropriate timing (e.g., avoiding peak bee foraging periods), and application methods (e.g., prohibiting ultralow-volume spraying near water bodies). Simultaneously, clear activation and termination thresholds are established, ensuring that both vector-borne disease risk and ecological risk inform decision-making.

Monitoring and Diagnosis Stage

Initiated during and after application, this stage aims to capture ecological impacts in real time through an integrated monitoring network and a complete damage diagnosis. The monitoring system encompasses the following steps: (1) Chemical monitoring: Systematically collect water, soil, and sediment samples at predefined points (including application zones, transport pathways, and control areas) to analyze residues of target insecticides and their toxic metabolites via gas chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, tracking contamination levels and transport trends. (2) Ecological effect monitoring: Employ both traditional and modern techniques. In aquatic ecosystems, environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding is used to rapidly monitor shifts in aquatic insect communities (e.g., mayflies and caddisflies), supplemented by Daphnia magna acute toxicity tests to verify comprehensive water toxicity. For terrestrial ecosystems, pollinators (bees and hoverflies) and soil arthropods are monitored via standard quadrat and trapping methods to assess changes in species composition, abundance, and community structure. (3) Resistance monitoring: Regularly collect field mosquito populations to determine their resistance levels and dynamics to commonly used insecticides under laboratory conditions. On the basis of the collected data, risk quotients (RQs) quantitatively characterize risk to ecological receptors, and environmental economic accounting methods (e.g., the market value method and replacement cost method) are applied to monetize losses in impaired ecosystem services (e.g., pollination and water purification), providing intuitive evidence for subsequent decision-making.

Response and Restoration Stages

This stage focuses on the rapid response to monitoring signals and proactive intervention in damaged ecosystems. First, an early warning and suspension mechanism is established by setting explicit action thresholds for key ecological indicators (e.g., instantaneous bee mortality and water body RQ). Once monitoring data exceed thresholds, corresponding alerts are triggered, providing scientific recommendations for halting or adjusting spray operations, thereby forming a closed loop from monitoring to management. Second, targeted restoration measures should be initiated in confirmed damaged areas, such as the application of high-efficiency degrading microbial agents in water bodies with high pesticide residues or artificial propagation and the release of native natural enemies (e.g., dragonfly nymphs and parasitoid wasps) to reestablish biological control services and facilitate ecological recovery.

Review and Optimization Stages

As the critical component for self-improving the assessment process, comprehensive postassessment and auditing are conducted after each large-scale mosquito control campaign to compile a specialized report summarizing ecological impacts, management effectiveness, and identified issues. More importantly, empirical data obtained from the current assessment cycle (e.g., localized pesticide degradation rates and species-specific sensitivity distributions) are fed back into the GIS databases and system dynamics models used in the first stage to calibrate and optimize model parameters and structures. Through this mechanism, the entire assessment process forms a “learning–adaptation” cycle, ensuring that subsequent contingency planning becomes more scientific and precise, thereby driving the continuous optimization of urban vector management strategies.

Case Demonstration

To validate the feasibility and operability of the proposed assessment framework, this section presents a simplified demonstration of the complete ecological risk assessment process during a dengue fever emergency response using the hypothetical city of “X” as an example.

Scenario Setting: “X” is a subtropical coastal city. In August 2025, a dengue fever outbreak occurred, prompting emergency response measures involving spatial spraying of pyrethroid insecticides across the core urban area (50 km2). This zone encompasses three landscape lakes (total area of 0.12 km2), 12 community parks, and dense residential neighborhoods, with impervious surfaces covering 65% of the land area.

Step 1: Precaution and avoidance. GIS overlay analysis identifies landscape lakes as ecologically sensitive zones, demarcating no-spray zones (lakes), restricted-spray zones (parks), and routine-spray zones. On the basis of the local half-life of deltamethrin (7 days) and chironomid larval toxicity (48 h-LC50 = 0.5 μg/L), the model predicts a peak lake concentration of 2.3 μg/L 30 min postrunoff under routine dosage, yielding RQ = 4.6 (high risk). A differentiated protocol is accordingly formulated: Bti formulations substitute pyrethroids within 200 m of lake perimeters, dosages in remaining areas are reduced by 30%, and applications avoid peak honeybee foraging periods (09:00–16:00).

Step 2: Monitoring and diagnosis. Monitoring was initiated within 72 h postapplication, with the week preceding spraying used as the baseline and adjacent unsprayed areas used as a reference. The results indicate the following: District A, where adjusted protocols were implemented, has an aqueous concentration of 0.4 μg/L (RQ = 0.8, moderate risk); Districts B and C, where protocols were not strictly enforced, present high risk (RQ > 3), with acute mayfly nymph mortality observed.

Step 3: Response and restoration. Tiered restoration is implemented for Districts B and C: Week 1addition of degrading bacterial agents (10 mg/L); Week 2release of dragonfly nymphs (5,000 individuals per lake area); Weeks 3–4recovery monitoring. By Week 4, mayflies reappeared, and the pollinator visitation rate had recovered to 85%. The economic assessment revealed total emergency mitigation and restoration costs of 450,000 RMB, representing a 62% reduction compared with the estimated losses under the no-intervention scenarios.

Step 4: Review and optimization. A postapplication review at 3 months revealed the following: (1) actual degradation rates exceeded the model predictions by 20% (elevated temperatures and precipitation accelerated hydrolysis); (2) biological regulation contributed 35% to recovery; and (3) the average monitoring-response time was 6 h. These data are fed back into model parameter updates, providing localized foundations for subsequent assessment rounds.

The case parameters are established on the basis of typical scenarios; practical application requires local calibration. Monetary estimation of ecological and economic losses involves uncertainties; more refined methodologies may be explored in future research.

Construction of an Ecologically Rational Integrated Management System for Urban Mosquito Vectors

In response to the mounting pressures that large-scale chemical mosquito control exerts on ecosystems, urban vector management urgently requires a transition from reactive, chemically dependent approaches to an ecologically rational paradigm that is context-appropriate, hierarchically stratified, and technologically integrated. On the basis of the aforementioned framework and case validation, this study constructs a comprehensive governance system anchored in source control, supported by ecological regulation, supplemented by precision chemical intervention, and founded on social participation (Figure ). This framework fully accounts for variations in climate, geographic features, socioeconomic development levels, and control difficulty across cities, aiming to achieve synergistic gains in both public health security and urban ecosystem health.

Differentiation Control Strategies Based on Climate and Geographic Features

Climate conditions (e.g., temperature, humidity, precipitation, and seasonal variation) and geographic location (e.g., coastal, inland, river valley, and mountainous) jointly determine mosquito species composition, reproduction rates, annual activity cycles, and disease transmission potential. In tropical/subtropical rainy cities such as Guangzhou and Shenzhen, where mosquitoes reproduce year-round and dengue risk persists continuously, the control system should prioritize sustained environmental management and biological regulation. This involves fully implementing the “sponge city” concept to optimize stormwater infrastructure and eliminate standing water, widely establishing rain gardens and infiltration ponds in parks and communities, performing year-round larval control via Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti), and vigorously promoting Wolbachia technology for population suppression. − In cities with a temperate monsoon climate, such as Beijing and Zhengzhou, where mosquito activity is distinctly seasonal, a seasonally intensified control model is recommended: focusing on clearing overwintering habitats during off-peak seasons, deploying larvivorous fish and natural enemy insects before peak seasons, and initiating precision chemical intervention only when the epidemic risk escalates significantly. In arid/semiarid cities such as Urumqi and Yinchuan, where breeding sites are relatively limited and concentrated in artificial water bodies such as irrigation canals and reservoirs, a key point-source control strategy should be adopted, employing GIS mapping for list-based management of potential breeding sites and targeted deployment of larvivorous fish or precision Bti application in critical water bodies.

By interfacing with the Köppen–Geiger climate classification system, differentiated control strategies can be further extended to diverse global regions, expanding the framework’s international applicability. Importantly, the integrated management systemencompassing source reduction, biological control, and precision chemical interventionis contingent upon vector ecological characteristics rather than specific geographical settings, rendering this framework globally applicable.

Tiered Response Integrating Urban Characteristics and Control Difficulty

A city’s socioeconomic structure, built-environment density, and resource capacity directly influence the feasibility and effectiveness of control measures. In high-density built-up areas, where space is limited and public green spaces and water bodies are limited but population exposure risk is high, control should emphasize precision microenvironmental management: guiding residents and property managers to eliminate microscale standing water in households and buildings (e.g., flowerpot saucers, air conditioner condensates), installing pheromone traps or automatic lure-and-kill lights in adult mosquito habitats such as underground parking garages and stairwells, and, during outbreaks, strictly limiting vehicle-mounted ultralow-volume spraying to street spaces while avoiding peak pedestrian hours. In urban–rural fringe areas and undeveloped zones, where the environment is complex and sanitation infrastructure is inadequate, a strategy combining engineering governance with community empowerment is needed: government-led environmental remediation to fill depressions and dredge ditches, coupled with incentive mechanisms to mobilize community residents to voluntarily clear breeding sites such as vacant land and discarded tires. In water network-dense or ecologically sensitive cities with numerous landscape water bodies, wetlands, or proximity to nature reserves, where the ecological value is high, chemical spraying near water should be strictly restricted or prohibited. The control system must be “ecology-first,” vigorously developing and applying biological regulation technologies such as systematically releasing native larvivorous fish species, establishing protective buffer zones in areas rich in amphibians and aquatic insects, and exploring the use of insect growth regulators (IGRs) and other larval control agents with relatively little impact on nontarget organisms.

Intelligent Early Warning and Precision Intervention via Convergent Future Technologies

Future vector control will inevitably converge with intelligent and information technologies, transitioning from “human-wave tactics” to “smart decision-making.” By integrating multisource data from IoT sensors, remote sensing, drone aerial photography, and citizen science apps, we can construct a real-time predictive model for mosquito occurrence and disease transmission risk. In combination with weather forecasting, this enables proactive prediction of mosquito density hotspots and disease outbreak foci, providing a basis for precise resource allocation. The development of high-resolution remote sensing and precision spraying technologies based on unmanned aerial vehicles enables point-to-point application at inaccessible breeding sites (e.g., rooftops and high-rise building green spaces) and adult mosquito habitats, drastically reducing pesticide usage and nontarget exposure. Additionally, continuous attention should be given to advances in frontier technologies, such as genome-editing technologies for modifying mosquito populations, the development of highly efficient and environmentally friendly chemical pheromone attractants that target specific mosquito species, and the utilization of AI to analyze multisource heterogeneous data for continuously optimizing decision support systems. Building a Multistakeholder Social-Ecological Resilience System

The ultimate implementation of technological measures is dependent on effective social governance and broad public participation. A joint prevention and control platform comprising multiple departments, including public health, the ecological environment, housing and urban–rural development, landscaping and water affairs, and education and publicity, should be established to break information silos and achieve policy synergy and resource integration. Through scientific public communication, school education, and workplace programs, residents can be transformed from passive protection recipients to active control participants, continuously implementing core behaviors, such as “eliminating household standing water”, to build a social immune system for vector control. The public should be guided to understand the value of ecosystem services (e.g., natural enemy mosquito control) and recognize the long-term hazards of excessive chemical dependence, thereby fostering conceptual alignment with and supporting ecologically rational control strategies.

Effective implementation of the aforementioned control measures relies on robust risk monitoring, early warning mechanisms, and social mobilization systems. On the one hand, a data-driven intelligent monitoring and early warning network should be established by integrating GIS, remote sensing, IoT sensors, and citizen science reports to enable real-time monitoring and big data analysis of mosquito density, resistance levels, meteorological parameters, and disease cases, shifting the control paradigm from “postincident response” to “preemptive warning and in-process assessment.” On the other hand, a community-based whole-society participation mechanism should be implemented: through scientific public communication and health education, residents are guided to shift from passive protection recipients to active participants, continuously implementing core source reduction behaviors such as “eliminating standing water at home,” thereby building a social immune system for vector control.

Feasibility Optimization and Tiered Promotion Strategy for Framework Implementation

The proposed assessment framework and integrated governance system can be adapted through a tiered approach to accommodate cities with varying resource capacities, balancing scientific rigor with practical feasibility (Table ). Municipalities may begin with low-cost core indicators and progressively upgrade as capabilities permit. Furthermore, implementation and scaling can be facilitated through safeguard measures, including the establishment of technology-sharing platforms, tiered training programs, and dedicated subsidy schemes. For example, provincial-level ecological environments or public health departments could compile monitoring methodology manuals and streamlined assessment toolkits for free distribution to resource-constrained cities; deliver targeted training on basic sampling techniques, simplified risk assessment, and precision pesticide application; and provide equipment purchase subsidies to SMEs adopting eco-friendly vector control measures, thereby lowering implementation barriers.

Conclusions and Outlook

This study systematically analyzed the potential ecological impacts of large-scale chemical mosquito control on urban ecosystems and revealed that the ecological costs of this approach manifest not only as direct nontarget organism mortality, environmental media residues and transport, and declines in ecosystem service functions but also as systemic cascading effects, such as escalating vector resistance and increasing public health risks. We innovatively constructed an ecological risk assessment framework and indicator system covering acute nontarget organism effects, environmental media contamination and transport, functional impairment of urban ecosystems, and human health risks and proposed a comprehensive governance system anchored in source control, supported by ecological regulation, supplemented by precision chemical intervention, and founded on social participation with tiered adaptation strategies enabling implementation across cities with varying resource endowments. In the future, urban vector control should be a deepening of research and practice in the following directions. First, the standardization and localized application of the assessment framework should be promoted, and intelligent early warning platforms that integrate real-time monitoring and big data analytics to enhance risk identification and predictive capacity should be actively developed. Second, the research, development, and application of nature-based solutions for ecological regulation, such as optimizing the efficacy and adaptability of biological control methods, such as Wolbachia technology, should be strengthened. Furthermore, cross-sector collaborative governance mechanisms that incorporate ecological damage costs into decision-making considerations and enhance the sustainability and effectiveness of public participation through community empowerment should be established and improved. Ultimately, through multidisciplinary integration and technological convergence, vector management can be propelled from passive emergency response toward prospective, adaptive, and sustainable ecosystem management, resulting in the development of more resilient, healthy cities.
