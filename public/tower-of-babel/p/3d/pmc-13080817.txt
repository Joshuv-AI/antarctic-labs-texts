Thrombolysis for Obstructive HALT in Complex Valve-in-Valve TAVR
Authors: Massimo Baudo, Mohamed Rahouma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the management of severe aortic stenosis across the entire surgical risk spectrum. As the technology matures and is increasingly applied to younger patients and more complex anatomies, late complications related to bioprosthetic valve performance are emerging as a central clinical challenge. Among these, hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) has generated particular interest because it sits at the intersection of imaging advances, thrombosis biology, and long-term durability concerns.1

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Shrestha et al2 describe a striking case of obstructive HALT occurring 5 years after a highly complex valve-in-valve (ViV) TAVR, successfully treated with ultraslow, low-dose thrombolysis. Beyond its technical novelty, this report provides several important lessons for clinicians caring for the growing population of long-term TAVR survivors.

First, the case demonstrates that HALT is not merely an early or incidental imaging finding but can present later on and with severe hemodynamic consequences.3 The patient developed symptomatic prosthetic valve obstruction with a mean gradient of 66 mm Hg and a markedly reduced valve area, mimicking structural valve degeneration (SVD). In complex ViV anatomies—here a “tall-in-tall” configuration of 2 overlapping self-expanding valves—distinguishing thrombosis from irreversible SVD is critical, as management strategies diverge substantially. This report reinforces the indispensable role of multimodality imaging, particularly computed tomography, in identifying HALT and guiding therapy.4

Second, the authors highlight the unique vulnerability of complex TAVR constructs to thrombotic complications. ViV procedures, overlapping frames, altered flow dynamics, and areas of blood stasis may all predispose to leaflet thrombosis. As TAVR indications continue to expand and reinterventions become more common, such complex geometries are more likely to be encountered. This raises the question of whether current post-TAVR antithrombotic strategies are sufficient for these higher-risk subsets, or whether tailored approaches are needed.5

Besides leaflet thrombosis, a tall-in-tall configuration is more prone to coronary obstruction, commonly compromises subsequent coronary access, and may mechanically constrain the second prosthesis, leading to incomplete expansion, leaflet restriction, and potentially less favorable transvalvular gradients.6

Third, and most compellingly, this case illustrates that pharmacologic therapy can be a viable alternative to reoperation in carefully selected patients with obstructive HALT. Surgical explantation in this tall-in-tall setting and prior vascular grafting carried prohibitive risk. The successful use of ultraslow, low-dose alteplase administered without a bolus over 48 hours resulted in dramatic hemodynamic and symptomatic improvement, with no reported bleeding complications. This approach echoes prior experiences with low-dose thrombolysis in prosthetic valve thrombosis, adapted thoughtfully to the TAVR population.7 While anticoagulation alone is often effective for nonobstructive HALT,8 this case suggests that even severe obstruction may be reversible with carefully executed thrombolytic therapy when surgery is not an option.

From a broader perspective, this report invites reflection on how we conceptualize late TAVR failure. Traditionally, rising gradients years after implantation have been attributed to SVD, often prompting consideration of redo intervention.9 However, as this case demonstrates, thrombosis can masquerade as SVD even in the very late phase. A systematic diagnostic pathway that prioritizes exclusion of HALT before committing patients to high-risk reintervention seems warranted.

Looking forward, several knowledge gaps remain. The true incidence of late obstructive HALT in ViV and complex TAVR anatomies is unknown. Optimal antithrombotic regimens—both for prevention and treatment—are not well defined in this subset of patients, particularly in those without another indication for long-term anticoagulation. Finally, while thrombolysis proved effective here, standardized protocols, safety data, optimal surveillance strategies, and comparative effectiveness versus intensified anticoagulation are lacking. The relationship between HALT and subclinical neurological injury, cognitive outcomes, and cerebrovascular events remains incompletely understood. Prospective registries and collaborative studies will be essential to address these questions as the TAVR population continues to age and expand.

In summary, this elegant case challenges conventional reflexes toward reintervention and highlights the importance of nuanced diagnosis and individualized therapy in advanced TAVR care. Ultraslow thrombolysis emerges as a valuable tool in the armamentarium for obstructive HALT, particularly in patients with prohibitive surgical risk. As TAVR moves firmly into the realm of lifetime valve management, such thoughtful, patient-centered strategies will be increasingly important.
