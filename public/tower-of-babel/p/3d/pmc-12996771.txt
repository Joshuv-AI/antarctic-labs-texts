Research in context

Evidence before this study

Hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis are mold infections associated with high mortality rates and diverse clinical presentations, but the epidemiology and outcomes are not well understood in the United States. We conducted a systematic search in PubMed and Google Scholar for published studies on the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, and social determinants of health (SDOH) of hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis in the United States published before December 1, 2025. We used the search terms, “hyalohyphomycosis”, “phaeohyphomycosis”, and “epidemiology”. Most studies identified were from single-centers or focused on clinical characteristics. One study looked at phaeophyomycotic abscess-associated hospitalizations but did not include disseminated phaeohyphomycosis or any hyalohyphomycosis. No studies examined SDOH variables and hospitalizations from these mold infections.

Added value of this study

Our study leverages a nationally representative hospital-based database to describe hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, two groups of mold infections that are poorly understood. We combined epidemiological, clinical, and SDOH variables to equip clinicians and public health professionals with information to address these fungal infections. We found hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis had substantial mortality rates. Hospitalizations involving these infections were more common among patients residing in more socially vulnerable areas and were frequently associated with administration of incorrect antifungal medications.

Implications of all the available evidence

Hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, although rare, are public health problems given the causative fungi are widespread in the environment and the high mortality rates. Diagnosis often relies on cultures that take weeks to result, often leading to inappropriate antifungal treatment. Innovation in non-culture-based diagnostics to identify causative fungi, coupled with programs to address social determinants of health that increase risk of infections, could help improve patient outcomes. Public health surveillance would be useful to monitor for changes in causative fungi and resistance patterns and to identify emerging risk factors.

Introduction

Hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis are mold infections associated with high mortality rates and diverse clinical presentations (e.g., superficial and subcutaneous skin infections, pulmonary, central nervous system, disseminated).1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Infections are more common in immunocompromised hosts but can occur in anyone. Direct inoculation or inhalation of environmental mold spores are the most common routes of infection.1,6 Hyalohyphomycosis is caused by hyaline (colorless) fungi (generally excluding Aspergillus). Causative genera include Acremonium/Sarocladium spp, Fusarium spp, Lomentospora spp. Paecilomyces spp, Purpureocillium lilacinum, Scedosporium spp, and Scopulariopsis spp.6 Phaeohyphomycosis is caused by dematiaceous (melanin-containing) molds such as Alternaria spp, Cladophialophora spp, Curvularia spp, Exophiala spp, Fonsecaea spp, Phaeoacremonium spp, Phialophora spp, and Verruconis spp.1

A complete understanding of the epidemiological, microbiological, and clinical characteristics of hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis in the United States is lacking, as existing information about these infections is typically derived from case studies or outbreaks. The prevalence of chromoblastomycosis (caused by dematiaceous fungi but distinguished by presence of muriform cells) and phaeohyphomycotic abscess is estimated at approximately 15 cases per 1 million persons; the rate of hospitalization is estimated at 0.4 cases per 1 million persons. However, both estimates did not include disseminated disease.7,8 The prevalence of hyalohyphomycosis is unknown. Clinical treatment guidelines recommend a mold active azole (usually voriconazole), amphotericin B, or a combination depending on the mold that causes hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis.9 Only Fonsecaea pedrosoi, Scedosporium spp, and Lomentospora prolificans have Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)-approved epidemiolocal cutoff values; no causative fungi have CLSI-approved breakpoints.10,11

To better understand hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis in the United States, we examined hospitalizations associated with their causative fungi to inform potential public health and clinical interventions.

Methods

We used the 2019–2023 Premier Healthcare Database (https://offers.premierinc.com/rs/381-NBB-525/images/PremierHealthcareDatabaseWhitepaper.pdf), a hospital-based all-payer database that contains inpatient and outpatient visit data, including claims and billing information, financial, and pharmacy data from >1000 US hospitals; laboratory data (encompassing both in-house and send-out testing) are available from a subset (∼25%) of hospitals. The database includes nearly 9 million inpatient admissions per year, representing approximately one-quarter of all inpatient admissions in the United States.

We identified hospitalizations with positive cultures for molds causing hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, selecting those in which the patient received at least one dose of a systemic antifungal medication, a potential indicator that the clinician believed the infection was real and the mold was not a contaminant. For hyalohyphomycosis, we examined Fusarium spp and Scedosporium spp separately because of their documented resistance and reports suggesting that they are the most common causes of hyalohyphomycosis.12,13 We identified concurrent conditions and complications using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) discharge diagnosis codes listed in any position on the record (Supplemental Table S1). We described demographic characteristics, underlying conditions, laboratory testing, treatment, outcomes, and social determinants of health for hospitalizations with each type of mold infection. Summary statistics with 10 or fewer hospitalizations are not shown.

Social determinants of health were classified according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), a place-based index designed to identify and quantify communities experiencing social vulnerability.14 The SVI uses 16 US census variables and groups them into four themes (socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation) and then combined into a single measure of overall social vulnerability. Overall and theme scores are interpreted as percentile rankings and expressed from 0 to 100; higher scores represent more vulnerable areas.

Ethical statement

This activity was reviewed by CDC, deemed research not involving human subjects, and was conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (See e.g., 45 C.F.R. part 46; 21 C.F.R. part 56; 42 U.S.C. §241(d), 5 U.S.C. §552a, 44 U.S.C. §3501 et seq.).

Results

We identified 185 hospitalizations associated with Fusarium spp, 75 with Scedosporium spp, 139 with other fungi causing hyalohyphomycosis, and 196 with phaeohyphomycosis from a total of 117 hospitals (Table 1). By disease, the median age ranged from 58 (interquartile range [IQR] 45–68) years for patients with Fusarium spp-associated hospitalizations to 62 (IQR 47–72) for other hyalohyphomycosis-associated hospitalizations. Most hospitalizations were among male patients (55%–65%) and non-Hispanic White (63%–75%) patients. Most patients were admitted from non-healthcare settings (61%–74%) and hospitalized in the South (69%–79%) at teaching hospitals (58%–70%). Common concurrent diagnoses were autoimmune or inflammatory disease (63%–74%), acute kidney failure (41%–48%), smoking or tobacco use (39%–48%), and diabetes (33%–44%). Presence of an ICD-10-CM code for fungal infection was present in 31%–35% of hyalohyphomycosis- and phaeohyphomycosis-associated hospitalizations. Discussion

This analysis of hospital-based data represents one of the largest US case series of hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis from non-outbreak settings.15 Hospitalizations involving these infections were more common among patients residing in more socially vulnerable areas and were frequently associated with administration of incorrect antifungal medications (fluconazole and echinocandins) and with substantial mortality rates (≥27%).

Our study's findings that older men were most commonly affected align with those in previous, smaller studies of hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, and generally with invasive fungal diseases.16 For example, a prospective study of 99 phaeohyphomycosis cases found that 60% of patients were male, with a mean age of 59.1 Invasive fusariosis has also been reported more commonly in men,4 although onychomycosis caused by Fusarium spp is more commonly reported in women.17 Scedosporium spp infections seem to be more common in men but data are limited.18 Retrospective studies and literature reviews have also found that P. lilacinum and Paecilomyces variotii more commonly affect male patients.2,3 The predominance in males may be related to activities exposing them to causative fungi or genetic predisposition.16

Underlying conditions such as solid organ transplantation (10% vs. 33%) and neutropenia (6% vs. 22%) were substantially less common in our study than in a previous study of phaeohyphomycosis.1 Hyalohyphomycosis is also believed to be common in immunocompromised patients,2, 3, 4,18 but our study found that <50% had immunosuppression-associated ICD-10-CM codes. Our data suggest that disease can also occur in patients without obvious immunosuppression, and that clinicians might consider including these mold infections on their list of differential diagnoses even in non-immunocompromised persons. Deadly outbreaks of Fusarium spp and phaeohyphomycosis have occurred in healthy persons with infections caused by contaminated medicines.15,19,20 Patients with hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis may have underlying immune-dysfunction which we were unable to fully assess in our study; for example, phaeohyphomycosis has been associated with Dectin-1 deficiency and CARD9 mutation.21,22

Inappropriate treatment may have contributed to the high mortality rates seen in our study. Echinocandins were frequently used (in ∼40% of hospitalizations) among patients with hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, despite not being active and having high minimum effective concentrations against most molds except Aspergillus spp.23,24 Fluconazole was also commonly used (>40%) but is ineffective (or has little in vitro activity) against most molds included in this analysis.5,24 The use of non-specific diagnostic tests in our analysis (e.g., BDG in ∼20% of hospitalizations) might indicate that clinicians suspected a fungal etiology, leading to use of echinocandins or fluconazole as empiric treatment for suspected candidemia. Cultures are the only readily available diagnostic method for hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, and the average turnaround time of approximately 1–3 weeks may have further delayed administration of appropriate mold-active antifungal treatment. Alternatively, some of the echinocandin or fluconazole use could have been for prophylaxis of fungal infections among high-risk patients (e.g., those with transplant or hematologic malignancy).

Social determinants of health have not been extensively explored for many invasive mold infections, particularly for the molds described in this analysis. Our analysis identified many hospitalizations among patients residing in more socially vulnerable areas.14 These findings should be interpreted with caution, as we cannot determine individual-level risk factors or causality. Sub-optimal living conditions (e.g., leaks, indoor mold growth, poor ventilation) or barriers to healthcare access (e.g., limited transportation, limited English language proficiency) may contribute to increased risk,25 but our data do not allow us to directly assess these pathways. Most patients in this analysis were admitted to the hospital from non-healthcare settings, suggesting possible community acquisition, as opposed to healthcare-related mold exposure. These results are hypothesis-generating and highlight the need for future studies with more granular, individual-level data to clarify the role of social determinants of health in the epidemiology of these rare mold infections.

Our study has several limitations. First, positive cultures in this dataset might not necessarily represent true infection, as many of these molds are common laboratory contaminants and human commensals.26 For example, Cladosporium cultures commonly came from the lung, but the fungus is found in high concentrations in the air and is thought to be a frequent contaminant if culture results from lung specimen.24 However, the high mortality rate and inclusion of antifungal prescribing in the case definition make this possibility less likely. Exclusion of patients who did not receive antifungal therapy could also have missed patients with true infections who died before they could receive treatment. Second, mortality attributable to mold infections could not be ascertained, particularly given the slow and progressive nature of some of these organisms, while others are more common causes of invasive mold infections with more rapid morbidity and mortality. Third, these data are a convenience sample of hospitalizations and may not represent the entire country; the West is notably under-represented in the dataset. Fourth, use of ICD-10-CM codes might misclassify or under-detect concurrent conditions. We found that only around one-third of hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis-associated hospitalizations had an ICD-10-CM code for fungal disease, similar to a previous study of invasive mold infections, suggesting that ICD-10-CM codes likely under-detect these infections.27 Fifth, the number of cultures identified to the species-level was too small to report. Accurate fungal identification and reporting to the species level is essential for guiding targeted antifungal therapy. This requires specialized expertise and ongoing training for laboratory staff. Investment in laboratory education and resources is crucial to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes.

Hyalohyphomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, although rare, are public health problems given the causative fungi are widespread in the environment and the high mortality rates. Diagnosis often relies on cultures that take weeks to result, often leading to inappropriate antifungal treatment. Innovation in non-culture-based diagnostics to identify causative fungi, coupled with programs to address social determinants of health that increase risk of infections, could help improve patient outcomes. Public health surveillance would be useful to monitor for changes in causative fungi and resistance patterns and to identify emerging risk factors.
