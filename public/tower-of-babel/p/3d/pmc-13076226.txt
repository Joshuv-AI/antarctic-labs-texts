Ubiquitylation of DNA polymerase β via atypical K27 chains signals a DNA damage response
Authors: Qingming Fang, Christopher A Koczor, Wynand P Roos, Steve McClellan, Joel F Andrews, Robert W Sobol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA polymerase β (Polβ) stability is tightly regulated by specific binding partners that prevent its degradation by the proteasome. Ubiquitin (Ub) chains, differing in their structural arrangements (topology), contribute to distinct cellular functions. To investigate Polβ ubiquitylation, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9-modified cell lines expressing endogenous, mClover-tagged Polβ. Our results reveal that Polβ is primarily modified with ubiquitin chains linked through lysine residues 27 [Ub(K27)] and 29 [Ub(K29)]. We further observed that Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains impact the utilization of ubiquitin chains linked through lysine residues 6 [Ub(K6)] and 48 [Ub(K48)] upon Polβ ubiquitylation. The ubiquitin ligase TRIP12 preferentially attaches Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains to Polβ. While Polβ ubiquitylation with Ub(K27) appears to have a non-degradative role, mixed ubiquitin chains and Ub(K48) chains trigger Polβ degradation. Following DNA damage, Polβ and XRCC1 translocate from the cytosol to the nucleus, while ubiquitylated Polβ relocates from the chromatin to the cytosol. This translocation of Polβ, modified with Ub(K27) chains, promotes the dissociation of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex within chromatin, and oxidative stress enhances their association in the cytosol. These findings demonstrate that atypical ubiquitin chain modifications play crucial roles in DNA repair and the DNA damage response, underscoring the unexpected importance of these chain topologies in maintaining genome integrity.

Introduction

Maintaining genome stability requires efficient formation of DNA repair and DNA damage response protein complexes. Coordination of DNA repair protein complexes by ubiquitin ligases plays a key role in maintaining genome integrity [1]. For instance, BRCA1 and its associated E3 ubiquitin protein ligase BARD1 are critical for homologous recombination (HR); defects in these factors contribute to the etiology of breast and ovarian cancer [1, 2]. Base excision repair (BER) is essential for maintaining the integrity of both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, as BER mediates the repair of a broad range of endogenous and exogenous DNA lesions [3–5]. Proteins involved in BER are also subject to ubiquitylation, a process that regulates their activity, localization, and overall homeostasis [4]. For example, thymine DNA glycosylase is degraded following ubiquitylation by the Cul4-DDB1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex [6, 7], and 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase 1 (OGG1) is targeted for degradation via ubiquitylation by the C-terminus of Hsc70-interacting protein (CHIP) [8]. CHIP also targets free X-ray cross-complementing protein 1 (XRCC1) for ubiquitylation and degradation [9].

DNA polymerase β (Polβ) plays a pivotal role in BER as the primary polymerase involved in this pathway [10, 11]. Its two enzymatic activities—nucleotidyl transferase and 5′ deoxyribose phosphate lyase—are important for both gap-filling and gap tailoring during BER [10, 11]. Polβ homeostasis is crucial for proper BER and genome maintenance, and its overexpression has been observed in approximately one-third of solid tumors [12]. Polβ overexpression can lead to increased spontaneous mutation rates, chromosomal aberrations, and tumorigenesis [13–16], while reduced levels increase susceptibility to cancer [17, 18]. Polβ abundance is regulated by its interaction with XRCC1, which together form a heterodimeric complex [9, 19–21]. We have previously shown that this interaction is critical for retaining and localizing Polβ to chromatin, and that it regulates Polβ stability via a ubiquitin-dependent proteasome pathway [9, 22], a process mediated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase thyroid hormone receptor-interacting protein 12 (TRIP12) [23]. Cytosolic Polβ levels are also regulated by a ubiquitin-independent proteasome pathway involving interaction with NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) in an NADH-dependent manner [22]. The Polβ/NQO1 complex transitions to a Polβ/XRCC1 complex under conditions of oxidative stress [22]. The question of whether dissociation and association of the Polβ/XRCC1 heterodimer is regulated by Polβ ubiquitylation remains open.

Ubiquitin (Ub) is a prevalent post-translational modifier, and its conjugation to lysine (K) residues regulates important cellular functions [24–26]. The type of ubiquitin chain linkage dictates the fate of ubiquitylated proteins [25, 27, 28], and the topology of polyubiquitin chain linkages provides structural insights into how different linkage types signal distinct cellular processes [25, 27, 28]. Both K48-linked and K11-linked polyubiquitin chains typically signal proteasomal degradation [24–26]. The K63 linkage promotes diverse processes, including autophagic degradation and non-degradative functions like protein transport or DNA repair [25, 29–31]. Atypical ubiquitin linkages, including K6, K11, K27, K29, and K33, are increasingly recognized for their roles in various non-proteolytic functions, such as protein trafficking, signalling, and autophagy, in addition to their involvement in proteasomal degradation [25, 27, 28, 32]. Ring finger protein 168 (RNF168) promotes ubiquitylation of histone H2A/H2A.X with a Ub(K27) linkage to signal DNA damage [33], and WD repeat and SOCS box-containing complex 1 (WSB1) ubiquitylates leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 protein (LRRK2) with either Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains to signal aggregation and provide neuronal protection in Parkinson’s disease [34]. However, our understanding of Ub linkage roles continues to evolve. As previously mentioned, we have demonstrated that TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ, thereby controlling cellular Polβ levels and its chromatin loading [23], a function that is critical for Polβ’s DNA repair activity. The specific types of Ub linkages employed by TRIP12 in this process, however, remain unknown.

To determine the ubiquitin linkages involved in Polβ modification, we utilized LN428, HCT116, and U2OS cells expressing an mClover-tagged, C-terminal, version of endogenous Polβ. We observed that Polβ is primarily ubiquitylated with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) linkages in all three cell lines, while HCT116 cells also exhibited Ub(K48) chain ubiquitylation. Surprisingly, we found that the ubiquitylation of Polβ with Ub(K27), Ub(K29), and Ub(K11) chains appears to impact the utilization of ubiquitin with Ub(K6) and Ub(K48) chains in cells. TRIP12 preferentially ubiquitylates Polβ with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains in vivo and in vitro. The ubiquitylation status of Polβ responds differently to various types of DNA damage. Both Polβ and XRCC1 are primarily localized to the nucleus, while ubiquitylated Polβ is translocated from chromatin into the cytosol post-DNA damage. The translocation of ubiquitylated Polβ with Ub(K27) chains following DNA damage facilitates dissociation of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex from the chromatin, while oxidative stress enhances formation of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex in the cytosol. Collectively, these data suggest that Polβ ubiquitylation plays important roles in DNA repair and the DNA damage response.

Materials and methods

Materials

All materials and supplies are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Plasmid/vector construction

All plasmids and lentiviral vectors developed for this report, developed previously, obtained commercially, or from colleagues, are defined in Supplementary Table S2.

Construction of lentiviral vectors for expression of Polβ-specific gRNA

Guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting Polβ exon 14, which contains the stop codon for the POLB gene, were designed using the CRISPR design tool (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/#) so as to replace the POLB stop codon with the mClover-stop sequence, allowing for the expression of a Polβ-mClover fusion protein from the endogenous POLB promoter. The sequence of oligonucleotides specific to each gRNA used is listed in Supplementary Table S3. The gRNA-specific oligonucleotides were cloned into the pX330-based plasmids as described [35, 36]. The pX330 plasmid was digested with the restriction enzyme BbsI, and the large fragment was purified with the QIAquick gel extraction kit (Qiagen). Each of the gRNA-specific oligonucleotides was phosphorylated and annealed with the complementary oligodeoxynucleotide. The gRNA-specific oligonucleotide duplex and the purified fragment were ligated with Quick Ligase (New England Biolabs), transformed into Stbl3 bacterial cells, and plated onto LB plates containing ampicillin (100 µg/ml). Colonies were picked and screened by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Colonies with the correct PCR product were cultured, and plasmids were isolated and examined by a PCR reaction using two pairs of PCR primers (pX330-Fw / gRNA-Re and gRNA-Fw / pX330-Re). These primers are described in Supplementary Table S3. One plasmid with accurate PCR products was used for future experiments and is listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Lentivirus production and transduction

Lentivirus production and transduction were performed as previously described [9, 23, 37]. Briefly, lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfection of the shuttle plasmids (e.g. such as pX330-Polβ-gRNA) together with the packaging vectors pMD2.g(VSVG), pVSV-REV, and pMDLg/pRRE into 293-FT cells using FuGene HD transfection reagent. After transfection for 48 h, the lentivirus-containing supernatant was collected and passed through 0.45 μm filters to isolate the viral particles free from cellular debris. Lentiviral transduction was performed as follows: 1–2 × 105 cells were seeded into each well of a six-well plate. Twenty-four hours later, lentiviral particles were mixed with Polybrene (2 µg/ml) and then added to the cells in each well, and incubated at 32°C for 16–20 h. Cells were then cultured at 37°C for 48 h, followed by selection for 1–2 weeks.

Construction of plasmids

To construct the pUC19-Polβ-Arm1-mAID-mClover-Hyg plasmid that served as the homologous donor, plasmids pMK290 (plasmid #1704) and pUC19-Polβ-Arms1 (plasmid #1792) were digested with BamHI, and the desired fragments were purified with the QIAquick gel extraction kit (Qiagen). The fragments were ligated and transformed into Stbl3 cells. The process to construct the plasmid was performed as described earlier. Colonies were picked and screened by a PCR reaction with two sets of primers (1521/1550 and 1525/1551; see Supplementary Table S3). Colonies with the correct PCR products were cultured, and plasmids were isolated and examined by another PCR reaction with two sets of primers (1521/1550 and 1525/1551; see Supplementary Table S3). Plasmids with correct PCR products were validated by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics). The primers for sequencing are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The plasmids with the correct sequence were used for the experiments.

To make plasmids expressing HA-Ub(K6R/K27R/K29R), HA-Ub(K11R/K27R/K29R), and HA-Ub(K27R/K29R/K48R), plasmid pRK5-HA-Ub(K27R/K29R) (plasmid #2150, see Supplementary Table S2) was used as template, and primers pairs (1634/1635, 1652/1653, or 1636/1637) with the corresponding mutation, were designed for site-directed mutagenesis and used for PCR with pfu DNA polymerase. The PCR product was digested by DpnI for 1 h, and 1–2 µl of PCR product was mixed with Top10 competent cells and transformed at 42°C for 30 s. Colonies were cultured, and plasmids were isolated and validated by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics). The primers for PCR and sequencing are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

To construct pGEX4T3 plasmids expressing GST fusion proteins to purify recombinant proteins [HA-ubiquitin (HA-Ub) and HA-Ub mutants] in Escherichia coli, primers with SalI and NotI restriction enzyme sites were designed and used for PCR to amplify the complementary DNA (cDNA) of the corresponding proteins [primer pairs 1607/1609 for HA-Ub, 1606/1609 for HA-Ub(K6), and 1608/1609 for HA-Ub(KO), HA-Ub(K27), HA-Ub(K29), HA-Ub(K48), and HA-Ub(K63)]. The sequence of the primers used is listed in Supplementary Table S3. Both the PCR products and the pGEX4T3 plasmid were digested by SalI and NotI, and the fragments were purified by gel purification with the QiaQuick Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen). The fragments were transformed into BL21-CodonPlus-RP cells. The positive plasmids were then isolated and validated by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics). The primers for sequencing are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Validated plasmids were used for protein expression and purification.

Development of cell lines that express endogenous Polβ tagged with mClover

LN428 cells were cultured in Alpha-MEM supplemented with heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10%), gentamycin (5 µg/ml), penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin, and L-glutamine (2 mM). HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s media supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. U2OS cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with heat-inactivated FBS (10%) and penicillin/streptomycin. Cells (1.5–3.0 × 105) were seeded in six-well plates. Twenty-four hours later, 1 µg of pX330-Polβ-gRNA (oligonucleotides 1823,1824, and 1825; see Supplementary Table S3) plus 1 µg of pUC19-Polβ-Arm1-mAID-mClover-Hyg (plasmid 1831) or, 1 µg of plasmid 1831 only, was transfected into cells with FuGene HD transfection agent (see Supplementary Table S2 for the plasmid list). Two days after transfection, cells were passaged and cultured in 10 cm dishes under selection with 200 µg/ml hygromycin (Hyg). After 10–12 days, transfected cells were sorted by flow cytometry as detailed below and were collected and cultured.

After the cells reached 70%–80% confluency, a fraction of the cells was then cultured to prepare a whole-cell lysate. Immunoblots were performed, and the level of mClover, Polβ-mClover, and Polβ was examined. The cells in the pool that show a high level of Polβ-mClover and a low level of mClover were then seeded in 96-well plates at 1 cell/well (for LN428 and U2OS cells) or 1.5 cells/well (for HCT116 cells). Following 7 days in culture, the colonies were examined, and those wells with a single colony were identified, and the media was changed. Following an additional 7 days in culture, wells with a single colony were passaged by trypsin digestion, and the cells were further cultured in 24-well plates. After the cells reached 70%–80% confluency, cells were passaged, with most of the cells seeded into six-well plates for immunoblot analysis and a small fraction of the cells seeded in a 24-well plate to prepare frozen stocks once the corresponding cells were shown to harbor the mClover tag on the C-terminus of the endogenous POLB gene. Whole-cell lysates were prepared from six-well plates, and the expression levels of Polβ-mClover, mClover, and Polβ were examined by immunoblot. Those single-cell clones with no expression of endogenous Polβ, together with a high level of expression of Polβ-mClover, were chosen for further confirmation and analysis. All the cells were cultured at 5% CO2 and 37°C. All the cell lines developed and used in this study and their growth media are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Flow cytometry and mClover cell sorting

Flow cytometric analysis and cell sorting were performed with a FACS Aria II SORP cell sorter (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) using Diva software Ver8.3. Gating was based on the non-transfected parental cell lines as a negative control (Supplementary Fig. S1.I, panel a). Cells with either high or low mClover expression were selected (Supplementary Fig. S1.I, panel b) and sorted. Purity of post-sorted cells was confirmed (Supplementary Figs. S1.I, panels c and d).

Live cell imaging

For live image analysis, 8–10 × 104 cells were seeded in a four-chamber cover glass-bottom vessel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #155382). Media was removed 24 h later, and then cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #62249, 2.5 µg/ml), MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Cell Signaling Technologies, #9082, 100 nM), and CellMask Deep Red plasma membrane stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C10046, 1:1000 dilution) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for 20 min. The staining buffer was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS three times. Finally, the cells were cultured with normal media, and the images were collected with a Nikon A1r laser scanning confocal microscope, equipped with a stage-top incubator (Tokai Hit), and using a Plan Apo λ 60× Oil immersion objective (N.A. 1.4).

Laser-induced DNA damage and protein recruitment analysis

For laser micro-irradiation, 5 × 104 cells were seeded onto an eight-chamber glass-bottom vessel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #155409). Laser micro-irradiation was performed 24 h later using a Nikon A1r confocal microscope. Live cells were imaged with a Nikon A1rsi laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with six visible wavelength lasers (405, 441, 514, 561, and 647 nm, Coherent), customized to add a UVA 355 nm laser (PicoQuant) controlled by a Brueker XY galvanometer, and equipped with a live-cell incubation chamber (Tokai Hit) maintained at 5% CO2 and 37°C, using a 40× oil-immersion objective for 355 nm laser micro-irradiation or a 20× objective for 405 nm micro-irradiation. For each experiment, a field was selected, a region of interest of 3 × 3 pixels was created within each selected cell nucleus, and cells were micro-irradiated using a custom micro-irradiation script implemented in NIS-Elements. A 355 nm laser (100% power, 2.5 s duration per site) or a 405 nm laser (100% power, 0.125 s duration per site) was used for micro-irradiation. Time-lapse images were collected every 15 s during a 10-min interval. Images of focal recruitment were quantified using an in-house developed analytical program termed MIDAS (Modular Irradiation, Detection, and Analysis System) to both detect focal recruitment and statistically analyze image results [38]. Forty individual cells (two sets of 10 cells were performed on two separate days) were analyzed and used to generate recruitment profiles and kinetic parameters.

Cell extract preparation, immunoprecipitation, and immunoblot

Cell extract preparation

For newly developed stable cell lines, protein expression levels were determined by immunoblot using whole-cell lysate. The concentration of the whole-cell lysate was quantified with a DC protein assay, following the microplate protocol provided by the company (Bio-Rad) or with Bio-Rad dye reagent, depending on how the whole-cell lysate was prepared.

Immunoprecipitation

To study whether Polβ-mClover interacts with XRCC1 and to (i) confirm that endogenous Polβ is tagged with mClover, (ii) determine which types of ubiquitin linkages in ubiquitylated Polβ is formed, (iii) how ubiquitylated Polβ is affected by DNA damage in cells, or (iv) in subcellular fractions with different time courses; GFP-TRAP was performed as described by the manufacturer with minor modifications (ChromoTek, Cat# gta-20). Briefly, cells were cultured on a 150 mm dish. After cells were ready for experiments, they were washed twice with cold PBS, and the cell pellet was collected by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Cells were lysed with cell lysis buffer (300 µl) (10mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5% Nonidet P40, and protease inhibitor cocktail tablet; Roche) for 30 min with extensive vortexing of the suspension every 10 min. The suspension was centrifuged for 15 min at >20 000 × g to collect the cleared lysate. Next, 25 µl of the well-suspended bead slurry was transferred to a 1.5 ml centrifugation tube, and ice-cold dilution buffer (500 µl) (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5mM EDTA, and protease inhibitor cocktail tablet; Roche) was added to the beads. The beads were then separated with a magnet for 1 min, and the supernatant was discarded. This bead equilibration was repeated twice. The cell lysate and 500 µl of dilution buffer were added to the beads, and the mixture was rotated end-over-end for >3 h at 4°C. The beads were separated by a magnet for 1 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The beads were washed 3–4 times with dilution buffer (500 µl). After washing, the beads were resuspended with 30–40 µl of 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer (Laemmli buffer) and boiled for 5 min by incubation in a boiling water bath or 10 min at 95°C.

To study whether TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ with Ub-K27, Ub-K29, or Ub-K48 linkages, a TRIP12 antibody was used to pull down endogenous TRIP12, and protein A/G plus agarose was used to pull down the TRIP12 antibody. To study the ubiquitin linkages in LN428 cells expressing Flag-Polβ(T304I) or Flag-Polβ(T304I/K206A/K244A), an M2 affinity gel was used to pull down Flag-Polβ(T304I) or Flag-Polβ(T304I/K206A/K244A) after cells were transiently transfected with pRK5-HA-Ub or pRK5-HA-Ub mutants for 48 h. To study which ubiquitylation site(s) in Polβ are ubiquitylated with the Ub-K27 linkage, LN428/Flag-Polβ(L301R/V303R/V306R: TM), LN428/Flag-Polβ(TM/K206A), LN428/Flag-Polβ(TM/K244A), and LN428/Flag-Polβ(TM/K206A/K244A: TM/DM) were transiently transfected with pRK5-HA-Ub(K27), and anti-Flag-magnetic agarose (Pierce) was used to pull down Flag-Polβ mutants. To determine how the ubiquitylation of Polβ affects the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction, anti-Flag-magnetic agarose was used to pull down Flag-Polβ or its mutants from the subcellular fractions of LN428/Flag-Polβ, LN428/Flag-Polβ(K244A), or LN428/Flag-Polβ(K206A/K244A: DM) cells. Immunoprecipitation (IP) with antibody and M2 affinity gel was performed as described previously [22]. IP with anti-Flag-magnetic agarose was performed similarly to the GFP-TRAP described earlier.

To study whether TRIP12 and the HECT domain of TRIP12 ubiquitylate purified Polβ in vitro, Myc-TRAP was performed to pull down Myc-TRIP12 and Myc-HECT from LN428 cells expressing Myc-TRIP12 or Myc-HECT [23], respectively. Myc-TRAP (ChromoTek, Cat# ytma-20) was performed as for the GFP-TRAP, as described earlier.

Immunoblotting

Twenty to thirty micrograms of whole-cell lysate or 5–15 µl of immunoprecipitated proteins were loaded on a precast 4%–12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris protein gel (InVitrogen), and proteins were separated for 1–2 h at 120–140 V. The proteins in the gel were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, and the membrane was blotted with primary antibodies, as indicated. The information for the primary antibodies used herein is listed in Supplementary Table S5. After washing, Immun-Star goat anti-mouse-HRP conjugate (Bio-Rad) or Immun-Star goat anti-rabbit-HRP conjugate (Bio-Rad) secondary antibodies were used. The membrane was illuminated, and the bands were quantified using the Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Genomic PCR and sequence analysis

Cells (1–5 × 106) were collected, and genomic DNA was isolated using DNeasy Blood & Tissue kits (Qiagen) as described in the protocol provided by the company. The PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3. To identify Polβ tagged with mClover, 100–200 ng genomic DNA was used as template, and primer pairs 1550/1521 and 1584/1521 were used. PCR was performed as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by the cycle of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 60°C for 45 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min. This cycle was repeated 30 times and then incubated at 72°C for 5 min. The final PCR product was examined by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The genomic DNA of the clone with the correct PCR amplicon was used for a large-scale PCR reaction in 3 × 100 µl tubes, and then the PCR product was purified with QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) or the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit if there was more than one PCR band. Purified PCR products were then sent to Eurofins Genomics for sequence validation.

Purification of recombinant proteins expressed in E. coli

To determine the ubiquitin linkages of Polβ, we purified HA-Ub and HA-Ub mutants, including HA-Ub(K27), HA-Ub(K29), HA-Ub(KO), HA-Ub(K48), and HA-Ub(K63), as well as Polβ and GST-Tev. The procedure was performed as we have described previously [22]. Briefly, BL21-CodonPlus-RP cells expressing GST-HA-Ub, GST-HA-Ub mutants, and GST-Polβ were utilized for GST-tag protein expression. An overnight culture was added to fresh LB medium with a 1:100 dilution. The cells were cultured until OD600 reached 0.6–1, then 0.2 mM Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added, and the cells were cultured overnight at 18°C. The cell pellet was collected and washed with lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1× Sigma protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM EDTA). Cells were resuspended in lysis buffer and lysed by sonication (20 s on / 20 s off, for 2 min). Cell lysates were then centrifuged at 10 000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C twice to collect the cell lysate supernatant. Cell lysate supernatant was then mixed with glutathione resin (2 ml), washed with lysis buffer, and the mixture was rotated for 3–4 h. The resin was washed with lysis buffer and then with 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl (Buffer A) to exchange into a low-salt buffer. The resin was resuspended with Buffer A and 0.5 ml of 1mg/ml GST-Tev protease and incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation. The resin was poured onto a column, and the flow-through was collected. The column was washed with Buffer A, and the flow-through was collected. The flow-through was concentrated with Millipore Amicon Ultra-4 centrifuge filters. The concentrated flow-through was loaded onto a gel filtration column. The fractions containing targeted proteins were collected and concentrated with Millipore Amicon Ultra-4 centrifuge filters. The purity of the purified proteins was examined by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by staining the gel with GelCode blue safe protein stain.

XRCC1 knockout by CRISPR/Cas9 and re-expression of XRCC1 in LN428, HCT116, and U2OS cells

A lentiviral vector expressing gRNAs targeting exon 2 of the XRCC1 gene was constructed, and the detailed procedure to generate XRCC1 knockout cells was described by us previously [22]. The XRCC1-KO cells, developed in the LN428/Polβ-mClover-2.18, HCT116/Polβ-mClover-2.19, and U2OS/ Polβ-mClover-2.12 cell lines, were first validated by immunoblot. To re-express XRCC1 in these cell lines, we designed a pENTR vector containing the cDNA of XRCC1 that cannot be recognized by the XRCC1 gRNA. A lentiviral vector expressing the gRNA-resistant XRCC1 was then constructed by Gateway cloning using this vector. The XRCC1-KO cells (LN428/Polβ-mClover-2.18, HCT116/Polβ-mClover-2.19, and U2OS/Polβ-mClover-2.12) were then transduced to re-express XRCC1, and the new cell lines were developed, as listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Lentiviral vectors expressing two shRNAs to knock down TRIP12 were purchased from Sigma and were described previously [23]. The sequence of each shRNA is listed in Supplementary Table S3. Lentiviral preparation and cell transduction are described as earlier. LN428/Polβ-mClover-2.19 and HCT116/Polβ-mClover-2.19 were transduced by lentivirus particles expressing TRIP12-shRNA, and the new cell lines were developed, as listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Plasmid transfection

Transient transfection was performed using the FuGene HD transfection reagent when using the plasmids pRK5-HA-Ub and pRK5-HA-Ub mutants. Briefly, cells (3–5 × 106) were seeded onto a 150 mm dish and incubated overnight at 37°C, 5% CO2. The next day, plasmid DNA (12 µg) was mixed with 36 µl of transfection reagent and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The mixture was then added to the cells in a 150 mm dish, mixed gently, and the cells were then cultured for 48 h. Cells were collected, and cell lysates were prepared for GFP-TRAP, Myc-TRAP, or Flag-M2-IP.

Ubiquitylation activity assay in vitro

IP was performed with a TRIP12 antibody (Bethyl Laboratories, Cat# A301-814A) to capture TRIP12 or with the Myc-TRAP reagent (ChromoTek, Cat# ytma-20) to capture Myc-TRIP12 or Myc-HECT, in all cases for analysis of in vitro E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. TRIP12 antibody with antigen was incubated overnight, then incubated with protein A/G plus agarose for 4 h. Myc-TRAP was performed as described earlier. The agarose beads with TRIP12 and magnetic beads with Myc-TRIP12 and Myc-HECT were washed four times with TBS buffer, then washed twice with 500 µl ubiquitylation reaction buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM DTT). The in vitro ubiquitylation assay was performed in 30 µl of buffer mixture containing 1.5 µl of 20× UbE1, 10 µl of 20× E2 (UbcH1, UbcH3, UbcH5a, UbcH5b, UbcH5c, UbcH6, UbcH7, UbcH8, UbcH10, and UbcH13), 1 µM ubiquitin aldehyde, 3 µl of 10× ubiquitylation buffer, 6 µl of 5× pyrophosphatase, 0.6 µl of 500 mM DTT, 1.5 µl of 20× magnesium/ATP cocktail provided by Enzo ubiquitylation kit, and 10 µg of HA-Ub and HA-Ub mutants, with or without 1µg of purified Polβ, as we have described [23]. Samples were incubated for 1 h at 37°C or 4°C. The samples were then mixed every 20 min and centrifuged for 30 s at 10 000 rpm. Samples were then transferred to a new tube. The beads were washed twice with TBS buffer, then 35 µL of 2× sample buffer (with β-mercaptoethanol) was added to each sample. The beads were boiled at 95°C for 5 min, and the IP elution was collected. An immunoblot was carried out to detect the ubiquitylated Polβ with different Ub linkages with anti-HA antibodies and Polβ and TRIP12 with corresponding Polβ (clone61) and TRIP12 antibodies, respectively. The scheme of the process is shown in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S4B.

Cell treatment with MMS, IR, and H2O2

To study whether the ubiquitylation status of Polβ is affected by DNA damage, HCT116/Polβ-mClover-2.19/XRCC1-KO-2.1 cells were transiently transfected with plasmids expressing HA-Ub(K27), HA-Ub(K29), or HA-Ub(K27R/K29R). Two days later, cells were treated with 2 mM methyl methane sulfonate (MMS) for 0, 0.5, or 3 h, then cells were collected, and GFP-TRAP was performed; or cells were treated with 10 Gy ionizing radiation (IR). Post IR treatment for 0, 0.5, or 3 h, cells were collected, and GFP-TRAP was performed.

To study whether the status of Polβ ubiquitylation induced by DNA damage affects the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction, HCT116/mClover, HCT116/Polβ-mClover, and LN428/Polβ-mClover cells were transiently transfected with plasmids expressing HA-Ub or HA-Ub(K27). For MMS treatment, 2 days later, cells were treated with 2 mM MMS for 1 h, the media were replaced with fresh media, and cells were cultured for 0 or 5 h, then cells were lysed, and GFP-TRAP was performed. For IR treatment, 2 days later, cells were treated with 10 Gy IR. Post-treatment, cells were cultured for 0, 1, 3, or 6 h, then cells were lysed, and GFP-TRAP was performed.

Results

C-terminal mClover-tagged endogenous human Polβ is functional

Our previous reports demonstrated that the interaction between Polβ and XRCC1 is essential for regulating Polβ’s location within chromatin and its stability through a process involving ubiquitin modification in human cells [9, 22, 23] and in mice [39]. To advance our mechanistic understanding of Polβ ubiquitylation in human cells, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 technology to directly tag the endogenous POLB gene encoded in U2OS, LN428, and HCT116 cells with the mClover fluorescent protein (Fig. 1A). This allowed us to examine how Polβ is ubiquitylated, and the roles of different types of ubiquitin linkages, when Polβ is present at physiological levels. We selected individual cells (single-cell clones) that consistently produced a Polβ-mClover fusion protein, avoiding clones expressing free mClover. The presence of the Polβ-mClover fusion protein was confirmed using immunoblotting after isolation using a GFP-TRAP method in U2OS, LN428, and HCT116 cells (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Importantly, adding mClover to the C-terminus of Polβ did not interfere with its normal interaction with XRCC1, as confirmed by the presence of XRCC1 bound to Polβ-mClover after GFP-TRAP isolation (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figs S1B). We also verified that the mClover tag was inserted correctly into the POLB gene, in-frame with exon 14, to create the expected Polβ-mClover fusion protein. This was done by analyzing the DNA of the selected single-cell clones and confirming the sequence by PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1C and D).

To understand where Polβ-mClover is expressed within the cells, live-cell imaging was performed. The majority of Polβ-mClover protein was found in the nucleus (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1E). This finding is consistent with the known localization of Polβ and confirms that tagging Polβ with mClover does not change its normal subcellular localization.

Next, we tested whether Polβ-mClover is recruited to sites of DNA damage. For this, laser micro-irradiation was used to induce nuclear DNA damage in the cells. Polβ-mClover, but not free mClover, was recruited to these sites of DNA damage when treated with either a 355 or 405 nm laser (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1F–H), laser wavelengths that generate primarily base damage and DNA single-strand breaks, with no measurable induction of DNA double-strand breaks [38, 40]. Furthermore, the kinetics of recruitment, including time to peak and half-life of recruitment, were similar between the endogenously tagged Polβ-mClover and a version of Polβ expressed using a different, artificial system (EGFP-Polβ expressed following lentiviral transduction) (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1F–H). These results demonstrate that the endogenously tagged Polβ-mClover is functional and therefore suitable for investigating which types of ubiquitin linkages target Polβ and how these linkages regulate Polβ’s stability and function.

Knockout of XRCC1 promotes ubiquitylation and degradation of Polβ-mClover

We previously demonstrated that XRCC1 is crucial for stabilizing Polβ in both human and mouse cells, through the formation of a Polβ/XRCC1 heterodimer [9, 23, 39]. To further investigate this role, we generated XRCC1 knockout (KO) cells using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in the three cell lines expressing mClover-tagged Polβ (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2A). As expected, XRCC1-KO resulted in reduced basal levels of Polβ and of Polβ-mClover, a finding consistent with our earlier report using cells overexpressing GFP-Polβ [9] (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2A). Re-expression of XRCC1 nearly restores the basal level of Polβ-mClover (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2B). When XRCC1 was knocked out, rapid degradation and significant ubiquitylation of Polβ-mClover was observed. Importantly, re-expression of XRCC1 prevented these effects (Fig. 2C and D; Supplementary Fig. S2C and D). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 prevented the observed Polβ-mClover degradation following XRCC1-KO (Supplementary Fig. S2C), suggesting that the Polβ-mClover degradation is regulated by a ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Although mClover was expressed at higher levels than Polβ-mClover, immunoblot analysis revealed no evidence of mClover ubiquitylation (Supplementary Fig. S2D), indicating that ubiquitylation targets Polβ-mClover, but not mClover.

Polβ is primarily ubiquitylated with K27, K29, and K48 linkages in cells

To determine the types of Ub linkages involved in Polβ ubiquitylation, we performed a cellular ubiquitylation assay. Cells were transiently transfected to express either unmodified ubiquitin (HA-Ub), one of seven mutant ubiquitin transgenes (each with only one functional lysine residue for the specific Ub linkage), or Ub-KO (i.e., all seven lysine residues are mutated to arginine). Since the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction prevents Polβ ubiquitylation and degradation, we knocked out the XRCC1 gene in these cell lines to enhance detection of ubiquitylated Polβ. In all three XRCC1-KO cell lines, Polβ was primarily ubiquitylated with Ub(K27), Ub(K29), and unmodified Ub(WT), with smaller amounts of Ub[6] and Ub(K11) (Fig. 3A). Notably, a significant amount of Ub(K48)-linked ubiquitin was observed in HCT116 cells, but very little in LN428 or U2OS cells (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). Ub-K33 is theoretically capable of supporting both monoubiquitylation or a single cycle of polyubiquitylation, similar to the Ub-KO, and is often observed in cells associated with the innate immune response, interferon signaling, and protein trafficking, usually via non-proteolytic roles [41–44]. Therefore, it is not surprising that ubiquitylation of Polβ with Ub-K33 chains is minimal, comparable to that observed with the Ub-KO (Fig. 3A).

To further investigate Polβ ubiquitylation, LN428 cells were transduced with a Flag-tagged Polβ expression vector [Flag-Polβ(T304I)], a mutant that does not form the Polβ/XRCC1 complex and is robustly ubiquitylated [22]. Overexpressed Polβ(T304I) was also primarily ubiquitylated with Ub(K27), Ub(K29), Ub(WT), Ub[6], or UB(K11), with limited modification by ubiquitin with the K48 linkage [Ub(K48)]. As expected from our earlier reports [9, 22, 23], mutation of the Polβ ubiquitylation sites (K206A/K244A) prevented this observed ubiquitylation (Supplementary Fig. S3A), confirming the importance of Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) as primary linkages used in the ubiquitylation of endogenous Polβ and overexpressed Polβ in LN428 cells. We also note that Polβ is less efficiently ubiquitylated with Ub(K6) and Ub(K11) linkages (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A).

Next, we investigated the relative contribution of specific Ub linkages by expressing single-lysine mutant ubiquitin transgenes [lysine (K) to arginine (R)]. When compared to unmodified ubiquitin [Ub(WT)], expression of Ub(K27R), Ub(K29R), and Ub(K48R) resulted in reduced Polβ ubiquitylation in both the LN428 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3B), suggesting that Ub(K27), Ub(K29), and Ub(K48) are the predominant linkages adopted to ubiquitylate Polβ. Strikingly, the level of Polβ ubiquitylation increased when both lysine 27 and lysine 29 were mutated to arginine [Ub(K27R/K29R)] (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3B), suggesting that Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) linkages might inhibit the incorporation of other Ub chains. Conversely, mutations of either Ub(K6R) or Ub(K48R) decreased Polβ ubiquitylation when expressed alongside Ub(K27R/K29R) (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3C), suggesting engagement between these linkages.

We have previously shown that TRIP12 is the major E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for Polβ ubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation in human cells [23]. To explore the role of the Ub(K11) linkage—known to target the tumor suppressor FBW7 for degradation via TRIP12 [45]—we compared Polβ ubiquitylation using compound mutant transgenes: Ub(K11R/K27R/K29R), Ub(K6R/K27R/K29R), Ub(K27R/K29R/K48R), or Ub(K27R/K29R). The addition of the Ub(K11R) mutation increased Polβ ubiquitylation compared to cells expressing the Ub(K27R/K29R) mutations alone (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3C). Conversely, addition of the Ub(K6R) or Ub(K48R) mutation decreased Polβ ubiquitylation in both the HCT116 and LN428 cells [when expressing Ub(K27R/K29R)] but most prominently in the LN428 cells (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3C). These findings suggest ubiquitin chains with the K11 linkage impact Polβ ubiquitylation differently than those with the K6 or K48 linkage if Polβ cannot be ubiquitylated via Ub(K27) and/or Ub(K29). Without Ub(K11), Ub(K27), and Ub(K29) chains, Polβ appears to be robustly ubiquitylated with Ub(K6)/Ub(K48) chains in these cell lines. However, we should point out that distinguishing true enzymatic competition between these linkages from either pathway or ubiquitin-linkage bias would require additional experimentation in future studies.

To confirm these findings, we employed linkage-specific antibodies. Antibodies recognizing Ub(K27) and Ub(K48) linkages detected strong Polβ ubiquitylation in cells expressing HA-Ub(K27) and HA-Ub(K48), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3D). These antibodies also detected weak Polβ ubiquitylation in cells expressing HA-Ub but not in cells expressing other linkages (Supplementary Fig. S3D), confirming their specificity and indicating that Polβ is ubiquitylated with a mixed pool of Ub chains.

Finally, to investigate the involvement of previously identified ubiquitylation sites (K206 and K244) on Polβ, we utilized our LN428/Polβ(TM) cells [LN428/Flag-Polβ(L301R/V303R/V306R)] expressing a Flag-tagged Polβ mutant that disrupts the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction, resulting in robust ubiquitylation of Polβ [9, 23]. Mutation of either K206 or K244 to alanine (K206A or K244A) resulted in decreased Polβ(L301R/V303R/V306R) ubiquitylation, while mutation of both residues (K206A/K244A) almost completely blocked Polβ ubiquitylation with the Ub(K27) chain (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S3E). These data therefore confirm our previous reports [9, 23] and indicate that Polβ residues K206 and K244 are the primary ubiquitylation sites of Polβ with Ub(K27) chains.

TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ with K27 and K29 linkages

To investigate the specific Ub chain types used by TRIP12 during Polβ ubiquitylation, we next performed an in vitro ubiquitylation assay. Purified Polβ, HA-Ub, and various HA-Ub mutants—including HA-Ub(K27), HA-Ub(K29), HA-Ub(K48), HA-Ub(K63), and HA-Ub(KO) as a negative control—were prepared (Supplementary Fig. S4A). In vitro, TRIP12, isolated from cells by IP, ubiquitylated Polβ primarily with unmodified Ub [Ub(WT)] but with reduced efficiency using Ub(K27) or Ub(K29). Importantly, TRIP12 did not ubiquitylate Polβ in vitro when using HA-Ub(K48) or HA-Ub(K63) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4C).

To further confirm these findings, Myc-TRIP12 and Myc-HECT (the HECT domain of TRIP12) were expressed in cells and captured using Myc-TRAP beads (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Addition of purified Polβ and either Myc-TRIP12 or Myc-HECT resulted in increased Polβ ubiquitylation with both purified HA-Ub and HA-Ub(K27), compared to control samples and samples lacking purified Polβ (Fig. 4C and D; Supplementary Fig. S4D and E). This suggests that TRIP12, and specifically its HECT domain, ubiquitylates Polβ using a mixed pool of Ub chains, including Ub(K27) chains. Furthermore, the results indicate that both Myc-TRIP12 and Myc-HECT ubiquitylate Polβ with a mixed pool of Ub chains, preferentially over Ub(K27) chains (Figs. 4C and D; Supplementary Figs S4D and E), corroborating the findings from the TRIP12-IP assay (Fig. 3A).

To validate this observation further, we knocked down TRIP12 expression using shRNA in LN428/Polβ-mClover and HCT116/Polβ-mClover cells (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Loss of TRIP12 decreased the intensity of Polβ ubiquitylation in cells expressing HA-Ub, HA-Ub(K27), and HA-Ub(K29), but not HA-Ub(K48) (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S4G). This suggests that TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ using Ub(K27), Ub(K29), and mixed-chain linkages, with limited Ub(K48) linkages in these cells, reinforcing the conclusions from the in vitro ubiquitylation assay.

Ubiquitylation of Polβ is affected by cellular exposure to genotoxins

Polβ plays an essential role in BER, a critical pathway for maintaining genome stability in response to a wide range of DNA lesions [46–49]. Notably, we have observed that Polβ mutants that cannot be ubiquitylated exhibit distinct phenotypes when exposed to different classes of DNA damage [9, 23]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the ubiquitylation of Polβ may be regulated by the type of DNA damage present. To investigate this, we assessed the level of Polβ ubiquitylation in XRCC1-KO cells expressing Polβ-mClover and various HA-Ub mutants [(HA-Ub(K27), HA-Ub(K29), or HA-Ub(K27R/K29R)]. Treatment with MMS decreased Polβ ubiquitylation, whereas IR slightly increased it, regardless of the Ub linkage (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S5A). This suggests that the status of Polβ ubiquitylation is influenced by the type of DNA damage present.

Next, we explored the potential role of different Ub linkages in Polβ ubiquitylation in cells expressing XRCC1. Without MG132 treatment (a proteasome inhibitor), we detected strong Polβ ubiquitylation with the Ub(K27) linkage, weaker ubiquitylation with the Ub(K29) linkage, and considerably weaker ubiquitylation with unmodified Ub [Ub(WT)] and Ub(K48) in both cell lines (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S5B). Upon MG132 treatment, the levels of Polβ ubiquitylation with Ub(WT) and Ub(K29) increased in both cell lines. Interestingly, we observed a greater level of Polβ ubiquitylation with Ub(WT) than with Ub(K27) under these conditions. Furthermore, MG132 treatment increased the levels of Polβ ubiquitylation with Ub(K48) in HCT116 cells, but not in LN428 cells (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S5B). This finding is consistent with earlier data demonstrating that Polβ is minimally ubiquitylated with Ub(K48) in LN428 or U2OS cells (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). These results suggest that Polβ ubiquitylation with the mixed chains [Ub(WT)] or with Ub(K48) likely targets Polβ for degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, whereas Polβ ubiquitylation with the more abundant Ub(K27) chain may play a non-proteolytic role.

To confirm that Ub(K27)-mediated Polβ ubiquitylation is more stable than Ub(K29)-mediated ubiquitylation, we performed GFP-TRAP assays in cells expressing HA-Ub(K27) or HA-Ub(K29). We detected higher levels of Polβ ubiquitylation with Ub(K27) compared to Ub(K29) in both cell lines, and these levels increased with MG132 treatment (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S5C), indicating that Ub(K27)-mediated Polβ ubiquitylation is more stable than Ub(K29)-mediated ubiquitylation, implicating an important role for the K27 linkage in Polβ ubiquitylation. Finally, we examined the impact of MMS exposure on the level of Polβ ubiquitylation. We observed that treatment with MMS reduced Polβ ubiquitylation, followed by a return to normal levels within 5 h across all three cell lines examined (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S5D).

Polβ subcellular localization and complexation with XRCC1 in chromatin are regulated by ubiquitylation and DNA damage

Ubiquitin chain topology plays critical roles in controlling protein localization, complex formation, and stability [25, 27, 28, 50]. Given that Ub(K27) ubiquitylation of Polβ likely serves a non-proteolytic role (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S5), and Polβ forms a complex with XRCC1 in response to DNA damage [38], we investigated whether the formation of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex is regulated by ubiquitylation and/or genotoxic stress. Treatment with MMS decreased the levels of Polβ mixed-chain (Ub) and Ub(K27) ubiquitylation, promoting dissociation of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex (Supplementary Fig. S6A), suggesting that Polβ ubiquitylation may regulate the assembly of this complex within cells. However, when examining whole-cell lysates, Polβ ubiquitylation did not appear to directly impact the assembly or disassembly of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex, whether in response to MMS or IR (Supplementary Figs S6A and B), nor did DNA damage induced by MMS or IR alter the total cellular levels of Polβ or XRCC1 when evaluated in whole-cell lysates (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B, and E).

Interestingly, we observed that the levels of both Polβ and XRCC1 decreased in the cytosol, while increasing in the nuclear soluble fraction following MMS and IR treatment (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S6C), suggesting that DNA damage promotes the translocation of Polβ and XRCC1 from the cytosol to the nucleus. Furthermore, MMS and IR treatment promoted retention or localization of XRCC1 in the chromatin fraction, while IR treatment (but not MMS treatment) enhanced localization of Polβ in the chromatin fraction in both HCT116 and LN428 cells (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Figs S6C and D). However, 6 h post-MMS and IR treatment, the levels of ubiquitylated Polβ decreased in the nuclear soluble fraction and in chromatin, increasing in the cytosol (Fig. 6A and B; Supplementary Fig. S6C and D).

Since ubiquitylated Polβ efficiently localizes and is retained in the chromatin fraction [23], we hypothesized that ubiquitylated Polβ may interact with XRCC1 within chromatin, and that translocation of ubiquitylated Polβ from the nucleus into the cytosol could promote dissociation of the Polβ/XRCC1 complex. To test this hypothesis, we performed Flag-M2 IP using subcellular fractions from cells expressing Flag-Polβ and Flag-Polβ(K206A/K244A), where the K206A/K244A mutation blocks Polβ ubiquitylation [9, 23] (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S3A and E). Compared to Polβ(WT), a significantly smaller fraction of Polβ(K206A/K244A) was found in the chromatin fraction (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S6F), supporting the idea that ubiquitylation aids Polβ retention in chromatin, as previously suggested [23]. Consistent with this, we observed that the level of XRCC1 bound to Polβ(K206A/K244A) by IP was significantly higher than in the cytosol, reduced in the chromatin fraction, and comparable when evaluating proteins in the nuclear soluble fraction (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S6F). This suggests that ubiquitylation of Polβ may promote the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction within chromatin. Oxidation of XRCC1 has been shown to enhance its binding affinity with Polβ and promote the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction [21, 22, 51]. To investigate this, we performed a ubiquitylation assay in subcellular fractions in cells treated with H2O2. Following H2O2 treatment, ubiquitylated Polβ translocated from the chromatin fraction to the cytosol, while the level of XRCC1 bound with Polβ decreased in the chromatin fraction and increased in the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. S6G). Collectively, these findings support the idea that oxidative damage promotes the association of Polβ/XRCC1 in the cytosol.

Discussion

Polβ and XRCC1 are key BER proteins that function as a heterodimer. Specifically, the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction aids Polβ recruitment to sites of DNA damage [9, 23, 38, 52] and helps retain and localize Polβ in the chromatin to maintain genomic integrity [22]. Previously, we demonstrated that binding of Polβ to XRCC1 regulates Polβ ubiquitylation and degradation, suggesting that DNA repair protein complex formation (e.g. Polβ/XRCC1) can function as a regulatory mechanism for DNA repair pathway choice during cell cycle progression and genome surveillance [9, 23, 39]. We have since discovered that TRIP12 is the primary E3 ligase that targets Polβ for ubiquitylation [23], highlighting that TRIP12 controls cellular levels and chromatin loading of Polβ. Further, TRIP12 influences Polβ involvement after radiation-induced DNA damage, a process regulated by TRIP12-mediated ubiquitylation of Polβ [23]. Here, we report a key biological role of Polβ ubiquitylation concerning atypical ubiquitin linkages.

In XRCC1-KO cells, Polβ is ubiquitylated and degraded by a proteasome-mediated pathway (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S2), supporting our earlier finding that the Polβ/XRCC1 heterodimer stabilizes Polβ in both human and mouse cells [9, 39]. Interestingly, we find here that the presence of XRCC1 does not alter Polβ ubiquitylation induced by DNA damage, nor does it affect the recovery of Polβ ubiquitylation post-DNA damage (Figs 5 and 6; Supplementary Figs S5 and S6). This suggests that only free Polβ (not Polβ bound to XRCC1) is ubiquitylated. This is consistent with our earlier finding that TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ and physically interacts primarily with free Polβ, not Polβ bound to XRCC1 [23]. Here, we find that Polβ is ubiquitylated predominantly with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains in most cells examined, although Ub(K48) ubiquitylation is also found in HCT116 cells (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Without MG132 treatment, Ub(K48) ubiquitylation of Polβ is undetectable in HCT116 cells (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S5B), consistent with a role for Ub(K48) as a signal for proteasome-mediated degradation [24, 25]. This is further supported by our finding showing that Polβ degradation is faster in HCT116 cells than in LN428 cells [22]. Here, we also found that TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ with both Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains, but little or no Ub(K48), in vivo and in vitro. HCT116 cells also appear to exhibit higher overall levels of Polβ ubiquitylation, including elevated K6 and K11 linkages, compared to LN428 and U2OS cells. This may suggest potential cell-line (HCT116) specific factors that might contribute to these differences, suggesting the presence of an as yet identified E3 ligase that can ubiquitylate Polβ with Ub(K48) or other linkages, consistent with our earlier report [23], or may suggest unique deubiquitylases in HCT116 cells, yet to be determined. For example, it has been found that TRIP12 assembly of K48 ubiquitin chains, Ub(K48), but not K29 chains, Ub(K29), can be reversed by the deubiquitylase OTUD5 [53].

Ubiquitin linkages with the K27-linkage are the least solvent accessible and the most ordered, making them the most resistant to deubiquitylases [54]. Currently, no specific deubiquitylase has been discovered that targets K27-linked polyubiquitin [55, 56]. Ubiquitylation with Ub(K27) has been reported to signal non-proteolytic roles, including DNA repair, DNA damage response, and the regulation of innate immunity [25, 32, 33, 57]. Under physiological conditions, we find that ubiquitylated Polβ is primarily composed of Ub(K27), likely due to the instability of ubiquitin with other linkage types [25, 27, 28] (Fig. 5B and C; Supplementary Figs S5B, C and S6A). Specifically, we find that ubiquitylated Polβ is detected in HCT116 cells by antibodies recognizing ubiquitylation with both Ub(K27) and Ub(K48) linkages (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Further, TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains in vivo. However, TRIP12 ubiquitylates Polβ inefficiently with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains in vitro (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S4). Our finding that TRIP12 targets Polβ with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) chains in vivo is consistent with recent findings that TRIP12 can assemble K29-linked ubiquitin, targeting SUV39H1 and BRD4 [53, 58–60], and can assemble K27, K29, and K33-linked ubiquitin, targeting pro-IL-1β [61]. Interestingly, both the K29- and K33-linked ubiquitin chains may be reversed by the TRABID deubiquitylase [43, 62]. Finally, low levels of Polβ Ub(K48) ubiquitylation were detected in LN428 cells, and the level of Polβ ubiquitylation is reduced in cells expressing HA-Ub(K48R) (Figs 3A, B and 5B; Supplementary Figs S3B and S5B), suggesting that the Ub(K48) linkage is an important component of the mixed-chain ubiquitylation of Polβ in LN428 cells. It is known that most degradative ubiquitin linkages consist of mixed chains, with or without the Ub-K27 or Ub-K29 linkage. The recent discoveries of such non-canonical Ub linkages certainly add to the complexity of the ubiquitin code [63]. However, as to how this dynamic of non-degradative versus degradative Polβ ubiquitylation is regulated is unclear at this time.

The level of ubiquitylated Polβ is decreased post-MMS treatment but remains unchanged following IR treatment (Figs 5A and 6A; Supplementary Figs S5A and S6A–D). Since Polβ is predominantly ubiquitylated with the physiologically stable Ub(K27) molecule, it is perhaps unsurprising that the level of Polβ does not change following DNA damage (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S6A, B, and E), suggesting that the decreased level of Polβ ubiquitylation induced by MMS treatment may be primarily due to deubiquitylation. We can therefore speculate that alkylating agent-induced DNA damage, but not oxidative stress-induced DNA damage or single strand breaks, activates as-yet-defined deubiquitylases to target the Ub(K27)-modified Polβ. USP47 was reported to deubiquitylate Polβ and respond to oxidative stress [64], but we have not yet determined if USP47 is activated by MMS treatment. Future efforts may help discover the first specific deubiquitylase cleaving the K27-linkage of ubiquitin.

Polβ is primarily ubiquitylated with Ub(K27) and Ub(K29) linkages but can also be ubiquitylated with Ub(K6), Ub(K11), and Ub(K48) linkages, albeit with lower efficiency (Fig. 3A and B; Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Surprisingly, we find that Polβ ubiquitylation with Ub(K27), Ub(K29), or Ub(K11) linkages competes with Ub(K6) and Ub(K48) in cells (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3C). This phenomenon has not been reported, and its biological role remains unclear, although it is plausible that polyubiquitin, comprised of different Ub linkages, recruits distinct factors to process DNA damage and signal the DNA damage response, a concept consistent with the “ubiquitin code” [25, 32, 33, 50, 65]. This may suggest that ubiquitylation of Polβ is related to the coordination of BER with other DNA repair pathways, such as HR and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) [23].

DNA damage by MMS and IR treatment promotes the translocation of Polβ and XRCC1 to the nucleus, consistent with our finding that oxidative stress-induced DNA damage promotes Polβ translocation [22]. As TRIP12 localizes to the nucleus and targets free nuclear Polβ [23], Polβ ubiquitylation likely disrupts the Polβ/XRCC1 interaction. However, ubiquitylated Polβ enhances retention in chromatin, likely promoting the formation of the Polβ/XRCC1 heterodimer (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S6F). Conversely, translocation of ubiquitylated Polβ from the nucleus into the cytosol suppresses interaction of XRCC1 with the ubiquitylated form of Polβ and may help dissociate the Polβ/XRCC1 complex from chromatin. Further, oxidative DNA damage induced by H2O2 and IR results in the translocation of ubiquitylated Polβ into the cytoplasm, allowing Polβ to form a complex with XRCC1, suggesting that Polβ may need to be deubiquitylated by as-yet-identified deubiquitylases before it can interact with XRCC1 following oxidative DNA damage. This ubiquitin dynamic may also facilitate independent functions of Polβ and XRCC1 [23], as XRCC1 not only plays a role in BER but also functions in NHEJ and nucleotide excision repair [66–68], and free Polβ is suggested to function in alternative end-joining processes [69] and in mitochondrial BER [3, 5]. Collectively, we demonstrate a mechanism regulating the Polβ/XRCC1 complex in different subcellular compartments that may be important in maintaining genome integrity via translocation of ubiquitylated Polβ mediated by DNA damage (Fig. 7).
