Prognostic nutritional index, sarcopenia, and risk of mortality: a national population-based study
Authors: Qian Wu, Wenquan Ding, Dongqing You, YunPeng Ji, Shenghao Wang, Dinghua Jiang, Lixin Huang, Wu Xu, Lisong Li, Jiangnan Xu, Yajie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of nutritional status in predicting prognosis in patients with sarcopenia has not been fully elucidated. This investigation sought to evaluate the link between prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and sarcopenia, as well as its influence on overall and cardiovascular death rates in adults diagnosed with sarcopenia.MethodsThis retrospective observational study utilized data from individuals aged 18 years and older extracted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) during 1999–2004 and 2011–2018. The PNI calculation incorporated initial serum albumin measurements and complete lymphocyte numbers. To investigate the link between PNI and sarcopenia, researchers employed multiple analytical approaches, including multivariate logistic regression, stratified group evaluation, restricted cubic spline, and threshold and saturation effect analysis. The investigation utilized Cox regression modeling and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to examine the link between PNI and both overall and cardiovascular-related mortality among subjects with sarcopenia.ResultsAmong the 24,661 patients examined, sarcopenia was detected in 2760 individuals (11.19%). Throughout a median monitoring duration of 132.01 months, all-cause mortality claimed 959 (34.75%) subjects with sarcopenia, while cardiovascular-related fatalities accounted for 321 (33.47%) cases. Subjects in the uppermost PNI quartile (Q4) exhibited markedly decreased likelihood of sarcopenia (odds ratio [OR] 0.43, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.37–0.49) and lower risks of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 0.64, 95% CI 0.53–0.78; and 0.60, 95% CI 0.43–0.84, respectively) versus those in the lowermost quartile (Q1). These findings were consistent across subgroup analyses, restricted cubic spline, and threshold and saturation effect analysis.ConclusionsThe PNI is an independent predictor of sarcopenia, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality in U.S. adults. It can be a valuable tool for identifying individuals at elevated risk of unfavorable health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01005-z.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Prognostic nutritional index, Independent predictor, Mortality

Introduction

Sarcopenia, characterized by reductions in muscle strength, mass, and function, is linked to elevated risks of mortality, falls, disability, hospitalizations, and diminished independence [1]. Its prevalence ranges between 3.2% and 26.3% [2]. Aging is a primary factor contributing to the decline in skeletal muscle performance, impacting muscle mass, strength, and stamina [3]. However, sarcopenia is not exclusive to older adults; Pang et al. reported a prevalence of 6.9% among adults aged 21 to 59 years, highlighting its broader relevance [4]. Given its association with numerous adverse outcomes and its contribution to rising healthcare costs, sarcopenia has become a significant public health concern. Among its modifiable risk factors, malnutrition plays a pivotal role [5]. Currently, a wide array of tools is available for the screening and diagnosis of sarcopenia; however, it is important to distinguish between these two categories. Screening tools are intended for the initial identification of individuals at risk and are generally simple, inexpensive, and feasible in large-scale or primary care settings, whereas diagnostic tools are used to confirm the condition and often require specialized equipment and trained personnel. These tools can be broadly categorized into seven groups: questionnaires, serum biomarkers, predictive formulas and scoring models, physical performance tests, integrated assessment tools, anthropometric indices, and imaging techniques [6–8]. Among these, the most used questionnaires include SARC-F, SARC-CalF, and MSRA-7; handgrip strength and the Ishii score are the most widely utilized physical and formula-based assessments, respectively; and the sarcopenia index based on serum creatinine and cystatin C, as well as calf circumference, represent frequently used serum biomarkers and anthropometric indices. In terms of imaging, ultrasound has emerged as a feasible option for screening, while dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) are typically used as diagnostic methods due to their higher accuracy but greater cost and technical requirements [9]. Despite their widespread application, these tools face notable limitations in routine clinical practice. Physical performance tests depend on participant cooperation and are easily influenced by age, comorbidities, and cognitive status, making them less feasible among frail or hospitalized populations. Diagnostic imaging techniques such as DXA and BIA, while accurate, are often costly, time-consuming, and reliant on specialized equipment and trained personnel, thereby limiting their scalability in primary care or large-scale screening contexts. Although questionnaires like SARC-F are simple and convenient, they suffer from low sensitivity, particularly in detecting early or mild sarcopenia [10]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop simple, objective, cost-effective, and widely applicable biomarkers in large sample populations to effectively identify people at high risk of sarcopenia and its related adverse consequences.

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), introduced by Onodera in 1984, is a composite metric derived from albumin concentrations in serum and lymphocyte numbers in peripheral blood. Initially, it was developed as a nutritional marker to evaluate surgical risk in individuals with gastrointestinal cancer [11]. The PNI has evolved into a widely used, non-invasive biomarker reflecting nutritional and immune status. It is commonly applied in clinical practice to predict outcomes in cancer [12], cardiovascular diseases [13], hemodialysis [14], and those with diabetic nephropathy [15]. Given the interconnected roles of nutrition and immunity in sarcopenia [16], PNI offers distinct advantages over traditional screening tools: it is objective, inexpensive, easily obtainable from routine blood tests, and scalable for large epidemiological studies. Moreover, beyond detecting sarcopenia, PNI has demonstrated robust associations with all-cause mortality [17]. Since malnutrition and systemic inflammation-key components captured by PNI-are significant predictors of mortality, PNI may serve not only as a screening tool but also as a prognostic indicator to identify individuals at elevated risk of death. Investigating the relationships among PNI, sarcopenia, and mortality thus holds promise for elucidating underlying mechanisms and guiding targeted clinical interventions.

This investigation employed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a comprehensive population-based dataset, to investigate the link between PNI, sarcopenia, and all-cause mortality. A lower PNI was hypothesized to be significantly linked to elevated sarcopenia occurrence and heightened mortality risk. By exploring these associations, this investigation sought to highlight PNI’s capability as a practical screening instrument for detecting individuals with elevated risk and directing specific interventions.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized publicly available data from the NHANES, a continuous, nationally representative survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which is part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized civilian population in the United States. The survey protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board with protocol numbers: Protocol #98 − 12, Protocol #2005-06, Protocol #2011-17, and Protocol #2018-01. All participants provided written informed consent. Detailed survey files and datasets are publicly accessible on the NHANES website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes), and no additional permissions were required to access or analyze the data.

This investigation examined information from 70,285 subjects throughout seven biennial periods: 1999–2004 and 2011–2018. Individuals were removed according to the subsequent requirements: age below 18 years (n = 29,396), gestation (n = 1,174), incomplete appendicular lean mass (ALM)/ body mass index (BMI) measurements (n = 13,458), unavailable PNI records (n = 1,547), and lacking survival information (n = 49). Following these eliminations, the ultimate sample consisted of 24,661 subjects (Fig. 1).

Flowchart illustrating the screening process for the study population

This study was based on a secondary analysis of publicly available NHANES data; therefore, no priori sample size calculation was conducted. However, a post hoc power analysis indicated that the final sample size of 24,661 participants provided over 90% power to detect an odds ratio of 1.5 for the association between PNI and sarcopenia, assuming a sarcopenia prevalence of 10%, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a 1:4 exposure ratio. These calculations were performed using the power.logistic function in the R Hmisc package.

The PNI evaluates nutritional status using clinical markers and is calculated using the formula [18]:

Lymphocyte counts are determined through complete blood cell count analyses, employing the Beckman Coulter method for parameter enumeration and sizing. Serum albumin concentrations, an indicator of nutritional status, are quantified in the NHANES database utilizing the bromocresol violet dye technique. A low PNI reflects poor dietary status and has been correlated with elevated mortality rates in previous research [19].

Assessment of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia, the principal outcome measure in this study, was evaluated based on its association with muscle mass. Total ALM was assessed using Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scans performed with the Hologic QDR 4500 A fan-beam densitometer, following standardized NHANES protocols. Participants were excluded from DEXA assessments if their height exceeded 192.5 cm, weight exceeded 136.4 kg, or if they were pregnant, consistent with NHANES exclusion criteria. The sarcopenia index was calculated as the ratio of total appendicular skeletal muscle mass (measured in kilograms) to body mass index (BMI, in kg/m²), yielding a unitless value often termed BMI-adjusted ALM [20]. Sarcopenia was defined using sex-specific cut-off points of 0.789 for men and 0.512 for women, as recommended by the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health [21]. These thresholds were used to identify individuals with sarcopenia.

Assessment of survival status

Mortality data were procured from the NHANES public use linked mortality file, updated through December 31, 2019, and the National Death Index from the NCHS. These datasets were combined utilizing a probabilistic matching protocol. The International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), served as the basis for classifying death causes. Deaths from cardiovascular conditions encompassed ICD-10 codes relating to heart diseases (I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51) and cerebrovascular diseases (I60-I69) [22].

Assessment of covariates

The following covariates were considered: gender, age, race/ethnicity, educational level, marital status, and family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR). Additional factors included a smoking history (established through the questionnaire, “Smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life?”, with responses classified as affirmative, negative, or indeterminate) and alcohol intake (evaluated via the questionnaire, “Had at least 12 alcohol drinks/1 year”, with responses categorized as affirmative, negative, or indeterminate). Diabetes status was confirmed using the Basis for Determination of Diabetes Questionnaire, which inquired if a healthcare provider had diagnosed participants, carefully excluding gestational diabetes [23]. Hypertension was determined by self-reported medical diagnosis, consumption of blood pressure medications, or detected high blood pressure readings. Furthermore, lipid markers, encompassing total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG), were examined [24].

Nutritional intake was assessed by evaluating energy, carbohydrate, and protein consumption. These data were derived from two non-consecutive 24-hour dietary recalls collected using the Automated Multiple-Pass Method, a validated approach developed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture [25, 26]. The first dietary recall was administered in person at the Mobile Examination Center, and the second was conducted via telephone 3–10 days later. Average daily intakes of total energy (kcal), carbohydrates (g), and protein (g) were calculated by averaging the two days’ data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were executed per the protocols (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html). Survey findings are denoted as mean figures with associated standard errors for continuous measurements and as frequencies with percentages for categorical parameters. Between-group assessments were executed utilizing one-way analysis of variance for continuous measurements and chi-square analyses for categorical parameters. PNI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and by quartiles (Q1-Q4) in this study.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis with multiple variables examined the link between PNI and sarcopenia, presenting outcomes as the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) through three analytical frameworks. The initial framework remained uncontrolled, while the second framework incorporated adjustments for gender, age, and ethnicity. The third framework introduced additional controls for education level, marital status, PIR, alcohol use, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, carbohydrate intake, protein intake, energy intake, TC, and TG. Subgroup analyses were performed utilizing the methodology outlined in Model 3, stratified by gender (man or woman), age groups (< 45 years and ≥ 45 years), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and other), PIR (< 1.3, 1.3–3.5, and ≥ 3.5), educational level (< high school and ≥ high school), marital status (partnered vs. single), and health factors including hypertension (yes or no), diabetes status (yes, no, or borderline), alcohol intake (yes or no), and smoking (yes or no). Interaction tests were employed to evaluate the homogeneity of the links across subgroups. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve analysis was performed in each subgroup to assess nonlinear relationships, threshold and saturation effect analysis was further conducted in all subgroups.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazard analyses were utilized to examine the link between PNI and death rates among those with sarcopenia, presenting outcomes as the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI. The adjustment strategies paralleled those employed in the logistic regression analyses. Kaplan-Meier survival plots were employed to illustrate survival patterns across time, while the log-rank test examined distinctions between survival distributions. Additional analyses using identical statistical approaches were performed to explore possible differences across sociodemographic and health-related categories, encompassing gender, age, ethnicity, educational background, marital condition, poverty-to-income proportion, hypertension, diabetes, smoking habits, and alcohol intake.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and EmpowerStats (version 2.0) [22, 24, 27, 28]. The following R packages were utilized: survey for complex sampling design analysis, rms for spline regression and threshold effect modeling, survival and survminer for Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier plots, and ggplot2 for data visualization. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study

The median age of the 24,661 enrolled participants was (42.87 ± 17.74) years, with men comprising 50.10% of the cohort. Based on ALM/BMI scores, sarcopenia was observed in 2760 (11.19%) individuals. Baseline characteristics, stratified by PNI quartiles, are depicted in Table 1. Individuals within elevated PNI quartiles demonstrated a higher tendency to be younger age, male, unmarried, more educated and indicate increased intake of alcoholic beverages, carbohydrate-rich foods, protein-rich foods and overall caloric consumption. Conversely, hypertension and diabetes were more prevalent in Q1, while the prevalence of sarcopenia declined progressively from Q1 to Q4. Further analysis stratified participants with sarcopenia into PNI quartiles, as depicted in Supplementary Table 1. During a median follow-up of 132.01 months, 959 participants with sarcopenia (34.75%) succumbed to all-cause mortality, while 321 (11.63%) of them died from cardiovascular causes. Among participants with sarcopenia, those in the Q4 group were generally younger, predominantly male, and had higher carbohydrate, protein and energy intake. They exhibited a reduced occurrence of hypertension, diabetes, and mortality versus those in Q1 (all P < 0.05). To further explore mortality differences, we compared death rates between individuals with and without sarcopenia. The all-cause mortality rate was significantly higher in the sarcopenia group (34.75%) compared to the non-sarcopenia group (12.34%, P < 0.001). Similarly, the CVD mortality rate was markedly elevated among participants with sarcopenia (11.63%) compared to those without sarcopenia (3.79%, P < 0.001).

When stratified by PNI quartiles, participants in Q4 consistently exhibited the lowest odds of sarcopenia. In Model 3, the adjusted OR for Q4 was 0.43 (95% CI: 0.37 to 0.49), reflecting a substantial decrease in risk compared to Q1.

Association between PNI and mortality in sarcopenia

Table 3 summarized Cox regression analysis results, highlighting a notable inverse link between PNI and mortality in individuals with sarcopenia. Across all models, higher PNI values were consistently linked to reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each unit evaluated in PNI was connected to an HR of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92 to 0.96) for all-cause mortality and 0.94 (95% CI, 0.90 to 0.97) for cardiovascular mortality, both with P-values < 0.001. Within Model 3, an increment of one unit in PNI yielded a 36% decline in all-cause mortality probability (95% CI, 0.53 to 0.78) and a 40% decline in cardiovascular mortality probability (95% CI, 0.43 to 0.84) when contrasting Q4 with Q1. The trend across PNI quartiles remained significant in all models, confirming the inverse relationship between PNI and mortality risk.

Discussion

Our research suggests that PNI exhibits a negative link to sarcopenia in U.S. adults, especially in those aged 45 years and those with education beyond high school. PNI was significantly and inversely linked to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with sarcopenia. Individuals with elevated PNI values demonstrated substantially lower odds of sarcopenia and mortality. Specifically, subjects within the top PNI quartile displayed a 57% reduction in sarcopenia development compared to those in the bottom quartile. Correspondingly, these individuals showed a 36% decrease in all-cause mortality risk and a 40% decline in cardiovascular mortality risk.

Sarcopenia manifests through the deterioration of skeletal muscle and diminished muscle volume [29]; it is a complex syndrome with intricate pathophysiological mechanisms. It is a recognized risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [30] and is linked to unfavorable outcomes like heart failure and elevated mortality across various conditions [31]. Inflammation plays a pivotal role in sarcopenia’s pathogenesis, with systemic inflammation-measured through inflammatory markers derived from blood cells-associated with sarcopenia and mortality in adults [32]. Nutritional factors also contribute significantly to sarcopenia, with diets rich in antioxidants and high-quality nutrients potentially offering protective benefits [33]. For instance, higher protein intake has consistently correlated with greater muscle mass in older adults [34], while malnutrition is a significant predictor of sarcopenia and its severe forms [35].

The PNI, which combines serum albumin levels and peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, has become a significant marker of nutritional and immune status [11–13]. Initially developed to assess surgical risk in patients with gastrointestinal cancer, PNI’s utility has since broadened. For instance, Shi et al. identified PNI as the most predictive nutritional tool for breast cancer outcomes, with a time-dependent receiver operating characteristic value of 0.58 [17]. A meta-analysis demonstrated PNI’s diagnostic accuracy for acute kidney injury, yielding a sensitivity value of 0.67, a specificity measure of 0.74, a positive likelihood coefficient of 2.49, a negative likelihood coefficient of 0.46, diagnostic odds proportion of 5.54, and an area beneath the curve of 0.76 [36]. In those with stroke, PNI has shown prognostic value during the acute phase of ischemic stroke [37]. A PNI < 38.8 was connected to a considerably higher risk of perioperative ischemic stroke in non-cardiac surgeries, with OR of 1.884 (95% CI, 1.559 to 2.267, P < 0.001) and 1.306 (95% CI, 1.061 to 1.602, P = 0.011) in univariate and multivariate logistic regression, respectively [38]. PNI reflects nutritional status and immune function and is a comprehensive marker of overall health. PNI’s relevance in clinical outcomes, including postoperative recovery, hospital length of stay, complication rates, and mortality risk, has been highlighted in previous research. For instance, Zhang et al. demonstrated that among individuals with diabetic nephropathy, a one standard deviation increase in PNI reduced the likelihood of progression to end-stage renal disease by 29.5% (95% CI, 0.523 to 0.952) [15]. Due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and non-invasive nature, PNI has become an invaluable clinical tool, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Our findings indicate a reduced likelihood of sarcopenia in patients with higher PNI values, supporting the hypothesis that improved nutritional and immune status may help prevent sarcopenia. Although the molecular mechanisms linking PNI to clinical outcomes remain unclear, malnutrition and immunological impairment likely contribute to poor prognoses in patients with sarcopenia [39]. Serum albumin, an indicator of nutritional status, is frequently reduced in malnourished individuals [40]. Protein deficiency, a common feature of malnutrition, impairs muscle protein synthesis and promotes atrophy, exacerbating sarcopenia [41]. Low PNI levels reflect poor nutrition [42], which may favor muscle degradation over synthesis, leading to muscle mass. Similarly, peripheral blood lymphocyte count, a marker of immune function, is frequently diminished in immune-suppressed individuals [43]. Immune dysregulation, particularly chronic low-grade inflammation, is critical in sarcopenia’s pathogenesis [44]. Elevated inflammatory markers like tumor necrosis factor-α and C-reactive protein are associated with muscle atrophy, promoting protein breakdown, inhibiting synthesis, and promoting degeneration [45]. Low PNI values correlate with compromised immune competence and chronic inflammation [46], potentially exacerbating muscle loss and physical decline. These conditions can lead to systemic dysfunction [47], decreased resistance, accelerated atherosclerosis [48], worsening cardiovascular disease, and malignant tumor progression [49], all of which are significant contributors to mortality. While nutritional and immune factors are central to these outcomes, additional mechanisms underlying the PNI-sarcopenia association warrant.

To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first large-scale, population-based investigations exploring the association between the PNI and sarcopenia, as well as its prognostic implications for mortality risk. Several strengths enhance the credibility of our findings. First, the substantial sample size of 24,661 adults from a nationally representative cohort ensures the robustness and generalizability of the results. Second, the use of standardized protocols, comprehensive covariate adjustment, and multiple analytic strategies-including restricted cubic spline analysis and subgroup testing-strengthens the internal validity. Furthermore, this study provides novel insights into the linear inverse relationship between PNI and both sarcopenia and mortality, indicating that higher PNI levels are continuously associated with more favorable outcomes.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant discussion. Given the observational nature of NHANES, the possibility of residual confounding and reverse causality cannot be excluded. Despite being composed of readily available clinical markers (serum albumin and lymphocyte count), the PNI has not yet been integrated into routine screening workflows, and its interpretation may be confounded by acute illness, fluid status, inflammation, or liver dysfunction, which can independently influence its components. In addition, while the BMI-adjusted appendicular lean mass index is widely adopted as a core diagnostic criterion for sarcopenia-especially since its endorsement by the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health -it is not without limitations. The thresholds of 0.789 for men and 0.512 for women, though derived from large-scale population data, may not fully account for the physiological and ethnic heterogeneity observed in clinical settings. Moreover, while PNI has shown utility in diverse clinical populations, our analysis did not identify a clear inflection point or threshold at which the risk of sarcopenia or mortality sharply changed. Instead, a linear protective association was observed, suggesting that PNI may be better utilized as a continuous variable rather than a categorical classifier in this context. Another limitation lies in the absence of universally accepted cut-off values for PNI in relation to sarcopenia or mortality risk, which limits comparability across studies and clinical application. Future research should focus on identifying population-specific or outcome-specific thresholds, validating them in prospective cohorts, and evaluating the feasibility of incorporating PNI into multidimensional risk prediction models.

Conclusion

This large-scale, population-based study indicated a clear association between PNI levels and a reduced prevalence of sarcopenia, as well as lower mortality risk among individuals with sarcopenia. These findings highlight the critical role of nutritional and immune status in the prevention and prognosis of sarcopenia, underscoring the potential utility of PNI as a simple and cost-effective biomarker for risk assessment and clinical management.
