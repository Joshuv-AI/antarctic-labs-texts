Prognostic Clinical Predictive Models for Dental Caries Using Artificial Intelligence: Methodological Considerations
Authors: Sergio E. Uribe, Alonso Carrasco-Labra, Falk Schwendicke, Ilze Maldupa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is currently used to develop clinical predictive models for dental caries. However, most prognostic models lack key methodological components. Few reach clinical practice, and fewer demonstrate clinical benefit.SummaryThis narrative methodological review examines considerations for developing, validating, and implementing AI-based caries prognostic models. Based on established prediction model frameworks (TRIPOD+AI, PROBAST+AI, and PROGRESS), we contextualize eight critical phases for caries-specific application. Each phase addresses challenges specific to caries, e.g., clustering of teeth within patients, dominance of baseline caries experience as a predictor, and frequent absence of external validation. Addressing these phases reduces bias, improves reproducibility, and supports meaningful evaluation of clinical impact. AI-based prognostic models should serve as decision-support tools that inform clinician and patient choices rather than substitutes for clinical judgment.Key Messages(i) Most AI-based caries prognostic models are never implemented and fewer of those implemented demonstrate clinical benefit. Methodological deficiencies explain this gap. (ii) Caries prognostic research faces field-specific challenges such as retrospective design, clustering of teeth within patients, and dominance of baseline caries experience. Valid caries prognostic models require attention to eight phases, from problem selection through deployment and ongoing monitoring. (iii) Better discrimination (higher AUC) does not indicate clinical usefulness. Calibration and decision curve analysis are essential yet frequently absent from published studies. (iv) Researchers should report regulatory considerations and plan prospective impact assessment from study inception. Models generating risk estimates that influence clinical decisions qualify as software as a medical device, requiring regulatory compliance and demonstrated clinical impact before deployment.

Introduction

Caries diagnosis and management strategies remain largely reactive [1], detecting lesions only after tissue damage has occurred [2]. This approach has failed to reduce the overall disease burden [1, 3]. Untreated caries lesions affect 2.3 billion people [4] and cost more than USD 540 billion annually in direct treatment costs and productivity losses [5]. The global prevalence of early childhood caries has remained unchanged for decades [3]. Even in Europe, caries prevalence is the highest globally despite high dentist availability [6], showing that current dental care models are insufficient [1]. If clinicians can identify high-risk individuals before caries reaches clinical detection, targeted personalized prevention might succeed [1, 7]. Predictive modeling generate a mathematical description of a process to make accurate, data-driven predictions about future outcomes. However, even with many predictive models developed, methodological limitations rather than statistical sophistication explain why few of them have reached clinical implementation [8] and even fewer demonstrate clinical benefits [9, 10]. Nowadays, machine learning (ML) is increasingly used to develop clinical predictive models (CPMs) for caries [11–13]. Caries prognostic research faces several unique challenges. First, the hierarchical structure of dental data creates clustering: teeth and surfaces cluster within patients, violating independence assumptions in most statistical methods. Using surfaces as the unit of analysis artificially inflates sample sizes and biases standard errors. Second, baseline caries experience dominates prediction in most models, meaning models often identify risk after disease onset rather than before, limiting their preventive value. Third, caries occurs in high-prevalence settings where treating all individuals may be more efficient than risk stratification, requiring careful consideration of when prognostic models add clinical value. Fourth, longitudinal caries data require accounting for censoring (loss to follow-up) and competing events (tooth extraction and restoration). Although TRIPOD+AI [14] and PROBAST+AI [15] provide reporting and risk-of-bias guidance, caries research generally requires additional domain-specific methodological considerations. For instance, systematic reviews using these frameworks identify some of the issues in caries predictive research, such as single data splits without external validation, reporting only discrimination without calibration assessment, inadequate sample sizes for the number of predictors, and failure to account for clustering [11–13]. These field-specific failures justify caries-focused methodological guidance that contextualizes general frameworks to caries research realities (Table 1).

Previous reviews [11–13] have identified recurring methodological issues in caries prediction studies using TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI, among others. This narrative review extends that work by adding the PROGRESS framework [17] to contextualize the different types of caries prognostic research, identify where ML may support further development, and address caries-specific methodological considerations. Its aim is to provide field guidance for researchers developing caries prognostic models, clinicians evaluating artificial intelligence (AI) tools for caries prediction and implementation, and editors reviewing manuscripts on caries prediction models.

CPMs: Definition and Evolution

CPMs estimate the probability of an individual having a condition (diagnostic models) or developing a future outcome (prognostic models) [18] to answer questions such as “Will this patient have caries in the next year?” (binary outcome) or “How many new caries lesions will this patient develop in the next 2 years?” (continuous outcome). The prognosis research strategy (PROGRESS) framework divides prognosis research into four stages [17]: (1) describing the natural history and outcomes of a condition, (2) identifying specific predictors that alter the disease course, (3) creating models to calculate the probability of outcomes for individuals, and (4) using these predictions to match patients with appropriate treatments (Table 2). Most caries prognostic research is type 2: identifying risk factor associations, ranking predictor importance, or reporting odds/relative risk, without generating individual prediction tools [7]. Type 3 prognostic model research remains rare in cariology [12] and the available evidence reveals several methodological flaws [11, 13]. This review focuses exclusively on type 3: the methodological requirements for developing and validating CPMs that reliably predict individual caries risk before disease onset.

In caries research, prognostic CPMs differ in their presentation of risk estimates. At the simplest level, some tools use additive or rule-based approaches to stratify individuals into ordinal risk categories (low/moderate/high) without estimating the absolute future risk. The ADA Caries Risk Assessment exemplifies this approach by classifying caries risk without a defined prediction horizon [20]. More structured approaches apply weighted scoring systems to produce refined risk strata linked to management strategies. CAMBRA represents this intermediate complexity by formalizing the combination of disease indicators, risk factors, and protective factors without providing an individual-level probability of future caries development [25]. Although risk scores may improve clinical usability, simplification often comes at the cost of predictive accuracy and calibration [26]. At the highest complexity level, probability estimation tools use statistical models to generate absolute risk estimates over defined time horizons. In the 1980s, de Dombal et al. [27] demonstrated that computer-aided multivariable models could generate calibrated, prospectively validated individual prognostic estimates to support clinical decision-making. Later, the Cariogram [28] integrated multiple predictors to estimate the 1-year probability of avoiding new caries [28]. Overall, these caries risk tools show modest predictive performance [20, 29]. Since the prediction is largely driven by baseline caries experience [30, 31], they often identify risk after disease onset.

AI for Caries Prognostic Models

Recently, AI and ML have been used to develop prognostic CPMs for caries research [12, 32]. A new CPM for medical conditions using AI is now developed approximately every 1.5 h worldwide [33], but few models reach clinical implementation [8], and even fewer demonstrate clinical benefits [9]. The increasing use of AI/ML for caries prognosis is driven by three factors. First, larger datasets, including electronic health records, insurance claims, and longitudinal cohorts, now provide access to hundreds of thousands of patient records [34]. Second, richer data sources beyond traditional clinical risk factors, such as salivary biomarkers, oral microbiome profiles, genomic variants, dietary tracking, and imaging, have become available [35]. Third, novel statistical methods and ML algorithms can handle high-dimensional data without requiring researchers to prespecify predictors or interactions [36].

However, systematic reviews comparing ML algorithms to traditional logistic regression for clinical prediction found no performance benefit [37]. Traditional regression methods can model nonlinearities effectively using splines without requiring massive datasets [38]. Furthermore, flexible ML algorithms frequently suffer from poor calibration [39, 40], and complex ML algorithms demand enormous amounts of data unless the signal-to-noise ratio is high [41]. Available studies on AI models for predicting dental caries demonstrated significant risks of bias and methodological deficiencies. Most models were developed outside real clinical settings, and many exhibited unclear or high risks of bias [12]. These methodological deficiencies limit the clinical applicability and effectiveness of the ML approaches and suggest that specific field guidance covering both reporting and the development of caries prediction models could be helpful. This narrative review focuses exclusively on AI methods based on supervised ML for caries prognosis using structured clinical data.

Problem Selection

van Smeden [18] proposed general preliminary questions before developing a prognostic model. In this review, we apply and adapt them to caries prediction. Is a model needed for the intended clinical decision? Is the target population clearly defined? Are sufficiently high-quality data and enough outcome events available? Does an existing model already address the same question in a similar setting? Are the candidate predictors routinely available in practice? Here, outcome events are defined at the patient level because caries risk factors, such as sugar intake and fluoride exposure, primarily operate at the individual level. If not, model development should generally not proceed. Before developing a new model, search for, critically appraise, and validate existing models for the same outcome and population. For caries, systematic reviews have identified multiple existing AI models with a high risk of bias and poor reporting [11–13]. Few have been externally validated and none implemented. It is recommended to develop a new caries predictive model when existing models use impractical predictors, predict different outcomes, lack sufficient reporting, or show poor performance that updating cannot fix (Table 3). Answering these preliminary questions requires patient and end-user involvement from the start. Clinicians are encouraged to identify workflow barriers and data availability constraints. Patients clarify which outcomes matter the most and what risk information influences decisions. For instance, parents may prioritize preventing pain over preventing noncavitated lesions. Patient and clinician collaboration ensures that the model addresses real clinical needs and produces precise clinical questions. Is it helpful to compare vague formulations like “Predict caries risk in children” with specific ones: “Among caries-free children aged 2–3 years attending primary care clinics, what is the probability of developing at least one cavitated lesion in permanent first molars by age 7 years?” defining the population, outcome, time horizon, and setting explicitly.

Beyond precisely defining questions, three implementation criteria determine whether prediction offers clinical value. First, does prediction change clinical decisions? Prediction windows must precede the current detection and allow intervention time. Predicting permanent molar caries at age 2–3 for outcomes at age 7 creates a 4-year prevention window. Second, are predictors routinely available? Models requiring sophisticated analysis, such as microbiome analysis, currently fail in routine implementation and models using salivary flow and fluoride exposure may succeed. Third, does prediction enable differential treatment? Without differential treatment strategies, prediction rarely offers clinical benefits.

Data Quality and Preparation

Data quality determines model validity more than algorithm choice, resting on three pillars: adequate sample size, complete predictor measurement, and standardized outcome definition. Traditional rules suggesting 10–20 events per predictor may often not apply [42], and stable predictions commonly require hundreds or thousands of outcome events. The required total sample size depends on the model complexity and caries baseline prevalence [42, 43]. Teeth and surfaces cluster within patients, creating correlated observations. In association studies, ignoring this clustering biases standard errors; in prognostic models, it influences sample-size calculations, model development, and performance estimation. Because incident caries events generally occur at the patient level, the patient is the natural unit of analysis unless strong justification exists for tooth-level prediction, in which case mixed-effects models or generalized estimating equations can account for within-patient correlation. Sample size calculations are based on patients (independent units), not teeth or surfaces. A study with 100 patients and 2,800 teeth still has only 100 independent observations. Treating teeth as independent units inflates effective sample size and biases standard errors [44]. If 30% of children develop cavitated lesions over 4 years, a cohort of 1,000 children yields 300 events (37.5 events per predictor for eight predictors), meeting minimum criteria. Formal sample size calculation before data collection is advisable [42, 43]. We provide worked examples with R code in https://github.com/sergiouribe/sample-size-prediction-models.

But even with sufficient volume, data completeness poses major challenges. Electronic health records provide large samples but suffer severe completeness problems. Analysis of 543,363 dental visits from the BigMouth dataset revealed pain ratings missing in 98–99% of records, alcohol use in more than half, dietary sugar in 40–60%, and drug use in >60% [45]. Missing data require handling based on the mechanism: missing completely at random, missing at random, or missing not at random [46]. Complete case analysis introduces bias unless data are missing completely at random, which is rare, and reduces effective sample size [26]. Multiple imputation is preferred, preserving sample size and reducing bias when data are missing at random [47]. However, imputation assumes that missingness can be predicted from observed data. Children missing dietary questionnaires may differ systematically from completers (missing not at random), making imputation unreliable [26]. Here, it is recommended to report missingness for each predictor and assess whether it relates to observed characteristics. Behavioral predictors with >50% missingness should generally be excluded or prospectively collected. For deployment, specify whether missing predictors will be excluded or handled using an imputation strategy aligned with model development. Standardizing measurements supports replication and valid prediction. Predictors should be measured consistently across participants using validated methods, as inconsistent measurements introduce noise that algorithms cannot overcome. Each predictor should be defined in detail: measurement tools, units, timing, and handling of abnormal values. For example, dietary sugar intake is best assessed with validated questionnaires rather than informal recall, and fluoride exposure benefits from clear documentation of water fluoridation, varnish use, toothpaste concentration, and brushing frequency. Similar precision applies to outcomes. Outcome definitions should be explicit, clinically meaningful, and consistent. Composite outcomes combining events of different clinical relevance can obscure prediction targets. Defining a new lesion, for instance, benefits from specifying diagnostic criteria such as an ICDAS code threshold. Transparent reporting of timing, detection methods, and examiner training strengthens outcome measurement. When using routine clinical data, describing existing documentation practices and considering heterogeneity in outcome assessment as a source of uncertainty is advisable. Reliance on routine care visits alone can bias outcome detection, as high-risk individuals may be examined more frequently.

Study Design

Cohort designs provide valid prognostic inferences [48]. Cross-sectional designs cannot establish temporal sequence between predictors and outcomes. Case-control designs introduce bias through retrospective predictor measurements. Prospective cohorts collect predictor data before outcomes occur and follow participants forward until the outcomes develop. Retrospective cohorts were used to extract predictor and outcome data from the existing records. Both can produce valid models if data quality requirements are met; however, retrospective designs face greater risks of incomplete predictor measurements and outcome misclassification. Cohort studies usually involve some loss to follow-up. Loss to follow-up exceeding 20% threatens validity. Regardless of the timing, preregistration of the study protocol before data collection is critical. Prospective registration documents predictor selection, outcome definitions, analysis plans, and sample size calculations before examining the data. This prevents data-driven decisions that inflate overfitting risk and reduce publication bias. Preregistration also increases transparency and allows readers to identify deviations from the planned analyses [49].

Also, enrollment criteria determine model validity. Inception cohorts enroll participants at a uniform point in their disease trajectory: the moment prediction would occur clinically [17] for example, children must be caries-free at enrollment (age 2–3 years). Including children with varying baseline caries experiences violates the inception cohort principles and confounds incident disease prediction with disease progression.

Finally, competing events require explicit consideration. Competing risks are events that prevent an outcome from occurring. For caries prediction, tooth extraction before the end of the prediction window represents a competing risk. Participants experiencing competing risks should preferably not be excluded without appropriate statistical handling [26]. If permanent first molars are extracted before age 7 years due to severe caries, excluding these children underestimates the true caries risk. Competing risk models or composite outcomes (cavitated lesion or teeth extracted due to caries) may be needed.

Model Development

Model development requires prespecified predictor selection, appropriate algorithm choice, and explicit overfitting control. Predictor selection in most cases is guided by prior prognostic evidence, clinical knowledge, and biological plausibility before examining the data [26]. Data-driven selection based on univariate p values increases overfitting and produces unstable models. Candidate predictors ideally have strong prior evidence and be routinely available in practice; each additional parameter increases sample size requirements. Continuous predictors should remain continuous unless clinically justified thresholds exist, as categorization wastes information and degrades performance [48, 50]. Nonlinear effects are recommended to be modeled using restricted cubic splines [51].

A distinction exists between association studies (PROGRESS type 2) and prognostic models (PROGRESS type 3). Association studies identify risk factors and estimate effects, requiring biological plausibility and causal interpretability. Prognostic models aim to predict individual outcomes accurately. Shmueli (2010) distinguished explanatory modeling (understanding mechanisms) from predictive modeling (forecasting outcomes), arguing each requires different variable-selection strategies. In predictive frameworks with large datasets, data-driven selection can identify patterns that improve accuracy even without clear biological mechanisms. However, for clinical prediction models intended for implementation, it is recommended to start with biologically plausible predictors. First, small dental datasets (typical N = 200–1,000) make overfitting a major risk when testing many variables. Second, biological plausibility improves transportability across populations. Third, clinicians trust models with mechanistically grounded predictors. Fourth, pre-specification reduces researcher degrees of freedom (Steyerberg et al. [38]). Data-driven selection can be appropriate when sample sizes exceed 5,000, prediction accuracy outweighs interpretability, the goal is hypothesis generation, or the model will undergo rigorous external validation before deployment.

Algorithm choice depends on data structure and sample size. Logistic regression suits binary outcomes with modest samples and provides interpretable coefficients. Random forests and gradient boosting handle nonlinear interactions but require larger samples and reduce interpretability. Deep learning demands tens of thousands of outcome events, rarely available in dental research [45]. Online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553309) summarizes algorithm characteristics. If logistic regression achieves adequate discrimination and calibration, more complex algorithms offer no advantage [50].

Outcome events should be defined at the patient level, as most caries risk factors act at the individual level. Sample size calculations must be based on patients experiencing the outcome [42, 43]. If prediction targets are tooth- or surface-level, clustering must be accounted for using the design effect, which depends on the number of units per patient and the intraclass correlation (typically 0.1–0.4 for dental caries).

When outcomes are imbalanced, for instance when few children develop new dentine caries over follow-up, then model stability and calibration suffer [39, 40]. Synthetic oversampling techniques such as SMOTE can distort outcome prevalence and degrade calibration, so they are best avoided [52]. Penalized regression methods (ridge or elastic net) are preferable under imbalance [38], combined with appropriate metrics including calibration plots and decision curve analysis.

Overfitting prevention requires penalization and adequate sample size. Hyperparameters must be optimized using resampling within the development dataset only, never using validation data (de Hond et al. [53]). The traditional rule of 10 events per parameter is context-dependent and often insufficient [54, 55]. We provide worked examples at https://github.com/sergiouribe/sample-size-prediction-models.

Model Validation

Validation assesses whether models perform accurately beyond the development dataset. There are two types of validation: internal validation using the development dataset and external validation using new patients from different settings. We acknowledge that “validation” is problematic terminology because models require continuous performance assessment rather than definitive validation [56]. We use “validation” for consistency with existing literature, though “testing” is preferable.

Internal validation detects overfitting by evaluating the performance on data not used for development. Single random data splitting should be avoided [50]. Splitting data once into development and testing sets wastes information and produces unstable estimates; any performance difference may reflect a random split rather than true overfitting [50]. Using the full dataset for training with bootstrap resampling (e.g., 500 iterations) for performance evaluation is preferable because it provides stable discrimination and calibration estimates while maximizing development data [54]. Internal validation/testing alone is insufficient and cannot assess model transportability to different populations.

External validation tests model performance in new patients not used for development. Table 4 presents the validation types assessing different aspects of transportability. External validation often reveals poor performance due to different outcome prevalence, predictor distributions, measurement methods, or model overfitting [56]. For instance, a model developed in dental school clinics, where 45% of children develop caries, may perform poorly in private practice, where only 15% develop caries. The sample size for external validation differs from development. The aim of the development is to build a stable model that does not overfit. For external validation, the aim is to precisely measure the model performance. Validation sample size must ensure precise estimation of performance measures, including calibration slope, C-statistic, and observed/expected ratio [57]. The required sample size depends on the target precision for confidence intervals (CIs; e.g., ≤0.1 for C-statistic, ≤0.3 for calibration slope) and the expected model performance in the validation population. Performance may vary across patient subgroups defined by sex, age, socioeconomic status, or baseline risk. Assess discrimination and calibration separately in relevant subgroups and transparently report performance heterogeneity [26]. Poor external validation does not necessarily indicate model failure. When external validation reveals miscalibration, updating the model for the new setting should be considered rather than discarding it [46]. Begin with recalibration of the intercept, then re-estimate the coefficients if needed [26]. Updating often improves performance more efficiently than developing new models. A model is never “validated” permanently; continuous assessment across settings and time establishes trustworthiness. Also, federated learning offers a potential approach for multicenter model development and validation while preserving data privacy [58]. Models are trained across institutions without sharing patient-level data: the algorithm moves to the data rather than data to a central repository. This approach could facilitate external validation across diverse caries populations while maintaining compliance with data-protection regulations. However, federated learning for caries prognostic models remains largely unexplored and requires infrastructure investment before adoption.

Performance Metrics

Predictive model performance reports ideally must include (1) discrimination, (2) calibration, and (3) clinical utility. (1) Discrimination assesses whether individuals who develop caries are at a higher risk than those who do not develop caries; (2) calibration assesses whether the predicted risks correspond to the observed outcome frequencies; and (3) clinical utility evaluates whether using the model leads to better clinical decisions than using alternative strategies. All three are required before implementation can be decided, and discrimination alone is insufficient [59]. Table 5 explains these concepts for clinical readers (adapted from [59, 60]).

Performance metrics in prognostic models

(1) Discrimination is commonly quantified using the C-statistic (AUC), ranging from 0.5 (no discrimination) to 1.0 (perfect), with 0.70–0.80 considered acceptable [26]. A C-statistic of 0.75 means the model assigns higher risk to the affected child 75% of the time when comparing one who developed caries with one who did not. However, CIs frequently span 0.10–0.15 units, so small differences between models are clinically meaningless [61]. For example, a model with C-statistic 0.75 (95% CI: 0.70–0.80) in development and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.65–0.79) in validation shows overlapping intervals; the apparent decline reflects random variation, not true performance loss. CIs must always be reported. More importantly, good discrimination does not mean predicted risks are accurate. A model may correctly rank children by risk while systematically over- or underestimating absolute probabilities (poor calibration), leading to inappropriate preventive decisions. This is where (2) calibration becomes essential. Calibration plots compare predicted risks against observed outcome frequencies; perfect calibration follows a 45° line. The calibration slope quantifies this relationship: 1.0 indicates perfect calibration, <1.0 indicates overfitting, and >1.0 indicates underfitting. Calibration-in-the-large assesses whether the average predicted risk matches the observed prevalence. Calibration is the “Achilles heel” of predictive models [39, 40]. A model with C-statistic 0.80 but calibration slope 0.60 predicts 60% caries risk for children whose observed risk is 40%, prompting unnecessary treatment and potentially harming low-risk children through overtreatment. Beyond discrimination and calibration, models must demonstrate (3) clinical utility. Decision curve analysis estimates net benefit by weighing true positives against false positives according to clinical consequences [62]. For instance, if treatment intensity increases when predicted risk exceeds 40%, the analysis compares treating all children intensively, treating none, or using model-based stratification. A useful model shows higher net benefit across relevant thresholds. However, decision curve analysis on development or validation data only estimates potential utility. Demonstrating actual outcome improvement requires prospective impact studies comparing model-guided care with usual care, ideally through pragmatic randomized trials [53].

Reporting

Transparent reporting enables readers to assess validity, reproduce findings, and implement models. TRIPOD+AI provides comprehensive guidelines for predictive model studies using regression or AI/ML methods [14]. Its 27-item checklist includes AI-specific requirements such as hyperparameter tuning, model architecture, computing environment, and code availability, not just “we used a random forest” but specifying the number of trees, maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf, software version, and random seed. Despite this guidance, reporting quality remains poor [12], and current omissions render most published caries AI models unusable. Table 6 summarizes essential reporting elements with complete and incomplete examples. Risk predictions carry inherent uncertainty from three sources (Barreñada et al. [63]). Estimation uncertainty arises from finite sample sizes and can be quantified through resampling or Bayesian methods. Model uncertainty reflects variability from modeling choices, including predictor selection and algorithm. Applicability uncertainty arises from differences between development and deployment settings in populations, measurements, and workflows. Model and applicability uncertainty often dominate estimation uncertainty but remain invisible without external validation. Reporting ideally includes uncertainty around individual risk estimates, not only population-level performance, and full model publication [63]. Beyond reporting, PROBAST+AI provides quality assessment for critical appraisal in systematic reviews and self-assessment during study design [64], evaluating risk of bias across four domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis.

Deployment and Maintenance

Most prognostic models never reach clinical implementation. Few progress beyond external validation to deployment, and fewer still demonstrate improved decision-making or patient outcomes [9]. This represents substantial research waste: models developed, validated, and published but never used. Table 7 summarizes implementation barriers, strategies, monitoring approaches, and updating methods. Models requiring data not collected in routine workflows face immediate implementation failure regardless of statistical performance [53]. Planning for implementation from problem selection onward, with multidisciplinary teams of clinicians, data scientists, and IT specialists, increases the chances of success. Models integrated into existing patient management systems achieve higher adoption than standalone applications [9]. Regulatory considerations also apply. In the European Union, prognostic CPMs are generally classified as Class IIa medical devices (MDR 2017/745, Rule 11). UK guidance similarly regulates software as a device when its internal calculations cannot be easily verified by the user, as is typical of complex AI models. In the USA, software calculating disease risk probabilities is typically ineligible for exemption as clinical decision support and remains subject to FDA regulation [65].

Deployment and maintenance framework for caries prognostic models

Demonstrating clinical value calls for formal impact assessment, evaluating whether models improve outcomes compared with current practice. Without this step, models risk causing harm through overtreatment of false positives or missed prevention from false negatives. Even successfully implemented models require ongoing maintenance. Populations change, disease epidemiology shifts, and clinical practice evolves, making performance drift inevitable [56]. A model developed when fluoride varnish was uncommon may miscalibrate where varnish is now routine. Yet only 13% of published medical AI-CPMs include updating strategies [9]. Monitoring protocols benefit from specifying reassessment intervals (e.g., annually), performance thresholds triggering updates (calibration slope <0.9, C-statistic drop >0.05), and subgroup analyses examining equity across populations. When drift is detected, updating options include neural-network-specific approaches (adjusting retrained layers), ensemble methods (reweighting members), and model-agnostic strategies [66]. Updating an existing model is generally more efficient than developing a new one, preserving accumulated knowledge while adapting to new contexts. Governance structures, clarifying who monitors performance, initiates updates, and approves changes, help ensure systematic maintenance. Documenting all model versions, changes, and performance metrics over time is equally important; models without updating strategies face eventual degradation and potential patient harm. Conclusion

AI methods offer opportunities to predict which patients will develop caries or experience lesion progression, but current evidence shows that most caries prognostic models remain early stage, overoptimistic, inconsistently reported, and rarely linked to interventions proven to modify the disease course. Most models prioritize discrimination metrics while neglecting calibration, uncertainty, and clinical impact. Better predictions do not automatically translate into better outcomes. Experience with diagnostic AI shows that earlier detection can increase intervention without improving health if predictions trigger premature or unnecessary treatment [67]. Individual risk predictions remain inherently uncertain due to estimation, model, and applicability uncertainty, even when population-level performance appears strong [63]. Transparent reporting, external validation, and explicit communication regarding uncertainty are essential. Conceptual frameworks for AI-driven precision dentistry propose linking caries risk estimates to personalized preventive strategies [7]; however, such approaches have rarely been evaluated in pragmatic trials with caries endpoints. Many AI-based prognostic models qualify as software as a medical device when they provide risk estimates that influence decisions, requiring a clear definition of intended use, rigorous validation, and post-deployment monitoring. Academic incentives prioritize novelty over implementation: publishing another model advances careers more than integrating an established model into clinical workflows. This systemic bias against implementation prevents patient benefits. Successful models share characteristics beyond statistical performance: they use routinely available data, link to clear clinical actions (What specific decision does this prediction inform?), and function outside academic centers of excellence. Implementation planning preferably begins during protocol development, not after publication. Every prediction model manuscript should include an implementation section: What needs to happen for dentists to use this tool? What validations are needed? If the model serves hypothesis generation rather than patient care, state this explicitly [68]. Journals should require evidence of clinical utility or concrete implementation plans before accepting prediction model manuscripts. Funders should prioritize prospective validation and implementation of existing models over development of new models addressing the same questions. Researchers should begin with the clinical problem and implementation barriers, not with available data and algorithms.
