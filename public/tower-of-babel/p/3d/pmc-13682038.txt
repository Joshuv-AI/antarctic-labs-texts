Ethnobotanical study on wild edible plants in The Kuancheng Traditional Chestnut Eco-Planting System, a globally important agricultural heritage system in Hebei, China
Authors: Shuai Li, Qing Zhang, Yan Li, Xinxin Wu, Jingyong Wang, Chunlin Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems (GIAHS) are living agroecosystems shaped by the coevolution of traditional knowledge (TK) and agrobiodiversity. As a core GIAHS criterion, agrobiodiversity should be assessed beyond crops and livestock to include wild resources that communities regularly use and manage. Wild edible plants (WEPs) and associated TK that guide their identification, harvesting, processing, and habitat care are therefore a key but poorly documented part of agrobiodiversity in the mountainous regions of northern China. The Kuancheng Traditional Chestnut Eco-Planting System (KTCES) in Hebei Province, northern China, is a chestnut-based GIAHS with great diversity and value of WEPs. Along with the rapid economic development and fast reduction of TK, it is necessary to document WEPs and associated TK in KTCES, both to contribute to the conservation of agrobiodiversity and to provide a reference for the sustainable development of GIAHS.MethodsFrom February 2024 to July 2025, ethnobotanical surveys were conducted in six townships of Kuancheng Manchu Autonomous County, Hebei, China. The investigation methods include free listings, semi-structured interviews, key informant interviews and participatory observations. A total of 124 respondents were interviewed. Information was collected on the edible parts, categories, consumption methods, and multiple uses of WEPs. The cultural significance of these plants was assessed by calculating the relative frequency of citation (RFC) and the cultural food significance index (CFSI).ResultsA total of 87 species of WEPs belonging to 37 families and encompassing various categories, such as wild vegetables (67), wild fruits (16), spices (10) and nuts (3), were documented in this study. The primary parts utilized include tender leaves, fruits, roots, seeds, and flowers, with harvesting predominantly taking place in spring and summer. The most frequently used families were Asteraceae (20), Rosaceae (9), Fabaceae (6) and Amaranthaceae (5). On the basis of the CFSI and RFC values, 30 species were identified as the most significant in local dietary culture, including Gypsophila oldhamiana, Leptopus chinensis, Indigofera kirilowii and Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa and so on.ConclusionKTCES possesses abundant WEPs resources, and local residents possess a wealth of TK related to WEPs utilization. The diverse ways in which WEPs are used form the foundation for regional ecological adaptation and cultural identity. High-value WEPs have driven local economic development, ecological protection, and cultural innovation. It is necessary to investigate, document, and preserve WEPs and associated TK in GIAHS, as they hold particular significance for the conservation of agrobiodiversity and GIAHS.Keywords: Ethnobotany, Globally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems, Agrobiodiversity, Wild edible plants, Traditional knowledge

Introduction

The Globally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems (GIAHS), launched by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) in 2002, are long-evolved agroecosystems in which rural communities have coexisted with their environments over centuries, maintaining remarkable levels of agrobiodiversity, landscape heterogeneity, and traditional knowledge (TK) under changing climatic and socioeconomic conditions [1]. These systems embody not only productive functions but also multi-layered ecological, cultural, and social values, and are increasingly recognized as living laboratories for sustainable agriculture and biocultural conservation [2]. Within the GIAHS framework, agrobiodiversity and TK are tightly interwoven: diverse crops, landraces, and associated wild species are managed through fine-tuned local knowledge, while knowledge itself is continuously renewed through interaction with dynamic ecosystems [3, 4]. In the context of global climate change, biodiversity loss, and food system homogenization, understanding how TK and agrobiodiversity play a role in GIAHS is crucial for rural revitalization strategies [5].

The Kuancheng Traditional Chestnut Eco-Planting System (KTCES) in Hebei Province, northern China, is a representative GIAHS that exemplifies long term coevolution between people and nature. Centered on old chestnut (Castanea mollissima Blume) cultivation, KTCES integrates many mountain farming techniques into a complex agroforestry landscape adapted to the hilly environment of the southern Yanshan Mountains [6]. Designated as a National Important Agricultural Heritage System in 2014 and officially recognized as a GIAHS in 2023, the system has more than 50,000 ancient chestnut trees over 100 years old, rich associated flora and fauna, and a wealth of TK related to soil and water conservation, pest control, and multi-species interactions [7]. Owing to the region’s unique topography and climate, local residents have developed a vertically stratified structure of forest, chestnut, farmland, villages, and rivers (Fig. 1). This multilayer agricultural landscape is highly complementary and provides favorable habitats for WEPs [8]. However, current research has primarily focused on chestnut varieties, cultivation techniques, and landscape functions, while the broader biocultural dimensions—especially the role of wild plant resources and everyday food practices—have received relatively limited attention [9, 10]. As a result, KTCES is not fully recognized for its contributions to agrobiodiversity conservation and food system resilience in national and international GIAHS discussions.

Wild edible plants (WEPs) have been fundamental to human survival and cultural development before the domestication of crops [11, 12]. They provide essential nutrients, vitamins, and bioactive compounds, diversify diets, and act as critical “safety nets” during food shortage, ecological disturbance, or socioeconomic crisis in many rural regions worldwide [13, 14]. Ethnobotanical studies across South Africa, Ethiopia, India have demonstrated that WEPs contribute not only to nutrition and food security but also to household income, traditional medicine, ritual practices, and local gastronomy [15–17]. The sustainable use of WEPs is inseparable from TK, including sophisticated systems of plant identification, seasonal calendars, harvesting rules, processing techniques, and culturally embedded norms that regulate access and use [18]. Within agricultural heritage systems such as KTCES, WEPs is an indispensable part of agrobiodiversity, co-occurring with cultivated crops in forests, field margins, terraces, and village surroundings, and serving as important carriers of memory, identity, and place-based ecological wisdom [19].

However, the TK and practices related to WEPs in agricultural systems are increasingly at risk. Rapid urbanization, labor migration, agricultural modernization, and market integration are reshaping local livelihoods and dietary habits, leading to reduced dependence on wild foods and declining familiarity with surrounding plant resources, especially among younger generations [20–22]. At the same time, land use changes, infrastructure development, and the simplification of farming systems may diminish the habitats that support WEPs diversity, further accelerating the erosion of biocultural diversity [23–25]. Because WEP related TK is transmitted primarily through oral traditions, household work, and collective foraging instead of formal schooling, it is especially vulnerable to loss as intergenerational learning spaces shrink [26]. Elderly residents still retain rich experience in identifying, collecting, processing, and using WEPs, whereas many young people have limited plant literacy and show less interest in traditional foodways, intensifying the risk of both knowledge loss and agrobiodiversity decline within the heritage system [27].

Agrobiodiversity is a core criterion for GIAHS designation and a priority in global agendas on biodiversity conservation and sustainable development. Despite the expanding literature on TK, farming practices, and ecosystem services across GIAHS worldwide, empirical research that explicitly addresses WEPs and their associated knowledge within agricultural heritage systems remains limited, particularly in northern China [28]. To address this gap, this study by integrating ethnobotanical methods with the GIAHS framework, aims to fill this gap by: (1) record the diversity and utilization of WEPs in KTCES; (2) Evaluate the cultural significance and use patterns of WEPs in KTCES; (3) Clarify their importance for conserving agrobiodiversity in GIAHS and maintaining agriculture heritage stability. This research provides a case study that highlights the central role of WEPs and TK in sustaining biocultural resilience and offers insights for the conservation and sustainable use of agricultural heritage in China and beyond.

Methods

Study area

Kuancheng Manchu Autonomous County (Kuancheng County) is located in the northeast of Hebei Province under the administration of Chengde City (40°17’-40°45’N, 118°10’-119°10’E). Kuancheng County is 80 km away from Chengde City and 260 km from Beijing, the capital of China (Fig. 2). Kuancheng County is situated in a temperate continental monsoon climate zone characterized by distinct seasons: cold, dry winters and hot, rainy summers [29]. The region has an average annual temperature of approximately 9 °C and an average annual precipitation of 600–800 mm, with rainfall mainly concentrated from June to August. The area is crisscrossed by rivers, with the Luan River and its tributaries flowing through the county, providing abundant water resources [7]. The terrain is predominantly mountainous, with marked topographical variation, a high forest coverage rate, diverse vegetation types, and a favorable ecological environment. The dominant soil types are brown earth and cinnamon soil, both of which are loose and well aerated and rich in organic matter and trace elements such as magnesium (Mg), iron (Fe), and copper (Cu), thus creating favorable natural conditions for the growth of wild plants [10].

Overview of the study area

Kuancheng County governs 18 townships, 205 villages, and 5 communities, with a total population of approximately 260,000. Among them, the Manchu ethnic group accounts for 75.56% of the population. Around 300 years ago, during the Qing Dynasty, Manchu people settled in Kuancheng through state-organized land reclamation, military garrisoning, and the establishment of postal stations, gradually integrating their way of life and culture with those of the local residents (mainly Han people). Over the course of this long historical process, agricultural knowledge was gradually shaped and consolidated, becoming closely intertwined with local ritual practices, seasonal festivals, and broader folk traditions [30].

Field investigation and data collection

Prior to conducting fieldwork, a literature review was undertaken to gather fundamental information about Kuancheng County, including its geographical location, demographic composition, topography, climate, local customs, and religious beliefs. The research team conducted six rounds of fieldwork in the core area of the KTCES in February and August 2024, and in April, May, June, and July 2025, covering six townships that together constitute the heritage core area, thereby enhancing the representativeness and scientific rigor of the data collected. These field visits were designed mainly to cover the principal collection period of wild vegetables, which in the study area is concentrated in spring and early summer, and to include one late-summer round for documenting part of the fruiting season. Information on species harvested later in autumn was supplemented through online interviews, and records of collection months provided by local respondents.

Field data were collected via a combination of free listing, key informant interviews, and semi-structured interviews [31]. The respondents were selected through snowball sampling, facilitated by recommendations from local community leaders and the interviewees themselves [32]. To reduce the risk of overrepresenting only highly visible knowledge holders, we attempted to include respondents from different townships, villages, genders, age groups, education levels, and occupations. In addition to key informants, general residents with varying degrees of familiarity with WEPs were also included in the interviews. During the interviews, a local key informant provided assistance in facilitating data collection and field observations. The participants were asked to freely list the WEPs species they had used and their related TK. Simultaneously, information was recorded, including local names, acquisition methods, edible parts, processing methods, frequency of use, collection months, and medicinal uses of the plants mentioned [33, 34]. The age, education level and occupation of informants were also recorded. Before each interview, the participants were informed of the aims of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to refuse or withdraw at any time. Prior informed verbal consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Code of Ethics.

During the investigation, a total of 124 local residents were interviewed, including 24 from Huajian Town, 18 from Songling Town, 23 from Nianziyu Town, 25 from Boluotai Town, 20 from Tashan Town, and 14 from Kuancheng Town. Table 1 shows that the respondent pool was relatively broad, although it was mainly composed of adults. Male respondents accounted for 61.3% (76/124) of the sample, while female respondents accounted for 38.7% (48/124), indicating that knowledge from both men and women was represented. In terms of age, the largest groups were respondents aged 20–39 years (40.3%, 50/124) and 40–59 years (35.5%, 44/124), followed by those aged 60 years and above (18.5%, 23/124), whereas respondents aged 19 years or younger accounted for only 5.6% (7/124). Regarding education level, most respondents had secondary education (38.7%, 48/124) or high school education and above (36.3%, 45/124), while smaller proportions had only primary education (17.7%, 22/124) or were illiterate (7.3%, 9/124). The interviewees were engaged in diverse occupations, mainly farmers, retailers, students, and salaried workers. The respondents were local residents from the core area of KTCES and were drawn mainly from the Man and Han communities. These results suggest that the survey included respondents from diverse educational backgrounds, while the documented knowledge was reported mainly by adult community members.

During the fieldwork, voucher samples were collected, and photographs of the surveyed plants were taken for subsequent identification. Voucher specimens were compared with reference works such as the Flora of China and Flora of Hebei, as well as with online resources (http://www.iplant.cn/ and http://www.worldfloraonline.org/). All the vascular plants were named following the Flora of China, and all the samples were identified by Professor Long Chunlin. Voucher specimens were prepared and deposited in the herbarium of the College of Life and Environmental Sciences, Minzu University of China (MUC), Beijing, China.

Food categories: The WEPs used by local people from KTCES include wild vegetables (Wv), wild fruits (Wf), spices (Sp), nuts (Nu), liquor brewing (Lb) and oils and fats (Of)

These WEPs belong to 37 families, with the most represented being Asteraceae (20), Rosaceae (9), Fabaceae (6) and Amaranthaceae (5). Together, these families account for approximately 46.0% of the total species. Twenty-one families are represented by only one species (Fig. 3a). In terms of growth habits, the majority of WEPs is herbaceous (57 species, 65.5%), followed by trees (16 species, 18.4%), shrubs (8 species, 9.2%), and lianas (6 species, 6.9%) (Fig. 3b). Most of the WEPs are distributed in chestnut forests, field margins, gullies, and hillsides, with some also found near villages. The local people consumed WEPs as 6 food types, including vegetable (67 species), fruit (16 species), spice (10 species), nut (3 species), liquor brewing (2) and oil (1 species) (Fig. 3c). There are 12 WEPs that can be consumed as more than one food type. The utilized parts of WEPs are highly diverse and primarily include tender leaves, tender stems, fruits, tender shoots, flowers, bulbs, roots, nuts and kernel (Fig. 3d). Among these, tender leaves and tender stems are the most commonly consumed parts. The tender leaves of 62 species and young stems of 16 species are collected for consumption. Fruits of 17 species are also harvested, and the underground bulbs or roots of certain species are consumed as well, often with additional medicinal value. The methods used to consume WEPs are diverse. Wild vegetables are commonly eaten boiled, stir-fried or making cool dish after blanching, whereas wild fruits are typically consumed freshly. Some WEPs are also used as spice (Fig. 3e). The collection of WEPs clearly shows seasonal characteristics. Spring, especially April to June, is typically the peak season for collecting tender leaves and shoots of wild vegetables, whereas summer (June to August) is the peak period for collecting wild fruits (Fig. 3f).

Wild vegetables

Wild vegetables constitute the majority of WEPs, with a total of 67 species recorded. Most of these wild vegetables are herbaceous plants, providing local residents with abundant fresh greens and dietary fiber. Common wild vegetables include Gypsophila oldhamiana, Amaranthus retroflexus, Dioscorea nipponica, Leptopus chinensis, Indigofera kirilowii, Aralia elata var. glabrescens (Fig. 4). The collection and use of these wild vegetables are mainly concentrated between April and July, with a few species harvested until September or October. The primary edible parts are tender leaves and tender stems or young shoots, which are typically consumed after making boiled or stir-fried dishes, or making cool dishes after boiled.

Some wild vegetables in the study area. a Gypsophila oldhamiana; b Amaranthus retroflexus; c Dioscorea nipponica; d Leptopus chinensis; e Indigofera kirilowii; f Aralia elata var. glabrescens

Wild vegetables also reflect a deep-rooted culinary culture. Gypsophila oldhamiana, known for its crisp and refreshing texture, is widely recognized as a household WEP. Taraxacum mongolicum and Ixeris polycephala, commonly referred as “bitter herbs” (ku cai) locally, are seasonal delicacies consumed around the Qingming Festival, one of China’s most important traditional festivals in Spring for honoring ancestors and enjoying outings. Portulaca oleracea and Amaranthus retroflexus are popular during summer and are also valued for their medicinal properties, such as clearing heat and detoxifying the body. Aromatic plants such as Allium senescens and Mentha canadensis are often used as condiments or flavor enhancers. Owing to their pleasant flavors and regional significance, wild vegetables remain an essential component of farmhouse meals and seasonal diets.

Wild fruit

Wild fruits constitute an important category of consumption, with 16 species recorded in total. The harvesting period typically occurs between July and October. Collection is carried out mainly for household consumption, although some high-quality fruits are also sold at local markets. Most wild fruits are eaten freshly such as Rubus phoenicolasius (Fig. 5a) and Prunus sibirica (Fig. 5b), and while certain species such as Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa (Fig. 5c), Crataegus pinnatifida (Fig. 5d) and Vitis amurensis (Fig. 5e) may be sun-dried or fermented to increase their flavor and shelf-life, and Juglans mandshurica (Fig. 5f) can be used to extract oil.

Some wild fruits in the study area. a Rubus phoenicolasius; b Prunus sibirica; c Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa; d Crataegus pinnatifida; e Vitis amurensis; f Juglans mandshurica

Some wild fruits are highly valued for their nutritional and medicinal properties. In addition to being consumed fresh, they are often processed into herbal teas or health products. For example, according to respondents, Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa is widely used not only as a food but also as a traditional medicinal plant, and it is considered helpful for stimulating appetite and improving sleep. The dual functions of food and medicine among wild fruits significantly contribute to dietary diversity and health security within the local community.

The collection, processing, and consumption of wild fruits have long been embedded in local subsistence practices and cultural life. In the past, farmers often gathered wild fruits during fieldwork to relieve thirst and restore energy, while children used to go into nearby hills in groups to forage for seasonal fruits, which served as a natural supplement to their diet. Such practices not only diversified local food sources but also became an integral part of collective memory and rural identity. Over time, the knowledge associated with wild fruits such as species recognition, harvesting techniques, and seasonal availability has contributed to the transmission of traditional ecological knowledge within the community.

Food substitutes

WEPs not only enriched the dietary structure of local residents but also played an irreplaceable role during specific historical periods and under special environmental conditions. Plants such as Salsola collina and Chenopodium album (Fig. 6a), Amaranthus retroflexus was widely collected in the past as famine foods to substitute for staple grains or vegetables during times of scarcity. Many elderly villagers recalled that in times of food shortages, these wild vegetables often became the main component of meals, effectively alleviating malnutrition. Species such as Portulaca oleracea (Fig. 6b) and Gypsophila oldhamiana are commonly mixed with elm bark flour, which acts as a binder and then combines with cornmeal or sorghum flour to make a locally distinctive dish known as “wild vegetable pies (shan cai he zi)” (Fig. 6c), a type of steamed bun that becomes a surviving food with a unique regional flavor.

Other

Wild fruits such as Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa, Crataegus pinnatifida and Vitis bryoniifolia are important sources of sugar when commercial fruits are scarce. In addition to being consumed fresh, they are traditionally processed into jams, dried fruits, beverages and fruit wines, significantly enhancing resource utilization through time-tested preservation techniques. Nut-producing species such as Corylus heterophylla not only fulfill energy and nutritional needs but also serve as vital sources of plant-based oils. Aromatic herbs such as Perilla frutescens (Fig. 6d) and Mentha canadensis (Fig. 6e) are widely used as substitutes for commercial spices in daily cooking because of their distinctive flavors and fragrances. At the same time, they also have important medicinal value [41].

Many WEPs possess both nutritional and medicinal properties. Evaluation of WEPs using CFSI and RFC

To compare the multidimensional importance of different WEPs, we calculated eight ethnobotanical indices and visualized them in a heatmap (Fig. 8) [42]. In the heatmap, deep orange indicates relatively higher standardized values, whereas deep purple indicates relatively lower standardized values for each ethnobotanical index. By contrast, Commelina communis and Allium senescens ranked relatively higher in RFC than in CFSI, indicating that they were more widely recognized than deeply embedded in the broader food culture. These differences indicate that RFCs mainly reflect the level of cognition of respondents, while CFSI better represents the multicultural significance of WEPs.

Discussion

Diversity of WEPs and regional ecological adaptation

Kuancheng Manchu Autonomous County in Hebei Province is located at the southern margin of the Yanshan Mountains. The complex terrain and climate conditions in this area have nurtured abundant WEPs. This survey recorded a total of 87 WEPs, covering various life forms, such as herbs, shrubs, vines and trees. These plants are distributed in diverse habitats, such as the understory, field ridges, slopes and valleys, which not only demonstrate the diversity of the local ecosystem but also reflect the long-term adaptation and utilization of the environment by villagers.

Compared with the southern or southwestern regions, the WEPs in Kuancheng emphasize cold resistance, drought tolerance and early spring development more strongly [43]. Wild vegetable use is especially prominent in KTCES, with collection concentrated mainly in spring and early summer, reflecting local adaptation to temperate mountain conditions. The present study shows that northern mountainous agricultural heritage systems also retain rich knowledge and practices related to WEP use. In this sense, the Kuancheng case extends current ethnobotanical evidence beyond the better-studied mountain regions of southern China and highlights the value of further research in northern agroecological settings [22, 34, 44–46]. These findings can also be understood in connection with mountain GIAHS or GIAHS-like systems in the Himalayas. Ethnobotanical studies conducted in the mountainous regions of Nepal, India, Bhutan, and Pakistan have likewise shown that wild edible plants contribute to seasonal food supplementation, livelihood maintenance, and the transmission of traditional ecological knowledge, while also reflecting the continuity of food access pathways and strong seasonal adaptation in mountain areas [33, 47–50]. However, compared with these cases, the KTCES exhibits more distinctive characteristics: the collection and use of wild vegetables are particularly prominent in spring and early summer, and the related traditional knowledge is deeply rooted in this chestnut-based GIAHS of northern China. These comparisons suggest that, across different mountain agricultural systems, WEPs should not be regarded as marginal resources, but rather as an important component of agricultural biodiversity, food sources, and biocultural diversity.

Detoxification of some species of WEPs in KTCES

Some wild edible plants, especially certain wild vegetables, contain toxins or substances that are not suitable for consumption. Some of the WEPs recorded in the KTCES should be treated with caution, as their edibility often depends on accurate identification, the plant parts used, and whether they are properly processed. For example, Clematis brevicaudata may cause oral and gastrointestinal irritation if eaten raw or inadequately prepared, only tender organs are typically collected and consumed after blanching. Species in Simaroubaceae, which are characterized by pronounced bitterness and may lead to digestive discomfort, are generally used in small amounts and not as staple foods. By contrast, most Poaceae species are mainly utilized as fodder or functional resources rather than as regular food sources; in a few cases, tender stems and leaves may be collected for consumption, but intake is usually limited. Locals use detoxification methods for processing WEPs in KTCES before they can be consumed. For example, Clematis brevicaudata is typically blanched several times to reduce undesirable flavors and irritation before cooking. After blanching, the young leaves of Ailanthus altissima are eaten only in small amounts and are not used as a staple food. Lespedeza bicolor and Leptopus chinensis are likewise blanched before being stir-fried to reduce potential toxicity, and they are generally consumed in moderation to avoid discomfort. These preparation routines highlight the presence of food-safety-related-knowledge within TK systems, operating as practical risk mitigation measures that help reconcile household health with sustained use of local resources. The TK related to food safety plays a role in risk management, helping to balance the relationship between family health and resource utilization.

The naming habits and causes of WEPs in local culture

Vernacular name of plants refer to the locally specific names used for particular plant species within a given community, which not only facilitate their recognition and differentiation in local contexts, but also embody distinctive dialectal features, patterns of use, and other culturally salient dimensions of human–plant relationships [51]. Plant vernacular names often incorporate elements such as collection season, appearance and use, making them more useful [52]. The local naming of WEPs in KTCES generally adopts the structure of “xx zi”, such as Indigofera kirilowii (“fan hua zi”), Flacourtia ramontchi (“ye li zi”), Vitis bryoniifolia (“lao gua wan zi”) and Leptopus chinensis (“heng zi”).

The naming method reflects villagers’ direct grasp of the edible parts, uses and characteristics of plants, which is the product of folk experience and practical needs [53]. In northern Chinese dialects, the morpheme “zi” commonly conveys meanings such as “small”, “tender” or “fruit”, and its presence in plant names usually indicates that the edible part is either the fruit or the tender shoots and leaves. In this sense, the widespread use of the suffix “zi” in WEPs names information about the main edible organs and their preferred harvesting stage, helping local residents to memorize which parts of the plant can be eaten and how they should be collected and used.

As biocultural heritage, the vernacular names and utilization culture of WEPs are likewise integral components of GIAHS [3], Once these names are lost, it becomes extremely difficult to recover their underlying ecological and cultural meanings. Therefore, documenting folk names together with the associated TK is an urgent task, especially for culturally salient or frequently used species. In this sense, safeguarding the naming traditions of WEPs is not merely a matter of preserving language, it is also a strategic measure to sustain the local knowledge systems that underpin the conservation and sustainable use of agrobiodiversity in the KTCES [54, 55].

WEPs as Key Contributors to the Maintenance and Conservation of GIAHS

Our survey indicates that, within the KTCES, WEPs play a distinctive role in residents’ livelihoods, agrobiodiversity and carrying TK, while also being of great importance for the conservation and development of GIAHS. Figure 9 summarizes these relationships by showing how WEPs connect resident’s livelihoods, agrobiodiversity, and traditional knowledge, which together contribute to the conservation and development of the KTCES as a living GIAHS. WEPs provide farmers with seasonal food sources and nutritional supplements, reflecting the community’s sustainable use and management of natural resources [56]. In the KTCES, when staple crops or vegetables are in short supply, local residents collect WEPs at specific times and process them into food to alleviate hunger while supplying vitamins, minerals and dietary fiber. Through the collection and use of WEPs, communities not only reduce their dependence on external food supplies but also maintain diversified livelihood strategies. During harvesting, local residents flexibly adjust the timing, species composition and intensity of WEPs collection in response to phenological changes and environmental conditions, thereby ensuring the sustainable utilization of these resources.

Conceptual relationships showing how WEPs connect residents’ livelihoods, agrobiodiversity, and traditional knowledge in KTCES, thereby contributing to the conservation and development of this living GIAHS

From the perspective of environmental change, the WEPs recorded in this study are mainly collected from chestnut-forest understories, gullies, hillslopes, and areas surrounding villages. These habitats largely overlap with everyday production and living spaces and are therefore more likely to be affected by changes in land use. For example, intensified clearing of the chestnut understory, hardening of hillslopes, and road improvement or regrading may influence the growth and harvest of some WEPs. Meanwhile, WEPs collection shows marked seasonality and is concentrated within a short window from late spring to early summer, which suggests that extreme climatic fluctuations could shift the growth period and harvesting time of certain species and, in turn, affect how local residents supplement their diets with WEPs foods. Notably, local communities have developed relatively systematic knowledge and processing practices around WEPs use, such as selecting different species by season, harvesting only tender parts (e.g., young leaves), and using blanching to improve palatability. This can be understood as a contribution of WEPs to local food sovereignty, in that communities draw on practical experience to select and use WEPs that match local preferences.

WEPs play a key role in the conservation of agrobiodiversity and sustainable development of agriculture [57]. A wide diversity of WEPs is closely embedded within the chestnut-based agroforestry system, forming complex and reciprocal relationships with chestnut trees, staple crops and free-ranging poultry. Occupying different niches, WEPs on the one hand increase vegetation cover within the agroforestry system, and on the other hand provide important forage resources for poultry, thereby strengthening trophic interactions among different levels of the food chain [58]. These processes enhance species richness within the system and, in turn, make a significant contribution to the maintenance of agricultural biodiversity in the KTCES.

In the KTCES, local communities have developed a distinctive traditional agricultural system. In the context of modern agricultural transformation, the TK associated with WEPs—including their identification, classification, harvesting, processing and storage—provides critical insights into how the biological resources embedded in this system can be used in a sustainable, low-input and biodiversity-friendly manner, while at the same time fostering the transmission and renewal of related biocultural traditions. In this sense, WEPs and their associated TK have become indispensable components of the GIAHS, and incorporating the conservation and dynamic utilization of WEPs into the management of the KTCES is essential for strengthening ecosystem functions, maintaining the diversity of traditional agricultural landscapes and ensuring the long-term sustainability of this agricultural heritage system.

Utilization and protection strategy: Combination of sustainable development and cultural identity

The integrated analysis of CFSI and RFC highlights the central role of WEPs in both local societies and ecosystems while also reflecting their significant potential in knowledge transmission, ecological adaptation and value-added development [59]. During our field investigation, the Gypsophila oldhamiana was widely known. Almost every person we interviewed would mention the vernacular name “ma shan cai” for the Gypsophila oldhamiana and describe its usage methods. WEPs with both high CFSI and RFC values should be prioritized for future resource conservation, innovative utilization, development of region-specific food products, and safeguarding TK [60]. Moreover, local residents have accumulated rich traditional ecological knowledge through the identification, collection, and utilization of WEPs, which also warrants attention.

Our findings indicate that conservation strategies for the KTCES should not focus exclusively on chestnut orchards and landscape features, but explicitly recognize WEPs and their associated TK as integral components of the heritage system. The water and soil conservation measures based on terraces and the full utilization of WEPs help ensure food security, promote nutrient intake, achieve income diversification, and preserve cultural practices. However, they are also threatened by habitat changes, the shift to market-oriented dietary patterns, and the loss of local knowledge. Equally important, the long-term conservation of WEPs depends on the vitality of the cultural identities and knowledge systems that sustain them [61].

When collecting wild vegetables and fruits, many villagers always follow the principle of “appropriate amount, rotation and seed retention”, actively avoid over-picking wild plant resources, and protect species diversity and ecosystem stability. With the development of the local economy and cultural tourism, some high value WEPs have been developed into characteristic agricultural products or healthy food, which has promoted the diversification of the rural economy and increased farmer income. For example, tea beverages made from inflorescence of Castanea mollissima have become a locally distinctive product. On the other hand, villagers still attach importance to the unique role of WEPs in cultural identity, homesickness and the national spirit, which not only reflects profound local ecological knowledge but also enhances community cohesion and cultural confidence.

Our findings have important implications for agricultural biodiversity policy at both the national and international levels. Within the FAO GIAHS framework, GIAHS is regarded as a living system maintained by communities through the interactions among agricultural biodiversity, traditional knowledge, cultural values, and landscape management. In this context, WEPs and the associated traditional knowledge in the KTCES should be explicitly incorporated into the dynamic conservation and management strategies of GIAHS [62]. Our results are also relevant to the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, particularly to the targets related to the sustainable use of wild species and biodiversity-friendly agriculture. In addition, the role of WEPs in seasonal food supplementation, dietary diversity, and the maintenance of mountain ecosystems indicates their clear relevance to Sustainable Development Goal 2 (Zero Hunger) and Sustainable Development Goal 15 (Life on Land). These findings suggest that conserving WEPs and the associated traditional knowledge not only helps enhance local livelihood resilience, but also contributes to broader policy goals related to biodiversity conservation, sustainable agriculture, and food security.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive ethnobotanical assessment of WEPs in the KTCES, a chestnut-based GIAHS in northern China. We documented 87 WEP species from 37 families and 70 genera, as well as their edible parts, food categories, consumption methods and other uses. These plants are harvested mainly in spring and summer and processed through a variety of culinary practices, forming an important seasonal complement to local diets. Quantitative evaluation using RFC and CFSI revealed a set of culturally salient species such as Gypsophila oldhamiana, Leptopus chinensis and Indigofera kirilowii. Vernacular naming habits like the widely used “xx zi” naming pattern directly encodes information on edible parts, harvesting stages and sensory properties, thereby becoming an important component of local biocultural diversity.

It is the first systematic ethnobotanical survey of WEPs conducted within an agricultural heritage system in northern China, revealing the region’s rich biodiversity and TK. Our study elucidates the pivotal role of WEPs within the KTCES. Specifically, WEPs serve as a crucial foundation for sustaining local livelihoods by providing both a reliable food source and supplementary nutrition. These plants constitute a vital component of agrobiodiversity, contributing to the stability and resilience of the ecosystem. Importantly, WEPs are also reservoirs of rich TK, embodying the cultural wisdom developed through long-term interaction and adaption with the natural environment. In the future, ethnobotanical knowledge within the GIAHS framework, as well as that in northern China, deserves further attention and exploration. Multidisciplinary collaboration and community participation should be promoted, together with public science outreach, to achieve the sustainable development of GIAHS.
