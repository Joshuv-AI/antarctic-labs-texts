Association Between Symptomatic Knee Osteoarthritis and Target Glycemic Control in Individuals With Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Lauren K. King, Alanna Weisman, Baiju R. Shah, Robert Goldberg, Amish Parikh, Ian Stanaitis, Vivian Hung, Rosane Nisenbaum, Abdalmohsen Almodahka, Lorraine Lipscombe, Gillian A. Hawker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveKnee osteoarthritis (OA) commonly affects individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and is associated with increased risk of diabetes‐related complications. To better understand potential mechanisms, we examined the association between symptomatic knee OA and glycemic control in individuals with T2DM.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, we recruited individuals with T2DM aged ≥45 years from three academic centers in Canada. Online questionnaires assessed demographics, medical history, and joint symptoms. We abstracted glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) from clinic records. Knee OA was defined as fulfilling the National Institute of Clinical Excellence criteria. Target glycemic control was defined as an HbA1c level ≤7.0%. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the association between knee OA and target glycemic control, adjusting for age, gender, education level, and body mass index. Secondary analyses assessed associations between knee OA with pain ≥20/100 (and knee OA with walking difficulty) and target glycemic control.ResultsAmong 351 participants (mean age 66.2 years, 50.7% women), 28.5% met the criteria for knee OA and 43.9% were at glycemic target. In unadjusted analyses, those with knee OA had lower odds of being at target glycemic control (odds ratio [OR] 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.37–0.97), but the association was not statistically significant after adjusting for confounders (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.39–1.08). In those with knee OA with pain ≥20/100, a negative association with target glycemic control was statistically significant in adjusted analysis (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.34–0.99).ConclusionIndividuals with T2DM and painful knee OA are less likely to be at glycemic target, increasing their risk of diabetes complications.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, 1 affects approximately one in nine adults worldwide, 2 and its prevalence continues to rise. 3 Despite advances in therapies, T2DM remains a substantial public health issue. 4 , 5 Optimizing glycemic control in individuals with T2DM is a central part of disease management to reduce the risks of microvascular and cardiovascular complications. 6 Identifying modifiable barriers to achieving glycemic targets is therefore crucial to improve care and outcomes for the large and growing numbers of individuals living with T2DM. This has, in turn, implications for the health system and greater societal impact of the disease.

Knee osteoarthritis (OA), the most common form of arthritis, is characterized by chronic joint pain and functional limitations. It frequently co‐exists in people with T2DM 7 and may pose a challenge to engaging in effective diabetes self‐management. Knee OA is the leading cause of mobility impairment and disability among middle‐aged and older adults, 8 yet it is often underdiagnosed and undertreated, 9 , 10 particularly in individuals with multimorbidity who have competing medical demands. 11 Among individuals with both T2DM and knee OA, our prior study using a prospective cohort study linked to health administrative data found that OA‐related disability was associated with a higher risk of developing diabetes‐specific complications and cardiovascular events. 12 In qualitative work, we found that individuals with T2DM and knee OA saw their OA‐related symptoms, such as joint pain and related mobility impairments, as barriers to effective diabetes management, including engaging in recommended levels of physical activity. 13 Although these studies suggest that symptomatic knee OA may be a modifiable barrier to optimal diabetes management, it remains unclear if the presence of knee OA impacts glycemic control. Clarifying this relationship is essential for understanding and communicating how OA affects diabetes outcomes and for informing integrated care strategies.

Our objective was to evaluate the association between symptomatic knee OA and being at diabetes glycemic target in individuals with T2DM and to understand how symptom severity, including burden of OA pain and mobility impairments, impacts this association. We hypothesized that knee OA would be negatively associated with being at target glycemic control, even after adjusting for potential confounders, and a stronger association would be found among those with greater pain and those with difficulty walking.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a cross‐sectional study. Reporting was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist. 14 We received research ethics approval from the Women's College Hospital Research Ethics Board (REB #2020‐0129‐E), Mount Sinai Hospital REB (REB # 21‐0068‐E), the REB of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre (project ID# 4889), and the University of Toronto Office of Research Ethics (#40735).

Participants

We recruited participants between March 2022 and August 2023 from outpatient diabetes clinics at three academic hospitals in Toronto, Canada. Individuals aged ≥45 years with a physician diagnosis and clinic visit for T2DM within the past 1 year were invited to participate by mail. Exclusion criteria included inability to read and comprehend English or moderate‐to‐severe cognitive impairments that would limit ability to complete study questionnaires, lower extremity amputation, active diabetes‐related foot ulcer(s), or use of wheelchair to mobilize that would limit ability to ascertain OA impact.

Assessments

Consenting participants completed an online questionnaire that assessed sociodemographics (age, self‐identified gender [categorized as man, woman, and other], race and ethnicity [self‐reported: fixed categories with additional open‐ended response], and education level), self‐reported height and weight to calculate body mass index (BMI), comorbidities from a list to calculate the functional comorbidity index (a sum of the presence of 18 specific conditions, with each condition receiving 1 point), 15 diabetes treatments being used, and joint symptoms. Participants were asked about, in one or both knees, the presence of activity‐related knee joint pain (yes/no) and presence of morning knee joint stiffness (none, ≤30 minutes, 30–60 minutes, or >60 minutes). Those reporting knee joint pain were asked to indicate the average pain severity over the past week on a 0 to 100 numeric rating scale. All participants were asked about sleep disturbance (Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [PROMIS] Sleep Disturbance Short Form 4a), fatigue (PROMIS Fatigue Short Form 4a), and depressive symptoms (PROMIS Depression Short Form 8b). As recommended, PROMIS measures raw scores were converted into a t‐score that rescales the raw score into a standardized score with a mean of 50 (average for the US general population) and an SD of 10. 16 Participants were also asked about the presence of walking difficulty (from the Health Assessment Questionnaire, ability to walk outdoors on flat ground: without ANY difficulty, with SOME difficulty, with MUCH difficulty, or UNABLE to).

We abstracted, from clinic records, the glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) value assessed closest to the date of questionnaire completion. As HbA1c approximates glycemic control over 3 months, this was required to be within 3 months (before or after) questionnaire completion.

The questionnaires were administered confidentially using a secure web application (REDCap). All participants provided electronic informed consent through REDCap's e‐Consent Framework. Questionnaires were piloted by two patient research partners before study recruitment and revised based on feedback.

Outcome

Our outcome of interest was being at target glycemic control (yes/no), defined as HbA1c ≤7.0%. HbA1c is a valid and reliable measure of 3‐month average blood glucose in individuals with T2DM. 17 In most individuals with T2DM, an A1c ≤7.0% is recommended to reduce the risk of microvascular and, if implemented early in the course of disease, cardiovascular complications. 18 We chose to categorize HbA1c for clinical relevance and interpretability, aligning with clinical guidelines. 19 , 20

Exposures

Our exposure of interest was symptomatic knee OA (yes/no), defined using National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE) criteria (aged ≥45 years, activity‐related knee joint pain, and either absence of morning stiffness or morning stiffness lasting ≤30 minutes). 21 To minimize the potential for misclassification, individuals classified as having knee OA could not report the presence of inflammatory arthritis (eg, rheumatoid arthritis) or connective tissue disease (eg, systemic lupus erythematosus). The NICE criteria for knee OA have a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 85% for a rheumatologist diagnosis of knee OA in individuals with T2DM. 22

We had two secondary exposures. The first exposure was “painful knee OA,” defined as fulfilling the NICE criteria for OA and reporting a minimum threshold of average pain severity of ≥20/100. This threshold was selected to denote a meaningful amount of pain (in line with clinically important difference 23 ) while maintaining an adequate sample for analysis. The NICE criteria do not specify a timeframe for or severity of symptoms 22 ; therefore, this exposure was intended to better capture individuals actively experiencing the illness of knee OA. The second exposure was “knee OA with walking difficulty,” defined as fulfilling the NICE criteria for OA and reporting any difficulty walking (walking “with SOME difficulty,” “with MUCH difficulty,” or “UNABLE to”).

Covariates

Based on the existing literature 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 and expertise of our research team, we considered age, gender (dichotomized for analysis as woman and man/other), educational level (postsecondary education, yes/no), and BMI as potential confounders of the association between symptomatic knee OA and target glycemic control. Older age is associated with the presence of symptomatic knee OA 27 and worse T2DM control. 24 Women are more likely to have symptomatic OA compared with men, 27 and gender‐based differences in glycemic control are reported. 28 Lower education (a proxy for socioeconomic status) 27 and higher BMI 27 are associated with presence of knee OA and worse glycemic control. 26 Given the potential for knee OA–related sedentary behavior to result in weight gain, 29 we recognized that BMI could also be a potential mediator in the association between knee OA and glycemic control. Sample size

Our a priori sample size calculation was completed with HbA1c as a continuous variable. To detect a 0.5% clinically important difference in HbA1c in individuals with/without knee OA, 30 with estimated HbA1c SD of 1.4, 31 we assumed that 25% of participants would have knee OA based on the NICE criteria. A total sample size of 328 individuals was required for 80% power and a two‐sided alpha of 0.05. Before analysis, our research team decided to present HbA1c as a dichotomized outcome (at target glycemic control of HbA1c ≤7, yes/no) for clinical relevance and interpretability. We have not performed a post‐hoc power calculation given its limited usefulness for data interpretation. 32

Statistical analysis

Only participants with a valid measurement for HbA1c were included in the analyses (seven participants were excluded). Among included participants, variable missingness was low (education level 2.0%, BMI 0.3%). Participant characteristics were summarized using frequencies, means and SDs, or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, and compared by knee OA status (t‐test or Wilcoxon two‐sample test for continuous variables and chi‐square test or Fisher exact test for binary or categorical variables).

Using multivariable logistic regression, we assessed the association between symptomatic knee OA and being at target glycemic control before and after adjusting for age, gender, and education level. As BMI may be a potential confounder and also a potential mediator, we further adjusted for BMI in a separate model to assess the effect on glycemic control before and after this adjustment. Before regression modeling, we assessed potential multicollinearity among independent variables using the variance inflation factor where values >4 indicate collinearity; none were collinear.

In secondary, exploratory analyses, we repeated modeling above using our two secondary exposures, “painful knee OA” and “knee OA with walking difficulty.” We performed analyses using SAS Studio Version 3.81 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). Statistical significance was defined if two‐sided P values were <0.05.

RESULTS

Participants

There were 351 participants (Supplementary Figure 1). The mean ± SD age was 66.2 ± 9.8 years, 50.7% were women, 81.1% had a postsecondary education, the mean ± SD BMI was 29.1 ± 6.8 kg/m2, and 28.5% fulfilled the NICE criteria for knee OA. The mean ± SD HbA1c was 7.4 ± 1.2 and 43.9% had HbA1c at or below glycemic target (≤7.0%). Those with knee OA were less likely to be at target glycemic control (35.0%) compared with those without knee OA (47.4%) (P = 0.03). Individuals with knee OA were also more likely to report difficulty walking and greater sleep disturbance, fatigue, and depressive symptoms compared with those without knee OA (all P < 0.01). Association between knee OA overall and target glycemic control

In unadjusted analysis, the presence of knee OA was associated with lower odds of being at target glycemic control (odds ratio [OR] 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.37–0.97). The association was similar in magnitude but no longer statistically significant after adjusting for age, gender, and education level (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.38–1.01) and when further adjusting for BMI (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.39–1.08). DISCUSSION

In this cross‐sectional study of individuals with T2DM, after adjusting for age, gender, education level, and BMI, we found those with symptomatic knee OA who had a minimum threshold of average pain severity (≥20/100) were approximately 40% less likely to be at target glycemic control. We also found strong effect estimates for knee OA overall and knee OA with walking difficulty. Although effect estimates were minimally attenuated after adjusting for potential confounders, CIs around point estimates were wide and not statistically significant in the adjusted analyses. Our findings suggest that symptomatic knee OA may impact diabetes glycemic control, particularly when symptom severity meets a minimum threshold. Results should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis generating given the cross‐sectional nature of the study and serve as an important foundation for future research.

Our findings align with prior research demonstrating that knee OA is associated with a higher incidence of and worse outcomes of various chronic conditions. 12 , 33 , 34 Incident physician‐diagnosed knee OA has been linked to an increased risk of developing comorbidities, such as T2DM, cardiovascular disease, and depression. 35 Moreover, symptomatic OA has been associated with increased risk of hospitalization for multiple ambulatory care sensitive conditions, 36 highlighting OA's potential for deleterious effects on chronic disease management. In a population cohort of individuals with both T2DM and knee OA, each worsening level of walking difficulty on the Health Assessment Questionnaire was associated with a 24% increased risk of first diabetes complication. 12 Few studies, however, have examined mechanistic links. Results from the current study could reflect several different mechanisms.

Symptomatic knee OA may impact diabetes control through multiple different potential pathways. 37 A key mechanism may be through the effects of OA‐related physical inactivity and sedentary behavior on that result in reduced glucose uptake by muscle 38 and, in turn, worse glycemic control. 39 Regular physical activity is recommended to control blood glucose in individuals with T2DM. 6 , 40 A previous cross‐sectional study, using data from the Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study, found that <10% of individuals with knee OA meet the recommended levels of physical activity (150 min/week of at least moderate intensity activity). 41 However, they also found little impact of knee pain levels on physical activity level, 41 results that are hard to reconcile with the results of the current study and prior research. 42 A possible explanation is that even a low threshold of symptom severity is sufficient to change physical activity behavior. Avoidance of physical activities that exacerbate OA symptoms is a strategy frequently employed by individuals with OA to self‐manage their joint symptoms. 43 Further, physical inactivity because of pain may contribute to increased fat mass and/or sarcopenia, 44 negatively impacting glycemic control. 45 In the current study, those with knee OA had a higher BMI than those without, which could be a cause or consequence of their OA.

There may also be effects of knee OA pain on glycemic control independent of physical activity. Worse pain may be associated with greater catecholamine release, a counterregulatory hormone that increases insulin resistance. 46 As evident in this study, individuals with symptomatic knee OA have a higher prevalence of depression than those without. 47 In individuals with T2DM, depression has been linked with worse diabetes outcomes and quality of life, 48 potentially because of the effects of depression on adherence to self‐management strategies, pharmacologic treatments, and/or medical visits. This may be compounded by poor sleep and fatigue that are common in patients with OA 49 and also demonstrated in the current study. Although this study provides important initial data on the association between knee OA and glycemic control, exactly how knee OA leads to hyperglycemia remains to be fully elucidated.

This study has many strengths. This is the first study, to our knowledge, to evaluate the association between symptomatic knee OA and glycemic control in people with T2DM. We used a clinical diagnosis of knee OA (NICE criteria) that overcomes limitations of using radiographs, physician‐reported diagnosis, or patient‐reported diagnosis (misclassification). This approach most closely reflects the true spectrum of OA, and OA symptom severity, seen in the community. This also means that not all individuals classified as having knee OA in our study had OA‐related symptoms that had prompted them to seek care, which may underestimate the effect of OA given the importance of symptom severity.

This study also has limitations. First, our sample size was originally intended to assess HbA1c as a continuous outcome and we may have been underpowered to detect a true effect of knee OA overall and knee OA with walking difficulty using the binary outcome of being at target glycemic control. Nonetheless, we felt the clinical relevance of this outcome outweighed this limitation. Related to this, CIs were wide; an increased number of participants could have improved the precision of estimates of effect. As such, these results should be viewed as hypothesis generating, helping to guide future, larger studies. Future, larger studies may also facilitate important additional analyses, including assessing the effects of OA on target glycemic control by different thresholds of pain and walking difficulty and considering a combined approach. Second, although an HbA1c target of ≤7.0% is most frequently recommended across clinical guidelines, 19 , 20 most guidelines allow for some more lenient targets for those with fragility or who are institutionalized. This is unlikely to be relevant for the population in this study who attended outpatient clinic visits. Third, this was a highly educated sample (>80% had a postsecondary education) and therefore results may not be generalizable to those with lower educational attainment. Fourth, we were unable to adjust for income because of high missingness (16%); this may confound the association between knee OA and glycemic control as those with lower income may be more likely to have OA and worse glycemic control. We believe, however, that education level is a reasonable proxy for socioeconomic status. 50 Fifth, recruitment from within diabetes clinics at tertiary hospitals might limit generalizability of results to those with most severe or difficult to manage T2DM. Sixth, our study lacked body composition data that could add further mechanistic insights in future work. The addition of accelerometry in future studies could also add important insights related to effects of physical (in)activity. Seventh, given the cross‐sectional nature, we cannot infer causation; reverse causation may be possible (worse glycemic control results in knee OA). 7 , 37

In conclusion, in this cross‐sectional study of individuals with T2DM, we found that knee OA with a minimum threshold of average pain severity of ≥20/100 was associated with lower odds of being at target glycemic control. This adds to the current literature and suggests symptomatic knee OA may be a clinically relevant, modifiable, risk factor for diabetes complications through worse glycemic control. Future studies are required to confirm or refute these results and, if confirmed, identify implementation strategies to enhance knee OA care in T2DM and evaluate the effects of these strategies on diabetes management and outcomes.
