A Rare Case of Chilaiditi Syndrome as a Driver of Chronic Respiratory Failure
Authors: Paige C. Adams, Daniel Kramer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChilaiditi sign is a rare radiographic finding demonstrating interposition of the colon between the liver and the diaphragm; Chilaiditi syndrome is the manifestation of gastrointestinal or respiratory symptoms secondary to this finding. This syndrome rarely can be associated with acute and chronic respiratory failure. We report a case of a 66‐year‐old woman with chronic hypercapnic and hypoxemic respiratory failure due to severe restrictive pulmonary mechanics in the setting of Chilaiditi syndrome. Imaging showed an elevated right hemidiaphragm initially concerning for hemidiaphragm paralysis; however a fluoroscopic Sniff test suggested normal diaphragm function. Despite hospitalisation for aggressive bowel decompression combined with noninvasive ventilation (NIV), this patient's chronic respiratory failure ultimately proved fatal. This case illustrates a rare presentation of Chilaiditi syndrome with chronic hypercapnic and hypoxemic respiratory failure refractory to standard management. Recognition of this entity and multidisciplinary care are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and optimise patient outcomes.

Introduction

This report outlines a rare case of refractory chronic hypercapnic and hypoxemic respiratory failure secondary to Chilaiditi syndrome, an interposition of the bowel between the liver and the diaphragm that produces symptoms. Respiratory failure is an unusual presentation, and is frequently resolved with conservative management. This case highlights a chronic, fatal disease course and difficulties in management with noninvasive ventilation (NIV). We provide the first demonstration to our knowledge of a fluoroscopic sniff test in the workup of Chilaiditi syndrome. The discussion emphasises a multidisciplinary approach.

Case Report

A 66‐year‐old woman first presented to our outpatient pulmonology team in 2020. At the time of presentation, she carried a diagnosis of COPD requiring 4 L of home oxygen and nocturnal BPAP for 10 years, though she had no smoking history, evidence of emphysematous changes on prior imaging, nor a clear obstructive picture on prior pulmonary function testing. She reported dyspnea with minimal activity and intermittent wheezing improved by nebulisers. She also had a history of a chronically elevated right hemidiaphragm; this was initially thought to be a sequela of her complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), which primarily affected the bilateral lower extremities, as well as her small fibre neuropathy. Notably, she had congenital small bowel malrotation and redundant sigmoid colon, complicated by chronic colonic pseudo‐obstruction. Chest radiography demonstrated marked elevation of the right hemidiaphragm with hepatic flexure distension causing compressive atelectasis of the right lung (Figure 1); CT imaging confirmed bowel loops in the right upper quadrant (Figure 2).

The patient endorsed sporadic nocturnal use without diurnal use of her home BPAP, as she found both the full facemask and ventilation settings difficult to tolerate. Pulmonary function testing revealed a mixed obstructive–restrictive ventilatory pattern. Her care team determined that it was unlikely that she had a true diagnosis of COPD, though she still lacked a unifying diagnosis.

Over the next several years, she was hospitalised frequently with intractable nausea and vomiting, most frequently secondary to colonic ileus requiring rigorous bowel regimens. She was also frequently hospitalised with chief complaints of dyspnea and lower extremity edema, typically with findings of acute on chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure. Her dyspnea was often comorbid with her abdominal symptoms, but was often misattributed to CRPS or COPD exacerbations. Her dyspnea often resolved spontaneously with resumption of BPAP, though the resolution of her symptoms often coincided with abdominal decompression. With each hospitalisation it was difficult to ascertain what led to her presentation as she often had imaging in the form of CT scans that did not show an acute pulmonary process (see Table 1 for details of each hospitalisation). She typically presented to the hospital requiring her baseline supplemental oxygen and her blood gases consistently showed a compensated hypercapnia, suggesting more of a subacute or chronic process rather than an acute insult leading to her hospitalisations. We believe that her pulmonary reserve was so limited in the setting of severe restriction and severe chronic hypercapnia that even a small insult to her physiology could lead to an acute presentation. These insults typically were in the form of volume overload related to right ventricular dysfunction and/or worsening constipation that likely impaired her diaphragm mechanics further.

She remained on BPAP with poor adherence until January of 2024, when her respiratory assist device (RAD) was switched for a home ventilator in a volume assured pressure support (VAPS) mode. Repeat pulmonary function testing revealed near‐universal decreases from prior testing and suggested she did not have obstructive lung disease. Decreased DLCO and elevated KCO were consistent with extrapulmonary restriction without significant underlying parenchymal lung disease or pulmonary vascular disease.

In July of 2024, she was again admitted to the hospital with worsening dyspnea, lower extremity edema and constipation. The patient was maintained on her home oxygen and NIV and initiated on a rigorous bowel regimen by gastroenterology. Despite this, her carbon dioxide level remained as high as 91 mmHg on venous blood gas. Her stool burden decreased with an escalated bowel regimen, corresponding to an eventual decrease in her oxygen needs. A fluoroscopic sniff test conducted after improvement of her acute respiratory failure showed bilaterally reduced excursion of the diaphragm, worse on the right side (shown in Video 1). There was no evidence of diaphragm paralysis.

She was discharged home with the continuation of nocturnal NIV. Given her radiographic and fluoroscopic findings and the improvement of her acute respiratory failure with bowel decompression, she was formally diagnosed with Chilaiditi syndrome. She reported continued difficulty adhering to nocturnal NIV, and through the end of 2024, she required several more hospitalisations for dyspnea secondary to Chilaiditi syndrome. The patient was recommended for a gastrointestinal workup, but unfortunately died from the sequelae of her disease shortly prior to the submission of this report.

Discussion

Chilaiditi sign is the radiologic finding of an abnormal interposition of the colon between the liver and the diaphragm; Chilaiditi syndrome is the clinical manifestation of symptoms secondary to this anomaly [1]. Interposition may occur in the anterior or posterior subphrenic spaces, or in the extraperitoneal space; these locations are classified as hepatodiaphragmatic interposition types 1–3, respectively [2]. Congenital or iatrogenic anomalies in the falciform ligament or suspensory ligaments of the transverse colon, or other malpositions of the anatomic contents of the right upper quadrant, may all contribute to this finding [3]. Diaphragmatic deconditioning, chronic changes in intrathoracic pressure and phrenic nerve paralysis may also be contributing pulmonary factors [4]. There is a comparatively high incidence of Chilaiditi syndrome in patients with chronic lung disease; this may be due to enlargement of the lower thoracic outlet allowing space for bowel interposition [2, 5]. Other conditions such as chronic constipation with colonic redundancy, hepatic atrophy and pregnancy may also contribute to the development of this condition [2, 3].

Chilaiditi sign is a rare but important cause of pseudo‐pneumoperitoneum. Unlike true pneumoperitoneum, the apparent air under the diaphragm in Chilaiditi sign will not be position‐dependent. Chilaiditi syndrome is also commonly misdiagnosed as a diaphragmatic hernia, and cases of these disorders coexisting have also been reported [3, 6]. Radiography may be sufficient to confirm the diagnosis; however, a CT scan may be performed in cases of diagnostic uncertainty. Bronchoscopy is uncommonly conducted in the setting of respiratory symptoms, but may show signs of extrinsic compression [7].

Gastrointestinal symptoms commonly include abdominal pain, vomiting and symptoms of obstruction [8]. Less commonly, pulmonary symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, or even respiratory failure may accompany the syndrome [3]. Severe Chilaiditi syndrome may lead to lung compression, and the interposed bowel prohibits the complete excursion of the diaphragm with inspiration. This may result in hypoxic respiratory failure, if there is significant atelectasis or other ventilation/perfusion mismatch, or hypercapnic respiratory failure, if the degree of diaphragmatic restriction is significant. Additionally, the altered diaphragm positioning creates a mechanical disadvantage in generating negative intrathoracic pressure; affected patients may demonstrate decreased residual volume, decreased tidal volumes and increased recruitment of accessory muscles, with the potential for dramatically increased work of breathing. Elevated KCO values on pulmonary function testing, in combination with a decreased DLCO, are a helpful indicator of extrapulmonary lung compression when pulmonary function testing is nonspecific. Severe respiratory distress and acute hypoxic respiratory failure are rare manifestations of Chilaiditi syndrome, and appear to be somewhat more commonly reported in the literature in paediatric cases; interestingly, hypercapnic respiratory failure is less commonly reported across both age demographics [4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Pulmonary manifestations of Chilaiditi syndrome can be conceptualised similarly to other compressive or restrictive etiologies of respiratory failure, including diaphragmatic hernia, diaphragmatic paralysis, obesity hypoventilation, or intra‐abdominal hypertension. High‐flow nasal cannula has been shown to be an appropriate means of oxygenation in a case of hypoxemic respiratory failure in Chilaiditi syndrome [9]. While some of the aforementioned parallel restrictive causes of respiratory failure lend themselves to positive‐pressure ventilation, care must be taken with this approach in the case of Chilaiditi syndrome, as positive‐pressure ventilation may theoretically cause worsening bowel distention and increase intra‐abdominal pressures [15, 16]. Nonetheless, NIV was shown to be safe and effective in a case of acute hypoxic and hypercapnic respiratory failure secondary to Chilaiditi syndrome when used in conjunction with colonic decompression [10]. Our patient used her home NIV regimen throughout her hospitalisation, which helped to manage her acute‐on‐chronic hypercapnia and did not preclude resolution of her abdominal distension and overall clinical improvement. Decompressive measures may reduce the need for positive pressure interventions [16].

This case presents one of the first reported descriptions of a fluoroscopic sniff test to characterise the elevated right hemidiaphragm in the workup of Chilaiditi syndrome. Chilaiditi syndrome itself is a cause of elevated right hemidiaphragm due to colonic obstruction of diaphragmatic excursion; however, an elevated hemidiaphragm from alternate causes, such as diaphragmatic paralysis or eventration, may be a predisposing underlying factor to developing Chilaiditi syndrome [4, 17]. Thus, Chilaiditi syndrome may be the primary driver of diaphragmatic elevation, or may be secondary to an underlying diaphragmatic pathology. Fluoroscopic evaluation of diaphragmatic function may elucidate if surgical interventions such as diaphragmatic plication are practical.

Conservative management of Chilaiditi syndrome, regardless of the nature of symptomatology, prioritises a rigorous bowel regimen, fluid supplementation and decompression. Therefore, Chilaiditi syndrome is an important differential diagnosis, as conservative management often suffices and prevents unnecessary surgical interventions. Surgical management is typically reserved for complications such as perforation, ischemia, or refractory disease. In cases of underlying lung disease, conservative management may only yield a partial response [5]. A multidisciplinary approach including pulmonology, gastroenterology, radiology and surgery colleagues is prudent.

In this case, her combined Chilaiditi syndrome and chronic hypoxemic and hypercapnic respiratory failure unfortunately persisted until her death. This was despite hospitalisation for aggressive bowel decompression and more sustained nighttime ventilation in a VAPS mode. The combination of home NIV non‐compliance and refractoriness of her Chilaiditi syndrome causing chronic respiratory failure makes this a particularly unique case. While some may consider high‐flow nasal cannula for patients with chronic dyspnea and respiratory failure, this was never utilised as it is a much less ideal form of respiratory support for patients with chronic hypoventilation due to thoracic restrictive disorders. It is our practice typically to employ high‐flow nasal cannula in patients with profound hypoxemia which was never an issue in this case. It is important to note that while it was difficult to obtain accurate weight measurements due to her wheelchair‐bound state, her underlying obesity likely further exacerbated her underlying extrapulmonary restriction and increased the mechanical load on the diaphragms. Multi‐disciplinary work with gastroenterology and pulmonary experts to determine if her condition was reversible was unfortunately cut short as her disease proved fatal.
