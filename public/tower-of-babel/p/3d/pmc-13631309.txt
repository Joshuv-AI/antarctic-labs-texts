PRRC2A-mediated m6A modification on NCOA4 promotes thymic epithelial tumor progression through PKM2-induced glycolysis
Authors: Tao Wang, Lang Xia, Lei zhang, Yuntao Feng, Haoran E, Jueming Yu, Hongbo Huang, Junqi Wu, Chang Chen, Yue Zhao, Deping Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are heterogeneous neoplasms. While most early-stage cases are curable after resection, advanced or aggressive subtypes have limited treatment options and poor outcomes. The molecular mechanisms underlying their metabolic reprogramming remain poorly understood. Proline-rich Coiled-coil 2a (PRRC2A), a N6-methyladenosine (m6A) reader protein, is significantly upregulated in TET tissues and is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Multi-omics analyses and molecular experiments revealed that PRRC2A binds to m6A-modified sites within the NCOA4 mRNA, thereby stabilizing NCOA4 transcripts and enhancing its protein expression. Elevated NCOA4 interacts with PKM2, a rate-limiting glycolytic enzyme, resulting in increased glycolysis and promoting TETs progression. In vivo experiments showed that inhibition of PRRC2A suppressed tumor growth and metastasis in TETs, while combined treatment with the PKM2 inhibitor C599 and PRRC2A knockdown produced enhanced antitumor effects. These findings provide novel insights into the glycolytic metabolic regulation of TETs and suggest that the PRRC2A-NCOA4-PKM2 axis represents a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), including thymoma and thymic carcinoma, are rare malignancies with limited therapeutic options for advanced disease [1, 2]. According to the current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) clinical guidelines, the recommended chemotherapy regimen for TET (including both thymomas and thymic carcinomas) is a platinum-based combination therapy [3–6]. However, the overall objective response rate (ORR) is only 50–70%, and the complete response (CR) rate is less than 10% [7, 8]. Complete surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality [2]; nevertheless, for unresectable or metastatic cases, platinum-based chemotherapy represents the standard regimen but provides only modest clinical benefit [9, 10]. Therefore, further elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying TET initiation and progression is essential for identifying more effective therapeutic targets.

Epigenetic dysregulation has emerged as a critical driver of tumorigenesis across multiple cancer types. Among RNA modifications, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most abundant internal modification of eukaryotic mRNA and plays a pivotal role in post-transcriptional gene regulation, including RNA stability, splicing, nuclear export, and translation [11–13]. The biological effects of m6A modification are mediated through a coordinated system of methyltransferases (“writers”), demethylases (“erasers”), and RNA-binding proteins (“readers”) [14–16]. Dysregulation of m6A signaling has been implicated in tumor initiation and progression, influencing processes such as proliferation, metastasis, and metabolic adaptation [11, 12, 17]. In TETs, recent studies have begun to implicate dysregulated m6A modification in tumor progression; however, the functional roles of individual m6A readers in this disease context remain largely unexplored.

Proline-rich coiled-coil 2A (PRRC2A) is a recently identified m6A reader protein that was initially reported to play an essential role in spermatogenesis. Subsequent studies have extended its functional relevance to cancer biology, demonstrating that PRRC2A promotes tumor progression in digestive system malignancies through activation of oncogenic signaling pathways [18–21]. Despite these emerging insights, whether PRRC2A contributes to TET pathogenesis and the molecular mechanisms by which PRRC2A-mediated m6A recognition regulates tumor-associated pathways in TETs remain undefined.

Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) was initially characterized as a mediator of ferritinophagy and iron homeostasis [22–24]. Beyond its canonical role in iron metabolism, emerging evidence indicates that NCOA4 acts as a transcriptional coactivator and is critically involved in the regulation of gene expression in cancer cells. By cooperating with specific transcription factors, NCOA4 enhances the transcription of target genes, thereby orchestrating various cellular programs [25]. Notably, dysregulation of NCOA4 has been increasingly associated with the pathogenesis of various cancers. Emerging evidence positions NCOA4 as a critical regulator of metabolic reprogramming that drives tumor growth, particularly the shift toward aerobic glycolysis - known as the Warburg effect [26, 27]. This reprogramming allows cancer cells to preferentially generate energy and biosynthetic precursors rapidly, even under oxygen-sufficient conditions [28]. A central player in this glycolytic surge is the pyruvate kinase M2 isoform (PKM2), which is frequently upregulated in tumors [29]. As a key rate-limiting enzyme, PKM2 catalyzes the final step of glycolysis and diverts metabolic flux toward anabolic pathways required for cell proliferation [30]. Although recent studies suggest that NCOA4, through its core function as a transcriptional coactivator, may regulate the expression or activity of PKM2 either directly or indirectly, the precise mechanisms by which NCOA4-mediated facilitation of PKM2 function sustains the glycolytic phenotype, drives tumor bioenergetics, promotes biomass accumulation, and ultimately contributes to the progression and aggressiveness of TETs have not been elucidated.

In our study, we demonstrate that m6A modification and PRRC2A expression are both increased in thymic epithelial tumors, and that elevated PRRC2A expression is associated with more advanced clinicopathological features in TETs. Functional and mechanical studies revealed PRRC2A as a key m6A reader that stabilizes NCOA4 mRNA, which subsequently interacts with PKM2 to drive glycolytic reprogramming. Knockdown of PRRC2A or pharmacological inhibition of PKM2 markedly reduces TET growth and metastasis. These findings provide new insight into the metabolic regulation of TET and suggest that the PRRC2A-NCOA4-PKM2 axis could be a potential therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Human specimens, mice, and study approval

This study comprised 12 TET tissues with their corresponding adjacent normal tissues, together with 58 additional thymic epithelial tumor specimens collected from Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. The clinical characteristics of the 54 patients included in the clinicopathological analysis are summarized in Table S1. All participants signed written informed consent prior to sample collection. Male BALB/c nude mice aged 4–6 weeks were obtained from the Animal Center of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. Ethical approval for this research was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (No. K24-567).

Cell culture

The Ty-82 cell line, originally established from a tumor diagnosed as thymic carcinoma at the time of derivation, was kindly provided by Prof. Ze-Hong Miao from the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Subsequent molecular characterization showed that Ty-82 harbors a BRD4-NUT fusion and is thus consistent with NUT carcinoma of the thorax, a rare molecularly defined subtype within thymic epithelial tumors. The IU-TAB-1 cell line, derived from type AB thymoma, was sourced from Applied Biological Materials Inc. (Abm, Canada). The ThyL-6 cell line, derived from an undifferentiated human thymic carcinoma, was obtained from the International Patent Organism Depositary (IPOD, NITE, Japan), with assistance from the Research Promotion Division of the University of Fukui. HEK 293T cells (human embryonic kidney cells expressing the SV40 large T antigen; obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) were used exclusively for lentiviral packaging. Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Ty-82 and ThyL-6 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Procell, China), IU-TAB-1 cells and HEK 293T were grown in DMEM (Procell), both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Hyclone, Cytiva, USA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere and periodically tested for mycoplasma.

Transfection and construction of stable overexpression and knockdown TET cell lines

Transfection procedures were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Cells were collected 48 h post-transfection for downstream analyses. PRRC2A knockdown and overexpression lentiviruses were purchased from OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). First, TET cells were seeded in a six-well plate. Cells were seeded in six-well plates and transduced upon reaching 70% confluence. After 24 h of infection, the media were replaced with fresh medium containing 10 µg/mL puromycin to select stably transduced cells. Complete medium was added with 10 µg/mL puromycin for continuous delivery. The shRNA sequences are listed in Table S2.

Western blot

Tissues or cultured cells in 10 cm dishes were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay (Beyotime, China), and equal amounts were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck, Germany). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% skim milk in TBST. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was achieved using an ECL kit (Enlighten biotech, China). Primary antibodies were listed in Table S3.

Real-time reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from tissues or TET cell lines was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Scientific, USA), and concentrations were measured using a Nanodrop 1000. cDNA synthesis was performed using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was carried out on an ABI PRISM 7300HT Sequence Detection System using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR Kit (Takara, Japan), and gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S4.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissues were sliced into thin sections and deparaffinized in dimethylbenzene and alcohol at different concentrations. The sections were then repaired with sodium citrate, blocked with goat serum, and incubated with Primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following day, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio, China) and incubated with DAB solution (Servicebio, China). Hematoxylin solution (Servicebio, China) was used to stain the nuclei. Primary antibodies were listed in Table S3. Immunohistochemical staining was independently assessed by two experienced pathologists with expertise in thymic tumor histopathology, who were blinded to the clinical information. Discrepancies in cell identification or scoring were resolved by consensus review. Representative images were selected from areas with clearly discernible epithelial tumor components to minimize potential interference from lymphocyte-rich regions.

Protein expression was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system based on staining intensity and the proportion of positively stained tumor cells. Staining intensity was graded as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). The proportion of positive tumor cells was categorized as 0 (0–5%), 1 (6–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), or 4 (76–100%). A composite score ranging from 0 to 12 was calculated for each case by multiplying the intensity score by the percentage category score. Patients were stratified into low and high-expression groups using the median composite score within the cohort as the cutoff. Cases with scores equal to the median value were assigned to the low-expression group. This cutoff was applied uniformly across all subsequent correlation and survival analyses.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

TET cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well for cultivation. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with a medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) at various time points (12, 24, 48, and 72 h), and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance of each sample was then quantified at 450 nm.

Colony formation

2 × 103 TET cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and cultured for 14 d. The colonies were fixed with 4% neutral paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min at room temperature. After washing to remove floating color, the colonies were counted using ImageJ software.

Cell migration and invasion assay

For the cell migration assay, 1 × 105 TET cells were suspended in 200 µL of FBS-free medium and placed in the upper chamber of the transwell filter. The lower chamber of the filter was filled with 600 µL of 10% FBS medium. After 48 h of culture, cells migrating through the filter were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with a crystal violet solution (Solarbio, China). For the cell invasion assay, the front side of the filter was pre-coated with Matrigel matrix. Subsequently, 1 × 105 cells were placed in the upper chamber, and cells invading the filter were fixed and stained. The average number of cells in five fields was calculated using ImageJ software. The results are representative of three independent experiments.

Xenograft model

For in vivo tumor growth, four-week-old BALB/c nude mice were used. TET cells (1 × 107 cells in 100 µL PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank. Tumors were collected 2 months post-injection, and the tumor length, width, and weight were measured. The tumor volume (mm3) was calculated as follows: (longest diameter) × (shortest diameter)2 × (π/6).

Lung metastasis model

In the lung metastasis model, mice were injected intravenously with 150 µL of cell suspension containing 1 × 106 cells via the tail vein. After 2 months, lungs were harvested, and metastatic nodules were counted.

N6-methyladenosine-modified RIP

MeRIP-seq was executed in the following manner: Initially, we isolated total RNA utilizing the Trizol reagent (Takara, Japan), which was followed by an mRNA purification process implemented through the GenEluteTM mRNA Miniprep Kit (Sigma, Louis, MO). Following this, we deployed RNA fragmentation reagents (NEB, UK) to fragment the RNA in a random manner. During the immunoprecipitation procedure, a specific anti-m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202003) was previously combined with Protein G magnetic beads in a reaction buffer. The fragmented mRNA was then exposed to a 4 °C atmosphere for an hour-long incubation with the mixture. After a washing process using low salt and high salt reaction buffers, Buffer RLT (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract the m6A-antibody-bound RNA from the Dynabeads. A further incubation was conducted with Dynabeads MyOne Silane (Life Technologies, West Palm Beach, FL). The precipitation was completed with 100% ethanol, a 70% ethanol wash, and a resuspension of the RNA and Dynabeads mixture in nuclease-free water. The supernatant, containing the m6A-bound RNA, was carefully collected following bead separation with a magnetic field.

RNA-seq

RNA-sequencing experiments were performed following standard protocols. Total RNA was extracted, quality-checked, and libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, USA). Paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sequencing was conducted on a high-throughput platform, and raw reads were aligned to the reference genome. Differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For immunofluorescence, TET cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates. The next day, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked in 5% goat serum. After washing, primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4, followed by fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were obtained using an Olympus inverted fluorescent microscope. Primary antibodies were listed in Table S3.

RIP-qPCR and RIP-seq

For RIP-qPCR and RIP-seq, 2 × 107 TET cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed in RIP buffer with RNase inhibitors. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation, and a portion was saved as input. The remainder was incubated with Protein A Dynabeads conjugated to anti-PRRC2A or IgG control antibodies. After incubation and washing, RNA-protein complexes were eluted, treated with proteinase K, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol. Purified RNAs were analyzed via qPCR or sequenced.

RNA pull-down assay

RNA pull-down assays were conducted using the Pierce Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit (Thermo Fisher). Biotin-labeled NCOA4 RNA probes were incubated with cell lysates and streptavidin magnetic beads. After washing and elution, proteins were analyzed by Western blot using anti-PRRC2A antibody.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Dual-luciferase reporter assays followed Promega’s Dual-Glo protocol. A 2-kb PRRC2A promoter fragment containing or lacking ATF1-binding sites was cloned into pGL3-basic vectors and co-transfected with phRL-TK into Ty-82 cells. Mutations at ATF1-binding regions were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis. After 36 h, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured. Ratios of firefly to Renilla signals were normalized to control values. To assess PRRC2A regulation of NCOA4 via the 3′UTR, wild-type or mutant NCOA4-UTR plasmids were co-transfected with PRRC2A siRNA or overexpression plasmids. Luciferase activity was measured after 36 h and analyzed as above. Normalization was performed using Renilla luciferase.

mRNA stability assay

For mRNA stability, actinomycin D (5 µg/mL) was added to transfected TET cells at time points of 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. Total RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and subjected to RT-qPCR to quantify target mRNA degradation.

m6A dot blot

The GenEluteTM mRNA Miniprep Kit (Sigma, MO) was utilized on pre-extracted total RNA to isolate polyadenylated mRNA. Following this, the poly(A) + RNA samples were positioned onto a Hybond-N + membrane (GE Healthcare, UK) and subsequently crosslinked with UV. The membrane was then subjected to blocking with 5% skim milk for one hour, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202003). The membrane then underwent probing with anti-mouse IgG, and signal visualization was accomplished with the ECL detection system. For comparison, an equal volume of poly(A)+ RNA samples was applied to the membrane and stained with 0.02% methylene blue (MB) in 0.3 M sodium acetate (pH = 5.2). The ImageJ software was leveraged to quantify the relative signal intensity of each dot. The results pertaining to m6A levels were demonstrated as the relative m6A dot blot intensity standardized to the methylene blue staining density.

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement

ECAR values were measured using a Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, USA) to quantify glycolysis in TET cells. Before the experiment, the culture medium was replaced with fresh KRPH medium. Glucose, oligomycin, and 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) were then added to ports A-C, respectively, and the program was started to measure. Each procedure consisted of mixing, spacing, and measurement, and the result of each measurement cycle was the ECAR value at a specific point.

Measurements of glycolysis-related indices

For the measurement of fructose-1,6-diphosphate (FDP) content, LDH activity, lactate level, and acetyl-CoA content, cells were lysed using ultrasound and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, supplemented with working solutions, and centrifuged repeatedly. The supernatant was collected, and the absorption value was measured using a spectrophotometer. For the measurement of glucose uptake, the culture medium was collected before and after the cells were cultured for 24 h to assess the level of glucose uptake. FDP content, 540 nm (Solarbio, China); LDH activity, 450 nm (Solarbio, China); glucose uptake, 505 nm (Solarbio, China); lactate level, 570 nm (Solarbio, China); and acetyl-CoA content, 340 nm (Abbkine, China).

EDU

Cell proliferation was detected using a 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) kit (Ribobio, China). EdU solution (10 µM) was added into cells in the logarithmic phase of growth and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After 2 h, cells were fixed with 4% neutral paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. Next, Apollo 643 was added to the cells and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min in the dark. After repeated washes, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. The EdU-positive cells fluoresced red, and the proportion of EdU-positive cells among all cells was analyzed using ImageJ. The results are representative of three independent experiments.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

A PierceTM co-IP kit (Thermo, USA) was used to identify the interaction between NCOA4 and PKM2. Briefly, primary antibodies and magnetic beads were incubated at 25 °C for 15 min with constant rotation for conjugation. Cells were lysed with RIPA solution and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min to acquire the proteins. The total protein solution was incubated with conjugated antibodies at 25 °C for 1 h. After elution, the supernatant containing the target antigen was collected and analyzed using Western blot and mass spectrometry.

Mass spectrometry

Proteins that directly interacted with NCOA4 were obtained using the co-IP assay. Proteins were isolated using SDS-PAGE. Follow-up treatment was performed when protein bands were visible. The gel was then decolorized using a decolorizing solution, reduced with dithiothreitol, and alkylated with iodoacetamide. The protein was digested into polypeptides with trypsin at 37 °C overnight, then the peptides were extracted and desalted. Peptides were identified using an EASY-nLC 1000 (Thermo, USA) coupled with Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo, USA). Peptide information was matched against the SwissProt database using Proteome Discovery 2.4 software (Thermo, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate unless otherwise noted. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U-test for nonparametric data. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for normally distributed data or the Kruskal–Wallis test for nonparametric data. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (P < 0.05; P < 0.01).

Results

Single-cell analysis identifies PRRC2A as a key regulator in TET and links its overexpression to poor prognosis

To systematically investigate the epithelial compartment and intratumoral heterogeneity of TETs, we integrated and analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets comprising tumor samples from 6 TET patients and 1 normal thymus sample (GSE147520, HRA002334). These analyses were further validated using our own patient samples.

We first performed global clustering of all cells from the integrated datasets. UMAP analysis revealed ten major cell populations, including B cells, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, monocytes, plasma cells, T cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) (Fig. 1A). To accurately define each population, we visualized the expression of canonical marker genes across these clusters using a dot plot, enabling precise cell type annotation (Fig. 1B). A stacked bar plot further highlighted the differences in immune microenvironment composition between normal thymus and TET samples, revealing a marked increase in malignant epithelial cells and a shift in immune cell proportions in tumors (Fig. 1C). From these datasets, epithelial cells were then extracted and re-clustered, revealing marked heterogeneity within the epithelial compartment (Fig. 1D). To further delineate genomic features, we performed copy number variation (CNV) analysis using the InferCNV algorithm. This revealed extensive chromosomal instability among tumor-derived epithelial cells compared with normal epithelial cells, a hallmark of malignant transformation (Fig. 1F). According to CNV scores, tumor-derived epithelial cells were subdivided into high CNV (H-CNV) and low CNV (L-CNV) groups, providing a refined classification of malignant potential (Fig. 1G). By integrating tissue origin and CNV status, we defined a population of malignant epithelial cells, which were subsequently re-clustered to enable downstream transcriptional and functional analyses (Fig. 1H). To identify potential key regulators driving malignant transformation, we combined differential expression analysis with gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Among the top-ranked hub genes, PRRC2A consistently exhibited strong upregulation and the highest network centrality, indicating a potential pivotal role in coordinating tumor-associated transcriptional programs (Fig. 1I). Given that PRRC2A encodes an m6A reader protein previously implicated in cancer progression, we focused subsequent analyses on elucidating its function in TETs. Spatial mapping of PRRC2A expression further revealed distinct PRRC2A⁺ and PRRC2A⁻ subpopulations within the malignant epithelial compartment (Fig. 1J). Enrichment analysis of PRRC2A⁺ cells showed significant activation of key oncogenic pathways, including m6A RNA modification regulation, hypoxia response, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and glycolysis programs, indicating that PRRC2A⁺ cells represent a transcriptionally distinct and potentially more aggressive subset of malignant epithelial cells in TETs (Fig. 1K).

To validate the expression pattern of PRRC2A in TET, we analyzed 12 paired tumor and adjacent normal thymic tissues, as well as 42 additional thymic epithelial tumor samples collected from Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. Western blot analysis demonstrated significantly elevated PRRC2A protein levels in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1L and Fig. S1A). Consistently, immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed markedly higher PRRC2A staining scores in tumor samples, confirming its overexpression in TET (Fig. S1B). We then assessed the clinical significance of PRRC2A expression. High PRRC2A levels were significantly associated with advanced TNM stage (Fig. 1M).

Collectively, these findings indicate that PRRC2A is a critical gene enriched in high-risk tumor epithelial subpopulations and is strongly associated with aggressive clinicopathological characteristics in TETs, suggesting its potential role as both a biomarker and a driver of tumor progression.

PRRC2A enhances the proliferative and metastatic capacity of TET cells in vitro and in vivo

To further explore the potential biological roles of PRRC2A in TET. Stable PRRC2A-knockdown and PRRC2A-overexpressing Ty-82, IU-TAB-1, and ThyL-6 cell lines were established using lentiviral transduction, respectively (Fig. S1C–E). Firstly, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that PRRC2A knockdown significantly reduced TET cells proliferation compared with controls, whereas PRRC2A overexpression markedly enhanced TET cells proliferation (Fig. S1F–H). This finding was further confirmed by colony formation assays, which revealed markedly fewer colonies in PRRC2A-knockdown TET cells and more colonies in PRRC2A-overexpressing TET cells (Fig. 2A, B; Fig. S1I–K). Next, to further investigate the role of PRRC2A in the metastasis of TET cells, transwell assays were performed. Knockdown of PRRC2A significantly reduced the migration and invasive capabilities of TET cells, whereas its overexpression markedly enhanced these phenotypes (Fig. 2C–F and Fig. S1L–O). To investigate the in vivo role of PRRC2A in TET cells, we utilized a subcutaneous xenograft model in Balb/c mice to assess the impact of PRRC2A on the proliferative capacity of Ty-82 cells. The results demonstrated that, compared to the control group, knockdown of PRRC2A in TET cells led to significantly reduced tumor volume and tumor weights in subcutaneous xenografts (Fig. 2G–I). In contrast, overexpression of PRRC2A in Ty-82 cells resulted in a marked increase in both tumor volume and tumor weight compared with the control group (Fig. 2J, K). Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissue for Ki-67 revealed that the Ki-67 positivity rate in subcutaneous tumors derived from PRRC2A-knockdown Ty-82 cells was significantly decreased compared to the control group. Conversely, tumors from PRRC2A-overexpressing Ty-82 cells showed a significant increase in Ki-67 positivity compared with the control group (Fig. 2L). Additionally, a tail vein injection lung metastasis model was employed to evaluate the effect of PRRC2A on the metastatic potential of Ty-82 cells. The results indicated that, relative to the control group, knockdown of PRRC2A in Ty-82 cells significantly reduced the number of metastatic nodules and the Ki-67 positivity rate within metastatic lesions (Fig. 2M–O). In contrast, overexpression of PRRC2A in Ty-82 cells led to a significant increase in the number of metastatic nodules and the Ki-67 positivity rate compared with the control group (Fig. 2P–R).

Together, these results indicate that PRRC2A functions as an oncogenic regulator that drives TET cells' proliferation and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo.

Given the established role of PRRC2A as an m6A reader [19, 20], m6A RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) was performed to profile transcriptome-wide m6A modifications in both the PRRC2A-KD and control groups (Fig. 3D), Consistent with previous reports [31]. Motif analysis further revealed a conserved “GGAC” sequence within these enriched regions (Fig. 3F).

To identify transcripts directly bound by PRRC2A, we integrated RNA-seq, MeRIP-seq, and RIP-seq datasets. Considering that PRRC2A has been previously reported to play a critical role in regulating mRNA stability, we first intersected the differentially expressed genes identified by MeRIP-seq and RNA-seq to screen for transcripts whose expression changes were associated with altered m6A modifications. Next, we overlapped these candidates with the targets identified by anti-PRRC2A RIP-seq. Through this integrative approach, we defined a set of downstream mRNAs whose expression was potentially regulated by PRRC2A in an m6A-dependent manner. This analysis revealed 456 genes that were differentially expressed upon PRRC2A knockdown compared with the control group. Further integration with the RIP-seq dataset highlighted GABRD, NCOA4, RER1, and NIPAL3 as the most significantly enriched PRRC2A-bound targets (Fig. 3G).

Discussion

Although the incidence of TETs has remained stable or increased in recent years, mortality rates have declined, likely as a result of improvements in surgical techniques and pharmacological treatments [34]. Recent clinical trials have demonstrated that the tyrosine kinase inhibitors sunitinib and lenvatinib possess potential efficacy in patients with chemotherapy-refractory TET. However, overall response rates to these agents remain suboptimal [35, 36]. With regard to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), pembrolizumab and nivolumab have been utilized as non-first-line therapies for TETs [37, 38]. Nonetheless, only a small subset of patients achieves partial responses, and the frequency of immune-related adverse events remains relatively high. These limitations underscore the urgent need to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying TET pathogenesis to facilitate the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. In our study, we identified a novel mechanism in which PRRC2A upregulates NCOA4 expression in an m6A-dependent manner, thereby establishing a unique NCOA4-PKM2 regulatory axis that drives glycolytic reprogramming in TETs. This discovery provides a new perspective for metabolic targeting in TETs and holds significant translational potential.

Recent studies have increasingly implicated dysregulated RNA modifications, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m6A), in the pathogenesis of TETs [11, 13]. Aberrant expression of m6A methyltransferases (such as METTL3) or demethylases (such as FTO and ALKBH5) can alter the modification status of transcripts encoding oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, thus promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [39–41]. However, compared with writers and erasers, the functional roles of m6A “reader” proteins in TETs remain poorly characterized.

PRRC2A is a recently identified m6A reader protein that was initially described as a regulator of oligodendrocyte specification and myelination [20]. In cancer biology, PRRC2A has been shown to promote colorectal cancer progression via activation of the CK1ε-mediated WNT and YAP signaling pathways [21]. Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating that PRRC2A is also upregulated in TETs and is significantly associated with adverse clinicopathological features and unfavorable prognosis. These results suggest that PRRC2A may function as a broadly relevant oncogenic m6A reader across multiple tumor types, rather than exerting tissue-restricted effects.

Mechanistically, prior studies have indicated that PRRC2A recognizes m6A-modified transcripts via a conserved GGACU motif, thereby enhancing translation efficiency or stabilizing target mRNAs such as Olig2 [19, 20]. Consistent with these reports, our integrated MeRIP-seq, bulk RNA-seq, and RIP-seq analyses identify NCOA4 as a direct downstream effector of PRRC2A in TETs. Functional assays further demonstrate that PRRC2A stabilizes NCOA4 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner, reinforcing the concept that PRRC2A primarily acts by regulating transcript stability within the epitranscriptomic network.

NCOA4 is well recognized for its role in mediating ferritinophagy and maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis [24]. More recently, studies have shown that NCOA4 can also regulate metabolic enzyme activity through non-ferritinophagy pathways in malignancies such as ovarian and liver cancer [26, 27]. In our study, immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, co-immunoprecipitation, and rescue experiments revealed for the first time a direct interaction between NCOA4 and PKM2, thus establishing an NCOA4-PKM2 regulatory axis and providing new insights into the metabolic functions of NCOA4 in cancer.

Tumor cells depend on aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) to generate biosynthetic precursors that support rapid proliferation [42]. In TETs, key glycolytic transcription factors or enzymes, including c-Myc and GLUT1, are overexpressed [43, 44]; however, the upstream regulatory mechanisms governing their expression have not been fully elucidated. Our study demonstrates that interaction between NCOA4 and PKM2 promotes glycolytic activity in TET cells, whereas PKM2 knockdown abolishes the glycolytic effects mediated by NCOA4. These findings indicate that PKM2 is a critical downstream mediator of NCOA4. PKM2 not only directly promotes lactate production but also translocates to the nucleus, where it facilitates STAT3 phosphorylation and c-Myc expression, thereby upregulating GLUT1, LDHA, and HK2 and further enhancing glycolysis, promoting the Warburg effect, and driving TET cell proliferation and metastasis.

From a therapeutic perspective, PKM2 has attracted increasing attention as a druggable metabolic target. The small-molecule PKM2 inhibitor C599 has previously demonstrated antitumor activity in aggressive glioblastoma models. Here, we extend these findings by showing that C599 effectively suppresses glycolysis, tumor growth, and metastasis in TET models in vivo. Importantly, combined PKM2 inhibition and PRRC2A knockdown produced synergistic antitumor effects, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting the PRRC2A-NCOA4-PKM2 axis.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. A substantial proportion of the mechanistic and in vivo experiments were performed using the Ty-82 cell line, which was originally derived from a thymic carcinoma but was subsequently reclassified as a thoracic NUT carcinoma based on the presence of a BRD4-NUT fusion, according to the 2021 WHO classification. Given that NUT carcinoma represents a rare, molecularly defined subtype within thymic epithelial malignancies, its biological behavior may not fully recapitulate that of conventional thymomas or thymic carcinomas. To mitigate this limitation, we validated the core regulatory features of the PRRC2A-NCOA4-PKM2 axis in additional TET cell lines, including IU-TAB-1 and ThyL-6, where consistent effects on glycolysis and tumor-associated phenotypes were observed. Nonetheless, further validation in larger patient cohorts and additional experimental systems will be required to establish the broader clinical relevance of these findings.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that PRRC2A enhances the stability of NCOA4 mRNA through recognition of m6A modifications, leading to the accumulation of NCOA4 protein. NCOA4 directly binds PKM2 and facilitates its nuclear translocation, thereby promoting STAT3 phosphorylation, increasing glycolytic flux, and enhancing the Warburg effect, ultimately facilitating TET proliferation and metastasis. Furthermore, the PKM2 inhibitor C599 significantly suppresses the progression of TET. Knockdown of PRRC2A further enhances the inhibitory effect of C599 on TET cells. This regulatory axis not only elucidates a novel mechanism underlying TET tumorigenesis and progression but also provides new potential targets for therapeutic intervention.
