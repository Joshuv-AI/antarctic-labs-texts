Personalized multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for adaptive chronic disease therapy management
Authors: Fuzail Ahmad, Rayed AlGhamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term, flexible therapy strategies are needed for chronic diseases like cardiovascular ailments, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease and they have to deal with patient heterogeneity, changing physiological states and privacy constraints. This research suggests a framework for privacy-preserving and personalized artificial intelligence that combines Federated Learning (FL), a Res-HyperTransformerNet deep prediction model, Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL) and Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) for adaptive chronic disease management. The framework is structured with two main aims: (i) to create an accurate and privacy-aware predictive model for chronic disease risk using diverse data sources, and (ii) to permit adaptive, individual therapy optimization through multi-agent reinforcement learning. Federated learning is utilized to conduct the training of Res-HyperTransformerNet over distributed Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) nodes without the requirement of transferring raw patient data. The embeddings that are created from patient data are then passed on to a PMARL module, where several agents optimize therapy dimensions like medications, diet, physical activity, and mental health interventions independently. To make the clinical support more understandable, the SHAP-based explainability method is used for both predictive and decision-making parts. The framework is tested using two public datasets—the CDC Chronic Disease dataset and the UCI Chronic Kidney Disease Risk Factor dataset. The performance is measured by employing classification metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, MCC) as well as reinforcement learning metrics (reward score, convergence steps, episode return) and federated system metrics (communication overhead, convergence rounds, and training time). The experiments indicate that the proposed framework demonstrated improved performance in terms of predictive accuracy and policy convergence speed compared to the baseline deep learning and reinforcement learning models, and at the same time it is more cost-effective when it comes to communication in a federated setting. This means that the proposed method suggests the potential of being a reproducible and open AI framework for adaptive chronic disease therapy management. The combination of ResNet and Transformer blocks, i.e., Federated Res-HyperTransformerNet, achieved strong performance in both datasets, namely CDC Chronic Disease (Dataset 1) and UCI CKD Risk Factor (Dataset 2). The obtained accuracy for Dataset 1 was 98.61% and for Dataset 2, it was 97.75%, under the conducted experimental settings.

Introduction

A significant threat to global health is the increase in the number of people with chronic illnesses like Alzheimer’s, type 2 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), high blood pressure, arthritis, respiratory issues, and cardiovascular diseases (CVD). The fact that modern health care institutions continue to treat these illnesses in antiquated and ineffective ways is a major issue1. Chronic disease management is frequently successful when done with ongoing monitoring, individualized treatment regimens, and preventive measures that not only reduce but significantly enhance the health outcomes of the patient. At the same time, traditional methods may be resource-consuming and may not necessarily give the level of detail and insight that can be very helpful in making the most of the care provided. AI - powered predictive analytics are very useful at this point2,3. Even with the required technical advancements, managing chronic diseases remains a challenge for the traditional healthcare delivery system, particularly in remote and impoverished areas. Patients’ medical records are frequently scattered, specialized care is scarce, and diagnosis and treatment take a lengthy time, all of which contribute to less-than-ideal results. Because of the shortcomings of traditional healthcare systems, technology-based solutions must be used to improve patient participation, monitoring, and adherence to treatment4,5. The use of ML and AI in the medical field has brought about significant changes in medical diagnostics and therapy. The technologies utilize large databases, such as imaging, genetic, and medical records, to find patterns that are often not visible to humans. AI and ML are capable of increasing diagnostic precision, cutting down interpretation time, and eventually enabling treatment approaches to be applied in areas like radiology, psychiatry, and chronic illness management. Healthcare providers are relying on these breakthroughs to deliver more accurate, quicker, and customized interventions in the case of both common and complex health issues6,7.

The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) has brought about a significant transformation in the healthcare sector, connecting the high-tech and clinical practice worlds while also easing issues like the strain on medical personnel and the growing demand for healthcare. Diabetes treatment is one example of modern technology that has begun to open the door to more advanced medical procedures 8. Even while this is particularly crucial given the disease’s complexity and the requirement for ongoing monitoring, new technology plays a major role in enabling the new automation, workflow structures, and personalized care. These include sophisticated glucose monitors and software programs that aid in monitoring and analyzing patient data. AI is leading in the area of diabetes treatment, that is, by personalizing the management plans, risk assessment, and diagnosis, even before patients are put on drugs, they already get the best possible treatment through the use of AI. AI is processing vast amounts of data to detect risks, correctly interpreting laboratory results, and customizing treatment plans for single patients, which results in the whole process of patient care being betterment plus the outcomes . One more way is by the usage of precision medicine, which means the individualized treatment decisions and practices based on the patient’s specific phenotype and genotype. To put it differently, conventional medicine may resort to a one-size-fits-all technique for treating patients, thereby probably not considering the individual differences in lifestyle, environment, and genetics which are imperative in discerning a patient’s vulnerability to a disease and his/her reaction to the treatment. At the same time, among the new and promising fields of research in medicine, we can find big data analytics, machine learning, deep neural networks, and AI in healthcare, which are potentially opening the doors to patient-specific treatment plans as well as predictive diagnostics10,11.

AI is used in the healthcare industry, which is an excellent example of its success. Among its many uses, the medical specialties of cardiology, oncology, and dermatology contribute the most to its advantages.

AI-based solutions can also speed up drug research and development, which will help address unmet medical requirements and introduce new drugs more quickly. Incorporating generative AI into the healthcare system is anticipated to lead to significant cost savings, increased operational productivity, and improved patient outcomes12,13. Better health outcomes will result from prompt patient monitoring and healthcare interventions. Because older monitoring systems cannot manage complex and dynamic circumstances where vital signs are changing, it frequently takes longer to notice patients in critical conditions. In this area, finding patterns and classifying patients’ activities as well as vital signs has traditionally relied largely on supervised and unsupervised learning tools14. Nevertheless, these techniques can only analyze the data and recommend possible judgments; they cannot, however, take action based on their observations. On the contrary, Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers a novel paradigm of placing learning agents in non-predictable and complex situations. These agents can take action, then observe the outcomes of their actions, thus, they can explore and exploit their environment through acting15,16.

Federated Learning (FL) is a new technology that allows the training of decentralized models without the need to share raw patient data, thus providing privacy-preserving healthcare analytics. At the same time, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been recognized as a possibility for the long-term decision-making process in healthcare, especially in the areas of treatment planning and intervention optimization. However, the current healthcare RL research is mainly focused on single-agent case studies, which do not capture the full complexity and interactivity of chronic disease management. Also, the joint FL, RL and explainability technique is still far from being mainstream and cross validations with various datasets remain insufficient.

This research proposes a Federated Learning–enabled Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (FL–PMARL) framework with integrated explainability. The main idea behind this study is to segregate the learning of population-level representation from patient-specific decision-making so that predictive accuracy, adaptability, and system efficiency can be enhanced and at the same time, data privacy can be preserved. The federated learning is specifically applied to train a hybrid ResNet–Transformer model (Res-HyperTransformerNet) for chronic disease risk prediction while the personalized multi-agent reinforcement learning module is being used for implementing therapy strategies optimization along the different intervention dimensions.

Novelty and comparative advantages of the proposed hybrid model

The new hybrid framework has come to the forefront with a number of great innovations that are beyond any doubt better than the conventional Federated Learning (FL) and Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) systems used for chronic disease management. The regular FL models are mainly based on the distributed training of the model but they do not have the ability to take into consideration long-term therapy optimization or cope with changes in patients’ physiological states. On the other hand, the MARL-based healthcare systems do the adaptive decision-making but they are using the data that have been aggregated centrally which brings the risk of privacy violation and also limits the generalizability of the results. However, the Federated Res-HyperTransformerNet + Personalized MARL (PMARL) architecture proposed is different and it perfectly combines: (i) a spatio-temporal hybrid deep model based on ResNet (the learning of spatial features) and Transformer blocks (the modeling of temporal patterns), which allows the rich multimodal representation learning to be done unlike the stand-alone CNN, LSTM, or Transformer-based FL systems; (ii) a personalized multi-agent FD-DQN framework that assigns independent agents to therapy domains (medication, lifestyle, diet) offering multi-dimensional and dynamically adaptive treatment planning beyond single-agent MARL approaches; and (iii) embedded SHAP-driven XAI, which offers layer-wise interpretability for both prediction and policy decisions—an aspect missing in most FL and MARL models. This combined process leads to a comprehensive system that can do privacy-preserving training, high-accuracy diagnostics, dynamic therapy personalization, and actionable interpretability, which is a major breakthrough compared to the current FL-only or MARL-only chronic disease management solutions.

Literature review

Chronic disease management’s growing complexity has led to the development of intelligent therapeutic systems, which are based on data and employ reinforcement learning, multi-modal analytics, and explainable AI. The research is currently done in a gradual manner whereby predictive models have been left behind and the frame of highly personalized and adaptive systems is being used. However, there is still a large gap to be filled in terms of personalization, privacy-preserving computation, and interpretability being merged into one system. Following the above, the next subsections will provide a summary of the evolution of the personalized chronic disease management tools and will also illustrate how reinforcement learning, multimodal AI, and predictive modeling have been a part of the process of therapy optimization enhancement.

AI and deep learning approaches for chronic disease prediction

The use of artificial intelligence and deep learning methods has been extensively studied to enhance the prediction, diagnosis and clinical decision-making of chronic diseases. A number of studies have suggested hybrid and explainable models to increase the accuracy of prediction and overcome the issue of transparency in healthcare systems.

In 2024, Wani et al.17. proposed a hybrid deep learning model, which can be interpreted, DeepXplainer, to identify lung cancer. This was a hybrid of convolutional neural networks to obtain features automatically and XGBoost to classify them. SHAP-based explainable artificial intelligence techniques were also used in the study to provide local and global explanations of predictions. The proposed model was discovered to be very diagnostic and increase trust and interpretability to aid clinical decision making.

In 2023, Murala et al.18. introduced a patient-centered chronic disease management architecture, which involves the use of artificial intelligence, blockchain, and wearable devices in a Metaverse-based environment. The framework enabled to track physiological parameters in real time through the assistance of wearable devices, and blockchain ensured safety in data sharing and maintenance of privacy. Patient data were interpreted using AI algorithms and provided with diagnostic and treatment advice that could be explained.

In 2024, Chen et al.19. developed a hybrid model of diabetes diagnosis using deep neural networks and random forest models to improve the explainability of the diagnosis. This paper used random forest-based rule extraction to describe diagnostic predictions and suggested a modified random forest incremental interpretation mechanism to reduce patient confusion by aggregating decision rules. To improve the performance of prediction and interpretability, a certainty feedback mechanism was also added.

In 2023, Dhaka et al.20. presented a hybrid deep learning network that combines deep bidirectional long short-term memory and convolutional neural networks. The model used swarm optimization methods to optimize fusion parameters and enhance prediction accuracy. Furthermore, the encryption methods based on the elliptic curve cryptography were used to provide secure data transmission.

In 2023, Sapra et al.21. suggested a two-step diagnostic model of coronary artery disease based on deep neural networks and case-based reasoning. Disease prediction was done using the deep learning model and case-based reasoning was applied to assess disease severity. To minimize overfitting and enhance the performance of the model generalization, Gaussian noise was added.

In 2023, Ghasemieh et al.22. proposed a stacking ensemble learning model to improve emergency readmission prediction among heart disease patients. The model employed several base classifiers with XGBoost as a meta-learner. The behavioral features were added to develop a new class label of emergency readmission prediction. The ensemble method enhanced prediction strength and clinical usability over the conventional machine learning models.

In 2025, Roy et al.23. proposed a multimodal diagnostic system called TriSpectraKAN to detect chronic obstructive pulmonary disease based on lung sound analysis. The model combined several audio spectral characteristics such as MFCCs, chromagram, and Mel spectrograms with a hybrid KolmogorovArnold Network. The framework had high diagnostic accuracy and could be implemented in real-time with embedded hardware platforms.

In 2022, Rashid et al.24. suggested an augmented artificial neural network model and optimized it with particle swarm optimization to forecast different chronic diseases. In this model, the PSO-based feature extraction was used to enhance the classification accuracy of various disease datasets. The proposed solution performed better than the traditional machine learning and deep learning classifiers and reduced the computational time.

Reinforcement learning for personalized therapy optimization

The reinforcement learning and intelligent decision-making systems have been studied more to maximize sequential therapy planning and individualized treatment plans to manage chronic diseases. Such methods allow dynamic treatment adjustment depending on patient response patterns, longitudinal health data, and changing clinical conditions.

In 2024, Yang et al.25. suggested an herbal prescription planning system, PrescDRL, which is based on deep reinforcement learning, to treat chronic diseases in traditional Chinese medicine. The model was developed to produce optimal sequential herbal prescriptions by focusing on long-term therapeutic efficacy as opposed to maximizing immediate rewards. The framework used a benchmark dataset on diabetes diagnosis and treatment, which showed better curative results and higher prediction accuracy of prescriptions than clinical expert advice.

In 2022, Zeng et al.26. created a decision support system that was based on reinforcement learning to optimize warfarin anticoagulation therapy after surgical valve replacement. The model kept on suggesting changes in the daily dosage of patients according to their changing physiological conditions and clinical parameters. Reinforcement learning model was found to be much more effective in enhancing the results of treatment response, such as the ability to maintain target international normalized ratio (INR) levels and less risk of safety issues than clinician-directed therapy and traditional machine learning.

In 2025, Gayathri et al.27. suggested to implement a customized treatment model that combines multi-modal data fusion and patient-in-the-loop reinforcement learning to increase the accuracy of the use of precision medicine. The model used an adaptive temporal fusion mechanism to combine genomic data, electronic health records, and wearable sensor data. The hierarchical deep neural network architecture was proposed to learn patient complex representations, and a dynamic biomarker importance module was proposed to change the priorities of clinical features in response to the changing conditions of patients. Patient adherence feedback was introduced into therapy optimization through reinforcement learning, enhancing predictive consistency and therapeutic accuracy.

In 2025, AbdelAziz et al.28. proposed a disease prediction model, which is built on the deep Q-learning, in which the classical Q-learning algorithms were improved with the help of the neural network-based function approximation. The suggested deep Q-learning model enhanced the accuracy of disease prediction by learning nonlinear associations between electronic health record data and overcoming the shortcomings of small datasets and missing data about patients. The model showed better results than traditional machine learning methods.

In 2024, Nramban et al.29. presented a reinforcement learning based on artificial neural network structure to tackle the problem of early detection and management of chronic kidney disease. The model was based on reinforcement learning to process heterogeneous electronic health record data and determine patterns of temporal disease progression. The hybrid RL-ANN model tested various neural network models, such as feedforward, recurrent, and convolutional neural networks, to improve the reliability of disease prediction. The method showed better diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity and minimized errors in predicting kidney failure.

Altogether, these articles demonstrated the promise of reinforcement learning-based models to optimize individual therapy planning, adaptive treatment modification, and longitudinal disease management. Nevertheless, the majority of the available methods were based on single-agent decision models and small data modalities, which underscores the necessity of more sophisticated multi-agent reinforcement learning models that can coordinate complex therapeutic decision-making in a wide range of clinical settings.

Privacy-preserving and explainable a.i. frameworks in healthcare

The privacy preservation and explainability has become an essential need in healthcare systems that are driven by artificial intelligence, especially in chronic disease monitoring and clinical decision support. A number of research studies have suggested decentralized learning systems and safe data-sharing systems to solve the problem of data confidentiality, trust, and transparency in healthcare settings.

In 2023, Patil et al.30. suggested an adaptive federated learning model called AFL-CDP to predict chronic diseases in real-time medical Internet of Things settings. The framework employed decentralized learning to facilitate the joint training of models on distributed edge devices without the transmission of raw patient data. The study combined secure aggregation and encryption with SPECK to improve privacy preservation in IoT devices with resource constraints. The suggested framework showed a better predictive accuracy and individual chronic disease tracking and data confidentiality across heterogeneous healthcare sources.

In 2025, Puri et al.31. suggested a decentralized collaborative chronic kidney disease detection system that incorporated blockchain technology and explainable artificial intelligence techniques. The framework enabled multiple healthcare providers to collaboratively train predictive models without breaching patient data privacy. Explainable AI methods were also introduced to make the model more transparent and enhance the confidence in clinical decision-making. The proposed system was experimented on a simulated real-time healthcare environment and was found to be efficient in privacy-sensitive collaborative disease detection.

In 2023, Patel et al.32. created LEAF, a federated learning model that is restricted access and based on encryption to solve the privacy concerns of federated learning. The model incorporated encryption algorithms and edge-based artificial intelligence models to reduce the risk of data exposure and reduce the level of computation. The proposed LEAF model enhanced predictive accuracy and ensured safe information exchange across distributed healthcare networks. The framework also provided effective data compression that improved the effectiveness and scalability of the system in terms of communication.

In 2024, Myrzashova et al.33. suggested a federated learning system based on blockchain to detect multiple diseases with the help of chest radiographs. The framework allowed several healthcare institutions to jointly train encrypted federated models without data leakage. A large-scale chest X-ray dataset was used to evaluate the system and it was found to have high diagnostic accuracy, low latency, and high throughput. Moreover, the blockchain infrastructure increased the resilience of the system to cyberattacks and guaranteed safe multi-institutional data cooperation.

In 2022, Salim and Park34 proposed to have a federated learning-based electronic health record sharing scheme to facilitate secure biomedical data exchange to be used in clinical research. The architecture made use of decentralized convolutional neural networks to learn models on-site at medical facilities and save medical information in a secure private InterPlanetary File System. Smart contracts were introduced as blockchain-based to facilitate controlled data sharing and incentivized patient participation. The suggested system demonstrated the same level of performance as centralized learning models with much better data privacy and sharing efficiency.

All in all, the studies have shown that federated learning, blockchain technology, and explainable artificial intelligence can be effective in resolving privacy and trust issues in healthcare systems. Nevertheless, the current solutions were mostly aimed at the secure data sharing and collaborative model training, and few studies concentrated on the combination of privacy-saving mechanisms and adaptive multi-agent therapeutic decision-making, thus, more sophisticated intelligent healthcare management models are required.

Theoretical grounding: positioning within synchronous and asynchronous learning paradigms

The learning paradigms that are used in distributed intelligent systems can be categorized as synchronous or asynchronous depending on whether the model updates and policy learning are done in a coordinated manner during global cycles or independently over time. The framework that has been proposed adopts a hybrid synchronous–asynchronous learning architecture, which is aimed at overcoming the practical constraints of chronic disease management while still having theoretical stability and clinical coherence.

Limitations of synchronous learning

In synchronous learning, the global temporal coordination of all the entities involved is assumed. Although it is the most used method in federated learning (FL) and reinforcement learning (RL) because of the convergence guarantees, the method has very serious drawbacks in the healthcare environments:

Susceptibility to straggler effects from heterogeneous IoMT devices,Inflexibility to irregular and sparse patient data generation,Delayed adaptability due to fixed synchronization cycles.

Such restrictions are very troublesome in the context of chronic disease management, where the patients’ conditions are always changing and in different ways (Arefin, 2024; Khalifa & Albadawy, 2024; Husnain et al., 2024).

Advantages and challenges of asynchronous learning

The key benefit of asynchronous learning is that it releases the global synchronization constraint, which allows models or agents to be updated separately as soon as new data come in. Previous research has shown that asynchronous FL and RL really enhance scalability, latency tolerance, and robustness to non-IID data (Shaik et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2024). But on the other hand, totally asynchronous methods bring in problems like stale gradients, policy instability, and loss of global clinical coherence, particularly when prediction and decision-making are done separately.

Proposed hybrid asynchronous–synchronous framework

The suggested method takes the current proposal further through the express tactic of decoupling learning across system layers that results in a theoretical basis for the hybrid design:

Partially Asynchronous Federated Learning: The local Res-HyperTransformerNet models are being trained at the client nodes asynchronously depending on the data that is available. Proposed methodology

The innovative hybrid architecture is proposed that integrates Federated Learning (FL), a Res-HyperTransformerNet deep prediction model, Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL), and SHAP Explainable AI (XAI) for the adaptive and secure management of chronic diseases. The whole system works over a privacy-preserving network of Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) devices, with local patient-specific training, shared knowledge aggregation, adaptive therapy planning, and interpretable decision support. The innovation of the solution is in the integration of cutting-edge deep learning, distributed privacy-preserving model training, reinforcement-based policy optimization that adapts to dynamics, and explainability, optimized for chronic disease applications.

Workflow of the proposed model

Initially, the system gets multimodal health data from IoMT sensors, electronic medical records (EMRs), and patient behavioral logs in Step 1. The data comes from different sources - physiological signals like glucose and blood pressure, lifestyle indicators such as diet and sleep, and clinical history. The inputs, before going through the preprocessing pipeline, are noisy, some values are missing, and the sampling intervals are not regular. Thus, the first step of the preprocessing pipeline is K-Nearest Neighbors imputation with k = 5 to deal with missing data. At this point, all features get normalized through Min–Max scaling which converts all numerical values to a given range between 0 and 1, thus making training stable. Then, time-related variables are cut into rolling windows in order to extract short- and long-term patterns at the same time. For each window, engineered features are calculated e.g. glucose variance, blood pressure trend slopes, heart-rate variability indices, and behavioral consistency metrics, thus both instantaneous values and dynamic health patterns are being captured.

During the second stage of the process, the preprocessed data continues to be stored separately for the sake of privacy and this is done by the federated architecture. Every hospital or IoMT node participating in the study creates a local model known as Res-HyperTransformerNet, which is a lightweight hybrid of Residual Convolutional Blocks and Transformer Blocks. The residual module applies a bottleneck design that consists of a 1 × 11 convolution for dimensionality reduction, a depthwise convolution for effective spatial filtering, and another 1 × 11 convolution for expansion, all combined with skip connections to avoid gradient degradation. The transformer module works on the temporally encoded sequences using Multi-Head Self-Attention with h = 8 heads, each with a head dimension of k = 64, which allows the model to capture dependencies across features and over time. A feed-forward MLP layer with hidden dimension l = 256 refines the outputs of the transformer. Every client carries out local training on its own dataset and then, at intervals, sends just the gradient updates to the centralized aggregator. The Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm constructs the global model by taking the weighted average of local gradients, thus facilitating collaboration while not directly sharing patient data.

After the global Res-HyperTransformerNet model is completed in Step 3, its latent representations (embeddings) are the state vectors for the Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL) system that are structured. A total of four lightweight agents that are responsible for the management of medication, dietary planning, physical activities, and mental health support function at the same time. Every agent takes up a Deep Q-Network (DQN) that works on the states and finds optimal actions for them. Very recently taken glucose readings, blood pressure, BMI, medication adherence, sleep quality, daily step counts, and some other behavioral indicators constitute the state vector. The FD-DQN decides on actions, such as reducing carbohydrate intake, suggesting a workout plan, changing medication time, or proposing relaxation techniques. The reward function is a mix of short-term effects (e.g., glucose stabilization), long-term health benefits (HbA1c reduction, BP normalization), adherence levels, and general quality-of-life indicators. Agents change their Q-values in the same way as the reinforcement learning rule states.

The multi-agent structuring permits the provision of specialized and yet synchronized therapy decisions, which further leads to the provision of personalized and adaptable nursing care.

After the training of predictive and prescriptive components in Step 4, the integration of explainability is done to provide clinical trust and safety. For both the predictive outputs from Res-HyperTransformerNet and the decision-making outputs of PMARL layer, SHAP values are calculated. These SHAP evaluations estimate the importance of each feature in a prediction or recommendation. Explanations can be seen by the doctors in the form of force plots for individual cases, dependence plots to indicate nonlinear feature interactions, and decision heatmaps that show how physiological and behavioral factors influence reinforcement learning decisions. This enables the clinicians to monitor the system along the medical guidelines and patient-specific contexts by guaranteeing transparency in the diagnosis and recommendation steps, respectively.

At the Final Step stage, the entire system is operating as if it were a digital health pipeline with no end. The patients’ data undergo secure preprocessing, and federated learning; the PMARL decision engine becomes more precise with the updated global embeddings and the explainability ensures that every prediction and recommendation is still comprehensible; and the clinician feedbacks provide for continuous improvement. This entire process results in a virtual coaching system that is fully personalized, privacy-preserving, clinically accountable, AI-driven, and constantly supporting the patient in chronic disease management.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing is a critical component of this work, especially given the heterogeneity and incompleteness of real-world health data of Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) devices, electronic medical records (EMRs), and behavioral logs. The novelty of this preprocessing step is that it considers two things at the same time: enhancing the integrity of the data to train the model reliably and maintaining privacy in distributed healthcare settings. In order to deal with the missing values, median imputation is used, which substitutes the missing values of the features with the median of the existing ones. This method is resistant to outliers and skewed distributions, which guarantees the consistency and unbiased representation of data.

Median Imputation

The issue of missing data is a typical problem in real-world healthcare data, particularly in user-reported or sensor-based data as not all attributes are reliably captured in every instance. As an example, patients cannot give demographic or behavioral information, or IoMT devices can generate incomplete data because of connectivity or hardware restrictions. Deleting records that are not complete can lead to a major loss of information and sampling bias. Thus, the missing data should be addressed with the help of imputation methods.

Median imputation replaces missing values with the median of the observed values for the corresponding feature, which is particularly effective for handling skewed or non-Gaussian data distributions. Federated local res-hypertransformernet based training deep prediction model

Maintaining patient confidentiality while enabling high-performance predictive modeling is crucial in the field of modern healthcare, particularly for the treatment of chronic diseases. In pursuit of this goal, this study proposes Res-HyperTransformerNet, an innovative federated deep learning framework that utilizes the strength of Transformer blocks and Residual Networks (ResNet) and is tailored for federated learning environments. The model’s ability to support decentralized training over dispersed Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) nodes is crucial. The model begins at the architectural level with a feature extractor that is modeled after ResNet. The most significant addition of ResNet is its residual learning technique, which allows information to bypass certain layers utilizing shortcut (skip) connections, hence resolving the issue of disappearing gradients in deep networks. The block usually consists of a 1 × 1 pointwise convolution layer for channel expansion, followed by depthwise convolutions (DWConv) for spatial filtering at a lower computation cost. Deep feature extraction relies heavily on a block influenced by ResNet. In order to increase the dimension of the feature maps and enable cross-channel interaction without sacrificing spatial precision, the block begins with a 1 × 1 pointwise convolution. A depthwise convolution (DW Conv), a cost-effective procedure that applies filters independently to each channel to provide spatial filtering at extremely low computational cost, comes next. A further 1 × 1 convolution blends these spatially filtered features between channels to give a more expressive feature map. To enable deeper learning without degradation, a residual connection (engaged by an “Add” operation) skips the block, adding the input directly to the output. Residual learning enables the model to learn the residual differences instead of the whole transformation, which speeds up convergence and enhances performance in deep networks. After the high-level representations of the input data are produced by the ResNet module and passed into a Transformer block, which is modified for temporal and sequence-to-sequence learning, the Transformer applies a Multi-Head Attention (MHA) mechanism to learn attention over multiple subspaces at the same time to pick up on subtle relations and temporal dependencies.

Personalized multi-agent reinforcement learning for adaptive therapy management

The second central element of the methodology presents a Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL) framework. This element acts downstream from the federated prediction model, capitalizing on its output to provide adaptive, personalized therapy choices in long-term disease management. Chronic diseases like diabetes or cardiovascular disease are dynamic in nature and usually need to be adjusted with medications, diet, lifestyle, exercise, and behavioral care. A single-agent reinforcement scheme might not have the parallelism and specialization to handle these multidimensional treatments. Therefore, the architecture proposed here instantiates several autonomous agents, each for optimizing a different component of the therapy regimen.

When the patient information is processed through this hybrid deep model, the output embeddings are forwarded to a Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL) system. This system consists of several independent agents, each tasked with optimizing a different aspect of chronic disease treatment for example, medication regimen, nutrition planning, physical activity, and mental health. These agents run concurrently but also share important state representations and policy updates to maintain coherence and safety in total patient management. Figure 2 shows the Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL) framework, where separate agents work in parallel to learn and personalize various components of chronic disease treatment (e.g., medication, lifestyle). It situates how agents acquire knowledge from patient-specific states to make decisions in accordance with dynamic health objectives.

Every agent in the proposed Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL) framework employed a Fully Decoupled Deep Q-Network (FD-DQN) to learn optimal treatment policies using dynamically shaped reward signals derived from patient health indicators, behavioral patterns, and therapy compliance trends. FD-DQN extends conventional Deep Q-Network architectures by decoupling nested value estimations to improve policy stability, reduce value overestimation bias, and enhance decision reliability in non-stationary healthcare environments.

The reinforcement learning agents learned from patient-specific state representations that encapsulated individualized medical histories, comorbidity patterns, response trajectories to previous therapies, lifestyle behaviors, and contextual environmental factors influencing long-term treatment outcomes. These agents operated within a simulated clinical environment constructed from longitudinal Electronic Health Records (EHRs), wearable sensor streams, and behavioral monitoring logs, enabling safe policy exploration without direct patient risk.

The reward functions are well-crafted to reflect the long-term health outcomes, including glucose levels stabilization, blood pressure normalization, increased medication adherence, patient-reported quality of life, and decreased adverse clinical events. The implementation of FD-DQN into PMARL allowed personalizing therapy planning continuously and considering the dynamics of disease progression and changing patient behaviors.

The most pertinent characteristics that contribute to the identification are found via the XAI method. Medical practitioners are capable of checking the advice, comprehending both the feature importance1 globally and locally, and giving reasons during the consultation. This results in the elimination of barriers to transparency and accountability, and consequently, the establishment of patient trust which is a prerequisite that cannot be given up in the digital health ecosystem.

Dataset description

Dataset 1: The Kaggle CDC Chronic Disease dataset (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/cdc/chronic-disease) presents complete health measures and chronic disease behavior risk factors among U.S. populations. Gathered through the “Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System” (BRFSS), this yearly survey dataset consists of anonymous answers from more than 400,000 adults about their health, incidence of chronic diseases, and linked risk features such as physical inactivity, smoking, and obesity. Temporal span (usually 2015–2020) allows for longitudinal analysis of trends in chronic diseases. As one of the largest publicly available health datasets, it facilitates predictive modeling of risk of disease while introducing challenges such as class imbalances (usually < 15% prevalence for the majority of conditions) and self-reporting biases which need appropriate preprocessing for use with machine learning algorithms.

Dataset 2: The UCI Machine Learning Repository’s Risk Factor Prediction of Chronic KidneyDisease(https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/857/risk+factor+prediction+of+chronic+kidney+disease) dataset comprises clinical and demographic characteristics that can be used to predict the course of chronic kidney disease (CKD). It is made up of 400 patient records with 24 features, such as biochemical markers, medical history (hypertension, diabetes, anemia), and demographic variables (age, gender). Critical measurements like glomerular filtration rate (GFR), albumin, and red blood cell count assist in measuring kidney function. The data is binary-labeled (CKD or not CKD) and contains both numerical and categorical features, thus can be used to classify.

Performance analysis based on dataset 1

Table 12; Fig. 3 show the comparative performance analysis of the proposed framework and the current approaches on Dataset 1. The proposed approach has the best classification accuracy of 0.9861, which is better than AFL-CDP (0.9773), TriSpectraKAN (0.9666), PrescDRL (0.9576), and DeepXplainer (0.9486). This high accuracy is mainly explained by the Federated Res-HyperTransformerNet hybrid architecture that combines spatial feature extraction with ResNet and temporal dependency modeling with Transformer blocks. This combination allows strong multi-modal feature representation and at the same time, data privacy is maintained using federated learning, thus enhancing predictive reliability in distributed healthcare settings.

Equally, the proposed model has the best F1-score of 0.9841, which is better than AFL-CDP (0.9754), TriSpectraKAN (0.9639), PrescDRL (0.9557), and DeepXplainer (0.9467). The enhanced F1-score indicates equal accuracy and recall rates, which is improved by the incorporation of the PMARL framework with the use of FD-DQN agents. These agents dynamically optimize treatment plans in a variety of treatment areas, allowing coordinated, adaptive, and personalized treatment planning that enhances classification strength in different patient groups.

The suggested framework also shows better precision (0.9855), recall (0.9830), specificity (0.9907), MCC (0.9712), and NPV (0.9913) which shows high predictive consistency and low misclassification rates. Moreover, the model has a lower FPR (0.0135) and FNR (0.0213) than all the baseline methods, which shows that it is more reliable in detecting chronic disease risk conditions.

In general, the findings support the idea that federated hybrid deep learning and multi-agent reinforcement learning are highly effective in terms of prediction accuracy and efficiency of therapy optimization.

Rationale and need for inferential validation

The starting point of “equivalent learning outcomes” purely on descriptive performance metrics (accuracy, F1-score, reward score) is not satisfactory from a statistical rigor perspective. It is impossible to reveal whether noticed performance gains are of a statistical significance or were simply a result of sampling fluctuation without inferential analysis. To overcome this difficulty, we added formal statistical hypothesis testing and confidence-based validation, thus making the equivalence and superiority claims to be statistically justified and reproducible.

In this respect, we carried out three inferential analyses that complemented each other:

Repeated stratified k-fold cross-validation with confidence intervals.Paired significance testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank test).Equivalence testing using the Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) procedure.Effect size estimation (Cohen’s d).

Discussion

This paper presents a new approach for managing chronic diseases that integrates Federated Learning (FL), Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL), and SHAP-based Explainable AI (XAI) to form a hybrid framework. This new idea of FL-PMARL-XAI has led to huge improvements in the performance of predictions, policy evaluation, and interpretability all in one go as it has been compared to the baseline models with the accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity all showing significant gains across the CDC Chronic Disease and UCI CKD data sets, along with a number of clinical metrics. Bringing together online learning of representation at the population level with federated learning for generalization allows for quick and easy adaptation to the data of individual patients while still keeping consistency at the population level, and SHAP-based interpretability makes the process clear to both doctors and patients, thus, increasing the amount of trust and support in the deployment of ethical AI. On the other hand, the limitations of this work should be acknowledged. The employed datasets, although extensive, may still not completely cover the diversity of chronic disease patients and self-reported patient data or data from small samples might be biased and not easily generalizable. The model might also not be sensitive enough for the minority classes, which is a consequence of class imbalances that characterize certain chronic diseases. The large differences between the models and baseline have been confirmed by inferential tests and effect size calculations, but there is a possibility that real-life performance varies because of factors like environmental differences, IoMT device diversity, and patient compliance. Moreover, the RL agents were tested in a simulated environment and not in the real clinical setting, plus the high computational requirements of the hybrid architecture could be a barrier to its deployment in low-resource areas.

The framework shows a considerable increase in metrics that are clinically relevant. One example is in Dataset 1 where the False Negative Rate (FNR) of 0.0097 is a lot lower than that of ResNet (0.0418) and MLP (0.0520), thus indicating a decrease in the probability of not detecting chronic disease cases. The same goes for the Negative Predictive Value (NPV) of 0.9893 which makes it possible to rule out non-disease cases with a very high degree of certainty, consequently preventing the occurrence of unnecessary treatments. Reinforcement learning agents came to a policy faster by 25–32% than the baseline indicating the system’s potential for timely, adaptive therapy optimization through higher episode returns. Such developments imply that hybrid asynchronous–synchronous learning can facilitate personalized, longitudinal disease management especially for chronic conditions that are influenced by changing physiological and behavioral patterns.

The designed framework is placed right in the middle of the two learning paradigms, synchronous and asynchronous. It does not suffer like the synchronous FL methods from their negatives such as straggler effects, rigid update cycles, and low adaptability in varying healthcare scenarios (Arefin, 2024; Khalifa & Albadawy, 2024). The only difference is that our framework allows client-level asynchronous FL updates and then periodically synchronous aggregation through FedAvg. This mixed model guarantees the consistency of the whole population while at the same time preventing the delays that are usually caused by the irregularity of patient data streams. In a similar manner, regarding the reinforcement learning area, the studies done previously mostly rely upon single-agent or synchronous multi-agent RL for healthcare optimization (Shaik et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2024) and in doing so overlook the longitudinal variability of patient-specific interventions. With the use of asynchronous PMARL, every agent—representing medication, diet, activity, or mental health—does its policy optimization independently while taking advantage of the shared state embeddings from the global model to guarantee clinical coherence. This method is in theory perfectly consistent with the Markov Decision Process formulations for longitudinal care and decentralized MARL, and hence it is able to support stable policy updates even in non-stationary environments.

Ultimately, the SHAP-backed XAI method brings in a facet of real-time interpretability, offing medical doctors globally and locally able feature attributions, hence, trust issues, ethical alignment, and regulatory compliance are all boosted. The mixed-type model still, on the one hand, had a quick patient-specific adaptability and on the other hand, learned from the whole population, thus, moving forward from the classical predictive-only or decision-only AI frameworks (Duckworth et al., 2024; Mahmood et al., 2025).

Future research should prioritize validation of the clinical approach, enlarging and diversifying the datasets, optimizing for edge deployment, developing agent-specific interpretability methods, and ensuring compliance with rules and ethics. Justifying the research and the use of FL–PMARL–XAI was successful in the areas of predictive accuracy, adaptive policy optimization, and clinical interpretability among other things. The framework as a whole is thus a very patient-friendly and scalable approach to chronic disease management that not only addresses the existing gaps between synchronous and asynchronous learning paradigms but also paves the way for future precision health systems. However, ethical and regulatory issues, as well as privacy concerns, particularly those related to HIPAA/GDPR requirements, still need to be thoroughly understood before the system can be deployed in practice. It is suggested that the future research validation of the framework should be conducted using larger, multi-center, longitudinal studies, and in addition, it would be beneficial to incorporate more multi-agent interpretability mechanisms, improve computational efficiency, and initiate pilot clinical trials to evaluate safety, efficacy, and user acceptance. All in all, the results point out that the suggested framework does not simply reflect a theoretically and practically grounded improvement in precision chronic disease management from the viewpoint of balancing predictive performance, personalization, interpretability, and system efficiency; it also acknowledges the current limitations that need to be cleared up before the real-world implementation.

Conclusion

This study presented a hybrid privacy-preserving framework that integrates Federated Learning (FL), a Res-HyperTransformerNet predictive model, Personalized Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PMARL), and SHAP-based Explainable AI for adaptive chronic disease management. The proposed framework was designed to address three key challenges in intelligent healthcare systems: (i) secure distributed learning from heterogeneous health data, (ii) dynamic and personalized therapy optimization across multiple intervention domains, and (iii) transparent clinical interpretability of both predictive and prescriptive decisions.

Experimental evaluation on the CDC Chronic Disease dataset and the UCI Chronic Kidney Disease dataset demonstrated improved predictive performance and reinforcement learning efficiency compared to selected benchmark models under controlled experimental settings. The federated hybrid architecture showed stable convergence behavior, reduced communication overhead relative to centralized approaches, and consistent classification metrics across cross-validation folds. Additionally, the integration of SHAP-based explanations enabled both global and local interpretability, supporting clinician-oriented transparency.

However, the study was conducted using retrospective public datasets and simulated federated and reinforcement learning environments. Real-world deployment may introduce additional challenges related to data heterogeneity, system scalability, regulatory compliance, and prospective clinical validation of therapy recommendations. These aspects require further investigation before practical implementation in live healthcare systems.

Overall, the results suggest that combining federated representation learning with personalized multi-agent reinforcement learning is a promising direction for adaptive chronic disease therapy management. Continued research focusing on real-world validation, scalability, and clinical integration will be essential to translate such frameworks into operational healthcare systems.
