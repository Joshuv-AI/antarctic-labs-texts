Diagnosis-to-Ablation Time and Atrial Arrhythmia Recurrence After Ablation

ABSTRACT
This cohort study evaluates whether diagnosis-to-ablation time is associated with atrial arrhythmia recurrence following radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and increased risks of stroke and heart failure.1,2,3 Maintenance of sinus rhythm improves clinical outcomes,4,5 and catheter ablation (CA) is the most effective strategy for achieving rhythm control.6,7 AF reoccurs in 20% to 40% of cases less than 1 year after CA, and long-term success remains suboptimal.6,7,8 Growing evidence underscores the prognostic significance of timely AF rhythm control.9,10 The interval between AF diagnosis and ablation—the diagnosis-to-ablation time (DAT)—has been reported as an important predictor of procedural efficacy.11,12 These findings highlight the importance of optimizing patient selection and DAT and understanding the association with ablation outcomes as a function of DAT to help guide standardized practice. We analyzed the independent association between DAT and recurrent AF following CA for AF in the prospective, multicenter, rigorously monitored, clinical practice population, Real-World Experience of Catheter Ablation for the Treatment of Symptomatic Paroxysmal and Persistent Atrial Fibrillation (REAL-AF) Registry.

Methods

Study Population and Design

REAL-AF (NCT04088071) is a prospective, multicenter, clinical practice registry conducted within the US and Canada enrolling up to 20 000 participants undergoing radiofrequency (RFA) or pulsed field (PFA) ablation for AF. The WCG institutional review board approved the registry, and patients signed an informed consent (unless approved waiver). The Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice were followed. This report was constructed using the STROBE guidelines checklist recommendations for cohort studies.13 The registry follows standard of care as described previously.14 Adult patients with symptomatic AF were included; long-standing and persistent AF (PsAF) were excluded. The DAT was defined as the earliest year of AF documentation in the medical record, based on electrocardiogram (ECG), ambulatory monitoring, or wearable ECG tracings. Between January 2018 and June 2023, 8189 index REAL-AF ablations were performed; 633 had missing DAT data, 2834 had incomplete (late) 12-month follow-up, and 998 had missing primary end point data excluded from this analysis.

Ablation Procedure

Catheter ablation was performed with cardiac topography (CARTO)–enabled RFA catheters with contact force-sensing (Biosense Webster, Inc, part of Johnson & Johnson MedTech). All participants underwent wide-area circumferential ablation for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) and additional strategy per operator discretion. Procedural time was measured from femoral venipuncture to catheter withdrawal. Pulmonary vein RFA time was the duration from first to final lesion achieving durable PVI. Total RFA time represented the cumulative duration of all ablation energy applications completed. Left atrial scar burden was assessed using intraprocedural electroanatomic mapping performed at the time of ablation. High-density bipolar voltage maps of the left atrium (LA) were acquired using a multipolar mapping catheter in conjunction with a 3D electroanatomical mapping system (CARTO, Biosense Webster, a part of Johnson & Johnson MedTech). Scar was predefined as areas demonstrating bipolar voltage amplitude less than 0.5 mV; normal voltage was defined as 0.5 mV or greater. Scar burden was quantified as the proportion of the total mapped LA surface area exhibiting bipolar voltage less than 0.5 mV. Surface area calculations were derived from the 3D LA figure, generated during mapping. Areas not adequately mapped or excluded due to poor signal quality were excluded.

Patient Follow-Up

After index ablation, participants underwent 1-year follow-up with ECGs at 3 months and 1 year. Continuous rhythm monitoring was performed at 6 months and 1 year; duration and monitoring modality was standard of practice. Detection of AF recurrence was facilitated through implantable cardiac devices, implantable loop recorders (ILRs), Holter or patch monitors, and commercial wearables. When patients reported AF-related symptoms during follow-up, supplementary cardiac monitoring via event recorders or ILRs was conducted per standard of care.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was freedom from all atrial arrhythmia (symptomatic and asymptomatic) at 1-year follow-up. Atrial arrhythmia recurrence was defined as any documented AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia lasting 30 seconds or greater, identified through routine clinic ECG, continuous outpatient ambulatory monitoring at 3 months and 1 year, or transmissions from cardiac implantable electronic devices or consumer ECG platforms. Arrhythmias occurring 90 or more days postablation were censored per standard blanking period. Secondary outcomes included (1) procedural characteristics encompassing procedural time, rates of first pass-PVI, fluoroscopy time, and dormant conduction with adenosine or isoproterenol, and (2) long-term clinical outcomes: freedom from AF-related symptoms and antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) use through 1-year follow-up. Safety outcomes included acute and long-term procedure-related complications (eg, transient ischemic attack [TIA] or stroke, pericarditis, phrenic nerve injury, atrio-esophageal fistula, pulmonary vein stenosis, vascular injury, and death).

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using complete case datasets, and the sample size for each model was reported. The DAT groups were categorized as short- (<1 year), intermediate- (1-4 years), and long-term (≥5 years) DAT. Prespecified DAT cutoffs were selected by the investigator group as clinically relevant, allowing for variation, since access and timing is influenced by sex, geographic region, socioeconomic factors, race, and ethnicity.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables and categorical variables as relative frequencies (percentages). Parametric tests were used for normally distributed data and nonparametric tests for nonnormal distributions. Kaplan-Meier survival (KM) analysis and Cox proportional hazards models assessed the association between DAT and all atrial arrhythmia-free survival at 1-year postindex ablation. Time-to-event analyses were anchored at the ablation date, with arrhythmia recurrence assessed after a 90-day blanking period. Follow-up was censored at 459 days to accommodate routine visit windows while ensuring at least 12-month outcomes post–blanking period. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs and P values were reported. Two-sided statistical significance with a P < .05 threshold of significance was used.

Multivariable models included patient-level covariates as fixed effects and clustering at the physician level. Scar burden was a potential confounder using linear regression and analysis of variance (ANOVA), with multicollinearity assessed by generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), with values less than 2.5 considered acceptable. Interactions DAT and scar burden, age, AF type, and CHA2DS2-VASc (congestive heart failure, hypertension, age 75 years or older, diabetes, stroke or TIA or thromboembolism, vascular disease, age 65 to 74 years, sex category [female])score were tested in separate models, and subgroup analyses were performed using multivariable Cox models in predefined cohorts adjusted for patient covariates. Due to missing left ventricular ejection fraction percentage (LVEF %) and scar burden data, the total number of complete cases was reduced. Therefore, robustness was assessed using sensitivity models excluding LVEF % and scar burden.

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 2026.01.0 (R Core Team). Adjusted survival analyses were conducted using the survival, survminer, and adjustedCurves packages.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The final analytic cohort included 3724 patients with a mean (SD) age of 67.0 (10.0) years; 1492 (40.1%) were female, 1084 (29.1%) had DAT of less than 1 year, 1843 (49.5%) had DAT of 1 to 4 years, and 797 (21.4%) had DAT of 5 or more years. Median (IQR) DAT was 1 (0-4) years. The prevalence of paroxysmal AF (PAF) vs PsAF did not differ significantly across DAT groups (700 of 1084 [64.6%], 1194 of 1843 [64.8%], 518 of 797 [65.0%] for short, intermediate, and long DAT, respectively). Mean (SD) body mass index (BMI; calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) and CHA2DS2VASc score were 31.0 (6.8) and 2.8 (1.5), respectively. Mean (SD) ages were 66.3 (10.1), 66.8 (10.2), and 68.3 (9.3) years for the less than 1-year, 1- to 4-year, and 5- or more–year groups, respectively (P < .001), and 401 of 1083 (37.0%), 790 of 1843 (42.9%), and 301 of 797 (37.8%) were female in the short, intermediate, and long DAT groups, respectively (P = .003). The mean (SD) CHA2DS2-VASc score was slightly higher with longer DAT (2.6 [1.5], 2.8 [1.6], and 2.8 [1.5]; P = .003). Obstructive sleep apnea was more frequent with longer DAT (<1 year, 409 of 1052 [38.9%]; 1-4 years, 803 of 1778 [45.2%]; and ≥5 years, 370 of 779 [47.5%]; P < .001). Use of AADs before ablation increased progressively (<1 year, 377 of 1081 [34.9%]; 1-4 years, 786 of 1841 [42.7%]; and ≥5 years, 409 of 797 [51.3%]; P < .001). The mean (SD) LVEF % (<1 year, 53.8 [11.9%]; 1-4 years, 55.4 [13.3%]; and ≥5 years, 55.9 [9.6%]; P = .02) and left-atrial volume index (<1 year, 30.1 [15.4] mL/m2; 1-4 years, 31.7 [13.0] mL/m2; and ≥5 years, 33.6 [17.5] mL/m2; P = .02) were also slightly higher in patients with a longer DAT (Table 1). Study Outcomes

Median [IQR] follow-up times were similar across the less than 1 year (377 [349-408] days), the 1-4 years (376 [342-406] days), and the 5 or more years (374 [336-408] days) DAT groups (eTable 9 in Supplement 1). During 1-year follow-up, 610 patients (16.4%) experienced an atrial arrhythmia recurrence after the 90-day blanking period, of whom 474 (77.8%) had AF as the recurrent arrhythmia. Furthermore, compared with participants with PAF, those with PsAF reported a higher recurrence (PAF, 343 of 2412 [14.2%] vs PsAF, 267 of 1312 [20.4%]; P < .001). Recurrence rate (within 459 days postindex procedure) was lowest in the less than 1 year DAT group (133 of 1084 [12.3%]), was higher in the 1 to 4 years DAT group (303 of 1843 [16.4%]) and was highest in the 5 or more years DAT group (174 of 797 [21.8%]) (eTable 10 and eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Overall procedural complications occurred in 64 patients (1.7%), with acute complications in 33 (0.9%); complication rates were similar across DAT groups (Table 3).

Adjusted Analyses

In a Cox proportional hazards model (2052 patients) adjusting for DAT, age, sex, CHA2DS2-VASc score, LVEF %, scar burden, preprocedural AAD use, AF type, and clustered at the physician level, participants with DAT 1 to 4 years had a 48% higher risk of arrhythmia recurrence through 1 year (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.08-2.01; P = .01) and those with DAT 5 or more years had 110% higher risk (HR, 2.10; 95% CI, 1.46-3.02; P < .001) compared with those with DAT less than 1 year. Figure 2 displays the difference in time to recurrence by DAT group and describes the model estimates. When modeled as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines, DAT demonstrated a nonlinear, dose-response association with atrial arrhythmia recurrence (χ22 = 11.15; P = .004). Results were directionally consistent with the categorical analysis and did not alter the overall interpretation (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Sensitivity Analyses and Subgroup Analyses

To evaluate potential selection bias associated with missing preablation LVEF and scar burden, we performed sensitivity analyses refitting adjusted Cox models excluding these covariates. Across all model specifications, longer diagnosis-to-ablation time remained consistently associated with a higher risk of atrial arrhythmia recurrence, supporting the robustness of the primary finding.

To further explore potential associations between DAT and all atrial arrhythmia recurrence, we examined whether scar burden differed across DAT categories. Scar burden was similar across DAT categories, with median (IQR) scar burden percentages of 1.3% (95% CI, 0.0%-10.0%), 2.0% (95% CI, 0.0%-10.0%), and 1.0% (95% CI, 0.0%-10.0%) in the short- (<1 year), intermediate- (1-4 years), and long-DAT (≥5 years) groups, respectively. In multivariable linear regression, neither DAT 1 to 4 years nor DAT 5 or more years was associated with scar burden percentage. However, scar burden was independently associated with all atrial arrhythmia recurrence in the Cox model (HR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.01-1.02; P < .001), and its inclusion did not attenuate the effect of DAT. No interaction was observed between DAT and scar burden on recurrence, suggesting DAT and scar burden contribute independently to recurrence risk (refer to eTables 2-7 in Supplement 1).

In additional subgroup analyses, results were consistent. Among 2412 patients with PAF, DAT 5 or more years was significantly associated with higher recurrence risk (HR, 1.77; 95% CI, 1.15-2.71; P = .009), while DAT 1 to 4 years was not (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 0.97-1.65). In 1312 patients with PsAF, DAT 5 or more years was also associated with higher recurrence (HR, 2.34; 95% CI, 1.37-4.00; P = .002), and DAT 1 to 4 years was not (HR, 1.53; 95% CI, 0.99-2.38; P = .06). Although statistical significance for the 1 to 4 years DAT group was attenuated after stratification, the direction of the findings remained consistent across all subgroups, supporting the robustness of the overall trend observed in the primary model. Among 1759 AAD-naive patients, risk of recurrence was elevated in those with DAT 5 or more years (HR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.39-4.13; P = .002), while those with DAT between 1 to 4 years did not differ from the reference group (HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 0.91-1.90; P = .14). In 847 patients with prior AAD failure, DAT 5 or more years was associated with higher recurrence risk in unadjusted models (HR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.21-3.60; P = .009), but this association did not persist in the adjusted model. All multivariable models demonstrated GVIF values below 2.5 (all adjusted GVIF <2), indicating no evidence of multicollinearity among the included covariates (eTables 2-7 in Supplement 1). A sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the association of the blanking period on the present analysis of recurrence and showed the association between DAT and recurrence remained statistically significant (see eTable 8 in Supplement 1). Refer to eTables 1 to 10 in Supplement 1 for complete results.

Discussion

In this analysis of participants undergoing de novo RFA for AF, DAT varied widely, with a 1 year median. After adjusting for clinical covariates, prolonged DAT remained independently associated with higher odds of recurrent AF after RFA. Our findings align with previous analyses demonstrating similar associations between DAT. We expand upon prior reports by providing results from a large, multicenter, prospective, clinical practice registry, with scar burden and procedural complexity considered. Prior studies evaluating DAT have included retrospective, administrative datasets, or were from outside of the US. A retrospective study with 11 143 participants showed postatrial ablation recurrence was associated with DAT (20% increase in recurrence among patients with DAT >1 year)15 more than by age and heart failure. The Catheter Ablation for Persistent Atrial Fibrillation study reported16 escalating recurrence rates across quartiles of increasing DAT, emphasizing early intervention.17

The results support the hypothesis that long periods of untreated AF worsen structural and electrical atrial remodeling, reducing procedural success. These also confirm independent associations with higher all-cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and a higher incidence of stroke after ablation.17 The progressive remodeling, marked by atrial dilation, fibrosis, and conduction heterogeneity, demonstrates AF begets AF18. However, recent research has demonstrated that atriopathy can advance without AF-related ECGs through frequent, dyssynchronous, or ectopic premature atrial contractions.19 Essentially, even if a patient is not exhibiting AF, the atriopathy can progress if triggers are not controlled.20 Compared with AAD therapy, ablation can slow progression of fibrotic atrial cardiomyopathy and progression from paroxysmal to PsAF decreases. The Does Eliminating Coffee Avoid Fibrillation trial identified AF as a powerful independent predictor of arrhythmia recurrence, demonstrating that greater degrees of scarring significantly reduce long-term ablation success.21

Inflammation is another pivotal factor. Prolonged AF episodes provoke elevated interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein predictive of recurrence.2 Increased preablation inflammatory indicators are highly associated with diminished procedural success, underscoring the role of inflammation in sustained electrical instability and atrial fibrosis. Given that atrial fibrosis and prolonged inflammatory process can lead to irreversible remodeling, early management probably averts significant structural changes, thereby enhancing ablation effectiveness. Early ablation may disrupt this harmful inflammatory cycle by reducing atrial exposure to the resultant oxidative stress and endothelial damage.22

In this cohort study, we showed that recurrence risk is especially significant with extended DAT in PsAF. The association between DAT and recurrence remained consistent across all patient categories, although the statistical significance for the 1- to 4-year DAT group was attenuated after stratification, likely reflecting smaller sample sizes with reduced power, rather than an absence of effect. Notably, DAT of 5 or more years remained associated with recurrence across all analyses, reinforcing the overall dose-response association between increasing DAT and arrhythmia recurrence risk. Prior studies have shown patients with PsAF experience expedited structural and electrophysiological decline—increasing recurrence risk relative to those with PAF.23 The importance of early rhythm control was demonstrated in the Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention 4 trial. Initiation of rhythm control at 1 or less year of diagnosis significantly reduced the composite end point of cardiovascular death, stroke, or hospitalization for worsening heart failure or acute coronary syndrome compared with usual care. Our results and EAST-AFNET 4 underscores the therapeutic value of minimizing the DAT-shifting toward early intervention to preserve the atrium and improve long-term cardiovascular outcomes.4 Data from EARLY-AF, EAST-AFNET 4, and this analysis demonstrates that prompt rhythm-control strategies translate into improved rhythm stability, reduced morbidity, and better long-term cardiovascular health.4,14

Clinically, DAT is a modifiable risk factor vs a nonmodifiable predictor.24 This makes the effort to decrease DAT particularly important from a global public health perspective. The implementation of an AF clinical protocol-based integrated care model has proven effective in improving DAT from a median time of 789 days to 127 days with a 42% decrease in the odds ratio for AF recurrence.25 Finally, the highest-yield strategy for improving clinical CA in AF may be from prompt action rather than specific catheters or advanced techniques.26,27

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Although it was a prospective observational analysis, the inherent bias associated with nonrandomized designs cannot be excluded. This risk was mitigated through consecutive enrollment and the inclusion of mostly comparable groups. Next, procedural parameters were influenced by operator experience that were minimized by models accounting for individual operator and center-level variability. Third, factors contributing to delays in AF diagnosis may reduce the accuracy of DAT as a surrogate marker of atrial remodeling. Moreover, because of the DAT interval, the true onset of AF may have preceded clinical diagnosis by a variable period. Consequently, DAT may be underestimated in patients whose arrhythmia was present but undetected prediagnosis. Scar burden was quantified using voltage-based mapping, which may underdetect diffuse fibrosis and is sensitive to mapping parameters, potentially contributing to the low absolute values and lack of differences across DAT groups. The lack of standardized rhythm monitoring may have contributed to undetected arrhythmias but reflects clinical practice. Any potential underdetection of recurrence would be expected to be nondifferential across the study population, thereby preserving the internal validity. Finally, the modalities to detect AF recurrence were not predefined or standardized across sites and may introduce bias, yet represent practices’ lack of standardized detection methods.

Conclusions

In this large, prospective, multicenter, cohort study of patients receiving care in the clinical practice setting, we identified that DAT was independently associated with AF recurrence risk post-RFA with a higher risk for participants with 1 or more year DAT. The magnitude of these findings was greater in PsAF patients. Moreover, earlier CA intervention may not only increase procedural success but may preserve atrial structure and function. Ongoing efforts to accelerate early AF ablation should be pursued.
