Negative‐Pressure Induces Epithelial‐Mesenchymal Transition via Thrombospondin‐1 Upregulation in Intact Diabetic Skin
Authors: Toshifumi Yamashiro, Toshihiro Kushibiki, Yoshine Mayumi, Masato Tsuchiya, Miya Ishihara, Ryuichi Azuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNegative‐pressure wound therapy (NPWT) is widely used in clinical practise to enhance wound healing; however, its biological effects on intact skin remain poorly understood. Given the expanding applications of NPWT, understanding its impact beyond open wounds is increasingly important. This study aimed to evaluate the biological responses of intact skin to negative‐pressure, specifically focusing on epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT). The effects of negative‐pressure loading were assessed using an in vitro model of non‐diabetic human keratinocytes and an in vivo model of intact diabetic mouse skin. Human keratinocytes exposed to negative‐pressure exhibited increased expression of thrombospondin‐1 (THBS1), transforming growth factor‐beta 1 (TGF‐β1), plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 (PAI‐1), and hypoxia‐inducible factor 1‐alpha (HIF‐1α), alongside decreased epithelial markers and increased mesenchymal markers. These EMT‐related changes were mitigated by inhibiting the THBS1‐TGF‐β1 interaction. Similarly, in diabetic mice, intermittent negative‐pressure loading applied to intact dorsal skin significantly increased THBS1 and TGF‐β1 levels, resulting in epidermal and dermal thickening, and promoted hypoxic, prothrombotic and angiogenic responses, as evidenced by increased HIF‐1α, PAI‐1, fibrinogen and vascular endothelial growth factor expression. These findings suggest that negative‐pressure loading can induce EMT‐like responses and tissue remodelling in intact skin primarily via mechanisms involving the THBS1‐TGF‐β1 signalling axis. This study expands the understanding of the biological influence of NPWT beyond traditional wound treatment applications, potentially informing future therapeutic considerations and safety guidelines.

Introduction

Since its widespread clinical implementation following the landmark studies of Morykwas et al. [1], negative‐pressure wound therapy (NPWT) has been extensively applied to various types of wounds, including chronic wounds, traumatic injuries, infectious wounds and postoperative incisions [2, 3, 4, 5]. Its clinical efficacy is widely recognised across diverse fields, with proposed mechanisms of action including exudate management, edema reduction and stimulation of angiogenesis [6]. However, despite its broad clinical use, the molecular biological mechanisms underlying its effects, particularly regarding epidermal responses, remain poorly understood [7, 8, 9, 10].

In recent years, NPWT technology has expanded [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16], with novel adaptations such as closed incision negative‐pressure therapy (ciNPT) being increasingly applied not only to surgical incisions but also to intact skin adjacent to wound sites [5, 11, 17, 18]. These novel applications have broadened therapeutic indications but have simultaneously raised concerns regarding adverse effects, such as skin blistering occurring on non‐wounded skin subjected to negative‐pressure (NP) loading [5]. Issues such as inflammation and blistering at wound margins, particularly where foam contacts healthy skin, have long been recognised with conventional NPWT for open wounds. To mitigate these complications, clinicians have developed protective strategies, including the use of skin barriers and modified application techniques [19, 20]. Nonetheless, detailed investigations into the underlying biological mechanisms of these skin‐related adverse effects remain limited [20]. This lack of detailed knowledge highlights the need to better understand how NP affects intact skin, particularly in vulnerable populations such as individuals with diabetes.

Diabetic skin frequently represents a target for NPWT due to its inherently impaired healing capacity [21]. It exhibits unique biological characteristics, including chronic low‐grade inflammation, abnormal extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling and increased susceptibility to fibrosis [22, 23, 24]. Collectively, these alterations result in enhanced skin fragility and compromised responses to mechanical stress or injury. Despite the known susceptibility of diabetic skin to NPWT‐related effects, the specific biological effects of NP loading on intact diabetic skin, independently of open wounds, remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, we investigated the biological effects of NP loading on intact diabetic skin using in vitro and in vivo experimental models. Initially, we conducted a protein screening using a membrane antibody array (Proteome Profiler; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) to examine culture supernatants and cell lysates obtained from keratinocytes exposed to NP. This preliminary screening identified increased expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 (PAI‐1) and thrombospondin‐1 (THBS1), both known mediators of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [25, 26]. EMT is a biological process characterised by the loss of epithelial characteristics and the acquisition of mesenchymal traits, which is often associated with fibrosis and reduced epithelial integrity [27, 28].

Based on these preliminary findings, we hypothesised that NP loading might induce EMT‐like changes even in intact diabetic skin. To test this hypothesis, we conducted detailed protein expression analyses in cultured keratinocytes and diabetic mouse skin subjected to NP. Given the established role of transforming growth factor‐β1 (TGF‐β1) as a pivotal regulator of EMT [29, 30], the recognised function of THBS1 as a key activator of latent TGF‐β1 [26], and the known involvement of hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α) in tissue remodelling, fibrosis and angiogenesis [31, 32], we specifically focused on these molecules in our analysis. Our study aims to clarify the molecular responses of intact diabetic skin to NP loading, especially considering the expanding clinical use of NPWT technologies such as ciNPT.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of the National Defence Medical College (approval number: 21065). All procedures were conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and relevant institutional regulations. Humane care was provided throughout the study. An electronic laboratory notebook platform was not used.

Cell Culture and Negative‐Pressure Incubation

PSVK1 human keratinocytes were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan). Cells (6 × 105) were seeded into 35‐mm culture dishes and maintained in Keratinocyte‐SFM (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 50 μg/mL bovine pituitary extract and 100 μg/mL streptomycin–penicillin for 24 h. Following medium replacement, cells were divided into two experimental groups: ambient pressure (AP) and NP, and incubated for an additional 24 h. The AP group was cultured under standard atmospheric conditions in a CO2 incubator. The NP group was cultured in an air‐tight container containing humidified water and equipped with a data logger for environmental monitoring, as described previously [33]. Continuous NP of 120 mmHg was applied using the RENASYS TOUCH device (Smith & Nephew; Watford, Hertfordshire, UK) via a standard accessory, RENASYS Softport (Figure S1). All experiments were conducted using biological replicates.

In Vitro Protein Analysis

Culture supernatants and cell lysates were collected from AP and NP groups after 24 h of treatment. Quantitative protein analysis was performed using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Concentrations of THBS1, TGF‐β1 and E‐cadherin in supernatants, and PAI‐1, HIF‐1α, vimentin and alpha‐smooth muscle actin (αSMA) in cell lysates were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's protocols (Table S1). Total protein concentrations in lysates were determined using the TaKaRa BCA Protein Assay Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), and ELISA values for lysates were normalised to total protein (mg/mL).

Gene Expression Analysis in PSVK1 Cells

PSVK1 cells (6 × 105) were seeded into 35‐mm dishes and treated with either 100 μM Leucine‐Serine‐Lysine‐Leucine (LSKL) peptide (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA) or 10 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as vehicle control in 2000 μL serum‐free medium. After 24 h of treatment, cells were further exposed to either AP or NP conditions for an additional 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and purified with the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands). Complementary DNA was synthesised using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed using the TaqMan Universal Master Mix II with UNG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a ViiA 7 Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct method (ΔΔCt), with 18S rRNA serving as the internal control and the vehicle + AP group used as the calibrator. Animals and Treatment

Female C57BLKS/J db/db mice, an established model for obesity and diabetes, were purchased from SLC Japan (Shizuoka, Japan). Experiments were initiated when the mice were 9 weeks old. Anaesthesia was induced using a combination of 0.75 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride, 4 mg/kg midazolam and 5 mg/kg butorphanol tartrate, and reversed using 0.75 mg/kg atipamezole hydrochloride. Eighteen mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): a control group and two treatment groups (Figure S2). The sample size was determined based on preliminary experiments and feasibility considerations, in line with prior studies using similar experimental designs. On the day before treatment (Day 0), the dorsal skin was circumferentially depilated. No surgical incision or skin defect was created throughout the experiment. On Day 1, two 2 × 3‐cm areas were bilaterally marked on the back of each mouse, with the left side designated as the non‐intervention side and the right as the intervention side. For the treatment groups, the intervention side was covered with RENASYS Granufoam (Smith & Nephew), a porous polyurethane dressing, and sealed with polyurethane film. In the NP group, negative pressure of 120 mmHg was applied continuously using the RENASYS TOUCH system (Smith & Nephew) during each 3‐h session, resulting in an intermittent loading protocol (3 h/day). This intermittent pressure duration was selected to elicit mechanobiological responses whilst minimising excessive mechanical burden on intact skin during prolonged application. The AP group underwent identical dressing procedures without aspiration. The control group received anaesthesia only and no dressing. During the non‐treatment hours, all mice were housed under standard conditions without any additional management, as the skin remained intact. Treatments were conducted daily for four consecutive days. At the conclusion of the final treatment, mice were euthanized, and skin specimens were harvested from the centre of the treated area for further analysis.

In Vivo Protein Analysis

Circular skin biopsies (1 cm in diameter) were homogenised in 1000 μL of cold phosphate‐buffered saline containing 1% Triton X‐100 (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, California, USA) and 0.1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma‐Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) using a μT‐12 tissue homogeniser (TAITEC, Saitama, Japan) at 3200 rpm for 2 min. Homogenates were centrifuged at 15000 ×g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected for quantification of THBS1, TGF‐β1, PAI‐1, HIF‐1α and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) using specific ELISA kits (Table S1). Protein concentrations were normalised per milligramme of protein. Final values were calculated as the difference in protein levels between the intervention and non‐intervention sides for each mouse.

Histological Analysis

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded skin tissues were sectioned at 4 μm and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE) or processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC) using specific antibodies (Table S2). HE staining was used to assess general tissue architecture, including epidermal and dermal thicknesses. IHC was performed to evaluate the expression of THBS1 as a marker of epithelial migration; HIF‐1α, PAI‐1 and fibrinogen to assess hypoxia, fibrosis and thrombotic vascular occlusion, respectively [34]; and CD31 to evaluate angiogenesis. All images were captured at 200× magnification. Histological and immunohistochemical quantifications were performed in a blinded manner. Quantitative measurements were averaged from at least 10 randomly selected fields per sample. Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ FIJI Software (US NIH, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) [35], and results were expressed as the difference between the intervention and non‐intervention sides for each mouse.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.9.5.1 (GraphPad, San Diego, California, USA). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two‐tailed t‐test was used. For comparisons amongst three or more groups, one‐way or two‐way ANOVA was conducted, followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference test for post hoc analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and a p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

EMT‐Related Responses Induced by Negative‐Pressure in Vitro

Protein expression analysis of culture supernatants and cell lysates revealed significantly increased expression of EMT‐related proteins in the NP group compared to the AP group, including THBS1 (p = 0.008), TGF‐β1 (p = 0.008), PAI‐1 (p = 0.001) and HIF‐1α (p = 0.022) (Figure 1a). In parallel, EMT marker analysis showed significantly decreased expression of the epithelial marker E‐cadherin (p = 0.031) and increased expression of the mesenchymal marker αSMA (p = 0.031). Additionally, vimentin expression tended to be higher under NP conditions (p = 0.051) (Figure 1b).

THBS1–TGF‐β1 Blockade Suppresses EMT‐Associated Gene Expression

Two‐way ANOVA demonstrated significant main effects of treatment (vehicle vs. LSKL) and pressure conditions (AP vs. NP) on thrombospondin‐1 (THBS1) expression (treatment: p = 0.008; pressure: p = 0.006), with a significant interaction between factors (p = 0.004). Post hoc analysis indicated that THBS1 expression was significantly reduced in the NP group compared to the AP group under LSKL treatment (p < 0.001), and notably higher in the LSKL group compared to the vehicle group under AP conditions (p = 0.001).

Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1) expression showed a significant main effect of treatment, with vehicle treatment resulting in higher expression than LSKL (p = 0.004), and a trend towards lower expression in the LSKL group under AP conditions (p = 0.09). Cadherin 1 (CDH1) exhibited a significant effect of pressure (AP > NP, p < 0.001); post hoc tests confirmed significantly lower expression in the NP group under both vehicle (p = 0.007) and LSKL conditions (p = 0.03). Vimentin (VIM) demonstrated a significant treatment effect (vehicle > LSKL, p < 0.001) and a significant interaction between factors (p < 0.001); NP conditions induced higher VIM expression compared to AP under vehicle conditions (p = 0.02), whereas NP significantly decreased VIM expression under LSKL treatment (p < 0.001). Finally, actin alpha 2 (ACTA2) showed a significant treatment effect (vehicle > LSKL, p < 0.001), with notably lower expression in the LSKL group under AP and NP conditions (both p < 0.001) (Figure 2).

Epidermal and Dermal Thickening Under Negative‐Pressure

Histological analysis using HE staining revealed significantly greater epidermal and dermal thickness in NP‐treated skin compared to both control and AP‐treated skin (all p < 0.001) (Figure 3b).

Hypoxic and Prothrombotic Responses to Negative‐Pressure

IHC analysis showed significantly increased epidermal expression of PAI‐1 in the NP group compared to the control and AP groups (p < 0.001). The number of HIF‐1α‐positive cells per mm2 was also significantly higher under NP conditions (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the number of fibrinogen‐positive vessels per mm2 in the dermis significantly increased in the NP‐treated group (p < 0.001) (Figure 3c). Consistently, ELISA demonstrated significantly higher protein levels of HIF‐1α (p = 0.004 vs. control, p = 0.001 vs. AP) and PAI‐1 (p < 0.001) in the NP‐treated skin samples compared to control and AP groups (Figure 4).

Angiogenesis Enhancement Under Negative‐Pressure Conditions

IHC analysis demonstrated significantly increased density of CD31‐positive vessels per mm2 in the dermis of the NP‐treated group compared to the other groups (p < 0.001) (Figure 3d). In agreement, ELISA analysis showed significantly increased VEGF protein levels in the NP‐treated group (p < 0.001) (Figure 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that NP loading is associated with EMT‐like changes and angiogenic responses in intact diabetic skin. Our investigation began with preliminary protein screening using a membrane antibody array, which identified increased expression of THBS1 and PAI‐1 in keratinocyte cultures exposed to NP. Both proteins are associated with EMT, a process closely linked to tissue remodelling and fibrosis. Based on these initial findings, we focused our subsequent analyses on factors related to EMT and angiogenesis to better understand the biological effects of NPWT, particularly on intact skin without wounds. Notably, these molecular changes were observed not only in vivo but also in monocultured human keratinocytes. Our cellular model consisted of monolayer cultures exposed to low‐pressure conditions in liquid media without direct physical contact with foam [33]. This approach excluded confounding factors such as microdeformation and immune responses typically associated with standard NPWT. Such conditions strongly suggest that epidermal responses observed primarily reflected the effects of NP rather than secondary consequences of foreign‐body reactions. Amongst the proteins differentially expressed, THBS1 was of particular interest as it has recently been identified as a mechanotransduction molecule linking mechanical stimuli to biochemical responses [36, 37]. Its role in conditions such as aneurysm formation [38] and osteogenesis [26] has been reported previously. Our findings suggest it may similarly mediate mechanical stress during NPWT.

Previous research has primarily focused on the beneficial effects of NPWT, such as promoting granulation tissue formation, enhancing local blood flow and reducing bacterial loads [39]. However, the potential of NPWT to induce adverse epithelial changes has received comparatively limited attention. Nuutila et al. [40] investigated gene expression profiles at skin graft donor sites treated with NPWT in burn patients, reporting a unique epithelial gene expression pattern compared to conventionally treated wounds. Although NPWT accelerated early‐phase epithelialization, their data suggested possible impairment in long‐term epithelial recovery due to disrupted differentiation. Our findings potentially provide a mechanistic explanation for this observation, as EMT is typically characterised by compromised epithelial integrity and function [27, 41].

During EMT, epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics, playing essential roles in embryonic development, wound healing and cancer progression [42, 43]. In skin wound healing specifically, EMT contributes to re‐epithelialization from wound edges, scar formation and subsequent fibrosis [27, 28]. TGF‐β1, a crucial EMT inducer, is vital for wound repair and tissue regeneration [26, 29, 30]. THBS1, an ECM glycoprotein, is a primary physiological activator of TGF‐β1, regulating EMT through the TGF‐β1/SMAD pathway [26, 44]. In skin, THBS1‐positive keratinocytes facilitate epidermal wound healing [45]. Consistent with these established roles, our study demonstrated increased expression of EMT‐related proteins, including THBS1, TGF‐β1, PAI‐1 and HIF‐1α, following NP loading in vitro and in vivo (Figures 1a and 4). Furthermore, EMT marker analysis in human keratinocytes revealed decreased epithelial markers and increased mesenchymal markers upon NP treatment (Figures 1b and 2). These molecular changes were notably suppressed by the peptide inhibitor LSKL (Figure 2), which specifically blocks THBS1‐mediated activation of TGF‐β1 [46], indicating a central role for THBS1 in NP‐induced EMT. These findings align with previous studies reporting similar protein expression patterns in response to NP conditions [9].

Furthermore, the NP environment may mimic pathophysiological conditions observed in pressure ulcers (PU), where tissue ischemia and microthrombosis are critical factors. Kaneko et al. [34] demonstrated that the HIF‐1α‐PAI‐1 axis contributes significantly to thrombus formation and subsequent tissue damage in PU. Although PAI‐1 is involved in normal wound repair, excessive expression of this protein promotes scar proliferation, tissue fibrosis, and impairs keratinocyte migration [25, 47, 48]. In our study, NP loading resulted in increased expression of HIF‐1α and PAI‐1, formation of fibrinogen‐positive vessels, and thickening of skin layers (Figures 1a, 3b,c, 4), suggesting hypoxia driven tissue remodelling. Collectively, these results indicate that NPWT‐induced epithelial changes may result from multiple interrelated mechanisms, including EMT‐driven structural destabilisation and ischemia‐mediated microvascular injury, partially resembling aspects of PU pathogenesis.

One of the most established mechanisms of NPWT is its capacity to enhance local blood flow [49, 50]. This occurs due to the pressure difference between the internal and external areas of the dressing, which compresses the wound surface and subsequently induces angiogenesis via the HIF‐1α‐VEGF signalling pathway in response to tissue ischemia [31, 32]. This angiogenic effect is widely recognised as central to the therapeutic efficacy of NPWT [49, 50]. Building on this principle, a novel strategy termed preconditioning NPWT has emerged, whereby NP is applied to intact skin prior to flap surgery to improve subsequent flap perfusion [51, 52]. In contrast to traditional NPWT methods that target open wounds, this preconditioning approach, similar to ciNPT, specifically targets intact skin. In our experiments, NP loading significantly enhanced the expression of HIF‐1α and VEGF and promoted CD31‐positive vessel formation in diabetic mouse skin (Figures 1a, 3c,d, 4). Similar angiogenic responses were also observed in vitro using keratinocyte monolayer cultures, suggesting that NPWT can promote angiogenesis through the HIF‐1α‐VEGF axis even in intact skin. These findings reinforce the potential use of NPWT as a non‐surgical preconditioning technique to enhance flap viability [51, 52]. Nevertheless, applying NPWT directly to healthy skin, as in ciNPT or preconditioning methods, raises concerns regarding potential adverse effects, particularly epithelial disruption related to EMT processes. Given our previous findings that NPWT enhances keratinocyte migration [33], the current results provide additional mechanistic insight supporting the beneficial role of NPWT in skin graft stabilisation and flap survival, particularly in postoperative skin‐grafting scenarios [53].

Ranieri et al. [54] reported enhanced EMT in keratinocytes under simulated microgravity conditions. Our findings extend this observation, demonstrating that EMT‐like changes can occur even under the relatively mild mechanical stress of approximately 0.1–0.15 atm, as typically applied in clinical NPWT. This suggests that even low‐intensity mechanical cues may be sufficient to trigger epithelial plasticity. To our knowledge, this study is amongst the first to propose a potential link between NPWT and EMT, highlighting important implications for both therapeutic benefits and possible adverse effects of NPWT on intact skin.

A key limitation of our study is the mismatch between the in vitro and in vivo models: human keratinocytes from non‐diabetic donors were used for the in vitro experiments, whereas diabetic mice were employed in the in vivo studies. Nevertheless, consistent changes in EMT‐ and angiogenesis‐related markers were observed across both models, suggesting partial conservation of keratinocyte responses to mechanical stress. Systemic factors associated with diabetes, however, may still influence the magnitude and regulation of these responses. Diabetic skin exhibits chronic inflammation, increased fibrosis and impaired tissue repair [22, 23, 24], all of which potentially influence keratinocyte behaviour, including EMT, under mechanical stress conditions. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemic conditions can modulate EMT in keratinocytes [55]. Future studies should therefore incorporate diabetic keratinocytes in vitro and apply NPWT to non‐diabetic skin in vivo to comprehensively assess how disease status modifies the cellular response to NP.

Additionally, Nuutila et al. [40] suggested that keratinocyte behaviour under NPWT evolves over time. Further studies are thus required to clarify temporal aspects of NPWT‐induced EMT, particularly to distinguish beneficial epithelialization effects from potential epithelial damage arising from prolonged NPWT application. Such insights would be valuable for optimising NPWT protocols, enabling clinicians to maximise wound healing efficacy whilst minimising potential adverse effects.

Collectively, our findings reveal the dual biological effects of NPWT on intact diabetic skin, emphasising its capability to promote vascular remodelling and epithelial mobilisation, as well as its potential to induce pathological tissue remodelling through EMT. These insights can inform safer and more effective clinical applications of NPWT, including ciNPT, flap preconditioning and post‐grafting care. Furthermore, a deeper understanding of epithelial plasticity in response to mechanical stimuli will be essential for refining NPWT protocols. Achieving an optimal balance between regenerative benefits and the risk of epithelial disruption will ultimately ensure safer, more effective patient care across various clinical settings.
