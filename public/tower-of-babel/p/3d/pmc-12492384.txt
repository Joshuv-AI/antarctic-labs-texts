An Online Buffer Exchange Platform for Charge Detection Mass Spectrometry Analysis of AAVs and AAV-Antibody Complexes
Authors: Chen Du, Victoria C. Cotham, Garima Thakur, Sheldon Mink, Andrew D. Tustian, Sven Møller-Tank, Shunhai Wang, Ning Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) are leading vectors in gene therapy that have demonstrated great potential in combating a wide range of human diseases. To enhance specificity and reduce dosing requirements, antibody-retargeted AAVs have emerged as a promising strategy to redirect vectors to novel receptors, thereby achieving improved efficacy and safety. However, effective characterization of AAVs and AAV-antibody complexes is complicated by heterogeneities that arise from variations in capsid protein assembly, genome integrity, and antibody decorations, demanding high-resolution techniques beyond traditional methods. Charge detection mass spectrometry (CDMS) is an emerging technique that effectively characterizes complex AAV samples by directly measuring ion charge. This technique is typically performed using static infusion-based nanoelectrospray ionization with long acquisition times, which is highly manual and subject to unstable spray and sample instability. To address limitations of static infusion in CDMS, we have developed a novel automated platform: size-exclusion chromatography-based flow injection coupled with CDMS (SEC-FI-CDMS). This platform streamlines and automates sample introduction with a dual-flow setup, enabling fast online desalting in high-flow mode and stable, prolonged infusion in low-flow mode for Orbitrap-based CDMS analysis. It accurately determines AAV genome packaging ratios and masses, differentiates capsid assembly and payload variations, and resolves antibody decoration on AAV-antibody complexes. This comprehensive characterization supports AAV manufacturing and development, paving the way for more effective next generation gene therapies.

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is a nonpathogenic virus that serves as an effective vector for delivering therapeutic genes into various host cells. Recombinant AAVs (rAAVs) are actively being used as gene therapy and vaccine delivery systems for a range of diseases, with several FDA-approved AAV therapies currently available. However, their widespread application is often hindered by issues such as broad tropism and pre-existing immunity in patients. To address those challenges, innovative strategies have been developed to reprogram these vectors for selective targeting of specific tissues. One such strategy involves AAV-antibody conjugation or complexation, which allows for the precise attachment of antibodies to AAV capsids. , This method redirects the vectors to novel cell-surface receptors, thereby enhancing the precision of gene therapy interventions and minimizing immune responses. The development of antibody-retargeted AAVs primarily focuses on modifying AAV capsid proteins, screening antibodies, and optimizing the binding reaction. The number of antibody decorations is known to alter transduction efficiency, making it essential to determine not only the averaged AAV-antibody binding level but also the distribution of the antibodies on individual capsids. Proper distribution and optimal decoration levels are critical for ensuring effective targeting and maximizing the therapeutic potential of antibody-retargeted AAVs, allowing for reduced dosing, and thus minimizing AAV-associated toxicities.

Characterizing AAVs, especially AAV-antibody complexes, is challenging due to the intrinsic heterogeneity of the capsid composition and genome integrity. The AAV capsid is composed of 60 viral proteins (VPs) at an estimated 1:1:10 ratio, contributing to a total mass exceeding 3.8 MDa. Current recombinant AAV production methods can result in over 70% empty capsids, and DNA truncations may lead to partial AAVs, highlighting the importance of assessing the quality and purity of manufactured AAVs. Anion exchange chromatography (AEX) is widely used for the purification and enrichment of full AAV particles, but the application of analytical AEX for accurate genome packaging quantification is limited due to the poor resolution between partial and full capsids. Assays like analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) and mass photometry (MP) are commonly used in assessing the relative abundance of the empty, partial and full capsids. Nevertheless, these techniques do not possess the resolution necessary to resolve the distribution of antibody decoration at single particle level. Recent advances in mass spectrometry, particularly native MS, allows large noncovalently bound assemblies to be transferred from solution into the gas phase, providing insights into stoichiometry, population distribution, and the efficiency of antibody coupling reactions of the complex biological assembles. , However, due to compositional heterogeneity, arising from varying VP ratios and post-translational modifications (PTMs), intact AAV particles cannot be directly resolved using conventional native MS, resulting in broad spectra without charge state resolution or mass information. While conventional native MS struggles with this complexity, charge detection mass spectrometry (CDMS) provides a more effective means of characterizing these complex systems. −

CDMS is a single ion technique that directly measures ion mass by simultaneously determining the frequency (mass-to-charge ratio, m/z) and charge (z) of ions. Many of the advancements in CDMS were initially accomplished using custom-built electrostatic linear ion traps, , and the recent adaptation of Orbitrap mass spectrometers for CDMS has expanded the accessibility of charge detection on commercial instruments. , This method has proven particularly powerful for assessing large or highly heterogeneous samples, like AAVs, − where charge states cannot be resolved, making conventional MS techniques inadequate for mass interpretation.

To generate statistically informative CDMS measurements, it is necessary to collect hundreds to thousands of individual ion scans, which often require data acquisition over long periods of time. Traditionally, this is achieved using variations of static nano infusion, such as applying emitter tips pulled from borosilicate glass tubes. A major benefit to static infusion is that it avoids sample dilution and produces very small droplets for sensitive detection. However, samples must be buffer-exchanged into MS-compatible solvents before analysis, which can lead to precipitation or aggregation in AAV samples. Another practical limitation is that these approaches are subject to frequent tip clogging, requiring reoptimization of spray conditions with each tip change, and leading to acquisition ″dead zones″, where no meaningful CDMS data is collected. Additionally, these techniques are highly manual and demand a highly trained analyst, restricting their use to laboratories with the necessary expertise and equipment. Several efforts have been made to integrate CDMS with online separation techniques. − However, the number of ions that can be captured within the chromatography timeframes is limited, potentially compromising the quality of spectra, particularly for large and complex molecules, such as AAVs. Altogether, this highlights the need for developing a more robust and automated sample introduction system that can support extended infusion times for CDMS.

In this work, we have addressed many of the limitations of conventional static infusion through the development of a novel automated sample introduction platform: size-exclusion chromatography-based flow injection (SEC-FI). This platform combines the principles of native online buffer exchange (OBE) , with extended loop trapping and low-flow infusion to achieve stable, prolonged sample introduction for Orbitrap-based CDMS analysis. When coupled with CDMS, this platform provides a sensitive and robust approach for analyzing AAVs and AAV-antibody complexes. Leveraging the extended and high-quality CDMS data acquisition, this platform can effectively quantify AAV genome packaging ratios and provides precise mass measurements, crucial for assessing viral particle integrity. Compared to other techniques, the high-resolution mass measurement from CDMS facilitates the identification of subtle variations in capsid assembly stoichiometry due to process changes and resolves dual vector AAVs with minor differences in genome length. Furthermore, the SEC-FI-CDMS platform has been explored to resolve and quantify antibody decorations on AAV-antibody complexes, clearly distinguishing individual AAVs with different numbers of noncovalently bound antibodies. The unique charge detection capability in CDMS provides valuable insights into the surface charge of AAV-antibody complexes. This comprehensive characterization of AAV-antibody noncovalent complexes highlights the platform as an improved analytical tool for advancing the development of next-generation human gene therapies that are safer and more effective.

Experimental Section

Materials

All AAVs and anti-AAV mAbs were produced and purified at Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc. (Tarrytown, NY). Ammonium acetate (LC-MS grade) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). 2-Propanol (IPA; HPLC grade) was purchased from Honeywell (Muskegon, MI). Deionized water used in sample and buffer preparation was provided by a Milli-Q integral water purification system equipped with a MilliPak Express 20 filter (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA). Pyruvate kinase (PK) from rabbit muscle, glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) from bovine liver, and β-Galactosidase (β-Gal) from Escherichia coli were purchased from Millipore Sigma (St. Louis, MO). GroEL from Escherichia coli was also purchased from Millipore Sigma and refolded with the presence of ATP using a published protocol.

Instrument Setup of SEC-FI

The online buffer exchange flow injection was achieved through a dual high/low flow configuration on an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with both a high flow loading pump and a low flow nanopump. Native SEC chromatography of AAVs was performed on a custom Acquity BEH200 SEC column (1.0 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm, 200 Å; Waters, Milford, MA) with the column compartment set to 30 °C. A 60 μL sample loop was added between a six-port valve and a splitting tee to trap the SEC-desalted analytes.(a)High flow configuration: the loading pump is directly coupled to the SEC column to enable online native MS analysis under higher flow conditions (20 μL/min) as shown in Figure A. The high flow was split into a microflow (<10 μL/min) for nanoelectrospray ionization (nESI)-MS detection using a postcolumn splitting tee that diverted the remaining flow to a viper waste line (20 μm × 15 cm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). A native MS survey run is usually performed to evaluate the desalting efficiency and determine the appropriate time for valve switching to trap the desalted analytes in the sample loop.(b)Low flow configuration: once the valve is switched, the nanopump is directly connected to the extended sample loop and facilitates a stable spray with flow rates as low as 1 μL/min shown in Figure B. The flow is unsplit with the viper tubing connecting to a viper plug to provide constant back pressure to maintain stable spray. With a 60 μL sample loop, approximately 60 min of infusion time is achieved, allowing for CDMS data acquisition.

Online nMS and CDMS Analysis

The online nMS and CDMS analysis were performed on a Thermo Q Exactive UHMR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a microflow-nanospray electrospray ionization (MnESI) source and a microfabricated monolithic multinozzle (M3) emitter (Newomics, Berkeley, CA). For instrument parameters, spray voltage was 3.0 kV, capillary temperature was set to 400 °C and the S-lens RF level was 200. The ion transfer target m/z and detector optimization were set to “high m/z”. In-source trapping was enabled with a desolvation voltage of −50 V and a source DC offset of −50 V. Trapping gas pressure readout was in the range of 3e-10 to 6 e-10 mbar. Native MS data were acquired at 25K at m/z 400 resolution settings with transient averaging enabled. Online SEC-FI-CDMS spectra were acquired using Direct Mass Technology (DMT) mode with most of the tuning parameters the same as for native MS. The resolution was set at 25K at m/z 400 with transient averaging disabled. The injection time was manually set to a fixed value between 500 and 1000 ms to minimize the occurrence of double ions at the elution apex.

MS Data Analysis

CDMS data sets were processed using Proteinaceous STORIboard with frequency correction function developed by Thermo Fisher Scientific. Data for noncovalent AAV-antibody complexes were further processed using UniDecCD and detailed settings are listed in Supporting Information.

CDMS Charge Calibration

To establish CDMS charge calibration, online SEC-FI-CDMS analysis of the protein standards was performed in a similar setup using an Acquity BEH200 SEC column (4.6 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm, 200 Å) and 200 μL sample loop. A dopant-modified desolvation gas (2 L/min, nitrogen), achieved by passing the gas flow through the headspace of a DURAN pressure plus bottle (1 L; SCHOTT North America, Inc., Elmsford, NY) containing 200 mL of 2-propanol modifiers was applied to assist the MnESI as described previously. CDMS acquisition time was over 120 min to ensure a reliable fitting of charge calibration coefficient. Automatic ion control (AIC) was used to account for variations in ion flux across the elution profile. The calibration slope coefficients were determined from linear calibration curves derived from the five calibrants, with variation of less than 2%, as shown in Figure S1.

Results and Discussion

Coupling SEC-FI to CDMS for Sensitive and Accurate AAV Analysis

The SEC-FI platform streamlines and automates the sample introduction by using a dual high/low-flow configuration. In high flow mode, efficient SEC-based online desalting is achieved, which eliminates the need for prior offline buffer exchange into mass spectrometry (MS) compatible solvents. This approach streamlines the workflow from buffer exchange to MS detection, reducing the risk of AAV aggregation or precipitation that can occur when the formulation buffer is removed for extended periods. A native MS survey run is typically performed to evaluate the desalting efficiency and determine the appropriate time for valve switching to trap the desalted sample in the extended sample loop. After switching the valve, the nanopump was directly connected to the sample loop, enabling the low flow configuration. A continuous and stable spray with flow rates as low as 1 μL/min was achieved using Newomics multinozzle emitter, effectively mitigating clogging issues due to the presence of multiple nozzles. , These low flow conditions facilitate stable and robust sample infusion ranging from approximately 30 min to several hours, thus allowing for extended and high-quality Orbitrap-based CDMS analysis (Figure A).

(B) The CDMS spectra of an empty capsid-enriched AAV8 sample with injection amount of 1285 ng, 129 ng, and 13 ng, respectively. (C) SEC-FI-CDMS spectrum of an AAV1 sample. (D) SEC-FI-CDMS spectrum of an AAV5 sample.

When the platform was first applied for analyzing AAVs using a regular SEC column (4.6 × 150 mm) and a larger sample loop (200 μL), it faced challenges due to low signal intensity and ion counts. This suggested that the samples were significantly diluted under the standard configuration used for initial CDMS charge calibration with protein standards. To address this issue and minimize dilution, modifications were made to the setup. Specifically, a smaller SEC column with dimensions of 1.0 × 50 mm was employed, along with a reduced sample loop size of 60 μL. These adjustments not only achieved efficient desalting but also considerably increased the effective ion counts, leading to improved analytical performance for AAV analysis.

With the optimized platform, the ability to detect and analyze AAVs at low concentrations was evaluated by varying the injection amounts of an empty capsid-enriched AAV8 sample (>98% empty capsids by AUC). For each sample, 5 μL was injected, with titers ranging from 3E11 to 3E13 capsids/ml, and corresponding viral protein concentrations ranging from 0.00257 mg/mL to 0.257 mg/mL. Figure S2 illustrates that both native MS and CDMS responses exhibit a strong linear correlation with the injection amount. Notably, the platform successfully detected and accurately determined the mass of empty AAV8 capsids even at a low titer of 3E11 capsids/ml, as shown in Figure B. Given the often low concentrations of AAV samples, the platform’s high sensitivity underscores its effectiveness and great potential in supporting AAV development, providing mass measurements of viral particles with limited sample amount, as low as 13 ng (or 1.5E9 capsids).

With sufficient signal detected for AAVs using SEC-FI, the CDMS mass accuracy and resolution was improved by optimizing the source conditions to accommodate flow injection-based sample introduction and engaging frequency correction in data processing. The distorted and asymmetric peak shapes were initially observed in individual ion spectra. This suggests that frequency shifts occur during Orbitrap measurement, likely due to higher solvation levels in SEC-FI, given its higher flow rate of 1 μL/min compared to sub-nL/min in conventional static infusion. Optimization of the flow injection source conditions, particularly the removal of sheath gas, improved peak shapes to near-Gaussian distribution (Figure S3A). Additionally, the frequency correction function in STORIboard was utilized to address the remaining frequency discrepancies during single ion measurements, as shown in Figure S3B. This led to more accurate mass assignments and better-resolved peaks in CDMS spectra, which are essential for precisely identifying and quantifying mixtures of viral particles. Following these optimizations, the mass accuracy and resolving capability of CDMS measurements were accessed using various AAV serotypes, particularly those with higher levels of partial capsid contamination. As detailed in Table S1, the masses derived from CDMS measurements aligned well with the predicted values calculated using estimated VP ratios and sequence masses of VPs and DNA genome. In conventional native mass spectra, partial capsids are typically difficult to distinguish while CDMS offers clearer identification of partial capsid species, as depicted in Figures C and D. The quantification of partial capsids is consistent with the trend measured by AUC (Table S1), which provides resolution of partial capsids but is limited by low throughput and high sample consumption. The ability of the platform to detect and quantify partial capsids enhances the quality and purity assessment of manufactured AAVs, ensuring that AAV products are compliant with regulatory standards for clinical applications.

Quantifying Empty/Full AAV Ratios

The quantification performance of the SEC-FI-CDMS platform was further evaluated to determine empty/full (E/F) ratio of AAVs using the dual-flow configurations. High-flow conditions allow for fast assessment of the E/F ratios using the distinct m/z clusters from the native survey scans and more precise quantification can be achieved based on the CDMS spectra obtained in the low-flow mode. To evaluate this, a full capsid-enriched AAV8 sample was spiked into an empty capsid-enriched AAV8 sample at a series of mixing ratios. This resulted in a mixture of empty and full capsids with the full capsid percentage ranging from 0% to 89%, as calculated using known AUC results (Table S2). In Figure A, the native MS spectra of the five mixed capsid samples acquired under high-flow conditions show the signal clusters of the empty and full capsids in the range of m/z 20000–25000 and 26500–32500, respectively. The E/F ratios can be quickly estimated by integrating the area under the curve of these distinct m/z signal clusters in the native MS spectra, similar to a previous report. More precise quantification can be achieved through the direct mass measurement as shown in Figure B. This is achieved by integrating the capsid peak areas from CDMS spectra obtained under low-flow conditions. The relative abundance of full capsids was highly comparable between native MS spectra using the high-flow configuration and CDMS spectra under low-flow mode, as detailed in Table S2. The measured E/F ratios from two flow configurations are validated by comparing these results with the established benchmarks derived from AUC analysis. The measurements using SEC-FI-CDMS platform exhibited strong linear correlations with AUC (Figure S4), highlighting the accuracy and reliability of both methods for quantifying empty/full capsid ratios.

(A) Native mass spectra generated using the high-flow configuration; (B) CDMS spectra obtained using the low-flow configuration for varying ratios of empty and full AAV8 capsids. The expected percentage of full capsids was calculated based on known AUC data. In addition to the unbiased E/F ratio assessment, the CDMS under low-flow mode also provide precise mass measurements and detailed charge information. The CDMS spectra of the five mixed capsid samples reveal mass distributions for empty capsids in the range of 3.5–4.2 MDa and for full capsids in the range of 4.8–5.3 MDa. The masses calculated from the CDMS measurements are in good alignment with the predicted masses, as listed in the Table S2. The mass measurements of the empty and full AAV8 capsids could be reproduced in triplicate with a minimal coefficient of variation of less than 0.6%. This small percentage indicates high repeatability of CDMS measurement using the SEC-FI platform. Furthermore, a similar charge state range from 165+ to 185+ was observed for both empty and full AAVs (Figure S6), suggesting that the surface charges of the capsids remain similar despite the addition of the genome in the full capsids, aligning well with previously published studies. , Overall, the SEC-FI-CDMS platform demonstrates remarkable capacity in delivering reliable E/F quantification of AAVs, while also providing accurate mass measurements. These capabilities are crucial for monitoring the integrity and consistency of AAV products, supporting their successful development.

Probing Variations in Capsid Assembly Stoichiometry and Resolving Dual Vector AAVs with Different Genome Lengths

Capsid assembly stoichiometry is an important quality attribute during the development of AAV-based gene therapy programs. However, due to the stochastic nature of the assembling process, the capsid protein stoichiometry may be subjected to variations due to process changes during AAV production, necessitating rigorous monitoring. To demonstrate the utility, the SEC-FI-CDMS platform was applied to compare AAV8 capsids produced from two manufacturing processes. As described elsewhere, mass analysis at the intact VP-level by hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (HILIC-MS) revealed that AAV8 capsids manufactured from process A (AAV8-Process A) have a slightly higher abundance of VP1 and VP2 proteins compared to those from process B (AAV8-Proecess B). This difference in VP protein ratio results in a theoretical mass increase of 80 kDa for empty capsids of AAV8-Process A compared to those in AAV8-Process B, as listed in Table S3. When these capsids are fully loaded with the same genome, a similar mass shift is expected for the full capsids. This difference in VP composition is also reflected at the intact capsid level and depicted in the CDMS spectra as shown in Figure A. Higher average masses were observed for both empty and full capsids of AAV8-Process A, as compared to those from AAV8-Process B. Empty capsids of AAV8-Process A were found to be 24 kDa higher than those from AAV8-Process B. For full capsids, which were the predominant species in both samples, the mass difference was more pronounced at 93 kDa, aligning better with the expected mass shift based on the VP ratio measurement. As a minor impurity, the empty capsids exhibited smaller-than-expected mass difference between the two processes, suggesting the downstream process parameters (e.g., purification steps) may also contribute to the selective enrichment of empty capsids with specific stoichiometries; however, further validation is necessary. Thus, one single VP ratio may be insufficient to represent the composition of the entire population of capsids. The ability of SEC-FI-CDMS in probing subtle mass changes arising from variations in capsid assembly stoichiometry provides in-depth insights into capsid heterogeneity and enables comprehensive comparability assessment to support AAV process development.

(A) CDMS spectra of AAV8 capsids produced from two manufacturing processes (Process A: blue trace; Process B: red trace). Dual vector AAV-based gene therapy approaches, which allow for encoding larger transgenes, have shown great promise in treating various diseases in preclinical and clinical studies. For coformulated dual vector AAVs, resolving the two AAV components, which often differ only slightly in genome length, presents an elevated challenge for routine analytical techniques. To test the capability of SEC-FI-CDMS, the platform was utilized to distinguish between two AAV1 capsids packaged with different DNA payloads, as illustrated by the CDMS spectra in Figure B. Specifically, the two AAV1 vectors were engineered to deliver a gene of interest (GOI), with one vector designed to express the 5′ portion of the GOI (4.2 kb, AAV1-5′) while the other vector to express the 3′ segment of the GOI (4.6 kb, AAV1-3′). Unlike AAV8 vectors from different manufacturing processes, the empty capsids of these two AAV1 vectors displayed similar masses. As detailed in Table S4, the mass variation between the empty AAV1 capsids was within the error range of triplicate measurements, indicating similar capsid assembly for both vectors. A notable mass difference of 94 kDa was identified between the full capsids of the AAV1-3′ and AAV1-5′ vectors. This mass discrepancy aligns with the ∼400 nucleobase size disparity (∼130 kDa) between the 3′ and 5′ payloads. In addition to identifying the AAV1-3′ and AAV1-5′ separately, these vectors were mixed in a 1:1 genome ratio to mimic the final drug product and evaluated using the SEC-FI-CDMS platform. As shown by the gray trace in Figure B, the empty capsids display a narrow mass profile similar to that of their individual components, whereas full capsids exhibit a significantly broader mass profile, suggesting the presence of two distinct payload components. Furthermore, the individual components containing the 3′ and 5′ payloads within the full capsids were effectively distinguished and partially resolved, with their masses aligning with those of the individual components. Additionally, by employing a simple Gaussian peak fitting-based deconvolution strategy, the ratio of 3′ and 5′ vectors can be readily calculated, which aligns well with the expected value (Figure S7). Resolving such a mass difference (∼130 kDa) on AAV capsids is very challenging using other analytical methods. This unique capability to resolve dual vector AAVs highlights the great potential of SEC-FI-CDMS to support the development of next-generation AAV gene therapies.

Resolving Antibody Decorations on AAV-Antibody Complexes

The application of the SEC-FI-CDMS platform was further extended to the analysis of more complicated AAV-antibody complexes. Using as a model system, the noncovalent AAV-antibody complexes were prepared by mixing and incubating an empty capsid-enriched AAV8 sample with an anti-AAV monoclonal antibody (mAb) at six different mixing ratios. The CDMS spectra, as shown in Figure A, reveal clear mass shifts upon mAb binding, with each spectrum displaying distinct numbers of antibody decorations. A closer examination highlights a sequential mass addition of approximately 150 kDa, corresponding well to the mass of the anti-AAV mAb (Figure S8 and Table S5). As the concentration of mAb increased, the predominant number of antibody decorations on each capsid also increased. At the highest mAb concentration, up to 12 antibody decorations were identified, which aligns well with MALS (multiangle light scattering)-based mass measurement (data not shown), indicating full saturation of the binding sites at this mAb concentration. The broad distribution of antibody decorations at a given mixing ratio suggests a high level of heterogeneity arising from the binding process (Figure S9), which may be affected by both the intrinsic AAV-antibody binding equilibrium and the variations in AAV binding sites. Nevertheless, the ability to measure the distribution of antibody decorations using this method represents a major advancement over conventional analytical techniques.

Characterizing antibody decorations of AAV-antibody complexes by SEC-FI-CDMS. (A) CDMS spectra of AAV-antibody complexes at varying mixing ratios. (B) Charge vs mass heatmaps of AAV-antibody complexes at varying mixing ratios.

In addition to revealing the distribution of antibody decorations, CDMS provides an additional layer of information on charge states of virus particles, which often correlate with the surface area of macromolecular assemblies. Charge versus mass heat maps corresponding to the AAV-antibody complexes at various ratios are shown in Figure B. Intriguingly, a slight decrease in the charge states of the capsid was observed upon the initial mAb binding, but the average charge state increased with subsequent mAb additions. The observed charge profile aligns well with previous studies showing antibody-induced surface charge changes on AAV. Molecular modeling of the interaction between the anti-AAV mAb and AAV8 suggests that binding is primarily driven by electrostatic interactions. Upon complex formation, such electrostatic interactions may induce minor rearrangement of AAV local structures, likely explaining the observed initial decrease in surface charge (data not shown). Figure S10 illustrates the linear correlation between the average number of antibody additions per capsid and the average charge states of the mAb-decorated capsids. Despite the initial decrease, the sequential antibody bindings resulted in an increase in averaged charge states of the AAV capsids by approximately 2.2 charges per antibody addition. As more antibodies bind to the exterior of the virus capsid, they effectively enlarge the overall size and surface area of the capsid, thereby increasing the overall charge. The increase in charge with additional antibody binding reflects the cumulative effect of these interactions, providing insights into the structural dynamics and stability of the virus-antibody complex. It is worth noting that an empty capsid-enriched AAV sample was used for this proof-of-concept study to remove the data complexity potentially introduced by the genome heterogeneity. It is anticipated that resolving antibody decorations on full capsids will be more challenging. Application of ultralong transient Orbitrap CDMS data acquisition, as recently introduced by Deslignière et al., may provide necessary resolution to improve this analysis.

Conclusion

The combination of SEC-based flow injection with CDMS provides a robust, sensitive and accurate platform for analyzing AAVs and AAV-antibody complexes. This innovative approach streamlines the process by facilitating online buffer exchange and automated sample infusion for Orbitrap-based CDMS measurements. By eliminating the need for offline buffer exchange, the platform minimizes the risk of aggregation, precipitation, and tip clogging, particularly for complicated biological samples. Optimized for AAV analysis, the platform has demonstrated high sensitivity, remarkable repeatability, and good mass accuracy. The ability to detect and quantify empty, partial, and full capsids significantly enhances the assessment of AAV quality and purity, making it an invaluable tool for monitoring the integrity and consistency of AAV-based products. This platform’s application extends to examining minor differences arising from variations in capsid assembly stoichiometry and resolving dual vector AAVs with different genome length, providing valuable insights into AAV process and product development. Furthermore, the platform successfully resolves antibody decorations on AAV-antibody complexes, confirming binding stoichiometry and saturation levels. In addition to revealing the distribution of antibody decorations on AAV-antibody complexes, this approach also offers complementary insights into the effects of antibody binding on particle charge states and potential structural changes. With growing interest in AAV and retargeted AAV-based gene therapies, this novel platform is anticipated to become a valuable addition to our analytical toolbox, aiding the design and optimization of next-generation gene therapy treatments.
