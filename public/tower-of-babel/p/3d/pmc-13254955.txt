Percutaneous antegrade-retrograde recanalization (rendezvous technique) of a complete ureteropelvic junction disruption after robotic-assisted partial nephrectomy: A case report
Authors: Motoki Fujita, Toshiki Kijima, Hirotaka Fuchizawa, Gaku Nakamura, Toshitaka Uematsu, Kohei Takei, Shigeyoshi Soga, Takao Kamai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary fistula is a known complication of partial nephrectomy (PN), but complete disruption of the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) is extremely rare. Surgical reconstruction in a post-operative field is challenging due to scarring and adhesions. We present a case of complete UPJ disruption following robotic-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) that was successfully managed with a minimally invasive interventional radiology (IVR) technique. A 48-year-old female underwent RAPN for a 5.5 cm renal tumor in the right lower pole. Two months post-operatively, she developed a urinary fistula and was found to have a complete UPJ disruption. Due to the high risk associated with surgical revision, a combined antegrade-retrograde recanalization was planned in collaboration with interventional radiologists. Nine months after the initial surgery, under general anesthesia, a guidewire was advanced from the ureter and successfully punctured the renal pelvis under fluoroscopic guidance. The wire was captured in the renal pelvis using a snare and externalized through a pre-existing nephrostomy tract (rendezvous technique). A double-J stent was subsequently placed across the newly created tract. The patient has been followed for 2 years with regular stent exchanges every 6 months, and has maintained good renal function without evidence of hydronephrosis or recurrent fistula. A combined antegrade-retrograde IVR approach is a feasible and effective minimally invasive treatment for complete UPJ disruption following PN, particularly in cases where surgical reconstruction is deemed high-risk. At 2 years of follow-up, renal function has been fully preserved, demonstrating that this technique offers a valuable kidney-sparing alternative to nephrectomy.

Introduction

Nephron-sparing surgery (NSS), or partial nephrectomy (PN), is the standard of care for many T1 renal masses [1]. While robotic-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) has been shown to have a lower risk of urinary fistula compared to open or laparoscopic approaches, this complication still occurs in approximately 0.75%-4.4% of cases [2,3]. Most urinary fistulas can be managed conservatively with ureteral stenting or percutaneous drainage. However, delayed ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction is a much rarer complication, with an estimated incidence of 0.35% after PN [4].

The standard treatment for UPJ obstruction is surgical reconstruction, such as pyeloplasty, which has high success rates of over 94% [5,6]. However, re-operation in a post-operative field with significant scarring carries a high risk of complications. Endourological approaches, such as the "cut-to-the-light" technique or laser incision, have also been described for severe ureteral strictures [7,8]. However, these techniques rely on the presence of a residual, albeit narrow, lumen to guide the incision, and are therefore not applicable to cases of complete luminal obliteration or physical disruption.

Here, we report a case of complete UPJ disruption following RAPN, in which the renal pelvis was entirely disconnected from the ureter with no residual lumen. This was successfully treated with a minimally invasive interventional radiology (IVR) technique involving a combined antegrade-retrograde rendezvous approach to recanalize the obliterated segment.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old female with no significant past medical history, including no history of tuberous sclerosis complex, was incidentally found to have a right renal tumor on a health screening. She was not on any regular medications. Baseline renal function was normal (serum creatinine 0.52 mg/dL, eGFR 97.7 mL/min/1.73m², BUN 12.7 mg/dL). Imaging studies revealed a 55×45×50 mm mass in the lower pole of the right kidney. The patient elected to undergo RAPN. The procedure was completed successfully, and the pathological diagnosis confirmed angiomyolipoma (AML) with negative surgical margins and no evidence of malignancy.

The patient's immediate post-operative course was uneventful. The surgical drain output was minimal, and she was discharged approximately 1 week after surgery with no signs of infection or elevated inflammatory markers.

Two months after the surgery, the patient presented with right flank pain and fever. A CT scan showed a fluid collection with an enhancing rim around the resection site, consistent with a urinary fistula and an associated abscess (Fig. 1A). A retrograde pyelogram (RP) demonstrated a blind-ending proximal ureter, with no contrast entering the renal pelvis (Fig. 1B). A percutaneous nephrostomy tube was placed into the remaining renal pelvis. Subsequent antegrade pyelogram also showed no communication with the ureter, with extravasation of contrast from the resection site (Fig. 2). Given the high risk of re-operation in the setting of post-operative scarring and inflammation, and the intra-renal location of the disruption (at the calyx-pelvic junction rather than the extrarenal pelvis), a surgical approach was deemed extremely difficult. After a multidisciplinary discussion between urology and interventional radiology, a minimally invasive recanalization was planned. Since renal function was preserved by the nephrostomy drainage, the procedure was intentionally delayed to allow for resolution of acute inflammation and to permit thorough treatment planning. The patient recovered without complications. The pigtail catheter was clamped on post-operative day 7, and a non-contrast CT scan performed 5 hours later confirmed no hydronephrosis or fluid collection. The pigtail catheter was removed on day 8, and the patient was discharged on day 10.

At the first stent exchange, 6 months post-procedure, the tract was dilated to 14 Fr using sequential dilators (Fig. 5A). A retrograde pyelogram performed after tract dilation confirmed a fine, patent tract (Fig. 5B). The patient has now been followed for 2 years, with DJ stent exchanges performed every 6 months under general anesthesia. Discussion

We report a rare case of complete UPJ disruption following RAPN, successfully managed with a kidney-sparing, minimally invasive IVR technique.

The development of UPJ obstruction after PN is a rare event, with an incidence of 0.35% (4 of 1154 cases) in the series by Singh et al. [4] In their report, lower pole tumor location was identified as a risk factor, and the etiology was attributed to ischemic or mechanical injury. Our case is consistent with these findings. The tumor was large (5.5 cm) and occupied nearly the entire lower pole, requiring a wide resection and extensive renorrhaphy. This likely compromised the delicate blood supply to the UPJ, leading to delayed necrosis and fibrotic obliteration. In the series by Singh et al., 3 of 4 patients with post-PN UPJ obstruction were successfully managed with endoscopic approaches [4]. However, these cases involved strictures rather than complete disruption as seen in our patient.

The management of such a complication presents a significant challenge. While robotic-assisted pyeloplasty is highly successful for primary UPJ obstruction, with success rates exceeding 94% [5,6], its application in a re-operative field is difficult due to adhesions. Furthermore, the obstruction in our case was located within the renal parenchyma (calyx-pelvic junction) rather than the extrarenal pelvis, making a surgical approach even more hazardous. Endourological techniques, such as the "cut-to-the-light" technique or cold-knife incision, have been reported for complete ureteral strictures with success rates of 67%-81% [7,8]. However, these techniques are typically performed for fibrotic strictures and would have been ineffective against the complete physical separation seen in our patient. Therefore, the main alternatives in our case were the IVR approach or a total nephrectomy. To preserve renal function, the multidisciplinary team opted for the novel recanalization procedure.

The success of this procedure hinged on several key technical aspects and the close collaboration between urology and interventional radiology. Firstly, detailed pre-procedural 3D imaging using VINCENT software was crucial for planning the precise trajectory for puncture. Secondly, the use of a stiff, tapered-coil guidewire (Astato XS 9) was essential to penetrate the dense scar tissue after a standard microcatheter had failed. Thirdly, the rendezvous maneuver, capturing the wire with a snare under fluoroscopic guidance, ensured safe and successful creation of a through-and-through access.

To our knowledge, there is only one other report of a similar IVR technique for a urinary tract disruption after PN. Chopra et al. described 2 cases of an excluded calyx that was successfully recanalized into the renal pelvis using a similar rendezvous technique [9]. Our case is unique in that the disruption was at the UPJ, completely disconnecting the kidney from the rest of the urinary tract. Our technique demonstrates that this principle can be extended to salvage a kidney that would have otherwise required removal.

At 2 years post-procedure, the patient continues to undergo stent exchanges every 6 months to maintain patency of the tract. Given the relatively long distance traversed through dense scar tissue during the recanalization, the risk of re-stenosis following stent removal is considered very high. Therefore, we anticipate that long-term intermittent stent exchange will be necessary for the foreseeable future. Although this places an ongoing burden on the patient, we believe it represents the optimal strategy, as it has successfully preserved renal function and avoided the morbidity of nephrectomy.

This study has several limitations. First, this is a single case report, and the generalizability of this technique to other patients with similar complications remains to be established. Second, the long-term durability of the recanalized tract is unknown, and the patient will likely require permanent stenting. Third, the procedure requires specialized equipment and expertise in both urology and interventional radiology, which may limit its applicability in centers without such resources.

In conclusion, a combined antegrade-retrograde recanalization is a feasible, safe, and effective minimally invasive option for the management of complete UPJ disruption after partial nephrectomy. This case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary team approach, involving both urology and interventional radiology, in managing complex urological complications. This technique provides a valuable kidney-sparing alternative for patients in whom surgical reconstruction is deemed high-risk.
