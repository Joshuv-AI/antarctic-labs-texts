Preinfection with Mycoplasma pneumoniae exacerbates subsequent influenza infection in young adults: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lijuan Yang, Wanting Meng, Cheng Wu, Bo Shi, Kang Chang, Heng Zhao, Shuoyao Qu, Liqiang Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, alternating epidemics of multiple respiratory pathogens have appeared globally, with recurrent infections in some individuals. However, there is a lack of evidence from clinical studies on the effects of the initial pathogen on the incidence, severity, and regression of infections with the subsequent pathogen. This study involved college students as survey respondents during the successive outbreaks of Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) and influenza epidemics to obtain evidence regarding MP–influenza interference.MethodsMP and influenza epidemics occurred successively between September 2023 and February 2024. Internet-based questionnaires were administered to all freshmen enrolled in August 2023 at three closed-management public universities in northern China after the end of the first wave of the MP epidemic and the second wave of the influenza epidemic, respectively. The participants self-rated their perceived severity for 16 symptoms, and the scores were summed to obtain a combined symptom score for each individual. Multiple logistic and linear regression analyses were used to investigate the effect of previous MP infection on the incidence and severity of subsequent influenza infection.ResultsA total of 1803 cases of MP-like illness and 2111 cases of influenza-like illness (ILI) were observed among 5790 freshmen across two waves of respiratory infection outbreaks. Moreover, 709 and 1244 cases were voluntarily tested for pathogens at the university clinic, of which 549 (77.4%) and 1035 (83.2%) cases were finally confirmed as having MP and influenza virus infections, respectively. The ILI infection rate after MP-like illness was higher than that among students without MP-like illness [39.3% (708/1803) vs. 35.2% (1403/3987), P = 0.003]. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that previous confirmed MP infection was significantly associated with the development of subsequent influenza infection (odds ratio: 1.321; 95% confidence interval: 1.214–1.451; P < 0.001). Furthermore, multiple linear regression models revealed that previous MP infection was an independent predictor of a higher symptom severity score for influenza infection (adjusted β = 0.137, P < 0.001).ConclusionsRecent infection with MP may be associated with an increased rate of subsequent influenza infection. Moreover, previous MP infection was a predictor of higher symptom severity during subsequent influenza infection.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11912-4.

Introduction

In the wake of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, unusual surges of respiratory infections were observed worldwide [1, 2]. “Immunity gap,” “immunity theft,” and changes in pathogen characteristics may lead to a surge in infections [3–6]. The occurrence of successive epidemics has necessitated the consideration of the presence of a correlation between epidemics caused by different pathogens. Some studies have reported that, at the host level, the infection caused by the first pathogen could improve or attenuate the infection and replication of the second pathogen, indicating a positive (synergistic) or negative (antagonistic) interaction [7]. An epidemiological survey revealed a 6.4% risk of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection in children with previous COVID-19 infection compared with 3.19% risk in matched children without previous COVID-19 infection [8]. Another study demonstrated a negative association between human rhinovirus (HRV) and RSV and revealed that children recently infected with RSV exhibited a lower rate of HRV infection than those who were not infected [9]. More clinical studies are required to explore the correlation between outbreaks of different pathogens.

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) and influenza viruses are known to be prevalent pathogens of community-acquired pneumonia over the years. We investigated the correlation between MP and influenza infection. The potential interference between Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (Mhp) and the swine influenza (H1N1) virus has been assessed in a pig model [10, 11]. Previous infection of pigs with Mhp improved the outcomes of infection with the H1N1 virus (loss of appetite, hyperthermia, and pneumonia) under experimental conditions [10], which may be associated with the oxidative stress induced by Mhp infection [11]. The immune response to respiratory pathogens and the mechanisms of immune escape may be different in animals and humans. However, to date, no study has systematically investigated the interaction between MP and influenza viruses in humans. Therefore, we examined whether humans demonstrate a similar phenomenon when successively infected with MP and influenza viruses.

China experienced a widespread outbreak of COVID-19 infections between the winter of 2022 and the spring of 2023 due to the abandonment of the “dynamic zero-COVID-19” policy [12]. Recurrent outbreaks of alternating epidemics caused by various pathogens then occurred. A large number of patients with MP infection have been seen across China since August 2023, with up to 50% positive cases at the peak of the epidemic in October-November, and then gradually fell back [13, 14]. According to the influenza surveillance network of the National Influenza Center of China (http://www.chinaivdc.cn/cnic), in 2023, the northern provinces of China experienced an influenza epidemic in late October, which peaked in December and then gradually declined. Because of geographical and temporal differences, the peak months for detection of each pathogen fluctuate slightly from province to province. In Xi’an, the peak detection rate of MP (45.96%) in 2023 was in October, and the peak detection rates of influenza B (32.89%) and influenza A (22.23%) were both in December, and both of them showed an increasing and then decreasing trend [15].

We conducted this cohort study, which includes all freshmen at three closed-management public universities in northern China as the study population during the MP and influenza outbreaks in China, to determine the presence of MP–influenza interference in humans. These students attend classes every day in a large classroom with a capacity of 200, eat in groups in the cafeteria, and have one dormitory for every six people, this relatively closed and densely populated environment is susceptible to respiratory infection outbreaks. Additionally, compared to the epidemic of multiple pathogens in open environments, the transmission of pathogens in campus environments where closed management is characterized by a significant epidemic of a single dominant strain [16]. Importantly, students at these closed-management institutions were highly compliant with the survey.

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of MP infection on the incidence and severity of subsequent influenza infection when MP and influenza infections are acquired successively. This study represents the first comprehensive investigation of the correlation between respiratory MP infection and subsequent influenza infection in a large population. The findings of this study will provide clinical evidence regarding the interactions between different pathogens, thereby providing valuable insights for the development of effective prevention and control strategies for infectious diseases.

Methods

Study design, participants, and case definition

MP and influenza epidemics occurred successively between September 2023 and February 2024, which was the first respiratory disease epidemic in China since the COVID-19 pandemic. A retrospective cohort study was designed to explore the effect of MP infection on the incidence and severity of subsequent influenza infection. The study was conducted on all freshmen at three closed-management public universities in Xi’an, China. In Xi’an, according to the respiratory pathogen surveillance data, the MP epidemic started in September 2023, with a peak in October, followed by a gradual decline; the number of influenza patients gradually increased from November, and the epidemic peaked in December [15]. It was consistent with the respiratory pathogen epidemics on campus monitored by the outpatient clinics of three universities. We used the peak month of the epidemic as the dividing node, regarded September-October 2023 as the first wave of the MP epidemic and December 2023-February 2024 as the second wave of the influenza epidemic. Epidemiologic surveys of respiratory infections were conducted among these participants by self-administered questionnaires in early November 2023 and early March 2024, respectively. During September-October 2023, students who developed respiratory symptoms such as paroxysmal irritating dry cough, sore throat, runny nose, or nasal stuffiness as well as systemic symptoms such as mild fever or headache were considered to have MP-like illness. During December 2023-February 2024, students who reported fever (or feeling feverish) and at least one of the four respiratory symptoms, namely, runny nose, cough, nasal stuffiness, or sore throat, were classified as having an influenza-like illness (ILI) [17]. An MP-confirmed case was defined as an individual who had an MP-like illness and tested positive for MP via nucleic acid testing. An influenza-confirmed case was defined as an individual who had ILI and tested positive for influenza virus via viral nucleic acid or antigen testing. The study population was divided into the suspected cohort and the confirmed cohort. We focused our analyses on the confirmed cohort, which included only patients who tested positive for MP and influenza viruses in the two previous and subsequent epidemics, so cases positive for other respiratory pathogens and co-infections were excluded. Not all individuals in the suspected cohort were tested for respiratory pathogens, so confounding by other respiratory pathogens could not be completely ruled out, and the results of the suspected cohort were placed as an addition to the Supplementary Material. Reinfection was commonly defined as the process of re-occurrence of an infection after a previous respiratory infection has completely resolved due to the invasion of a new pathogen or a different strain of the same pathogen [18]. In our manuscript, reinfection was defined as the process of reoccurrence of influenza-like illness after at least 28 days of recovery from MP-like illness. Students who presented with symptoms of respiratory infections self-reported their illnesses via an online structured questionnaire during the two surveys. Students who did not develop respiratory infections reported their baseline information. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to completing the questionnaire. No financial incentives were provided to the study participants, and participation was entirely voluntary.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was used for data collection. The online structured questionnaire was constructed after an extensive literature review and expert evaluation based on the study objectives. The questionnaire comprised 46 questions with 4 categories of variables: (a) demographic characteristics (age, sex, height, and weight), smoking status, and history of influenza vaccination, snoring, and comorbidities (respiratory diseases and other acute or chronic illnesses); (b) duration of symptomatic respiratory infections, symptoms experienced (fever, sneeze, runny nose, nasal stuffiness, cough, sputum, sore throat, wheezing, chest pain, headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, fatigue, and myalgia/arthralgia), and symptom severity (mild, moderate, or severe) (symptom was recorded as none if not experienced); (c) learning, exercise, and living conditions during symptomatic respiratory infections; (d) health-seeking behavior (seeking treatment at a clinic or hospital, taking medicines, being hospitalized, whether chest imaging was performed and whether it indicated pneumonia, and whether testing for respiratory pathogens was conducted and the relevant results). Supplementary “Questionnaire” presents details of the self-administered questionnaire. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on participants’ height and weight.

Data quality assurance

We have taken a series of measures to ensure data quality. The project team, which consisted of student counselors and doctors from the school clinics, was responsible for questionnaire distribution and interpretation, as well as data collection and checking. Before formal data collection, we focused on the design of the structured questionnaire, trained the data collectors, and provided them with practical exercises on data collection. The questionnaire was pretested among several sophomores, and the clarity and flow of some questions were amended as required to suit the respondents according to the actual situation of answering the questionnaire. After the beginning of actual data collection and before completion of the questionnaire by the participants, trained project administrators explained them how to complete the questionnaire to minimize bias in data collection. Members of the project team promptly checked the completed questionnaires to ensure data completeness, accuracy, and consistency. Feedback and recompletion of the questionnaire were performed if errors or omissions were detected during the checking.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were influenza incidence and symptom severity score. Symptom severity score was evaluated with a subjective self-rating of 16 symptoms reported in the questionnaire. The severity of each symptom was rated on a scale of 0 (absent), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), and 3 (severe), and the sum of the 16 individual symptom-based scores was calculated as the combined severity score with a possible range of 1–48 points, where greater scores indicated higher symptom severity [19]. The secondary outcomes included illness duration and the extent of learning, exercise, and life affected by the illness.

Statistical analysis

The skewness and kurtosis normality test was conducted to detect the normal distribution of the continuous data. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as the mean (standard deviation), and the Student’s t-test was utilized for analysis. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as the median (interquartile range [IQR]) and analyzed with Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages, and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact tests were utilized for analysis. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were generated to investigate the independent risk factors for the development of influenza. Multivariate logistic regression analysis included all of the variables shown in Table 3, using the forward logistic regression method (entering a variable if P values less than 0.05, removing a variable if P values more than 0.10). Results were expressed as odds ratio (OR) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Univariate and multiple linear regression models were utilized to determine the associations between previous MP infection and the severity of subsequent influenza infection, and the results were expressed as β and 95% CI. Three models were created to stepwise adjust for con founding variables. These 3 models are a unadjusted model (Model I, uncorrected for any covariates), adjusted Model 1 (Model II, corrected only for age, gender, BMI, smoking status, vaccination status, snore, chronic pharyngitis, and asthma), and adjusted Model 2 (Model III, further corrected for age, gender, BMI, smoking status, vaccination status, snore, chronic pharyngitis, asthma, health seeking behavior, used antibiotic, used OTC medication, radiological investigation, pneumonia, and hospitalized). Statistical analysis was conducted with IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 software, R software (version 4.1.1), and Origin 2022 SR1 software. Two-tailed P-values of < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

There were 5790 freshmen at the 3 closed-management public universities. A total of 1803 (31.1%) cases of MP-like illness were reported during the first wave of respiratory infections (MP epidemic; September–October 2023), of which 709 (39.3%) cases were voluntarily tested for pathogens at the university clinic, and 549 (77.4%) were confirmed to have MP infections through laboratory testing. A total of 2111 (36.5%) cases of ILI were reported during the second wave of respiratory infections (influenza epidemic; December 2023–February 2024), of which 1244 (58.9%) cases were voluntarily tested for pathogens at the university clinic, and 1035 (83.2%) were confirmed to have influenza infections through laboratory testing.

The cohort of suspected and confirmed influenza cases was categorized into two groups according to the presence or absence of previous MP infection (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 1, the median age of confirmed influenza patients was 18 years, most of them were male (n = 869, 93.2%), the median BMI was 22.3 (IQR: 20.8–23.8) kg/m2, 6.9% of them were smokers, 10.3% indicated that they snored during sleep, and 9.4% and 1.6% reported a history of chronic pharyngitis and asthma, respectively. There were no reports of comorbidities such as immunosuppression, cardiovascular diseases, or hematologic disorders that might affect the course of influenza. Additionally, 1.4% of the participants reported that they had received an influenza vaccine within 1 year before the survey. No differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the two groups (Table 1). Similar trends were observed among the suspected ILI cases (Table S1).

Comparison of clinical outcomes

As shown in Fig. 2B, the symptoms of fever, runny nose, nasal stuffiness, sputum, sore throat, chest pain, headache, diarrhea, fatigue, and myalgia/arthralgia were significantly more frequent in confirmed influenza cases with previous confirmed MP infection than in those without previous MP-like illness (all P < 0.05). Similar trends were observed among the suspected ILI cases (Fig. 2A).

Confirmed influenza cases with previous confirmed MP infection exhibited higher median values of symptom severity score (16 vs. 14, P < 0.001) and illness duration (5.0 vs. 4.0 days, P = 0.007), were more likely to visit a health care professional (47.1% vs. 37.6%, P = 0.011) and use over-the-counter medications (93.4% vs. 83.5%, P < 0.001), and demonstrated higher rates of pneumonia (8.4% vs. 0.7%, P < 0.001). Confirmed influenza cases with previous confirmed MP infection were more likely to demonstrate poor concentration in class (23.3% vs. 11.3%, P < 0.001), to decrease the intensity of exercise (23.8% vs. 16.5%, P = 0.013), and to suffer sleep problems (15.0% vs. 7.5%, P = 0.001) compared with those who did not have previous MP-like illness. Besides, students with previous confirmed MP infection demonstrated longer times of absence from classes (1.75 vs. 1.24 days, P = 0.041), extracurricular activities (5.05 vs. 3.65 h, P < 0.001), and social activities (10.15 vs. 8.12 h, P < 0.001) due to illness (Table 2). Similar trends were observed among the suspected ILI cases (Table S2).

Impact of previous MP infection on the incidence and severity of subsequent influenza infection

The ILI infection rate after MP-like illness was higher than that among students without MP-like illness [39.3% (708/1803) vs. 35.2% (1403/3987), P = 0.003]. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the independent risk factors for the development of influenza (Table 3). Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that previous confirmed MP infection (odds ratio [OR]: 1.345; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.219–1.471; P < 0.001) was significantly associated with the development of influenza. Baseline variables shown in Table 3 were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The P-value for the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was 0.757. Multivariable logistic regression analysis also found that previous confirmed MP infection (OR: 1.321; 95% CI: 1.214–1.451; P < 0.001) was independent risk factor for influenza infection (Fig. 3).

Discussion

University campuses, as relatively closed and crowded places, are more likely to cause the spread and reinfection of respiratory pathogens, especially during the epidemic season [16]. The present study involved all freshmen at three closed-management public universities as respondents, which demonstrated a relatively higher level of overall health, thereby minimizing the effect of confounding factors, such as underlying diseases, on the study results. Our survey revealed that respiratory infections and reinfections were prevalent in this cohort. In addition, we revealed that young adults recently infected with MP demonstrate a higher rate of influenza infection. Furthermore, MP infection history was a predictive factor for higher symptom severity in patients with influenza. Our study presented more information about the negative effects of respiratory infections on students’ learning, exercise, and life, indicating that school absenteeism and impaired quality of life were crucial indirect costs related to illness.

Reinfection was commonly defined as an infection occurring after the recovery of an infection caused by the same infected pathogen or a new pathogen [18]. From a pathogen perspective, many respiratory pathogens may be re-infected, including novel coronavirus, RSV, mycoplasmas, and influenza (influenza A, influenza B) [20, 21]. From the perspective of susceptible populations, immunosuppressed individuals and those with previous bronchiectasis, advanced age, and comorbidities (obesity, asthma, type 2 diabetes mellitus) are associated with an increased risk of reinfection [22–24]. A few clinical studies have focused on the interaction between the infection rates of different pathogens. A study revealed that children recently infected with COVID-19 virus demonstrated a higher rate of RSV infection [8], and another study showed that children recently infected with RSV showed a lower rate of HRV infection [9]. Our survey revealed a higher ILI infection rate after MP-like illness than that among students without MP-like illness (39.3% vs. 35.2%, P = 0.003). This may be associated with a potentially higher susceptibility to respiratory infections in individuals prone to MP-like illness. Importantly, this may indicate that MP infection is associated with an increased risk of influenza reinfection. The interaction of infection rates between MP and influenza viruses still requires further investigation.

A previous infection affects not only the incidence of the subsequent infection but also the severity of the disease. Reinfection with the same pathogen within a short period is often milder because the immunity elicited by the previous infection continues to exist [24]. Simultaneous or sequential infection of the respiratory tract by different respiratory pathogens can lead to positive (synergistic) or negative (antagonistic) pathogen–pathogen interactions [7]. In particular, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 and influenza A (H1N1) virus coinfection in the golden Syrian hamster model led to a positive interaction that increased pathogenesis and disease severity [25]. A negative interaction between influenza A (H1N1) virus and RSV was observed in the ferret model, considering that infection with the former virus reduced the infection and replication of the latter [26]. Moreover, preinfection of pigs with Mhp induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and a response to the oxidative stress, thereby improving H1N1 infection outcomes [11]. Our study revealed that preinfection with MP increased the severity of subsequent influenza infection in humans. This result was consistent with those of the abovementioned animal studies.

The mechanisms for positive MP–influenza interactions are unclear, but they may be associated with mucosal/epithelial damage [27], host immune dysregulation, persistent immune infiltration [28], and normal microbial community disruption in the respiratory tract [29] due to the previous infection. First, MP infection directly damages the respiratory mucosa/epithelia, causing tight junctions and cytoskeletal dysfunction, which may contribute to the increased susceptibility to secondary influenza infection [27, 30]. Moreover, MP infection affects the number and function of alveolar macrophages, recruit inflammatory monocytes, inhibit neutrophil and natural killer cell chemotaxis and activation, as well as influence the interaction of multiple cytokines through various mechanisms, thereby decreasing the antimicrobial immune response of the lungs, which, thereafter, facilitates the invasion and colonization of influenza viruses [28, 31]. Studies have revealed that unique microbial communities exist on the mucosal surfaces of the respiratory tract, and this separate “organ system” may help shape the rigor of the immune responses and the ecological associations between pathogens and hosts [29]. Inflammatory responses caused by MP infection can alter the environment of the lungs, thereby contributing to the growth or abatement of specific microorganisms. Resistance to pathogens is reduced when the lung microbiome is imbalanced, making individuals more susceptible to influenza infections [32, 33]. More research is warranted to investigate the mechanisms of pathogen interaction when humans are sequentially infected with MP and influenza viruses.

Our study revealed that school absenteeism and impaired quality of life were important indirect costs associated with illness in college student populations. Public health interventions exhibit potential societal and health benefits. Vaccinations are the most effective way to prevent pandemics and seasonal respiratory infections [34]. However, the seasonal influenza vaccination rates remain low in most regions of China. The survey indicated that the overall vaccination rate in China was 3.2% and 2.5% in the 2020–2021 and 2021–2022 epidemic seasons, respectively [35]. The overall vaccination rate for freshmen was 1.4% in our cohort. These numbers are well below the 40% target threshold established by the modeling studies [36]. Awareness about vaccines needs to be raised, and appropriate vaccination strategies should be implemented for different populations to increase vaccination coverage, especially among those with high-risk factors.

This study had certain limitations. First, selection or recall bias could not be completely avoided when completing questionnaires, and due to the lack of precision in the data on the time interval between two infections in reinfected individuals, we did not analyze it further. Second, this study was conducted among college students; thus, the results cannot be generalized to other populations. Further studies are warranted to confirm the interaction of different pathogens in older patients and those with underlying diseases. Third, due to incomplete data on influenza strains in confirmed influenza cases, we did not analyze the different influenza strains in more detail. Fourth, our research did not include the important pathogen exposure risk of clustering relationship (dormitory, classroom, or university), which is an important confounding factor.

Conclusions

Recent infection with MP may be associated with an increased rate of subsequent influenza infection. Moreover, previous MP infection was a predictor of higher symptom severity during subsequent influenza infection. Therefore, in patients with recent respiratory infections, measures should be taken to actively prevent infection with another pathogen during the high-risk period of infection, and early treatment should be considered in case of reinfection to prevent further exacerbation of the disease. Further studies are warranted to investigate the immunological mechanisms of the interaction between MP and influenza viruses in humans.
