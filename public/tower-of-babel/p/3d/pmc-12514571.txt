Proximity and metabolic activity proxies in the tumor microenvironment as predictors of survival in high grade serous ovarian cancer
Authors: Naomi Berrell, Aaron Kilgallon, Meg L. Donovan, Clara Lawler, Chin Wee Tan, Kidane S. Embaye, Rafael Tubelleza, James Monkman, John F. Fraser, Ken O’Byrne, Ruby Huang, Arutha Kulasinghe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHigh grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) is a lethal gynecological malignancy, often detected at the late stages of disease. There is a need to identify predictors of therapeutic response in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Here, we used spatial proteomics to characterize the TME of 51 patients with HGSOC and identify features that contribute to patient survival. Using a 48-plex immunofluorescent protein panel, we analyzed tumor and immune cell interactions. We identified that more CD66+ cells in a 50 μm radius of tumor cells and more cytotoxic CD8 T-Cells within a 10 μm radius of the tumor boundary were associated with improved overall survival. The proximity of Tregs to some metabolically active tumor cells was favourable, while their proximity to less metabolically active tumors was less favourable. Our data suggest that a comprehensive functional and metabolic understanding of the TME is needed to quantify the therapeutic benefits in HGSOC.

Introduction

Globally, ovarian cancer (OC) has an incidence of over 313,000 cases and results in over 207,000 deaths per year (GLOBOCAN 2020).1 Symptoms, such as menstrual cramps, are generally mild, and coupled with unreliable screening tools, patients often receive a late-stage diagnosis with poor treatment outcomes.2 Prognoses for patients with advanced disease stages are usually poor, with a five-year survival of less than 17%.3 When detected early, most patients will initially recover with radical surgery and/or adjuvant chemotherapy but can experience reccurrence.2,4,5 At later stages of disease, however, surgery is insufficient to clear microscopic lesions and treatment success relies on the efficacy of chemotherapy agents.2 With limited systemic and/or targeted therapies, it is becoming increasingly important to understand the properties of the underlying TME, which may provide insights into more effective therapeutic strategies.

As therapy resistance in this tumor type is common, there is an unmet clinical need for improved treatments targeting OC during late-stage disease, as well as biomarkers to accurately predict a patient’s response to therapies.6 Altered cellular energetics stands as one of the hallmarks of cancer and contributes to therapy resistance and immune evasion. Over several decades, many studies have revealed that the alteration to various metabolic pathways, such as amino acid metabolism, increased glycolysis, or fatty acid metabolism, can contribute to tumor proliferation, therapy resistance and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments.7,8,9,10,11 Results from a number of pre-clinical and clinical studies suggest that the exploitation of these dysregulated pathways and metabolic addictions may improve the effectiveness of current and future therapeutics.12,13,14

Spatial proteomics allows for insights into the TME by a comprehensive profiling of the cellular, tissue, and organ immune contexture.6 Spatial proteomics facilitates the analysis of many biomarkers across the TME and in the tumor and stroma simultaneously, enabling us to profile the tumor-immune composition and cell-cell interactions in tissues at multiple levels of complexity; including capturing cell types and their functional and metabolic states.6,15 Targeting antibodies for enzymes associated with metabolic activity allows for the enhanced profiling of dysregulated metabolic pathways within the TME. Moreover, these approaches allow for the sub-phenotyping of the individual cell types using functional characterization. This approach enables the development of large, targeted panel proteomic profiles in a non-tissue-destructive and high-throughput manner for translational cancer research.

In this study, we present a spatial proteomic study of the TME of 55 patients with OC using a custom-developed 48-plex mIF protein panel. Our panel encapsulates the major hallmarks of cancer and spatially characterizes HGSOC tumor and immune cells and their functional and metabolic states. The spatially resolved HGSOC TME profiles were measured against the clinical endpoint of overall survival (OS). These analyses include identifying cellular neighborhoods common across the cohort, highlighting proximity density trends between two distinct cell types, profiling cells located at the tumor boundary, and characterizing spatial patterns occurring within the tumor that associate with OS.

Results

Spatial metrics

Neighborhoods were generated based on functionally annotated immune cells, where 5 neighborhoods were selected from the elbow point found using a distortion plot (Figure S2). Neighborhoods were annotated based on the abundance of cell types using a heatmap and a proportion bar chart (Figures 2A and 2B). Mapping of neighborhoods onto tumor samples was done to visualize the differences among neighborhoods in-situ (Figure 2C). Based on the median proportion of neighborhoods in each sample, only the tumor dominant neighborhood was significantly associated with overall survival, as per the Kaplan Meier (KM) analysis (p = 0.0371), where patients with a higher proportion of these tumor neighborhoods had worse survival (Figure 2D).

SPACEc patch analysis identifies clusters of one cell type or neighborhood and determines the cellular proportions present within user defined radii surrounding the outside of these patches.18 We performed patch analysis around clusters of the tumor dominant neighborhoods to identify differences in the proportions of surrounding cells between patient groups. Clusters were generated by extracting continuous regions of one cell type using the SPACEC package, which is based on running HDBSCAN clustering over all centroids, selecting the outermost cells as the edge of the patch. An example of this is shown in Figure 2E, where three tumor immune low clusters were identified in one core. Proportions of cell types were calculated at distances of 5 μm, 10 μm, 15 μm, 20 μm, and 25 μm from the edge of each patch. For each distance, we performed a CoxPH model fit to identify cells within each radius that might be contributing to patient outcomes (Table S1; Figure S3). Cells were excluded from the model if the variance of an outcome group was less than 0.01. At 5 μm and 25 μm, there were no statistically significant findings. At 10 μm radius, cytotoxic CD8 T cells were enriched in patients with a hazard ratio (HR) < 1, indicating an association with improved patient outcomes (p = 0.03) (Figure 2F). At 15 μm, tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) were identified to have an HR > 1 (p = 0.04) (Figure S3B; Table S1) and at 20 μm, double-negative T cells had an HR > 1 (p = 0.04), whereas B cells (p = 0.05) and monocytes (p = 0.02) had HR’s < 1 (Figures S3C and S3D; Table S1).

Proximity density analysis was performed to identify trends that were occurring within the tissue that suggest common cellular distributions between tumor cells and other cells within the TME and whether these were associated with survival outcomes (Figure 3A). The same method was employed for cell type combinations at a distance of 50 μm, and over a range of distances 25 - 100 μm in 5 μm increments, where the area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess the distribution of cells. At 50 μm, the CoxPH model of cell locations in relation to tumor cells showed that exhausted CD8+ T cells (p = 0.05), fibroblasts (p = 0.035), and PDL1+ TAMs (p = 0.0077) were associated with worse survival (HR > 1), whereas increased proximity density between CD66+ cells (p = 0.017) or activated CD8+ T cells (p = 0.021) were associated with improved outcomes (HR < 1) (Figure 3B; Table S2). KM analysis of these cell types revealed that only the proximity density scores between tumor cells and PD-L1+ TAMs (p = 0.0123) (Figure S4B; Table S2) and CD66+ cells (p = 0.0254) (Figure 3C) were statistically significant. Higher proximity density scores between PDL1+ TAMs and CD8+ T cells (p = 0.018), exhausted CD8+ T cells (p = 0.023), cytotoxic CD8 T cells (p = 0.0495), and activated CD8 T cells (p = 0.034) were all associated with worse OS (Figure S5). Furthermore, the KM analysis modeled on the proportions of both PDL1+ TAMs (p = 0.0168) and CD66+ cells (p = 0.0151) at a whole core level were also statistically significant (Figures S4A and S4C). Higher proximity density scores between CD66+ cells and structural cells (p = 0.019) were also associated with improved outcomes (Figure S4D). Over multiple radii, we see that patients alive past two years tend to consistently have a higher proximity density score between tumor and CD66+ cells compared to patients who died prior to two years (Figure 3D). The tumor-metabolic profile

The metabolic profile of tumor cells is known to alter the TME and can lead to impaired immune cell function, immune evasion, and drug resistance. Proximity density analysis was performed at a 50 μm radius to identify interactions between immune and tumor cells based on metabolic dysregulation. Tumor cells that did not highly express any metabolic markers in our panel were classed as “metabolic low.” A proximity density analysis was performed between the metabolically distinct tumor phenotypes and immune cells (Figures 4A–4C). Hazard ratios from fitted CoxPH models were calculated for each tumor subtype and non-tumor cells (Figure S7; Table S3). Functional markers expressed on tumor cells are associated with impaired immune response

When assessing interactions between tumor and immune cells, tumor cells were functionally annotated to be either positive or negative for the IDO1, iNOS, Ki67, CD44, and IFN-γ functional markers using a GMM classifier. Based on these annotations, we assessed patterns of immune cells in proximity to any of these functional markers and whether these correlated with survival outcomes. Using the proximity density score, we identified several patterns consistently occurring across multiple immune types, when in proximity to functional tumor phenotypes, that were identified to be significantly associated with overall survival. High proximity density scores between PDL1+ TAMs and CD44+ (p = 0.0123), Ki67+ (p = 0.0123), iNOS+ (p = 0.0275), and IFNG+ (p = 0.0123) tumor cells were associated with worse outcomes (Figure 5). The same relationship was seen for Ki67+ (p = 0.0010), iNOS+ (p = 0.0291), IFNG+ (p = 0.0283) and IDO1+ (p = 0.0150) tumor cells with fibroblasts (Figures S8A–S8D). High proximity density scores between PD1+ CD8 T cells and either Ki67+ tumor cells (p = 0.0418) or IDO1+ tumor cells (p = 0.0487) were found in patients who had shortened overall survival (Figures S8G and S8H). To confirm the validity of our interpretation of the univariate selected features, we implemented a stability-selection method on the full set of considered features – including proportions, cell counts, neighborhood proportions, proximity density, and proximity volume. As we wanted to use these features as proxies for biological interactions and relevant biological processes that can be applicable across the whole cohort, we did not include clinical information, such as disease stage, in the modeling process. The stability selection method bootstrapped and fit CoxPH models to the cohort’s OS endpoint data, selecting 52 features of interest after a final regularized CoxPH fit (Figure 6A; Table S4). A number of these selected features, particularly vessel and fibroblast density features, are correlated with disease outcome and were therefore not considered in the univariate analysis. Some selected features are spatial proximity volume metrics, which were not considered because this metric was less biologically interpretable than proximity density. Nine out of 50 of these selected features align with the univariate-selected features as interpreted in this analysis. The selection frequencies of all features are shown in Figure 6B. Confidence intervals for 15 of these 50 features spanned the log HR < 0 or log HR > 0 range, and these are listed in Table S4. Of these 15, 7 features align with the univariate selected features, providing a robust indication that these considered features were selected in a false discovery rate-controlled manner. The HR directionality also aligns with the predicted survival endpoints in the univariate analysis for all features other than one. Discussion

HGSOC is the most common type of ovarian cancer, and because of mild or misleading symptoms, it is often diagnosed at a late stage when therapy resistance is common.2 Understanding the immune contexture of HGSOC in a treatment resistant landscape is important for improving future therapies and clinically overcoming mechanisms of resistance. Ovarian cancer is an immunogenic tumor that exhibits spontaneous antitumor immune response,20 where the infiltration of lymphocytes into the TME is more often associated with a favourable prognosis.20 However, while some subtypes of these immune cell populations exert anti-tumor activity, other subtypes can promote tumor growth. Here, we used a high-plex spatial proteomic analysis to gain greater insight into the distribution and heterogeneity of immune cells and their functional states, thereby understanding how these cells may impact disease progression. This study highlights the existence of location-based patterns among TME cells of patients with HGSOC, with a focus on their functional and metabolic states that are associated with overall survival.

Previous studies have shown that tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILS) have a prognostic benefit in ovarian cancer, and in particular, the presence of CD8+ T cells in the tumor has been associated with improved survival.21 Multiomic insights into the HGSOC TME has highlighted that immune evasion mechanisms are unique to the tumor’s mutational subtype.22 In line with our findings, this study showed different patterns in the location of CD8 subtypes depending on the tumor phenotype.22 CD8+ T cells are considered integral in anti-tumor activity, where they secrete cytotoxic molecules, including Granzyme B to trigger tumor cell apoptosis.21 Within our cohort, we found that patients who had an the increased proportion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (characterized by granzyme B positivity) within a 10-μm distance of the tumor dominant neighborhoods had improved survival. When we increased the radius from the tumor, the cytotoxic CD8+ T cells no longer appeared to be prognostic, reasonably indicating that, for effective CD8+ T cell function, these cells may need to be in close proximity to the tumor to have benefit. Tumor infiltrating scores have proven to be a reliable prognostic tool in a range of cancers; however, it has been stated that the spatial organization of these cells provides additional valuable information.23 Here, we highlight the importance of location-based metrics for predicting clinical outcomes. Reasons that these cells may not be reaching the tumor, or exerting anti-tumor activity at these close distances may be due to immune evasion mechanisms that have evolved in the tumor, including dysregulated metabolism and expression of immunomodulatory markers.24 It is important to appreciate that previous studies have indicated that the immune evasion mechanisms in ovarian cancer are highly heterogeneous and can by influenced by the mutational profile, metastatic spread, and the exact location of tissue samples.25,26

Dysregulated tumor metabolism plays a role in tumor growth and immune evasion.8 We assessed how the changes in metabolic profile of tumor cells associated with the location of immune cells within the TME and subsequently, how this impacted patient outcomes. Previous work in understanding the tumor-metabolic profile of HGSOC has identified that these profiles shift post-chemotherapy.27 A temporal RNA-seq study revealed that the metabolic profile tends to shift their metabolic profile to favor a high metabolism and proliferation archetype after treatment.27 When we looked at the proximity between tumor metabolic phenotypes and the immune cells, we identified that when tumor cells that were categorized as metabolically low, due to not highly expressing any of the metabolic markers in the current IF panel, had a higher proportion of their Treg cells within a 50 μm radius, patients had a worse overall survival. This is not surprising as the presence of Treg cells in the tumor microenvironment often suppresses the activity of the CD8+ T cells by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines.28 We suspect that there are alterations in the metabolic profile that are contributing to these patterns but is not captured by the targeted panel. Interestingly, in tumors with high citrate synthase or inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression, the presence of Tregs was associated with improved survival, leading us to speculate that the byproducts of these dysregulated metabolic pathways may negatively affect the function of Tregs. Furthermore, when looking at the iNOS tumors, cytotoxic CD8+ T cells were also associated with improved outcomes, which could the result of a favourable TME or Treg dysfunction allowing the cytotoxic CD8 T cells to be more effective. Nitric oxide in high concentrations can be anti-tumorigenic, contributing to cytotoxicity and may have pro-apoptotic effects. Overexpression of iNOS in other cancers, including prostate and colon cancer, has shown an increased sensitivity to cisplatin or radiotherapy. Studies have reported that NO modulates the T cell response and T cell differentiation.29 For example, it has been shown that the exogenous production of NO is important for cytotoxic CD8 T cells to target and kill tumor cells and can inhibit Treg induction.29

The presence of tumor associated macrophages in HGSOC has been found to be negatively associated with overall survival.30 Depending on the origin of TAMs, they can display M1 or M2 macrophage characteristics. Typically, in ovarian cancer, TAMs are more often of an M2 phenotype and are known to have immunosuppressive properties in the TME by releasing pro-tumor cytokines and chemokines.30 Furthermore, the expression of PDL1 by TAMs can directly suppress the activity of cytotoxic CD8 T cells.30 In our cohort, we identified PDL1+ TAMS to be associated with worse overall survival. Within our dataset, however, we have seen a higher proportion of PDL1 TAMs in a 50 μm radius of tumor cells, activated CD8 T cells, CD8 T cells, cytotoxic CD8 T cells, or exhausted CD8 T cells that are associated with worse overall survival (Figure S5).

CEACAM1 (CD66a) is a cell adhesion molecule that has been characterized to have both pro-tumor and anti-tumor effects depending on the tumor type. In ovarian cancer, the CEACAM1 RNA expression and protein expression of CEACAM1 has been found to be an independent prognostic marker for improved overall survival.31 It was also included as one of the six markers for a 6-plex assay that aimed to improve and model diagnostics of ovarian cancer.32 This study found that in plasma, CEACAM1 was higher in patients with benign ovarian tumors compared to patients with cancerous lesions and suggested that CEACAM1 plays a role in tumor suppression.32 Aside from the above-mentioned findings, there is currently limited published data available describing CEACAM1 in ovarian cancer. Our data supports CD66 as a positive prognostic marker at a whole core level, and when these cells localise closer to tumor cells, patients have improved survival outcomes. Given that the CD66 antibody used in this panel targets CD66a, c, and e epitopes, we are unable to definitively support these findings. However, our data does suggest that potentially we are seeing a similar relationship. CD66a, b, c, and d epitopes are known to be expressed on neutrophils, but can also be expressed on epithelial, plasma, and malignant cells.33,34 The role of neutrophils in ovarian cancer is controversial, with a study finding that neutrophilia and the presence of granulocyte colony stimulating factor in the TME are linked with chemotherapy and radiotherapy resistance, metastases, immune suppression, and cancer cell stemness.35 However, neutrophils can undergo polarization to either an anti-tumor phenotype (N1) or a pro-tumor phenotype (N2) depending on the inflammatory state of the TME.36 The characterization of N1 and N2 phenotypes using surface markers remains a challenge of the field, and with our antibody panel we were unable to characterize these CD66+ cells further. N1 neutrophils can contribute to anti-tumor immune activity through the excretion of cytotoxic mediators including TNF-α, NO, and H2O2.37 Given that we see iNOS upregulated for our CD66+ cells, this indicates a potential anti-tumor effect from this population of cells. In the future, this CD66+ population should be further characterized to delineate more information regarding the cell type and functional activity.

Spatial profiling of HGSOC revealed that patient overall survival may be impacted by dysregulated metabolism of tumor cells and the effects this has on nearby immune cells. Additionally, the function of immune cells could be associated with good or poor survival depending on the main metabolic pathway upregulated in the tumor. Furthermore, the presence of CD66+ cells and PDL1+ tumor associated macrophages appeared to play a significant role in disease outcomes. The results from this study highlight the importance of utilizing the spatial location of cells. This was particularly evident, where we identified that the distance of cytotoxic CD8 T cells from the tumor boundary was significant for overall survival. Our results highlight the importance of spatial profiling as the localization of immune cells within the TME can indicate whether these immune cells are effective.

Limitations of the study

This study provides valuable insight into the TME of HGSOC; however, there are limitations of this study that we acknowledge. One limitation of this cohort is that there was no information available regarding the time until recurrence and whether, and potentially which, post-surgery treatments were administered. Differences in treatment regimens will impact patient outcomes and are something that we were unable to control. The differences in the preservation of the samples and the dates of collection mean that sample quality varied throughout the TMA. While future studies would benefit from a larger sample size and increased tumor area captured, this study provides comprehensive mapping and analysis of the tumor microenvironment of HGSOC, allowing insight into the metabolic and functional state of many cells. One recent study has identified a significant difference in survival based on the stromal composition and spatial distribution of these cells in ovarian cancer.38 We believe that a natural progression of this study would include an expanded panel that features more stromal markers to better characterize fibroblast and mesenchymal populations. Furthermore, because the tumor microenvironment is dynamic, temporal analysis of samples would provide better information about the evolution of the tumor microenvironment.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study profiled formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue microarray (TMA) consisting of single 1mm diameter tissue cores from 51 women with HGSOC (TA4359, SKU: 69574359, Tristar Technologies, USA). The tissues consisted of samples collected between 2009 and 2017. After quality control, three additional samples were excluded due to poor tissue quality or staining artifacts. Patient tissues were assessed by a histopathologist and a representative tissue core was used to prepare the TMA. Apart from two stage-2 patients, all patients had advanced disease at diagnosis (stage 3 or higher). Patients received chemotherapy (1-4 rounds of Carboplatin/Paclitaxel (n=42), 3-6 rounds of single agent carboplatin (n=10), or unspecified treatment (n=3) before undergoing interval debulking surgery. All patients had overall survival information, where the median survival of the cohort was 862 days (range 172 - 2691 days), and n=40 patients had died and n=11 patients were alive. The median age of participant at the time of tissue collection was 65 but the cohort ranged from 47 to 86 years (Table 1). Information regarding treatment received in the post-surgical setting was not available, but a number of patients did die from metastatic disease (Table 1). All patients included in this study provided informed consent for the donation of their tissues to be used for research. This study has University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Approval (2021/HE001936).

Method details

Tissue profiling

The TMA was subject to high-plex cyclic-IF using the PhenoCycler Fusion (Akoya Biosciences, USA), following established protocols as previously described.41 A panel of 48 antibodies was custom-designed using the Hallmark Features of Cancer,42 including markers for cell typing, cell functionality, and markers of cellular metabolism15 (Table 2, key resources table). Briefly, FFPE tissue sections were baked at 60°C for 1 hour before being submerged in Histochoice Clearing Agent (VWR) to remove wax. Sections were then hydrated using decreasing concentrations of ethanol, followed by heat antigen retrieval in AR9 buffer (AR9001KT) using the standard settings of a conventional pressure cooker for 20 min. Following this, sections were washed in ddH2O, hydration buffer, and staining buffer solutions before staining with the customised antibody cocktail and allowed to incubate overnight at 4°C in a humidity chamber. H&Es images were reviewed by a histopathologist independent of the mIF cell typing. Cell typing annotations were checked against this ground truth annotations for tumour, stroma, and immune cells.

Cell segmentation and data QC

Qptiff files were imported into QuPath for visualisation, initial analysis, and quality control.43 Stardist was used to perform cell segmentation based on nuclei staining.44 A pixel classifier was user-specified using PanCk+ and PanCk– regions to define tumour and stromal regions, respectively. This approach allows for the interrogation of tumoural and stromal markers within the TME. Quality control of antibody staining was performed by manual inspection of marker specificity, with markers displaying ubiquitous and non-specific staining removed from the analysis, as previously described.45 The median expression of each marker for each cell was exported as a csv file, containing each cell’s unique object identifier, x-y coordinates, cell morphology features and core ID.

Cell typing analysis

Using a subset of cell markers (CD44, CD68, CD3e, CD45, CD56, E-cadherin, Ki67, PanCK, CD20, CD14, CD8, SMA, nuclear size and nuclear diameter), unsupervised clustering was performed on Phenograph16 cluster cell groups to assign tumour and non-tumour phenotypes. Same slide H&Es were annotated by a histopathologist and were compared to tumour/non-tumour annotations to validate the tumour/non-tumour annotations. This marker subset were normalized using a percentile transformation prior to cell typing. Generated cell type annotations were then joined to the original AnnData structure that contained the raw data. For further phenotype classifications, when data were subset from the raw file, the same normalisation method was applied. Unsupervised clustering was initially performed over a range of resolutions and nearest neighbours but generally failed to generate meaningful clusters, necessitating supervised cell typing. To characterise cells further, the non-tumour cell phenotypes were classified using the gating method, mmochi,46 that is based on a gaussian mixed model (GMM) combined with a user-defined hierarchy (Figures 2 and S1). Iteratively, cell types were imported back to QuPath for visualisation and threshold cutoffs manually adjusted to reflect true cell types. Broad cell types were assigned by interplay between cell-type heat maps and visualization of cell types on the raw image. Relevant functional markers were chosen to better define subsets of T cells, B cells, macrophages and tumour cells (Figure 2). Using a GMM, positivity for relevant markers was assigned to identify functional cell types. Tumour cells were phenotyped for functional markers and metabolic markers by the GMM and unsupervised clustering methods, respectively. For functional markers, tumours were labelled to be positive or negative for iNOS, IDO1, CD44, and IFN-g based on the GMM positivity classification. This approach allowed us to focus on specific functional interactions between immune and tumour cells. For the metabolic phenotyping, we opted to use an unsupervised clustering method to annotate these cells as we wanted to assess tumours in their whole metabolic state, which could include having multiple dysregulated metabolic pathways.16 Data were subset to include the CPT1A, pNRFR2, ASCT2, Citrate-Synthase, G6PD, GLUT1 and iNOS markers, and unsupervised clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm. A search over a range of k and r parameters was performed using a GPU-accelerated adaption of the Phenograph method from Scanpy to identify optimal parameters.16 At a k-nearest neighbours value of 20 and Leiden resolution of 0.25, cells separated into distinct tumour phenotypes. Groups were then annotated based on the expression profiles displayed on a heatmap.

Neighbourhood analysis

Cellular Neighbourhoods were created using a K Nearest-Neighbours (KNN) approach by specifying the number of neighbours reported per cell and the number of neighbourhoods to define ecosystems that have similar cells present near each other.47 Three neighbourhoods were defined using the tumour/non-tumour annotations to create tumour-dense, stromal-dense and mixed neighbourhoods (immune infiltrated tumour regions or tumour interface zones). The SPACEc bioinformatics package was used to generate more complex neighbourhoods across the cohort.18 To determine an optimal number of neighbourhoods, a Distortion Score Elbow for miniBatchK-means was calculated, where the distortion score over a range of 20 neighbourhoods was determined to identify the elbow point.

Proximity analysis

Using cell-type and spatial information, we computed various spatial metrics to compute relationships between sets of cells that could be associated with disease outcome. Using the Scimap package, we performed spatial density and volume analyses.48 Proximity density and volume metrics from the Scimap package were calculated between cell type combinations for broad and functionally annotated cell types.48 The proximity density measure estimates how often two cell types co-occurred within a pre-determined radius, divided by the total number of those cell types. Higher scores indicate that these cell types were more frequently associated with one another. The proximity volume metric evaluates how often two cell types co-occur within a radius relative to the total number of cells within the KNN subgraph. Proximity density and volume scores were calculated per tumour core over a range of distances between 5 and 100 μm by increments of 5 μm.18 Scores were calculated using the area under the curve and were then compared by survival time. Using SPACEc, patch analyses were performed around specified neighbourhoods for each tissue core.18 Edges of cell clusters were determined, followed by identification of cells within radii of 5 - 25 μm.18 The proportions of cells in each radius were compared across survival groups and survival times.

Validation using publicly available datasets

The Xena Browser, provided by the University of California Santa Cruz, was used to validate statistically significant findings from cell count and proportion analyses.19 This interactive web-based tool allows users to assess publicly available datasets (including RNA, protein and spatial datasets) and perform statistical analysis based on overall survival or other available clinical information. Using data from the GDC TCGA Ovarian Cancer RNA-seq dataset, the gene expression from 425 patients was assessed by applying insights gained from evaluating hazard ratios on overall survival estimated by a Kaplan Meier analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

This study utilised several statistical methods to identify statistically significant features that correlated with survival. Details of statistical experiments including exact number of patients (n) and definitions of the centre and dispersion measurements, can be found in figure legends and results. To identify whether features are associated with overall survival, a univariate survival analysis was performed using a Kaplan Meier estimate between upper and lower quartiles and median cut-offs.49 Statistical significance was estimated from a log-rank test using the Lifelines package.49 Figures generated from Kaplan Meier analysis display the 95% confidence intervals and p-value. Additionally, using the Lifelines package,49 a univariate Cox-Proportional Hazard (CoxPH) model was evaluated to compare the effect of various features on patient outcome. CoxPH figures display the log-hazard ratio with 95% confidence intervals. To identify features that may be associated with survival over three years, the average findings for each patient were compared using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test. Statistical significance was considered using unadjusted p-values where p < 0.05.

The stability selection method was implemented in the sharp R39 package with CoxPH models implemented from40 the glmnet library. The LASSO l1-regularized fits of CoxPH models to the cohort survival data selected 86 features at the optimal stability point of the model, and a final l1-weighted CoxPH model reduced this feature set to 52 features of interest with non-zero coefficients. Model parameters were sampled on a grid of threshold selection frequencies from 0 to 0.5. The final regularized CoxPH model to reduce the feature space was fit with the minimal regularization parameter (λ = 0.03) suitable for a stable fit to the OS clinical data. Estimates of the partial effects significance were implemented using CoxPH fits in the lifelines library.
