Pneumococcal vaccination awareness and uptake among high-risk patients attending internal medicine outpatient clinics: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Nazlı Pelin Kırkayak, Bora Vergül, Fatma Bürde Ulukaya, Merve Feyza Demir Gürdal, Betul Erismis, Enes Seyda Şahiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPneumococcal diseases are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in high-risk patient groups. Despite national and international guideline recommendations for pneumococcal vaccination in all adults aged ≥ 65 y and in younger adults with established risk conditions, uptake remains critically low in Turkey. This study aimed to evaluate the association of factors such as age, education level, comorbid risk conditions, disease awareness, frequency of physician counseling, and free-of-charge vaccine availability with pneumococcal vaccination uptake among high-risk patients attending internal medicine outpatient clinics. This cross-sectional survey study was conducted between January and June 2024. Data were collected from 116 participants aged 65 and above or aged 18 and above with risk factors attending internal medicine outpatient clinics. The survey consisted of 16 questions Among participants, 45.7% were aged ≥65 y, 34.5% had diabetes, 6.9% had COPD/asthma, and 4.3% had heart failure. Only 20 participants (17.2%) had received pneumococcal vaccination, of whom 85.0% were vaccinated following physician recommendation. Among non-vaccinated individuals (n = 96), 61.5% reported not being informed about their risk status by a physician. Pneumococcal vaccination was significantly associated with awareness of risk group status (p < .01), knowledge of free vaccination (p < .01), and prior physician recommendation (p < .01). After receiving information, 65 of 96 (67.7%) previously non-vaccinated participants expressed vaccination intention; this reflects short-term receptiveness and should not be interpreted as confirmed behavioral change. These findings suggest that physician counseling and recommendations may be important determinants pneumococcal vaccination uptake among high-risk groups.

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is among the leading bacterial causes of community-acquired pneumonia globally and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly among older adults and those with underlying comorbid conditions.1 The clinical spectrum ranges from non-bacteremic pneumonia to bacteremic pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis, with disease severity increasing substantially in immunocompromised individuals and the elderly.2 In Turkey, pneumococcal disease represents a significant but underaddressed burden; vaccination rates in high-risk adult populations have consistently been reported at below 20% in outpatient and inpatient settings alike, suggesting a persistent gap between guideline recommendations and clinical practice. Established risk factors for pneumococcal disease include chronic lung disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney or liver disease, smoking, alcohol use, and immunosuppression.3–5 Pneumococcal infections therefore constitute a significant component of preventable morbidity and mortality in adults and remain one of the primary concerns of preventive medicine in primary care and outpatient practice.

Pneumococcal vaccines confer protection by eliciting antibodies directed against the polysaccharide capsule of S. pneumoniae. Polysaccharide vaccines (e.g., PPSV23) induce a T-cell-independent B-cell response and are therefore less immunogenic in certain high-risk groups, including the very elderly and immunocompromised patients. Conjugate vaccines (e.g., PCV15, PCV20, PCV21), by contrast, link polysaccharides to carrier proteins, thereby generating a T-cell-dependent immune response that produces immunological memory and higher, more durable antibody titers. This distinction is of particular clinical relevance in the populations most commonly encountered in internal medicine outpatient clinics, in whom attenuated humoral responses may influence both vaccine selection and timing.6,7

National and international guidelines have long recommended pneumococcal vaccination for all individuals aged 65 y and older and adults aged 19 y and older with risk conditions.8,9 Based on data indicating a significant disease burden in the 50–64 age group, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) expanded their pneumococcal conjugate vaccine recommendation in 2024 to include all adults aged 50 and older who have not previously received PCV.8 In practice, the use of conjugate vaccines (PCV15/PCV20/PCV21) and polysaccharide vaccines (PPSV23) is tailored to the individual’s vaccination history, risk factors, and immune status.8,10,11 Although vaccination schedules vary by country, the primary goal is to close the vaccination gap in high-risk adults and reduce pneumococcal-related hospitalizations. The clinical case for vaccination in these age groups is well-supported by randomized trial data. In the landmark CAPiTA trial involving 84,496 adults aged ≥ 65 y, PCV13 reduced vaccine-type community-acquired pneumonia by 45.6% (95% CI, 21.8–62.5) and vaccine-type invasive pneumococcal disease by 75.0% (95% CI, 41.4–90.8) in the per-protocol analysis; efficacy persisted throughout the mean follow-up period of approximately four years.7 More recently introduced conjugate vaccines (PCV20, PCV21) offer broader serotype coverage and are expected to extend protection across additional risk strata.8,10

The main barrier in adult vaccination is often not the availability of the vaccine, but rather a breakdown in the chain of recommendation and access.12 Key determinants of successful vaccination include strong physician recommendation, clear communication of risk group status, awareness of free vaccination availability, and co-recommendation with influenza vaccination.12,13 Vaccine hesitancy, doubts about safety and efficacy, and cognitive barriers such as the perception that “it is not necessary for me” can also weaken this process.14 Reports of low pneumococcal vaccination rates in high-risk groups in Turkey suggest that these factors are not sufficiently addressed in local practice.15,16 It is therefore important to examine how the quality of physician recommendations and the content of clinical communication in outpatient practice translate into vaccination behavior, to identify local barriers systematically, and to develop feasible intervention steps. It is also worth noting that Turkish national guidelines (KLİMİK 2018) recommend pneumococcal vaccination for adults aged ≥65 years and younger adults with specific risk conditions. The 2024 ACIP recommendations expanded age-based vaccination to include all adults aged ≥50 years, reflecting a more recent update in vaccination policy. Future revisions of national recommendations may consider emerging evidence together with local epidemiological data and healthcare priorities.

However, data on the association of physician counseling, awareness of personal risk group status, and knowledge of free vaccination eligibility with vaccination behavior in Turkish internal medicine outpatient settings remain scarce. The present study was therefore designed to: (1) determine pneumococcal vaccine awareness and self-reported vaccination status among adults aged ≥65 y and adults aged ≥18 y with at least one established risk condition attending internal medicine outpatient clinics; (2) identify factors associated with vaccination uptake; and (3) assess post-counseling vaccination intention following provision of standardized information.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study designed to assess pneumococcal vaccine awareness and self-reported vaccination status among adults visiting internal medicine outpatient clinics. The study was conducted in two phases: first, participants completed a structured questionnaire assessing baseline knowledge and vaccination status; second, they received standardized information about pneumococcal vaccination, and their post-counseling vaccination intention was recorded. The study protocol was prepared in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Clinical Research Ethics Committee No. 2 (Approval date: 24/01/2024, decision no: E2–23–5846). The purpose and scope of the study were explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. The data were coded anonymously and analyzed for scientific purposes only.

Study population

The study population consisted of adults who visited the internal medicine outpatient clinics at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Ankara, Türkiye between January 2024 and June 2024. Eligible participants included individuals aged ≥65 y and adults aged ≥18 y with at least one pneumococcal risk condition. Participants were recruited consecutively during outpatient visits, and data collection was conducted in a single session via face-to-face interviews. Participants who met the eligibility criteria and provided written consent were included, forms with missing data were excluded from the analysis.

The selection of adults aged ≥65 y as an independent eligibility criterion reflects the longstanding national and international guideline recommendation that all individuals in this age group receive pneumococcal vaccination regardless of comorbidity status. Inclusion of adults aged ≥18 y with at least one established risk condition mirrors the complementary recommendation tier of the same guidelines, thereby defining the full population for whom vaccination is currently indicated.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for the study were defined as follows: (1) being 65 y of age or older or being an adult aged 18 y or older with risk factors for pneumococcal disease, (2) agreeing to participate in the study and providing written informed consent, and (3) having the clinical and cognitive competence to answer the survey questions. Exclusion criteria were as follows: refusal to participate, absence of written consent, inability to complete the questionnaire due to cognitive impairment or acute clinical instability, and missing data that would preclude assessment of the primary outcome variables.

Definition of pneumococcal risk conditions

Risk conditions for pneumococcal disease have been adapted and simplified for outpatient practice based on the recommendations of the American Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices and the 2018 KLİMİK guidelines of the Turkish Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases.17–20 Risk group classification was based on medical history and self-reported information obtained during the outpatient consultation. Where clinically indicated, classification was verified using existing medical records and documented medication use. Accordingly, risk conditions were defined as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma, chronic liver disease, chronic renal failure, history of hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, chronic heart failure, diabetes mellitus, use of immunosuppressive therapy and clinical conditions suppressing the immune system, and alcohol or substance dependence.17–20

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews during a single outpatient visit. The questionnaire was developed drawing on previous vaccination awareness studies and expert consultation; however, formal psychometric validation was not performed. No pilot testing or formal reliability assessment (e.g., test-retest or internal consistency analysis) was conducted prior to study implementation. This limits the reproducibility of the awareness measures and should be considered when interpreting item-level findings. The instrument consisted of 16 items with predefined response options assessing the following domains:

Sociodemographic characteristics

Gender and education level (categorized as illiterate, primary/middle school, high school, bachelor’s degree, or postgraduate).

Risk factor assessment: Presence of pneumococcal risk conditions including age ≥65 y, COPD/asthma, chronic renal failure, chronic heart failure, diabetes mellitus, and immunosuppressive therapy.

Disease and vaccination awareness

Knowledge of pneumonia, awareness of personal risk group status, and knowledge of free vaccination eligibility.

Vaccination history: Prior pneumococcal vaccination status; prior receipt of other vaccines (Hepatitis B, influenza, tetanus, MMR, and COVID-19).

Healthcare recommendation

History of vaccination recommendations received at healthcare facilities (categorized as “1–2 times” or “Never”).

Reasons for non-vaccination/information source

For vaccinated participants, the primary source of vaccine information was recorded; for non-vaccinated participants, the main reason for non-vaccination was assessed using predefined options (lack of physician recommendation, safety concerns, efficacy doubts, unawareness of free availability, and general hesitancy).

Post-counseling vaccination intention

After completing the baseline questionnaire, participants received brief, standardized verbal information about pneumococcal disease, vaccination indications, risk groups, and free vaccination eligibility. Immediately afterward, their vaccination intention was assessed with the question, “Do you intend to get vaccinated after your clinic visit?.”

Sample size

The minimum required sample size was calculated for estimating a single proportion. Assuming a pneumococcal vaccination rate of approximately 15% based on previous Turkish studies, with at a 90% confidence level and an 8% margin of error, the required sample size was calculated as 115 participants. A total of 125 patients were assessed for eligibility; 4 declined to participate and 5 were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires. Thus, 116 participants were included in the final analysis.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 software. Variables were analyzed at the item level; no total or subscale scores were calculated. Descriptive statistics were reported as counts and percentages for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables (age). For comparative analyses, the chi-square test was used for categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell frequencies were <5. For age comparisons between two groups, the independent samples t-test was applied orthe Mann–Whitney U test when the assumption of normality was not met. Associations between categorical variables and pneumococcal vaccination status were assessed using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Post-counseling vaccination intention was analyzed as a secondary outcome among participants who had not previously received pneumococcal vaccination. Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach. A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of prior pneumococcal vaccination status. Variables with p < .20 in univariate analysis were entered simultaneously into the model. Given the small number of vaccinated participants (n = 20), the regression model should be considered exploratory; results may be subject to instability and should not be interpreted as definitive independent predictors. The two-tailed p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline awareness and vaccination status

Half of the participants (50.0%) were aware of pneumonia as a disease, while only 34.5% knew they were in a high-risk group and 31.0% were aware of free vaccination eligibility. Only 20 participants (17.2%) had received pneumococcal vaccination, with physician recommendation being the primary information source (85.0%) (Table 1). The low overall vaccination rate (17.2%) alongside moderate awareness of pneumonia (50.0%) but substantially lower awareness of personal risk group status (34.5%) and free vaccination eligibility (31.0%) indicate a pronounced knowledge-to-behavior gap in this population.

Post-counseling vaccination intention

Among the 96 previously non-vaccinated participants, 65 (67.7%) expressed intention to get vaccinated after receiving standardized information (Table 5). No significant association was found between post-counseling vaccination intention and gender, education level, or risk group. The willingness-to-vaccinate rate of 67.7% following a single brief standardized counseling session suggests short-term receptiveness to vaccination when information is proactively delivered; however, this may not reflect confirmed behavioral change, and actual post-visit vaccination rates were not assessed. Notably, participants who were previously unaware of pneumonia, their risk status, or free vaccine availability were more likely to express vaccination intention after counseling (p < .05 for all). Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 116 high-risk patients attending internal medicine outpatient clinics, we found that only 17.2% had received pneumococcal vaccination. The most striking finding was that 85% of vaccinated participants were informed by physicians, while 61.5% of non-vaccinated participants reported never being informed about their risk status. These findings suggest that physician recommendation may play an important role in pneumococcal vaccination uptake.

Despite strong evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of vaccines, vaccine hesitancy is becoming an increasingly significant problem. This trend can lead to the reemergence of preventable diseases, increased hospitalizations, and broader global health consequences.21 The World Health Organization (WHO) listed vaccine hesitancy as one of the 10 threats to global health in 2019. The reported 30% increase in measles cases serves as a notable example of this threat.22

Reasons for vaccine hesitancy include complacency, difficulty accessing vaccines, lack of confidence, and insufficient information. The WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE) which makes recommendations on immunization policies, has published two important reviews on combating vaccine hesitancy. The first review highlighted interventions that yielded positive results, including raising social awareness, training healthcare workers, providing incentives, and using reminder systems.23 The second review emphasized the importance of provider training, community-based interventions, and face-to-face communication.24 However, a key limitation of these findings is that these interventions have generally been carried out in high-income countries. Therefore, the applicability of such strategies in low- and middle-income countries warrants further investigation.

The WHO focuses on both individual and environmental determinants of vaccination behavior and recommends the use of evidence-based theoretical models to understand these behaviors. To this end, the WHO has adapted the “COM-B Model” within the framework of the Tailoring Immunization Programs (TIP) approach. The COM-B Model stands for “Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behavior” and is an approach to understanding behavior change. This model analyzes the factors required for individuals to perform a behavior and is widely used in public health improvement programs, health education, and immunization programs.25

The COM-B model addresses three key elements: capability, opportunity, and motivation. Among these, healthcare provider recommendations are particularly influential.26 In a 2024 US survey, providers with greater trust in public health authorities recommended vaccines more frequently to their patients.27 A multicounty systematic review reached similar conclusions – provider recommendation, patient awareness, and regular healthcare use were the strongest drivers of adult vaccination.28 Taken together, these data reinforce that physicians serve a dual role: they deliver vaccines and shape patient attitudes toward vaccination. Mapping our findings onto this framework: capability was deficient in the majority of non-vaccinated participants, most of whom lacked knowledge of their personal risk status and the availability of free vaccination. Opportunity was structurally present – vaccines are provided free of charge at public hospitals in Turkey – but functionally inaccessible, as physicians had not communicated eligibility to 61.5% of non-vaccinated participants. Motivation was less of a barrier than might be expected: 67.7% of previously non-vaccinated participants expressed vaccination intention after a brief information session. This suggests that once patients understand their risk status and know the vaccine is free, willingness follows relatively quickly. In this context, improving physician communication represents a potentially high-yield intervention in routine outpatient practice.

In our study, it was observed that the vast majority (85.0%) of participants who received the vaccine were informed by physicians, and that this information positively affected vaccination rates. Awareness alone, however, is not sufficient – our cross-sectional data suggest that even patients who reports awareness of their risk status may decline vaccination when other barriers exist. Informing individuals in high-risk groups that vaccines are free further strengthens this tendency. These findings are broadly consistent with international evidence demonstrating persistently low adult pneumococcal vaccination rates despite guideline availability. A large Canadian longitudinal study (CLSA) found that approximately half of adults aged ≥65 y and over 80% of those aged 47–64 with at least one chronic condition had never received a pneumococcal vaccine; both recent contact with a family physician and prior influenza vaccination were significantly associated with lower odds of non-vaccination in that cohort, reinforcing the importance of healthcare engagement as a structural determinant of uptake.12 Closer to home, a Turkish primary care study demonstrated that targeted physician counseling – delivered either by telephone or face-to-face – increased pneumococcal vaccination rates from 2.5% to 73.5% among adults aged ≥65 y, with face-to-face encounters proving substantially more effective (81.6% vs 59.8%); this finding directly mirrors our observation that 85.0% of vaccinated participants in the present study cited physician recommendation as their primary information source.13 Our overall vaccination rate of 17.2% is consistent with rates reported in other Turkish high-risk adult populations and remains substantially lower than rates reported in high-income European settings, highlighting opportunities to further strengthen adult pneumococcal vaccination strategies in Turkish outpatient practice.15,16

When examining the reasons for not getting vaccinated, the most common reason (61.5%) was that physicians did not inform participants that they were in a high-risk group. Beyond lack of awareness, smaller proportions cited safety doubts (7.3%), efficacy concerns (1.1%), or unawareness of free availability (13.5%). Among hesitant individuals, no single barrier dominated, pointing to the need for tailored rather than uniform messaging. These findings underscore the need for more targeted communication, particularly regarding vaccine safety and efficacy.

In a 2021 study by Gatwood and colleagues examining the reasons for vaccine hesitancy among adults at high risk for pneumococcal disease, it was found that healthcare providers were inadequate in their vaccine recommendations.29 Taitel and colleagues showed that recommending the influenza and pneumococcal vaccines simultaneously led to a significant increase in vaccination rates. In this context, strategic efforts to co-administer the pneumococcal vaccine with other vaccines may be important for increasing vaccination rates.30

When pneumococcal and influenza vaccines are recommended together, especially in older adults and individuals with chronic diseases, the likelihood of receiving both vaccines concurrently is high. When participants are advised to receive influenza vaccine, they may become more inclined to accept the pneumococcal vaccine as well. This suggests that individuals tend to assess their health risks holistically. Individuals who act on one health recommendation are generally more inclined to adopt others; for example, those who receive the influenza vaccine tend to also undergo regular medical check-ups or receive other vaccines. Jointly promoting pneumococcal and influenza vaccines during outpatient visits may be an effective strategy for increasing uptake of both vaccines, especially in high-risk populations.

The vast majority of vaccinated participants reported receiving their last dose between three and five years prior; however, a notable subset showed low vaccination continuity.

In the present study, demographic factors (such as education level, gender, and risk group) were not significantly associated with vaccination uptake. This points to the importance of raising awareness of vaccination throughout society. Efforts to address vaccine hesitancy should therefore target all education levels and demographic groups.29 Age, however, was an exception. Vaccinated participants were on average five years older than non-vaccinated participants, and awareness of free vaccination was also more common among older individuals. This pattern likely reflects more frequent clinic attendance and greater cumulative exposure to health information over time, rather than any intrinsic difference in health literacy between age groups.

Healthcare workers are one of the factors that most influence vaccination decisions. However, available evidence suggests that information provided in the outpatient setting does not always translate into vaccination behavior. A study has shown that healthcare workers who are knowledgeable about vaccination recommend vaccines more frequently and feel more confident when discussing the topic with patients.31 A 2018 survey of 165 family physicians in Elazığ examined the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of primary care physicians regarding vaccines. The survey found that only 47.5% of family physicians actively sought information on vaccines, a rate considered suboptimal for a vaccination-recommending specialty.32

A notable pattern emerged after the counseling session. Participants who had been unaware of their risk status were more likely to express vaccination intention after counseling, though this association should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design. In contrast, those who already knew they were at risk – but had still declined – remained more resistant, suggesting that deeper barriers: entrenched hesitancy, distrust, or unresolved safety concerns that brief information alone cannot overcome. It should be noted that post-counseling intention was assessed immediately after a brief verbal information session; whether this translates into actual vaccination behavior remains unknown and constitutes an important limitation of the present findings.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and reduces the generalizability of the results. Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences to be drawn. Third, data were based on self-report and are therefore subject to recall bias; vaccination status was not verified against pharmacy or clinical records. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single healthcare institution using consecutive convenience sampling, which substantially limits external validity. Patients attending a tertiary outpatient clinic may differ meaningfully from those in primary care or community-based settings in terms of disease burden, health literacy, and prior exposure to physician counseling. Findings should therefore not be generalized to community-based populations or healthcare settings outside tertiary urban hospitals in Turkey. Multi-center studies drawing from primary care and rural settings are needed to establish more generalizable estimates of pneumococcal vaccination awareness and uptake. Fifth, the 16-item questionnaire was developed de novo based on prior studies and expert consultation but did not undergo formal psychometric validation. No pilot testing, test-retest reliability assessment, or internal consistency analysis was performed. The absence of psychometric validation limits the reproducibility of the awareness and attitude measures and may affect the internal validity of findings. Future studies should use validated instruments or undergo rigorous pre-testing procedures. Sixth, post-counseling data reflect stated vaccination intention immediately after a brief verbal information session and may not reliably predict actual vaccination behavior. Seventh, the logistic regression model was likely underpowered given the small number of vaccinated participants (n = 20). With multiple predictors entered simultaneously, the model is susceptible to instability and overfitting, and the apparent significance of male gender may not be reproducible in larger samples. All regression findings should therefore be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Finally, the long-term effects of the counseling intervention were not evaluated, and epidemiological data (serotype distribution, antibiotic resistance) were not included. These findings suggest that pneumococcal vaccination rates may be improved, particularly in high-risk populations, by promoting free vaccination opportunities and ensuring more targeted information provision by healthcare professionals. However, raising vaccination rates at a population level requires broader awareness campaigns and more systematic approaches to information dissemination. These findings may provide useful guidance for preventing pneumococcal diseases and improving public health.

Further research is needed in this area. Larger, prospective multicenter studies from different regions of Turkey would help validate our findings. Reminder systems, physician training programs, and co-administration of influenza and pneumococcal vaccines are among the interventions worth evaluating. The 2024 ACIP recommendation for adults aged ≥50 years represents a recent update in vaccination policy. Future revisions of national recommendations may consider emerging evidence together with local epidemiological data and healthcare priorities.

At the level of clinical practice, our findings suggest an association between brief, standardized physician counseling and more positive patient attitudes toward vaccination, though a causal interpretation is not possible from this study design. Integrating pneumococcal vaccine assessment into routine outpatient visits – similar to existing influenza vaccination practices – and providing continued education to clinicians on adult immunization guidelines may therefore represent practical and achievable steps toward reducing the burden of pneumococcal disease in high-risk populations in Turkey.
