Maternal Socioeconomic Opportunity and Race‐Ethnicity Intersect to Influence Fetal Brain Volumes in Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: William T Reynolds, Chunyan Wang, Valeria Rosas, Divya Patel, Jodie K Votava‐Smith, Jacqueline Weinberg, Julia Wallace, Nhu Tran, Rafael Ceschin, Anthony Fabio, Vidya Rajagopalan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurodevelopmental outcome disparities, partially linked to socioeconomic status, race‐ethnicity, persist in congenital heart disease (CHD). Fetal brain development is a key predictor of subsequent neurocognitive outcomes.MethodsMagnetic resonance imaging was performed on fetuses with and without complex CHD at 2 sites. Childhood Opportunity Index (COI) and maternal race‐ethnicity were used to assess neighborhood and individual‐level maternal social determinants of health, respectively. The contributions of COI and race‐ethnicity on fetal brain volume, controlling for fetal gestational age and sex, were compared using multivariable regression between both groups.ResultsAmong 168 fetuses in this analysis, 106 (63.10%) were from non‐Hispanic White mothers, 43 (25.60%) were from Hispanic mothers, and 19 (11.31%) were from non‐Hispanic Black mothers. Total brain volume in the group with CHD (n=49) was significantly associated with the interaction of maternal race‐ethnicity and the COI Social and Economic subdomain Score (β=−0.74, P<0.001). White matter volume increased with maternal COI Social and Economic subdomain Score within the group with CHD (β=0.42, P=0.04). Maternal race‐ethnicity, COI Social and Economic subdomain Score, and their interaction accounted for 30% of the variance in total fetal brain volume in the group with CHD, although the same effect was not noted in the control group.ConclusionsResults indicate that maternal COI likely modifies the relationship between maternal race‐ethnicity and fetal brain development in CHD. The impact of maternal race‐ethnicity and COI on brain development differs between fetuses with and without CHD. Addressing maternal social determinants of health could reduce disparities in neurodevelopmental outcomes in CHD. Although limitations in sample size temper conclusions, initial analysis suggests that maternal social determinants of health and environment can contribute to neurodevelopmental outcome variance in individuals with CHD.Keywords: fetal brain, maternal race and ethnicity, neurodevelopment, social determinants of healthSubject Categories: Race and Ethnicity, Pregnancy, Pediatrics, Social Determinants of Health

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Fetal brain development in congenital heart disease may be shaped by maternal social determinants of health and race‐ethnicity, with disparities in neighborhood‐level resources, captured by the Childhood Opportunity Index, associated with altered brain volumes.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Brain developmental disparities among children with congenital heart disease and from minority and low‐resourced families may begin as reduced fetal brain volume, which indicates that social determinants of health‐focused interventional programs to alleviate outcome disparities in congenital heart disease should start prenatally.

Over the past decade, steady advances in prenatal diagnosis, surgical techniques, and cardiac care have ensured that children born with critical congenital heart disease (CHD) will now live into adulthood. As the focus of the medical community shifts to ensuring the quality of life in survivors of CHD of all ages, neurodevelopmental deficiencies have emerged as its most significant determinant. 1 Although its contributors are multifactorial and multifaceted, neurodevelopmental deficiencies in CHD are known to begin as impaired fetal brain development. 2 , 3 This has revealed a unique window of opportunity when improving fetal brain development could mitigate neurodevelopmental deficiencies across the lifespan. Leveraging this window begins with understanding the impact of various prenatal factors that not only affect fetal brain development in CHD but are also amenable to interventions. However, neurodevelopmental interventions for CHD have long been hindered by the substantial variability in outcomes, which cannot be adequately explained by patient‐specific medical factors alone. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Although biological factors play a significant role in brain development, it is well established that social, cultural, and environmental factors may also strongly affect it. 8 , 9 These disparities are especially apparent when examining the unequal burden of neurodevelopmental impairments across different disease models that are often tied to social determinants of health (SDOH). 10 This is similarly observed in studies of survivors of CHD at various ages where variability in neurodevelopmental outcomes, despite similar medical risks, is consistently attributed to an individual’s socioeconomic status (SES). SES includes insurance coverage, access to specialty care centers, family income, and educational background. 11 , 12 However, SES is just 1 element within the complex, multilayered web through which SDOH influences neurodevelopment alongside the pathophysiology of CHD. The Childhood Opportunity Index 13 (COI) is an innovative and comprehensive metric that quantifies neighborhood‐level resources and opportunities paramount for achieving optimal child development at the neighborhood level. The COI, consisting of 29 indicators spanning education, health and environment, and social and economic subdomains, provides a validated quantitative tool for evaluating and comparing the resources and opportunities in neighborhoods that impact healthy child development across the United States. COI is increasingly used to understand the multidimensional pathways through which SDOH affects outcomes in various diseases. 14 , 15 , 16 Emerging literature shows that low COI is independently associated with poor CHD surgical outcomes. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 However, no studies investigate the associations between multidimensional SDOH measures on fetal brain development in CHD despite evidence of the known impact of prenatal SDOH on brain development. 21 , 22 , 23

In this paper, we leverage a geographically and sociodemographically diverse cohort to examine how maternal neighborhood opportunity, measured by COI, influences fetal brain development in CHD. Race is not a biological or genetic factor in brain development. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Maternal race‐ethnicity, as a social construct, is a proxy for other latent, individual disadvantages (eg, disparities in access, quality of care, literacy and communication, implicit bias, structural and interpersonal racism, etc) known to affect maternal and fetal outcomes. 28 , 29 , 30 Neighborhood opportunity frequently correlates with the racial and ethnic composition of the population living in the neighborhood. 31 Fetal brain volume (BV) is a robust predictor of subsequent neurodevelopmental outcomes in children with CHD. 2 Therefore, we used maternal COI and race‐ethnicity to compare the impact of community and individual‐level SDOH factors, respectively, on fetal BVs of those with and without CHD.

METHODS

Data Availability

Derived volumetric data and limited patient demographic information are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and completion of institutional data‐sharing agreements.

Ethics

The protocol for this study was approved by and followed the guidelines of each respective site’s institutional review board. Written and approved consent was received for each subject unless the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was obtained retrospectively from a clinical MRI, for which data approval was granted through the institutional review board of either site. All analyses were conducted in agreement with institutional guidelines and regulations.

Subject Recruitment

Pregnant mothers were prospectively recruited at 2 sites, Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh and Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles. Participants included in this study were recruited as part of an ongoing, longitudinal study. Mothers in Los Angeles were recruited from November 2016 to January 2022 using flyers, social media ads, and referrals from partner clinics at Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles. Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh recruited pregnant mothers from February 2014 to January 2022 using informational flyers placed at cardiac clinics, obstetrics and gynecology offices, maternity wards, and through referrals via pediatric cardiologists. For the group with CHD, inclusion criteria were prenatal diagnosis of CHD based on fetal echocardiogram and fetal MRI performed between 24 and 38 weeks gestational age. For the group without CHD, the inclusion criterion was documented evidence of an uncomplicated pregnancy (such as an ultrasound screening report). Some enrolled participants without CHD underwent fetal echocardiograms, which was normal, due to increased risk for CHD (eg, positive family history). Exclusion criteria for both groups were (1) severe congenital brain malformation detected on prenatal imaging; (2) significant chromosomal abnormality/syndrome; (3) significant birth trauma or hypoxic–ischemic injury; (4) maternal contraindication to MRI (eg, claustrophobia, pacemaker); or (5) prenatally diagnosed extracardiac abnormalities.

Childhood Opportunity Index

The COI is a measurement tool used to assess and quantify the quality of opportunities available to children in various communities or geographic areas. It aims to provide insights into the conditions and resources that can have a significant impact on a child’s development, well‐being, and future prospects, including the SDOH factors of education, health care, economic stability, neighborhood environment, social and community support, family and social connections, access to technology and information, and nutrition. By quantifying these disparities, it becomes possible to target interventions, allocate resources more effectively, and advocate for policies that aim to improve children’s well‐being and life prospects in disadvantaged communities. 13 This index has been nationally normed, providing a standardized measure of the quality of these resources.

Using self‐reported maternal zip code information collected at the time of the MRI visit, 4 COI scores were derived from DiversityDataKids.org’s COI 2.0 database 13 for each mother–fetus pair: an overall score and scores for 3 subdomains—health and environment, education, and social and economic. Nationally normed values were used to compare individuals across both sites. Maternal addresses recorded during prenatal study visits were used to derive COI scores for each participant.

Race‐Ethnicity Reporting

Expectant mothers completed a maternal demographic questionnaire at the time of the MRI. Racial categories in the questionnaire included White, Black, Asian, unspecified, or other. Participants were allowed to include multiple race values. Ethnicity categories included non‐Hispanic, Hispanic, and unspecified. Electronic medical records were used to fill in missing data. Values from the maternal questionnaire were prioritized over electronic medical records values if a discrepancy was noted. Consistent with national findings, Hispanic participants in our study did not self‐identify with any of the racial categories. 32 Because race and ethnicity are social constructs, we combined them into a single variable to accurately represent participant experience while agreeing with recommended practices. 33 We used 3 categories to represent maternal race‐ethnicity: Hispanic, non‐Hispanic Black (NHB), and non‐Hispanic White. We excluded 13 Asian participants and 8 participants with race‐ethnicity marked as “unspecified” or “other.” In agreement with Circulation’s guidelines to not use a combined “Other” race or ethnicity category to capture smaller population groups, no combined group was used for this study. 34 , 35

Cardiac Classifications

Cardiac defects were classified by a pediatric cardiologist (J.V.S.) into 5 groups based on prenatal and postnatal echo. The groupings are as follows: biventricular without arch obstruction, biventricular with arch obstruction, single ventricle without arch obstruction, single ventricle with arch obstruction, and transposition of the great arteries.

Image Acquisition

Pregnant mothers were imaged at either site between 24 and 38 weeks gestational age. Imaging sessions conducted at Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh used a 3T Skyra Siemens with a multichannel body coil. Half‐Fourier acquisition single‐shot turbo spin echo imaging was performed with parameters of echo time=78 ms, repetition time=1100 ms, 3 mm slice thickness, no interslice gap, and 1.33×1.33 mm in‐plane resolution. Two images were collected for each anatomic plane of the brain, resulting in 6 images per subject. To achieve harmonized image quality, Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles used the following parameters on a 3.0 T Philips Achieva scanner (Netherlands): multiplanar single‐shot turbo spin echo imaging was performed with parameters of echo time=160 ms, repetition time=9000–12 000 ms, 3 mm slice thickness, no interslice gap, 1×1 mm in‐plane resolution. At both sites, subjects were scanned 3 times in each of 3 planes, resulting in 9 stacks per subject. Image acquisition was monitored in real time to ensure a minimum of 6 stacks (2 per plane) of low‐motion images.

Image Processing

Fetal MRI images from both sites were reconstructed after motion correction and segmented using an identical pipeline previously validated by our group. 36 An example of reconstructed images and subsequent brain parcellation can be seen in Figure 1. Volumes of the following tissue regions were extracted from segmentations: cortical plate, developing white matter, intraventricular cerebrospinal fluid, extra‐axial cerebrospinal fluid, deep gray tissues, cerebellum, hippocampal‐amygdala complex, and brainstem. Total BV (TBV) was calculated for each subject as the sum of all tissues and is used as the primary outcome for this study. Regional volumes are used as secondary outcome measures.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in SAS 9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Because gestational age and the effect of fetal sex are significant predictors of fetal BV, both were regressed out, and the residuals were used in this study. 37 The residuals of BVs indicate the difference between the individual’s total (or regional) BV and the expected value. These residuals are used throughout all analyses and will be called BVs for simplicity.

The primary dependent variable was TBV. Secondarily, separate regression models used each regional BV as a dependent variable. Univariate linear regression models, with TBV as the dependent variable, were explored for variable selection before undertaking multivariable modeling. Independent variables of interest included were CHD status, site (Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh or Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles), race‐ethnicity, COI scores (overall, health, education, social‐economic subdomain scores), and an interaction term between COI scores and race‐ethnicity. Independent variables with P values reaching a significance of ≤0.20 were included in the final model. 38 Among all COI scores, the subdomain, and the overall score, only the Social and Economic subdomain Score (COI‐SES) was determined to be significant. This univariate regression also confirmed no site‐specific differences in our data. Our final multivariable linear regression model was stratified by CHD status and included race‐ethnicity, COI‐SES subdomain score, and their interaction as independent variables. Standardized regression coefficients (number of SDs of change in outcome variable for 1 SD change in independent variable) are reported for ease of interpretation.

Model Explained Variance

Using adjusted R‐squared as an unbiased estimator for multiple regression, we computed the proportion of variability in TBV or regional BV explained by the multivariable model.

RESULTS

Subject Demographics

Among the 200 participants included in this cohort, 11 were excluded for missing values of sex, race, or ethnicity. Additionally, 21 participants who did not match 1 of the 3 categories listed previously were excluded from the analysis. The final sample included 49 participants in the group with CHD and 119 participants in the group without CHD. Table 1 lists the demographic and medical characteristics of our study sample. The cohort contained more male than female fetuses in the group with CHD compared with the group without CHD (P=0.022). Additionally, fetuses with CHD were older at the time of MRI compared with those without CHD (P=0.028). Across both sites, Hispanic and NHB participants were primarily from the lowest COI tertile, whereas non‐Hispanic White participants were primarily in the highest COI tertile. None of the fetuses in the study had major brain malformation or acquired brain injury on MRI, as confirmed by a pediatric neuroradiologist (A.P.). To examine the distribution of gestational ages within participants, overall COI was split into 3 groupings: low (0<X≤33), medium (33<X≤66, high), and high (66<X≤100). Gestational ages were not significantly different between the groups (P=0.109), and the average gestational ages for low, medium, and high were 32.58 (±4.12), 32.98 (±3.02), and 31.62 (±3.16) weeks, respectively. Association of Race‐Ethnicity

A marginally significant negative relationship existed in the group with CHD between NHB race‐ethnicity and hippocampus‐amygdala volumes (P=0.08) (Table 2). There were no significant differences in TBV based on maternal race‐ethnicity.

In the group without CHD, deep gray brain (P=0.02) and brainstem (P=0.01) volumes were significantly larger in Hispanic versus non‐Hispanic White mothers’ fetuses. Fetuses of NHB mothers had larger cortical plate volumes than non‐Hispanic White mothers (P=0.05).

Interaction of COI and Race‐Ethnicity

In the group with CHD, we found a significant negative interaction of COI‐SES and NHB status with TBV (P<0.01). We also noted a marginally significant interaction of COI‐SES and NHB with hippocampus‐amygdala volume (P=0.08) in the group with CHD. In the group without CHD, there were no significant interactions between COI‐SES and race‐ethnicity with TBV or any other regional brain structures.

Model Explained Variance

Table 3 shows the adjusted R2 value, as a measure of explained variance, for all models from Table 2 with statistical significance. After accounting for gestational age and sex, our model shows that race‐ethnicity, maternal COI, and their interaction explained 30% of the variance in TBV for the group with CHD compared with 17% in the group without CHD. For regional tissue volumes, our multivariable model explained 10.05% of the variance in developing white matter volume for the group with CHD. Within the group without CHD, our model explained variance for brainstem, cortical plate, and deep gray volumes are 11.82%, 1%, and 23.69%, respectively.

DISCUSSION

Our results show that differences in BV are likely linked to the interplay of maternal race‐ethnicity and neighborhood opportunities in fetuses with CHD. Specifically, within the group with CHD, BV was the lowest among fetuses of NHB mothers residing in neighborhoods with more adverse social and economic opportunities (low COI‐SES). Moreover, our findings emphasize that the impact of race‐ethnicity and neighborhood opportunity on BV is different for fetuses with and without CHD. Our findings illustrate, for the first time, the influence of prenatal SDOH on early fetal brain development, which can impact early neurodevelopmental outcomes in CHD.

The well‐established disparities in CHD outcomes related to race‐ethnicity and socioeconomic status raise critical questions about their interplay and impact. Studies consistently show that non‐White racial backgrounds and lower SES are associated with adverse cardiac care outcomes, including higher mortality rates, poor surgical outcomes, and increased hospital readmission rates. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Neurodevelopmental outcomes, recognized as significant long‐term morbidities of CHD, also exhibit disparities along racial‐ethnic and socioeconomic lines. 44 , 45 , 46 However, limited data exist on the role of SDOH in shaping neurodevelopmental outcomes in CHD, with only a few studies investigating insurance status as a potential predictor of school‐age outcomes. 47 , 48 There is a particular paucity of data regarding how SDOH disparities may affect early brain development despite mounting evidence around the importance of this period on long‐term outcomes.

Leveraging a uniquely high representation of minority families from our 2 sites, our study is the first to systematically demonstrate that maternal race‐ethnicity and SDOH interact to affect fetal brain development in CHD rather than acting in isolation. This finding aligns with existing literature, which highlights the additional challenges faced by children with CHD from racial‐ethnic minority families due to the intergenerational effects of adverse SDOH. Furthermore, consistent with other findings, 20 , 49 , 50 our cohort reflects a link between neighborhood opportunity (COI) and race‐ethnicity, warranting further investigations into the role of systemic race‐based disadvantages for minorities with CHD. Traditionally, race‐ethnicity and SES were treated as independent risk factors in CHD health disparities research. In line with evidence from broader health disparities research and emerging findings in CHD outcomes, 20 our study also underscores the effectiveness of multifaceted indices of SDOH at the neighborhood level in better explaining inequalities in health outcomes. 51 , 52 Additionally, employing a nationally normed measure of neighborhood opportunity provides a quantitative means to compare geographic regions with varying costs of living and economic opportunities, including the extent of public insurance coverage. Our findings demonstrate differences in regional BVs based on maternal race‐ethnicity among those without CHD. Here, we reiterate that race‐ethnicity is not a physiological driver of brain development but a proxy for individual disadvantages affecting underrepresented, minoritized people in the United States. We purport that interventions addressing neurodevelopmental disparities in CHD should not only target economic factors but also be grounded in sociocultural competence.

Higher COI‐SES is associated with increased white matter volumes within the CHD group. This association between fetal white matter volume and parental SES has been observed in healthy fetuses but was not seen in our cohort. 22 These differences in findings are likely due to a more substantial representation of families with lower SES and considerations for race‐ethnic variations in our study. Impaired maternal‐fetal environment and fetal brain dysmaturity are associated with an increased risk of postnatal white matter injury in CHD. 53 , 54 , 55 This raises the question of the contribution of low neighborhood opportunity and related maternal health risks to postnatal brain injury risks in children with CHD. 56 , 57 Larger, longitudinal studies are necessary to unravel discrepancies in fetal brain maturity and white matter injury prevalence among children from various levels of neighborhood opportunity. In contrast, our studies found differences in the brainstem, cortical gray matter, and deep gray matter volume based on maternal race‐ethnicity in fetuses and those without CHD. These brain regions are often implicated in studies of the impact of parental social adversities and psychological stress on non‐CHD, postnatal brain development. 58 , 59 , 60 Given the high collinearity between race‐ethnicity and social adversities, common underlying mechanisms may be at play. Nevertheless, differences in how parental socioeconomic adversities are characterized and the inconsistent inclusion of race‐ethnicity limit direct comparisons of findings across studies. Interestingly, these subcortical structures have also shown impairment in fetuses 61 and infants with CHD, 62 , 63 , 64 suggesting that regional vulnerabilities in CHD may differ between the intrauterine versus postnatal time points. In CHD, fetal white matter vulnerability is potentially driven by the cumulative impact of genetic, placental, hypoxia, and SES disturbances on common developmental pathways. This finding contrasts with environmental influences, which may postnatally affect both subcortical and white matter structures.

This work shows the impact of race‐ethnicity and neighborhood opportunity on total and regional BVs in fetuses with and without CHD. Second, our study demonstrates the differential impact of race‐ethnicity and SDOH factors on brain development in fetuses with and without CHD. We hypothesize that these differences are attributable to complex mechanistic interactions between CHD and adverse SDOH‐related perturbations in developmental physiology, genetics, and epigenetics during the gestational period. Nevertheless, this underscores the importance of exercising caution when translating interventions designed to enhance neurodevelopment in populations without CHD to those with CHD. More extensive longitudinal studies are needed to examine if observed differences in fetal brain development, based on maternal race‐ethnicity and neighborhood opportunity, persist into childhood and correlate with neurodevelopmental outcomes.

STUDY LIMITATIONS

Our findings should be interpreted considering some limitations. Given the smaller sample size, our analysis should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating. This study suggests a potential relationship between maternal SDOH and fetal brain development. Future research with larger sample sizes is needed to elucidate better the complex interrelationships among various SDOH factors, CHD‐associated medical factors, and fetal development. Second, the distribution of participants across multiple races and ethnicities and neighborhood opportunity levels was nonuniform but consistent with the local demographics.

Additionally, our study uses race‐ethnicity as a social construct and does not reflect genetic influences. Due to many Hispanic families not indicating a specific race, the need for combined race‐ethnic categories limited our ability to differentiate between various racial and ethnic groups. Next, we did not include assessments of maternal psychological stress, maternal health conditions, nutritional status, or environmental exposures (eg, lead, air pollutants) in our analysis, which are disproportionately higher in low‐opportunity neighborhoods and are linked to fetal brain development. Our study assumed that the impact of SDOH is uniform across the gestational ages studied and does not capture the possible time‐varying impact of SDOH. We recognize the effect of SDOH can vary across gestational ages, stemming from brain structures maturing at varying rates. Future studies with larger sample sizes could disentangle the time‐varying impact of SDOH on fetal brain development.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings underscore the intricate interplay between maternal race‐ethnicity, neighborhood opportunity, and CHD in shaping fetal brain development. The disparities observed in BVs among fetuses with CHD, particularly for those of non‐Hispanic Black mothers from low‐resourced neighborhoods, highlight the critical impact of adverse maternal neighborhood opportunity on neurodevelopment. Addressing SDOH disparities, especially in socioeconomic aspects, emerges as a crucial factor independently associated with reduced fetal BV in CHD. Our findings provide early evidence for leveraging neighborhood‐level SDOH measures to identify vulnerable families who could benefit from targeted socioeconomic support programs. Our findings indicate a relationship between maternal SDOH and fetal brain development; however, future studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to better understand the impact of maternal SDOH in addition to medical or clinical factors on this critical development period.
