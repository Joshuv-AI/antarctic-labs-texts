Occurrence of Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and Carbapenemase-producing Escherichia coli isolated from Childhood Diarrhoea in Yaoundé, Cameroon
Authors: Saleh Mahamat, Raspail Carrel Founou, Luria Leslie Founou, Thibau Flaurant Tchouangueu, Brice Davy Dimani, Omer Aurelle Nkengkana, Lethicia Danaëlle Mafo, David Chelo, Marie Christine Fonkoua, Yap Boum-II, Hortense Gonsu, Michel Noubom, Paul Olivier Koki Ndombo, Donatien Gatsing
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExtended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing pathogenic E. coli is a global public health issue, especially in sub-Saharan African countries such as Cameroon. It contributes to increase significantly hospital length of stay, morbidity, mortality and economic costs because of treatment failures. This study aims at determining the resistance background and virulence profiles of ESBL-E. coli isolates among childhood diarrhoea during the cholera outbreak occuring in Yaoundé, Cameroon.Materials and methodsDuring a four-month periods, from March 1st to June 30th, 2023, a total of 84 stool samples were collected from 90 under five children presenting clinical signs of gastroenteritis and attending four hospitals in Yaoundé, Cameroon. Bacterial identification was done using API20E and antimicrobial susceptibility test was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method. After extraction, genomic DNA was subjected to conventional and multiplex polymerase chain reaction methods (PCRs) for detection of resistance and virulence genes. Statistical analysis was performed using Epi info™ (7.2.5.0). Statistical significance was considered at a p-value < 0.05.ResultsOut of 150 patients contacted, 90 patients were enrolled, 84 samples were collected, 52.38%(44/84) and 3.57%(03/84) were confirmed as extended-spectrum β-lactamase and carbapenemase-producing E. coli respectively. The risk factors were analyzed, and children who drank natural fruit juice (OR: 0.4, p-value: 0.03) were found to be significantly associated with ESBL-producing E. coli. The ESBL-producing E. coli isolates showed a high level of resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime, colistin, and tetracycline. The blaCTX−M was more prevalent ß-lactamase resistance gene. The tetracycline resistance genes tet(A) and tet(B) were also detected. The most important virulence genes detected were FimH (81.81%) and papA (79.54%).ConclusionThese findings suggest implementing routine surveillance and screening for antimicrobial resistance among children under five. Antimicrobial stewardship strategies (ASP) need to be implemented to curb the emergence and dissemination of ESBL-producing E. coli. In addition, a national surveillance program for antimicrobial resistance needs to be implemented at local and regional levels in order to reduce morbidity in Cameroon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03559-z.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an important public health concern associated with socio-economic and clinical consequences worldwide [1–4]. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-Ec) has been described as bacteria responsible for gastroenteritis leading to diarrhoea among vulnerable populations such as children under five years old. In 2017, diarrhoeal diseases were globally associated with nearly 1.7 billion cases of childhood diarrhoeal diseases and 525 000 deaths among under five children annually. In 2010, numerous foodborne bacteria were involved in diarrheal diseases causing 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths globally and the most common causative agents responsible for childhood diarrhoea were pathogenic Escherichia coli [5].

Diarrhoeagenic E. coli (DEC) including Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC) and Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) have been described as the leading cause of acute infant diarrhoea associated with abdominal cramping, watery diarrhoea and fever/nausea [6]. The Global Enteric Multicenter Study (GEMS) report’s, investigating the aetiology of diarrhoea among children under five in seven countries reported that DEC, particularly EPEC were the leading causes of severe childhood diarrhoea in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [7]. Diarrhoeagenic E. coli isolates exhibit numerous virulence factors (VFs) including the P-fimbrae encoding gene (papA) and the type 1 fimbriae gene (FimH), which play a key role in bacterial infections and disease severity.

The 2019 global burden of antimicrobial resistance revealed E. coli as the top of six leading pathogens responsible for deaths associated with AMR [3]. A report in Ghana shown a high prevalence of AMR associated with diverse bacteria including E. coli implicated (49.1%] in diarrhoeal diseases among children under five [4]. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing-E. coli (ESBL-Ec) is commonly implicated in community and hospital -acquired infections [8]. It exhibits numerous ESBLs enzymes such as CTX-M, SHV and TEM to escape the activities of third generation of cephalosporins reducing there by effective therapeutic options [9]. This ability acquired by E. coli to produce these enzymes is due to the misuse and overuse of antibiotics such as cephalosporins in human medicine [8]. This situation was exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic as well as cholera outbreak recently observed in Yaoundé, Cameroon. In Cameroon, financial constraints preclude the active surveillance of AMR in general, leading to overlook increasing multidrug resistance producing- Escherichia coli (MDR-Ec) and ESBL-Ec in particular [10]. Neither the prevalence of MDR-Ec nor that of ESBL-Ec colonizing or infecting under five children with diarrhoea in communities and hospitals is known in the country [11, 12]. This situation was exacerbated during COVID-19 pandemic and cholerae outbreak recently observed in Cameroon. The antimicrobial resistant E. coli is worsened due to the lack of capacity to diagnose and manage efficiently diarrheagenic ESBL-Ec among children under five in Cameroon. This study aims at determining the antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence factors associated with diarrheagenic ESBL-Ec among childhood diarrhoeal during cholera outbreak occuring in Yaoundé, Cameroon.

Materials and methods

Study design, area and period

The cross-sectional study was conducted among four hospitals namely Cite verte District Hospital (CDH), Effoulan District Hospital (EDH), Biyem-Assi District Hospital (BDH) and Mother and Child Health Center of the Chantal Biya Foundation (CMB) in Yaoundé, Cameroon. During a four-month periods, from March 01th, to June 30th, 2023, children under five attending these hospitals were recruited upon informed consent provided by their parents or legal guardian. All patient parents or guardians provided written informed consent to participate. All the physical examination, the sociodemographic, health history and clinical information of participants were collected from the parent or legal guardian that met all inclusion criteria (being a child less than five years of age; having diarrhoea that fulfil the World Health Organization criteria., 2024).

Sample collection, bacterial culture and identification

A total of 84 stool samples were collected and analysed in the study. One stool sample was collected from each child in a sterile container and transported to the microbiology laboratory in cool box for immediate examination. The samples were examined for consistency, and the presence of blood, mucous or occult blood. The samples were inoculated into MacConkey agar (ThermoScientific, United States) and incubated at 37 °C in aerobic condition for 24 h. After incubation during 24 h, the suspected growing colonies were purified and identified by colonial characteristics, Gram staining and biochemical testing using API 20E (BioMerieux, Marcy L’Étoile, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions respectively.

.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

The Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method was used to perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and interpretation was done according to the Antibiogram Committee of the French Society of Microbiology (CA-SFM) 2023. All ESBL-Ec isolates were subjected to a panel of thirteen antibiotics including amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefixime (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg), imipenem (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), fosfomycin (30 µg), Tetracycline (30 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg) and trimethoprim (30 µg) (Thermo Scientific™ Oxoid, UK) for evaluation. To ensure the quality of our results, E. coli ATCC 35218, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 reference strains were used as control strains for the media, antimicrobial susceptibility testing and ESBL screening.

ESBL-phenotype screening methods

Extended-spectrum β-lacatamase-producing E. coli were double screened using the first time a double-disk synergy test (DDT) combining clavulanic acid and ceftazidime discs and secondly, the confirmation was done using the ESBL chromogenic agar CHROMagar™ ESBL (CHROMagar, Paris, France). More specifically, all strains were firstly tested against cefotaxime and ceftazidime for ESBL screening using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. When the inhibition zone’s was under 22 mm for ceftazidime and under 27 mm for cefotaxime coupled with synergy test positive, they were considered as potential ESBL producers as interpreted by following the (CA-SFM) 2023 guidelines. These strains were selected for a further phenotypic confirmatory test by plating onto ChromAgar ESBL media most specific for ESBL producer strains. The isolates were then stored at -20 °C in cryotubes containing trypticase soya supplemented with 20% glycerol.

Polymerase chain reaction methods (PCRs)

The genomic DNA of ESBL-Ec was extracted using a method previously described [13]. Briefly, the extraction occurring by mixed two pure colonies onto 400 µL of a solution of Tris EDTA (10 mM Tris; 0.1 mM EDTA). The mixture was boiled at 95 °C for 25 min using a dry bath (MIULab DKT200-1, Lasec International Ltd., Johannesburg, South Africa). This was followed by centrifugation of the suspension at 9500 rpm for 5 min. Finally, to ascertain the purity of our DNA, quantification was done using Qubit fluorometer 5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A final volume of 150 µL of the supernatant containing the DNA was transferred to an eppendorf tube and frozen at -20 °C for further analysis. Amplification of resistant and virulence genes including blaCTX−M,blaTEM,blaSHV,blaOXA−48,tet(A), tet(B), FimH and papA were performed by conventional and multiplex methods using a thermal cycler BIO-RAD T100 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). The amplification runing was followed the different steps including: initial denaturation (95ºC for 3 min), 30 cycles of denaturation at 95ºC for 4s, annealing at 46.9 °C for 45s, elongation at 72 °C for 60 s, and final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min as previously described [11]. The annealing temperatures and primer sequences of primers are shown (Supplementary Table I).

Agarose electrophoresis gel and visualization

Data fragment of all PCR products were detected using electrophoresis analysis. To ascertain the presence of these genes, an agarose gel of 1.5% (w/v) was prepared and run at 90 V for 45 min along with a 100 bp molecular ladder (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). After staining, the electrophoresis gel’s was visualised under UV light using a gel documentation system G-BOX Chemi-XL (Syngene, Cambridge, UK). Four isolates in the laboratory biobank were used as quality control strains including E. coli ATCC 35218, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 for β-lactam resistance genes and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 was used to ascertain the sterility of culture media. An internal laboratory strains already sequenced carried tetracycline resistance and virulence genes namely PR11 was also used as a positive control.

Data management and analysis

The results were obtained after analysis using Epi info 7.2.5.0 software. All results were presented in the tables and figures including percentages and frequencies of socio-demographic characteristics, antimicrobial resistance profiles as well as resistance and virulence determinants. The association with risk factors and ESBL-E. coli carriage was determined using Chi-square test and fisher exact test. All the results with p-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

The cohort of this study included 90 under five children with acute community-acquired diarrhea, of which 84 provided samples (Fig. 1). Out of the 84 children who provided samples, males (58.33%, 49/84) were more represented than females (41.67%, 35/84). The most represented age group of participants were infant < 1 year old (64.29%, 54/84). Analysis of socio-demographic data showed that 70.24% of children had three episodes of diarrhoea per day. The presence of mucus and blood was reported in 8 (9.52%) and 1 (1.19%) of the samples respectively. Vomiting was detected in 45 (53.57%) of cases (Table 1).

Risk factors associated with ESBL-E. coli

The prevalence of ESBL-E. coli was 52.38% and was associated with numerous risk factors including drunk natural fruit juice (OR: 0.4, p-value: 0.03) and artificial juice (OR: 0.4, p-value: 0.06) were found to be significantly. Concerning the source of drinking water, the majority of children used mineral water 55/84 (67.45%) and 44/84 (52.38%) had previous (within one month) antibiotics usage. On the other hand, breastfed children (52.38% vs. 47.62% OR: 1.8; p-value: 0.13) had higher risk of ESBL-Ec colonization although without statistical significance (Table 2).

Phenotypic screening of ESBL-E. coli

Out of a 84 E. coli isolated and subjected to the ESBL-phenotype screening using DDT, 44/84(52,38%) were ESBL producers. All these ESBL producers isolates were also positive for confirmation using ChromAgar ESBL media.

Antibiotic resistance profile of ESBL-E. coli

Among the 84 E. coli, eleven were colonized by two isolates leading to a total of 44 ESBL-E. coli isolates. Figure 2 presents the resistance profile of ESBL-E. coli to a panel of 13 antibiotics. The majority of isolates were resistant to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (100%) and cefotaxime (100%), followed by ceftazidime (97.77%), cefepime (95.55%), tetracycline (75.55%) and trimethoprim (71.11%). ciprofloxacin (31.11%), gentamicin (31.11%), nitrofurantoin (24.44%), chloramphenicol (15.55%) and fosfomycin (11.11%) displayed the lowest resistance.

Antibiotic resistance genes and virulence determinants

Six resistance genes belonging to three families including ß-lactamase genes (blaCTX−M, blaSHV, and blaTEM), carbapenemase gene (blaOXA−48) as well as tetracycline genes (tet(A) and tet(B)) were investigated (Fig. 4). Genes encoding for the production of β-lactamases, carbapenemases and tetracycline detected in ESBL-E. coli isolates were blaCTX−M (63.63%; 28/44), blaTEM (15.91%; 7/44), blaOXA−48 (6.81%; 3/44), tet(A) (31.8%; 14/44) and tet(B) (27.3%; 12/44) respectively. The co-carriage of blaCTX−M and blaTEM genes were (15.91%; 7/44), while two isolates (4.54%; 2/44) harboured all three resistance genes (blaCTX−M, blaTEM and blaOXA−48). In addition, no blaSHV resistance gene was found. A high frequency of FimH (81.8%; 36/44) and papA (79.5%; 35/44) were also detected (Fig. 5) with the high prevalence of isolates co-harbouring papA and FimH (65.9%; 29/44). Discussion

Extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing-E. coli (ESBL-Ec) has already been described as a public health issue that needs to be urgently addressed in humans [10, 11, 14] and animals in Cameroon [15, 16]. This study aims at determining the resistance genes and virulence factors of ESBL-Ec isolated from childhood diarrhoea during cholera outbreak occuring in Yaoundé, Cameroon. The results of this study shown that drinking natural fruit juice was associated with ESBL-Ec infections in this study. Strengthen awareness on the hygienic practices and mass education through all available public and social media as well as regular fruit washing are also important in the control of infectious diseases caused by foodborne pathogens as pathogenic ESBL-Ec among children under five. Strengthening the routine epidemiological surveillance is also essential for better management infectious diseases including diarrheal diseases among children under five.

A high prevalence of ESBL-Ec (52.38%) was detected in our study which is higher than the studies findings of Saka et al (2020) and Dela et al (2022) conducted in Nigeria and Ghana where 17.83% and 13.2% prevalence of ESBL-Ec were detected among patients with diarrhoea attending healthcare facilities respectively [6, 8]. Our result is similar to a study conducted in Iran, where the prevalence of ESBL-Ec was 51.9% among clinical samples in the North of the country [17]. This high rate of colonization with ESBL-Ec may be related to extensive and inappropriate usage of antibiotics in Yaoundé, where antimicrobial agents consumption are administrated over the counter without prescription and are available for all. Numerous risk factors have been investigated and drinking natural fruit juice was a common factor associated with ESBL-Ec infections in this study. The lack of safe drinking water, sanitation and hygiene measures in the country can further explain this high prevalence of ESBL-Ec in our study. This suggests that heightening awareness on the food and water security by increasing the drinking dwell water in the community settings, could contribute to curb the dissemination of diarrheagenic ESBL-Ec among children under five.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed the high resistance level to numerous antibiotic classes, especially those belong to the third generation of cephalosporins including cefotaxime (95%) and ceftazidime (95%). These results disagree with the previous studies in Iran among children suffered from diarrhoea, where the highest antibiotic resistance rates were co-trimoxazole (100%) and ceftriaxone (81.3%). But, it also agrees with the study in Ethiopia (2021), where the high resistance rates of cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime and amoxicillin clavulanic acid among ESBL-producing E. coli were reported [9]. In addition, a high resistance rate of tetracycline was also observed with 77% among ESBL-Ec. This result is lower than a previous study carried out from hospitalized patients in Rwanda, where 100% of Enterobacterales were resistant to tetracycline [21]. However, a study conducted in Burkina Faso shown 83.9% of tetracycline resistant among Enterobacterales [18]. In contrast, it is higher than the results reported in Egypt (55.4%) by Tawfick et al [18, 19]. Numerous reports have already shown that in sub-Saharan Africa, community and hospitalized patients received antibiotic treatment without antimicrobial susceptibility testing, which contribute to the selective pressure on the gut microbiome, thus increasing antimicrobial resistance [7]. The high level of MDR isolates observed in this study could also be related to the excessive or inappropriate usage of antibiotics over the counter coupled with counterfeit antibiotics, and absence of antimicrobial stewardship program in the country. In addition, the dearth of legislation and suboptimal infection prevention and control measures contribute to increase MDR strains.

The blaCTX−M in combination with blaTEM was found in 15.90% of ESBL-Ec, which is lower than the data obtained among children (0–60 months) with diarrhoea in Nakuru, Kenya during 2017 to 2019 (58.3%) [20]. Additionally, the prevalence of blaCTX−M genes reported was 63.63%. This result is an agreement with the previous studies conducted in numerous countries including Rwanda (87.5%) [21], United States (70%) [22], and Kenya (70.8%) [20], where the blaCTX−M was the most represented ß-lactamase genes. These results could be explained by the horizontal gene transfer (HGT) mechanisms commonly observed within Enterobacterales family. This finding confirms that blaCTX−M is widely distributed in Cameroon, as already demonstrated worlwide.

The combinations of blaCTX−M+blaTEM and blaCTX−M+blaTEM+blaOXA−48 carried by some strains were resistant to a greater number of antibiotics including β-lactams and non ß-lactam antibiotics. These results could be explained by the fact that ß-lactam genes can be carried by some plasmid frequently harboured genes conferring resistance to many others antibiotic families as described by Tawfick et al (2022) [19]. The prevalence of blaOXA−48 (7%) was detected among ESBL-Ec isolates. This prevalence is lower than those reported in Burkina Faso and Rwanda, where prevalence of ESBL-Ec producing blaOXA−48 were 100% and 63.3% respectively [18, 21]. Additionally, we report a rate of 4.54% among ESBL-Ec harbouring concomitantly blaCTX−M, blaTEM and blaOXA−48. This result disagrees with the study of Eger et al in 2021, who investigated the circulation of ESBL-Ec among hospitalized patients, caregivers, community members and animals in Rwanda [21]. The frequency of genes conferring tetracycline resistance tetA (31.8%) and tetB (27.3%) observed in our study is comparable to those obtained by Tawfick et al. (2022) in Egypt, who investigated commensal E. coli in the gut of adult patients and reported higher prevalence of tetA(48.2%) and tetB(25%) [18]. This variation could be attributed to the difference of age between patients combined with dissimilarity of antibiotic usage across different countries.

These strains carried also many virulence genes including FimH and papA with the prevalences of (81.82%) and (97.14%) respectively. This result agree with the previous study conducted by Eger et al (2021) in the University Teaching Hospital of Butare in Rwanda where the frequence of papA among ESBL-Ec was 70.5% [20]. In addition, the results of a study investigated by Hussain et al (2020) reveals that the most prevalent virulence gene were papC and FimH detected from broiler farms of central China, suggesting that this pathogen could be transmitted from poultry and/or farms to human [22, 23].

Conclusion

Here we report a high prevalence of pathogenic ESBL-Ec among childhood diarrhoea in Yaoundé, Cameroon. Our findings confirm the existence of antimicrobial resistance DEC among stool isolates, limiting the therapeutic options for diarrhoeal treatment in Yaoundé. Our findings revealed the need to implement a real-time surveillance of this important foodborne pathogen across this vulnerable population. Our study further highlighted the necessity for reinforce infection prevention and control measures to curb the dissemination of pathogenic ESBL-Ec. It is indispensable to implement antimicrobial stewardship programme to reduce the burden of antimicrobial resistance in Cameroon. Finally, it will be very important to integrate genomic surveillance of ESBL-DEC at the local and regional levels to reduce morbidity caused by diarrheal diseases among children under five in Yaoundé, Cameroon.

Limitations

The limitations of this study is the inability to selected the similar healthcare facilities (confessional vs. public), hence the comparative results may not reflect the reality. The discrepancies observed across other studies may be explained by the fact that our study occurred during a cholera outbreak that certainly triggered hospital attendance from a majority of severely ill children, allowing an inherent bias toward positivity. Finally, we are not able to characterize all genes conferring carbapenems resistance due to the lack of quality control strains in our lab, this not reflect the real percentage of carbapenemase-producing E. coli in Yaoundé.
