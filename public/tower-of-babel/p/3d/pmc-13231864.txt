The relationship between psychological stress and SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid-antibody response following natural infection: longitudinal findings from the Aegis study
Authors: Erika T Beidelman, Ashley Judge, David B Allison, Stephanie Dickinson, Lilian Golzarri Arroyo, Anna L M Macagno, Michael J Ricciardi, Jon Macy, Stanley Perlman, Christina Ludema, Kevin C Maki, Molly Rosenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsychological stress can cause diminished immune response to infectious challenges. The extent this holds true during severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection and the modifying effect of vaccination status is untested. We explored these relationships in a non-random sample standardized to the US adult population. Across 2178 participants in the Aegis study (2021-2022), we identified 507 eligible laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infections. We estimated the natural log of nucleocapsid-binding antibody absorbance (N-antibody) at study visits up to 225 days following a positive SARS-CoV-2 test. Linear mixed effects models were fit to estimate the association of baseline perceived stress and, separately, allostatic load with N-antibody trajectories. Standardization was performed using iterative post-stratification weighting. Consistently, lower stress groups had higher N-antibody response. Low perceived stress was associated with greater antibody response up to 26 days post-positive test (P = .047) while low allostatic load was associated with greater antibody response up to 24 days (P = .049). Pre-infection vaccination modified the relationship between stress and N-antibody trajectories, where the observed relationships attenuated to the null for vaccinated individuals (P = .031). Overall, we found consistent evidence that high stress was associated with reduced N-antibody response following SARS-CoV-2 infection and that vaccination may narrow the antibody response across high versus low stress groups.

Introduction

Chronic psychological stress can cause diminished immune response to natural infectious challenges, likely influenced by systemic inflammation, sustained elevations of stress hormones, and suppression of cells involved in immuno-protection.1,2 Multiple investigators have found an association between chronic stress and immune response to pathogens including the reactivation of latent infections,3 increased immune aging,3,4 and higher odds of infection.5,6 However, no studies have explored the association between psychological stress and antibody response following natural infection with the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Additionally, while there is a wealth of evidence that both acute and chronic stress are associated with reduced immune response following vaccination,7-9 there has been little exploration on how vaccination may modify the stress and natural infection relationship.

Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), the disease resulting from infection with the SARS-CoV-2 virus, is highly variable in severity with a wide range of contributing risk factors. While most cases are mild, some still require hospitalization.10 These cases of severe disease disproportionately affect older, minority, rural, and low socioeconomic status populations.11-13 It is possible that psychological stress could be a distinguishing factor that impacts immune response to SARS-CoV-2 among vulnerable groups. Indeed, recent publications have reported associations between stress proxies (eg, depression/anxiety symptoms, caregiving, low social cohesion, and high loneliness) and decreased immune response following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination.14-17 However, little research has been done to investigate immune response after natural infection, and prior work has relied exclusively on self-reported outcomes and subjective stress measures.18-20

In the context of stress-inducing pandemics like the COVID-19 pandemic, it is critical to examine the role of stress in disease transmission and pathogenesis. In this longitudinal study, we sought to estimate the association between psychological stress and antibody trajectories following infection with SARS-CoV-2, standardizing our results to the US adult population. We also assessed the modifying role of pre-infection vaccination status on this association. This study is novel due to its use of both a measure of perceived stress and a clinical measure of chronic stress (allostatic load). Additionally, this is the first study, to our knowledge, to examine the relationship between psychological stress and SARS-CoV-2 antibody response following natural infection.

Methods

Study sample

The sample for this analysis was sourced from participants of the Aegis study. Aegis was a prospective cohort study conducted between February 2021 and January 2023. The primary objective of this study was to assess infection/re-infection rates among participants with and without prior SARS-CoV-2 infections. A total of 2806 individuals were screened for participation in the Aegis study across nine clinical study sites in metropolitan areas of Illinois, Florida, Texas, Louisiana, and Oklahoma. Study sites used various methods of convenience sampling to recruit potential participants into the non-random sample of US adults. To be included in the study, individuals must have (1) been 18 years or older, (2) had a laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection OR no documented prior SARS-CoV-2 infection and was seronegative for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, (3) intended to reside in the study area for the next 12 months, and (4) had an elevated risk of future SARS-CoV-2 exposure (eg, occupation, high-occupancy home, high population density residence, regular use of public transportation, member of high risk population, regular interpersonal contact with no facial coverings, or anticipated attendance at large gatherings). Individuals were excluded if they had (1) an acute illness within 72 h of screening, (2) a contraindication to phlebotomy, (3) were immunosuppressed or immunodeficient, or (4) received systemic immunoglobulins within 3 months of screening.

Three hundred and seven individuals failed Aegis study screening, and 321 individuals did not complete the first study visit, resulting in a sample of 2178 individuals who enrolled and completed the first study visit. Participants could complete up to seven study visits at approximately 8-week intervals following the baseline enrollment visit. At each study visit, participants completed health and symptomatology questionnaires, blood sample collection for antibody testing, and life change updates. In addition to the study visits, participants completed at-home saliva samples for SARS-CoV-2 testing on a weekly basis. Blood samples for cardiometabolic testing were collected at the baseline, 6-month, and 12-month visits.

The sample for this analysis was limited to participants with a documented positive SARS-CoV-2 test after the date of their baseline study visit. We did not analyze participants with positive SARS-CoV-2 infections reported at or prior to the baseline study visit to ensure that the stress exposure ascertainment occurred prior to infection with the SARS-CoV-2 virus. This resulted in 507 SARS-CoV-2 infections identified for analysis. Ultimately, 43 infections were excluded from the analysis due to missing covariate data resulting in a final sample of 464 for which we could calculate weights. No significant differences were found across cases that were missing and not missing covariates. See Figure 1 for the sample flow chart and a description of data collection points.

Ethical considerations

The Aegis study protocol was reviewed and approved by Midwest Biomedical Research’s Institutional Review Board (ref. #20203947). Ethical approval was received from the Indiana University Institutional Review Board (ref. #22953). Informed consent was obtained from all participants at study enrollment.

Exposure: perceived stress

Self-reported perceived stress was estimated using Cohen’s 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10).21 The PSS-10 is a valid and reliable measure used to assess the degree to which participants perceived their life as stressful in the past month.21-23 During each study visit, participants rated each of the ten perceived stress items as Never, Almost Never, Sometimes, Fairly Often, or Very Often. A score of 0 to 4 was then assigned to each response, with 4 indicating the response associated with the greatest level of stress. Responses were summed across all 10 items in the direction of increasing stress to calculate the PSS-10 sum score (range: 0-40). Distribution of the PSS-10 in the study sample can be seen in Figure S1.

We dichotomized the PSS-10 sum score into “low perceived stress” and “moderate/high perceived stress” categories.21,24,25 Low perceived stress was defined as a sum score of less than 14 points. Moderate-to-high perceived stress was defined as a sum score of equal to or greater than 14 points. Though formal diagnostic cutoffs do not exist for the PSS-10, the threshold of 14 points aligns with the established categorical bands widely used in epidemiologic research (0-13 points = low, 14-26 points = moderate, and 27-40 points = high).21,24-28 In line with prior work, we combined the moderate and high stress categories for interpretability and comparability.29-31

Exposure: allostatic load

We calculated an allostatic load index as a proxy measure of accumulated chronic stress. Allostatic load is an estimate of the cumulative wear and tear on the body from repeated or chronic exposure to life stressors.32 We estimated allostatic load with a biomarker-based sum score measured at Aegis Visit 1. The biomarkers in the sum score were high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol, blood glucose, triglycerides, C-reactive protein (CRP), body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, and resting heart rate. This biomarker panel represents measures of immune, metabolic, and cardiovascular function, chosen based on biomarkers available in the Aegis dataset in combination with a review of the biomarkers most often utilized to calculate allostatic load within the literature.33 To calculate the allostatic load score, we used a count-based approach that equally weighted all biomarkers. First, we created a binary variable for each biomarker representing whether the participant had normal/low measurements (“0”) or high measurements (“1”) using nationally established clinical thresholds. In the case of HDL-C, the binary measure represented whether the participant had normal/high measurements (“0”) or low measurements (“1”). Next, we summed the nine binary biomarker indicators across each participant to calculate the total sum score. The continuous sum score ranged from 0 to 8, with eight representing the highest levels of allostatic load. The sum score was then categorized as “low allostatic load” for sum scores lower than three and “high allostatic load” for sum scores greater than or equal to mirror thresholds established in the literature.33

Outcome: SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid-binding antibodies

We estimated antibody response against SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein. The rationale for using the N protein is that this allowed us to isolate the response to natural infection,34 as available vaccinations only produce an antibody response against SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) and not N proteins. N proteins are also more sensitive (ie, produce more true positives) for early infection detection when compared to S protein antibodies.34 We measured titers of IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan Strain N protein using SST-serum processed from venous blood samples collected at each study visit for up to 7 total visits. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were used to estimate the antibody titers. Plates were coated overnight at 4°C using 2 μg/mL of SARS-CoV-2N protein. They were then washed and blocked with a 5% blocking solution for 1 h at 37°C. Then serial dilutions of participant serum samples done in duplicate and diluted in the blocking agent were added to the plate and incubated for 1 h at 37°C and again the plate was washed. A goat anti-human IgG-HRP secondary, diluted in blocking buffer, was added and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Finally, the plate was washed and TMB substrate was added for the development of 5 min at room temperature. Stop solution was added and the plates were read at 450 nm. We then calculated the average of the two absorbance estimates for each dilution and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) of the dilutions using the trapezoid method. The AUC values represent the magnitude of the antibody response through an estimation of the absorbance of the samples. The natural log plus 1 of N-antibody AUC values were estimated at each study visit up to 225 days following a positive SARS-CoV-2 test.

Covariates

We used several covariates to describe the study sample and account for confounding within our analyses. These covariates included age at baseline, sex (male or female), pre-infection vaccination status (fully vaccinated or not/partially vaccinated), prior SARS-CoV-2 infection (yes or no), predominant SARS-CoV-2 strain at the time of infection (Delta or Omicron), education level (more than high school or high school or less), employment status (employed or not employed), household income (<$25K, $25K-$75K, $75K-$150K, or >$150 K), and race/ethnicity. Predominant strain was measured at each time point to capture the most prevalent SARS-CoV-2 strain in circulation using CDC Laboratory Surveillance data estimations of SARS-CoV-2 variant proportions.35

Statistical analysis

Cross-tabulations and Cohen’s κ were calculated to determine agreement between the perceived stress and allostatic load exposures (see Table S1). Linear mixed effects models (LMMs) were fit to estimate the association between each stress exposure and N-antibody trajectories following natural SARS-CoV-2 infection (see eqn 1). Time was measured as the number of days since the first positive SARS-CoV-2 test. A non-linear relationship was observed between time and N-antibody levels; therefore, we estimated the relationship with time using a natural cubic spline with 4 degrees of freedom following assessments of model fit. Additionally, an interaction term between the binary stress exposure and time was included in all models. This allowed us to estimate different N-antibody trajectories over time for each exposure group. We accounted for the correlation between repeated measures with a random intercept for the individual. Models were adjusted for age, age-squared (to capture non-linear trends between age and antibody response), sex, pre-infection vaccination status, prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, education level, employment status, household income, race/ethnicity, and predominant SARS-CoV-2 strain at the time of infection. Our adjustment set was selected a priori using a conceptual framework and prior literature, with the goal of reducing confounding while avoiding adjustment for variables on the causal pathway.

Additional models were fit with a term for the interaction between each stress exposure and pre-infection vaccination status to examine effect measure modification by pre-infection vaccination status. Adjustment sets, interactions, and random effects were decided a priori. We calculated marginal predictions from the LMMs to estimate population average N-antibody absorbance levels over time across levels of each stress exposure. These predictions were used to determine the peak value and peak timing of N-antibody levels across the exposure groups. Additionally, we estimated pairwise interaction contrasts to determine differences in antibody levels at each timepoint. Marginal predictions and interaction contrasts were estimated using the mean value (continuous variables) and reference level (categorical variables) of all covariates included in the models.

We used data from the 2021 to 2023 round of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to standardize our results to the general population of adults in the US. An iterative post-stratification weighting approach was taken to adjust for sampling bias and non-response in our non-probability sample.36 Weights were calculated from NHANES distributions for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, employment status, obesity, CRP level, and self-reported health status. Variables were chosen due to their relationship with COVID-19 risk, psychological stress, and health service utilization. Individuals with high health service utilization are more likely to volunteer for and be represented in clinical studies. Weights were applied during the modeling step of our analysis. Standardization was not undertaken for the models investigating effect measure modification by vaccination status as vaccination data were not available in the NHANES dataset for weighting.

To account for selective attrition over time, we also estimated inverse probability of censoring weights. For each individual, the predicted probability of remaining uncensored was estimated via extended Cox Proportional Hazards Models from the point of SARS-CoV-2 infection until the point of censoring or the end of the observation period. Models were fit with standardization weights alone as well as with the product of the standardization and censoring weights. All statistical analyses were performed in RStudio version 4.4.1.37 Generalized LMMs were estimated using the lme4 package,38 post-stratification weights were calculated using the survey package,39 and inverse probability of censoring weights were calculated using the ipw package.40 Statistical significance was set with a two-tailed alpha of 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings to alternative operationalizations of our two stress exposures, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, we modeled the PSS-10 as a continuous score to assess whether results were dependent on our chosen threshold. Second, we recalculated the allostatic load index excluding BMI due to the breadth of literature indicating a potential bidirectional relationship with stress. For each alternative specification, we re-estimated the models using the PSWs and IPCWs. Results were consistent with the main analyses and are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S2 and S3 and Figures S2 and S3).

Results

Sample characteristics

We identified 464 positive SARS-CoV-2 tests with sufficient longitudinal data that occurred following the participant’s first study visit. Omicron was the most prevalent SARS-CoV-2 strain at the time of 85% of the identified positive tests. Participants were on average 49 years old (SD: 15.5) and 65% were female (see Table 1). Approximately two-thirds (67%) of the sample were employed at the time of their positive test and 86% had completed at least a high school education. At the time of their positive SARS-CoV-2 test, approximately 78% were fully vaccinated and 22% had a prior reported SARS-CoV-2 infection (see Table 1). Agreement between the perceived stress and allostatic load exposures was no greater than chance (Cohen’s κ = −0.02, 95% CI, −0.11, 0.07).

Pre-infection vaccination status modified the relationship between perceived stress and N-antibody trajectories (Interaction β: 0.61, 95% CI, 0.05, 1.16) (see Figure 3). The observed association between stress and antibody levels was greater in the sample without full vaccination. Among those fully vaccinated, there were no statistically significant differences in N-antibody levels across the low and high perceived stress groups at any time point. Among those without full vaccination, differences in N-antibody levels were sustained until 28 days post-positive test. AUC values for N-antibodies were 9.0% higher at 28 days post-positive test for the low perceived stress group compared to the high perceived stress group (38 days = 0.765, P = .048). Prior SARS-CoV-2 infection did not significantly modify the relationship between perceived stress and antibody levels over time.

Discussion

This is the first study, to our knowledge, to connect psychological stress measures, laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infections, and longitudinal N-antibody trajectories following infection. We found evidence that high stress was associated with reduced N-antibody response following natural SARS-CoV-2 infection, particularly within the first month following a positive test. These results were consistent across two measures that represented each current and chronic stress as well as across standardized and unstandardized analyses. We observed that individuals with low stress generally reached a higher peak of N-antibody absorbance, and reached it sooner, compared to their high stress counterparts. Additionally, we provide evidence that vaccination could overcome the reduction in antibody response associated with high perceived stress.

Our results provide clinical evidence that supports earlier work on the association between psychological stress and SARS-CoV-2 immune response. Previous work found that measures of increased psychological stress were associated with increased risk of self-reported long-term COVID-19 symptoms,19 increased odds of self-reported COVID-19 infection,18 as well as increased symptoms and symptom severity following COVID-19 infection.18 Our work addresses several limitations of this existing evidence base, primarily through our use of clinical and objective antibody measurements. After employment of these robust measures, we provide further confirmation that psychological stress is associated with reduced immune response with respect to N protein binding antibodies within the first month following a SARS-CoV-2 infection.

While our results showed a significant increase in antibody response associated with the low stress group in the month following infection, the data suggest that the high-stress group may maintain anti-N IgG levels for a longer duration and may experience a delayed decline compared to the low-stress group. These differences did not reach statistical significance but do provide a signal that the effects of stress on immune response may be modified by the elapsed time since infection. If high stress inhibits immune response, it is plausible that sustained antibody levels could be due to a delayed clearance of the virus. However, future studies should work to confirm this time dependency and assess if persistent N-antibody response in high stress groups is related to delayed viral clearing or, alternatively, to enhanced protective effects against severe disease.

While initial immune response following vaccination has been shown to differ by stress level,14-16 our findings extend this literature by highlighting that the stress effect may be dependent upon whether the immune system is responding to a de novo or secondary SARS-CoV-2 exposure. Specifically, we found that the observed association between perceived stress and antibody response was greater among individuals who were not fully vaccinated prior to infection. This is consistent with findings reported in the literature suggesting that stress most strongly blunts the antibody response following vaccination among individuals who are immunologically naïve. We observed no association for individuals who were fully vaccinated prior to infection, a scenario representing a secondary immune response. Our findings, combined with the prior literature on stress and vaccine response, suggest that stress may be particularly detrimental for de novo immune responses, whereas the impact may be attenuated for secondary immune responses. This finding provides more evidence in support of a rapid vaccine response in pandemic scenarios, as these crises are known to be population-level, high-stress events.41-43

However, in this study, we only examined one feature of immune response, nucleocapsid-binding antibody titers. There are numerous other immune responses that contribute to the full picture of immune protection including additional complex innate and humoral responses. We focused on N-antibodies to isolate the response to natural infection34 rather than vaccination due to difficulties fully disentangling the temporality across vaccination and infection within our study design. We did not examine neutralizing antibody titers in this analysis which are more commonly accepted as a correlate of protection against SARS-CoV-2,44,45 though, evidence shows that binding titers can be accepted as a proxy for neutralizing titers and, thus, protection.46 Due to our examination of a singular measure of immune defense, the longitudinal trends observed will likely not track perfectly with immune protection. Future work should expand this analysis to other immune response measures.

The primary strength of this study was our ability to appropriately assign temporality across our exposure and outcomes. Through the longitudinal study design, we ensured that the stress exposures were assessed prior to both infection and antibody measurement, reducing the opportunity for bias. We also used robust measures of stress, laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 diagnoses, and clinical antibody measurements of immune response. Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitations. First, our measure of allostatic load lacks a biomarker representing neuro-endocrine function which is commonly included in the literature. As neuro-endocrine markers include cortisol and epinephrine which are directly involved in stress pathways, it is possible that our allostatic load measure is an under-representation of holistic stress. This could then explain the discrepancy in the magnitude of effect observed across our allostatic load and perceived stress analyses. Second, the results of our effect modification analysis may not be representative of the overall US adult population. We were unable standardize this model due to a lack of information about COVID-19 vaccination status in the NHANES dataset. In our primary analysis, however, the standardized results did not differ meaningfully from the unstandardized results, providing some reassurance that this trend may hold for the vaccine-specific analysis as well.

In this robust longitudinal study, we found consistent evidence that two measures of stress were associated with reduced N-antibody response in the first month following infection and that full vaccination may narrow the antibody response between high and low stress groups. These results emphasize the importance of swift vaccine response in stress-inducing pandemic scenarios. As minority groups tend to be at greater risk of high stress,11-13 infectious disease outbreaks in no or low vaccine environments could widen the disparity in the risk of severe disease for these already vulnerable groups. Future research should examine whether the observed associations translate to meaningful differences in symptom severity, adverse outcomes, and long-term protection against re-infection.
