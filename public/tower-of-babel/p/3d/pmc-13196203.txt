Adsorption performance and mechanism of modified rice hull biochar for lead and cadmium removal in single and competitive systems
Authors: Chunmei Wei, Jie Luo, Wanyue Hong, Luping He, Xudong Zhang, Ting Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While adsorption in multi-metal systems is crucial for practical wastewater treatment, its efficacy and mechanisms remain insufficiently explored. This study investigates the adsorption performance and underlying mechanisms of Pb(II) and Cd(II) using modified rice hull biochar in single and binary solute systems. Biochar was functionalized through β-cyclodextrin and NaOH modifications, and characterization revealing that NaOH more effectively improved porosity, endowing NaOH-modified carbon (NBC) with a larger specific surface area and pore volume. In single-metal systems, β-cyclodextrin-modified carbon (BBC) demonstrated superior adsorption capacities. In binary systems, Pb(II) exhibited a strong competitive advantage over Cd(II). Sequential adsorption experiments confirmed that pre-sorbed Cd(II) could be displaced by subsequently added Pb(II), indicating higher affinity for the latter. Kinetic studies revealed that Pb(II) adsorption was better fitted by the pseudo-first-order model, while Cd(II) adsorption was better described by the pseudo-second-order model. Isotherm analysis demonstrated Pb(II) adsorption on NBC was better described by the Freundlich model, whereas on BBC it followed the Sips model. Cd(II) adsorption on NBC was better fitted by the Sips model, while on BBC it exhibited perfect agreement with the Langmuir model. The superior affinity for Pb(II) is attributed to its smaller hydrated radius, lower hydration energy, and stronger complexation capability. This work establishes NBC as an efficient and selective adsorbent for Pb(II) removal from complex wastewater systems.

Introduction

Heavy metal contamination in aquatic environments poses public health and environmental safety [1, 2]. Pb(II) and Cd(II) are among the most concerning contaminants due to their high toxicity, persistence, and potential for bioaccumulation. Current methods for removing these metals from wastewater include chemical precipitation, membrane filtration, and adsorption [3–7]. Among these, adsorption is widely preferred because of its high efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and operational simplicity. In recent years, biochar has emerged as a promising adsorbent, owing to its large surface area, developed pore structure, and modifiable surface chemistry [8, 9].

The management of agricultural waste, a substantial biomass resource, remains a pressing environmental challenge. Large quantities of agricultural residues are discarded, composted, or incinerated, leading to resource depletion and secondary pollution. Converting these wastes into biochar through pyrolysis offers a sustainable approach for waste management by transforming low-value biomass into a functional material for water treatment, which aligns with the principles of a circular economy and sustainable development. However, the practical application of raw, unmodified biochar is often constrained by its limited adsorption capacity and low specificity towards particular metal ions, especially in complex effluents containing multiple coexisting heavy metals. Table 1 summarizes the adsorption performance of unmodified biochars for Pb(II) and Cd(II) reported in recent studies [10–15]. To address these limitations, various chemical modification strategies have been developed. Among them, alkaline treatment with NaOH is a common and effective method to enhance porosity and increase the specific surface area of biochar, thereby creating more physical adsorption sites [16, 17]. Concurrently, the introduction of specific organic functional groups that can form complexes with metal ions is another promising route to improve selectivity and adsorption capacity. In particular, β-cyclodextrin is a kind of nontoxic and environmental-friendly compliant containing a hydrophilic outer edge and an inner cavity hydrophobic structure which can facilitates the attachment of contaminants. In addition, the cross-linking of β-cyclodextrin with biochar can effectively help the insoluble β-cyclodextrin to separate in aqueous environment [18–20].

Although the enhanced uptake of single metal species by modified biochar is well-documented, predicting its efficacy in multi-metal systems remains challenging. The competitive interactions between different metal ions, particularly the dynamic processes influenced by factors such as the order of their introduction to the adsorbent, are still not fully understood. A deeper insight into these competitive dynamics is essential for designing effective treatment strategies for real wastewater.

To address these challenges, this study focuses on biochar derived from rice hulls and modified with β-cyclodextrin and NaOH to enhance its adsorption performance for Pb(II) and Cd(II). The research involves preparing and characterizing the modified biochars, evaluating their adsorption performance in single and mixed solute systems, and analyzing competitive adsorption with an emphasis on the effect of metal addition sequence. In addition, kinetics, isotherms, and material characterization were combined to reveal the underlying adsorption mechanisms. This work provides theoretical and methodological support for the targeted design of waste-derived biochars for selective heavy metal removal and promotes sustainable wastewater treatment technologies.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received. Rice hull was obtained from a local source in Sichuan Province, China. β-cyclodextrin, glutaraldehyde (50% wt% in H2O), NaOH, CdCl2·2.5H2O, Pb(NO3)2, and HNO3 were purchased from Chengdu Cologne Chemical Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China). KBr was supplied from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of modified biochar

Rice hull was rinsed with deionized water to remove impurities and then dried at 80 °C for 12 h. The dried rice hull was ground into a fine powder and sieved through an 80-mesh sieve to ensure uniformity. The powder was then placed in a covered crucible and subjected to pyrolysis in a muffle furnace at 400 °C under limited oxygen conditions for 2 h, resulting in the production of base biochar, referred to as BC.

For the preparation of β-cyclodextrin-modified biochar (BBC), a modification solution was prepared by dissolving 2 g of β-cyclodextrin and 1 mL of glutaraldehyde in 50 mL of 7% NaOH, followed by stirring at 200 rpm for 1 h. Glutaraldehyde served as a cross-linking agent, stabilizing β-cyclodextrin on the biochar surface. After solution preparation, 1 g of BC was added and stirred at 200 rpm for 10 h at 25 °C to facilitate β-cyclodextrin grafting. The modified biochar was rinsed thoroughly with ultrapure water to remove impurities and dried at 80 °C for 8 h, yielding BBC with functional groups optimized for selective heavy metal adsorption. The product obtained through the same process but without the addition of β-cyclodextrin and glutaraldehyde was designated as NaOH-modified biochar (NBC).

Characterization analysis

The ash content of the biochar was determined following the Chinese National Standard GB/T 12496.3-1999. Biochar pH was measured by mixing 1 g of sample with 20 mL of ultrapure water. The suspension was shaken at 180 rpm for 48 h at 25 °C, and the pH of the supernatant was then measured using a pH meter. The point of zero charge was determined by the pH drift method. The specific surface area was measured by nitrogen adsorption-desorption using a multilayer surface area and porosity analyzer (V-Sorb X800, Beijing Guoyi Precision Testing Technology Co., Ltd, China). The surface morphology and elemental composition were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; ZEISS Gemini SEM 300, Carl Zeiss, Germany) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX; Smartedx, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Spectrum 3 FT-IR, PerkinElmer, USA) was employed to identify the chemical functional groups on the biochar surface.

Batch adsorption experimental

Adsorption in single-metal systems

Batch experiments for Pb(II) and Cd(II) removal were conducted in a thermostatic shaker at 25 °C. Specifically, varying masses of biochar (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 g) were added to 50 mL of Cd(II) or Pb(II) solution at different initial concentrations. The test concentrations ranged from 150 to 400 mg/L for Pb(II) (150, 200, 250, 300, 350 and 400 mg/L) and from 25 to 150 mg/L for Cd(II) (25, 50, 75, 90, 125, 150 mg/L ). The mixtures were agitated at 180 rpm for predetermined time intervals (0, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1200, 1400 and 1600 min). After agitation, the mixtures were filtered using a 0.22 μm syringe filter to separate the biochar from the solution.

Competitive and sequential adsorption

In the competitive adsorption experiments, the performance of BBC and NBC was evaluated in Pb(II)-Cd(II) binary system, Experiments were conducted at two concentration levels (100 mg/L and 300 mg/L for each ion) to assess the biochar’s adsorption capacity under varied conditions. The solution pH was maintained at 5.5, and the temperature was held at 25 °C with stirring at 180 rpm until equilibrium was reached.

In a separate series, the effect of ion addition sequence was investigated by sequentially adding BBC or NBC to the solutions. Identical conditions (pH 5.5, 25 °C, and 180 rpm) were maintained to ensure consistency.

Selection of initial concentrations

The initial concentration of Pb(II) and Cd(II) were selected based on environmental relevance and analytical considerations. The range of 200–300 mg/L for Pb(II) represents typical levels found in industrial wastewater while avoiding precipitation. Conversely, the lower range of 50–100 mg/L for Cd(II) reflects its common environmental occurrence. These distinct ranges preclude a direct comparison of the absolute adsorption capacities between the two metals in single-metal systems.

For the competitive adsorption experiments in the Pb(II)–Cd(II) binary system, equal initial concentrations of 100 mg/L and 300 mg/L were applied to both metals. These concentrations were selected based on preliminary single-system adsorption tests. This equal-concentration design eliminates the confounding effect of different initial concentrations, enabling a direct comparison of intrinsic affinity between Pb(II) and Cd(II) under identical driving force conditions.

Desorption experiments

The desorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) was studied by mixing 0.1 g of metal-loaded biochar with 50 mL of 0.01 mol/L NaNO3 solution, followed by shaking at 180 rpm and 25 °C for 12 h. The concentration of desorbed metal ions in the supernatant was analyzed by ICP-OES. A control using ultrapure water as desorption medium was performed in parallel.

Na⁺ exchange experiment

BBC and NBC samples (0.1 g each) were equilibrated with 50 mL of metal solutions (Pb(II): 200/300 mg/L for BBC; Cd(II): 50/100 mg/L for NBC) in 0.01 mol/L NaNO₃ background electrolyte. After 24 h shaking (25 °C, 180 rpm), solutions were filtered (0.22 μm) and analyzed by ICP-OES. The cation-exchanged metal amount was calculated based on the differential Na+ concentration before and after adsorption.

Results and discussion

Characterization of modified biochar

SEM analysis reveals distinct morphological differences among the biochar samples (Fig. 1). The BC presents a heterogeneous surface covered with particulate deposits and exhibits irregular, poorly defined pores. In contrast, NBC develops a well-porous, honeycomb-like structure. The observed smoothing of the internal pore walls in NBC is attributed to the dissolution and removal of superficial ash particles that originally coated and roughened the pore surfaces in BC. This observation aligns with the known mechanism of NaOH activation, which cleans and etches the carbon surface, as supported by previous studies [21, 22].

EDX analysis provides complementary evidence, showing a noticeable decrease in the intensities of ash-related elements (Si, K, Ca) alongside a clear increase in C and Na content in NBC. This elemental shift is consistent with bulk ash content measurements (Table 2), which decrease in the order BC > BBC > NBC. The concurrent increase in carbon content and sodium incorporation, coupled with the formation of the honeycomb porous structure, validates the role of NaOH activation in consuming disordered carbon fractions and creating porosity, as documented in the literature [23].

These structural modifications result in significantly enhanced textural properties for NBC, which exhibits a specific surface area of 12.051 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.042 cm³/g, surpassing the values for BC. For the β-cyclodextrin modified biochar (BBC), SEM images show that the modification primarily involves the attachment of particles to the existing surface, without substantially altering the fundamental pore structure. Consequently, BBC shows a more moderate improvement in surface area (15.134 m2/g) and pore volume (0.048 cm³/g).

The FTIR spectra (Fig. 2) confirmed the presence of characteristic functional groups and revealed modification-induced structural changes in the biochar. The interpretation of band intensity changes, which reflects the relative abundance of functional groups, was conducted following established principles of FTIR spectroscopy [24]. The –OH stretching vibration shifted from 3425.06 cm−1 in BC to 3417.97 cm−1 in BBC and 3434.26 cm−1 in NBC, indicating alterations in the hydrogen-bonding network. Both modified biochars showed attenuated C–H and C=C vibrational peaks, with the C–H band at 2918.72 cm−1 nearly disappearing in NBC, suggesting substantial structural alteration of the carbon matrix. The diminished intensity of the COO− peak at 1591 cm−1 in BBC confirms successful β-cyclodextrin cross-linking [24]. Furthermore, the weakened Si–O vibrations at 785.04 cm−1 and 466.43 cm−1 indicate effective removal of mineral components during modification, consistent with the ash dissolution effect of NaOH activation reported in previous studies [25]. Importantly, the observed reduction of interfering inorganic groups (Si–O) and the preservation of oxygen-containing functional groups align with the established mechanism wherein NaOH activation enhances relative carbon content through removal of mineral constituents while simultaneously creating porous structures through sodium intercalation [26]. These modifications collectively contribute to developing a more favorable surface chemistry for potential environmental applications.

Effect of adsorption process parameters

Effect of solution pH on adsorption

Solution pH critically influences the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) on biochar by influencing the ionization states of surface functional groups and metal ion speciation [27, 28]. As shown in Fig. 3 (adsorbent dosage: 0.1 g; time: 1440 min; initial Pb(II) concentration: 200 and 300 mg/L; initial Cd(II) concentration: 50 and 100 mg/L; temperature 25 °C), the adsorption capacities for both metals increased with rising pH before reaching equilibrium, with notable differences observed between BBC and NBC.

For Pb(II) adsorption in a 200 mg/L solution, BBC attained equilibrium at pH 3.5, while NBC required pH 4.5. This difference stems from their dominant adsorption mechanisms. The β-cyclodextrin in BBC facilitates stable coordination with Pb(II), a process effective over a broad pH range [29]. Conversely, NBC relies more on electrostatic attraction, which is enhanced at higher pH as surface deprotonation increases negative charge density. At low pH, high H+ concentration competes with metal ions for binding sites, particularly inhibiting this electrostatic process. This behavior commonly reported for biochar modified with metal salts like Fe₂(SO₄)₃ [30].

A similar trend was observed for Cd(II). In a 50 mg/L solution, BBC reached equilibrium at pH 4.0, whereas NBC’s adsorption increased steeply within a narrower, higher pH window, consistent with an electrostatically driven mechanism.

The distinct pH-dependent behaviors of BBC and NBC are attributed to their primary adsorption mechanisms: stable coordination for BBC versus electrostatic attraction for NBC. This interpretation is consistent with the measured biochar pH values (Table 2) and established modification principles [21, 25].

Effect of adsorption time

The effect of adsorption time on the removal of Pb(II) and Cd(II) by BBC and NBC was investigated under fixed conditions: adsorbent dosage 0.1 g; solution pH of 5.5 for Pb(II) and 6.0 for Cd(II); initial Pb(II) concentration, 200 and 300 mg/L; initial Cd(II) concentration, 50 and 100 mg/L; temperature, 25 °C. The results are illustrated in Fig. 4. For Pb(II), both biochars exhibited rapid adsorption within the first 15 min. BBC reached adsorption capacities of 82.88 mg/g (removal efficiency: 82.88%), while NBC achieved 85.97 mg/g (removal efficiency: 85.97%). In the 300 mg/L solution, BBC adsorbed 94.02 mg/g (removal efficiency: 60.46%), compared to 104.99 mg/g (removal efficiency: 69.99%) for NBC. Following this initial rapid phase, the adsorption rate decreased as the available surface active sites became saturated, with equilibrium being attained around 240 min.

For Cd(II) adsorption, a rapid initial uptake was observed within the first 5 min, especially in the 50 mg/L solutions. In this low-concentration system, BBC and NBC achieved adsorption capacities of 22.48 mg/g and 23.98 mg/g, corresponding to high removal efficiencies of 89.9% and 95.95%, respectively. In contrast, equilibrium in the 100 mg/L solutions was delayed until 120 min. Under this higher concentration, BBC attained an adsorption capacity of 27.99 mg/g (removal efficiency: 55.97%), while NBC demonstrated a superior performance with a capacity of 30.55 mg/g (removal efficiency: 61.11%).

In summary, NBC consistently exhibited superior adsorption capacities and removal efficiencies, particularly at higher metal concentrations. This performance can be attributed to its enhanced surface area and optimized pore structure, which promoted stronger electrostatic interactions and improved ion diffusion. These findings highlight the critical role of biochar modifications in improving its effectiveness for sustained heavy metal removal [31].

Effect of initial metal ion concentration

As shown in Fig. 5 (adsorbent dosage: 0.1 g; solution pH of 5.5 for Pb(II) and 6.0 for Cd(II); time: 1440 min; temperature 25 °C), and the results are illustrated in, both NBC and BBC achieved nearly 100% removal efficiency for Pb(II) (below 200 mg/L) and Cd(II) (below 50 mg/L) at lower initial concentrations. However, as initial concentrations increased, the adsorption capacities of both biochars increased, while removal efficiencies gradually declined. NBC consistently outperformed BBC in adsorption performance for both Pb(II) and Cd(II), particularly at elevated concentrations. This enhanced performance is attributed to NBC’s optimized pore structure and surface properties, which facilitate greater accessibility to adsorption sites and improved ion diffusion.

NBC’s superior adsorption behavior underscores its potential as a highly effective and versatile adsorbent for heavy metal removal in wastewater treatment, providing a robust foundation for advancing biochar-based remediation technologies.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

The effect of adsorbent dosage on the removal of Pb(II) and Cd(II) by BBC and NBC was investigated under fixed conditions (initial Pb(II) concentration, 200 and 300 mg/L; initial Cd(II) concentration, 50 and 100 mg/L; solution pH of 5.5 for Pb(II) and 6.0 for Cd(II); time: 440 min; temperature 25 °C), as a function of biochar dosage is presented in Fig. 6. For Pb(II), removal efficiency increased steadily with rising biochar dosage, reaching nearly 100% at 3 g/L. However, beyond 1 g/L, adsorption capacity declined due to particle aggregation, which restricted access to active sites. At the same dosage, the adsorption capacity followed the order NBC-300 > BBC-300 > NBC-200 > BBC-200. The maximum adsorption capacities were observed at 1 g/L, with BBC and NBC achieving 130.98 mg/g and 145.79 mg/g in a 200 mg/L Pb(II) solution, and 144.59 mg/g and 162.91 mg/g in a 300 mg/L solution, respectively. At dosages exceeding 3 g/L, both biochars achieved nearly 100% removal efficiency.

A similar trend was observed for Cd(II). At a dosage of 1 g/L, both biochars exhibited their maximum adsorption capacities but achieved their lowest removal efficiencies. Compared to BBC, NBC consistently exhibited superior adsorption performance for both Pb(II) and Cd(II), owing to its optimized pore structure and enhanced surface properties. These findings highlight the critical role of biochar dosage optimization in maximizing adsorption efficiency by providing more accessible active sites, providing key insights for effective heavy metal remediation in wastewater treatment.

Competitive adsorption

Competitive adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) on modified biochar

The adsorption behavior of BBC and NBC in a Pb(II)–Cd(II) binary system was evaluated (Fig. 7). When Pb(II) and Cd(II) were present at initial concentrations of 100 mg/L, the adsorption capacity for Cd(II) decreased to 20.15 mg/g for BBC and 30.82 mg/g for NBC, while both biochars nearly achieved 100% removal of Pb(II). At higher concentrations of 300 mg/L (Fig. 7), the adsorption capacities for Pb(II) were 88.36 mg/g for BBC and 110.03 mg/g for NBC, with Cd(II) adsorption decreasing further to 14.67 mg/g for BBC and 17.09 mg/g for NBC. These results demonstrate the competitive advantage of Pb(II) over Cd(II), particularly at higher concentrations, where Pb(II) consistently exhibits stronger adsorption affinity [32].

This competitive behavior aligns with prior studies. Saravanakumar et al. reported that, in a binary system using jujube-derived biochar, Cd(II) adsorption decreased by 26.5% in the presence of Pb(II) [33], whereas Pb(II) adsorption was reduced by only 1.2%. Similarly, Zhang et al. found that Pb(II) exhibits preferential adsorption due to its stronger interaction with oxygen-containing functional groups on the biochar surface compared to Cd(II) [34].

To further explore the competitive dynamics between Pb(II) and Cd(II), we varied the ion introduction sequence. When Pb(II) was adsorbed first, followed by Cd(II), the remaining Pb(II) concentrations were 11.11 mg/L for BBC and 7.16 mg/L for NBC, while the Cd(II) concentrations were 64.28 mg/L and 43.79 mg/L, respectively. This suggests that Pb(II) was preferentially adsorbed, leaving higher residual concentrations of Cd(II) in solution. Conversely, when Cd(II) was introduced first, followed by Pb(II), the Pb(II) concentrations rose significantly, reaching 127.9 mg/L for BBC and 51.11 mg/L for NBC, while the Cd(II) concentrations were 43.79 mg/L and 40.38 mg/L, respectively. This indicates that Pb(II) displaced Cd(II) from the biochar surface, resulting in increased residual Cd(II) concentrations in the solution.

The displacement of Cd(II) by Pb(II) is consistent with findings by Xiong et al., who demonstrated that Pb(II) effectively occupies adsorption sites and can displace previously adsorbed Cd(II) [35]. This displacement is attributed to the stronger interaction between Pb(II) and negatively charged functional groups on the biochar surface. Moreover, the presence of Pb(II) in multi-metal systems significantly reduces the adsorption of Cd(II), reinforcing Pb(II)’s dominant competitive behavior in such systems.

In single-metal systems, biochar nearly completely removed both Pb(II) and Cd(II) at a dosage of 4 g/L. However, in the binary system, neither ion achieved complete removal when the ion introduction sequence was altered. When Pb(II) was adsorbed first, its concentration remained largely unchanged, while the introduction of Pb(II) after Cd(II) led to a significant increase in Cd(II) concentration. This confirms that Pb(II) competes more effectively for adsorption sites, displacing Cd(II) and causing its desorption from the biochar surface.

Mechanism of competitive adsorption

The competitive adsorption behavior of Pb(II) and Cd(II) on the biochars is governed by the synergistic effects between the adsorbent surface characteristics and the metal ion properties. Zeta potential measurements confirm that all biochars carry negative charges under experimental pH conditions (Fig. 8), providing the fundamental basis for electrostatic attraction of metal cations.

Fig. 8BC, BBC and NBC zero-point charges

BC, BBC and NBC zero-point charges

Building upon this electrostatic interaction, the difference in hydrated radius determines the spatial accessibility of ions to adsorption sites. The smaller hydrated radius of Pb(II) (0.401 nm) enables it to approach adsorption sites more readily than Cd(II) (0.426 nm). The difference in hydration energy affects the energy barrier for the dehydration process. The higher hydration energy of Cd(II) (1826.7 kJ/mol) makes it more difficult to shed its hydration shell compared to Pb(II) (1500.6 kJ/mol) [36, 37].

The difference in electronegativity determines the interfacial bonding strength. The higher electronegativity of Pb(II) enables it to form stronger covalent bonds with oxygen-containing functional groups [38]. This observation aligns with the Hard and Soft Acids and Bases (HSAB) theory: oxygen-containing functional groups, acting as hard bases, preferentially coordinate with the harder acid Pb(II) [39], while the softer acid Cd(II) forms weaker complexes [40].

In summary, the competitive advantage of Pb(II) stems from the electrostatic driving force provided by the negatively charged biochar surface, along with its sequential advantages in size, dehydration energy, and bonding capability. These factors collectively establish a complete competitive adsorption mechanism.

Potential adsorption mechanism

The adsorption mechanisms of Pb(II) and Cd(II) on biochar were investigated using FTIR spectroscopy to analyze shifts in functional group vibrations. As shown in Fig. 9a and b, both NBC and BBC exhibited distinct changes in the –OH and –C=O stretching vibration peaks after Pb(II) and Cd(II) adsorption. These results indicated that –OH and –COOH groups played a significant role in complexation with Pb(II) and Cd(II) in the adsorption process.

Fig. 9FTIR spectra of BBC (a) and NBC (b) before and after Pb(II) and Cd(II) adsorption, electrostatic interactions (c) and Na⁺ exchange after adsorption (d)

FTIR spectra of BBC (a) and NBC (b) before and after Pb(II) and Cd(II) adsorption, electrostatic interactions (c) and Na⁺ exchange after adsorption (d)

Pb(II) adsorption caused a pronounced reduction in the intensity of –OH and –C=O peaks, highlighting a stronger interaction with these functional groups compared to Cd(II). In contrast, Cd(II) adsorption resulted in minimal intensity changes, indicating a weaker affinity. These findings confirm the preferential adsorption of Pb(II) onto biochar, likely due to its greater ability to form stable complexes with surface functional groups.

Desorption experiments were conducted by immersing metal-loaded biochar in sodium nitrate solution and pure water to evaluate the role of electrostatic interactions in adsorption. As shown in Fig. 9c, the contributions of electrostatic interactions to Cd(II) removal were minimal, measuring 0.64 mg/g for NBC and 1.21 mg/g for BBC. Similarly, for Pb(II), the contributions were slightly higher, at 1.28 mg/g for NBC and 1.58 mg/g for BBC. Furthermore, desorption in pure water was negligible, underscoring the strong stability of biochar after metal adsorption and suggesting a low risk of heavy metal leaching during practical applications. These results confirm that electrostatic forces contribute minimally to the overall adsorption mechanism and emphasize the robustness and safety of biochar as an effective material for heavy metal remediation [46].

The role of cation exchange was evaluated by quantifying Na+ release after metal adsorption. This is a standard methodology for discerning the contribution of ion exchange to the overall adsorption mechanism [25]. To ensure validity, all biochars were thoroughly rinsed to neutrality prior to experiments, minimizing interference from free Na⁺ and ensuring that the measured release primarily resulted from stoichiometric exchange with metal cations. As illustrated in Fig. 9d, both NBC and BBC exhibited similar Na⁺ exchange levels for each metal.

The key observation is the comparable Na⁺ release from both biochars for a given metal, despite NBC’s significantly higher total adsorption capacity. This conclusively demonstrates that the superior performance of NBC is not attributable to enhanced cation exchange. Instead, it confirms that Na⁺ exchange is a substantial pathway for Cd(II) uptake on both materials. For Pb(II), the markedly higher adsorption must be governed by stronger, non-exchange mechanisms, such as chemisorption via coordination to oxygen-containing functional groups [47].

The observed displacement of pre-adsorbed Cd(II) by subsequently added Pb(II) in the sequential adsorption experiments (Fig. 7) was primarily attributable to competitive adsorption rather than ion exchange. The Na⁺ exchange experiment (Fig. 9d) provided direct evidence for this, demonstrating that cation exchange contributed minimally to the adsorption of both metals. If ion exchange were the dominant mechanism underlying the displacement, a substantial release of Na+ would be anticipated upon Pb(II) addition. However, the measured Na⁺ release was limited and comparable for both metals, indicating that ion exchange was not the primary process governing the displacement.

This displacement behavior was instead explained by the higher intrinsic affinity of Pb(II) for oxygen-containing functional groups on the biochar surface. As discussed previously, this enhanced affinity arises from the smaller hydrated radius, lower hydration energy, and stronger complexation ability of Pb(II), characteristics that align with the principles of HSAB theory. Collectively, these properties enable Pb(II) to effectively compete with and displace pre-adsorbed Cd(II) from binding sites. This interpretation was fully consistent with the affinity sequence Pb > Cd established throughout the study.

Conclusion

This research demonstrates that modified rice hull biochar effectively enhance heavy metal removal from complex wastewater. Systematic evaluation of β-cyclodextrin and NaOH activated biochar revealed distinct adsorption behaviors for Pb(II) and Cd(II) in single and binary systems.

Experimental results establish NBC as superior for creating optimal porous structures. The resulting NBC consistently outperformed BBC in adsorption capacity and efficiency. Pb(II) demonstrated dominant adsorption site occupation in competitive tests, with sequential experiments confirming its ability to displace pre-sorbed Cd(II). Kinetic and isotherm analyses provided mechanistic insights into these differential affinities. These findings offer practical wastewater treatment strategies through material selectivity and mechanistic understanding of competitive adsorption.

Being laboratory-scale with simulated wastewater, future work should test practical efficacy in real industrial effluents while examining long-term stability, regeneration potential, and economic feasibility. Research extension to additional heavy metal combinations and fixed-bed systems represents promising next steps.
