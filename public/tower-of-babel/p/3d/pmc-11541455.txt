Concomitant open aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic replacement, coronary artery bypass grafting and open infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm repair
Authors: Mohammed M Chowdhury, Ali Navi, Ismail Vokshi, Ravi De Silva, Simon M Kreckler, Shakil Farid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcurrent occurrence of coronary artery disease, aortic valve pathology, and abdominal aortic aneurysm presents a complex challenge for surgical intervention. Traditional staged approach bears considerable risks, including two major operations and potential aneurysm rupture during initial surgery. This case report outlines the procedural intricacies and successful outcome of a simultaneous approach to address all pathologies, offering insights into operative considerations.Keywords: Abdominal aortic aneurysm, Valve replacement, Coronary artery bypass grafting

Abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) are often linked to clinically significant concurrent conditions such as coronary artery disease and valve pathology. Approximately 80% of patients with AAAs exhibit angiographic signs of atherosclerosis in at least one coronary vessel, and among them, around 18% have surgically treatable disease.1 Consequently, it is well-established to recommend cardiac revascularization, if necessary, before addressing AAAs.2,3

The optimal timing for surgery has been a subject of considerable debate. Traditionally, the consensus suggests approaching AAA repair between 6 weeks to 6 months after coronary revascularization. Numerous studies have showcased the safety and effectiveness of a unified procedure involving coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) followed by AAA repair, whether performed with or without cardiopulmonary bypass.4, 5, 6

Conducting a single operation offers advantages such as avoiding repeat anesthesia and eliminating the need for separate recovery periods. The rationale behind this approach hinges on the belief that there is minimal to no additional operative risk for the patient.

In this context, we present the operative strategy employed by a specialized complex aortic surgical team at a tertiary center, involving combined cardiothoracic and vascular surgery.

Case report

A 76-year-old gentleman presented to the general cardiology team with shortness of breath, peripheral edema, and a B-type natriuretic peptide of 11,000 pg/mL. Past medical history included hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, and smoking. His echocardiogram revealed critical aortic stenosis (peak velocity of 5.2 m/s, peak pressure gradient 107 mmHg, valve area 0.8 cm2) with an ejection fraction of 35%. Coronary angiogram revealed mild disease to the left anterior descending and left main stem and moderate to severe disease at the left circumflex and right coronary artery.

As part of the workup for the initially planned transcatheter aortic valve implantation, his computed tomography angiogram demonstrated incidental findings of large 82 mm infrarenal AAA and dilated ascending aorta of 49 mm, precluding transcatheter aortic valve implantation (Fig 1).

The AAA had multiple adverse anatomical features for conventional endovascular repair, including unsuitable proximal landing zone and access. The infrarenal ‘neck’ measured 10 mm (precluding endovascular aneurysm repair), and, given the extent of cardiac disease and comorbid pathology, fenestrated stent graft repair was also precluded.

He was reviewed in the joint cardiothoracic and vascular complex aortic clinic, and merits of intervention were thoroughly discussed. Due to the presence of significant aortic valve disease and large AAA, a staged procedure could not be performed. Aortic valve repair had increased the perioperative risk of rupture for the large AAA. Open repair of the AAA was not feasible with significant aortic valve disease. The recommendation, therefore, were for CABG × 2 (vein grafts to the first obtuse marginal artery and posterior descending artery/distal right coronaray artery), aortic valve replacement (AVR) with bioprosthesis, ascending aortic replacement, and open AAA repair.

The patient gave full agreement and written consent to publish their case details and images, as part of this manuscript.

Clinical outcome

The patient experienced a positive recovery after the operation, spending 6 days in the cardiac intensive care unit. However, a prolonged ileus persisted for 4 days, ultimately resolving with supportive management. Discharge took place on postoperative day 10. Thirty-day recovery was uneventful.

Discussion

The coexistence of AAAa and coronary artery disease presents a common medical challenge in the elderly population. Nearly 20% of all patients with AAAs exhibit surgically correctable three-vessel disease. Numerous studies advocate for a thorough cardiac assessment and intervention before addressing AAAs.1

The heightened risk of AAA rupture following major abdominal and thoracic operations is well-documented.7,8 Hemodynamic instability, leading to fluctuations in systemic blood pressure, can complicate the post-CABG course, contributing to further aneurysm dilation and eventual rupture. The inflammatory response induced by CPB grafting may directly impact the aneurysm wall, diminishing its tensile strength and promoting further dilation. Increased collagenase activity emerges as a crucial factor in early rupture post-major surgical interventions.9,10 Additionally, perioperative malnutrition plays a role in reduced collagen production and the risk of aneurysm rupture.

The authors advocate for a single operative approach encompassing CABG, AVR, ascending aorta replacement, and open AAA repair as a safe and effective procedure. Our experience indicates no significant 30-day morbidity, and the specialized team continues to perform these concurrent procedures with ongoing success. Continued monitoring and additional clinical studies are imperative to ensure sustained low operative morbidity and mortality, as well as to mitigate the risk of long-term complications for these patients.

The advantages of a single operative approach include reducing the risk of rupture, minimizing the anesthesia risk in a high-risk cohort, and mitigating the potential for catastrophic emboli during the procedure.

Conclusion

The authors assert that a single procedure encompassing CABG, AVR, and open AAA repair is both safe and effective. Careful patient case selection is deemed crucial, but the simultaneous approach ensures comparable patient care while mitigating the risk of postoperative aneurysm rupture and reducing the length of stay after the procedure.
