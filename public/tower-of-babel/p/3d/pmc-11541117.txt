Randomized Controlled Trial of a Novel Cementless vs. Cemented Total Knee Arthroplasty: Early Clinical and Radiographic Outcomes
Authors: Enze Zhao, Xiaoyan Zhu, Haiwei Tang, Zhenyu Luo, Weinan Zeng, Zongke Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrevious cementless total knee arthroplasty (TKA) designs faced challenges with insufficient initial fixation on tibial side, resulting in inferior functional outcomes and survival rates. The Zoned Trabecular Bone Cementless Knee is a novel implant designed for cementless TKA which aims to achieve excellent initial fixation, promoting effective osseointegration. The aim of this research was to compare the early clinical and radiographic results of this cementless TKA with cemented TKA.MethodsBetween September 2021 and April 2022, 64 patients (64 knees) were recruited in this prospective randomized controlled trial to receive either cementless 3D‐printed trabecular metal TKA or a cemented posterior stabilized TKA. Preoperative and postoperative clinical evaluations, including the range of motion (ROM), Knee Society Score (KSS), and the Reduced Western Ontario and MacMaster Universities Score (WOMAC), were conducted and analyzed for comparison. Radiographs and computed tomography scans were utilized to assess the initial fixation. The complications between the two groups were also recorded and compared. Continuous data were analyzed for significance using independent‐samples t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test and categorical data were analyzed using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact test.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant enhancement at 12 months follow‐up in the ROM compared with baseline (ROM: 94.7 ± 23.4 vs. 113.1 ± 12.3 in cementless group and 96.5 ± 14.7 vs. 111.0 ± 12.8 in cemented group, p < 0.05). However, no statistical differences were observed between the two groups in postoperative ROM, KSS, or WOMAC score. The radiographs and computed tomography scans showed similar results, including radiolucent lines and osteolysis in either femoral or tibial. Additionally, there was no statistical difference in the overall complication rate between the two groups. Notably, one patient in the cementless TKA group required revision for periprosthetic infection as the end point.ConclusionsThis novel 3D‐printed trabecular metal cementless TKA achieved comparable clinical outcomes and initial fixation to cemented TKA in early stage. Longer‐term examination is necessary to validate these results.Keywords: 3D Printing, Cemented, Cementless, Total Knee Arthroplasty, Trabecular Metal Components

Introduction

While cemented fixation remains the gold standard in total knee arthroplasty (TKA), cementless TKA is increasingly seen as the future trend. 1 , 2 Theoretically, cementless fixation could offer advantages such as bone stock preservation, shorter operation time, avoidance of cement‐related complications, and potential long‐term biological fixation of the prosthesis to bone. 3 , 4 , 5 However, previous designs have faced challenges with insufficient initial fixation, particularly on the tibial side, leading to inferior functional outcomes and survival rates. 6 , 7 , 8 Additionally, studies have reported a higher rate of aseptic loosening of cementless tibial components compared to cemented TKA in radiostereometric analysis. 9 , 10

Various strategies have been employed to improve clinical outcomes and initial fixation of cementless implants, including the use of screws, extra pegs, high porosity, and roughened surface. 11 , 12 Previous studies have shown satisfactory mid‐term outcomes with Trabecular Metal (Zimmer, Warsaw, Indiana), which features a roughened surface and high porosity resembling cancellous bone. 13 , 14 Similarly, the Zoned Trabecular Bone Cementless Knee (Just Medical Devices, Tianjin, China) is a novel posterior‐stabilized (PS) titanium alloy implant designed for cementless TKA (Figure 1). Its tibial component surface mimics the morphological and mechanical characteristics of native trabecular bone in the proximal tibia. Additionally, the stem of the tibial component incorporates a mechanical locking structure that deploys piercing claws forward and backward after implantation. These design features aim to achieve excellent initial fixation, promoting effective osseointegration in the tibial cut osteotomy and yielding satisfactory functional outcomes compared to conventional cementless TKA.

Although many studies have confirmed the long‐term survival and clinical results of designed cementless prostheses, 15 , 16 , 17 limited studies have explored short‐term results during the early osseointegration stage. During this stage, there is apprehension that patients may undergo severe postoperative pain due to early prostheses micromotion, potentially impacting their experience, increasing opioid administration, and limiting functional exercise during the important early postoperative stage of TKA recovery. 18 In addition, no studies have reported on the functional and radiographic outcomes of this newly designed cementless implant. Therefore, we conducted this randomized controlled trial (RCT) to (1) determine whether the use of this novel cementless TKA could achieve satisfactory clinical rating during the early osseointegration stage and (2) ascertain the initial stability provided by its distinctive mechanical locking structure design.

Materials and Methods

This prospective RCT was approved by the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100052714) and the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital (HX‐IRB‐AF‐18‐V4.0). All included patients provided written informed consent prior to participation, and all procedures adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations.

Patient Recruitment

Between September 2021 and April 2022, patients diagnosed with osteoarthritis who were scheduled for primary unilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA) at our hospital were considered for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included age > 70 or < 40 years, severe osteoporosis or bone defects, knee deformity (flexion deformity ≥30° or varus‐valgus deformity ≥30°), body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2, history of knee surgery or knee infection, history of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE), long‐term opioid consumption, diagnosis of neuromuscular disorders, or refusal to participation.

Out of 79 patients evaluated for eligibility, nine were found ineligible based on the criteria, while six opted not to participate, resulting in the enrollment of 64 patients. Finally, this study included 30 patients in the cementless TKA group and 34 patients in the cemented TKA group. Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), operation side, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status, direction of knee deformity on the coronal plane, knee range of motion (ROM), femoral tibial angle (FTA), the Knee Society Score (KSS), and the Reduced Western Ontario and MacMaster Universities score (WOMAC), revealed no statistical differences (Table 1).

Randomization

Each eligible patient was assigned a unique number and was randomized using a computer‐generated table to receive either cementless or cemented femoral and tibial components. Two outcome assessors were blinded to the group assignment. Surgeon and patients were all blinded to the group assignment. Data analysis was conducted by one researcher, who was also blind to the group assignment.

Surgical Procedure

A single surgeon conducted all TKA in this research using medial parapatellar approach and general anesthesia. Tibial resection was performed with extramedullary alignment guide and femoral resection was conducted using intramedullary alignment guide. Patella resurfacing was not performed. Based on the results of randomization, eligible patients received either cementless or cemented PS prostheses (Just Medical Devices, Tianjin, China). After cementless components were implanted, the surgeon rotated the top bar, initiating its downward rotation along the internal threads of the ring plate. The lower end of the top bar slotted between two axes, causing the two piercing claws to diverge. These piercing claws, with their fan‐shaped dual‐layer structure, extended from the vertical apertures and embedded into the cancellous and cortical bone of the tibia, firmly anchoring the tibial plateau and facilitating mechanical compression. The degree of mechanical compression could be finely adjusted by turning the top bar using a torque wrench. Apart from the implantation of different prostheses, all procedures were standardized across both groups. In addition, the piercing claws of the tibial component can move freely in one dimension, providing fixation through the top bar. During revision surgery, unscrewing the top bar allows the piercing claws to retract, facilitating smooth removal of the tibial component by allowing it to pull out without obstruction.

Perioperative Management

Preoperative medication included alprazolam (0.4 mg) the night before surgery. A drainage tube was used for 24 hours postoperatively. Imrecoxib was given orally 100mg twice a day for postoperative pain. Enoxaparin was administered 2000 IU subcutaneously 8 hours postoperatively, followed by 4000 IU once daily, with rivaroxaban (10 mg) orally once daily for 10 days post‐discharge. Physical therapists supervised patients in active ROM exercises and ambulation based on standard protocols.

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Patients underwent routine evaluations preoperatively and at 6 and 12 months postoperatively, including knee ROM, KSS, and WOMAC score. 19 , 20 Radiographs, including serial standardized anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of knee and long‐leg weight‐bearing anteroposterior films, were obtained under fluoroscopic control at 6 months postoperatively. Radiographic assessments included the femoral tibial angle (FTA, the angle between the mechanical axis of femur and the mechanical axis of tibia), the coronal femoral inclination (CFI, the medial femoral angle relative to the mechanical axis), the femoral flexion–extension angle (FFA, the angle formed by the line drawn through the midshaft of femoral component and the anatomical axis of femur), the coronal tibial inclination (CTI, the medial tibial angle in relation to the mechanical axis), the tibial slope (TS, the angle between the line drawn via the midshaft of the tibia and a line perpendicular to the tibial component's plateau), posterior condylar offset (the maximum thickness of the posterior condyle projected anteriorly to the tangent of the posterior cortex of the femoral shaft), and radiolucent lines at the cement‐bone or cement‐prosthesis, or cementless prosthesis‐bone interfaces. 19 , 21 A computed tomography (CT) scan was used to assess the osteolysis. Osteolysis was defined as nonlinear cancellous bone loss around protheses. Complications such as intramuscular vein thrombosis, superficial infection, end‐of‐stem pain, peroneus communis nerve palsy, falls after surgery, and revisions for aseptic loosening or infection were recorded. Interobserver variability was assessed by two blinded investigators independently performing all clinical and radiological measurements.

Statistical Analysis

A clinically significant difference in KSS evaluation was set at 5 points (SD ± 6). Power analysis determined a sample size of 24 patients per group to reach 80% power at a significance level of 0.05. Continuous data were showed as means and standard deviations and analyzed using independent‐samples t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were showed as numbers and analyzed using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact test. Statistical significance was determined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Clinical Rating

The clinical evaluations after index surgery were presented in Table 2. The two groups exhibited significant improvements in the postoperative KSS (knee and function) and the WOMAC score (pain and function) compared to baseline. The mean KSS‐knee score showed no significant difference between the two groups at the 6‐month follow‐up (cemented group: 90.0 ± 7.4; cementless group: 87.4 ± 9.6; p = 0.227) or the 12‐month follow‐up (cemented group: 92.7 ± 5.9; cementless group: 93.6 ± 4.5; p = 0.500). Similarly, comparable results were observed in the mean KSS‐function score at 6‐month follow‐up (cemented group: 90.3 ± 8.3; cementless group: 88.0 ± 10.4; p = 0.329) or at 12‐month follow‐up (cemented group: 93.1 ± 6.7; cementless group: 94.5 ± 6.1; p = 0.388). There was no significant difference in the WOMAC score (pain and function) between the two groups, indicating comparable quality of life outcomes. At 6 months post‐surgery, the mean range of knee motion was 110.5 ± 13.0 for the cemented group and 109.1 ± 15.2 for the cementless group. At 12 months post‐surgery, the range of knee motion was 111.0 ± 12.8 for the cemented group, and 113.1 ± 12.3 for the cementless group.

Radiographic Evaluations

The majority of TKA implants in this study were well‐embedded in favorable positions (Figure 2). No statistical differences were observed between the two groups concerning the knee alignment, femoral and tibial component positions in the coronal and sagittal planes, posterior slope and posterior condylar offset of the tibial component, or incidence of radiolucent lines (Table 3). Radiographs and CT scans revealed no evidence of femoral and tibial osteolysis in the two groups.

Complications

Complications were presented in Table 4. Infection was detected in one patient in each group: one with superficial infection in the cemented TKA group and one with periprosthetic infection in the cementless TKA group. The patient with superficial infection receiving intravenous antibiotics recovered without sequela. The infecting organism cultured was Staphylococcus epidermis. Periprosthetic infection occurred in the case of cementless TKA group 6 months postoperatively, also attributed to Staphylococcus epidermis (Figure 3). The patient underwent debridement, two‐stage exchange arthroplasty and chronic antibiotic suppressive therapy, resulting in a stable knee prosthesis with no reactivation of infection at the final follow‐up. Additionally, two cases in the cementless TKA group and one case in the cemented TKA group underwent intramuscular vein thrombosis, all of whom recovered after 3 weeks of anticoagulant therapy.

Discussion

Our prospective randomized controlled study showed that no statistically significant differences were found between the cementless and cemented groups in clinical ratings, radiographic evaluations, and complications, suggesting that the novel cementless TKA design could achieve encouraging short‐term clinical and radiographic results, with no clear disadvantage compared to cemented TKAs in the early postoperative period.

Clinical Rating

In this study, no statistically significant differences were observed in clinical evaluations between the two groups, involving KSS, WOMAC score, and knee ROM, suggesting encouraging short‐term results. However, it was noteworthy that a trend toward a higher clinical rating was observed in cemented TKA group compared with cementless TKA group at 6 months after surgery. A recent meta‐analysis also indicated significantly worse postoperative KSS and ROM outcomes in cementless TKA. 22 Several interpretations may account for the better clinical scores observed in the present study: first, the knee swelling and anemia associated with extra intra‐articular blood loss from cementless fixation may compromise early functional recovery; second, to reduce baseplate migration, cementless TKA with stems, keels, pegs, or combinations reported by multiple studies may cause unsatisfactory clinical ratings. For example, stem insertion for requiring a tight press‐fit in a patulous femur or tibia may cause mild to moderate pain 23 ; third, during the early osseointegration phase within 6 months of TKA, patients undergoing cementless TKA may have increased pain with poorer knee function due to micromotion. 24 Although the mechanical piercing claw design used in this study penetrates a limited amount of cancellous bone to minimize its impact on postoperative clinical outcomes, longer‐term follow‐up is necessary to verify the safety and efficacy of this device.

Radiographic Evaluations

Cemented fixation remains widely regarded as the gold standard in knee arthroplasty, outperforming uncemented TKA based on knee arthroplasty registers. 25 However, no statistical differences were observed between the two groups on radiographic results in this study. Research indicates that the ideal position of the tibial component should be perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis, as slight varus alignment may increase the risk of early implant loosening. 26 In our study, we observed no such events, suggesting that the prosthesis design employed may tolerate slight varus alignment. Given the typically early indication of aseptic loosening in TKA by radiolucent lines exceeding 2 mm, suggestive of inadequate bone ingrowth or bonding with fibrous connective tissue alone, 27 we conducted radiographic evaluations at 6 and 12 months post‐TKA to assess such lines. In the present study, the majority of radiolucent lines around the femoral and tibial components resolved with bony ingrowth over the course of 1 year, indicating robust fixation to the bone without evidence of aseptic loosening. Although radiolucent lines persisted in three knees at the final follow‐up, their size remained minimal (<2 mm) and non‐progressive suggesting that the tibial component achieved ample bone fixation for cementless TKA and is likely to maintain stability postoperatively. Considering previous reports of more frequent radiolucent lines at the tibial base plate in cementless TKA, 28 we believed this cementless prosthesis could promote osseointegration and enhance interface strength aligning with findings from similar improved biomaterials including porous tantalum and highly porous titanium. 29

Cemented PS implants are associated with increasing revision risk compared to cemented cruciate‐retaining implants, 30 possibly because of the bone‐cement interface's inability to withstand increased forces resulting from the greater constraint of the PS prosthesis. 31 Cementless PS implants face similar challenges: in a study comparing the results of radiostereometric analysis between cemented and cementless PS prosthesis, the migration of the cementless prosthesis was notably greater and continuously increased across time, suggesting the problems tolerating these forces 32 ; in addition, the micromotion that existed between the tibial insert and baseplate is inevitable and its effect on the prosthesis cannot be ignored. However, recent studies have shown stabilization of migration in trabecular metal (TM) PS implants after 3 months, similar to CR implants. 33 Hence, the similar trabecular metal design employed in the present study may also withstand these forces at the interface. Moreover, the tibial component utilizing piercing claw locking mechanism might also minimize micromotion and backside wear, although long‐term clinical follow‐up is warranted.

Complications

Although cemented prostheses tend to have a greater complication rate compared with cementless prostheses due to factors such as longer operation duration, thermal damage, and release of radicals, 34 our study found no statistical differences in the complication rate. This may be attributed to the typically younger patients receiving cementless TKA, who are more likely to have better physical fitness and functional bone metabolism, thus minimizing the risk of postoperative complications. Therefore, further research is warranted, particularly in older patients (>70 years), to ascertain if short‐term results are comparable in more sedentary individuals with lower bone mineral density or osteoporosis. In addition, Mohammad et al. suggested that in patients receiving uncemented TKA, the revision ratio of infection was higher than the cementless patients. 35 Nevertheless, there was one case in the cementless TKA group receiving revision for periprosthetic infection but none in the cemented TKA group in the present study. The reason may be attributed to the absence of significant difference in operation duration between the two groups, likely influenced by the demanding procedure for bone preparation and the associated learning curve required for using this device. 36

Strengths and Limitations

The study presents several strengths. Firstly, our findings corroborate the favorable outcomes associated with the novel cementless total knee arthroplasty (TKA) design, thereby facilitating informed clinical decisions. Secondly, this research could set a foundation for future, larger‐scale, and longer‐term investigations into the potential of cementless TKA as a viable alternative. Several limitations also exist in the present study. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small. Secondly, short‐term follow‐up period may not be sufficient, and a greater number of participants and an extended follow‐up duration would yield more substantial and insightful findings. Thirdly, due the lack of equipment, radiostereometric analysis was not utilized to measure the relative movement of the implants in this study. Lastly, while a standardized imaging protocol was employed for all participants, several radiographs might be rotated, posing challenges in the interpretation of radiolucent lines or bony ingrowth.

Conclusion

While study was conducted on a relatively small cohort of patients, the results of the novel 3D‐printed cementless implant appear promising in early stage. Only long‐term follow‐up can definitively determine whether it represents a viable alternative to the traditional cemented implant.
