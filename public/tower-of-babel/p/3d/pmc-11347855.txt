Betaine and feed restriction as potential mitigation strategies against heat stress in two strains of laying hens
Authors: Renée De Baets, Kobe Buyse, Gunther Antonissen, Evelyne Delezie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Climate change is increasingly manifesting in temperate regions. Laying hens are highly sensitive to heat stress and mitigation strategies should be implemented to reduce the negative effects. The goal of this experiment was to evaluate the effects of betaine in drinking water (0.55 g/L) and 4 h feed restriction during peak heat stress on laying performance, egg quality, blood gas parameters, body temperature (Tb), and oxidative stress in 2 different breeds of laying hens. Therefore, 448 ISA Brown hens (25 wk) and 448 Lohmann LSL classic laying hens (26 wk) were housed in 128 cages (7 hens/cage). Thermoneutral (TN) data was collected for 21 d before cyclic heat stress (HS) (21d; 32 ± 2°C; 6 h daily). During HS, hens were divided into 4 treatments: 1) feed restriction (FR), 2) betaine supplementation in drinking water (BET), 3) feed restriction and betaine supplementation in drinking water (FR-BET), or 4) control (CON). The effects were evaluated after 1 wk of HS (acute heat stress; AHS) and 3 wk of HS (chronic heat stress; CHS). Laying rate and egg mass (EM) diminished during CHS but decreased more in white than brown hens (2.78% and 1.94%; −1.57% and −0.81%, respectively; P = 0.004) and remained unaltered by BET or FR. During AHS, average daily feed intake (ADFI) increased compared to TN, but the increase was higher in white than brown hens (6.36% and 2.62%, respectively; P = 0.001). Egg shell quality deteriorated during AHS and CHS, but was most affected in white hens, FR or BET did not impact this. Blood pCO2, HCO3− and base excess significantly decreased during AHS and CHS, but pH and iCa were unaltered. Blood glucose increased in white hens during AHS compared to TN (P < 0.001), while plasma malondialdehyde increased in brown hens (P < 0.001). Results indicated that laying hens experienced HS, but breed differences were observed and white hens were generally most affected. FR affected feed conversion ratio negatively during CHS. However, FR and BET could not improve laying performance, egg quality, Tb, or blood parameters during HS.

INTRODUCTION

Climate change is increasingly manifesting in temperate regions, leading to seasonal shifts, heat waves and more frequent and intense temperatures during summers with deleterious effects on farm animals (Hempel et al., 2019; Mikovits et al., 2019). Broilers and laying hens, in particular, are highly sensitive to heat stress due to their absence of sweat glands and their insulating feather coverage (Saeed et al., 2019). Moreover, birds have a high metabolism which contributes to a higher metabolic heat production, making them more susceptible to heat stress (Wasti et al., 2020). High productive laying hens need a balanced climate and good housing management to have optimal animal health resulting in good production. Modern laying hen strains are bred towards a high production rate that inadvertently intensifies metabolic heat production and as a consequence exacerbates their susceptibility to heat (Lin et al., 2006b; Lara and Rostagno, 2013). Subsequently, heat stress adversely affects animal welfare, health, egg production and egg quality (Lara and Rostagno, 2013). Although, those effects are influenced by age and genetics, heat stress impacts various genotypes differently and some breeds are more heat-tolerant than others (Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007; Melesse et al., 2011; Melesse et al., 2013; Mignon-Grasteau et al., 2015). To estimate the degree of these differences, 2 breeds of laying hens were studied in this trial.

It has been shown that even mild heat stress conditions can alter different physiological processes in these high-performing chickens, which are bred to perform optimally in thermoneutral conditions. This thermoneutral zone is regularly defined as a range with an upper and lower critical temperature. Between this range, body temperature can be maintained without extra mechanisms for heat loss (Saeed et al., 2019). When the thermoneutral range is exceeded, heat stress will cause birds to start panting to dissipate body heat (Abbas et al., 2022). Eventually, an elevated respiration rate will result in respiratory alkalosis characterized by a low blood pCO2, high pH and decreased blood HCO3− concentration (Borges et al., 2003; Borges et al., 2004; Abbas et al., 2017). The higher pH could affect the concentration of calcium bound to protein, hampering free calcium availability for eggshell production (Odom et al., 1986; Barrett et al., 2019). Moreover, lowered blood HCO3− reduces the amount of CO3− available for eggshell mineralization, further impacting egg quality (Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007; He, et al., 2019). When heat stress coping mechanisms are insufficient, the birds’ body temperature will increase, making cloacal body temperature (Tb) a reliable indicator for heat stress (Kilic and Simsek, 2013). Heat stress also disturbs the balance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant mechanisms, causing oxidative stress (Lin et al., 2006a; Akbarian et al., 2016), which can cause lipid peroxidation. In turn, lipid peroxidation induces cellular injuries which may compromise performance, immunity and animal welfare (Grotto et al., 2007).

To reduce these negative effects, mitigation strategies can be implemented. These strategies can be categorized into genetics, cooling systems, management strategies, nutrition, and additives (Abbas et al., 2019; He et al., 2019). A widely used additive, betaine, is a naturally occurring compound with antioxidant properties able to reduce oxidative stress (Abbas et al., 2021). It has two main functions: it acts as a methyl donor and as a compatible osmolyte (Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018; Wasti et al., 2020). As a methyl donor, betaine supports several methylation processes in the protein and lipid metabolism, especially carnitine and creatine synthesis (El-Ghany and Babazadeh, 2022). As an osmolyte, betaine stabilizes osmoregulation by preventing cellular dehydration (Al-Qaisi et al., 2023). Betaine supplementation was hypothesized to improve laying performance during HS because it improves nutrient digestibility (Ratriyanto et al., 2017; Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018). Moreover, betaine could stimulate the secretion of follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone which increases egg production (Attia et al., 2016; Ratriyanto et al., 2017; Attia, et al., 2018). Another study proved that including dietary betaine at 0.5% enhanced egg production by inducing hepatic lipogenesis and hypomethylation (Omer et al., 2020). In several heat stress studies, supplementation of betaine improved feed intake (Attia et al., 2016; Chand et al., 2017; Park and Kim, 2017) and laying performance (Attia, et al., 2016; Al-Qaisi, et al., 2023) and decreased body temperature (Al-Qaisi et al., 2023). However, heat stress reduces feed intake which may cause variability in the uptake of feed additives like betaine, causing variable effects (Attia, et al., 2016; Park and Kim, 2017; Al-Qaisi, et al., 2023). On the other hand, water intake increases during heat stress, thus supplementing betaine in drinking water may result in more beneficial and consistent effects. Studies of betaine in drinking water are limited, therefore, its use as a drinking water supplement was tested in this study. Another strategy against heat stress could be temporary feed restriction, reducing body temperature and mortality (Francis, et al., 1991; Abu-Dieyeh, 2006). By restricting feed intake, the heat produced from digestion can be reduced, thereby altering the balance of heat loss and heat load, which is crucial in managing heat stress (MacLeod and Hocking, 1993; Abu-Dieyeh, 2006; Barrett et al., 2019). Additionally, feed restriction may reduce plasma triglyceride concentrations, which decreases the severeness of heat stress-related cardiovascular issues (MacLeod and Hocking, 1993). However, feed restriction in laying hens is understudied and could be detrimental to egg production (Mashaly et al., 2004). Therefore, controlled feed restriction was tested as a secondary measure to mitigate heat stress.

The objectives of this experiment were to explore the effectiveness of betaine supplementation in the drinking water, feed restriction, and their combination during acute and chronic daily cyclic heat stress in minimizing the stress on health and production in 2 breeds of laying hens. This was done by assessing production, body temperature, egg quality, and blood gas parameters.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All experimental procedures in this study complied with the European guidelines for the care and use of animals in research (Directive 2010/63/EU) and were approved by the Ethical Committee of the Research Institute for Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (ILVO), Merelbeke, Belgium under authorization number 2023/436.

Birds and Experimental Design

A total of 448 ISA Brown hens (25 wk) and 448 Lohmann LSL classic laying hens (26 wk) were used in this trial. The birds were housed in the poultry experimental facility of the Institute for Agricultural and Fisheries Research (ILVO) in a furnished cage system of 128 cages according to an alternate block design. Each cage housed 7 laying hens (7 hens/m2) with dimensions measuring 117 cm wide, 60 cm deep and 57 cm high. Additionally, all cages were enriched with a laying nest (58 cm wide), perch, mirrors and plastic curtains to provide more hiding opportunities. Hens were housed and reared under conventional conditions for lighting (15 h light/d; 05:00–20:00), heating and ventilation (air inlet valves in sidewalls, outlet via the roof). The duration of the trial consisted of an 8-wk adaptation period and a 6-wk experimental period. The experimental period was divided into 3 periods. First, all hens were reared under conventional thermoneutral (TN) conditions at 18.5°C to 20.5°C with relative humidity (RH) of 45-55% for 21 d. After this period, all birds were subjected to cyclical heat stress (HS) for 21 d, the first wk was considered acute heat stress (ACS; d22-29), and the remaining period as chronic heat stress (CHS). During HS, the temperature was increased to 32 ± 2°C with a RH of 45% to 55% for 6 h (09:30–15:30) by using an indirect combustion portable heater (GE105; Biemmedue; Cherasco; Italy). During the rest of the day, temperature was maintained around 26 ± 1°C with a RH of 45% to 55%. The heaters were turned off to reduce the stable temperature to ambient temperature. This allowed the stable temperature to decrease gradually, thus hens were not exposed to a sudden temperature change. Four data loggers were divided over the stable, one of these loggers was placed at the front, 1 at the back, and 1 at the side of the stable. Each of them logged temperature and RH every 15 min (Testo 175-H1, Testo BE, Ternat, Belgium). The fourth one was placed in the middle of the stable and measured CO2 as well (Aranet4, Fresh Air Solutions B.V., Vught, The Netherlands). Additionally, CO2 concentration was monitored by using 2 supplementary data loggers, 1 at the front and 1 at the back of the stable (OD17EE, Eltek, Cambridge, United Kingdom).

Treatments were only applied during HS because it was hypothesized that these strategies could alleviate HS effects. During HS, both types of laying hens were divided into 4 treatments according to an alternate block design: treatment 1 received control water and a feed-restricted diet. During feed restriction, laying hens did not have access to feed for 4 h (FR; 11:30-15:30). In the second treatment, drinking water was supplemented with nonhygroscopic betaine HCl at 0.55 g/L betaine (BET) and standard supplied diet. A third treatment received drinking water supplemented with nonhygroscopic betaine HCl at 0.55 g/L and a feed-restricted diet for 4 h (11:30-15:30) feed restriction and betaine (FR-BET). The last treatment was given control water and a standard supplied diet control (CON). If laying hens were not feed-restricted, the feed was supplied ad libitum. All layers were fed a standard diet according to breed recommendations (Table 1). Drinking water was provided ad libitum via drinking nipples. Measurements

Laying Performance. Egg production per pen (%) was determined daily for each replicate throughout the experiment and the mean egg production per week was calculated. Egg weight (g), egg mass (g/hen/d), and feed intake (g/hen/d) were recorded weekly. Hen weight (g/hen) was determined at the start and the end of the trial. In addition, feed conversion ratio (FCR) was computed by taking the ratio of average daily egg mass and average daily feed intake. The mortality rate was recorded throughout the trial.

Egg Quality. Internal and external egg quality was assessed for 32 eggs per treatment (2 eggs per pen replicate) once every 2 wk at d 15 (TN), 29 (AHS), and 43 (CHS) to assess the effects of each climate period.

After collection, eggs were stored at 10°C for 12 h pending analysis. Egg quality assessment protocol included the following measurements in chronological order: whole egg weight (g), dynamic stiffness (Kdyn), shell deformation (µm), breaking force (N), albumen height (mm), Haugh unit (HU), yolk and dry shell weight (g), and shell thickness (mm) was measured as described by Molnár et al. (2017).

Blood Parameters. Blood was collected in 1 hen per pen replicate on d 11, 25 and 39 corresponding with TN, AHS, and CHS. On the days of heat stress (AHS, CHS) the hens were sampled 4 h after heat stress was initiated that day. On d 11, the sampled hen was marked with a leg band and the same bird was used throughout the trial. Blood samples (5 mL) were withdrawn from the brachial vein and collected in an EDTA tube. The samples were then centrifuged at 3,000 g for 10 min, plasma was aliquoted and frozen at −80°C until analysis. Additionally, blood-gas analysis was done in half of the birds, 64 hens (8 hens/treatment) with an i-STAT Alinity (Zoetis, Zaventem, Belgium). The i-STAT analyzer was placed in the poultry barn anteroom to minimize dust contamination and to keep under the recommended operating temperature (15–32°C). Additionally, the i-STAT cartridges were kept at 4°C until used. Blood was collected in a 500 µL heparin tube and subsequently pipetted in the cartridge. Samples were processed within 15 min of collection. i-STAT analysis was done using CG8+ cartridges, which measures the following parameters: sodium (Na; mmol/L), potassium (K; mmol/L), ionized calcium (iCa; mmol/L), glucose (Glu; mmol/L), hematocrit (Hct), hemoglobin (Hgb; g/L) pH, carbon dioxide partial pressure (pCO2; kPa), oxygen partial pressure (pO2; kPa), bicarbonate (HCO3−; mmol/L), total carbon dioxide (TCO2; mmol/L), base excess (BE; mmol/L), and oxygen saturation (sO2; %).

Cloacal Temperature. During the experimental period, cloacal temperature (Tb) was obtained 3 times at the same time as blood sampling. Tb was measured 4 h after HS was initiated in 1 hen per replicate. On d 11, the hen was marked with a leg band, and the same bird was measured over the following observation days. A standard digital thermometer (Kruuse; Denmark) was used to record the cloacal temperature.

Oxidative Status. Malondialdehyde (MDA) was determined in plasma. The thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method was used to quantify MDA as a marker for lipid peroxidation according to the method of Grotto et al. (2007), with slight modifications (Goethals et al., 2019). TBARS values were determined after several reaction steps involving alkaline hydrolysis, acid deproteinization, derivatization with TBA, and extraction with 1-butanol. The absorbance of the carbonyl-TBA complexes was measured colorimetrically at 532 nm, quantified using a standard curve with tetramethoxypropane (0–30 nmol/mL), and expressed as nmol MDA equivalents/mL sample.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with R version 4.1.2 for Windows (R Core Team, 2021). Plots were made using ggplot2, analysis was performed using the packages emmeans and multcomp. The pen was considered the experimental unit. To assess the presence and degree of effects of heat stress, the difference of each experimental unit between acute heat stress or chronic heat stress with thermoneutral conditions were calculated (ΔAHS = AHS - TN and ΔCHS= CHS - TN). On these differences, further statistics were performed. Two tests were executed, one to assess the presence of heat stress with the one of 1-sample T-tests with no effects of heat stress being not significantly different from 0. A second test calculated the degree of effect of heat stress by using linear mixed-effect models with the interaction of breed and treatment as fixed effects. The fixed effects were arranged following an alternate block design, block was included as a random effect. Linear model assumptions (normality and homoscedasticity) were verified by a visual check of the residuals plots (q-q plots and histograms). Post-hoc pairwise multiple comparisons were used to assess the differences between treatments. Tukey's range test (Honest Significant Difference, HSD) was used to obtain adjusted P-values to account for multiple comparisons, with the level of significance α = 0.05. Tables and figures report mathematical means and standard error of means.

RESULTS

Climate

Temperature during heat stress (09:30–15:30) was observed to be 30.71 ± 0.049, while values were 25.95 ± 0.138 during thermoneutral hours. Relative humidity increased to 41.14 ± 0.300 during HS and decreased to 36.06 ± 5.930 during cooler hours. Performance

Hen weight of brown hens was significantly higher than white hens in both TN (Brown: 1.74 ± 0.007; White: 1.53 ± 0.006; P < 0.001) and CHS (Brown: 1.79 ± 0.009; White: 1.50 ± 0.010; P < 0.001). Laying rate (LR) was not significantly affected during acute heat stress. Subsequently, the degree of difference in laying rate was not significantly different between treatments nor between breeds when comparing AHS to TN (Table 2). Laying rate was significantly reduced by chronic heat stress. Moreover, the degree of decrease was more distinct in white laying hens (−2.78%) than in brown laying hens (−1.94%) (P = 0.004) with no effects of the treatments. Egg mass (EM) was increased after AHS in brown (P < 0.001) and white laying hens (P = 0.002). Three wk later (CHS), EM decreased and was no longer different compared to TN conditions in brown laying hens whilst EM of white hens during CHS was significantly lower compared to TN (P = 0.004). Therefore, the degree of difference of EM decreased most during CHS in white hens (−1.57%). Average daily feed intake (ADFI) increased after AHS in both laying hen breeds. The degree of increase in feed intake was significantly higher in white laying hens (+6.36%) compared to brown laying hens (+2.62%) (P = 0.001). During CHS, feed intake decreased and was not significantly different from TN conditions. In this period there were no differences between breeds or treatments. During AHS, feed conversion ratio (FCR) decreased in brown laying hens (−0.83%), but increased in white laying hens (+4.93%) when evaluating the degree of difference between TN and AHS (P < 0.001). After 3 wk of heat stress (CHS), the degree of difference of FCR was slightly increased in brown hens (+0.26%), but this increase was significantly higher in white hens (+2.78) compared to TN (P = 0.002). Egg Quality

Egg weight in brown hens increased during AHS (P = 0.001) and remained high during CHS (P < 0.001) (Figure 2; Supplementary Table 1). The degree of this increase was not affected by treatment. Yolk weight increased significantly after AHS and remained elevated during CHS. There was a significant interaction between treatment and breed when the degree of difference was evaluated between TN and AHS (P = 0.041). Shell thickness and shell weight significantly decreased during AHS and decreased even more during CHS. There was a bigger decrease in white laying hens when the degree of difference during AHS was compared to brown hens (resp.: P < 0.001; P = 0.003). Treatment did not affect this change. Consequently, dynamic stiffness significantly decreased in both breeds and stayed low during CHS. The degree of decrease of dynamic stiffness between TN and AHS was significantly bigger in white hens compared to brown hens (P = 0.031). When comparing the degree of difference between TN and CHS, there was a significant interaction between treatment and breed (P = 0.048). Shell deformation was not significantly affected by AHS in brown hens but increased in white hens. Additionally, the degree of difference was significantly bigger in white hens compared to brown hens (P = 0.002). Although shell deformation significantly increased during CHS, the degree of difference of shell deformation between TN and CHS did not differ between treatments or breeds. The breaking strength of the eggs was significantly reduced during AHS and remained low during CHS. The degree of decrease during AHS was higher in white hens than in brown hens (P = 0.007). Additionally, haugh unit (HU) significantly increased during AHS and decreased during CHS again not being significantly different from TN. DISCUSSION

Performance

The phenomenon of decreased laying rates during heat stress is well-documented (Attia et al., 2016; Barrett et al., 2019; Al-Qaisi et al., 2023), a pattern also evident in our study. In contrast to what is described in the literature (Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007; Melesse et al., 2013), a more pronounced decrease in laying rates was observed in white laying hens compared to brown laying hens. Moreover, egg mass was significantly lower in white hens during CHS which could be related to the lower laying rate. In our trial, egg mass increased during AHS, but decreased again during CHS, which is similar to Barrett et al. (2019). Normally, we would expect reduced egg weight after HS due to reduced feed intake to lower the metabolic heat load (Kirunda et al., 2001; Abu-Dieyeh, 2006; Barrett et al., 2019). However, in this study, ADFI was increased in both laying hen breeds during AHS, but decreased during CHS as expected.

Egg Quality

AHS lowered shell quality in both genotypes but was lowest in white hens. Both egg and yolk weights increased during HS which could be related to the higher ADFI. Some HS studies suggested a shift of water from the albumen to the yolk to be the reason (Kirunda et al., 2001), while others hypothesized that HS attenuated lipid mobilization and increased lipogenesis which could increase lipid deposition in the yolk (Emami et al., 2020; Mayorga et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2023). However, most studies found decreased yolk weight during HS (Mashaly et al., 2004; Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2023) because HS activated the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis which inhibits the production of yolk precursor proteins (Yoshida et al., 2011; Lu et al., 2019). Haugh unit (HU) significantly increased after 1 wk of HS, but was reduced again during CHS, following a similar trend as albumen height. Other studies report a lower HU after a 2- or 4-wk HS period (Barrett et al., 2019) due to lower protein synthesis and movement of water from the albumen to the yolk (Kirunda et al., 2001). Both yolk weight and HU increased in this study and could be related to an overall higher egg weight.

Blood Parameters

Generally, birds in heat stress will try to lose heat via evaporation (Lin et al., 2006b). However, hyperventilation causes loss of CO2 which is why pCO2 already decreased after 1 wk of HS in our study and continued to decrease (Borges et al., 2004; Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007). Furthermore, when pCO2 decreased, respiratory alkalosis was induced. Alkalosis causes calcium to bind to proteins, decreasing free calcium (Barrett et al., 2019). However, pH did not increase in this study, which could explain why iCa remained unaffected. In contrast, (Barrett et al., 2019) reported an increased pH after several hours of HS until 2 wk of HS, but Odom et al. (1986) reported that a high pH due to respiratory alkalosis could already normalize after 2 h which may explain why we did not see those effects. Compensation of pH is maintained by buffering low CO2 by removing bicarbonate through the kidneys as indicated by both a decrease in BE and HCO3− in our study (Borges et al., 2003). After 3 wk, BE and HCO3− increased which could be related to the body's ability to better cope with HS. Lower blood HCO3− levels could reduce the amount of CO3− available for eggshell mineralization as indicated by the poorer shell quality in this study (Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007; He et al., 2019).

AHS caused an increase in glucose which could be related to a stress response as corticosterone induces gluconeogenesis (Ralph et al., 2015). Additionally, increased corticosterone levels might induce insulin resistance which results in hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia (Lu et al., 2019). Although other studies observed that chickens during HS did not show a typical spike in blood insulin levels (Brugaletta et al., 2022). These conflicting results are also related to sampling time to HS duration which influences glucose levels greatly (Mayorga et al., 2020). During CHS, values decreased again which could indicate an adaptation to heat stress.

Lastly, increased body temperature in both genotypes indicated the effectiveness of heat stress.

Betaine

There were no effects of betaine on performance in this study. Similarly, other studies could not prove a beneficial effect of dietary betaine on feed intake during heat stress either (Shakeri et al., 2018; Al-Qaisi et al., 2023), while others reported feed intake to be improved (Attia et al., 2016; Chand et al., 2017). Studies have shown that dietary BET supplementation increased protein and egg yolk precursors, i.e. very low-density lipoproteins and vitellogenin, thus increasing internal and external egg quality in hens (Attia et al., 2018; Omer et al., 2020) (Attia et al., 2011; Ezzat et al., 2011) and laying quails (Ratriyanto et al., 2017). However, we did not observe those effects in this study. It has been reported that BET supplementation could increase pCO2 and HCO3− concentrations in heat-stressed birds by increasing the respiration rate (Park and Kim, 2017). Moreover, HS studies reported that BET supplementation could increase Na+, K+, and Cl– levels to sustain osmosis (Park and Kim, 2017; Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018). The impact of BET on glucose levels is inconsistent (Al-Qaisi et al., 2023). some studies observed decreased glucose levels following BET supplementation (Attia et al., 2016). In this study, no changes in above mentioned parameters were observed when BET was supplemented in the drinking water.

We would expect a drop in the cloacal temperature of laying hens receiving BET as it was hypothesized that the osmoregulatory function of BET could support poultry in maintaining their body temperature (Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018). Al-Hassani and Alhshukri (2016) (Al-Hassani and Alshukri, 2016), found a reduced body temperature if BET was supplemented in drinking water at a similar concentration (0.055% BET) of this study. However, no significant differences were observed in this study.

BET has been shown to increase the activity of antioxidant enzymes in heat-stressed broilers and decreased lipid peroxidation indicated by lower MDA values (Akhavan-Salamat and Ghasemi, 2016). However, while MDA increased during AHS, no treatment effects were observed in this study.

Feed Restriction

Feed restriction (FR) did not influence ADFI, indicating that the hens compensated their feed intake during cooler hours when feed was provided again. FR did not affect shell weight, the shell thickness or internal egg quality which agreed with other studies (Anene et al., 2023; Rama Rao et al., 2023).

FR did not affect blood glucose in this study which agrees with other reports (Garriga et al., 2006). However, we would expect reduced blood glucose because decreased feed intake is accompanied by reduced carbohydrate consumption and an overall lower metabolic rate (Boostani et al., 2010). Although other studies report that hypoglycemia was prevented by gluconeogenesis as a compensatory fuel during fasting (Mohamed et al., 2019; Siddiqui et al., 2021).

Earlier studies demonstrate that feed restriction lowered heat produced by digestion, thus increasing heat tolerance which makes it easier to maintain a physiological body temperature (MacLeod and Hocking, 1993; Abu-Dieyeh, 2006). In this study, treatment and genotype combination results in different responses during AHS with feed restriction decreasing the body temperature only in white hens. Studies reported reduced lipid peroxidation and increased activity of antioxidant enzymes in feed-restricted hens (9–5 pm or 11–5 pm), but we did not observe those effects (Rama Rao et al., 2023).

Reduced feed intake is thought to be the main catalyst of negative effects on laying performance and egg quality (Kirunda et al., 2001). Thus, the initial increase in ADFI may explain why some traits were unaffected or improved during AHS, but deteriorated during CHS. On the other hand, white hens had the highest increase in ADFI during AHS. Still, they had the lowest egg shell quality, which is why it should be noted that laying performance is not only affected by feed intake, but also by a disruption of reproductive hormones during HS (Melesse et al., 2013). It has been reported that birds with heavier body weight are more sensitive to HS (Melesse et al., 2013). However, laying performance and eggshell quality were mostly affected in white hens, despite their lower body weight. The physiology behind thermotolerance is not fully clear and it is likely that heat shock proteins are involved as well (Franco-Jimenez et al., 2007). Besides, it is important to consider the effect of age when comparing genotypes as HS affects young and old hens differently (Mignon-Grasteau et al., 2015).

Overall, we observed little treatment effects. It could be that this heat stress study was too mild. The intensity and duration of heat stress are crucial in the effect size. Moreover, it is implied that cyclic heat stress is generally milder (Mashaly et al., 2004). Although, Emery et al., (Emery et al., 1984) found eggshell weight and thickness to be more reduced in cyclic heat stress with high peak temperatures compared to a constant 23.9°C and studies with similar heat stress as this study had negative effects on internal egg quality (Chen et al., 2023). This study mimics heat waves as expected in a temperate climate and should be able to induce treatment effects. However, the achieved RH was lower than intended which could reduce the induced heat load, as heat stress is the result of temperature and relative humidity combined (Lin et al., 2006b). On the other hand, it could be that our BET concentration was suboptimal and should be higher. Some studies that observed the beneficial effects of betaine report a level of 0.1% or more in broilers (Attia et al., 2011; Akhavan-Salamat and Ghasemi, 2016; Attia et al., 2016) and laying hens (Attia et al., 2016; Al-Qaisi et al., 2023) while we used 0.055% in our study. Lastly, the effect of administration could influence our results. BET supplementation in feed is more conventional, but drinking water could be more efficient. However, application in drinking water could also lead to uneven distribution and water hygiene is crucial to not interfere with the active substances. Microorganisms in water are highly susceptible to environmental conditions e.g., heat stress (Karimi Torshizi et al., 2010). Furthermore, BET was supplied as betaine hydrochloride, but anhydrous betaine was described to be more water soluble and it shows better osmotic capacity (Chen et al., 2022). Lastly, timing of feed restriction is important and some papers suggested that the initiation of feed withdrawal during the forenoon was optimal in hens during the summer season (Rama Rao et al., 2023). Feed restriction did not improve heat stress effects in this study, although it did not negatively affect laying hens either.

CONCLUSION

Our results emphasize the negative effects of heat stress in laying hens, as demonstrated by an increased Tb, decreased laying performance, decreased eggshell quality, and altered blood values. Moreover, the importance of thermotolerance differences between genotypes was highlighted by the observance of lower shell quality and laying performance in white hens. Limited treatment effects were observed which could be related to the heat stress or application of treatments. Further research is needed on the mechanisms behind the physiology of HS differences in different genotypes, optimal timing of feed restriction strategies in laying hens and optimization of BET supplementation in drinking water.
