A voltage trajectory deformation–based fault detectionand ITD–XGBoost classification framework forinverter-dominated transmission networks
Authors: Subhransu Padhee, Sanjeev Kumar, Mahesh K Singh, Sayed Ahmed Imran Bellary, Balram Yelamasetti, Mohammed Zubairuddin, Shahid Tamboli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing popularity of inverter-based resources (IBRs) within the modern power transmission networks presents a major challenge to traditional fault detection and fault classification schemes owing to the minimal contribution by fault currents, being controlled by the inbuilt control scheme, and non-stationary signal response. To overcome these difficulties, this paper will suggest a two-level protection scheme for IBR-concentrated transmission systems. In the first stage, a voltage trajectory deformation-based fault detection index is developed in the stationary reference frame using Clarke transformed voltage signals. The proposed analytical index utilizes the geometric distortion of the voltage trajectories and is independent of the magnitude of fault current, which ensures reliable fault detection in the situation of high fault resistance and weak grid. In the second stage, Intrinsic Time-Scale Decomposition (ITD) is employed to extract fault-relevant transient components from post-fault voltage signals, followed by statistical feature extraction and fault classification using an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier. The proposed methodology is validated on a modified IEEE 14-bus test system using detailed electromagnetic transient simulations in PSCAD. Rigorous simulations are performed under different types of faults, fault location (10% to 90%), fault impedance (0 to 100 Ω), fault-initiation angle (0°–180°), noise levels (up to 40 dB), and IBR operating conditions. The simulation results shows that the proposed scheme detect the faults within a quarter cycle of a voltage signal and the scheme classifies the faults with an overall accuracy of 99.65%. Introduction

The global electricity sector is in the midst of a rapid transition from conventional fossil fuel-based electricity generation to renewable energy sources such as wind and solar, driven by the twin imperatives of either reducing climate change or achieving energy sustainability. Renewable energy technologies have significant advantages such as lower emissions of greenhouse gases, cheaper operation costs, and increased energy security. Recent trends show a stable growth of the share of renewables in the global generation mix with projections of continuing growth throughout the decade. In parallel with the development of renewable generation, the reliable operation of power transmission systems is also critical to ensuring the safe delivery of electricity. Faults such as line-to-ground (LG), line-to-line (LL) and three-phase (LLL) short circuit are inevitable in transmission networks and if not detected and classified in time can cause equipment damage, system instability and widespread outages in the power transmission network. Effective fault detection and classification are the basis of the modern protection schemes to allow the quick isolation of faults without major disruption to the grid1–3.

The growing penetration of inverter-based resources (IBRs) brings new challenges to the traditional fault detecting methods. The power electronic interfaces of IBRs modulate the current and voltage waveforms, and typically introduce harmonics and lower the contribution of the fault current relative to the conventional synchronous generators4,5. This change makes classical fault detection indices, which are usually based on magnitude or waveform shape assumptions that may no longer be valid in the IBR-rich environment, less effective. Accurate fault classification in transmission networks with high IBR penetration is equally challenging. The non-linear behaviour and reduced short-circuit contribution of the power electronic converters may mask the distinguishing characteristics between various fault categories and give rise to misclassification by conventional algorithms6. Many recent studies have been done about the use of machine learning and signal processing technique for improving the transmission line fault classification performance. Several fault detection and classification techniques have been reported in the literature for transmission networks; however, the applicability of these techniques to IBR dominated systems is challenging. Travelling wave-based approaches have been used in7,8 for fast fault detection and classification. Although these approaches provide fast operating speed, they often need very high sampling rates, and precise measurement of reflected and refracted wavefronts. In IBR-rich networks, the weak fault signatures and complex wave propagation characteristics adjacent to the inverter-interfaced buses significantly degrade the reliability of such traveling wave-based schemes. Control-assisted fault detection techniques have been proposed in9,10 where the modifications in inverter control logic are used to make the faults more visible. Despite their efficacy, such methods are highly reliant on access to internal control parameters of the IBRs, which are often proprietary and vendor-specific. This limits their ability to be used in practical protection relays and multi-vendor power systems. Fuzzy logic-based approaches have also been explored for fault detection and classification in11–13. These methods are able to deal with nonlinearity and uncertainty, but the performance is very sensitive to the choice of the membership functions and rule bases. Under different operating conditions and fault scenarios of IBR, the tuning of these parameters becomes more and more complicated, which has an impact on robustness and generalisation of the protection method.

With the recent development of data-driven methods, many research efforts have been carried out using signal processing and machine learning based frameworks for better classification. Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) combined with Support Vector Machines (SVM) has been used in14,15 for fault detection and fault classification. Similarly, DWT based feature extraction with Decision Tree (DT) and K-Nearest Neighbours classifiers (KNN) has been reported respectively in16–18, and19,20. Although DWT gives a good time-frequency localization21–23, its performance is highly dependent on the selection of the mother wavelet and the level of decomposition, which has to be chosen a priori. An unsuitable selection may result in a loss in information and the accuracy of classification.

In order to overcome these drawbacks, adaptive decomposition methods such as Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) and Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (EEMD) have been investigated in24–27. These techniques do not require the predefined basis functions and adaptively break up signals into intrinsic mode functions (IMFs). However, EMD is plagued with mode mixing, end effects, and sensitivity to noise. EEMD although alleviates the mode mixing problem through noise assisted average but creates a residual noise which increases computational burden, and may not have strong mathematical convergence. These drawbacks restrict their use in real-time protective relaying application. In contrast, Intrinsic Time-scale Decomposition (ITD) has become a computationally effective alternative for nonstationary signal analysis28,29. Compared to EMD and EEMD, ITD provides better noise immunity, less mode mixing, better convergence and is more suitable for online implementation, which is attractive for protection-oriented feature extraction in IBR dominated systems.

Despite these improvements, there still have been challenges in achieving consistent performance of existing approaches under various fault conditions, fault impedances, measurement noise, and levels of IBR penetration. To address these gaps, this work proposes a two-stage protection framework where fault detection is accomplished using a voltage trajectory deformation-based analytical index in the stationary reference frame, and then fault classification is accomplished using an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)30,31 classifier, trained based on statistical features extracted from ITD-decomposed voltage signals. Motivation for advanced protection schemes

The above analysis demonstrates that conventional impedance-based fault detection and sequence-based fault classification techniques may not provide reliable performance in IBR-rich transmission systems. The altered fault current contribution, control-dependent dynamics, and non-stationary sequence behavior introduced by inverter-based resources necessitate protection schemes that do not rely solely on magnitude or steady-state phase relationships. These challenges motivate the development of robust fault detection and classification frameworks suitable for modern transmission networks with high IBR penetration, which are addressed in the proposed methodology presented in the following section.

Proposed fault detection and classification technology

trajectory based fault detection

In IBR-rich transmission systems, conventional current-based fault detection schemes face significant challenges due to the limited and controlled fault current contribution of inverters. To overcome this limitation, this work employs voltage signals for fault detection. The three-phase voltage signals measured at the relay location are first transformed into the stationary reference frame using the power-invariant Clarke transformation, which maps the phase voltages into stationary orthogonal components as

The power-invariant formulation is used so that the instantaneous power of the original three-phase system is retained in the plane, and thus appropriate to use in a geometric analysis of the voltage trajectories. When operating under balanced and healthy conditions the transformation of voltage trajectory in the plane is a perfect circle with a constant radius, indicating the symmetry of the three-phase system. In case of a fault, the balance in the voltage of the three phases is broken, causing phase shift and imbalance in amplitude between the phases. Consequently, the circular path in the plane is deformed into an ellipse or irregular curvy-shaped closed path. This geometry deformation is a good predictor of the occurrence of faults even in situations when the magnitude of fault current is small to be detected by conventional protection methods.

ML-based fault classification framework

After fault identification, to appropriately classify faults, discriminative features are extracted from nonstationary voltage signals. In IBR-rich transmission systems, the nature of transients caused by faults is often time-varying frequency content and sudden distortions of waveforms due to inverter control measures. Intrinsic Time-Scale Decomposition (ITD) is used to properly capture such charecteristics, in the form of a data-driven signal decomposition model25,26.

ITD is a signal decomposition method which breaks a signal into a finite number of intrinsic rotation components (IRCs) and a monotonic trend component without using a predefined basis. Because of local extrema, ITD separates a voltage signal, iteratively into a baseline component and a proper rotation component. The result of this process is a representation in the form of

In Eq. (11), denotes the ith intrinsic rotation component capturing oscillatory behaviour at a specific time scale, represents the residual trend, and K is the total number of extracted components.

ITD is particularly well suited for real-time protective relaying due to its high computational efficiency and adaptive signal decomposition capability. Unlike conventional time–frequency methods such as Wavelet Transform (WT), which require predefined basis functions and parameter tuning, ITD is fully data-driven, reducing preprocessing complexity and enhancing suitability for online implementation. Compared to Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) and its variants, ITD mitigates issues such as mode mixing, end effects, and iterative sifting, resulting in faster convergence and lower computational burden. Its decomposition based on local extrema enables efficient extraction of IRCs with minimal delay, making it highly suitable for high-speed protection applications. Furthermore, ITD provides a compact and physically meaningful representation of nonstationary signals, allowing effective isolation of fault-induced transients. The extracted IRCs support robust feature selection by capturing localized variations in signal energy, statistical distribution, and waveform distortion, which are discriminative across fault types and resilient to noise and operating variations. Overall, the low computational complexity and adaptability of ITD enable seamless integration into modern digital relays, ensuring fast and reliable fault classification in inverter-dominated transmission networks.

Hence, ITD is applied to the voltage signals following fault detection to isolate fault-relevant transient components. These characteristics are able to acquire the momentary markers of the various fault types and will not be sensitive to steady operating variations.

Complete protection framework

The proposed protection scheme is based on the two-step fault detection and classification model, the key characteristics of which are summarized in Fig. 5. The proposed technique works on locally measured three-phase voltage signals and is established to work with IBR-rich transmission systems. The first stage converts the measured voltages into the stationary reference frame by using (7). A sliding data window is used to build the voltage trajectory using (8), which gives the covariance matrix. The fault detection index η (as given in Sect. 3.1) is computed in real-time and fault is declared on the line when the condition in (10) is satisfied. When fault is detected, the algorithm shifts to fault classification. A short post-fault data window is chosen in which the features are to be extracted. The voltage signal is decomposed using ITD (as defined in Sect. 3.2), and the statistical properties are calculated with the chosen IRCs to compose the feature vector f. This feature vector is entered into the fault classification module. In addition, the type of the fault is identified with the help of the XGBoost classifier as described in Sect. 3.2. The classifier output along with the fault detection signal is used to take necessary protection measures like tripping or the alarm generation. The fact that the detection and the classification processes are decoupled implies that the proposed scheme can identify the fault very fast and still retain a high level of classification performance.

Results and discussion

Test system and simulation details

The efficiency of the proposed fault detection and classification scheme is verified on a modified IEEE 14 bus test system, by a detailed electromagnetic transient (EMT) model developed in PSCAD, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Single-line diagram of modified IEEE 14-bus test system.

An IBR of 200 MW capacity is connected at bus 13 in the test system. In order to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology in detail, the ten types of shunts faults are simulated on line 13 − 6, including three LG faults (AG, BG, CG), three LL faults (AB, BC, CA), three LLG faults (ABG, BCG, CAG), and one LLL fault (ABC). Faults are applied at different locations along the transmission line, along with the variation in the fault distance from 10% to 90% of the total line length.

To validate the robustness under extreme conditions, the fault impedance is also varied between 0 Ω and 100 Ω, thus examining both low and high-impedance fault (HIF) cases. High impedance faults are modeled in PSCAD by introducing a fault resistance in series with the fault path. The resistance is varied from 0 Ω to 100 Ω to emulate practical high-impedance fault conditions such as arcing or vegetation contact. The angle of fault inception is also varied from 0° to 180° in 10-step, to consider the varying conditions of voltage waveform initiation. Moreover, the proposed scheme is also investigated under noisy conditions by adding noise of up to 40 dB power to the voltage signals under fault conditions. In all the simulation cases, the faults are triggered at t = 1 s, ensuring steady-state operation prior to fault occurrence. The threshold value of ηth = 1.5 is chosen empirically after considering extensive simulations. However, variations in IBR penetration levels or changes in network topology may require recalibration of the detection threshold ηth. In such cases, an appropriate threshold can be determined through offline simulations under extreme operating conditions, such as maximum fault distance (90%), high-impedance faults (100 Ω), and high noise levels (40 dB), to ensure reliable performance across the network. The selected threshold ensures maximum separation between faulty and healthy states across all operating conditions. Total 5400 fault cases are simulated, out of which 80% cases are used for training and the rest 20% are used in testing the fault classifier performance. Figure 8(b) summarizes the classification performance under various noise levels. It is noted that the classification accuracy declines steadily from 99.64% under noiseless conditions to 95.21% at a noise level of 40 dB. This is especially applicable to real-world implementation, where measurement noise is inevitable in voltage sensing devices. In spite of the noise intensity, the proposed methodology ensures a reliable level of detection and classification, demonstrating its robustness under adverse fault and measurement conditions.

It is important to note that the considered high-impedance fault (100 Ω) and noise level (40 dB) represent extreme operating conditions for a typical IBR-dominated system. Beyond these limits, the performance of the proposed method degrades significantly, leading to unsatisfactory results. Under very high fault impedance conditions, the variation in voltage magnitude becomes minimal, resulting in negligible deformation in the α–β voltage trajectory. Similarly, noise levels exceeding 40 dB cause a substantial reduction in classification accuracy, dropping to approximately 86.31%. Such performance is not adequate for reliable relay operation, thereby indicating a practical limitation of the proposed methodology under extreme conditions.

Conclusion

This paper presented a robust and efficient fault detection and classification framework for the IBR dominated transmission networks. The proposed methodology deals with the challenges of the traditional protection schemes that appear due to decreased fault current contribution, control-dependent inverter dynamics, and non-stationary signal properties. An analytical index based on voltage trajectory deformation was created in the stationary α–β reference frame to provide reliable and fast fault detection regardless of fault current value. The geometric nature of the proposed index enables effective fault detection even under high-impedance fault and weak grid conditions.

After fault identification, an ITD-based feature extraction model was used together with an XGBoost classifier to classify faults. Intrinsic Time-Scale Decomposition is used to obtain adaptive and noise-resistant extraction of fault-relevant transient features without defined basis functions. The XGBoost classifier demonstrated strong generalization capability and robustness against noise and operating condition variations. The proposed methodology is validated on a modified IEEE 14-bus test system using detailed EMT simulations in PSCAD. The effectiveness of the proposed framework was verified by extensive performance assessment under different fault types, locations, fault impedances up to 100 Ω, fault inception angles and noise levels as high as 40 dB. The scheme was able to identify faults in a quarter cycle of the voltage waveform and has an overall classification accuracy of 99.65%. The classification accuracy of the system was over 95% even in high noisy conditions, indicating strong robustness and practical suitability.

In general, the proposed protection framework provides an effective, quick, and computationally efficient method of fault detection and classification in modern IBR-dominated transmission networks. Future work will focus on real-time hardware implementation and experimental validation based on hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) platforms to further evaluate practical implementation in digital protective relays.
