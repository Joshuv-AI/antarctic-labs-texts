Corneal and conjunctival abnormalities in a cohort of survivors from the Western African Ebola virus disease outbreak☆,☆☆
Authors: Tolulope Fashina, Nam V. Nguyen, Ye Huang, Casey Randleman, Caleb D. Hartley, John G. Mattia, Matthew J. Vandy, Lloyd C. Harrison-Williams, Jalikatu Mustapha, Jean-Claude Mwanza, Brent R. Hayek, Ian Crozier, Jessica G. Shantha, Steven Yeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To characterize corneal and conjunctival abnormalities (CCAs) and their impact on visual acuity in a cohort of survivors from the Western African Ebola virus disease (EVD) outbreak.Design:A post hoc analysis of 121 patients, who had previously undergone screening for the Ebola Virus Persistence in Ocular Tissues and Fluids study, was performed.Methods:Patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic exam at the Lowell and Ruth Gess Eye Hospital in Freetown, Sierra Leone. Demographic (including age, sex, and the number of days spent in the Ebola treatment unit and examination (such as visual acuity, and ocular complications) data were collected and used for the analysis. Visual acuity was either uncorrected or corrected with spectacles. Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:A total of 242 eyes (121 patients) were included in this study. The mean age was 31.5 ± 16.1 years, and 70 % were female. Of 242 eyes, 28 (11.5 %) eyes of 15 patients (12.4 %) presented with at least one or more corneal or conjunctival abnormalities. Within eyes that demonstrated CCAs, the findings included dry eye (28.6 % of eyes), band keratopathy (25 % of eyes), and corneal scar (21.4 % of eyes). Corneal edema was found in 14.3 % of eyes, while pterygium and epithelial bullae were each found in 7.1 % of affected eyes. Patients without CCAs had better visual acuity compared to patients with CCAs although the difference was not statistically significant (0.82 ± 1.17 vs 1.01 ± 1.29, p = 0.4). A subset of patients with CCAs also reported ocular symptoms including eye pain, light sensitivity, excessive tearing, and blurry vision.Conclusion:In this post hoc analysis, we observed CCAs in 12 % of EVD survivors. These findings highlight the potential for corneal and conjunctival sequelae during EVD survivorship and the need for ongoing ophthalmic care for this vulnerable population.

Introduction

As reported by the World Health Organization (WHO), the Western African outbreak of Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) led to approximately 28,600 cases and 11,300 deaths between December 2013 and March 2016.1,2 While the outbreak spanned six Western African countries and multiple nations globally, Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone were most impacted. This outbreak stands as the largest recorded outbreak of EVD to date, impacting both males and females in nearly equal distribution across all age groups.3

Uveitis, the most common ocular sequelae of EVD, has been reported in 13–34 % of EVD survivors, with phenotypes ranging from anterior uveitis to panuveitis.4–8 Among 341 EVD survivors from Guinea, uveitis was identified in 46 cases (13.5 %), predominantly manifesting as unilateral (78.3 %), and anterior (47.8 %) uveitis, and typically occurring within 2 months after discharge from the Ebola treatment center.4 In another cohort of 96 EVD survivors from Liberia, uveitis was reported in 21 (22 %) cases, predominantly manifesting as posterior (57.7 %) and panuveitis (26.9 %).5 Similarly, in a prospective Liberian cohort of 966 EVD survivors and a control group of 2,350 close contacts, uveitis was identified in 26.4 % of EVD survivors; notably, prevalence in this group further increased to 33.3 % at 1-year follow-up.9

Although corneal and conjunctival abnormalities (CCAs) including conjunctival injection, subconjunctival hemorrhage, hyperlacrimation, corneal scarring, and vernal keratoconjunctivitis, have been reported in EVD survivors, our understanding of the prevalence and character of CCAs in this population is still limited.4,8,10–12 In a cohort of 23 pediatric survivors, vernal keratoconjunctivitis was identified in 10 eyes (21·7 %), and corneal scarring in 5 eyes (10·9 %); significant conjunctival injection with Horner-Trantas dots and inflammatory clusters encircling the cornea were noted in patients with vernal keratoconjunctivitis.12 Given the risk of visual complications and limited existing data, we aimed to characterize the spectrum of CCAs and their impact on visual acuity in a cohort of adult survivors from the Western African EVD outbreak from 2013–2016.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) approvals were obtained from Emory University, the Office of Ethics and Scientific Review Committee, and the Sierra Leone Ministry of Health and Sanitation (MOHS). The purpose of this post-hoc analysis was to examine the spectrum of CCAs and their consequences in a cohort of EVD survivors from the Western African EVD outbreak. Available data from patients who underwent screening for the prospective Ebola Virus Persistence in Ocular Tissues and Fluids (EVICT) study were used for this post-hoc analysis.13 The primary goals of the EVICT study were to evaluate the persistence of EBOV RNA in ocular tissues and fluids and to determine the feasibility, safety, and efficacy outcomes of cataract surgery in EVD survivors.13 Patients who were anticipated to benefit from ophthalmic procedures (primarily manual small incision cataract surgery) or those who had active uveitis at the time of the screening, were enrolled in the EVICT study.13

Patient population and demographic characteristics

EVD survivors who underwent screening for the EVICT study from March 2015 to March 2016 were included in this post-hoc analysis. Survivors were identified via an ophthalmic screening program conducted by the Ministry of Health of Sierra Leone (MOHS) Eye Care Program and the Sierra Leone Association of Ebola Survivors or via referrals from local eye clinics for vision loss and cataract evaluation. Demographic data including age, sex, and days spent in the Ebola treatment unit (ETU) was collected and analyzed. Patients who were enrolled in the EVICT study underwent further assessment of eye symptoms including eye redness, eye pain, light sensitivity, blurry vision, and excessive tearing.

Ophthalmic examination and ocular surface abnormalities

Comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including visual acuity, slit-lamp examination, and funduscopic examination, was conducted at the Lowell and Ruth Gess Eye Hospital in Freetown, Sierra Leone. Snellen visual acuity (VA) was either uncorrected or corrected with spectacles. CCAs including band keratopathy, corneal scar, corneal edema, pterygium, epithelial bullae, and dry eye, were also recorded for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to evaluate CCAs in the EVICT study cohort. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and/or frequencies, while continuous variables were represented as means and standard deviations. VA was converted to LogMAR for statistical analysis. An unpaired t-test was employed to compare the degree of visual impairment, as measured by VA, between groups with and without CCAs. Unpaired t-test was performed to whether there was a difference in the length of stay in the Ebola treatment unit (ETU) between the CCAs group and the non-CCAs group. As we did not have access to details of disease severity or viral burden of patients within the ETU (i.e., real-time, reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction [RT-PCR] cycling threshold for Ebola virus RNA), the length of stay within an ETU was utilized as a surrogate marker for the severity of EVD during the acute illness. The analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05 for all comparisons.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 242 eyes of 121 patients were included in this analysis with the mean age of 31.5 ± 16.1 years. The study cohort was demographically diverse, 70.0 % of patients were female and 27.9 % of patients were children. The mean duration of ETU stay was 25.6 ± 18.2 days, and the mean Snellen VA was 20/140 (i.e., Mean logMAR 0.84 ± 1.2). Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Corneal and conjunctival abnormalities and ocular symptoms

Among 242 eyes in the study, a total of 28 (11.6 %) eyes from 15 (11.5 %) patients exhibited one or more corneal or conjunctival abnormalities. Of the 28 eyes identified with CCAs, specific diagnoses included dry eye (n = 8 eyes, 28.6 %), band keratopathy (n = 7 eyes, 25.0 %), and corneal scar (n = 6 eyes, 21.4 %). Corneal edema was observed in 4 eyes (14.3 %) while pterygium and epithelial bullae each manifested in 2 eyes (7.1 %). The detailed CCAs are summarized in Table 1, and the study summary is illustrated in Supplemental Figure 1.

A total of 49 (98 eyes) patients were enrolled in the EVICT study and underwent further assessment of ocular symptoms via additional ocular symptom questionnaires at the time of examination and enrollment. Of note, symptom data are unavailable from patients who were not enrolled in the EVICT study and were not specifically evaluated for ocular symptoms. Of the patients who underwent symptom assessment, ocular symptoms included eye pain (42.9 % of patients), eye redness (36.7 % of patients), light sensitivity (26.5 % of patients), excessive tearing (20.4 % of patients), and blurred vision (8.2 % of patients).

Among these 49 patients, 10 patients (20.4 %) were found to have CCAs at the time of examination. Of these 10 patients, 5 patients reported eye redness, 5 patients reported eye pain, 4 patients reported excessive tearing, 3 patients reported light sensitivity, and 2 patients reported blurry vision. One of these patients reported eye pain, light sensitivity, excessive tearing, and blurry vision at the time of examination. These findings indicate a range of ocular symptoms that may be associated with CCAs in this cohort of EVD survivors. The detailed CCAs and their associated ocular symptoms are summarized in Table 2.

Visual acuity impact of corneal and conjunctival abnormalities

The mean age was 26.1 ± 15.8 years and 32.4 ± 16.1 years in the CCAs and non-CCAs groups, respectively (p-value = 0.1). Both groups showed a female predominance, as 80 % of patients were female in the CCAs group, and 68 % of patients were female in the non-CCAs group. The mean LogMAR was 1.01 ± 1.29 (Snellen VA of 20/204) and 0.82 ± 1.17 (Snellen VA of 20/132) in the CCAs and non-CCAs groups, respectively. Although eyes with CCAs had worse VA compared to those without CCAs, the difference was not statistically significant (p-value = 0.4). The length of stay in the ETU (used as a surrogate marker for the severity of EVD in our cohort), was not significantly different between the groups (20.75 ± 12.90 vs 26.38 ± 19.06, p-value = 0.4).

Discussion

The 2013–2016 Western African EVD outbreak had a profound health and socio-economic impact on affected populations,14 including new awareness of systemic and ophthalmic sequelae in EVD survivors that prompted clinical research investigation during and after the outbreak. While uveitis has been identified as the most common ocular complication in survivors, CCAs including episcleritis, keratitis, and vernal keratoconjunctivitis were also reported.4,8,10–12 In this post-hoc analysis, we assessed a subset of patients from the EVICT study cohort in whom OSAs were identified. Of 242 eyes of 121 patients in the study, one or more CCAs were noted in 28 (11.6 %) eyes, including dry eye (the most common finding), band keratopathy, and corneal scar. At the time of examination, these patients also subjectively described ocular symptoms, including eye redness, eye pain, light sensitivity, blurry vision, and excessive tearing. Of note, a range of ocular symptoms was observed in the cohort, likely related to the high general burden of ocular comorbidities in this population including conditions such as uveitis, and cataract. Our results highlight the diversity of ophthalmic disease in the post-EVD setting and inform the need for ongoing ophthalmic care that includes attention to corneal and conjunctival disease in this high-risk population.

In our previous study evaluating the ophthalmic sequelae and psychosocial impact in pediatric EVD survivors and their close contacts, 23 pediatric EVD survivors and 50 close contacts (with Ebola virus IgG seronegative status) were evaluated, and ophthalmic manifestations were observed in 47.8 % and 31.9 % of the EVD survivor and close contact groups, respectively.12 Interestingly, vernal conjunctivitis was found in 21.7 % of pediatric EVD survivors compared to 9.4 % of close contacts.12 Moreover, corneal scarring was noted in 5 eyes among 46 survivors eyes, including one eye with poor vision (hand motion, light perception, or no light perception) at the time of examination.12 Herbert et al reported ophthalmic complications in 78 EVD survivors of a total of 314 survivors, including 3 patients with interstitial keratitis.4 Besides those noted in EVD survivors, CCAs have also been historically reported during the acute stage of EVD.10 Of 103 EVD patients reported in Bwaka et al, 89 patients showed signs of conjunctival injection during acute EVD, and 2 (of 19) EVD survivors were later found to have conjunctivitis.10 Of 6 eyes with scars in our post-hoc analysis, 4 eyes demonstrated VA of counting fingers or worse. Of 7 eyes with band keratopathy, 4 eyes demonstrated VA of hand motion or worse. Although these patients were also found to have concurrent mild to moderate cataracts at the time of examination, it is certainly possible that the CCAs contributed significantly to their poor visual acuity. Furthermore, a portion of patients with CCAs reported rare associations with ocular symptoms such as eye redness, eye pain, light sensitivity, blurry vision, and excessive tearing. As compared to patients without CCAs, patients with CCAs did not have statistically worse visual acuity in our study; however, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to characterize the true impact of CCAs on visual acuity and other outcomes typically more relevant to corneal and conjunctival disease (e.g. pain, irritation, photosensitivity, or excessive tearing).

While the pathogenesis of CCAs in EVD survivors is the subject of ongoing investigation, ocular inflammation due to viral persistence or delayed antigen clearance in the immune-privileged eye could potentially contribute to the development of some CCAs in these patients, particularly findings such as band keratopathy.15–17 The report of Ebola virus persistence in the aqueous humor of an EVD survivor with panuveitis,15 as well as the persistence of EBOV RNA18 or EBOV viral antigen19 in the eyes of non-human primate survivors, query a role for persistent virus (or virus RNA or antigen) in driving ocular immunopathology that may lead to band keratopathy, particularly in patients with chronic inflammation due to untreated disease and who are unable to access ophthalmic care. Interestingly, EBOV replication has been demonstrated in ex vivo cultures of different ocular tissues of cynomolgus macaque eyes including cornea, anterior sclera with bulbar conjunctiva, ciliary body, iris, lens, neural retina, and retina pigment epithelium.20 Of note, the histopathologic study of a rhesus monkey infected with Ebola virus also demonstrated significant infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages in the conjunctival subepithelial connective tissue.17 EBOV has been detected in the tear film during acute EVD, plausibly contributing to the development of CCAs given the common descriptions of conjunctivitis and subconjunctival hemorrhage during acute EVD.11,21,22 Further studies are needed to explore the pathogenesis of CCAs in this population.

Our descriptive study has several limitations, including the retrospective, post-hoc nature of the study, small sample size, and the absence of a control group. OSAs were not pre-specified variables collected in the EVICT study; as such, discrete observations are subject to detection bias and may not be reflective of targeted CCAs questionnaire and evaluation of all EVICT enrollees. Our cohort was also more broadly subject to selection bias as patients were referred for enrollment into the EVICT study only if they had vision impairment or ocular disease that potentially would benefit from cataract surgery. The study was conducted in a resource-limited setting with minimal access to ophthalmic subspecialty services, and some of these CCAs could have been present before the onset of EVD. Thus, the level of visual acuity impairment from untreated disease would likely be worse than populations of patients with access to specialists in corneal and conjunctival disease. Moreover, the high burden of uveitis in this population, as evidenced by prior studies in Western Africa, may also have contributed to the high rates of specific observed CCAs, such as band keratopathy.

However, in this group of EVD survivors screened for the EVICT study, in whom ocular findings of uveitis, cataract, and chorioretinal scarring have been reported previously, our post-hoc analysis of a subset of patients revealed additional CCAs. Although the precise prevalence of CCAs and any related impact on visual acuity needs further investigation, our findings highlight CCAs as part of the breadth and burden of ophthalmic disease and complications in EVD survivors. These findings argue the need for ongoing ophthalmic care that includes anterior segment evaluation in this vulnerable population who are also susceptible to a range of systemic post-acute sequelae of EVD. Further studies to understand the prevalence, consequences, underlying pathogenesis, and optimal management strategies for corneal and conjunctival disease in EVD survivors are needed.
