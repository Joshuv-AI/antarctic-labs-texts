Efficacy and Safety of Ecopipam for Tourette Syndrome
Authors: Donald L. Gilbert, Sarah D. Atkinson, David J. B. Kim, Meredith M. Miller, Patricia M. Rice, John A. Flatt, George B. Karkanias, Frederick E. Munschauer, Richard M. Bittman, Stephen P. Wanaski, Timothy M. Cunniff, Kinga K. Tomczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This randomized clinical trial evaluates the safety and maintenance of effect of ecopipam, a selective dopamine D1 receptor antagonist, for up to 24 weeks for children, adolescents, and adults with Tourette syndrome.

Introduction

Tourette syndrome (TS) is a neurodevelopmental disorder with symptom onset typically between ages 3 and 8 years1 and is characterized by motor and phonic tics.1 Peak tic severity commonly occurs between ages 8 and 12 years, although the individual clinical course varies,1,2 and tics may wax and wane in frequency across a day or week. Tourette syndrome is associated with impaired quality of life3 and functioning4 and increased risk of suicidality5 and all-cause mortality.6 Behavioral therapies are recommended as first-line treatment. When those are unavailable, ineffective, or not preferred, pharmacologic treatment with α-2 adrenergic receptor agonists or dopamine D2 receptor modulators is recommended7,8; however, these pharmacotherapies are limited by potential adverse events (AEs).9

Ecopipam is a first-in-class selective dopamine D1 receptor (D1R) antagonist under investigation for treatment of TS. A randomized, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial previously established the treatment effect of ecopipam, 1.8 mg/kg per day.10 Symptom improvements were also observed during 12 months of ecopipam treatment in the open-label extension trial.11 Both trials10,11 showed limited occurrence of multisystem complications commonly seen with current pharmacotherapies. To establish the long-term effectiveness of ecopipam, a randomized withdrawal trial design was selected for ethical and methodologic reasons, including minimizing the duration of placebo exposure for symptomatic individuals and reducing the overall sample size to demonstrate treatment effect maintenance.12 This phase 3, randomized withdrawal trial evaluated the safety, tolerability, and maintenance of effect of ecopipam treatment for up to 24 weeks in children, adolescents, and adults with TS.

Methods

Study Design

This phase 3 trial included a 12-week open-label period followed by a 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized withdrawal period. The design was modeled on a prior trial (aripiprazole for TS; NCT03661983) conducted as part of a regulatory postapproval commitment. Participants were enrolled at 77 sites in the United States, Bulgaria, Canada, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Poland, Romania, Serbia, and Spain between January 31, 2023, and February 4, 2025. The protocol, including age-based informed assent and consent forms, received written institutional review board approval. Participating sites obtained approval from site-specific independent ethics committees or institutional review boards (see Supplement 1 and Supplement 2 for the protocol and statistical analysis plan, respectively, and the eAppendix in Supplement 3 for the investigators list).

The study was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. For participants younger than 18 years at baseline, parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent and participants provided written assent. Participants 18 years and older provided written informed consent.

Participants

Eligible for inclusion were participants aged 6 years and older weighing 18 kg or more, with a TS diagnosis (according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision [DSM-5-TR] criteria), motor and vocal tics causing impairment with normal routines, and Yale Global Tic Severity Scale Total Tic Score (YGTSS-TTS) 20 or greater at screening and baseline visits (see eTable 1 in Supplement 3 for detailed eligibility criteria). Exclusion criteria included unstable mood disorder, according to DSM-5-TR criteria; unstable medical illness; significant suicide risk; history of other neurological conditions associated with abnormal movements; history of bipolar disorder, dementia, schizophrenia, or another psychotic disorder; or medications that would have unfavorable interactions with ecopipam. Stable doses of concomitant medications for treatment of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety, depression, or obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), including stimulants and some antidepressants, were permitted.

Randomization and Masking

For the double-blind withdrawal period, participants were randomized 1:1, using a web-based system and stratified by body weight, to receive oral ecopipam 1.8 mg/kg per day (active moiety, administered as ecopipam hydrochloride tablets 2 mg/kg per day) or placebo. Participants, caregivers, investigators, and study staff were masked to group assignment.

Procedures

During the 12-week open-label period, ecopipam was up-titrated over 3 to 4 weeks to a target dose of 1.8 mg/kg per day, using baseline weight categories (eTable 2 in Supplement 3), then maintained during the open-label period at the weight-based target dose (eFigure 1 in Supplement 3). Participants with at least a 25% decrease (improvement) in YGTSS-TTS from baseline at both weeks 8 and 12 were randomized 1:1 to continue taking ecopipam 1.8 mg/kg per day or taper to placebo during a 12-week double-blind, randomized withdrawal period. This response criterion was based on previous work showing that 25% improvement in YGTSS-TTS represented clinically meaningful improvement.13 Participants who did not meet responder criteria at weeks 8 and 12 discontinued the trial. Upon discontinuation, ecopipam was tapered by 22.4 mg/d over 2 to 8 days. Participants receiving placebo were tapered off placebo to preserve masking. Study participation ended when 49 pediatric relapse events occurred.

Clinic visits were conducted at baseline and weeks 4, 8, and 12 during the open-label period (±3 days); weeks 13 through 16, 18, 20, 22, and 24 (±3 days) during the randomized withdrawal period; and 7 and 14 (±3) days after the last dose of study drug. Telephone visits occurred at week 2 (±3 days) and at 30 (±3) days after the last study drug dose. Efficacy assessments included the YGTSS-TTS, Yale Global Tic Severity Scale–Impairment (YGTSS-I), Yale Global Tic Severity Scale–Global Score (YGTSS-GS), Clinical Global Impression of Tourette Syndrome–Severity (CGI-TS-S), Clinical Global Impression of Tourette Syndrome–Improvement (CGI-TS-I), Caregiver Global Impression of Change (CaGI-C; aged <18 years), and Premonitory Urge for Tics Scale (PUTS). YGTSS-TTS, YGTSS-I, YGTSS-GS, and CGI-TS-S were assessed at baseline, each on-treatment visit, and the first safety follow-up visit (except CGI-TS-S). CGI-TS-I was evaluated at each on-treatment visit except baseline and week 8. CaGI-C and PUTS were administered at baseline (PUTS only) and weeks 4, 12, 18, and 24.

Vital signs were collected, and physical (including neurologic) examinations were conducted at each visit. Electrocardiograms (ECGs) and safety laboratory tests were conducted at baseline; at weeks 4 (ECG only), 12, 18, and 24; and during safety follow-up visits. ECGs were interpreted by a board-certified pediatric or adult cardiologist. Adverse events were recorded during each visit and coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (version 27.0); participants were counted once for multiple incidents of same AE. A masked clinical adjudication committee determined whether prespecified neurologic AEs of special interest were related to the potential emergence of drug-induced movement disorders (eMethods in Supplement 3). Additional safety assessments included the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS; administered at all visits except the 30-day follow-up telephone visit) and administration of the Children’s Depression Rating Scale–Revised (CDRS-R); Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9); Pediatric Anxiety Rating Scale (PARS); Hamilton Rating Scale for Anxiety (HAM-A); Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham Scale, version IV (SNAP-IV-26); Conners Adult ADHD Rating Scale-Self Report, Short Version (CAARS-S:S); Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale II (Y-BOCS-II); Children’s Y-BOCS-II (CY-BOCS-II); and Extrapyramidal Symptom Rating Scale (ESRS; all administered at baseline and weeks 4, 8, 12, 13-16, 18 [ESRS only], 20, 22 [ESRS only], and 24).

Outcomes

The primary end point was time from randomization (week 12) to relapse for pediatric participants (aged 6-18 years at the time of consent/assent). The secondary end point was time from randomization to relapse for participants in the overall population (aged ≥6 years) receiving ecopipam vs placebo. Relapse was defined as loss of 50% or more of the YGTSS-TTS improvement previously experienced from baseline to week 12, initiation of additional medications to treat TS symptoms, or hospitalization due to worsening TS symptoms. All other end points were exploratory because of competing risks and included change from randomization to end of treatment with ecopipam vs placebo.

Statistical Analysis

The planned number of pediatric relapse events to provide 85% power to detect a difference between treatment groups (2-sided α = .05) was 49, assuming relapse rates of 65% (placebo group) and 34% (ecopipam group) and a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.40 for relapse, based on modeling of phase 2 pediatric data.10 A sample size of 98 randomized pediatric participants was estimated as sufficient for 49 relapse events to occur. Researchers planned to enroll approximately 196 pediatric participants in the open-label period to obtain 98 randomized; enrollment of 40 adults was also planned. The modified intent-to-treat population was selected for efficacy analyses and included all participants who received at least 1 dose of study drug postrandomization. The safety population included all participants who received at least 1 dose of study drug. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute). A preplanned interim analysis (requiring benefit of ecopipam over placebo and log-rank P < .005 to make a recommendation for early stopping) was conducted by an independent, unmasked data safety and monitoring board after 34 pediatric relapse events occurred. Following the board’s recommendation, the study continued without changes to sample size or study conduct.

Primary and secondary end points were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. Hazard ratios and 95% CIs were estimated from Cox proportional hazard models, with treatment as a factor. For exploratory efficacy analyses, mean changes from randomization to end of treatment were analyzed using a mixed model for repeated measures analysis of covariance, with an unstructured covariance matrix that included treatment, visit, treatment × visit interaction, age group (children [aged 6-11 years]; adolescents [aged 12-17 years]; adults [aged ≥18 years]), and region (North America or Europe) as fixed effects and score at time of randomization as a covariate. Safety outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics.

Results

Of 294 screened individuals, 216 were enrolled (pediatric [n = 167]; adult [n = 49]) and received open-label ecopipam (Figure 1 and eFigure 2 in Supplement 3). Seventy participants (32.4%) were female and 146 (67.6%) were male. Of 216 enrolled participants, 126 (58.3%) continued treatment through week 12, and 104 (48.1%) (pediatric [n = 90]; adult [n = 14]) were randomized to the double-blind treatment period. Reasons for discontinuation during the open-label period included AEs (n = 33 [15.3%]), administrative termination of study participation when 49 pediatric relapse events had occurred (n = 21 [9.7%]), withdrawal of consent by parent/caregiver (n = 6 [2.8%]), lost to follow-up (n = 3 [1.4%]), and other (n = 9 [4.2%]). Remaining discontinuations were due to lack of efficacy/inadequate therapeutic effect (n = 40 [18.5%]), with most of these not meeting responder criteria to continue in the study.

For the primary end point, there was, in the pediatric population, a significant reduction in the risk of relapse with ecopipam vs placebo (HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.26-0.84; P = .008) (Figure 2A). Relapses occurred in 32 pediatric participants (68.1%) in the placebo group and 18 (41.9%) in the ecopipam group. Median time from randomization to relapse was 4.0 weeks (95% CI, 2.4-6.1 weeks) in the placebo group and was not estimable (4.1 weeks–not estimable) for ecopipam because more than 50% of participants maintained improvement at week 24. For the secondary end point (all ages), there was also a significant effect of ecopipam (HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.28-0.81; P = .005) (Figure 2B). Relapses occurred in 36 participants aged 6 years and older (67.9%) treated with placebo and 21 (41.2%) treated with ecopipam. Median time to relapse was 4.0 weeks (95% CI, 2.4-6.1 weeks) for placebo and not estimable (4.1 weeks–not estimable) for ecopipam. For the exploratory end point (adults-only), the risk of relapse with ecopipam vs placebo was directionally similar (HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.11-2.30; P = .37) (eTable 3 in Supplement 3). Relapses occurred in 4 adults (66.7%) in the placebo group and 3 (37.5%) in the ecopipam group. Median time from randomization to relapse was 3.6 weeks (95% CI, 1.3 weeks–not estimable) in the placebo group and was not estimable (1.1 weeks–not estimable) for ecopipam.

Overall, 147 of 216 participants (68.1%) reported an AE during ecopipam treatment (open-label and double-blind periods); most AEs were mild or moderate in intensity (Table 3). The most frequently reported AEs during ecopipam treatment were somnolence (n = 24 [11.1%]), anxiety (n = 21 [9.7%]), headache (n = 21 [9.7%]), insomnia (n = 19 [8.8%]), tic (n = 17 [7.9%]), and fatigue (n = 14 [6.5%]). Over the study duration, no notable changes in vital signs, physical examination, laboratory values (including values for prolactin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and glycated hemoglobin), or ECG measurements were observed, and body mass index z scores remained stable.

Serious AEs were reported in 4 participants, of whom 2 had events considered possibly or probably related to treatment. One was an adolescent with a history of OCD and autism spectrum disorder who experienced an episode of affective and behavioral dysregulation requiring hospitalization while receiving ecopipam. Laboratory results showed mild acute kidney injury and moderate elevated blood creatine phosphokinase concomitant with dehydration. The second was an adolescent with a previous aborted suicide attempt 13 months before screening who expressed suicidal ideation without behavior (associated with escalating psychosocial stressors during double-blind placebo treatment), although they had tapered off ecopipam during the week before the event.

Among 34 participants (15.7%) who discontinued ecopipam because of an AE, the most frequently reported (≥3 participants) were tic (n = 7 [3.2%]), anxiety (n = 6 [2.8%]), somnolence (n = 6 [2.8%]), depressed mood (n = 3 [1.4%]), depression (n = 3 [1.4%]), insomnia (n = 3 [1.4%]), and suicidal ideation (n = 3 [1.4%]). Tremor and dystonia occurred in 1 participant each during the open-label period; both were adjudicated as unrelated to treatment. A dystonia event occurred after haloperidol administration for acute agitation associated with OCD.

Among 216 enrollees, 16 (7.4%) had a lifetime history of suicidal ideation, and 3 (1.4%) had a lifetime history of suicidal behavior, per C-SSRS at screening (eTable 4 in Supplement 3). The percentage of participants with suicidal ideation was 2.1% or less throughout the open-label period. There were no reports of suicidal ideation in the ecopipam arm during the randomized withdrawal period. One placebo-treated participant (1.9%) had suicidal ideation (C-SSRS item 1 “yes”) at the 7-day follow-up visit (16 days after randomization). No suicidal behavior occurred during the trial. Mean values over time for scale measures of neuropsychiatric comorbidities showed no evidence for worsening during ecopipam treatment for depression (CDRS-R, PHQ-9), anxiety (PARS, HAM-A), ADHD (SNAP-IV-26, CAARS-S:S), or OCD (CY-BOCS-II, Y-BOCS-II). During the randomized withdrawal period, scores were generally stable or slightly improved in the ecopipam group and generally stable or transiently worsened in the placebo group. No increase in drug-induced movement disorders (based on ESRS) was observed.

Discussion

This phase 3 trial of ecopipam demonstrates maintenance of clinically meaningful tic suppression for up to 24 weeks in pediatric participants. Maintenance of efficacy during the 12-week randomized withdrawal period was determined in participants with clinically meaningful (≥25%13) YGTSS-TTS improvement during the 12-week open-label period. The responder criterion of 25% or greater was a secondary end point in the phase 2b trial10 and was subsequently correlated with meaningful anchors (CGI-TS-S and CGI-TS-I).14 Both the pediatric (primary end point) and combined pediatric and adult (secondary end point) randomized populations had a 50% lower risk of relapse with ecopipam vs placebo. Although not powered for adults (exploratory end point), the trend toward improvement is supportive of further study.

Improvements from randomization to end of treatment were observed with ecopipam vs placebo for multiple exploratory efficacy measures. Adverse events were predominantly associated with the central nervous system, as in previous trials,10,11,15,16,17 with the most frequently reported AEs during ecopipam treatment being somnolence, anxiety, headache, insomnia, tic, and fatigue. Suicidal ideation AEs were reported in 5 participants (2.3%) during the open-label period and 1 participant (1.9%) in the placebo group during the randomized withdrawal period. The safety profile of ecopipam is notable for the absence of clinically relevant weight gain, adverse metabolic effects, and drug-induced movement disorders.10,11,15,16,17

An α-2 adrenergic agonist (eg, clonidine, guanfacine), followed by topiramate, is a common approach for initiating pharmacotherapy for tics,7,8 but in patients refractory to these therapies, clinicians may prescribe second-generation (eg, risperidone, aripiprazole) or first-generation (eg, pimozide, haloperidol) antipsychotics.7,8 Antipsychotics, currently the only approved medications for TS, are efficacious, but the risk of weight gain, adverse metabolic changes, and drug-induced movement disorders is a barrier to treatment.9 In this trial, ecopipam for up to 24 weeks was not associated with clinically meaningful changes in body mass index z scores, glycated hemoglobin, lipids, ECG measurements, or prolactin and did not cause drug-induced movement disorders. Additionally, ecopipam had no negative impact on scale measures for ADHD, anxiety, depression, and OCD.

Strengths and Limitations

Study strengths include the multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized assessment of maintenance of efficacy and use of validated clinician- and participant/caregiver-rated outcome assessments. Initial open-label treatment may increase participant/caregiver willingness to enroll. Subsequent responder-only randomization enriches the population, reducing the necessary sample size. Study limitations include minimal racial and ethnic diversity, study criterion (49 pediatric relapse events) occurring before all participants exited the open-label period, safety assessment limited to 24 weeks, low statistical power for the adult subgroup (analysis not prespecified), open-label responder assessment based on predefined operational criteria, and the small number of adults included. Participants who responded to ecopipam and were randomly assigned to receive placebo were required to taper their ecopipam dose in a masked fashion; this may have favorably impacted time to relapse of the placebo group. The randomized withdrawal period assessed maintenance of efficacy only among ecopipam responders; thus, generalizability across all individuals seeking TS treatment may be limited. That said, any randomized clinical trial in TS will likely have challenges in recruiting individuals broadly representative of those seeking treatment.18 Further, the randomized withdrawal design does not allow formal estimation of effect size but rather maintenance of efficacy. In addition, statistical modeling of exploratory end points did not adjust for missing data due to treatment- or disease-related relapse or dropout (censoring).

Conclusions

This trial demonstrated that the oral, once-daily D1R antagonist ecopipam was well tolerated and maintained tic-suppression efficacy for up to 24 weeks of treatment in pediatric and adult participants with TS. No signals for clinically relevant weight gain, adverse metabolic effects, or drug-induced movement disorders were identified. Based on its tolerability profile, efficacy, mechanism of action, and current and previous phase 2b10 positive results, ecopipam could be a novel pharmacologic treatment option for children, adolescents, and adults with TS.
