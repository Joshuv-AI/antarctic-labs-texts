Simultaneous gene engineering of T cells with multiple neoantigen-specific T cell receptors for adoptive cell transfer
Authors: Melinda Magna, Lior Levy, Krisztian Csomos, Aaron J Dinerman, Abraham A Hakim, Noam Levin, Sanghyun P Kim, Nikolaos Zacharakis, Neilesh B Parikh, Brian D Bui, Aarushi Bhasin, Rachel K Beyer, Stephanie L Goff, Frank J Lowery, Steven A Rosenberg, Sri Krishna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractT cell receptor (TCR) T cell therapy targeting tumor-specific antigens and neoantigens can mediate impressive tumor regressions in solid tumors. However, solid epithelial tumors are characterized by inherent tumor heterogeneity that can lead to tumor escape and thwart TCR T cell therapy. Targeting multiple cancer antigens in a single TCR T product may help overcome the antigen heterogeneity among metastatic deposits. However, the time and expense of preparing multiple TCR-transduced T cell populations under good manufacturing practice (GMP) for patient treatment has limited this approach to target 1 or 2 antigens. Here, we design strategies to enhance TCR T cell therapy efficacy by simultaneously targeting multiple neoantigens. We developed two novel manufacturing processes to introduce multiple neoantigen-specific TCRs into T cells, using gamma-retroviral delivery. These strategies resulted in multipotent neoantigen-reactive TCR T cell products, which demonstrated functionality against multiple tumor neoantigens and displayed cytotoxicity against heterogeneous tumor cells in vitro. High-dimensional single-cell and single-clone T cell analysis confirmed the presence of multiple TCR-expressing T cells in the products and demonstrated functional recognition of multiple neoantigens. This study, thus, addresses the challenges of tumor heterogeneity and immune escape mechanisms in solid tumors, paving the way for more effective TCR T cell therapies in cancer treatment.Keywords: adoptive cell transfer, immunotherapy, TCR T cell therapy, engineered T cell, cancer, solid tumor, mutated neoantigens, cell therapy manufacturing

Introduction

Over the last decade, immunotherapies utilizing the adoptive cell transfer (ACT) of tumor-reactive T lymphocytes have come to the forefront of personalized cancer treatments. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells have shown remarkable success in the treatment of hematological malignancies.1 Secondly, the clinical success of ex-vivo-expanded tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) recently led to the first Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of TIL treatment for metastatic melanoma.2 However, in both CAR T cell and TIL therapy settings, many metastatic cancer patients do not experience complete clinical response due to inherent tumor heterogeneity and phenotypic dysfunction of adoptively transferred T cells.

Multiple in vitro studies have shown that the antigen specificity of normal circulating peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) can be redirected to exhibit antitumor reactivity by transgene insertion of T cell receptors (TCRs) specific to cancer antigens. This approach, termed “TCR T” cell therapy, circumvents T cell dysfunction observed in TIL therapy, providing an alternative promising ACT strategy to patients who may not have a strong autologous T cell response against the tumor antigen.3 The ability of TCR-transduced redirected lymphocytes was shown to mediate tumor rejection in patients with metastatic melanoma and selected solid epithelial cancers.4,5,6 In August 2024, the FDA approved the first TCR T cell therapy utilizing an affinity-enhanced-MAGEA4-specific TCR for the treatment of synovial sarcoma or myxoid round cell liposarcoma.6,7 This significant milestone underscores the potential of TCR T cell therapy in treating solid tumors. We recently conducted a phase 2 clinical trial where we administered TCR T therapy targeting tumor neoantigens to metastatic colorectal cancer patients.8 In this study, we reported that TCR T immunotherapy targeting tumor mutations can mediate partial tumor regressions in patients with advanced metastatic disease.8

While considerable efforts are being devoted to the development of TCR T cell therapy for solid tumor treatment, major challenges remain.9 Immunosuppressive and nutrient-depleted tumor microenvironments and persistent antigen exposure can lead to T cell exhaustion and inhibit TCR T cell function and persistence.10,11,12 Tumor heterogeneity due to cancer evolution and the variable density of neoantigen expression on tumor cells can also hamper antitumor T cell responses. Furthermore, tumor immune escape mechanisms, such as downregulation or loss of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) or antigen expression and defects in antigen processing and presentation on the surface of the tumor cell have also been described as potential major contributing factors to the low efficacy of TCR T cell therapy.13,14,15 Consequently, one hypothesis is that targeting multiple neoantigens simultaneously with a combined TCR-engineered T cell therapy product may improve therapeutic outcomes by providing a higher coverage of tumor-presented neoantigens in heterogeneous metastatic disease.

Manufacturing multi-TCR T cell therapy products in parallel processes for each TCR—in order to target multiple neoantigens as a final heterogeneous product—presents financial and technical challenges due to manufacturing logistics. In our ongoing clinical trial at the Surgery Branch (NCT03412877), patients with metastatic cancer are being treated with TCR T cell products targeting up to five personalized neoantigens; to date, the highest number of TCRs administered concurrently has been three.8 In this clinical protocol, each engineered TCR T cell population is manufactured and tested separately before pooling into a final product on the day of treatment. The personnel and facility resources required for this approach have limited the ability to test the efficacy of multi-TCR T cell population in patients with metastatic epithelial cancer. Consequently, a next-generation schema for manufacturing and testing is warranted to adequately explore the hypothesis that tumors may require a multipronged T cell attack.

In this study, we designed and tested two different modifications to the current vector and cell production process of the individual patient TCR-transduced T cell therapy product to increase the number and diversity of TCRs administered per patient and reduce the manufacturing cost and effort. Using five neoantigen-reactive TCRs at a time, we successfully manufactured multi-TCR T cell products at research scale. Functional testing of the multi-TCR T cell products showed concurrent recognition of multiple neoantigens and tumors in vitro.

Results

Proposed manufacturing strategies for simultaneous delivery of multiple TCRs in an ACT product

In our current manufacturing process, each neoantigen-reactive TCR sequence is cloned into a retroviral plasmid backbone and packaged into retroviral particles in an individual manufacturing run. Each individual TCR (iTCR)-derived retroviral supernatant is used for the transduction of T cells, thereby generating single-TCR-transduced ACT products. To administer a TCR T cell therapy product that contains more than one TCR, the separately manufactured cells are combined pre-treatment (pooled cells, “pcell”) (Figure 1A). To increase the number of neoantigen-specific TCRs (neoTCRs) administered per patient while reducing the manufacturing cost and effort, we designed two modified manufacturing processes that combine the individual TCR products at earlier stages (Figure 1). In the first strategy (pooled supernatant, “psup”), we individually transfected each TCR-encoding DNA plasmid and generated separate retroviral vector supernatants. Next, equal volumes of the retroviral vector supernatants were combined, and this “psup” was used for a single transduction into PBLs, resulting in one cell product (Figure 1B). In the second proposed manufacturing strategy (pooled plasmid, “ppl”), we combined an equal amount of the DNA plasmids encoding each TCR as the first step of the vector production process, then proceeded with the subsequent production process as described above, without change. The DNA plasmid pool was used in a single transfection followed by a single transduction in the protocol, resulting in a single treatment product (Figure 1C). Both alternative procedures result in a single product with multiple neoantigen reactivities that can be subjected to the extensive quality control release assays required by regulatory agencies.

Off-the-shelf neoTCRs represented in a multi-TCR product can recognize multiple neoantigens simultaneously in vitro

To model an “off-the-shelf” multi-TCR infusion product, we generated a TCR product with five well-established neoTCRs derived from HLA class I-restricted CD8+ T cells targeting different individual cancer neoepitopes (Figure 2A), using the proposed manufacturing processes outlined in Figure 1. The “pcell” population was generated from five individually transfected and transduced TCR T cell populations. Each of the five transduced cell populations were subjected individually to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) at day 7 post-transduction for mTCRβ+ CD8+ T cells, and then to a rapid expansion protocol (REP) for two weeks. Following expansion, the five T cell populations were combined in equal numbers to generate the “pcell” product. The “ppl” and “psup” populations were also sorted and REPed 7 days post-transduction. The five TCRs were previously identified in TIL screening from 4 patients (4317, 4382, 4196, and 4095), as described previously.8,16,17 Two TCRs (4095_9 and 4095_10) were identified from patient TILs that recognized a 9-mer and a 10-mer peptide neoepitope of KRAS (G12D mutation), respectively.18

First, we tested the transduction efficiency of each of the individually transduced TCR products and the three different multi-TCR ACT products (ppl, psup, and pcell), using flow cytometry. Transduction efficiency was variable among the five TCRs, ranging between 26% and 68% when transduced individually, as measured 10 days post-transduction. The multi-TCR-engineered T cell products (ppl and psup) showed comparable and high murine TCR (mTCR) expression levels (61% and 66%, respectively; Figure S2A). After REP-induced expansion, the TCR expression level was highly comparable between the “ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell” products (between 84% and 86%) but showed variability among the individually transduced TCR T cells, ranging from 72% to 99% (Figure 2B).

To evaluate T cell function in the individual TCR-transduced and pooled TCR products, we co-cultured them with HLA-matched antigen-presenting cells (APCs) pulsed with titrated amounts of mutated (Mut) and wild-type (WT) peptides and independently measured the upregulation of the activation marker 4-1BB on T cells and secreted interferon γ (IFN-γ) levels as orthogonal measures of effector function in the supernatant. We compared the pooled TCR products with the “pcell” products resulting from single-TCR transductions (Figure 2C). In the 24-h co-culture, the “pcell” product exhibited response to all five mutated neoantigens, demonstrated by 4-1BB upregulation on TCR-transduced T cells and activation-induced IFN-γ secretion. However, the “ppl” and “psup” products showed measurable response to 4 out of the 5 peptide targets based on 4-1BB upregulation and IFN-γ secretion (Figures 2D, 2E, and S2B–S2D). The lack of measurable response to the PIK3CA peptide in the “ppl” and “psup” products was correlated with the low transduction efficiency of the 4317 TCR in general (Figure S2A). The average 4-1BB reactivity against all 5 neoantigens was not significantly different in the three pooled products (Figures 2F and S2E). Overall, these results suggest that off-the-shelf multi-TCR ACT products can successfully respond to multiple neoantigens, with no significant consistent differences compared with their individual counterparts. Additionally, no WT reactivity was observed in the pooled TCR T cell products.

Previous studies have suggested that phenotypic characteristics determined by the expression of activation, differentiation, and memory markers of tumor-antigen-specific cell products correlate with the antitumor function and treatment response.19 To determine whether the new manufacturing processes affect cell phenotype in the “ppl” and “psup” products, we evaluated the expression of surface markers of activation, memory, and exhaustion phenotypes (CD39, CD69, CD62L, CD45RO, PD1, and TIM3) after FACS and expansion. The cells generated with the “ppl” and “psup” process did not show any phenotypic difference based on these markers in comparison to the “pcell” product, while there were TCR-specific small variations among the individual TCR-transduced cells (Figure S3). Thus, the pooled TCR products largely recapitulated the phenotype of individually transduced TCR populations.

Multi-TCR T cell product comprising CD4- and CD8-restricted autologous TCRs successfully recognizes multiple neoantigens simultaneously

We next evaluated the use of the technologically and financially most feasible “ppl” manufacturing process with an autologous patient tumor model targeting private tumor neoantigens. In this assessment, we simultaneously targeted four private neoantigens with five autologous neoTCRs, derived from CD8+ and CD4+ T cells from a patient with colorectal adenocarcinoma (Figure 3A). To perform functional testing, we transduced autologous PBLs and two different healthy donor-derived PBLs with each of the five TCRs individually and prepared the “ppl” and “pcell” products from these five TCRs, as described above. All five single-TCR products as well as the “ppl” product showed highly comparable transduction efficiency among the TCRs and only a small variation among donors, with mTCR expression on both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells ranging between 60% and 90% on day 7 post-transduction (Figure 3B).

The T cell products were tested on day 8 post-transduction for neoantigen reactivity against autologous APCs presenting titrated concentrations of pulsed neoepitope peptides in vitro (Figures 3C–3E). In the APC co-culture assay, all five individually transduced TCRs showed specificity to their mutated cognate peptides in titration compared with the WT peptides (Figure S4). Stimulation with cognate neoepitope at the highest concentration (10 μg/mL) induced 4-1BB upregulation on 80%–100% of CD8+ T cells for each TCR (Figure S4B) but showed variability on CD4+ T cell activation (Figure S4C). Similarly, high TCR-specific variability in IFN-γ secretion was observed among the five TCRs (Figure S4D). These results imply that regardless of the HLA restriction of the originating T cells, at least some TCRs can induce T cell activation when transduced in both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, though the efficacy of response in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells may vary by TCR.

In the same APC co-culture assay, the “ppl” product showed higher 4-1BB upregulation against each of the four mutated target peptides than the “pcell” product, with comparable titration based on peptide concentrations, and with relatively similar sensitivity to low peptide concentrations (Figures 3C and 3D). As a measure of effector function, we also assessed IFN-γ production in the bulk T cell populations. The “pcell” population secreted higher levels of IFN-γ in response to two of the four peptides (VANGL1 and USPX9) than the “ppl” product (Figure 3E). “ppl” and “pcell” products showed comparable IFN-γ secretion in response to the TP53- and KRAS-mutated peptides (Figure 3E), suggesting that there is heterogeneity in IFN-γ response of pooled TCR-T products depending on the TCRs being assayed, with no consistent differences from that of single-TCR T products.

In patients, tumors likely present heterogeneous levels of neoantigens with varying levels of HLA expression simultaneously. To assess the response of the 4589 pooled TCR T cell products to multiple neoantigens presented simultaneously, we combined the four mutant peptides equally with a final concentration of 0.1 μg/mL/peptide in the mix (Mut peptide mix) and the four WT peptides similarly (WT peptide mix) and pulsed the two peptide mixes to autologous APCs for presentation. Next, we co-cultured the peptide mix-pulsed APCs with each of the five single-TCR-transduced cells and the “ppl” and “pcell” products and measured T cell response to the simultaneously presented antigens (Figure 4A). This assay showed 4-1BB upregulation on 90%–100% of CD8+/mTCR+ T cells in all single-TCR and pooled TCR T cell products, except for TCR2 cells where the 4-1BB rate was slightly lower (mean: 84%) (Figure 4B). We observed highly variable T cell activation among the five TCRs in CD4+/mTCR+ cells. While TCR1, TCR3, TCR4, and TCR5 induced 4-1BB upregulation in >60% of CD4+/mTCR+ cells, TCR2 activated, on average, only 14% of the CD4+/mTCR+ cells upon antigen exposure (Figure 4C). IFN-γ secretion was also variable among the five single-TCR cell products and correlated well with their responses observed above to the single-peptide stimulus (Figure 4D). Importantly, the “ppl” and “pcell” products showed comparable levels of 4-1BB upregulation in CD8+/mTCR+ T cells (94% and 92%, respectively) (Figure 4B). However, 4-1BB upregulation was significantly higher on CD4+ T cells in the “ppl” product than on those in the “pcell” product (mean: 75% and 51.6%, respectively) when challenged with all 4 mutant peptide targets simultaneously (Figure 4C). The “ppl” and “pcell” products also secreted comparable amounts of IFN-γ against the Mut peptide mix in this co-culture (Figure 4D). These results provide evidence that functional autologous multi-TCR ACT products that contain CD8+ and CD4+ T cell-derived TCRs can be successfully manufactured in a simplified process where TCR-coding vectors are combined before transfection. Importantly, the engineered T cells in the multi-TCR T cell products can be successfully activated by simultaneous presentation of multiple neoantigens.

Multi-TCR T cell products can overcome heterogeneous antigen presentation in vitro

We tested the efficacy of multi-TCR T cell products in an experimental model that mimics genomic loss or downregulation of HLA class I when the proper restricting HLA allele for the cognate TCR is not expressed to present some of the mutated neoantigens. Using the off-the-shelf neoTCR T cell model (introduced in Figure 2), we pulsed the five mutant peptides on APCs derived from patient 4095 that could present 4 out of the 5 antigenic peptides (PIK3CA, TP53, KRAS_9-mer, and KRAS_10-mer, but not SMARCA4) due to the expression of HLA-A∗02:01 and HLA-C∗08:02 but not HLA-A∗31:01 on the APCs (Figure 5A). The peptide mix-loaded APCs were co-cultured with all five single-TCR-transduced products and the three types of pooled TCR products (“ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell”). T cell activation was assessed after overnight co-culture. In this co-culture, the four single-TCR-transduced T cells (4317, 4196, 4095_9, and 4095_10) showed variable levels of activation measured by 4-1BB upregulation (between 18% and 50%) and IFN-γ secretion (between 5 and 24 ng/mL), while the 4382 TCR-T cells did not respond to the APC stimulus, as expected due to lack of cognate HLA-expression (Figures 5B and 5C). The “ppl” and “psup” products exhibited similar 4-1BB upregulation (36% and 34%, respectively) and IFN-γ secretion (10 and 12 ng/mL, respectively). Finally, the “pcell” product had significantly lower 4-1BB expression (20%) and significantly higher IFN-γ secretion (18 ng/mL) than the “ppl” and “psup” cells (Figures 5B and 5C). Thus, “ppl” and “psup” pooled TCR products may compensate for the lack of antigen presentation of one of the neoantigens when given as a mono-TCR therapy.

One of the biggest challenges of ACT in solid tumors is to mount an immune response against all cancer clones present within a heterogeneous tumor, where the expression or presentation of antigens is variable. To model this scenario, we tested the multi-TCR T cell products of the off-the-shelf neoTCR model against the two cancer cell lines TYK-nu and HPAC that present only one neoantigen each (mutant-TP53 R175H neoepitope or mutant-KRAS G12D neoepitope, respectively) (Figures 5A and 5D–5G). First, we created mixed tumor cell cultures by combining the TYK-nu and HPAC cells in different ratios and then co-cultured these mixed tumor cells with the relevant single-TCR-transduced products (E:T = 1:1) and the pooled TCR ACT products (containing all 5 TCRs) to assess T cell response (Figure 5D). As expected, T cell response in the single-TCR-transduced populations targeting TP53 R175H and KRAS G12D (4196, 4095_9, and 4095_10 cells) correlated with dose titration of the relevant tumor cell line for each single TCR with no recognition of tumors expressing the non-cognate HLA-epitope (Figure 5E). The “ppl” and “psup” products showed comparable activation profiles, with slightly higher 4-1BB levels than the “pcell” product in the mixed tumor co-culture and the highest 4-1BB expression (ppl, 58%; psup, 63%; pcell, 43%) in the 75/25 HPAC/TYK-nu% mix. Importantly, in contrast to single-TCR-engineered T cell products, 4-1BB activation was detectable (albeit at a lower level) in all three pooled products when only one of the two tumor cell lines were represented (100/0 and 0/100 HPAC-TYk-nu%) (Figure 5E). These results suggest additive and compensatory effects of multiple TCRs while targeting multiple neoantigens in a heterogeneous tumor population in vitro.

Next, we evaluated the tumor cell-killing ability of the “ppl” and “psup” products in comparison to “pcell” and single-TCR-transduced cells of fluorescently labeled TYK-nu (TYK-nu-Nuc) and HPAC (HPAC-Nuc) cell lines separately in an in vitro killing assay (Figures 5F and 5G). We plated the fluorescent TYK-nu cells with the 4196 single-TCR (targeting TP53 R175H on TYK-nu cells but not on HPAC cells) and the three pooled ACT products in an effector:target cell (E:T) ratio of 4:1 (Figure 5F). Because the theoretical representation of the relevant TP53 R175H-targeting 4196 TCR in the “ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell” products is ∼20% (1 in 5), we also tested the three pooled products in a 20:1 ratio to match the theoretical representation of the 4196 TCR-T cells to the number of mTCR+ T cells in the single-TCR-transduced product. In the killing assay with the TP53 R175H-expressing TYK-nu cells, the single-TCR-transduced T cells efficiently controlled the growth of the tumor cells in the culture, but the 4:1 plated “ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell” products showed low efficacy at variable degrees. However, all three pooled products with an E:T ratio of 20:1 successfully controlled the TYK-nu cell growth, comparable to the single-TCR T cell product (Figure 5F). Similarly, in the HPAC killing assay, only the relevant 4095_9 TCR (targeting KRAS G12D neoantigen on HPAC cells but not on TYK-nu cells) and the “psup” product containing this TCR showed some efficacy among the groups with an E:T ratio of 4:1 (Figure 5G). To adjust for the TCR dilution in the pooled products, where two out of five TCRs target the KRAS neoantigen on HPAC cells, we also plated the “ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell” products in an E:T ratio of 10:1. Consistently, the “ppl” and “psup” pooled TCR T cell products plated in an E:T ratio of 10:1 showed high efficacy in controlling the HPAC cell growth. Moreover, the “ppl” and “psup” products showed more efficient tumor killing than the “pcell” product in this assay (Figure 5G). Together these in vitro assays showed that the pooled TCR ACT products get successfully activated and are cytotoxic even when the antigen presentation is incomplete or heterogeneous.

TCR representation and functionality of TCRs at the single-cell level in the multi-TCR T cell products

In theory, transducing multiple TCRs altogether as one may result in unequal representation of TCRs in the ACT product and create the possibility of integration of multiple TCRs into the same cell. To characterize TCR expression in the multi-TCR ACT products, we used the off-the-shelf neoTCR model (corresponding transduction efficiency is shown in Figure S2A). We prepared neo-peptide-HLA-class I tetramer complexes for each of the five TCRs, labeled with distinct fluorophores, and performed a multiplex tetramer staining on the “ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell” ACT products at day 14 post-transduction to assess TCR expression on a per-cell basis (Figure 6).

Unsupervised tetramer-based clustering of CD8+ mTCR+ T cells in “pcell,” “ppl,” and “psup” samples revealed 24 heterogeneous clusters based on the TCR specificity to the five antigen tetramers (Figures 6A, S5A, and S5B). Specifically, as expected, only single tetramer-stained clusters were identified in “pcell” sample (cluster 2: PIK3CA; cluster 3: KRAS-9-mer, clusters 7 and 8: TP53; cluster 10: SMARCA4; clusters 12 and 24: KRAS-10-mer), representing 98.2% of the total cells (Figures 6B–6D). In contrast, simultaneous TCR transduction in the “ppl” and “psup” populations resulted in an additional 16 clusters, with combinatorial tetramer staining indicating the presence of multiple TCRs targeting distinct neoepitopes within the same T cell (Figures 6B–6E). Based on the HLA-tetramer staining, “ppl” product consisted of 45.2% single-TCR T cells and 54.3% multi-TCR-expressing T cells, with 44% of all cells expressing 2 TCRs, 10.3% expressing 3 TCRs, and 0.47% expressing 4 TCRs, indicating a distribution of single T cells expressing more than one engineered TCR. We identified similar antigen specificity distribution in the “psup” product (49.8% single-TCR and 49% multi-TCR cells, with 40.3% of all cells expressing 2 TCRs, 8.8% expressing 3 TCRs, and 1.03% expressing 4 TCRs) (Figures 6B–6E). Interestingly no cluster in either sample expressed all the five TCRs simultaneously, even though all 5 TCRs were clearly represented in the population as a whole. These results indicate simultaneous expression of multiple TCRs in the mTCR+ CD8+ T cell population in both the “ppl” and “psup” products.

To verify the presence and functionality of multiple TCRs in the individual cells of the “ppl” and “psup” products, we next performed serial dilution of the T cell populations to 1 cell/well in 96-well plates and expanded these clones in REP for 3 weeks (Figure 6F). Due to the difficulty of single-T cell clone expansion in general, out of approximately 300 single T cells (three 96-well plates per population) from each of the “ppl” and “psup” products, 3 clones (psup 1E10, psup 2A10, and psup 2H6) expanded successfully. We tested antigen recognition of these expanded clonal populations along with all five single-TCR-transduced cell populations as controls in single-peptide-pulsed APC co-culture assay against each of the 5 target mutant peptides and their respective WT peptides (Figure 6G). The three “psup” clonal populations showed IFN-γ release across different target mutant peptides, while the single-TCR-transduced control cells responded only to their cognate mutant peptide. It should be noted that the 4095_9 TCR recognized both 9-mer and 10-mer KRAS peptides processed by APCs (Figure 6G). Importantly, no reactivity was seen from these T cell clones against WT peptide-pulsed APCs.

Finally, we assessed the average vector copy number (VCN) integration in the cell products with droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), using TCR-specific primer-probe sets designed for each of the TCRs. The single-TCR-expressing T cells showed the average VCN/cell between 5.4 and 9.2, while the “ppl” product showed an average of 1.1–2.1 copies of each of the five TCRs for a total of 7.6 vector copy/cell, on average, in the cell population (Figure S6A).

Taken together, these data demonstrate that simultaneous transduction of TCRs targeting multiple neoepitopes results in a heterogeneous population of functionally active T cells targeting multiple neoepitopes at the single-cell level.

Multi-TCR-transduced T cells show target-specific reactivity only against HLA-neoepitope-matched organoids and tumor cell lines

The simultaneous transduction and heterogeneous expression of multiple TCRs in the “ppl” and “psup” T cell populations may permit unpredictable TCR α/β pairing and altered specificity. To assess the potential off-target activation, pooled TCR products were co-cultured with patient-derived tumor and normal organoids and tumor cell lines expressing at least one TCR-matched HLA allele and/or neoepitope (Figure 7).

Using the off-the-shelf neoTCR model, five single-TCR-transduced products and the three pooled products (“ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell”) were co-cultured with 12 organoids and 2 tumor cell lines (Figures 7A, 7B, and S7A). T cell activation was measured after overnight incubation by 4-1BB upregulation. No T cell activation was observed in response to five normal (4615-2 N, 4661-1 N, 4668-2 N, 4671-1 N, and 8018-N) and five tumor organoids (4650, 4659, 4661, 4634, and 4589) expressing only a matched HLA allele or only the neoepitope (Figure 7B). In contrast, 4-1BB upregulation was observed when both the TCR-matched HLA allele and cognate neoepitope were co-expressed (4424 and 4430 tumor organoids; TYK-nu and HPAC cell lines). TP53 R175H-specific 4196 single-TCR-transduced T cells and all three pooled products were activated by the 4424 organoid and TYK-nu cell line. Likewise, KRAS G12D-specific 4095_9 and 4095_10 single-TCR-transduced cells and all three pooled products responded to the 4430 organoid and HPAC cell line (Figure 7B).

To further evaluate off-target activity in an autologous setting, 4589 “ppl” and “pcell” populations, together with single-TCR-transduced cells, were co-cultured with an autologous tumor organoid, an autologous normal lung organoid, and an allogeneic tumor organoid (4516) (Figures 7C, S7B, and S7C). The 4516 organoid shares HLA-C∗07:01 with patient 4589 (not TCR relevant) and expresses KRAS p.G12D in the absence of the corresponding TCR-relevant HLA allele (S7B). Mild but significant 4-1BB upregulation was observed in the “ppl” and “pcell” products (Figure 7A) as well as single-TCR-transduced products (Figure S7C) in response to the autologous tumor organoid compared with normal lung and T cell-only controls. Most importantly, no significant T cell activation was detected against autologous normal lung or the allogeneic 4516 organoid compared with T cell-only controls.

Collectively, these findings support the notion that “ppl” and “psup” T cell products retain strict HLA-restricted, neoepitope-specific reactivity without detectable off-target activation under conditions of partial HLA and/or neoantigen mismatch.

Discussion

While TCR T cell therapies show promise, their clinical efficacy has been limited, in part, due to tumor heterogeneity and immune evasion mechanisms such as neoantigen and HLA loss.17,20,21 These challenges highlight the need for multi-targeted approaches to improve the durability of TCR T cell therapies in solid tumors.

In this study, we provide the first piece of evidence that a multi-neoantigen-reactive TCR T cell ACT product can be manufactured with simultaneous gamma-retroviral delivery from multiple off-the-shelf neoTCRs as well as multiple autologous neoTCRs by using a simplified manufacturing process (Figure 1). Here, we showed that combining the TCR-coding plasmid or combining the viral vector supernatant generated from individual TCR plasmids at early stages of the TCR T cell manufacturing process results in multi-functional ACT products (Figures 2, 3, and 4) capable of successfully overcoming heterogeneous antigen presentation and killing tumor cells in vitro (Figure 5).

As expected, compared with individual monoclonal TCR therapy products, pooled TCR products (“ppl,” “psup,” and “pcell”) displayed lower recognition of individual single target epitopes. However, when adjusting for the total TCR+ cell numbers to compensate for this “dilution effect,” the pooled products showed similar or better cytotoxic effects against a single target compared with the single-TCR-transduced cell products in in vitro killing assays (Figure 5). We note that while our findings are indicative of the utility of a pooled TCR-T products that can compensate for the presence or lack of different antigens in a mixed tumor model, it still does not capture the natural heterogeneity in HLA and antigen expression within a human tumor. Thus, future studies should evaluate whether pooled TCR products perform comparably to single-TCR-transduced populations using patient-derived tumors with mapped antigenic specificities combining subclonal and clonal neoantigen expression. Thus, careful consideration of the number and antigenic specificities of TCRs that are used to engineer a pooled multi-antigen targeting TCR product is needed.

With a similar objective to target tumor heterogeneity with a multi-TCR T cell therapy product, a previous study reported that 16 patients received personalized neoantigen-reactive TCR T cell therapy engineered using CRISPR-Cas9 precision gene editing.22 Although this approach demonstrated low toxicity and some clinical activity, improvements in TCR T cell manufacturing were noted. In our hands, concurrent retroviral transduction of multiple TCRs enabled the simultaneous integration of multiple TCRs into individual cells and demonstrated multi-antigen specificity (Figure 6). Although our analyses showed that there was a distribution of multiple combinations of TCR-expressing T cells in the pooled TCR products, it is unclear at this point whether there is an optimal number of TCRs targeting different neoepitope specificities that can be expressed on a single T cell that might have an antitumor effect superior to that of a single-TCR-transduced T cell product. This could, in part, explain why we observe a distribution of IFN-γ secretion in the pooled TCR products (Figures 2 vs. 3) when compared with the pooled TCRs, even when 4-1BB activation is comparable. Nevertheless, in the tumor-killing assay as shown in Figure 5, we did not observe significant differences in the tumor-killing capabilities of the “ppl” or “psup” TCR T cell products when compared with those of the pooled individual TCR T cell (pcell) products. Thus, future studies with fine-grained analyses on the activation and effector functions of each of the unique TCR specificities within the pooled TCR products to compare with single-TCR T cell products are warranted to develop quality control metrics for pooled TCR T cells.

In theory, the expression of multiple α and β chains of different TCRs in a T cell can potentially lead to TCR chain mispairing, and the expressed hybrid TCR heterodimers could have unknown specificity. Although we showed the presence of multiple TCR-expressing T cells in the “ppl” and “psup” populations, we did not observe significant reactivity against the APCs or WT peptide-loaded APCs with any of our models during any in vitro co-culture assays. Furthermore, our functional data using autologous and allogeneic tumor organoids further support the specificity of the multi-TCR T cell populations (Figure 7). Pooled TCR products exhibited strict dependence on matched HLA and cognate neoepitope for activation, with no detectable responses in partially matched or normal tissue models. These results suggest that any potential mispairing does not translate into functionally relevant off-target activity under the tested conditions. Even though we have not seen cross-reactivity of the “ppl” and “psup” ACT products in these models, unfortunately, it is not possible to test TCR mispairing and hybrid TCR expression on T cells or off-target reactivity against all possible human peptides with the currently available technologies.

It has been proposed that TCR mispairing between the endogenous and the introduced TCR chains in the engineered T cell products could lead to recognition of unrelated peptides and self-antigens, potentially causing off-target toxicities and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD).23,24 However, such effects have not been supported by clinical data of 100 patients treated at the Surgery Branch, with no mispairing-related adverse events with fully human TCRs or TCRs engineered with a murine constant region targeting a wide range of tumor antigens and neoantigens.8,25

Several engineering strategies have been proposed to mitigate TCR α/β chain mispairing when multiple receptors are introduced into the same T cell population, including the use of engineered or orthogonal pairing interfaces to promote preferential pairing of cognate α and β chains, murine or chimeric constant domains, as well as the introduction of additional cysteine residues to stabilize correct pairing through interchain disulfide bonds.16,26,27,28 Using the murine TCR constant domain, our group at the Surgery Branch of the National Cancer Institute observed no evidence of GVHD or off-target toxicity in 106 patients treated with autologous TCR-engineered T cells, where the engineered TCR T cell products maintained the expression of the endogenous TCRs.25

Nevertheless, in the scenario of multiple neoTCRs introduced into the same cell, potential off-target effects due to mispairing might still emerge between the engineered TCRs, despite our experiments indicating that multi-TCR products display TCR activation only when presented with both HLA and mutation-matched organoids that is comparable to background levels of single-TCR T cell products (Figure 7). Thus, while this study demonstrates that multi-specific T cell products can effectively target multiple neoepitopes and be generated using a streamlined engineering approach, incorporating TCR modifications that ensure correct α/β chain pairing or by introducing them as single-chain TCRs may further enhance their safety. Future studies exploring the antitumor in vitro and in vivo effects of multi-TCR-expressing single T cells are needed to evaluate their utility in TCR T cell therapy, and a careful evaluation of these multi-TCR products for toxicities in humans utilizing a dose-escalation approach is also warranted. Finally, we acknowledge that despite utilizing pooled TCR T cells against multiple target epitopes, neoantigens and HLAs can still be lost in patient tumors during ACT. Therefore, careful neoantigen prioritization efforts based on the expression of the mutation coupled with clonality analysis can be utilized to rationally prioritize multiple TCRs for discovery that can be used to design a pooled TCR T cell product to avoid neoantigen loss due to tumor heterogeneity.29

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel approach to address TCR T cell therapy limitations in solid tumors by developing multi-targeted neoantigen-reactive T cell products. By simultaneously engineering multiple TCRs through a modified retroviral delivery process, our study demonstrates a promising strategy to overcome tumor heterogeneity. The results showed that the multi-functional ACT products could target diverse neoantigens with maintained specificity. This method represents a significant step toward more effective and adaptable cancer immunotherapies, offering hope for improved treatment of complex solid tumors.

Material and methods

Human subjects and clinical protocols

Written, informed consent was obtained from all study participants, and all studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Belmont Report, and the US Common Rule. This study was performed in accordance with an assurance filed with and approved by the US Department of Health and Human Services and is registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov. Adults aged 18–70 years with upper or lower gastrointestinal, pancreatic, or breast cancer refractory to standard chemotherapy were recruited onto either NCT01174121 or NCT03412877 IRB approved protocols. Metastatic tumor lesions and leukaphereses were collected from each patient at the time of recruitment. Healthy donors were recruited under the IRB approved tissue procurement protocol NCT00068003 and underwent leukaphereses. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and APCs were obtained from patient and healthy donor pheresis.

Neoantigen-reactive TCR identification and cloning

Neoantigen-reactive TCRs were identified from TILs, as described previously,8,30 from 5 patients with chemorefractory metastatic epithelial cancers enrolled in the tissue procurement protocol NCT00068003. Briefly, metastatic lesions were resected from the patients, and the resected tumors were plated with high-dose IL-2 to culture and collect TILs. Immunogenic neoepitopes were predicted from mutation analysis based on whole-exome sequencing and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) performed at the Surgery Branch. Data were processed, and variants were called, as previously described.31,32 Synthetic peptides and tandem minigene RNA containing the mutation were synthetized and pulsed on autologous APC for co-culture with TILs. Mutant peptide-reactive T cells based on 4-1BB upregulation were subjected to FACS and next-generation sequencing to identify TCR α- and β-chain sequences, as described previously.17,33 For tumor 4589, T cells were isolated from tumor digest and subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) (10× Genomics). TCR clonotypes were scored according to their RNA expression of published neoTCR8 and neoTCR 4 scores.30 The human TCR Vα chain in conjunction with the mouse TCR Cα region and the human TCR Vβ chain with the mouse TCR Cβ region, separated by a furin SGSG P2A linker, were synthesized as a single DNA strand and cloned into the pMSGV1 retroviral vector plasmid, as previously described.16 TCR sequences were synthesized and cloned into pMSGV1 vectors at GenScript Biotech (Piscataway NJ), and TCRs were validated by testing for mutation specificity against the corresponding neoantigenic and WT peptides.

Primary cells

To obtain T cells, PBLs were thawed and washed, then cultured in 50/50 media (RPMI1640 media [#11875093], Gibco], supplemented with 10% human serum [#HS1021HI, Valley Biomedical], 1% Glutamax, 12.5 mmol/L HEPES [#15630080, Gibco], 1%Pen/Strep, and 5 mg/mL gentamicin [#120–099–661, QualityBiological]) mixed with AIM-V (#0870112BK, Gibco) in 1:1 ratio) supplemented with 1,000 IU/mL IL-2 (Aldesleukin, Nationaldrugcode:76310–002201, Clinigen). Immature dendritic cells (DCs) were generated from autologous PBLs by plating the cells in a T150 flask in dendritic cell medium (RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 5% human serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 800 IU/mL granulocyte-macrophage-colony-stimulating factor [GM-CSF; Peprotech], and 200 IU/mL IL-4 [Peprotech]). Adherent dendritic cells were collected by detachment with PBS containing 5% EDTA after 5 days of culture. Primary autologous B cells were isolated from autologous PBLs using CD19+ microbead selection (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-050-301). CD19+ selected B cells were immortalized and transformed with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), using EBV-containing supernatant from B95-8 cells (ATCC, #VR-1492) according to the manufacturer’s instruction without using feeder cells. EBV-transformed B cells were cultured in B cell media (Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium [Thermo Fisher, #12440053], 10% human serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 1% GlutaMAX, and 200 U/mL IL-4).

TCR T cell manufacture

TCR T cell manufacturing was performed at research scale based on our clinical trial (NCT03412877) manufacturing process as described previously.8 HEK293GP cells were seeded at 7 × 105 cells/well in poly-D-lysine-coated 6-well plates in DMEM culture (DMEM [#12491015, Gibco] supplemented with 10% FBS and Pen/Strep) and grown for 24 h in a 37°C incubator before transfection. The medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS without antibiotics 1 h before transfection. TCR-pMSGV1 plasmids containing the individual TCRs and RD114 envelope plasmid were transiently transfected into the HEK293GP cell line with Lipofectamine 2000 (#11668019, Invitrogen) delivery as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The HEK293GP cells stably express the gag and pol proteins of Moloney murine leukemia virus for retroviral packaging of the introduced TCR-coding plasmid. The transfection medium was carefully removed after 24-h incubation and replaced with fresh DMEM. Following the change of media, the transfected cells were cultured for 48 h in 37°C, then the viral vector containing supernatant was collected from the cells and used for transduction directly or cryopreserved in −80°C until use.

For transduction, cryopreserved PBLs were thawed and washed with 50/50 media (see above) supplemented with DNAse, then plated in 24-well non-tissue culture coated plates at a concentration of 4 × 106 cells/mL in T cell-stimulating media (50/50 media supplemented with 1,000 IU/mL rhIL2 and 50 ng/mL OKT3 anti-CD3 antibody [#130-093-377, Milteny Biotech]), and incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Twenty four-well non-tissue culture coated plates were pre-treated with 0.5 mL/well 20 μg/mL retronectin (Takara Bio, #T100B) in PBS and incubated for 16–24 h in 4°C before transduction. Retronectin was aspirated from the plates for transduction, then 1 mL vector supernatant/well was added to the coated wells and centrifuged for 2 h at 2000 g force at 32°C. After centrifugation, the OKT3/IL2-stimulated PBLs were added to each well (5 × 105 cells/well), and the mixture was again centrifuged at 400 g force at 32°C for 10 min. The cells were cultured and expanded in 37°C with media change (50/50 supplemented with 300 IU IL-2) for 10–14 days at every 2–3 days. Transduction efficiency was evaluated by flow cytometry staining for the mTCR constant region fused to the engineered human TCR on T cells with anti-mouse TCRβ fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, BD #553171) antibody (Figure S1). Subsequently, REP was used to expand FACS-sorted cells, as described previously.34 Briefly, T cells were incubated with 30 ng/mL OKT3, 3,000 IU/mL IL-2, and irradiated allogeneic feeders (50–100 times the number of T cells) in G-Rex 24-well or G-Rex 6-well plates (Wilson Wolf, Cat. 80192 M, 80240 M, and 80500, respectively). After 5 days, half the media was removed and replaced with fresh 50/50 media containing 1,000 IU/mL IL-2. T cells were similarly prepared and functionally tested in three independent biological repeats for the autologous neoTCR (4589) model, transducing the TCRs into autologous patient PBLs and two different healthy donor PBLs independently (Figure 3). For the off-the-shelf neoTCR model, reactivity testing against single peptides was performed in two independent technical repeats by FACS sorting and expanding the T cells twice independently from the same transduced PBL population (Figures 2 and S2).

Patient-derived tumor organoids

Patient-derived tumor organoids were generated according to previously described protocols.35,36 Briefly, fresh tumor specimens obtained from the surgical resection of solid metastases were enzymatically dissociated using a tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-095-929). Hemorrhagic samples were additionally treated with 1 mL of ACK lysing buffer to remove red blood cells. The resulting tumor cell suspension was resuspended in a matrix consisting of 80% Matrigel (Corning, #356238) and 20% histology-specific culture medium, plated along the perimeter of 10-cm culture dishes, and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 45 min to allow polymerization. After the Matrigel had solidified, pre-warmed histology-specific medium was added to the center of the dish, and cultures were maintained at 37°C in a CO2 incubator.

For organoid collection, Matrigel domes were treated with Dispase II (Gibco, #17105041) at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL for 2 h at 37°C. To preserve surface antigen presentation, pelleted cells were washed twice with PBS and directly replated in T cell medium for co-culture experiments. Organoids were plated at an approximate concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL in 1:1 ratio with T cells in the T cell-stimulating media for 24 h co-culture at 37°C in a CO2 incubator. As shown in Figure 7, unselected T cells were used 12 days post transduction. T cell activation was assessed by flow cytometric analysis of 4-1BB expression on mTCR+/CD8+ T cells.

Functional testing of transduced T cells in APC or tumor cell co-cultures

To test TCR-transduced cell functionality, APC co-culture assays were performed in a 96-well format with E:T = 1:1, 50,000 peptide-pulsed-DC/EBV-B cells along with 50,000 TCR T cells. APCs (DCs or EBV-B cells) were pulsed with synthetic peptides (final concentration of the peptides labeled for each experiment) for 3–4 h at 37°C in a cell culture incubator, then washed twice with 50/50 media and plated in 96-well U-bottom tissue culture plates. TCR T cells were added to the APCs immediately in 50/50 media without supplementation of cytokines and co-cultured for 24 h in a 37°C incubator. For tumor cell co-culture, the tumor cells were lifted using TrypLe Express (#12605010, Gibco) from their culture flask, washed twice with 50/50 media, then plated with the TCR T cells for 24-h co-culture. Following co-culture, IFN-γ secretion was analyzed from the co-culture supernatant with ELISA (catalog no. EHIFNG, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the co-cultured cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, as described below. Single-cell clones of TCR-transduced cells were evaluated for the recognition of peptide-pulsed EBV-B cells by measuring IFN-γ secretion by ELISPOT assay, as described previously.37

Synthetic peptides

For HLA class II-restricted TCR testing, 24-mer or 25-mer peptides centered around single-amino acid mutations were synthesized. For HLA class I-restricted TCR testing, minimal epitope 9-mer or 10-mer peptides were synthetized and purified to >90% by using high-performance liquid chromatography (GenScript, custom synthesis). The concentration of the peptides in APC-peptide titration pulse ranged from 10 ng/mL to 10 μg/mL as labeled. In a peptide mix-pulse, the concentration of peptides was 0.1 μg/mL/peptide.

Flow cytometry, FACS, and antibodies

Cells following a co-culture with APCs or tumor cells were stained for 30 min on ice with antibodies specific for the following human markers and mTCR: CD4 PE (BD #347327), CD8 PE-CY7 (BD #557746), 4–1BB APC (BD #309810), and CD3 APC-Cy7 (BD #557832) and anti-mouse TCR-β FITC (BD #553171) propidium iodide (#P1304MP, Thermo Fisher), or DAPI (BioLegend, #422801). Phenotyping panel: live/dead stain (eBioscience fixable viability dye eFluor 780, #65-0865-14), CD3-BUV395 (BD #564001), CD4-BUV805 (BD #612887), CD8-BV605 (BioLegend #344742), mTCR-FITC (BD #553171), PD1-BV650 (BioLegend #329950), TIM3-PE (BioLegend #345006), CD69-APC (BD #555533), CD39-BV785 (BioLegend #328240), CD62L-PE-Cy7 (BioLegend #304822), and CD45RO-BB700 (BD #566521). Neo-peptide-HLA-tetramers were generated from UV-exchangeable HLA monomers (BioLegend, USA or NIH Tetramer Core) with synthetic minimal peptides (see above), as previously described.19 Five peptide-HLA tetramers were prepared with distinct fluorophores: SMARCA4-APC-Cy7, PIK3CA-APC, TP53-PE, KRAS_9-mer-BV421, and KRAS_10-mer-PE-Cy7. For tetramer staining, the cells were washed in PBS twice and stained sequentially with 5 tetramers for 20 min on ice with one tetramer at a time, with two rounds of FACS buffer (PBS + 4% FBS + 0.5 mM EDTA) washes in between each tetramer staining. After the last tetramer staining, cell surface markers were stained with the cocktail of anti-human anti-CD3-BUV395/CD4-BUV805/CD8-BV605 and anti-mouse TCRβ-FITC antibodies for 20 min. Antibody-stained cells were washed with FACS buffer twice and reconstituted in propidium iodide for acquisition. Tetramer staining was performed on two independently sorted and expanded “ppl” and “psup” products, and representative analysis is shown. Phenotyping and functional assay samples were acquired on FACSymphony (BD Biosciences). Spectral flow cytometry was performed to analyze tetramer staining on Cytek Aurora analyzer (Cytek Biosciences). Gating strategy for each experiment is shown in the corresponding supplemental figures (Figures S1, S4, and S5). FACS was performed where indicated in the text after cell staining with the co-culture staining panel (for antibodies see above) on Fortessa sorter (BD Biosciences). Following dead cell exclusion and single-cell gating, CD3+CD4−CD8+mTCR+ cells were collected for expansion and assay.

Data analysis of TCR-HLA-tetramer binding

Flow cytometry data analysis was performed using FlowJo software v.10.10.0 (TreeStar) and, where indicated, further analyzed by FlowSOM (v.2.5), DownSample (v.3.0), t-SNE (v.2.0), and FlowJo plugins. After cleanup gating (single cells by FSC-A and FSC-H and total lymphocytes by FSC-H and SSC-H), live T cells were identified as propidium iodide− and hCD3+ cells. Then, antigen-specific TCRs (KRAS-9-mer, KRAS-10-mer, TP53, PIK3CA, and SMARCA4) expressing CD8+ T cells were identified as hCD4−, hCD8+, and mTCRB+ cells (Figures 6 and S5). CD8+ T cell composition by antigen-specific TCR expression was analyzed using unsupervised clustering with the FlowSOM plugin. Each sample (pcell, ppl, and psup) was downsampled to 15,000 hCD8+ T cells and concatenated into a single FCS file. FlowSOM analysis was performed on the concatenated file by using five markers (antigen-specific TCR expressions identified by HLA tetramers; see material and methods above or the relevant section): KRAS-9-mer, KRAS-10-mer, TP53, PIK3CA, and SMARCA4. Each cell was assigned to a 20 × 20 grid self-organizing map, resulting in 400 nodes, which were further classified into 24 metaclusters. Each metacluster represented a unique hCD8+ T cell subset expressing the five antigen-specific TCRs at various levels. To visualize hCD8+ T cell subset composition, t-SNE analysis was performed using the five markers and FlowSOM clusters as input parameters. The t-SNE algorithm was run in FlowJo, with 1,000 iterations and a perplexity value of 50. Clusters were overlaid on a two-dimensional plot defined by t-SNE-1 and t-SNE-2 parameters, with distinct colors representing each cluster. Proportions (expressed as percentages of total events in each sample) and the expression levels of each marker (measured as mean fluorescence intensities, MFIs) for each cluster were extracted for further analysis. Individual TCR expression levels by cluster were visualized using histograms, and MFI values were plotted as a heatmap. The heatmap was scaled to a range of 0–1 by using the min-max normalization method.

Cell lines

HPAC pancreatic adenocarcinoma (# CRL-2119) and SNU-1 gastric carcinoma, (#CRL-5971) cell lines were purchased from ATCC between 2016 and 2018. TYK-nu ovarian cancer cell line was purchased from Japanese Cell Bank. For all cell lines, passages between 5 and 20 were used. Mycoplasma testing and cell authenticity were not independently performed as we relied on the commercial vendor’s testing. All tumor cell lines were cultured in tumor cell media (RPMI1640 media [#11875093, Gibco] containing 10% FBS [#SH30071.03HI, Cytiva], 1% sodium pyruvate [#11360070, Gibco], 1% Glutamax [#35050061, Gibco], 1% minimum essential medium [MEM] non-essential amino acids [#11140050], 55 mmol/L 2-mercaptoethanol [#21985023], and 1% Pen/Strep [#15140122, Gibco]). For the killing assays TYK-nu and HPAC cells were fluorescently labeled with IncuCyte NucLight Lentivirus Red Reagent (EF-1 Alpha, Puro) (#4476, Sartorius) as per manufacturer’s instructions. The NucLight-labeled tumor cells, TYK-nu-Nuc and HPAC-Nuc, were cultured in puromycin-containing tumor cell media and used in Incucyte assays after 3 passages to ensure labeled cell purity.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxic T cell killing was assessed with TYK-nu-Nuc and HPAC-Nuc tumor cell co-culture in Incucyte (Sartorius) assay. Tumor cell lines were lifted from the culture flasks with TripLE Express, washed twice, and seeded in 96-well flat bottom tissue culture plates (5,000 cells/well) in 50/50 media. T cells were washed, counted, and normalized to the mTCR+ expression rate, then plated in a 96-well flat bottom tissue culture plate at 4:1, 10:1, and 20:1 E:T ratios as indicated in the figure labels. Samples were plated in quadruplicate for technical repeats. Red fluorescent signal was captured at every 3 h over a 5-day co-culture period by using the Incucyte instrument. Data analysis was performed by Incucyte Basic Analyzer software (Sartorius) based on the total fluorescence area. The mean values of four technical replicates were plotted.

VCN/ddPCR assay

DNA extraction was performed with DNeasy blood and tissue kit (Qiagen, #69506) Total DNA was quantified using the QX200 AutoDG Droplet Digital PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Primers and probe sequences specific to each TCR CDR3 region were designed. Each reaction was prepared in a final volume of 20 μL, consisting of 900 nM forward and reverse primers, 250 nM probe, 1× ddPCR SuperMix for probes (Bio-Rad cat # 1863024), and template DNA. The samples were loaded into the DG32 Automated Droplet Generator Cartridges (Bio-Rad). Automated Droplet Generation Oil for Probes (no dUTP) (Bio-Rad, #1864110) was then added, and droplets were generated using the Automated Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad). Droplets were transferred to a 96-well PCR plate and placed into a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler with the 96-deep well reaction module (Bio-Rad). PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 10 min at 95°C for DNA polymerase activation, followed by 40 cycles of 30 s at 94°C for denaturation and 1 min at 55°C for annealing and extension, and ending at 98°C for 10 min for DNA polymerase deactivation and 4°C for cooling. PCR plates were then loaded into the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad), and droplet signal was read as being either positive or negative amplification. Data were collected using the QX Manager software (v.2.3 Standard Edition, Bio-Rad).
