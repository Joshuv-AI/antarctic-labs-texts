Enrichment of T: A→A: T substitutions in the TP53 gene in esophageal carcinoma with high Fusobacterium burden
Authors: Tomomitsu Tahara, Takuya Shijimaya, Jumpei Yamazaki, Tsubasa Shimogama, Sanshiro Kobayashi, Naohiro Nakamura, Yu Takahashi, Takashi Tomiyama, Toshiro Fukui, Tomoyuki Shibata, Makoto Naganuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExogenous lifestyle and environmental risk factors impact mutation burden in both benign and cancerous tissues. Moreover, interactions between the mucosa-associated microbiome and such genotoxic changes have been implicated in several cancer types. This study aimed to characterize the TP53 mutation spectrum in esophageal carcinoma (EC) with high amounts of Fusobacterium species. Quantitative PCR analysis of Pan-Fusobacterium species and Fusobacterium nucleatum was performed in 112 EC cases (89 squamous cell carcinomas [SCCs] and 23 adenocarcinomas). Results were correlated with TP53 mutation spectra and clinicopathological features. Both Pan-Fusobacterium species and F. nucleatum were significantly enriched in EC tissues compared with adjacent normal esophagus (both P < 0.001). The TP53 mutation spectrum in Pan-fusobacterium-high EC was characterized by a decrease in C: G→A: T and an increase in T: A→A: T substitutions (P < 0.001). Pan-fusobacterium-high EC was associated with male sex, tumors located in the upper or middle esophagus, and squamous cell histopathology. Cases of EC with detectable levels of Pan-Fusobacterium and F. nucleatum were also associated with more invasive tumors and more advanced cancer stages at diagnosis. These results suggest that EC with high numbers of Fusobacterium species was associated with a distinct mutation spectrum. Future studies should be conducted to investigate the mechanisms of how Fusobacterium species induce specific somatic mutations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45806-5.Keywords: Esophageal carcinoma, Fusobacterium species, TP53 mutation, mutation spectrumSubject terms: Cancer, Genetics

Introduction

Esophageal carcinoma (EC) is an aggressive malignancy, with an annual incidence of 600 000 cases worldwide1. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common histologic subtype of EC in Eastern and Central Asia, accounting for about 80% of the total cases2. Esophageal adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype in Western countries, accounting for more than half of EC diagnoses3–5; its incidence rate is also gradually increasing in Japan6,7. Given the poor prognosis of late-stage EC, 3–5,8,9 deeper knowledge of the pathogenesis that drives the transition from normal epithelium to EC may provide new therapeutic alternatives and improve diagnostic and prognostic tools.

Growing evidence links abnormal changes in the gastrointestinal (GI) microbiota to several GI 10–12 and extra-intestinal diseases13–16, and various types of cancers17,18. One of the most studied examples is colon carcinoma, in which the GI microbiota has been shown to promote carcinogenesis by altering the tumor immuno-environment19,20. One previous study reported a relationship between intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. and colon carcinoma, identifying specific molecular changes such as epigenetic anomalies and a higher somatic mutation rate21. Although the presence of Fusobacterium has been associated with poor prognosis in esophageal SCC22, another study revealed that genotoxic feature of esophageal SCC, such as hypomethylation of long interspersed nuclear element-1 (LINE-1), a marker of global DNA hypomethylation, were significantly associated with the presence of Fusobacterium spp. in this tumor type23.

Exogenous lifestyle and environmental risk factors exposures have been shown to impact the mutation burden in both benign and cancerous tissues24,25. Moreover, dysbiosis of the mucosa-associated microbiome has been proposed as a driver of genotoxic alterations in several cancer types21,22. In this study, we investigated the association between EC mutation signatures and mucosa-associated bacteria composition, specifically Fusobacterium spp. Because TP53 is the most frequently mutated cancer gene in EC, we further aimed to characterize the specific TP53 mutation spectrum25 in relation to intratumoral Fusobacterium status.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This study examined 112 patients with EC at the Endoscopy Center of Fujita Health University and Kansai Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles included in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the Fujita Health University (ID: HM18-094) and the Kansai Medical University (ID: 2021046). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Clinical information was obtained from medical records. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy was performed for all patients, and endoscopic biopsies were obtained from EC tissues as well as normal-appearing esophageal mucosa.

Biopsy specimens were carefully cut into two pieces: one for pathological evaluation and the other immediately frozen and stored at − 80 °C for molecular analysis. Absence of dysplasia was confirmed in all non-cancerous esophageal tissues, and pathological assessment of tumor specimens confirmed that > 70% of cells were cancerous.

Quantitative PCR analysis for Fusobacterium

Genomic DNA was extracted from the frozen biopsy specimens using the genomic DNA precipitation method. Biopsy specimens were incubated overnight at 55 °C in a lysis solution containing 25mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 2% SDS, and proteinase K. The lysate was then precipitated with isopropanol and washed with 80% ethanol. Finally, the genomic DNA was suspended in TE buffer (pH 8.0). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis for Fusobacterium was performed using the iTaq™ Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and Rotor-Gene Q Real-Time PCR System (QIAGEN, Tokyo, Japan). TaqMan primer/probe sets for Pan-Fusobacterium (whole genus) and Fusobacterium nucleatum have been described previously26,27. For cases with detectable Pan-Fusobacterium or F. nucleatum, the cycle threshold values were normalized to the amount of human DNA in each reaction. To determine the relative abundance of Fusobacterium in mucosal samples21, a primer/probe set targeting the reference gene and prostaglandin transporter was used, as described previously28 and are also listed in the Supplementary Table 1.

For cases without detectable Pan-Fusobacterium or F. nucleatum, the successful amplification of prostaglandin transporter was confirmed, and these samples were considered bacteria-undetectable cases. The primer and probe assays used for F. nucleatum, Pan-Fusobacterium, and prostaglandin transporter are listed in the Supporting Information Table S1. All assays were performed in duplicate and the results were averaged. All PCR reactions were conducted under the same conditions as our previous study21.

Mutation analysis of TP53 gene

Exons 5–8 from TP53 were amplified from EC tissues and matched to non-neoplastic Barrett’s esophagus tissues using PCR. These PCR fragments were sequenced using Sanger sequencing. The PCR and sequence primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The sequence chromatograms were visually inspected with DNA Dynamo Sequence Analysis Software (Blue Tractor Software, Llanfair-fachan, Wales, UK). All mutations were confirmed by independent PCR and sequencing reactions. Only the nucleotide variations present in the tumor samples were considered mutations; variations detected in adjacent non-neoplastic tissues were excluded. Single nucleotide substitutions were analyzed using SIFT (sorting intolerant from tolerant) to predict potential effects on protein function. Mutation patterns were classified using a 6- or 96-substitution classification that was based on the substitution type and the immediate 5’ and 3’ sequence context. Information about substitutions at specific dinucleotides, such as 5’-CpG-3’ and 5’-TpC-3’, were also obtained.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for matched groups and Student’s t-test for unmatched groups. Categorical variables were compared between groups using the chi-square test.

Enrichment of Pan-Fusobacterium and F. nucleatum in EC tissues in relation to their clinicopathological characteristics and TP53 mutation spectrum

We examined Pan-Fusobacterium and F. nucleatum in 122 endoscopic biopsies from EC tissues and the adjacent normal mucosa. Both types of bacteria were heavily enriched in EC compared to the adjacent normal tissue (Pan-Fusobacterium, P = 0.0001; F. nucleatum, P = 0.0003; Fig. 1). Sanger sequencing identified 86 TP53 somatic mutations in 77 EC cases (68.8%). The detailed information for all TP53 somatic mutations is shown in Supplementary Table 3.

We also investigated whether Pan-Fusobacterium and F. nucleatum status of EC tissue was associated with TP53 mutation positivity and its specific mutation types. Pan-Fusobacterium was more frequently detected in EC tissues (83.9%, 94/112) than F. nucleatum (37.5%, 42/112), and its abundance was substantially higher (Fig. 1). Moreover, the distribution of Pan-Fusobacterium in EC tissue approximated a Gaussian distribution but was skewed toward lower values; therefore, we used a cutoff value of 0.04 (2^dCt) to define lower Pan-Fusobacterium-high tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1). Pan-Fusobacterium-high tumors were significantly associated with detectable levels of F. nucleatum (P = 0.00005, Supplementary Table 4).

Finally, we investigated the association between Pan-Fusobacterium-high status, F. nucleatum detectability, and TP53 mutation status including its substitution pattern. Although TP53 mutations seemed to be higher in Pan-Fusobacterium-high tumors than in others, this association was not statistically significant (85.7% vs. 64.8%, P = 0.06, Table 2). No similar trend was observed for cases with detectable F. nucleatum. We then further investigated whether Pan-Fusobacterium-high status, F. nucleatum detectable EC cases were associated with specific TP53 substitution patterns. A mutation pattern was depicted using a six substitutions classification such as C: G to A: T, C: G to G: C, C: G to T: A, T: A to A: T, T: A to C: G and T: A to G: C (Table 2). Mutation patterns were further classified into 96-substitution classification, grouped by whether an A, C, G or T respectively is 5’ to the mutated base, and within each group of four by whether A, C, G or T is 3’ to the mutated base (Supplementary Fig. 2). Pan-Fusobacterium-high EC cases showed significant increases of T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions compared with other cases (38.9% vs. 5.1%, P < 0.001). Importantly, SIFT analysis predicted that all T: A→A: T substitutions would result in truncated proteins (Supplementary Table 3). Although C: G→A: T single nucleotide substitutions appeared decreased in Pan-Fusobacterium-high compared to Pan-Fusobacterium-low tumors (5.6% vs. 22%), this association was not statistically significant (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2).

F. nucleatum-detectable cases also showed a trend toward increased T: A→A: T substitutions over F. nucleatum-undetectable cases (21.4% vs. 8.2%), although this association did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06, Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2).

We also investigated TP53 mutations at specific dinucleotide sites: 5’-CpG-3’ and 5’-TpC-3’. This analysis showed that Pan-Fusobacterium-high cases appeared to have more mutations at 5′-TpC-3′ than Pan-Fusobacterium-low, although the association was not statistically significant (22.2% vs. 6.8%, P = 0.06). In contrast, cases with detectable F. nucleatum mutations showed a significant enrichment of mutations at 5’-CpG-3’ sites over those without detectable F. nucleatum (35.7% vs. 16.3%, P = 0.02). In Pan-Fusobacterium-high and F. nucleatum-detectable cases, mutations at other sites were less frequent compared with the remaining tumors; however, this association was not statistically significant (50% vs. 72.9%, P = 0.06 for Pan-fusobacterium; 50% vs. 72.9%, P = 0.06 for F. nucleatum, Table 2).

Association between Pan-fusobacterium-high, F. nucleatum detectable EC cases and their clinicopathological characteristics

We next investigated whether Pan-fusobacterium-high and F. nucleatum detectable cases were associated with various clinicopathological characteristics of EC (Table 3).The results demonstrated that Pan-Fusobacterium-high EC was significantly associated with male sex (P = 0.02), location in the upper and middle esophagus (P = 0.017), and SCC histopathology (P < 0.05). Pan-Fusobacterium-high was also significantly associated with more aggressive clinicopathological features of EC, including deeper tumor invasion (T2, T3, and T4, P = 0.001), lymphatic invasion (P = 0.004), and advanced stage at diagnosis (stages II–IV; P = 0.002). Similarly, F. nucleatum-detectable EC was significantly associated with tumors located in the upper and middle esophagus (P = 0.01), deeper invasion (T2, T3 and T4, P = 0.007), lymph node metastasis (P = 0.003), and advanced stage at diagnosis (stages II–IV, P = 0.002). Although smoking history and alcohol consumption were not associated with Pan-fusobacterium-high nor F. nucleatum detectable cases, both factors are considered as major risk factors for EC. Since Pan-Fusobacterium-high EC cases showed significant increases of T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions (Table 2). We investigated whether these specific mutations would be associated with smoking history and alcohol consumption, but results were both negative (Supplementary Table 5), suggesting that these variables do not act as confounders in the association between Pan-fusobacterium status and the TP53 T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions.

Discussion

Epidemiological studies have identified several potential risk factors for EC, which vary by histological subtype. Alcohol consumption is strongly associated with SCC29,30,31, and high body mass index is a major risk factor for adenocarcinoma32,33. Smoking is a major risk factor for both. The study of mutational processes in human cancers and normal tissues has revealed that both exogenous mutagenic exposures and endogenous processes generate distinctive patterns of somatic mutations. 33,34 Some environmental risk factors have been associated with such mutational signatures24,25, but the etiology for many other signatures remains unknown.

Our data showed that Pan-fusobacterium-high cases of EC showed a distinct TP53 mutation spectrum, which notably included T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions. A similar trend was also observed in F. nucleatum-detectable cases. Previous studies have reported that C: G→A: T substitutions are associated with smoking25,35, while T: A→C: G substitutions are associated with alcohol consumption25. However, these prior studies did not address the T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions. Our findings suggest that intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. may have a role in shaping a previously unreported mutation spectrum. These findings raise the possibility that Fusobacterium spp. may contribute to tumorigenesis and could be associated with specific somatic mutations in EC, such as T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions in TP53. One might also speculate that EC with more genomic instability might be more permissive to Fusobacterium colonization.

One previous study linked intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. to specific molecular changes in cancers. For example, colorectal cancer tissues with a high level of Fusobacterium also had a high degree of CpG island methylation and a high rate of somatic mutations21. Another study indicated a potential association between intratumoral Fusobacterium and long LINE-1 hypomethylation status23, which has been linked to chromosomal instability and somatic mutations36,37. Taken together with these previous studies, our results indicate that interaction between mucosa-associated microbiome and genotoxic changes may have a role in promoting cancers with distinct molecular subtypes.

Stratification analysis showed that Pan-Fusobacterium-high EC was associated with several clinicopathological characteristics such as male sex, location in the upper and middle esophagus, and SCC histopathology. Moreover, Pan-Fusobacterium-high and F. nucleatum-detectable cases were associated with more invasive tumors and a more advanced stage at diagnosis. These trends suggested that high amounts of this bacteria were associated with more aggressive cancer features, which was in line with other studies22,38.

The mechanistic consequence of Fusobacterium spp. in more advanced tumors can be considered in the context of emerging evidence linking neoplastic cells with a permissive microenvironment that alters host immune responses19,20. In esophageal SCC, Fusobacterium spp. have been implicated in the activation of NF-κB and NOD1/RIPK2 pathways39, suggesting that an altered tumor immuno-environment may be the key to defining aggressive tumor behavior. Moreover, such an immuno-environment may be also relevant to the development of specific somatic mutation patterns. However, to our knowledge, our study is the first to report a potential association between Fusobacterium enrichment and a distinct mutation type in EC, specifically T: A→A: T single nucleotide substitutions. At this preliminary stage, it remains unclear how the interactions between Fusobacterium enrichment and these specific mutations influence the biological behavior of EC. Our findings mainly demonstrated correlations and did not provide experimental validation of microbial mechanisms. Detailed mechanisms by which Fusobacterium accelerates specific molecular and clinical features need to be clarified.

This study had several limitations. First, it included a relatively small number of patients in only a few institutions. Our findings linking Pan-Fusobacterium enrichment, distinct TP53 mutation spectra, and clinical characteristics of EC raise the possibility that Fusobacterium species other than F. nucleatum could be involved in disease pathogenesis. However, the specific mechanisms about this potential association remains unclear. Our study included 112 EC cases, but subgroup analyses produced small sample sizes, which reduced statistical power. Also, the possibility of Type I error could not be completely excluded. In the current data set, the frequency of TP53 somatic mutations was 68.8% (77/112) which was lower than other published data25. One study reported that TP53 mutations predominantly occurred in exons 5–8 in cancers40, while another reported TP53 mutations in EC occurred in exons 2, 3, 10, and 11 41. Therefore, the discrepancy in our data might have occurred because we only focused on exons 5–8. Currently, there is very little experimental evidences of how Fusobacterium may contribute to the development of specific histological variations of EC. We speculated that interaction between mucosa-associated microbiome and genotoxic changes appeared to be a mandatory step in the oncogenesis of distinct subtypes in EC, and further study of factors driving somatic mutations, as well as in-depth studies of EC tumor evolution, are likely to yield additional insights.
