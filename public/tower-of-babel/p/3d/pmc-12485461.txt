Steroid rotation in persistent hiccups associated with recurrent intra-articular glenohumeral dexamethasone injection
Authors: Alper Mengi, Melis Dilbil
Published: 2025

TO THE EDITOR

A 51-year-old male patient presented to the pain management clinic with an eight-month history of left shoulder pain and limited range of motion. He had previously undergone a 15-day rehabilitation program, which did not result in any pain relief. The pain intensity was rated as 7/10 on the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Shoulder ranges of motions were limited and painful in all directions. For pain palliation, the patient underwent an ultrasound (USG)-guided intra-articular glenohumeral injection of 8 mg dexamethasone in 2 mL mixed with 5 mL saline with the diagnosis of frozen shoulder. Approximately 24 hours post-injection, the patient developed persistent hiccups occurring 5–6 times per minute, lasting for about 60 hours. The hiccups were particularly exacerbated after eating, with only relief periods of 30 minutes occurring 1–2 times per day. The hiccups resolved spontaneously without intervention. Comprehensive laboratory tests and imaging studies, including brain, cervical, thoracic, and abdominal computed tomography, did not reveal any pathology.

Three weeks after the initial injection, the patient’s NRS was 6/10. A repeat USG-guided intra-articular dexamethasone injection was performed for pain relief. The patient developed persistent hiccups approximately 24 hours after the injection. The hiccups occurred 5–6 times per minute and lasted for about 72 hours. There were 2–3 brief hiccup relief periods per day, each lasting approximately 30 minutes. The hiccups eventually resolved spontaneously. Due to the recurrence of persistent hiccups and inadequate pain relief (NRS 6/10), the patient was started on lornoxicam, tramadol hydrochloride, and paracetamol, but without sufficient response. Consequently, oral methylprednisolone 16 mg/day was initiated. At the second-week follow-up, the NRS score improved to 1/10. The patient received methylprednisolone at 16 mg/day for 3 weeks. The dose was gradually tapered and discontinued over a 10-day period. Notably, the patient did not experience hiccups while on oral methylprednisolone therapy.

Hiccups result from sudden, involuntary contractions of the diaphragm and inspiratory muscles. Persistent hiccups are defined as lasting more than 48 hours and can significantly impact quality of life [1]. Among corticosteroids, dexamethasone has been most frequently associated with hiccups, possibly due to its effect on lowering the synaptic transmission threshold in the midbrain, thereby triggering hiccups [1,2]. While steroid use is common in medical practice, the exact incidence of steroid-induced hiccups remains unknown [2].

A review of the literature revealed a similar case in which a patient with frozen shoulder developed hiccups following a blind intra-articular betamethasone injection. In that case, hiccups began 12 hours post-injection and lasted 24 hours, with relief achieved via the Valsalva maneuver [3]. Another case report described hiccups following intravenous dexamethasone administration for lumbar disc pathology, where repeated doses led to increased hiccup severity, which resolved within 72 hours after discontinuing dexamethasone [4].

Treatment options for steroid-induced hiccups include pharmacological agents such as chlorpromazine, metoclopramide, and baclofen [5]. However, the primary concern is the management of steroid-induced hiccups in patients requiring corticosteroid therapy. In a study by Lee et al. [2], patients undergoing chemotherapy who developed dexamethasone-induced hiccups were successfully managed with methylprednisolone rotation, resulting in a significant reduction in both hiccup incidence and severity. Similarly, in our case, after two episodes of persistent hiccups following intra-articular dexamethasone injections, we opted to discontinue further injections and instead transitioned to oral methylprednisolone, which provided effective pain palliation without inducing hiccups. The disappearance of persistent hiccups after transitioning from dexamethasone to methylprednisolone may be related to the varying permeability of the blood-brain barrier to corticosteroids. The lower permeability of the blood-brain barrier to methylprednisolone may explain the resolution of the hiccups [2,5]. Due to the different administration routes, we could not ascertain whether oral dexamethasone would have similarly triggered hiccups. However, literature suggests that the route of dexamethasone administration may influence hiccup occurrence [5].

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first reported case of hiccups following intra-articular glenohumeral dexamethasone injection, with recurrence upon reinjection. Through this case, we emphasize the importance of steroid rotation in patients experiencing dexamethasone-induced hiccups after intra-articular administration. This experience highlights the potential benefit of considering alternative corticosteroid options in patients who develop hiccups following intra-articular dexamethasone use.
