Association Between the Root Canal Microbiome and Apical Lesion Size: An Observational Shotgun Metagenomic Study
Authors: Virginia Pérez‐Carrasco, David Uroz‐Torres, Ana Soriano‐Lerma, Miguel Soriano, Jose Antonio García‐Salcedo, Maria Teresa Arias‐Moliz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe aim was to characterize the taxonomic and functional composition of the microbiome involved in primary endodontic infections and to evaluate their association with the periapical lesion size using shotgun metagenomic sequencing.MethodologySamples from primary root canal infections diagnosed with apical periodontitis were analysed with shotgun sequencing. Samples were classified according to the lesion size as small (< 3 mm) or large (> 7 mm). The bacterial DNA copies in each group were quantified by qPCR. Taxonomic and functional annotations were made using Bracken/Kraken2 and HUMAnN3 software. Species richness, Shannon, Simpson and Pielou indices were used to measure alpha diversity. The similarity of the bacterial communities between study groups was evaluated by Principal Coordinate Analysis based on Bray–Curtis distances. The ALDEx2 package was used to infer the differences between species, and the edgeR package for KEGG pathways. For all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was considered as significant.ResultsA total of 49 samples were analysed, 27 with small lesions and 22 with large lesions. Species richness and Shannon indices showed differences between both groups, whereas no differences were seen according to Simpson and Pielou indices. A different community composition (PERMANOVA, p = 0.0019) was observed between the two groups. Three species were significantly enriched in the large lesion samples, Filifactor alocis , Lachnospiraceae bacterium oral taxon 500 and Olsenella uli , while three others were enriched in small lesion samples, Acinetobacter baumannii , Acinetobacter pittii and Cutibacterium acnes. Functionally, benzoate, flavonoid and steroid degradation, the sphingolipid signalling pathway and proteasome function were enriched in samples with large lesions. Monoterpenoid biosynthesis, phospholipase D signalling, the sulphur relay system and staurosporine biosynthesis were enriched in small lesions.ConclusionsTeeth with large periapical lesions harbour greater bacterial loads and exhibit a more diverse microbial community than those with small lesions. Differences in species‐level taxonomic composition were observed between both groups. Functionally, large lesions are enriched in pathways associated with immune evasion and pro‐inflammatory activity, whereas small lesions are characterized by pathways related to apoptosis, metabolic adaptation and anti‐inflammatory processes. These findings suggest that lesion severity is also shaped by the functional potential of the microbiome to modulate host inflammation.

Introduction

Apical periodontitis is an inflammatory condition of the periradicular tissues that arises as a consequence of microbial colonization of the root canal system. It is believed that both infection and host defences play definitive roles in determining the biological outcome of the root canal treatment. From a clinical perspective, the lesion size has been associated with prognosis, as teeth presenting with larger radiographic periapical lesions tend to show lower success rates and a higher likelihood of persistent disease when compared with teeth exhibiting smaller lesions (Ng et al. 2008; Restrepo‐Restrepo et al. 2019). Despite this association, the microbial mechanisms and functional pathways contributing to the lesion progress remain unclear.

Studies based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing reported that the microbiome associated with primary endodontic infections shows high diversity, with intra‐ and inter‐individual differences (Siqueira Jr et al. 2011; Tzanetakis et al. 2015; Sánchez‐Sanhueza et al. 2018; Ordinola‐Zapata et al. 2023). It is also accepted that no specific pathogen is responsible for the development of disease, and different microbial communities having similar functional profiles can cause the same pathogenic outcome (Simón‐Soro and Mira 2015), such as necrosis of the pulp tissue and the development of apical periodontitis. Although 16S rRNA gene sequencing has contributed substantially to the understanding of the endodontic microbiome, this approach provides limited taxonomic resolution and is subject to methodological biases related to PCR amplification and primer selection. Furthermore, it overlooks relevant information regarding the species‐level diversity and the functional capabilities of microorganisms present in the ecosystem (Hong et al. 2009; Hillmann et al. 2018; Baker et al. 2021).

Microbial functional traits play a central role in pathogenicity and persistence. Virulence‐associated factors, including toxins, enzymes, surface structures and metabolic by‐products, enable microorganisms to evade host defences, promote inflammation and contribute to tissue degradation (Dahlen et al. 2019). In endodontic infections, bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide and lipoteichoic acid have been associated with the initiation and maintenance of periapical inflammation (Gomes et al. 2012; Gomes and Herrera 2018). Nevertheless, a broader understanding of the functional pathways that sustain periapical disease is still lacking.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing overcomes many of the constraints associated with amplicon‐based approaches by enabling comprehensive analysis of all microbial genetic material present within a sample. This technique allows for species‐ and strain‐level taxonomic identification, while simultaneously providing detailed information on genes involved in metabolic and virulence‐related pathways that cannot be assessed through 16S rRNA sequencing alone (Ranjan et al. 2016). This technique could provide a better understanding of the association between the microorganisms, including their functional profile, and the progress of a lesion. The objective of the present study was therefore to characterize the taxonomic and functional composition of the microbiome involved in primary endodontic infections and to evaluate their association with the periapical lesion size using shotgun metagenomic sequencing. The null hypothesis was that no differences would be observed in microbial composition, gene content, or metabolic pathways between teeth presenting with small and large periradicular lesions.

Material and Methods

The present work is an observational study reported under the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) 2023 guidelines (Nagendrababu et al. 2023). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institution where the study was performed (ref: 2914/CEIH/2022). All patients that agreed to participate signed an informed consent form before their inclusion in the study.

Subjects and Specimens

Samples were obtained from patients attending a private practice. Included in the study were teeth with primary endodontic infections showing pulp necrosis and symptomatic and asymptomatic apical periodontitis confirmed by dental history, clinical examination and radiographic assessment. The absence of sensibility was confirmed by cold test (Villa‐Chávez et al. 2013), and mechanical allodynia was determined by percussion test as sensitive (symptomatic) or nonsensitive (asymptomatic). Only teeth with fully formed apices in patients 18–65 years old were considered. Patients having severe systemic diseases and teeth with periodontal pockets deeper than 4 mm, root fracture, or history of trauma were excluded.

In order to calculate the lesion size, CBCT scans of each tooth included in the study were taken with Carestream CS Select equipment (CS 81003D, Carestream Dental LLC, Atlanta, GA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions under standard exposure settings (84 kV and 5 mA). The voxel size was 75 μm, with a field of view of 5 × 5 cm and an exposure time of 20s. The images were reconstructed using CS 3D Imaging Software (CareStream 3D Imaging software, Rochester, NY, USA), and the lesion size recorded was the largest diameter of the radiolucent area located in the apical or lateral area of the root.

Primary infection samples were obtained before root canal treatment. In order to avoid external contamination, after rubber dam isolation, the caries and defective restorations were removed with a sterile bur (Komet, Lemgo, Germany). The crowns were then decontaminated with 5% sodium hypochlorite (Panreac Química, Castellar del Vallès, Spain) for 3 min and inactivated with 10% sodium thiosulfate (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, United States) for 1 min. The effectiveness of the decontamination protocol was verified by sampling the clinical crown (decontamination control sample) for subsequent 16S rRNA amplicon analysis. Teeth were accessed using sterile diamond burs, after which the walls of the cavity were decontaminated under the same protocol as described above. Microbiological pre‐operative samples were taken from the necrotic root canal space using a sterile rotary file (Endogal, Sarria, Spain) followed by three sterile paper points (Endogal), all inserted approximately 1–2 mm short of the estimated working length as previously determined by CBCT scans. The rotary instrument was removed from the contrangle using a sterile tweezer and it was carefully placed, together with the paper points, in a tube containing 500 μL of Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 8.0; Panreac Quimica, Barcelona, Spain). Samples were stored at −20°C until they were processed. In multirooted teeth, the sample was obtained from the root showing the periapical lesion. In the event of more than one root with an associated lesion in molars, the sample was taken from the distal or palatal canal.

The sample size was estimated by conducting a statistical power analysis based on preliminary data using GPower 3.1 software (University of Kiel). It was also based on the sample size of a previously published study (Ordinola‐Zapata et al. 2024). Finally, the statistical power obtained with the included sample size was assessed after data collection using the same software.

DNA Extraction

The bacterial DNA was extracted using a QIAmp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the protocol recommended by the manufacturer, preceded by a 30‐min preincubation step with lysozyme. Blank extraction controls were included in each extraction batch to check for any possible contamination. DNA quality and quantification were performed using a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 2000 UV—Vis; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

qPCR Assay

To quantify the total bacterial load in the samples, 16S rRNA gene‐targeted qPCR was performed using the QuantStudio 6 High‐throughput Realtime PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Power SYBR green PCR (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA) was used for the master mix in a total reaction mixture volume of 10 μL. The universal bacterial primers were 908 F: 5′‐AAACTCAAAKGAATTGACGGGG‐3′ and 1096 R: 5′‐GGGTTGCGCTCGTTRYGG‐3′. Primers in a final concentration of 0.5 μM each and DNA extract volume of 1 μL were added to the PCR master mix, in MicroAmp Fast 96‐Well reaction plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cycling conditions included 95°C for 10 min and 40 cycles consisting of denaturalization at 95°C for 15 s and annealing at 60°C for 1 min. Negative controls containing no template DNA were subjected to the same procedures. The number of DNA copies per sample was obtained based on standard curves, for which known concentrations of Escherichia coli 16S gene were used. All samples, negative controls (decontamination control samples, blank extraction controls and qPCR negative controls), and the standard curve were analysed in duplicate. Results were expressed as the mean of Log10 DNA copies.

Shotgun Sequencing and Bioinformatic Analysis

Libraries were prepared using the DNA protocol (1000000025416, Illumina, USA) with 25–500 ng of input DNA, according to the manufacturer's instructions. All samples were quantified using the Qubit system 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and pooled according to the lowest concentration. The final pool was sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 (300 cycles, S2) sequencer (Illumina, USA), giving a total of 1106 M raw reads. Negative extraction and amplification controls were included to control for external contamination. These controls were evaluated after PCR during library preparation to ensure they did not undergo amplification. Furthermore, all negative controls were sequenced, guaranteeing a minimal number of reads. The raw sequencing data obtained were uploaded to the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) of the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under Bioproject accession number PRJNA1054361. To assess whether the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the microbial diversity, rarefaction curves were generated using the rarecurve function from the vegan package in R statistical software.

Quality processing of fastq files was performed with the KneadData pipeline. Trimmomatic was used for the quality trimming, and Bowtie2 was used to remove host contaminating sequences belonging to human genomes. Taxonomic and functional annotations were made using Bracken/Kraken2 and HUMAnN3. Translated search and functional profiling was performed using the DIAMOND aligner and UNIPROT database, clustering sequences at 90% of homology to ensure good representation of gene families in the UniRef clusters; gene families were then regrouped using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthologs database.

Statistical Analysis

After checking the absence of normality in Log10 DNA copies and in the diversity indexes with the Shapiro–Wilk test, statistical differences between teeth with small and large lesions were analysed by the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test using GraphPad Prism v8 software (GraphPad Software). The similarity of the bacterial communities between study groups was evaluated by Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA), based on Bray‐Curtis distances and implemented in PRIMERe Permanova+ (PRIMER‐E Ltd.). A multivariant PERMANOVA test was also carried out using PRIMERe Permanova+ with square root transformation and permutation of residuals under a reduced model (9999 permutations). Differential abundance analyses were performed using ALDEx2 for species‐level comparisons and edgeR (v4.0.16) for KEGG pathway analysis, where count data were modelled using negative binomial generalized linear models (GLMs) with empirical Bayes dispersion estimation; statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For both taxonomic (ALDEx2) and functional (edgeR) differential abundance analyses, p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. For all statistical analysis, p < 0.05 was considered as significant.

Results

The collected samples were classified according to their lesion size into small (< 3 mm) or large (> 7 mm). A total of 73 samples were collected. Nine samples were excluded because they had intermediate lesion sizes between 3 and 7 mm. Of the remaining 64 samples, 15 were excluded because they showed no significant PCR amplification or an insufficient number of sequences (Figure 1). Therefore, a total of 49 samples were finally included, 27 with small lesions and 22 with large lesions. A statistical power of 0.85 was obtained according to the detected effect size (G*Power v.3.1.9.7 software). Bacterial Load in Small and Large Lesions

A higher bacterial Log10 DNA copy count was observed in large lesions as compared to small lesions (p = 0.0195), indicating that a greater number of bacteria are present in the root canals associated with large lesions (Figure 2). To ensure the absence of external contaminants in the samples, decontamination control samples and amplification negative controls were also included, showing no significant amplification with respect to the study samples.

Bacterial Log10 DNA copies obtained from qPCR analysis of small and large lesion‐associated samples. Differences between the two groups were assessed by Mann–Whitney test (*p < 0.05).

Microbial Diversity and Bacterial Communities in Small and Large Lesions

After bioinformatic processing and human DNA removal, an average of 20 M reads per sample were obtained. Disinfection, extraction and amplification controls were sequenced, showing a small number of sequences. Rarefaction curves showed that all samples reached a clear plateau indicating that the taxonomic richness was fully captured regardless of the total number of reads per sample (Figure S1).

In order to calculate the alpha diversity indices, data were normalized to minimum depth of sequencing. The microbiome in samples with large lesions showed a higher alpha diversity according to species richness and Shannon indexes (p < 0.05). No differences were observed in Simpson and Pielu indices (p > 0.05) (Figure 3). PCoA analysis revealed two distinct clusters corresponding to either group, which was confirmed by a statistically different community composition at bacterial species level in large versus small lesion‐associated primary infections (PERMANOVA, p = 0.0019) (Figure 4).

Alpha diversity indices (species richness, Shannon, Simpson and Pielou) of the microbiome in samples with small and large lesions. Differences between the two sites were assessed by Mann–Whitney test (*0.01 < p < 0.05).

Principal coordinate (PCo) analysis of the samples with small (pink) and large lesions (burgundy) at species level. Samples are represented by dots.

At the phylum level, Proteobacteria exhibited the highest relative abundance (29.96%), followed by Firmicutes (27.23%), Bacteroidetes (23.10%), Actinobacteria (9.85%), Fusobacteria (5.35%) and Spirochaetes (3.92%). Proteobacteria were more abundant in small lesions than in large lesions. In contrast, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria were relatively enriched in large lesions (Figure S2). At the species level, the microorganisms found in both groups at a relative abundance > 0.8% are shown in Figure 5, with Parvimonas micra , Acinetobacter baumannii , Filifactor alocis , Olsenella uli , A. nosocomialis , Lawsonella clevelandensis, Lactobacillus gasseri and Eubacterium minutum being the ones that show relative abundances > 3%.

Relative abundance of bacterial species found in abundances > 0.8% in samples with small and large lesions.

The identification of the species showing statistical differences between the two groups was assessed by Aldex2 analysis. Three species were significantly enriched in large lesion samples, F. alocis , Lachnospiraceae bacterium oral taxon 500 and O. uli (Figure 6A), while three were enriched in small lesion samples, including A. baumannii , A. pittii and Cutibacterium acnes (Figure 6B). The Spearman test showed that these six species were correlated with the lesion size: the former three species positively correlated with large lesion samples and negatively correlated with small lesion samples, and viceversa for the latter three species (Figure 7). Additionally, these species had a high representation in the infections, with relative abundances of 17.82% A. baumannii, 11.76% F. alocis , 7.75% O. uli, 2.35% Lachnospiraceae bacterium oral taxon 500, 0.84% C. acnes, and 0.57% A. pittii .

Spearman correlation between the altered bacterial species and the lesion size. The heatmap represents Spearman's rho values for pairwise correlations among lesion size and altered bacterial species. Asterisks indicate statistically significant correlations (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01).

The results of the functional analysis showed that five different metabolic pathways were enriched in samples with large lesions including benzoate, flavonoid, and steroid degradation, as well as the sphingolipid signalling pathway and proteasome function, whereas five others, tetracycline and monoterpenoid biosynthesis, phospholipase D signalling, the sulphur relay system, and staurosporine biosynthesis were enriched in small lesions (Figure 8).

Discussion

The biological aim of root canal treatment is to allow healing of periapical tissues or prevent its development. The size of a lesion has been considered a prognostic factor for treatment failure (López‐Valverde et al. 2023; Ng et al. 2008), highlighting the role of persistent bacteria in treatment outcome. The analysis of the microorganisms and their genes will improve the understanding of the association between the microbiome and the progress of periapical infections. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was selected because it has multiple advantages compared with 16S rRNA sequencing, including enhanced detection of bacterial species and genes and increased detection of diversity (Ranjan et al. 2016).

In the present study, lesions were classified as small (< 3 mm) and large (> 7 mm) based on CBCT measurements to enable comparison between clearly distinct stages of periapical disease. This approach was intended to enhance group contrast and facilitate the detection of potential differences in microbial composition. The selected thresholds were informed by the existing literature (Restrepo‐Restrepo et al. 2019; Ng et al. 2008), where lesion size has been associated with disease severity and success rate, traditionally assessed using periapical radiographs. Given the higher sensitivity and three‐dimensional accuracy of CBCT, smaller lesions can be detected more reliably, supporting the use of a lower threshold to identify early‐stage disease. Lesions with diameters between 3 and 7 mm were excluded to avoid overlap between intermediate stages, which could introduce heterogeneity and potentially obscure differences between groups.

The results of this study showed that teeth with large lesions harbour higher bacterial 16S rRNA gene copies as compared to teeth with small lesions. These results are in accordance with a previous study that found higher bacterial counts in canals having radiographic periapical lesions greater than 5 mm (Siqueira Jr. et al. 2007). Additionally, a higher prevalence of intra radicular biofilms has been reported in canals with radiographic lesions larger than 5 mm as opposed to canals with lesions ≤ 5 mm (Ricucci and Siqueira Jr. 2010).

Examining the results of the shotgun analysis, a greater alpha diversity according to the species richness and Shannon indices was obtained in teeth with large lesions, meaning that a higher number of different bacterial species is found in these teeth as compared to teeth with small lesions. However, the species in each group have similar uniformity of species distribution according to the absence of differences in the Inv Simpson and Pielou indices, suggesting the main driver of diversity differences is species richness. This result is in line with the findings of previous studies (Amaral et al. 2022; Rôças and Siqueira Jr. 2008), suggesting that as lesions expand, the microbial community becomes more ecologically complex. This could be explained by the more heterogeneous microenvironments in large lesions, with varying oxygen, nutrient, and immune gradients (Hirai et al. 2018; Song et al. 2025).

Regarding the beta diversity, differences in the species‐level taxonomy were observed between the two groups. Two Acinetobacter species, A. baumanii and A. pitti, were significantly enriched in small lesions, representing 17.82% ( A. baumannii ) and 0.57% ( A. pittii ) of the total relative abundance in this group. Both belong to the Acinetobacter calcoaceticus‐baumannii (Acb) complex, which includes opportunistic microorganisms often involved in nosocomial infections. Bacteria belonging to this genus have been identified in primary infections as well as periodontal infections (Brito et al. 2007; Duran‐Pinedo et al. 2014; Tak et al. 2025). They show capacity to survive in unfavourable conditions as a consequence of virulence factors such as biofilm formation and motility, as well as other factors—for example secretion systems, surface glycoconjugates and micronutrient acquisition systems—that facilitate Acinetobacter pathogenesis (Harding et al. 2018).

The obligate anaerobe F. alocis was found to be enriched in large lesions, representing 11.76% of the total bacterial abundance. This bacterium has been previously identified in periodontal infections as well as root canal infections (Brito et al. 2007; Ordinola‐Zapata et al. 2024). Important virulence factors help this species evade the host immune response, such as its capacity to resist oxidative stress and evade complement cascade (Ozuna et al. 2022). Additionally, it expresses an exotoxin responsible for extending the neutrophil lifespan as well as lipoproteins involved in systemic bone loss via TLR2 (Ozuna et al. 2022), which could be involved in the irresolution of the inflammation in teeth with large lesions.

Despite the taxonomic differences, both infections shared microbial species. Overall, the most abundant taxon was P. micra (14.10%), which ranks among the most frequent and abundant species in primary root canal infections (Bouillaguet et al. 2018; de Brito et al. 2020; Ordinola‐Zapata et al. 2023; Georgiou et al. 2025). Likewise, Dialister pneumosintes , Pseudopropionibacterium propionicum, O. uli , Atopobium spp., Desulfobulbus spp., Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Selenomonas sputigena were found in high relative abundances in previous shotgun and 16s rRNA sequencing based studies (de Brito et al. 2020; Gomes et al. 2015; Nardello et al. 2020; Ordinola‐Zapata et al. 2023). Overall, these infections appear to be dominated by facultative and obligate anaerobes known to collaborate in biofilm formation and maturation, creating microenvironments that sustain long‐term survival (Bronzato et al. 2021).

While taxonomic identification provides valuable insights into the microbial composition of teeth with necrotic pulps and periapical infections, the inter‐individual variability leads the gene combinations or collective virulence activity of these communities to play an important role in the disease progression (Hajishengallis and Lamont 2012). Therefore, using functional gene analysis to identify metabolic and virulence pathways that may be involved in sustaining inflammation and tissue destruction in periapical lesions might provide a clearer association between microbiome activity and the clinical presentation.

In our data, large periapical lesions were characterized by an enrichment of genes of functional pathways such as flavonoid and benzoate degradation, which may enable bacteria to metabolize host‐derived aromatic compounds while simultaneously neutralizing anti‐inflammatory molecules. Particularly, catecholamines, intermediate agents of benzoate degradation, can stimulate bacterial growth, upregulate virulence gene expression—involved in flagella‐mediated motility, toxin production and pilus adhesin—and trigger inflammatory responses (Flierl et al. 2008; Lyte et al. 2011; Yadav et al. 2021). This may explain the enrichment of benzoate degradation pathways in large lesions, previously identified in asymptomatic teeth with primary root canal infections (Georgiou et al. 2025). Similarly, flavonoid degradation may diminish the natural anti‐inflammatory properties of flavonoids, thereby contributing to an inflammatory microenvironment (Al‐Khayri et al. 2022).

The enrichment of bacterial steroid degradation pathway genes in large periapical lesions may also represent a survival strategy that influences host immune responses. Steroid catabolism allows certain bacteria, belonging to phyla such as Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria, to utilize host cholesterol as a nutrient source while simultaneously resisting intracellular oxidative and nitrosative stresses produced by macrophages (Holert et al. 2018). Furthermore, intermediates of steroid degradation have been shown to alter macrophage physiology and inflammatory signalling (Bah et al. 2017), potentially contributing to the disregulated immune responses that could sustain the lesion activity.

Similarly, the enrichment of sphingolipid degradation genes suggests microbial interference with host immune signalling through ceramide and sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P) pathways. Bacterial sphingomyelinases can hydrolyze host sphingomyelin into ceramide, which promotes immune cell apoptosis and amplifies TNF‐ and TLR‐mediated signalling. Downstream metabolites, such as ceramide‐1‐phosphate (C1P), sustain inflammation, while S1P regulates lymphocyte trafficking from lymphoid tissues into inflamed sites (Hannun and Obeid 2018). Collectively, these pathways suggest that bacterial sphingolipid degradation not only compromises host cell viability but also might perpetuate inflammatory signalling in the periapical microenvironment, thereby contributing to lesion persistence. Enrichment of sphingolipid degradation genes has likewise been reported in asymptomatic primary root canal infections (Georgiou et al. 2025).

The bacterial proteasome is a key determinant of pathogen survival under inflammatory conditions. By degrading damaged or stress‐induced proteins, resisting oxidative and nitrosative stress, and apparently recycling amino acids, the proteasome enables pathogens to persist in hostile host environments (Müller and Weber‐Ban 2019). These specialized degradation systems, found in some bacterial pathogens of the orders Actinomycetales and Nitrospirales, help them survive within macrophages and support long‐term dormancy inside host cells, thereby promoting persistence and potential reactivation of disease (Jastrab and Darwin 2015; Müller and Weber‐Ban 2019). Maintaining proteostasis under nutrient limitation and immune pressure is therefore a central virulence mechanism that contributes to infection and inflammation (Gandotra et al. 2007).

By contrast, root canals with small lesions showed enrichment of distinct pathways. Staurosporine, a protein kinase C inhibitor, induces apoptosis, a host defence mechanism that clears infected or damaged cells, limits excessive inflammation, and promotes tissue turnover and homeostasis (Sarkar et al. 2024). The sulphur relay system was also enriched, reflecting its role in essential cofactor biosynthesis such as molybdenum cofactor and tRNA thiolation, both critical for bacterial metabolism and translation (Kotera et al. 2010). These functions may support bacterial survival in nutrient‐limited and immune‐active environments. Additionally, tetracycline biosynthesis genes were overrepresented in small lesion samples, consistent with the expression of antibiotics as a potential mechanism of homeostasis in health‐associated microbiota (Duran‐Pinedo et al. 2014).

Phospholipase D (PLD) also appeared enriched. PLDs are exotoxins that act as virulence factors of several Gram‐negative microorganisms by disrupting host membranes, limiting bacterial opsonization, and facilitating colonization (Barros de Pinho et al. 2021). Finally, the enrichment of monoterpenoid biosynthesis in small lesions may be related to their strong anti‐inflammatory activity and potential role in bone health. Monoterpenoids have been found to reduce prostaglandin E2, nitric oxide, and pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α, which could help bacteria adapt to oxidative and immune stress within infected tissues (Ahmed et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2020). Experimental studies further indicate that certain monoterpenoids promote osteoblast differentiation, enhance extracellular matrix mineralization, and counteract inflammatory cytokines, thereby reducing bone resorption (Fang et al. 2024). These findings suggest a protective role of monoterpenoids against bone loss. Additionally, some monoterpenoids possess antimicrobial activity, which bacteria could exploit to outcompete other microorganisms and dominate the infection niche (Bordini et al. 2018; Potocka et al. 2023).

Taken together, our findings indicate that large periapical lesions are enriched in bacterial pathways that promote immune evasion and exacerbate inflammation. These pathways would highlight the presence of an inflammophilic microbiome (Hajishengallis 2014) that may lead to a reduction in the immune control that reduces microbial clearance and induces the progress of inflammation. Actually, a dose–response relationship seems to exist between apical periodontitis and the blood inflammatory markers, so that the larger the lesion the stronger the inflammatory response (Georgiou et al. 2019). Inflammation is also an important source of nutrients and can therefore exert a powerful influence not only in the composition but also in the numbers of the microorganisms (Hajishengallis 2014) as was observed in teeth with large lesions. In contrast, small lesions are associated with pathways linked to apoptosis, metabolic adaptation, anti‐inflammatory metabolites and antibiotic biosynthesis to protect the host environment. This functional dichotomy suggests that lesion size is not only determined by microbial composition but also by the functional capacity of the resident microbiome to either sustain or restrain host inflammation and tissue destruction.

Finally, the present study presents several limitations. Samples were obtained from a single private practice, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other patient populations. The lesion size was evaluated using only the largest CBCT diameter, which may underestimate or oversimplify the three‐dimensional extent of the lesion. Future studies using volumetric CBCT analysis could provide a more comprehensive assessment. Additionally, it is a cross‐sectional study and causality cannot be extrapolated. It provides a snapshot of the diversity of the microbiota and its function with little insight into its changes. It does not take into account the host conditions that might be responsible for sustaining inflammation. Future studies that address the host/microbiome interaction are required to appraise the different bacterial behaviour in teeth according to the lesion size.

Conclusion

Teeth with large periapical lesions harbour greater bacterial loads and exhibit a more diverse microbial community as compared to those with small lesions. Distinct differences in species‐level taxonomy were observed between the two groups. Functionally, large periapical lesions were enriched in bacterial pathways associated with immune evasion and pro‐inflammatory activity, including benzoate, flavonoid, and steroid degradation, as well as the sphingolipid signalling pathway and proteasome function. In contrast, small lesions showed enrichment of pathways linked to apoptosis, metabolic adaptation, and anti‐inflammatory metabolites, such as tetracycline and monoterpenoid biosynthesis, phospholipase D signalling, the sulphur relay system, and staurosporine biosynthesis. These findings suggest that the progression and severity of periapical disease are shaped not only by the microbial composition but also by the functional capacity of the resident microbiota to either amplify or modulate host inflammatory responses.
