Diagnosing method conditioned bias in mineral resource estimation using a mutual information and entropy uncertainty indicator
Authors: Xiaoqing He, Yuhan Huang, Bin Wu, Yingchun Ao, Haijun Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional evaluation of mineral resource interpolation emphasizes predictive accuracy and reporting compliance, but these criteria do not directly characterize the stability of estimation outputs with respect to method choice. This study introduces an Mutual Information and Entropy Based Interpolation Uncertainty Indicator (MUI) as a diagnostic measure of method-conditioned instability. MUI is defined from normalized entropy and normalized mutual information over controlled realization ensembles, and evaluates the coherence and stability of interpolation outputs under a prescribed realization protocol. In the case study, comparable multi-realization ensembles were constructed for inverse distance weighting (IDW) and ordinary kriging (OK) for Cu estimation in the skarn and hornfels domains of a heterogeneous porphyry-skarn deposit. The results show that OK produced a less coherent and less stable realization ensemble than IDW, with the contrast most evident in skarn. At the same time, conventional benchmarking against infill drilling data indicates that both methods remained comparatively accurate in both skarn and hornfels. These results show that realization stability and interpolation accuracy describe different aspects of estimation behavior. MUI therefore provides a useful complement to conventional validation by capturing method-dependent differences that standard accuracy-based frameworks do not directly express. Although MUI does not explicitly quantify total methodological uncertainty, it can serve as a practical diagnostic of potential method-induced bias. Interpreted together with infill-drilling benchmarking and constrained resampling, the results further provide a safety-oriented assessment of interpolation behavior in terms of accuracy, robustness, and explainability.Keywords: Mineral reserve estimation, Methodological bias, Mutual information, Interpolation uncertainty, Method stability, Technical review, Due diligenceSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Mineral resource and reserve estimates affect asset valuation in mining mergers and acquisitions. Reported tonnage and grade are therefore not only technical outputs, but valuation inputs. Their reliability depends not only on data quantity and coverage, but also on the match between data structure and method assumptions1–6. When this match is weak1,2,5, an estimate may remain formally compliant while still being method-biased. This is a persistent problem in resource and reserve estimation.

Current estimation practice does not isolate this problem directly. Kriging-based methods rely on assumptions such as stationarity and covariance regularity, and often smooth local variability and attenuate extremes1–3,5. Conditional simulation method, including sequential Gaussian simulation6 and uniform conditional simulation7, depend on variogram structures, Gaussian transformation and simulation design. Bayesian frameworks remain sensitive to prior specification and expert judgment8. Data-driven methods-such as implicit modeling9,10, machine learning11, and neural networks12 reduce some subjectivity but introduce others, such as over-fitting and and instability under sparse or heterogeneous sampling. Although uncertainty in mineral resource estimation has been widely studied, standard validation does not isolate the effect of method choice on output stability13.

Information entropy14 and related information-theoretic measurements provide useful basis for this purpose15. In geological modeling, entropy has been used to quantify structural uncertainty and compare alternative realizations16, as well as to examine how uncertainty distribution and model geometry evolve as progressive data are incorporated17. Related studies in machine-learning-based resource estimation and uncertainty-informed spatial-decision analysis further indicate that uncertainty itself is conditioned by modeling assumptions, especially in heterogeneous and sparsely constrained deposits3,6. This suggests that information-theoretic measures can be used not only to describe uncertainty, but also to evaluate the stability of estimation outputs under a fixed modeling framework.

The practical need in technical review is therefore not another global uncertainty measure. It is a compact indicator of whether an estimate is sensitive to interpolation method choice under fixed data conditions. Such an indicator should distinguish dispersion across realizations from structural agreement among realizations.

In this study, we introduce the Information-Entropy-Based Interpolation Uncertainty Indicator (MUI). MUI is defined from normalized entropy and normalized mutual information computed over controlled realization ensembles. It measures the coherence and stability of estimation outputs under a prescribed realization protocol. MUI is not intended to replace predictive accuracy assessment, and it does not quantify total methodological uncertainty. Its purpose is narrower: to identify method-conditioned differences that standard validation does not directly express, and thereby provide a diagnostic signal of potential method-induced bias. The empirical study focuses on Cu estimation using IDW and OK in skarn and hornfels domains, and interprets MUI jointly with local resampling and infill-drilling-based predictive benchmarking.

Because the evaluated estimators are interpolation and geostatistical models rather than black-box learning algorithms, explainability is assessed through model-dependent diagnostics. Specifically, MUI is interpreted by decomposing output-state dispersion and inter-realization structural agreement, and by locating high-entropy zones within the block model. This provides an interpretable basis for identifying where and why method-conditioned instability arises.

Methodology and framework

Characterizing method conditioned bias via MUI

The Information-Entropy-Based Interpolation Uncertainty Indicator (MUI) is designed as a diagnostic measure of method-conditioned instability under controlled realizations. For a candidate estimation method, a realization ensemble is constructed on a common spatial support. For deterministic interpolation methods, realizations are generated by controlled perturbations of model settings, data subsets, or initialization conditions. For intrinsically stochastic methods, realizations are generated directly by the simulation procedure itself. In both cases, the realization ensemble represents the joint response of the method and the data structure under a fixed evaluation design (Fig. 1).

These three evaluations are interpreted jointly. A method may perform similarly under one benchmark and differently under another. For this reason, the framework treats them as complementary rather than interchangeable, and uses their combined interpretation to provide a more differentiated basis for method comparison.

Safety-oriented assessment framework

Although this study does not develop a machine-learning predictor, the reliability criteria commonly emphasized in safe model assessment are relevant to computational interpolation models. Recent studies on explainable artificial intelligence have used post-hoc, model-agnostic tools, such as Shapley values and normalized Shapley–Lorenz values, to interpret black-box neural-network time-series models and to compare models in terms of accuracy, robustness, and explainability18. In the present study, this safety-oriented logic is adapted to interpolation and geostatistical estimators through a model-dependent assessment, rather than through feature-level post-hoc attribution.

In this study, accuracy is assessed by agreement with infill drilling data; robustness is assessed by constrained local resampling and controlled search-geometry perturbation; and explainability is assessed by decomposing MUI into entropy and mutual-information components, supported by spatial entropy maps and method-specific interpretation. This framework is used to evaluate whether an interpolation method is not only accurate, but also stable and interpretable under the prescribed realization protocol.

Case study: diagnostic evaluation in a polymetallic porphyry skarn deposit

The case study is based on a polymetallic porphyry-skarn deposit in the Gangdese metallogenic belt. Although only one deposit is analyzed, it contains substantial internal heterogeneity in geometry, grade distribution, and metal association. The deposit is therefore used as a structured test setting rather than as a basis for cross-deposit generalization. Four estimation domains were defined: Skarn I, Skarn II, Hornfels I, and Hornfels II. These domains differ in continuity, sampling support, distributional form, and dominant mineralization, and thus provide contrasting conditions for evaluating method-conditioned behavior (Fig. 3).

Experimental design and implementation protocol

The empirical design was restricted to cases in which comparable realization ensembles could be constructed and evaluated on a common support. The response variable was Cu block grade. Under this criterion, the analysis focused on IDW and OK in Skarn(i) and Hornfels(i), where data support and estimation behavior were sufficient for controlled multi-realization comparison.

Within each domain, all realizations were generated under a common estimation framework, including compositing, block support, variogram basis, cutoff, and reporting settings (Table 3). For MUI evaluation, realization ensembles were produced by slight perturbations of search-ellipse orientation, while variogram parameters, pre-processing, and the remaining estimation setup were held fixed. This design isolated method-conditioned instability without altering the underlying dataset. The resulting outputs were discretized by equal-width binning for MUI calculation. The perturbation variable was domain-specific. In Skarn (i)-Cu, eight realizations were generated by varying search-ellipse dip. In Hornfels(i)-Cu, eight realizations were generated by varying search-ellipse bearing. These perturbations were intentionally small and were used to test realization coherence rather than to redefine the estimation model.

Benchmark results: stability and accuracy

Conventional benchmarks were used to evaluate two properties not measured directly by MUI: sensitivity to limited sample perturbation and agreement with denser external data. These benchmarks provide reference points for interpreting MUI.

Local resampling stability

Constrained local resampling was used to test robustness to small, spatially limited sample perturbations. For each orebody-method combination, 15 perturbed realizations were generated by resampling 50 observations within restricted local ranges. The perturbation size was kept small relative to the full dataset, so that the procedure measured local stability rather than formal bootstrap uncertainty.

Across all four orebody-method combinations, output variation remained low in absolute terms (Table 4). Both IDW and OK were therefore stable under limited sample fluctuation, with only modest differences in center- and tail-sensitive statistics. Pairwise realization correlations were also uniformly high, indicating that sampling sensitivity was localized rather than structurally pervasive.

Predictive accuracy against infill drilling data

Predictive accuracy was evaluated against infill drilling data from two perspectives: summary statistics and directional trend consistency. The aim was to assess not only agreement in grade level, but also reproduction of spatial variation.

At the distributional level, both methods reproduced mean and median more closely than variance, skewness, and kurtosis. The upper tail was therefore attenuated in both domains, more strongly in Skarn and still visibly in Hornfels. At the domain scale, the statistical differences between IDW and OK were limited, and neither method fully recovered the sharpness of the infill distribution (Table 5).

As an external benchmark, predictive accuracy separated the methods more clearly at the sectional scale than at the level of aggregate statistics. Its scope, however, is limited to agreement with denser external data. It does not address sampling sensitivity or realization coherence1–3,5.

This pattern is broadly consistent with established geostatistical observations. Variogram-based estimators often reproduce large-scale spatial structure and regularized grade trends more effectively than distance-based weighting, while still attenuating local extremes and upper-tail variability. Predictive accuracy therefore separates the methods mainly at sectional scale. It does not address sampling sensitivity or realization coherence.

MUI results: method-dependent instability under controlled realizations

Under the fixed realization protocol, the two methods showed systematic differences in realization coherence. This contrast was reflected in both the entropy components and the integrated MUI values.

The strongest contrast occurred in Skarn. MUI was higher for OK than for IDW (0.156 vs. 0.135), together with higher normalized entropy and lower mutual information. The OK ensemble was therefore more dispersed and less coherent. The same ordering appeared in the realization-wise variation statistics, where OK also showed larger CVs for the mean, variance, and median.

The same method ordering remained in Hornfels, but at a much lower absolute level. MUI was low for both methods (0.014 for OK; 0.010 for IDW), and both entropy components indicated broadly stable realization structure. The difference was therefore detectable, but small in practical magnitude.

This decomposition provides the explainability of the MUI result. A higher MUI does not imply lower predictive accuracy in general, nor does it indicate that a method is unsuitable in all contexts. Instead, it indicates that, under the prescribed perturbation design, the method produced a less coherent and less stable realization ensemble (Table 6). In this case, OK remained comparatively accurate against infill drilling data, but showed weaker realization coherence under controlled perturbation, especially in Skarn. MUI therefore explains a method-conditioned difference that was not directly expressed by predictive benchmarking or local resampling3,6,13.

This component-wise reading constitutes a model-dependent explainability assessment of the interpolation outputs. In Skarn, the higher MUI of OK is explained by the joint effect of increased state dispersion and reduced inter-realization agreement, rather than by a general loss of predictive accuracy. In Hornfels, both methods show low entropy and relatively high mutual information, indicating that the same perturbation protocol produced only weak practical instability. MUI is therefore not interpreted as an opaque scalar index; its value is traced to the two mechanisms that generate it.

This result is also consistent with prior uncertainty studies in mineral resource estimation. Existing work has shown that acceptable predictive agreement does not eliminate sensitivity to modeling assumptions, especially for variogram-based estimators under heterogeneous conditions. The present case follows that pattern: OK remained comparatively accurate against infill drilling data, yet showed weaker realization coherence and higher MUI under controlled perturbation, particularly in skarn. The empirical contribution of MUI is therefore not to overturn conventional benchmarking, but to isolate an additional property of method behavior that related work has implied, but standard validation has not directly measured.

Spatial explainability and consistency with production-based evidence

The entropy maps add a spatial scale to the integrated MUI results. High-entropy blocks were not distributed uniformly, but concentrated in localized instability zones. This concentration was more evident in Skarn than in Hornfels (Fig. 9), consistent with the stronger method contrast observed at the domain scale. The maps therefore show not only how much instability is present, but also where it is expressed within the block model. In this sense, the entropy maps provide a spatial explainability layer for MUI by identifying localized zones where method-conditioned instability is expressed.

Practical implications and scope of inference

The practical implication is limited but clear. MUI distinguished method-conditioned instability even where conventional benchmarks showed only modest differences. The contrast was strongest in Skarn, where OK produced a less coherent realization ensemble than IDW. In Hornfels, the same ordering remained, but at a low absolute level.

This role should be kept distinct from that of the conventional benchmarks. Predictive benchmarking evaluates agreement with denser external data. Local resampling evaluates sensitivity to limited sample fluctuation. MUI evaluates realization coherence under a fixed perturbation design. The three benchmarks therefore address different properties of model behavior (Table 8).

Safety-oriented assessment of interpolation behavior.

This synthesis reframes the existing validation results in terms of accuracy, robustness, and explainability, without treating the interpolation methods as black-box machine-learning predictors. Accuracy was evaluated by external agreement with infill drilling data, robustness by response to constrained sampling and search-geometry perturbations, and explainability by decomposing MUI into its entropy and mutual-information components. The results show that the evaluated methods can remain accurate and robust under conventional benchmarks while still differing in realization coherence under controlled perturbation.

Accordingly, MUI should be used as a complement to interpolation accuracy evaluation rather than as a replacement for it. Its value lies in revealing method-conditioned differences that conventional validation does not directly express. Although it does not explicitly quantify total methodological uncertainty, it can provide a practical diagnostic signal of potential method-induced bias.

Discussion

Interpolation methods do not differ only in precision. They impose different structural assumptions on the same datasets. The resulting differences therefore reflect not only data limitation, but also differences in how methods respond to heterogeneity, sampling density, and modeling constraints. In this study, the distinction is visible in the joint reading of predictive benchmarking, local resampling, and MUI. Conventional benchmarking shows that both IDW and OK remain comparatively accurate in skarn and hornfels, whereas MUI shows that their realization coherence is not equivalent under controlled perturbation. The methodological difference is therefore not exhausted by predictive accuracy alone.

Effect of grid resolution on MUI

MUI is scale-dependent because it is defined on discretized realization fields over a chosen support. Changing grid resolution changes empirical state probabilities , and therefore changes both entropy and mutual information.

This affects the absolute value of the indicator, but not its interpretation, provided that support, discretization, and counting rules are fixed across methods. Under a common probability construction, MUI remains comparable as a relative diagnostic of method-conditioned instability.

In practice, grid resolution should be aligned with estimation support, data spacing, and geological continuity. Using a consistent support ensures that MUI reflects differences in method behavior rather than artefacts of discretization.

Method-conditioned approximation of MUI behavior

The model-dependent explainability of MUI follows from its two components: state dispersion and structural agreement across realizations. Its value depends on how method assumptions interact with the underlying data structure. Appendix B therefore provides heuristic, method-conditioned interpretations across common estimator classes, rather than closed-form universal derivations.

For geometry-based methods, MUI increases when partition stability breaks down. For variogram-based methods, it increases under covariance mismatch, anisotropy mismatch, or poor variogram specification (Fig. 11).

Mutual information functions for common resource estimation methods.

For variogram-based methods, MUI is low under stationary conditions and well-fitted covariance models. Deviations from stationarity, anisotropy, or poor variogram specification elevate MUI. This indicates that MUI increases proportionally with the degree of mismatch between assumed and actual spatial covariance, highlighting regions where kriging may misrepresent variability.

For simulation-based, Bayesian, and data-driven methods, elevated MUI similarly indicates loss of structural consistency relative to the assumptions of the method1,2,5,19. The present case study is consistent with this interpretation. OK, as the variogram-based estimator in the comparison, tracked sectional infill trends more closely, yet showed higher MUI and weaker realization coherence under controlled perturbation. This combination is compatible with a method that preserves large-scale structure under denser control, but remains more sensitive to covariance mismatch, search-geometry perturbation, and structural heterogeneity. By contrast, the lower MUI of IDW in the same domains indicates greater realization coherence under the prescribed protocol, even though its local predictive reconstruction was less accurate in some sections.

Across these classes, high MUI consistently corresponds to weaker method–data alignment and a greater likelihood of method-conditioned bias3,6. In this sense, MUI is not a universal uncertainty measure, but an interpretable signal of where method assumptions may be poorly matched to the data (Table 9).

Effects of preprocessing on MUI behavior

Preprocessing can reduce MUI for two different reasons. It may improve alignment between method assumptions and data structure. It may also suppress the variability that MUI is meant to detect. These two cases should not be conflated.

Clustering, denoising, discretization, and covariance calibration can stabilize estimation outputs when raw data are strongly clustered, highly skewed, or locally inconsistent with support requirements. In such cases, a lower MUI may reflect better method–data compatibility.

The same operations can also remove genuine heterogeneity. Over-aggressive clustering may merge distinct mineralization styles. Outlier removal may suppress real high-grade variability. Poor discretization may distort apparent stability. Covariance tuning aimed mainly at lowering MUI may produce over-smoothed continuity with weak geological support. A lower MUI therefore does not imply a better model. It may reflect either improved compatibility or reduced structural variability. For this reason, MUI should not be optimized in isolation. It should be interpreted together with pre-processing choices, support design, and geological justification.

Practical implications of method-conditioned differences

Method-conditioned differences matter because they propagate beyond the modeling stage. Even when two interpolation methods appear similarly acceptable under conventional validation, they may imply different grade distributions, continuity patterns, and orebody geometries. These differences can propagate to tonnage, classification, and valuation.

This is especially relevant in technical review, due diligence, and M&A, where block models are used directly as valuation inputs. If method sensitivity is not examined, smoothing, local amplification, or domain misalignment may enter economic interpretation as if they were data-driven. MUI does not quantify methodological uncertainty or economic risk directly. Its role is diagnostic: it identifies estimation settings in which method-conditioned distortion is more likely and where further verification is warranted.

Scope and limitations

The present results should be interpreted within a bounded scope. First, the empirical analysis is based on a heterogeneous porphyry-skarn deposit. Although the deposit contains substantial internal variation across domains and mineralization settings, it does not support broad claims of cross-deposit generalizability.

The comparison is restricted to data-method combinations for which comparable realization ensembles could be constructed and evaluated on a common support. In the present case study, this condition was met for IDW and OK in Skarn (i) and Hornfels (i), whereas Au, Mo, Skarn (ii), Hornfels (ii), and intrinsically multi-realization methods such as SGS were excluded because their realizations were not sufficiently comparable or structurally coherent under the current design.

MUI is conditional on spatial support and realization design. Its absolute value may change with these settings, and it should therefore be interpreted as a relative diagnostic under a fixed evaluation protocol rather than as a transportable scalar measure across arbitrarily different designs.

MUI does not decompose uncertainty into distinct sources or quantify total methodological uncertainty directly; its role is to complement conventional validation by identifying method-conditioned differences that may indicate potential method-induced bias and require further verification. The explainability assessment is therefore model-dependent and mechanism-based; it should not be interpreted as feature-level attribution in the sense of Shapley-type methods for black-box predictors. Instead, it is a diagnostic of dispersion, structural agreement, and spatial localization under a fixed interpolation protocol.

Conclusion

This study proposes MUI, an Information-Entropy-Based Interpolation Uncertainty Indicator, as a complement to conventional interpolation evaluation in mineral resource estimation. By combining normalized entropy and normalized mutual information across controlled realization ensembles, MUI characterizes the internal coherence and stability of estimation outputs under method perturbation.

In the case study, OK generated a less coherent and less stable realization ensemble than IDW, especially in skarn. However, both methods remained comparatively accurate in skarn and hornfels under infill-drilling benchmarking. This indicates that interpolation accuracy and realization stability are related but non-equivalent properties, and that conventional validation does not fully capture method-conditioned differences in estimation behavior.

MUI is not intended to replace accuracy assessment, nor does it explicitly quantify total methodological uncertainty. Its value lies in providing an additional diagnostic perspective on estimation methods by identifying differences that conventional validation frameworks do not directly reveal. Together, infill-drilling benchmarking, constrained resampling, and MUI decomposition form a safety-oriented assessment of interpolation behavior, covering accuracy, robustness, and explainability within the limits of the present geostatistical setting. In this sense, MUI can support the diagnosis of potential method-induced bias and provide a more differentiated basis for technical evaluation and method screening.
