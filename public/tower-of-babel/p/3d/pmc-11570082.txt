Determination of Optimal Treatment Plan for Papillary Tumor of the Pineal Region: Case Series With Literature Review
Authors: Brandi W. Pang, David J. Mazur-Hart, Nasser K. Yaghi, Seunggu Jude Han, Jesse J. Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPapillary tumor of the pineal region (PTPR) is a rare neuroepithelial tumor with Central Nervous System (CNS) World Health Organization (WHO) grade II or III classification. Due to its rarity, there is no clear census on treatment. The purpose of this study is to identify the optimal treatment plan focused on extending overall survival (OS).MethodsThis is an institutional case series with review of the literature. Fifty-three publications were analyzed. Only cases with histological diagnosis of PTPR were included. Data collected included demographics, treatment modalities, disease progression, and OS.ResultsThe analysis included 105 patients from the literature and 3 new cases (54 female, 50%) with an average age of 33.1 years (range 1–73 years). The average lesion size was 26.4 mm (range 5–50 mm) in longest axis. All patients underwent an initial resection. There were 46 cases of surgery alone. The remaining cases received adjuvant therapy including radiation (RT), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), chemotherapy (CT), or RT and CT. The average follow-up was 61.4 months (range 1–240 months). OS at 1 year was 96.9%, at 5 years was 87.5%, and at 10 years was 80.2%. Overall progression-free survival (PFS) was 57.4%. Statistical significance was observed in PFS in the surgery plus SRS group and surgery plus CT and RT group. Surgery with SRS had the best PFS (75%), and OS at 1 year (100%) and 5 years (88.9%). Surgery with CT and RT had the best OS at 10 years (85.7%).ConclusionWe describe a case series and literature review of PTPR to help guide the most effective treatment strategies for this rare disease entity. We recommend surgery followed by SRS as the treatment of choice because of its best PFS and 5-year survival rates. We would also recommend adding chemotherapy in the event of disease progression or recurrence as adjuvant radiation and chemotherapy provided the best 10-year survival.

INTRODUCTION

Papillary tumors of the pineal region (PTPR) are rare neuroepithelial tumors that are well-defined and typically large in size [1]. In 2007, they were added to the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System (CNS), but no major updates have been made since due its rarity and limited information available [12]. This tumor accounts for around 1% of intracranial neoplasms and does not arise from the pineal gland but is instead thought to originate from ependymocytes of the subcommissural organ [34]. Due to the lack of specific histological features, definitive diagnosis of PTPR is often difficult as many tumors in the region can demonstrate papillary features. The differential diagnosis for pineal region tumors includes pineal parenchymal tumors, papillary ependymomas, papillary meningiomas, choroid plexus papillomas, and metastatic carcinomas [567]. Diagnoses are often aided with immunohistochemical staining. Strong immunoreactivity to cytokeratins is frequently seen in PTPR [8]. Current diagnostic criteria for PTPR require the tumor to be located in the pineal region, presence of papillary growth pattern with epithelial-like cells, characteristic staining on histology, and, if needed, DNA methylation profiling [9].

PTPR can be designated as a WHO grade II or III neoplasm because they have been shown to be associated with a high recurrence rate [15]. However, given that the biological behavior of PTPR remains variable, histological grading criteria still need to be defined [29]. Surgical resection of these tumors continues to be the primary method of treatment [51011] which includes biopsy, partial resection (PR), or gross total resection (GTR). The role of radiation (RT), chemotherapy (CT), and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) as adjuvant treatment is still unclear as some studies have shown no added benefit regarding outcomes [510], while others did show some benefit, particularly in the setting of radiation and SRS [1213141516]. Due to the rarity of PTPR, minimal data exists in the literature regarding treatment for these patients and their follow-up. This has led to a lack of clear consensus on treatment pathways. With no clear consensus on treatment for PTPR, the authors present a case series describing three patients and a systemic review of the literature to identify a treatment plan that offers the best outcome for patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case presentation

Case 1

Our patient was a 46-year-old male who presented with sudden onset fronto-temporal headaches with associated nausea to the emergency department. A computed tomography scan performed demonstrated a pineal gland mass with some intraventricular bleeding within the third ventricle with associated hydrocephalus. A computed tomography angiogram did not demonstrate any vascular abnormalities.

The patient had a normal neurological exam without deficits. His cognition was intact, and his vitals were within normal limits. A review of systems was positive for headache. An MRI of the brain with and without contrast was performed. This demonstrated what was thought to be a primary pineal mass measuring 1.4 cm by 2.0 cm by 1.5 cm. Mild obstruction of the ventricles at the aqueduct with mass effect was also appreciated. The tumor did not infiltrate into the neighboring brainstem and had heterogenous enhancement with gadolinium contrast (Fig. 1A). A lumbar puncture was performed with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis for tumor markers as well as cytology. CSF test results demonstrated xanthochromic fluid with 5,000 red blood cells. Protein and glucose were within normal limits and cytology returned negative for malignancy. Tumor markers beta-human chorionic gonadotropin and alpha-fetoprotein returned normal. As CSF markers did not offer a definitive diagnosis, it was decided he would undergo surgical excision of the pineal mass.

The patient underwent a right frontal ventriculostomy and a bilateral posterior parieto-occipital craniotomy. A supratentorial transtentorial approach to the pineal region was used to access the tumor. Maximal decompression was achieved with subtotal resection. There were no intraoperative complications.

Tumor specimens were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as well as cytokeratin (CAM 5.2). H&E staining demonstrated tumor cells arranged in a pseudopapillary pattern as well as epithelial surfaces (Fig. 1B). Cytokeratin staining was diffusely positive (Fig. 1C).

Two pineal tumor specimens were sent to the Johns Hopkins surgical pathology consult service. Pathology report on these specimens explained tumor cells formed crude perivascular formations. The final diagnosis was determined to be a papillary tumor of the pineal region. The lesion was explained to also be prone to recurrence.

In the days following his procedure, the patient developed drowsiness and imaging demonstrated dilation of the lateral ventricles. Obstructive hydrocephalus was suspected so he had his right ventriculostomy catheter replaced with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. He tolerated the procedure well and was discharged without issues.

Nearly 2 months after discharge, he had a repeat head MRI with contrast to enable image mapping for his planned SRS. The results demonstrated decompressed ventricles and a homogenously enhancing mass in the pineal region on T1-weighted images with gadolinium contrast. The mass at this time measured 2.1 cm by 1.5 cm in the axial plane demonstrating a slight increase in size from its last preoperative measurements of 2.0 cm by 1.4 cm. It exhibited a mass effect on the adjacent thalami and cerebral peduncles (Fig. 1D).

Shortly after, the patient had CyberKnife (Accuray, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) SRS performed on the pineal region receiving 16 Gy in one fraction at 77% isodose. The treatment was delivered without complications, and he tolerated the procedure well. A follow-up MRI performed 6 months postoperatively did not show evidence of obstructive hydrocephalus. The pineal region mass, however, had undergone a morphological change and increase in size. The most pronounced being in the coronal plane where the mass increased from 1.5 cm by 1.5 cm to 1.8 cm by 2.1 cm. The mass was also appreciated to have a more heterogenous enhancement pattern with gadolinium.

Imaging performed roughly 11 months postoperatively was significant for an interval decrease in pineal mass size since the last study a month prior. Subsequent scans have been stable and demonstrated decreased enhancement of the mass. Roughly 30 months after his SRS, the patient has been radiologically stable. His most recent physical exam performed 36 months postoperatively demonstrated limited upward gaze and convergence nystagmus.

Case 2

Our second patient was a 73-year-old male who had presented to another hospital 10 months prior after a sudden collapse and was found to have hydrocephalus and a pineal region mass. He underwent a right occipital ventriculoperitoneal shunt by an outside neurosurgeon. His pineal mass was followed by an MRI 1 month prior to consultation with report to have nearly doubled in size for which he was referred to our institution.

The patient was without focal neurological deficits. His only complaints were of headache and a whooshing sound in his ears. His wife reported that he was more confused. MRI revealed a 2 cm, heterogeneously enhancing mass of the pineal region on T1-weighted imaging with gadolinium contrast (Fig. 2A).

He was indicated for and underwent a suboccipital craniectomy with supracerebellar infratentorial approach for resection of mass. A GTR was achieved.

The H&E stains showed tumor with a papillary architecture and pseudostratified columnar epithelium. The tumor had a fairly discrete border with pineal tissue. The cells had uniform, oval nuclei with prominent nucleoli. Mitotic figures were very rare, even with phosphohistone stain. There was no significant necrosis. The tumor stained for cytokeratin CAM 5.2, vimentin (diffuse), neuron-specific enolase, and NCAM (diffuse). The tumor was negative for EMA, neurofilament, and cadherin. The histological appearance and immunohistochemical profile were most consistent with papillary tumor of the pineal region.

Postoperatively, he recovered in the neurosciences intensive care unit (NSICU) with a restricted vertical and horizontal gaze. He was given a rapid dexamethasone taper. He had a perioperative non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) for which Cardiology was consulted. He was transferred to the ward on postoperative day 5 where his horizontal gaze restriction improved. Neuro-Oncology was consulted, and an MRI brain and spine were obtained. MRI spine showed no evidence of neoplasm. MRI brain showed no obvious residual disease. He was discharged to home with headaches, upgaze palsy, and diplopia worse on left gaze.

On outpatient follow-up, he was found to have bilateral subdural hygromas and meningeal enhancement concerning for over drainage for which he underwent a shunt revision roughly 3 months after his resection. His headaches and diplopia were improving. He was last seen by Neuro-Oncology roughly 15 months postoperatively with stable diplopia and headaches. At 22 months following surgery, the patient was not seen in clinic, but MRI showed no evidence of residual enhancing mass or recurrence (Fig. 2B). He received no adjuvant therapy. He had no additional neurological deficits at his last visit, which was 9 years after resection.

Case 3

Our third patient was a 61-year-old female who presented to our emergency department with 5 months of unsteady gait, frequent falls, and urinary incontinence. Imaging revealed a homogenously enhancing pineal region mass and hydrocephalus (Fig. 3A). Ophthalmology was consulted with no findings of papilledema.

Upon examination, the patient was noted to have left finger-to-nose dysmetria in addition to a mild right ptosis. The patient was also noted to have poor short-term memory. No other neurological deficits were identified.

The patient underwent a right-sided burr hole at Kocher’s point for endoscopic third ventriculostomy and pineal mass biopsy with a flexible endoscope. CSF was collected after entering the right lateral ventricle and sent for cytology and tumor markers.

H&E stains showed a papillary/pseudopapillary neoplasm composed of relatively round, epithelioid tumor cells with distinct cell borders and focal cytoplasmic vacuoles. Blood vessels showed hyalinization. There was no evidence of necrosis, microvascular proliferation, or increased mitotic activity. The Ki67 proliferation index was low (less than 1%). The tumor stained for cytokeratin CAM 5.2, vimentin, neuron specific enolase, and NCAM. The tumor was negative for EMA. The final histological diagnosis was a papillary tumor of the pineal region.

The patient initially recovered in the NSICU with no new neurological deficits. The patient was transferred to the ward and discharged to home.

At her 2-week follow-up, she endorsed improvements in gait, urinary continence, and cognition. At her 6-week follow-up, she endorsed continued improvements with an MRI showing slightly improved ventricular volumes and stable pineal region mass. The patient was last seen roughly 3-years postoperatively with resolutions of gait imbalance and urinary incontinence with stable size ventriculomegaly and pineal mass (Fig. 3B).

Literature review

Data was collected from 105 cases of PTPR by literature review (2003–2022) and 3 current case presentations [5681012131415161718192021222324252627282930313233343536373839404142434445464748495051525354555657585960]. The diagnosis was confirmed by histological examination. Patient sex, age at diagnosis, tumor size, initial therapy, adjuvant therapy, incidence of recurrence/metastasis, time to recurrence, time of last follow-up, and survival status were all used. Tumor sizes were reported as mean diameter along the longest axis from pre-therapy radiographic imaging unless otherwise noted. Biopsy, PR, and GTR were combined into the surgery alone treatment group as the details of resection are difficult to determine from the literature review. Additionally, the process of any surgical intervention yields similar results in that the patient is placed under general anesthesia, an incision is made, the tumor is debulked, a histological diagnosis is made, and an inflammatory response takes place. Therefore, biopsies were categorized as surgery if performed prior to additional treatment. Progression of tumor size was categorized as a recurrence. Cases were not included that did not have a recorded follow-up. At our institution, the Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviewed all studies and determined a formal release is not required for this case report of 3 subjects.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean±standard error of mean. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Statistical significance was determined by comparing groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) testing with p<0.05.

RESULTS

There were 108 total cases consisting of 54 male and 54 female patients. The average age was 33.1±16.2 years with a range of 1 to 73 years old (Table 1). The overall mean diameter of tumors was 26.4±9.6 mm (range 5–50 mm). Surgery alone was performed in 46 patients, 25 of which were GTR and 21 were PR. RT followed surgery in 37 cases, CT followed surgery in 2 cases, a combination of RT and CT followed surgery in 11 cases, and SRS followed surgery in 12 cases. Average follow-up was 61.4±53.6 months (range 1–240 months) with tumor progression/recurrence/metastatic disease occurring in 46 cases for an overall rate of 42.6%. Twelve patients were recorded as deceased at the last follow-up, with two of those deaths occurring by suicide. Kaplan-Meier estimates showed 1-year OS of 96.9%, 5-year OS of 87.5%, and 10-year OS 80.2% (Fig. 4).

Further evaluation was performed after grouping GTR and PR into a joint category of surgical intervention and was then compared to various adjuvant therapies. Surgery alone had a 43.5% PFS, 94.9% 1-year OS, 91.9% 5-year OS, and 81.7% 10-year OS (Table 1 and Fig. 5). Surgery with RT had a 70.3% PFS, 97.1% 1-year OS, 84.9% 5-year OS, and 84.9% 10-year OS. Surgery with RT and CT had a 63.6% PFS, 100% 1-year OS, 85.7% 5-year OS, and 85.7% 10-year OS. Surgery with SRS had a 75% PFS, 100% 1-year OS, 88.9% 5-year OS, and 71.1% 10-year OS. Surgery with CT had a 0% PFS. Survival data was not available and was withheld from one-way ANOVA analysis as it had only two recorded cases. No statistical significance was seen between treatment groups for OS. Statistical significance was seen between surgery and surgery plus SRS treatment groups, and surgery and surgery plus CT and RT groups for PFS, however (Fig. 5B).

DISCUSSION

Much uncertainty exists in regard to the most effective treatment plan for papillary tumors of the pineal region. This could be argued to be due in part to the fact this type of tumor is known for frequent local recurrence [5]. Initial treatments include partial to gross total surgical resection of the tumor, SRS, and biopsy with CSF diversion [51017]. Utilized adjuvant therapies include, but are not limited to, repeat surgical resections, CT, RT, and SRS [517]. In our literature review, we analyzed outcomes in cases of PTPR across multiple treatment modalities to determine which offered the best overall outcome. Our data found that surgery followed by SRS provided the best PFS and best 5-year OS in patients with PTPR. We found that surgery followed by RT and CT provided the best 10-year outcomes.

In evaluating 31 PTPR cases, Fèvre-Montange et al. [5] appreciated that the only clinical factor that tended to be associated with OS and recurrence was GTR. However, the correlation was not statistically significant. A subsequent review by Fauchon et al. [10] added an additional 13 patients and continued to follow up the initial 31 cases. Their results showed the initial treatment should include complete resection when safely possible when considering OS. Our review has added an additional 64 cases upon their review for a total of 108 cases. Of course, these statistics may be influenced by tumors so advanced they are deemed inoperable and, therefore, require further investigation. We categorized GTR with PR to form a surgical intervention group. Patients who underwent a biopsy were also included in the surgical intervention group. We did this because any surgical exposure includes a general anesthetic, similar pharmacologic exposures, systematic physiological response from incision and exposure, and excision of tissue with bleeding and an inflammatory response. The amount of tissue taken during a PR or a biopsy is difficult to describe and unclear in the literature. This is also a “high risk” area for a needle biopsy, which was likely not performed in the described literature cases. Biopsy was most likely completed through an open approach or endoscopic approach. During these approaches, most likely as much tissue as safely could be removed was resected. This would be more consistent with a PR. The high rates of recurrent and residual growth also make the difference in outcomes between incomplete tissue removal unclear. Given these limitations, we grouped surgical interventions as the first step of therapy should include resection of as much of the tumor as safely possible. Furthermore, any operative approach is required for a definitive histological diagnosis of PTPR. Grouping them allows for the evaluation of the efficacy of various adjuvant therapies, given that prior research suggested safe surgical resection was the best initial therapy. There was no significance in age or tumor size among groups utilizing surgical intervention.

Lancia et al. [61] looked at the role of radiotherapy in the treatment of PTPR and of the 84 patients who received RT, 59 were in an adjuvant setting. Results for adjuvant RT were not specified, but they found a 10-year survival of 72.5%, and PFS of 60% and 80% at 5 and 10 years, respectively. Interestingly, Edson et al. [13] also looked at outcomes following surgery plus RT and found good OS (100% at 5 years), but a PFS of 64% at 5 years. We found similar but slightly more optimistic results in surgery followed by RT alone.

Previous case reviews have also reported the use of CT as adjuvant treatment following surgery or for tumor recurrence [1819]. Temozolomide (TMZ) was used in both cases which could have some advantages as it can stop tumor cell growth at all stages of the cell cycle. Patients were both followed for 9 years after initial diagnosis and have not experienced tumor recurrence or disease progression, respectively. For both CT and RT, however, Fauchon et al. [10] found there was no effect on OS and PFS. We found that the addition of CT and RT had the best 10-year survival rates.

Of the 12 total cases, SRS was utilized as adjuvant therapy in 11 cases. Only 2 of the 12 cases revealed deceased patients, one at 91 months of follow-up [10], and the other at 60 months due to suicide [13]. Of the remaining cases, 7 were without tumor recurrence, and all were still living at the last follow-up visit [1213162021]. Our results showed good OS rates for patients with PTPR and no significant differences between treatment groups. Surgery with SRS therapy provided the best OS of 100% at 1 year and 88.9% at 5 years. We also report a trend toward better PFS with the combination of surgical resection with SRS therapy (75%). Furthermore, we recommend surgery to debulk the tumor and provide a tissue diagnosis, followed by adjuvant SRS. The higher dosage of radiation immediately following surgery may potentially provide an increased short-term benefit. With the associated risks of CT, as well as limited data on its use in the treatment for PTPR, we recommend utilizing CT in the setting of disease progression or recurrence for long-term survival at 10 years.

Limitations

This is a retrospective study with literature review, which is limited by the published data. We were unable to determine the extent of resection in the literature as we did not have access to operative reports and imaging. To control for this, we combined biopsies, PR, and GTR into a surgical intervention group. Previous reports have indicated no significant difference in survival depending on extent of resection. Additionally, the rarity of PTPR makes it difficult to study as we do not know the details of the events that occurred during the post-treatment period. Prospective studies and randomized control trials are needed to gain a better understanding of the complications and tolerance of these therapies.

Conclusion

With little consensus on management for PTPR, the purpose of our report is to determine the best treatment through retrospective analysis of clinical status posttreatment. We found that adjuvant SRS offered the best OS at 1 and 5 years as well as the best PFS at all time points. Surgery with adjuvant CT and RT after an initial attempt at surgical resection demonstrated the best OS at 10 years. Therefore, we recommend surgery with adjuvant SRS as the treatment of choice for patients with the addition of CT if there is progression or recurrence. However, given the limitations of literature review, more prospective data is needed to gain a greater understanding of the long-term survival and tumor recurrence after receiving treatment.
