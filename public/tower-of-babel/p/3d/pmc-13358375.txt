Metabolic Mechanisms of Modified Lingguizhugan Decoction in Obesity and Lipid Metabolism Disorders: A Multi‐Omics Approach
Authors: Fufu Li, Xiaoyan Liu, Ji Li, Haiyan Sun, Qingyang Han, Dong Liu, Ran Liu, Jieyu Chen, Zijun Ouyang, Mengting Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObesity and related metabolic disorders, including metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes, are pressing global health concerns. Traditional Chinese medicine, specifically modified Lingguizhugan decoction (MLGZGD), has shown promise for managing these conditions. MLGZGD contains bioactive compounds that may modulate lipid metabolism and gut microbiota, suggesting potential therapeutic benefits for obesity and associated metabolic disorders. However, the underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown. This study investigated the therapeutic effects of MLGZGD on obesity and lipid metabolism. Specifically, we sought to examine how MLGZGD influences gut microbiota, modulates lipid metabolism, and contributes to improved metabolic health using a multi‐omics approach.MethodsThis study identified the active compounds in MLGZGD using UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS/MS. The mice were divided into four groups (n = 8 per group) and received different treatments: model, positive control (atorvastatin 2.6 mg kg−1), low‐dose MLGZGD (7 g kg−1), or high‐dose MLGZGD (14 g kg−1). The effects of MLGZGD on the gut microbiota and lipid metabolism were evaluated using 16S rRNA sequencing and lipidomic analysis, and correlations between the two were explored.ResultsIn normal mice, MLGZGD significantly altered the gut microbiota composition, increasing the abundance of beneficial genera such as Akkermansia and Dubosiella, while reducing the abundance of Enterorhabdus. In high‐fat diet‐induced obese mice, MLGZGD restored the disrupted gut microbiota, notably reducing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, improving Akkermansia levels, and increasing Clostridiales abundance. Lipidomic analysis showed that MLGZGD significantly improved triglyceride and phosphatidylcholine metabolism, with a notable correlation between Akkermansia and Clostridiales abundance and the lipid profile.ConclusionsMLGZGD effectively ameliorates obesity and lipid metabolism‐related disorders by reshaping the gut microbiota and modulating lipid metabolism. These findings support the therapeutic potential of MLGZGD in treating obesity and related metabolic disorders, with modulation of gut microbiota and lipid metabolism as key mechanisms.

Background

According to the World Obesity Atlas 2025 released by the World Obesity Federation, the number of individuals with obesity worldwide is projected to increase from 524 million in 2010 to 1.13 billion by 2030, representing a growth rate exceeding 115%. Currently, long‐term use of lipid‐lowering drugs such as statins may lead to adverse reactions, including liver damage, muscle pain, and in severe cases rhabdomyolysis [1]. This highlights the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets and develop alternative treatments that can effectively manage obesity and its comorbidities while minimizing adverse effects.

Lingguizhugan decoction (LGZGD) was first documented in Zhang Zhongjing's Shang Han Za Bing Lun during the Han Dynasty. In modern medicine, LGZGD is widely used to treat obesity and related metabolic disorders, with substantial evidence supporting its efficacy in alleviating obesity, dyslipidemia and liver damage [2, 3]. Recently, its efficacy in reducing body weight in individuals with obesity or metabolic syndrome has been demonstrated in clinical settings [4, 5, 6, 7]. A meta‐analysis involving 1716 participants indicated that LGZGD significantly reduced triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), body mass index and body weight compared with clinical controls [6]. LGZGD was demonstrated to improve insulin resistance in patients with MAFLD in a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial [8].

Our previous preliminary studies have confirmed that LGZGD regulates lipid metabolism by modulating the gut microbiota, thereby slowing the progression of lipid metabolism‐related diseases [9, 10, 11, 12]. However, the clinical application of LGZGD remains largely traditional, primarily targeting specific syndromes (spleen‐yang deficiency pattern). Therefore, optimizing LGZGD to broaden its indications holds significant clinical value [7].

Modified Lingguizhugan decoction (MLGZGD), a variant of LGZGD enriched with Schisandra chinensis and Ganoderma lucidum, further enhances lipid metabolism by replenishing Qi and blood. The potential of Schisandra chinensis fruits in developing antidiabetic effects and improving liver injury has been demonstrated [13, 14], and they can ameliorate the MAFLD by alleviating ER stress [15]. Additionally, Ganoderma lucidum has been shown to improve MAFLD by upregulating the expression of hepatic energy metabolism enzymes [16] and to reduce obesity by modulating gut microbiota composition [17] Despite these promising results, the exact metabolic mechanisms and the therapeutic potential of MLGZGD remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

Recently, the integration of bioinformatics, multi‐omics technologies, and TCM has emerged as a powerful approach for drug discovery and target identification [18] Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of action of MLGZGD in obesity and lipid metabolism‐related diseases. Therefore, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to explore the regulatory effects of MLGZGD on the gut microbiome. A high‐fat diet (HFD)‐induced obesity mouse model was used to validate the efficacy of MLGZGD using serum lipidomics and microbiome analysis to provide insights into its metabolic mechanisms.

Method

Preparation of MLGZGD Drug Solution and Positive Control Drug

The herbal components of MLGZGD were purchased from Heshuntang (Shenzhen Wellsoon Herbals Ltd.), and their detailed composition ratios are listed in Supporting Information S1: Table S1. The comprehensive preparation process for MLGZGD was as follows: Herbal materials were initially soaked in 500 mL of double‐distilled (dd) water for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was boiled for 30 min and strained. Next, ddwater was added again, and repeat the appeal step. Finally, the filtered decoctions were combined and concentrated under reduced pressure to final concentrations of 0.7 g/mL (low dose) and 1.4 g/mL (high dose) based on the mass of the raw materials. The resulting solutions were then stored at −80°C for subsequent experimental use.

Animal and Experimental Design

Animal

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Guangdong Gem Pharmatech Biotechnology Co. (Foshan, China). Mice were housed in a pathogen‐free environment under a 12‐h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 21°C ± 2°C. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University (permit no. GYFY2024031).

Animal Experiment Design

MLGZGD Intervened in Normal Mice

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice were acclimatized for 3 days and randomly divided into two groups: control (without drug for intragastric gavage) and high‐dose MLGZGD (14 g kg−1). In this experiment, the dosage of MLGZGD was determined based on the “Equivalent Dose Ratio between Humans and Animals Converted by Body Surface Area” [19]. The mice were fed an irradiated diet and gavaged daily for 2 weeks. Body weight was recorded throughout the treatment period. After overnight fasting (12 h), blood was collected via orbital venipuncture, allowed to clot for 2 h at room temperature, and centrifuged (3500 rpm for 30 min). The serum was stored at −80°C for further analysis. The mice were euthanized and the liver and cecal feces were collected for subsequent experiments.

MLGZGD in the Treatment of HFD‐Induced Obese Mice

At 5 weeks of age, the mice were randomly divided into a model group (n = 32) and a control group (n = 8). After a 3‐day acclimatization period, the model group was fed a HFD (60% kcal fat, D12492, Research Diets, USA) for 10 weeks to induce obesity, whereas the control group was fed a normal chow diet (10% kcal fat, D12450K, Research Diets, USA). Body weight was monitored biweekly. After 10 weeks, the obese mice were randomly divided into four groups: model, positive control (atorvastatin 2.6 mg kg−1), low‐dose MLGZGD, and high‐dose MLGZGD. Each group continued their respective diets and treatments for 6 weeks, with body weight measured weekly. According to a previous study [7], 7 g kg−1 was designated as the low‐dose group and the high‐dose group was set at 14 g kg−1. One week before the end of the study, micro‐CT (Computed Tomography) imaging was performed to assess fat distribution. After a 12‐h fast, blood was collected as described above. The mice were then humanely euthanized and the liver, adipose tissue, serum, and cecal contents were collected for subsequent analyses.

Micro CT Imaging and Fat Extraction

Micro‐CT imaging was performed using a Quantum GX2 system (PerkinElmer, USA) after anaesthetizing the mice with isoflurane. Imaging parameters were as follows: 70 kV, 60 μA, FOV 86 mm, resolution 172 μm, and scan time 4 min per segment. Fat distribution was analyzed using Analyze software (version 12.0; PerkinElmer, USA).

Serum Biochemical Analysis

Serum lipid levels (TC, TG, and LDL‐C) and liver function markers (ALT and AST) were measured using commercial kits from Solarbio (BC1985, BC0625, BC1555 and BC1565, China) and Cell Biolabs (STA‐391, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Serum Lipidomics Testing

Metabolites were extracted from the serum samples using an organic reagent‐based protein precipitation method. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling equal volumes of all experimental samples to assess the reproducibility and consistency of the experimental procedure. The extracted samples, including the QC samples, were analyzed in a randomized order, with QC samples inserted before, during, and after the experimental samples to monitor the instrumental performance [20]. Mass spectrometry (MS) was performed analyze samples in positive and negative ion modes.

Raw data from the mass spectrometer were converted into a readable format using Proteowizard's MS Convert software. Peak extraction and quality control were conducted using the XCMS software. Ion annotation and initial metabolite identification were performed using the MetaboAnnotation tool. MS data were further analyzed by matching primary and secondary ion information to an internal standard database for metabolite identification. Candidate metabolites were annotated and matched to known compounds in the Human Metabolome and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes databases to obtain their physicochemical properties and biological functions. Quantitative analysis and screening of differential metabolites between groups were performed using the R programming language.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. For comparisons between the two groups, a t‐test or a separate variance estimation t‐test for independent samples was performed. Differences between categorical variables were evaluated using the chi‐square test. For the lipidomic data, partial least squares discriminant analysis was used for the supervised classification of variables between groups. A variable importance in the projection threshold of 1.0 was set for identifying significant variables. Statistical significance was determined with using a p‐value < 0.05.

Results

UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS/MS Was Used to Identify the Compounds in MLGZGD

To investigate the active compounds in MLGZGD and their potential role in improving obesity, this study used UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS/MS technology. The chemical composition was analyzed based on high‐precision molecular weights and secondary fragmentation patterns, which revealed the characteristic peaks of monomeric compounds (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1A,B). A total of 25 active compounds were identified in the positive ion mode and 39 compounds were identified in the negative ion mode. Detailed information, including compound names, molecular formulas, m/z values, and their sources, is provided in Supporting Information S1: Tables S2 and S3. Notably, compounds such as baicalein, naringenin, and schisandrin were identified as the active ingredients.

Ameliorative Effect of MLGZGD on HFD‐Induced Obesity

To evaluate the therapeutic effects of MLGZGD on obesity, the experiment employed an HFD‐induced obesity model in C57BL/6 mice. After 16 weeks of feeding, the body weight of mice in the model group was significantly higher than that of mice in the control group (Figure 1B,C). After 6 weeks of MLGZGD treatment, both the positive control and high‐dose MLGZGD groups exhibited significant reductions in body weight compared to the model group (Figure 1B,C). CT imaging revealed a reduction in fat content in the high‐dose MLGZGD group (Figure 1D), and fat volume analysis showed a significant decrease in the high‐dose and positive control groups compared to that in the model group (Figure 1E).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining of epididymal adipose tissue showed that MLGZGD improved adipocyte size and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 1F). Furthermore, MLGZGD treatment significantly lowered serum levels of TG, TC, and LDL‐C (Figure 1G–I), indicating that MLGZGD reduced fat accumulation and improved lipid metabolism in obese mice.

As the central organ involved in lipid metabolism, the liver was examined for signs of steatosis and to assess liver function. The model group exhibited severe hepatomegaly, which was significantly ameliorated by MLGZGD treatment (Figure 2A). Serum ALT and AST levels were significantly reduced in the high‐dose MLGZGD group (Figure 2B,C). Additionally, liver TG levels were significantly reduced in the MLGZGD‐treated groups (Figure 2D), although the reduction in TC levels was not significant (Figure 2E). Histological analysis of liver tissue revealed smaller lipid droplets in the MLGZGD‐treated mice, as shown by hematoxylin and eosin and ORO (Oil Red O) staining (Figure 2F,G). These results indicated that MLGZGD effectively reduced liver fat accumulation and improved liver function in obese mice.

MLGZGD Alleviates Intestinal Flora Imbalance in HFD Mice

To evaluate the effect of MLGZGD on the gut microbiota, this study treated normal C57BL/6 male mice with MLGZGD for 2 weeks and used 16S rRNA sequencing to assess the impact of the results. Beta diversity analysis showed significant differences in the microbial composition between the two groups (Figure 3A). At the phylum level, the dominant microbiota comprised Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, which together accounted for more than 70% of the microbiota (Figure 2B). Verrucomicrobia abundance was significantly increased in the MLGZGD group (Figure 2C). At the genus level, MLGZGD treatment improved the structure of the gut flora (Figure 2D) and significantly increased the abundance of Dubosiella and Akkermansia (Figure 2E,F).

The same method was used to analyze the intestinal flora of mice fed a HFD. Principal component analysis revealed distinct differences in the microbiota diversity between the groups, with the MLGZGD‐treated group clustering closer to the control group (Figure 3G). Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes dominated the microbiota in all the groups, constituting over 70% of the microbiota (Figure 3H). The Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio was higher in the model group than in the control group, but this ratio decreased in the MLGZGD group (Figure 3J). At the genus level, the abundance of Verrucomicrobia was significantly reduced in the model group but was restored in the MLGZGD‐treated group (Figure 3K). Conversely, the abundance of Desulfobacteriota showed opposite trends to those of Verrucomicrobia, indicating that MLGZGD effectively alleviated dysbiosis (Figure 3L).

Further analysis of the top 30 bacterial genera revealed that the high‐dose MLGZGD group exhibited significant improvements in the microbiota composition at the genus level (Figure 3I). The abundance of Akkermansia was significantly reduced in the model group but increased in the MLGZGD‐treated group (Figure 3M), and similar trends were observed for Clostridiales unclassified (Figure 3N). MLGZGD alleviated this abnormal increase in Alistipes in the model group (Figure 3O). These results demonstrate that MLGZGD modulates the gut microbiota in HFD‐induced obese mice.

Effect of MLGZGD on Serum Lipid Metabolism in HFD‐Induced Obese Mice

To further understand how MLGZGD affects lipid metabolism, this section conducted a lipidomics analysis of serum samples from HFD‐induced obese mice using LC‐MS/MS. A total of 205,321 and 261,478 lipid molecules were identified in the positive and negative ion modes, respectively.

Principal component analysis revealed clear differences in the lipid profiles between the groups (Figure 4A). Differential lipids were identified between the MLGZGD and control groups with 526 and 610 lipids extracted from the M/C and H/M groups, respectively (Figure 4B–E). The most significantly altered lipid classes were TG and phosphatidylcholine (PC) (Figure 4F). Spearman correlation analysis between differentially expressed lipids and the top 30 bacterial genera revealed that Akkermansia was negatively correlated with PC and positively correlated with TG, while Desulfovibrionaceae was unclassified, and Alistipes showed the opposite correlation with lipid molecules (Figure 4G). Notable differences were observed for PC (14:1 [9Z]/14:1 [9Z] and TG (16:1/16:1/18:2) (Figure 4G). This demonstrated that MLGZGD‐induced alterations in the gut microbiota are associated with triglyceride (TG) and phospholipid (PC) lipid metabolism.

Discussion

Component analysis of MLGZGD revealed that is composition contains bioactive compounds, such as baicalein, naringenin, and schisandrin, which have been shown to promote cholesterol efflux, reduce oxidative stress, and mitigate cellular apoptosis [21, 22], which likely contribute to the anti‐obesity effects of MLGZGD; however, it is not clear whether these active ingredients work by affecting the gut microbiota.

Our results also highlighted the impact of MLGZGD on gut microbiota composition. In normal mice, MLGZGD treatment significantly increased the abundance of Dubosiella and Akkermansia. Dubosiella has been identified as a potential probiotic with therapeutic potential for enteritis [23], whereas Akkermansia species, such as Akkermansia muciniphila, are inversely correlated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and low‐grade inflammation [24]. The increased abundance of Akkermansia and Dubosiella following MLGZGD treatment suggests that these microbiota alterations may be beneficial for managing obesity and the associated metabolic disorders.

In the HFD‐induced obesity model, gut microbiota composition and metabolic pathways were disrupted. The model group exhibited a significantly increased Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio, which is commonly associated with obesity, intestinal inflammation, and related metabolic disorders [25]. Additionally, the abundance of Verrucomicrobia, a phylum beneficial to metabolic health, was reduced, while the abundances of Akkermansia and Clostridiales were also decreased, and the abundance of Alistipes, which is associated with obesity, increased [26] However, after MLGZGD treatment, microbial imbalances were notably corrected. The Clostridiales genus, which is closely linked to short‐chain fatty acid production [27], showed improved abundance in the MLGZGD group, suggesting a beneficial effect on gastrointestinal inflammation. These findings suggest that MLGZGD can modulate gut microbiota, potentially offering therapeutic value in treating gastrointestinal inflammation and related disorders.

The results of the lipidomic analysis further revealed significant lipid metabolism abnormalities in the model group, particularly in the metabolism of TG and PC. MLGZGD treatment restores lipid metabolism in HFD‐induced obese mice. PC is the most abundant phospholipid in the cell membranes and plays a key role in regulating cholesterol aggregation. Elevated PC levels may act as a compensatory mechanism to mitigate increased cholesterol levels in the bloodstream [25]. Among these lipid molecules, TG (16:1/16:1/18:2) has been reported to be associated with the metabolic syndrome, and elevated levels of PC (14:1 [9Z]/14:1 [9Z]) are associated with an increased risk of atherosclerosis [28, 29]. Spearman's correlation analysis revealed associations between Akkermansia and Clostridiales unclassified with these lipid molecules, supporting the notion that MLGZGD may influence lipid metabolism through modulation of the gut microbiota regulation.

Conclusion

This study indicates that MLGZGD may improve obesity by modulating the gut microbiota, thereby affecting lipid metabolism, particularly that of TG and PC. The underlying mechanism may involve MLGZGD, which restores the microbial community balance disrupted by a HFD by promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia and Dubosiella. These findings suggest a promising therapeutic strategy for improving lipid metabolism and treating obesity‐related diseases. However, their potential application in Western medicine requires further in‐depth research.
