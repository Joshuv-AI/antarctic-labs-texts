Impact of restricted antibiotic use, hand hygiene, and sink trap replacement on carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales acquisition in parturients and newborns, Southeast Gabon (2022–2023)
Authors: Mesmin Moussounda, Sandra Dos Santos, Michelle Bignoumba, Laeticia Abang, Moussa Togola, Jean-Bernard Lekana-Douki, Patrice François, Nathalie van der Mee-Marquet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background In 2022, a study in the maternity and neonatology units of Franceville Hospital revealed high risk of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) acquisition in parturients and newborns potentially linked to suboptimal hand hygiene (HH) compliance, environmental contamination, and extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in newborns. To address this, a three-pronged intervention—HH promotion via hand rubbing, sink trap replacement, and restricted antibiotic use—was implemented.Methods We conducted a prospective post-intervention study. HH compliance was assessed in February 2023, before the intervention. From July to September 2023, HH compliance, CPE carriage in parturients and newborns, neonatal infections, environmental contamination, and antibiotic use were evaluated post-intervention. The impact on asymptomatic CPE carriage in parturients and newborns, neonatal CPE infections, and environmental contamination was assessed by comparing post-intervention results with pre-intervention data from 2022.Results HH compliance improved significantly (2.9% vs. 70.6%; p < 0.001), and neonatal antibiotic use declined (74.9% vs. 15.3%, p < 0.001). ESBLE carriage rates decreased among parturients, (15.8% vs. 40.1%; p < 0.001), and among newborns (0.5% vs. 5.4%; p = 0.007). CPE carriage rates decreased among parturients (4.6% vs. 1.0%; p = 0.059). No neonatal CPE infections occurred post-intervention. Despite sink trap replacement, contamination persisted. Genomic analysis of CPE strains suggested reduced parturient-to-parturient transmission and CPE acquisition from the environment.Conclusions Our findings suggest a reduction in the risk of CPE acquisition among parturients and neonates after implementing the intervention. Our findings strongly support the need for sustained HCW training, ensuring the availability of hand rub solutions, and campaigns promoting proper HH practices and rational antibiotic use to prevent nosocomial infections caused by hypervirulent CPE clones circulating in Gabon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01710-1.

Introduction

Carbapenem-producing Enterobacteriales (CPE) represent a serious threat to human health worldwide [1]. Nosocomial infections caused by CPE are associated with increased morbidity and mortality, particularly in vulnerable populations, such as newborns [2]. CPE described in studies carried out in African countries predominantly carry blaNDM and blaOXA-48-like genes [3], two families of genes that have spread globally [4]. In Gabon, the presence of colonizing and infecting CPE carrying blaNDM-5, blaNDM-7, blaOXA-48, and blaOXA-181 genes has been recently documented [5,6,7]. However, studies on digestive asymptomatic carriage of CPE in African parturients and their newborns remain limited. Reported parturient CPE carriage rates at delivery range from 2% in Kenya [8] to 4.6% in Algeria [9]. Among newborns at birth, intestinal carriage rates vary widely, from 1.6% in Algeria [9] to 57.1% in Libreville’s military hospital, Gabon [5] and as high as 83.3% in Nigeria [10].

The global spread of CPE is driven by several factors, including poor infection prevention and control practices (e.g., inadequate hand hygiene (HH) and environmental cleaning), the overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics, high adaptability linked to multidrug resistance, biofilm production, persistence in diverse environments, and limited access to diagnostics and surveillance in many regions. To mitigate the infectious risk associated with CPE in healthcare settings, current prevention measures recommended aim to reduce transmission and protect patients from nosocomial infections. These measures include strict HH compliance, isolation of colonized or infected patients, enhanced environmental cleaning and disinfection, surveillance of high-risk patients for CPE colonization, monitoring antimicrobial resistance trends, ventilation, maintening plumbing systems (e.g., sinks, drains, traps) to prevent them from becoming reservoirs for CPE, antimicrobial stewardship to restrict broad-spectrum antibiotic use, and continuous education and training of HCWs on CPE risks and prevention [56].

In 2022, we conducted a study in the maternity and neonatology units of Franceville hospital, southeastern Gabon, which revealed a high CPE carriage rate in parturients (4.6%) at delivery, a 0.5% CPE infection rate among newborns, widespread contamination of surfaces and sinks surrounding mothers and newborns by CPE, and three out of four newborns treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics in the first 28 days of life [7]. Additionally, whole-genome sequencing of CPE strains identified clonal relationships between clinical strains and between clinical and environmental strains, suggesting parturient-to-parturient transmission and parturient acquisition of CPE from the environment. The analysis of these findings led to the implementation of three key interventions. (a) introducing a HH educational program for all HCWs in the maternity and neonatology units to address likely inadequate adherence to HH practices during care ; (b) promptly replacing all sink traps to address the extensive contamination with CPE ; and (c) minimizing antibiotic pressure by requiring a physician’s evaluation of infectious signs before initiating antibiotic therapy in newborns.

The objective of this follow-up study was to evaluate the impact of this three-pronged intervention 11 months after the initial phase and seven weeks after implementation. Specifically, we aimed to assess their effect on the asymptomatic CPE carriage rate in parturients at delivery, the CPE carriage rate in newborns, the incidence of neonatal CPE infections, and sink trap contamination. Additionally, this study sought to propose a practical strategy to mitigate the risk of CPE acquisition in the maternity setting, particularly for countries similar to Gabon.

Materials and methods

Study setting and design

The present study was conducted in the maternity and neonatology units of the regional hospital in Franceville, Gabon, from February 1rst to September 30th, 2023. The study consisted of four parts, as outlined in figure 1.

First, an observational study was carried out to assess HH compliance prior to the implementation of an educational program. This was followed by the implementation of a three pronged-intervention, which included (a) the introduction of a HH educational program for all HCWs in the maternity and neonatology units to address likely inadequate adherence to HH practices during care ; (b) the replacement of all sink traps to address re-contamination with CPE; and (c) the requirement of a physician’s evaluation of infectious signs before initiating antibiotic therapy in newborns. Then, a second observational study was carried out to assess HH compliance after the implementation of an educational program. Finally, a prospective study was conducted to assess asymptomatic CPE carriage in parturients and newborns, the incidence rate of neonatal CPE infections after the intervention, and contamination of the newly installed sink traps. The impact of the intervention on key indicators, including the asymptomatic CPE carriage rate in parturients at the time of delivery, the CPE carriage rate in neonates, the incidence of neonatal CPE infections, and the sink trap contamination was evaluated by comparing post-intervention results with data obtained during the pre-intervention study, conducted from February 22nd to July 22nd, 2022 [7].

The three pronged-intervention

Hand hygiene educational program

Between April 18th and July 3rd, 2023, an educational program was conducted in accordance with WHO guidelines (11;12). The hospital director and leaders of the maternity and neonatology units were sensitized to the importance of HH in preventing cross-transmission and nosocomial infections. The director communicated the institution’s commitment to promoting HH to both leaders and HCWs. All the HCWs from the two units (n=52) participated in the program which comprised five 2-hour sessions held in small groups. Participants were informed about the evaluation of their personal HH compliance. Training emphasized proper alcohol-based hand rubbing technique, with the completeness of hand rubbing assessed using UV light following the application of a fluorescent hand rub solution. The WHO’s “My 5 Moments of HH” guidelines were featured, and posters illustrating proper technique and reminders were displayed beside sinks and on the walls of the doctor’s duty room, desk room, delivery room, and nurse’s rooms.

Introduction of alcohol-based hand rub

Beginning April 18th, alcohol-based hand rub was prepared locally using 95% ethanol, 3% hydrogen peroxide, and 98% glycerol as an emollient, following WHO recommendations [13]. The solution was provided in 500 mL dispensers at each point of care and was refilled regularly. Hand rub consumption was monitored by recording the date each dispenser was provided and when it was emptied. A logbook was maintained to document the provision date, volume delivered, consumption end date, and quantity consumed for each unit (delivery room, maternity, neonatology).

Sink trap replacement

Based on the findings of the pre-intervention study (February 22nd to July 22nd, 2022; Dos Santos et al., 2024), which identified significant contamination of sink traps by CPE, all sink traps in the units were replaced by new ones on April 19th, 2023.

Study of practices before and after intervention

Hand hygiene study

The WHO HH observation method was used to evaluate HH compliance both before and after the intervention [12]. In February 2023, HH practices were observed using a standardized grid that followed WHO recommendations for assessing compliance with HH opportunities and the quality of hand rubbing technique. The data collected included the observer’s qualifications, the conditions of observation (setting, indications, prerequisites), and the seven steps of alcohol-based hand rubbing (palm to palm, palm to back, interlaced fingers, back of the fingers, thumbs, nails, and wrists), performed for approximately 30 seconds using a sufficient amount of product [3 ml] to cover the entire hand surface. Observed HCWs included one pediatrician, two gynecologists, 12 midwives, and 18 nurses. Students and cleaning staff were excluded from the analysis, as they were not involved in direct patient care. HH was considered performed when an HCW washed hands with soap and water and dried them with single use paper, or used alcohol-based hand rub at specific moments during patient care, as defined by the WHO HH indications. The observational data were stratified by HCW category and HH indications (before patient contact, before aseptic procedures, after patient contact, and after contact with the environment surrounding parturients and newborns). The HH compliance rate was calculated as the ratio of the number of compliant HH actions to the total number of expected HH actions. The HH compliance study was repeated seven weeks after the intervention, using the same methodology as before. Observations were conducted by the same observer (M Moussounda, a PhD student) from June 1rst to 30th, 2023. Compliance rates from the post-intervention phase were compared with those from the pre-intervention phase to assess the impact of the educational program on HH compliance. The interval between the two observation periods spanned four months (March 2023 to June 2023).

Administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics to newborns

Before the intervention, empirical antibiotic prescriptions were based on clinical suspicion of infection by midwives, without medical consultation. Antibiotics were prescribed for any newborn presenting one or more of the following clinical signs: pallor, dyspnea, moaning, cyanosis, or respiratory distress. On March 2nd, 2023, the physicians in the maternity and neonatology units decided to revise the practices regarding the administration of antibiotics to newborns. During the post-intervention phase, unlike previous practices, empirical antibiotic prescriptions were strictly guided by clinicians based on clinical suspicion of neonatal infection. The frequency of broad-spectrum antibiotic administration during the post-intervention phase was compared with that of the pre-intervention period to objectively assess the change of practices.

Microbiological study

Study of CPE asymptomatic colonization of parturients and newborns, and CPE neonatal infection

The prospective post-intervention study was conducted from July 4th to September 30th, 2023, using the same study design as the pre-intervention study conducted from February 22nd-July 22th, 2022, described previously [7]. Briefly, all consecutive parturients who gave birth from July 4th to September 30th, 2023, along with their newborns were enrolled after receiving an individual letter of information and provided consent before enrolment. For mothers, were collected information on age and clinical data, including parity, underlying pathologies, gynecological and obstetrical history, as well as any prior hospitalizations or antibiotic therapies within the last 15 days. For newborns, the collected data included sex, gestational age, birth weight, use of invasive devices, and antibiotic usage before 28 days of age. Rectal swabs were collected using a sterile swab with Amies-type preservation medium (Copan Italia SPA, Italy) immediately before delivery for mothers and 30 minutes after birth for newborns. In the presence of clinical signs suggestive of infection (jaundice, respiratory distress, whimpering, fever, cyanosis) during the 28-day follow-up of the newborns, blood culture samples were taken (BacTAlert PF Plus pediatric bottle; bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) before initiating antibiotic therapy. Blood cultures were all plated on CHROMagar Orientation medium (CHROMagar, Paris, France) on day 7. Rectal swabs were screened for third-generation cephalosporin- and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales using CHROMagar ESBL and mSuperCARBA chromogenic agar plates (CHROMagar, Paris, France). After incubation at 37°C for 24 hours under aerobic conditions, each colony morphotype was identified using Matrix-assisted laser desorption-ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (microflex LT MALDI-TOF, MBT Compass software version 4.2.100.19; Bruker Daltonics, France). Enterobacterales were investigated for extended-spectrum beta-latamase production, and carbapenemase production when Ertapenem MIC > 0.5 mg/l, using both the immunochromatographic test RESIST-5 O.K.N.V.I (Coris BioConcept, Belgium) and a PCR test for molecular detection of the genes encoding for the five carbapenemases of primary public health concern (blaOXA-48, blaNDM, blaKPC, blaVIM and blaIMP genes) [14].

Study of CPE environmental contamination

Environmental swabs were taken from all sinks in the facilities, as well as from a multiple surface near parturients and newborns (i.e., all incubators, cradles, maternal beds, all tables and desks, the two baby scales, as well as furniture) (Table 6). The surfaces were sampled twice, firsty in August and secondly in September 2023. Sink samples were collected weekly using a sterile dry cotton swab, previously moistened with sterile saline, applied to the sink surface using horizontal and vertical strokes with slight pressure, then placed in a labeled transport tube and sent to the laboratory within two hours of sampling. Surfaces were selected based on their proximity to patients and frequent handling by healthcare personnel during care, as well as their direct contact with the newborn, in order to identify areas at high risk of bacterial transmission. from May to August 2023. Environmental swabs were processed such as rectal swabs for CPE detection.

Whole-genome sequencing

Pre-intervention WGS data were previously published [7], and WGS in the current manuscript was performed on post-intervention isolates. All clinical and environmental CPE isolates underwent WGS using next-generation sequencers. Purified genomic DNA (obtained using the DNeasy kit, Qiagen) for each strain was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, California), generating 100-base pairs paired-end reads and using barcodes following the Nextera XT kit (Illumina) per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Read quality was assessed using Trimmomatic v0.36 with the following parameters for paired end reads: SLIDINGWINDOW:10:30 and a minimum read length of 80. Genome assembly was performed using the SPADES v3.12.0 assembler. Assembled genomes and plasmids (identified using mlplasmid accessible via https://sarredondo.shinyapps.io/mlplasmids/) were annotated using the RAST server (https://rast.nmpdr.org/rast.cgi). Multilocus sequence typing analysis (in silico) was conducted using annotated genomes and submitted to the Center for Genomic Epidemiology database (accessible via https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/). The phylogenetic relationships of all isolates were investigated through genomic single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based analysis (CSIPhylogeny). Additional tools, available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/, were used for detecting antibiotic resistance (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/), mobile genetic elements. food.dtu.dk/services/MobileElementFinder/), and virulence factors. All genome and plasmid sequences have been deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive database under accession number PRJEB75950.

Impact of the three pronged-intervention

Three CPE indicators (e.g., CPE carriage rate in parturients, CPE carriage rate in newborns, and the incidence of CPE neonatal infection), and the contamination of sinks by CPE, were compared between the pre-intervention phase (from February 22nd-July 22th, 2022) and the post-intervention phase (from July 4th to September 30th, 2023). These comparisons aimed to investigate the impact of the intervention on reducing the risk of CPE acquisition by parturients and newborns in the maternity and neonatology units of the hospital.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test to compare categorical variables. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

The parturients and newborns enrolled

The parturients

A total of 203 parturients were enrolled in the present post-intervention phase of the study (table 1). They were predominantly young, from the local area, and gave birth mostly via vaginal delivery. Co-morbidities were observed in one out of four parturients, and risk factors for CPE carriage in one out of two, including recent hospitalization (24.6%), and recent antibiotherapy (36.4%). No significant difference was observed between the parturients included in the pre- and post-intervention phases of the study.

The newborns

A total of 209 newborns were included (table 2), with a gestational age of 39 weeks and a birth weight of 2,940 grams. One out of four presented at least one risk factor for infection, one out of three showed at least one sign of infection at delivery, and 32 (15.3%) received intravenous antibiotic therapy, including gentamicin for 3 days (3-5 mg/kg/24h) combined with ceftriaxone for 5-7 days (20-50 mg/kg/24h). Two newborns (0.9%) had a mother carrying a CPE. Four in utero deaths, and two deaths within the first 28 days of life were reported during this study phase. We observed between the newborns included in the pre- and post-intervention phases of the study : first, the frequency of mothers with premature rupture of membranes (2% during the pre-intervention phase vs 9.6% in the post-intervention phase; p = 0.002); and second, neonatal respiratory distress (18.7% during the pre-intervention phase vs 7.7% in the post-intervention phase; p = 0.001). The difference in the evaluation of infectious signs between the two periods, specifically the strict requirement for a medical evaluation of infectious signs by a pediatrician before administering antibiotherapy to newborns during the post-intervention phase, may explain the last difference mentioned between the two phases.

Study of practices before and after intervention

Hand hygiene compliance

Before intervention. A total of 646 observations were made on 33 HCWs before the implementation of the educational HH program and the introduction of hand rubbing (table 3): 99 observations on three physicians, 261 on 12 midwives, and 286 on 18 nurses. These 646 observations were distributed across 210 opportunities for HH before patient contact, 210 after patient contact, 69 before an aseptic procedure, and 157 after contact with the environment. HH compliance results are presented in table 3. Out of the 646 HH opportunities studied, compliant HH was observed in 19 cases (2.9%), accounting for 121 non-compliant HH gestures and 506 missed opportunities. HH compliance rate varied by HCW category and HH opportunity. It was significantly higher among physicians (9.1%) compared to nurses (0%) and midwifes (3.8%; p<0.001), with physicians occasionally observed with their own bottle of alcohol-based hand rub solution, provided by medical representatives. The HH compliance rate was relatively similar across different opportunities, with 5.8% of compliant HH before aseptic procedure, 5.2% before patient contact, 0% after patient contact, and 2.5% after contact with the environment. After intervention. A total of 574 HH opportunities were observed seven weeks after the intervention began, i.e., the implementation of the educational HH program and the introduction of hand rubbing. The HH compliance rate was significantly higher during the post-intervention phase (70.6% vs 2.9% during the pre-intervention phase; p < 0.001) (table 3). An increasing trend in compliance was observed across all HCW categories and HH moments.

HH was considered performed when an HCW used alcohol-based hand rub at specific moments during patient care, as defined by the WHO HH indications. Compliance was defined as adhering to the seven key steps of hand rubbing. These steps could be performed in any order but had to last approximately 30 seconds with a sufficient amount of product to cover the entire hand surface.

Administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics to newborns

In line with the decision to mandate that a physician’s evaluation of infectious signs in newborns before initiating any antibiotic therapy during the post-intervention phase, the frequency of broad-spectrum antibiotic administration significantly declined during the post-intervention phase compared to the pre-intervention period (74.9% of the neonates during the pre-intervention phase vs 15.3% in the post-intervention phase; p <0.001; table 2).

Finally, we did observe the two expected changes in practices during the post-intervention phase, namely a significant increase in HH compliance across all HCW categories, and a reduction in the number of newborns treated with antibiotics.

CPE indicators

CPE carriage in parturients

Extended spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBLE) digestive carriage was observed in 32 out of 203 parturients (15.8%) at the time of delivery, a rate significantly lower rather the one observed during the pre-intervention phase (40.1%; p < 0.001). Of these, 12 parturients carried at least two different species, resulting in the identification of 48 ESBLE, mostly belonging to E. coli (n=21) and K. pneumoniae (n=18). CPE digestive carriage was found in two parturients (1.0%), a rate also lower than the one observed during the pre-intervention phase (4.6%; p = 0.059). One parturient carried two species, leading to the identification of three CPE (table 4).

CPE carriage and infection in newborns

Thirty minutes after delivery, ESBLE carriage was observed in one out of the 209 neonates (0.5%), whereas no CPE carriage was observed, similar to the pre-intervention study phase. As with parturients, ESBLE carriage significantly declined after the intervention (5.4% during the pre-intervention phase vs 0.5% in the post-intervention phase; p = 0.007; table 5). Out of the 205 newborns followed up to 28 days of life during the post-intervention study phase, two (1.0%) presented clinical signs of infection, both within 48 hours of birth. Blood cultures taken for the two newborns yielded positive culture of Enterobacterales (table 5), specifically one E. coli and one K. pneumoniae.

None of the followed newborns developed infections from ESBLE or CPE within 28 days of birth. We did not observe an increase in bacteremia cases which could have been a harmful consequence of the reduction in antibiotic use. The incidence of early-onset and late-onset bacteremia significantly declined after the intervention (8.1% in the pre-intervention phase vs 1.0% in the post-intervention phase; p = 0.001), with a similar trend observed for ESBLE-associated bacteremia (5.0% of the neonates before intervention vs none after; p = 0.003). CPE environmental contamination

Although most surfaces were swabbed during both phases (pre- and post-intervention), some differences in sampling occurred between the two phases due to changes in the furniture present on the day of the post-intervention study (Table 6).

This made comparisons difficult, but in both phases, surface contamination remained low. During the post-intervention phase, one out of the 127 different surfaces sampled, showed contamination by a carbapenemase-producing Leclercia adecarboxylata. In addition, all the sinks used by HCWs were demonstrated to be contaminated by CPE (n=8; S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S9, S10). In all cases, CPE cultures obtained from surfaces and sinks were positive, with a high microbial load (>10⁶ CFU). To investigate the dynamics of sink re-contamination after their replacement, all the sinks were sampled once a week, from May 2nd to September 4th, 2023 (130 samples). Three weeks after replacement, two sinks (S1 and S10) were found to be positive, and from the nineteenth week onwards, all sinks were found to be positive at least once. A total of 38 CPE was identified, belonging to six species, and contaminating between one and six of the eight sinks: C. freundii (six sinks), E. hermannii (five sinks), E. cloacae (five sinks), K. pneumoniae (two sinks), K. oxytoca (one sink), and C. amalonaticus (one sink).

Whole genome sequencing analysis

The 43 CPE strains identified during the post-intervention phase (three CPE colonizing parturients and 40 environmental CPE) were subjected to WGS. The sequences of these 43 CPE strains were analyzed alongside those of the 25 CPE strains identified during the pre-intervention study phase (12 CPE colonizing parturients, one responsible for a neonatal infection, and 12 environmental CPE; table 7).

The virulome and the resistome of the CPE are provided in Supplementary table 1. Clinical CPE. The CPE isolated before and after intervention differed by species (E. coli accounted for 76.9% of strains during the pre-intervention phasec compared to none after intervention; p = 0.069) and by genes encoding carbapenemase production (69.2% of clinical CPE harboured blaOXA48-like genes during the pre-intervention phase, vs. 33.3% after intervention; 30.8% of clinical CPE harboured blaNDM genes during the pre-intervention phase, vs. 66.7% after intervention; p = 0.557). While these differences were not statistically significant, they document the disappearance of blaOXA48-like E. coli following the intervention. Environmental CPE. Unlike clinical CPE, no major differences were observed in environmental CPE contamination before and after intervention. During both phases, the majority of environmental CPE belonged to the Citrobacter, Klebsiella and Enterobacter genera (75.0% of contaminated surfaces and 85.7% of contaminated sinks during the pre-intervention phase, vs 100% of the contaminated sinks after the intervention). Genes associated with carbapenemase production were predominantly blaNDM genes (100% of contaminated surfaces and 80% (4/5) of contaminated sinks during the pre-intervention phase, vs 100% of contaminated surfaces and sinks (8/8) after intervention). Furthermore, blaNDM-7 remained the predominant resistance mechanism in both periods.

Groups of clonally related strains

The clonal relationships between the clinical and environmental CPE strains are shown in figure 2. Clonality between isolates was defined based on pairwise comparisons showing fewer than 12 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as previously suggested in a large-scale study of Escherichia coli clinical isolates (PMID: 34485958). SNP analysis did not reveal any genetic relatedness among the clinical CPE isolates recovered after the intervention, nor between clinical isolates from the two study phases. Contrarily to the intial study phase, SNP analysis did not reveal nay genetic relatedness between clinical and environmental strains identified after the intervention (Table 8). C. freundii ST91 carrying blaNDM-7 was found contaminating sinks both before and after their replacement. Different sinks were contaminated by the same clones : C. freundii ST91 blaNDM-7 in five sinks (S1, S2, S3, S6 and S9), C. freundii ST589 blaNDM-7 in four sinks (S1, S2, S3, and S10), E. hermannii blaNDM-7 in five sinks (S1, S3, S4, S5 and S10), and E. cloacae ST32 blaNDM-7in two sinks (S1 and S5). These findings suggest that these clones—particularly those of the C. freundii species—are able to persist in the wet environment despite bleaching and disseminate throughout the plumbing system.

Overall impact of the intervention

Following the implementation of the three-pronged intervention, significant changes were observed in CPE and ESBLE carriage and infections. These included a decrease in asymptomatic ESBLE and CPE carriage rates among parturients, a reduction in ESBLE carriage rate in newborns, a decline in the incidence of neonatal bacteremia caused by Enterobacterales, and fewer clonal relationships between clinical and environmental CPE, suggesting reduced parturient-to-parturient transmission and diminished acquisition of CPE from the environment. Notably, these changes were observed despite the replacement of the sink traps, as their contamination remained unaffected, with persistent evidence of CPE contamination at all sinks in both units during the post-intervention phase.

Discussion

The present study follows an initial study conducted in the maternity and neonatology units of Franceville Hospital in 2022 [7], which provided evidence suggesting a high risk of CPE acquisition in parturients and newborns, likely due to inadequate adherence of HCWs to HH practices during care, as well as the exposure of parturients and newborns to a care environment contaminated by CPE, all within a context of extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in newborns.

The prevention of the spread of CPE in healthcare settings relies on high HH compliance rate among HCWs, specifically hand rubbing with alcohol-based solutions before and after patient contact [11, 12], heightened vigilance regarding environmental reservoirs surrounding patients, such as contaminated sink drains, which are increasingly identified as significant sources of CPE contamination on nearby surfaces and drivers of hospital outbreaks [15–17], and the proper use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. In response to the findings of our first study, a three pronged-intervention was implemented, consisting of implementing hand rubbing and introducing an educational HH program, replacing contaminated sink traps in the maternity and neonatology units, along with restricting the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in newborns. This study, as the post-intervention phase, aimed to assess, 11 months after the first study, the impact of these interventions on CPE carriage rate in parturients, the incidence of neonatal CPE infections, and CPE environmental contamination in the units. The impact of such interventions on parturient and newborn CPE colonization and infection in a subtropical context had remained largely unexplored. To our knowledge, this study represents a unique investigation aimed at demonstrating the impact of these interventions on CPE acquisition in a maternity and neonatal setting.

Before alcohol-based hand rubs were made available to all healthcare professionals, handwashing with soap was the technique most frequently used by professionals for hand hygiene. However, water supply was often interrupted, and soap was not consistently available at all handwashing stations. Additionally, no single-use paper towels were provided for hand drying before the study and during the pre-intervention phase. Thus, by observing HCWs before or after contact with a parturient or a neonate, after contact with the environment, and before an aseptic procedure before the intervention, we demonstrated very poor HH compliance by HCWs prior to the intervention. The HH compliance rate was similar to those reported previously in Ethiopia (21;22), Mali [23], and Côte d'Ivoire [24], linked to the absence of satisfactory conditions for conducting HH (i.e., lack of hand rub solution, soap, or single-use towels to dry hands after washing), the absence of HH promotion during care, and the financial priorities focused on combating malaria and AIDS. Access to hand rubbing solutions, along with

Training on HH gestures, was well received by all the HCWs. The introduction of alcohol-based hand rubs, which require neither water nor paper towels, facilitated hand hygiene practices and was well received by the staff. Their regular availability strengthened caregivers’ motivation. Training sessions and educational materials enhanced understanding of the importance of hand hygiene, leading to increased awareness and lasting behavioral change. The second evaluation of HH compliance conducted three months after the intervention showed a highly significant increase among HCWs, confirming the effectiveness of the WHO multimodal strategy for improving HH compliance [25]. Our results are comparable to those obtained in studies evaluating the effectiveness of the same strategy for improving HH conducted in Switzerland (42%), Kuwait (42.9%), Taiwan (43%), South Africa (61.6%) and Taiwan (62.3%) [26–30]. However, as compliance rates did not reach 75%, our data highlight the need for further educational sessions to achieve a higher level of HH compliance among HCWs, as previously demonstrated [32]. Cross-transmission of pathogens via hands is the leading cause of nosocomial infections [19]. During patient care, HCWs’ hands become contaminated with microorganisms that are ubiquitous in the environment, with a statistically significant association between HCWs’ hand contamination and the type and duration of patient care activities [20]. Therefore, it is likely that, in contrast to the pre-intervention phase, the significant decline in the CPE carriage rate among parturients (1.0%) and the absence of evidence suggesting parturient-to-parturient transmission or parturient acquisition of CPE from the environment during the post-intervention phase can be attributed, at least in part, to the increased HH compliance observed following the implementation of the educational program and the provision of hand rub solutions to the HCWs.

The bacterial species responsible for asymptomatic CPE carriage in parturients differed significantly between the pre- and post-intervention phases, with the disappearance of E. coli in the latter. E. coli is known for its ease of transmission, particularly within family settings, due to close physical contact, the shared use of towels, and suboptimal HH practices [33]. During the pre-intervention phase, clonal relationships among E. coli strains colonizing parturients were evident when HH compliance was low. In the hospital settings where HH compliance is adequate, microbiological studies have demonstrated the absence of patient-to-patient transmisison of E. coli [34]. The absence of carbapenemase-producing E. coli in the post-intervention phase suggests a significant impact of improved HH compliance on reducing transmission between parturients in the maternity and neonatology units.

Successful HH campaigns have been linked to reductions in the incidence of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections in hospitals [31]. The single neonatal infection caused by a CPE in the study was diagnosed during the pre-intervention phase. Given that the CPE strain involved in the infection was not carried by the mother of the affected newborn, it is likely—although unproven—that the newborn acquired the CPE from the care environment. During the post-intervention phase, the increased adherence to HH among HCWs likely contributed to reducing newborns’ exposure to CPE, resulting in no increase in cases of infection among neonates.

Although the CPE carriage rate decreased following the three pronged-intervention, 1.0% of parturients still carried CPE upon arrival at the maternity unit during the post-intervention phase. The colonizing CPE species included Klebsiella, Citrobacter and Enterobacter, consistent with findings from studies on parturients in Algeria [9], and Kenya [8]. Additionally, CPE contamination of the environment surrounding parturients and neonates was evident during both study phases, with CPE isolates from the environment predominantly identified as K. pneumoniae, C. freundii, and E. cloacae−three species known for their ability to survive in biofilms and their frequent involvement in CPE outbreaks. Several studies have demonstrated that contaminated sink traps in hospitals can serve as reservoirs for CPE, facilitating their transmission to patients [35–37]. The transmission mechanism from sinks to patients is likely linked to contaminated aerosols generated by running water, which disperses bacteria from biofilms in the sink into the surrounding environment. These bacteria may contaminate the hands of HCWs and nearby surfaces through splashes when hygiene practices are inadequate. Studies investigating CPE outbreaks have identified several intervention strategies, including the replacement of sink traps and disinfection with sodium hypochlorite. In our study, CPE contamination affected most sinks in the units and was not significantly influenced by the replacement of the sinks prior to the second phase of the study. The persistence of CPE contamination, the rapid re-colonization of sinks by CPE following replacement, and the identification of the same C. freundii ST91 blaNDM-7 clone in the sinks before and after intervention corroborate previous reports of the challenges in decontaminating hospital plumbing systems. Given that the CPE carried by parturients during the post-intervention phase belonged to Klebsiella, Enterobacter, and Citrobacter species, which heavily contaminate the sinks in both units, it is possible—although we were unable to demonstrate it in this study—that the CPE may have been acquired by the parturients from unidentified environmental sources during the study period, potentially facilitated by the lack of regular cleaning of surfaces near the water points in the healthcare environment. These findings underscore the necessity of implementing a comprehensive environmental cleaning program in the maternity and neonatology units, which was not feasible at the time of the study.

The majority of CPE identified during both phases of the study belong to international clones known to cause severe infections in humans. For instance, E. coli ST167, reported in newborns and children from Tanzania [38], China [39], the United States [40], and Asia [41]; E. coli ST410, a major extra-intestinal pathogen isolated from humans, animals, and food products, responsible for septicemia and hospital outbreaks (42;43); E. coli ST2083, identified in children from Angola [44], Qatar [45], and India [46]; E. coli ST38 [47]; K. pneumoniae ST307 and K. pneumoniae ST14, two hypervirulent clones associated with hospital outbreaks [48–50]; and C. freundii ST95, previously linked to an environment-associated outbreak in an Australian hospital [51]. The genes associated with carbapenemase production carried by the CPE strains from our study were predominantly blaOXA-48-like in E. coli and K. pneumoniae strains, and blaNDM-5 and blaNDM-7 in strains from Citrobacter, Klebsiella, and Enterobacter species. These findings align with a recent review analyzing 141 studies on CPE across African countries [52], and several studies identifying K. pneumoniae blaOXA-48-like in Algerian [9] and Moroccan newborns [53], as well as E. coli blaOXA-48 and blaOXA-181 and K. pneumoniae blaOXA-181 in Central-, West- and North-Africa (7;54;55). The detection of these clones in Gabonese parturients who had not traveled supports that these international clones are circulating within the local population. Contributing factors may include the continued presence of mining, oil, and forestry operators from China, India, and Pakistan, who have established long-term interactions with local communities, as well as the growing importation of food products from China and India, and frequent travel for professional or medical reasons. However the precise mechanisms underlying CPE acquisition remain to be investigated. Overall, these findings highlight the growing threat of carbapenem resistance (2;4) and emphasize the need to adapt prevention strategies to address the challenges posed by antibiotic resistance in Gabon.

The combination of ampicillin and gentamicin is currently the first-line empirical treatment for neonatal infections, while third-generation cephalosporins are recommended for second-line therapy [18]. However, in the absence of diagnostic tools for neonatal bacterial infections at Franceville Hospital, the physicians in the maternity and neonatology units currently treat newborns with clinical signs of infection using gentamicin and ceftriaxone. This treatment was administered to three out of four newborns during the pre-intervention study, and to one out of seven newborns after medical validation of suspected infection signs became mandatory. While antibiotic therapy promotes colonization by multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales, it is likely that the reduction in the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics during the post-intervention phase may have limited the risk of colonization by these microorganisms in newborns.

Our study has some limitations

First, it was conducted in a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to all hospitals in Gabon. Second, the size of the population of parturients and newborns studied may have been insufficient to provide highly robust results, particularly regarding the infectious risk associated with CPE in newborns. The analyses relied entirely on unadjusted comparisons, as no multivariate models were applied to adjust for confounding variables (e.g., premature rupture of membranes, respiratory distress, antibiotic exposure). Given the multiple outcomes assessed, the risk of a type I error is substantial. Studying a larger population of parturients and newborns would have allowed for adjusted logistic regression models. Third, with only a single colonization assessment of the newborns conducted 30 minutes after birth and no follow-up, we did not investigate the colonization status of hospitalized newborns (57); Fourth, the direct observation method used to assess HH practices may have introduced bias. The observations were conducted by a doctoral student who was known to the healthcare staff. The presence of the observer could have influenced the behavior of the HCWs, a phenomenon known as the Hawthorne effect. Nevertheless, direct observation remains the gold standard for monitoring hand hygiene practices. Finally, before-after designs are vulnerable to confounding, secular trends, and regression to the mean, and causal inference should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Finally, regarding hand hygiene compliance, the sustainability of the intervention may have been overestimated, given the short interval between the intervention and the second evaluation of hand hygiene practices.

Conclusion

While the hospital environment remained heavily contaminated with CPE during the post-intervention study period, the absence of evidence for cross-transmission between parturients or for CPE acquisition by a parturient from the environment highlights the crucial role of healthcare workers' HH in the observed outcomes. This study strongly suggests a reduction in the risk of CPE acquisition by parturients and neonates following the implementation of hand rubbing, along with the optimization of broad-spectrum antibiotic use in newborns. The significant reduction in ESBLE and CPE carriage among parturients and neonates strongly supports the implementation of such programs across all maternity and neonatology units, as well as in intensive care units across the country. While hypervirulent clones circulate in Gabon, ongoing training for HCWs, access to hand rub solutions, and awareness campaigns on HH practices and the overuse of antibiotics should be rapidly implemented to prevent neonatal infections associated with these CPE.
