Dietary behavior and its association with sleep-disordered breathing symptoms in Taiwanese children
Authors: Hai-Hua Chuang, Ruei-Shan Hu, Wan-Ni Lin, Li-Jen Hsin, Tuan-Jen Fang, Hsueh-Yu Li, Li-Ang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep-disordered breathing (SDB) is highly prevalent in children and is associated with significant cognitive, metabolic, and quality-of-life (QOL) impairments. While obesity is a primary risk factor, the role of specific dietary behaviors as independent, modifiable contributors to SDB symptom severity remains underexplored.PurposeWe aimed to (1) examine the association between dietary intake and SDB symptom severity in children, and (2) develop and validate a dietary-based prediction model for moderate-to-severe SDB (msSDB).MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed baseline data from 406 children (aged 5.0–12.9 years) with and without SDB symptoms. Caregivers completed a validated 25-item Short Food Frequency Questionnaire to assess dietary intake. SDB symptom severity was measured using the obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)-18 questionnaire, with a total score ≥ 60 defining msSDB. Multivariable regression models were used to identify dietary associations. A dietary prediction score was developed and its performance assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, followed by external validation in a separate cohort (n = 24).ResultsAmong 406 participants (mean [SD] age, 8.0 [2.1] years; 174 girls [42.9%]), 80 children (19.7%) had msSDB. Higher intake of orange juice, sweets, chocolate, rice, and processed meats was significantly associated with greater total OSA-18 scores and worse subscale scores across domains. Conversely, fish for dinner and savory snacks were inversely associated with symptom burden. A dietary prediction model demonstrated acceptable discrimination for msSDB (area under the ROC curve [AUC] = 0.78; 95% CI, 0.72–0.84). The model showed 79.6% accuracy, 56.3% sensitivity, and 85.3% specificity, with comparable performance in the validation cohort.ConclusionsSpecific dietary behaviors are associated with SDB symptom severity in children. A simple dietary-based score can help identify children at risk for clinically significant SDB, supporting the investigation of dietary interventions in pediatric SDB management.Keywords: Sleep-disordered breathing, Dietary behavior, Child, Quality of life, Prediction model, Nutrition

Introduction

Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) in children encompasses a continuum of conditions ranging from habitual snoring to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and remains frequently underdiagnosed in clinical practice [1]. Epidemiological data estimate the prevalence of habitual snoring in children at approximately 10 to 12% [2], while OSA affects between 1.2% and 5.7% of the pediatric population [1, 3]. The clinical burden of pediatric SDB is considerable, with well-documented associations with cognitive and behavioral disturbances [1–4], metabolic dysregulation [3, 5, 6], and elevated cardiovascular risk [3, 5, 7]. Importantly, these symptoms can significantly impair health-related quality of life (QOL), underscoring the need to identify modifiable risk factors that may inform early intervention strategies [1, 8].

Although obesity is a recognized contributor to SDB, emerging evidence suggests that specific dietary behaviors may independently influence SDB severity [3, 8, 9]. Mechanistically, these effects may be mediated through systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [1, 6, 9, 10]. Prior studies have linked diets high in processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages to increased risk of OSA [8, 10–12]. However, much of the existing literature has focused on polysomnography-confirmed OSA or has examined diet primarily in relation to obesity [6, 13]. A critical gap remains in understanding how specific dietary patterns relate directly to the subjective symptom burden and QOL impairment experienced by children across the broader SDB spectrum.

To address this gap, the present study had two primary aims. First, we examined the association between specific dietary intakes, as measured by a validated 25-item Short Food Frequency Questionnaire (SFFQ) [14], and the severity of SDB symptoms, assessed using the OSA-18 questionnaire—a validated tool for evaluating symptom-related QOL in children [15]. Second, we aimed to develop and validate a novel, dietary-based prediction model to identify children at risk for clinically significant SDB symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study is a secondary analysis of baseline data from two single-center cohorts prospectively enrolled at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan between January 2017 and November 2022 [4, 8]. The original studies recruited children with polysomnography-confirmed OSA [4] and healthy peers without habitual snoring [8] to assess dietary behaviors and OSA-related QOL. Both studies received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Medical Foundation (Nos. 202000873B0 and 202201649B0). Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from children aged 6 years or older in accordance with institutional policies [4]. All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [16] and were reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [17].

Participants

Eligible participants were school-aged children aged 5.0 to 12.9 years recruited from a tertiary medical center in Taoyuan City, Taiwan. This specific age range was selected to represent the primary school-aged population, ensuring that the dietary behaviors captured—including culturally specific staples—were relevant to local regional habits.

Children with OSA were included if they had an apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) of ≥ 5.0 events per hour or ≥ 2.0 events per hour in the presence of comorbidities such as elevated blood pressure or learning difficulties [4]. Healthy controls were included if they had no OSA-related symptoms, including habitual snoring, sleep-disordered breathing, chronic mouth breathing, excessive daytime sleepiness, or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [8]. Children were excluded if they had a known history of chronic disorders—such as neuromuscular, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, craniofacial, or inflammatory conditions—requiring multimodal treatment, or if they were receiving long-term medications [4, 8].

According to the Department of Household Registration in Taiwan, 177,125 children aged 5 to 12 years resided in Taoyuan City in 2021. The mean annual family income in that year was 787,830 New Taiwan Dollars (approximately 25,000 USD). Most caregivers reported Han Chinese ancestry. Selected demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population have been reported previously [4, 7, 8, 18].

Dietary behavior assessment

Caregivers reported their child’s dietary intake using the validated 25-item SFFQ [8]. While the original SFFQ includes 23 food items or food groups [14], we explicitly supplemented the instrument for this study with two culturally relevant items—rice and noodles—to accurately capture the staple dietary patterns and actual consumption of children in Taiwan [8].

The comprehensive assessment included nine questions pertained to beverages, including full-fat milk, low-fat milk (1.5% fat), semi-skimmed milk (0.7% fat), skimmed milk, orange juice, fruit drinks, and soft drinks (with or without sugar). Additional items assessed the frequency of consumption of potatoes, vegetables, fruits/berries, potato chips, wholemeal bread, fish for dinner (excluding spreads), pizza, hamburgers/hot dogs/kebabs, sweets, chocolate, savory snacks, peanuts, cod liver oil/vitamin supplements, butter or margarine on bread, rice, and noodles. Participants and caregivers jointly completed the questionnaire, recalling dietary habits over the previous month. Drink consumption was scored using the following scale: 0 = never/seldom, 1 = 1–3 glasses/month, 2 = 1–3 glasses/week, 3 = 4–6 glasses/week, 4 = 1–3 glasses/day, 5 = 4–6 glasses/day, and 6 = ≥ 7 glasses/day. Half a liter was defined as equivalent to three glasses. For solid foods, frequency was scored as follows: 0 = never/seldom, 1 = 1–3 times/month, 2 = 1–3 times/week, 3 = 4–6 times/week, 4 = once/day, 5 = twice/day, 6 = three times/day, and 7 = ≥ four times/day. The question about butter/margarine usage on bread was dichotomous (1 = yes, 0 = no). The SFFQ has demonstrated moderate to excellent reproducibility (intraclass correlation coefficients: 0.58–0.84) and acceptable test–retest reliability [19, 20]. It has been validated for ranking food item/group intake [19], assessing dietary behaviors [8, 21, 22], and evaluating adherence to dietary guidelines [23].

SDB symptom and QOL measurement

OSA-related QOL was assessed using the caregiver-reported Chinese version of the OSA-18 questionnaire [24]. The OSA-18 includes 18 items scored on a 7-point ordinal scale, yielding a total score between 18 and 126. It assesses five domains: sleep disturbance (4 items), physical suffering (4 items), emotional distress (3 items), daytime problems (3 items), and caregiver concerns (4 items). The instrument has demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability [15]. SDB severity was categorized as mild (total score 40–59), moderate (60–79), or severe (≥ 80) [25]. Herein, msSDB was defined as a total OSA-18 score of ≥ 60 [15, 25], whereas none-to-mild SDB (nmSDB) was defined as a total score < 60.

Development and validation of prediction model for msSDB

A multivariable logistic regression including all variables with a forward selection method was conducted to identify independent predictors of msSDB, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subsequently, a dietary-based predictive model was developed. The optimal classification threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden index [26]. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Discriminatory ability was classified as follows: AUC 0.5–0.7 = poor, 0.7–0.8 = acceptable, 0.8–0.9 = excellent, and > 0.9 = outstanding [27]. Finally, an external validation was performed by applying this pre-defined model and cutoff to a separate, prospectively enrolled cohort of 24 children with SDB who were assessed using both SFFQ and OSA-18 questionnaires between July 1, 2025 and August 22, 2025. For both the primary and validation cohorts, standard classification metrics [28]—including accuracy, sensitivity (true-positive rate), specificity (true-negative rate), false-positive rate, false-negative rate, and F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall)—were calculated to evaluate the model's real-world predictive performance.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted between January 23, 2020, and August 22, 2025. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations; categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons based on SDB symptoms were performed using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables (with homogeneity of variance confirmed by Levene’s test) and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Associations between dietary intake and SDB symptom severity were examined using partial Pearson correlation coefficients and multiple linear regression models. Models were adjusted for age and sex. Adjusted |r-values| evaluated correlation as ‘weak’ (< 0.40), ‘moderate’ (0.40–0.69), ‘strong’ (0.70–0.99), and ‘perfect’ (= 1.0) [29]. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with a threshold of < 5 considered acceptable.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism version 10.3.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA).

Results

Participants

A total of 406 children participated (mean [SD] age, 8.0 [2.1] years; 174 girls [42.9%] and 232 boys [57.1%]; 66 had OSA diagnosis [16.3%] and 340 had no OSA diagnosis [83.7%]) were included in the final analysis. Based on the OSA-18 questionnaire, 80 children (19.7%) were classified as having msSDB, while the remaining 326 (80.3%) were classified into the nmSDB group.

The baseline characteristics of these two groups are detailed in Table 1. Compared to the nmSDB group, children with msSDB were significantly more likely to be boys (71.3% vs. 53.7%) and had a markedly higher prevalence of a prior physician diagnosis of OSA (71.3% vs. 2.8%). Age did not differ significantly between the groups. By definition, all five OSA-18 domain subscores were significantly worse in the msSDB group.

Difference in dietary behaviors between msSDB and nmSDB

Regarding dietary behaviors assessed by the SFFQ, children in the msSDB group had significantly higher mean intake frequencies of orange juice, fruit drink with sugar, hamburgers/hot dogs/kebabs, sweets, chocolate, rice, and noodles, compared to those with nmSDB. No group differences were observed for vegetable or fruit intake.

Correlation among dietary behaviors and OSA-related QOL

Weak correlations (|r|≤ 0.30) were observed between age- and sex-adjusted SFFQ item and group scales and OSA-18 total and subscale scores (Fig. 1). In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for age and sex, higher intake of orange juice, hamburgers/hot dogs/kebabs, sweets, and rice was independently associated with higher OSA-18 total scores, whereas fish consumption for dinner was inversely associated (Table 2).

Development of dietary-based predictive model for msSDB

In multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex, several dietary behaviors were independently associated with the likelihood of having msSDB (Table 3). Higher intake of orange juice (OR, 1.66; 95% CI 1.09–2.53), hamburgers/hot dogs/kebabs (OR, 1.85; 95% CI 1.23–2.75), sweets (OR, 1.84; 95% CI 1.23–2.75), chocolate (OR, 1.50; 95% CI 1.04–2.17), and rice (OR, 1.55; 95% CI 1.22–1.96) were significantly associated with increased odds of msSDB. In contrast, consumption of fish for dinner (OR, 0.81; 95% CI 0.67–0.98) and savory snacks (OR, 0.72; 95% CI 0.54–0.96) was associated with reduced odds.

Performance of the dietary-based msSDB prediction model

The AUC of the continuous msSDB score was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.72–0.84), indicating acceptable discriminative ability. Using a cutoff of > 56, the msSDB predictive model yielded an AUC of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.65–0.78) (Fig. 2A). Classification performance for this cutoff included an accuracy of 79.6%, sensitivity of 56.3%, specificity of 85.3%, false-positive rate of 14.7%, false-negative rate of 43.8%, and F1 score of 52.0% (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we found that specific, modifiable dietary behaviors were significantly associated with the symptom severity and QOL in pediatric SDB. Our results show that higher consumption of items like orange juice, sweets, chocolate, rice, and processed meats was independently linked to a greater symptom burden. In contrast, higher intake of fish and savory snacks was associated with protective effects. Building on these findings, we developed and validated a novel, dietary-based prediction score that demonstrated acceptable accuracy for identifying children with clinically significant SDB. These results highlight the potential of using simple dietary assessments as a practical, non-invasive tool to identify at-risk children.

The positive association between high-sugar and processed food intake and worse SDB-related symptoms is consistent with prior literature linking such dietary patterns to systemic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and obesity—factors known to exacerbate airway collapsibility and respiratory instability [30, 31]. High glycemic foods such as sweets, chocolate, and white rice may disrupt glucose homeostasis and provoke inflammatory responses, while processed meats are rich in saturated fats and preservatives that may impair upper airway muscle tone and promote inflammation [32, 33]. Additionally, calorie-dense, nutrient-poor diets contribute to weight gain and central adiposity, which are major risk factors for OSA [34, 35].

Conversely, fish consumption was inversely associated with msSDB, suggesting a potential protective role. Prior studies have demonstrated that regular intake of marine fish—particularly those rich in omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D—is associated with better sleep quality, shorter sleep latency, and improved daytime functioning [36]. In children, improved sleep quality has also been shown to partially mediate the positive effects of fish intake on cognitive performance [37]. These findings support dietary guidance that includes fish as a beneficial component of sleep health.

The inverse association between savory snack intake and msSDB observed in our study is novel and warrants further investigation. Although high intake of salty foods has been associated with increased SDB risk in some populations [38], savory snacks can include nutrient-dense items such as nuts, legumes, and whole grains, which are components of anti-inflammatory and metabolically supportive dietary patterns [39]. These foods may contribute to improved respiratory control and sleep stability, though the heterogeneity in snack composition makes definitive interpretation challenging. Further research is needed to distinguish between types of savory snacks and explore underlying mechanisms.

While these associations are compelling, causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design. Additional lifestyle factors—such as physical activity, screen time, and overall diet quality—may confound or mediate these relationships. Moreover, the bidirectional nature of diet and sleep in children should be considered [40]. For example, 100% orange juice has been associated with improved nutrient intake, glycemic control, and lower obesity risk when compared to sugar-sweetened fruit drinks [41]. The nutrient composition of orange juice, including vitamin C, potassium, folate, and magnesium, may provide both benefits and risks depending on consumption patterns and context.

This study's primary strengths lie in its novel development of a dietary-based prediction model for clinically significant SDB and its focus on symptom burden and QOL rather than solely on polysomnography-based diagnoses. By utilizing the validated OSA-18 questionnaire, our research directly links specific dietary patterns to the subjective experience of SDB, addressing a critical gap in the literature. The creation of a simple, non-invasive prediction score offers a practical clinical tool that can help identify at-risk children using easily obtainable dietary information. Furthermore, the study provides a nuanced perspective by identifying both risk-associated foods and potentially protective items, such as fish, highlighting specific, modifiable targets for future intervention and management strategies in pediatric sleep health.

Limitations

While this study provides novel insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents drawing causal inferences regarding the relationship between dietary behaviors and SDB severity. Consequently, the temporal and potentially bidirectional nature of these associations remains to be clarified through longitudinal studies. Second, the study population was recruited from a single tertiary medical center in Taiwan. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations with different dietary cultures, ethnic backgrounds, or socioeconomic environments. Third, SDB severity was assessed using the OSA-18 questionnaire rather than polysomnography, the clinical gold standard; while OSA-18 is validated and reflective of QOL impact, it does not provide objective measures of sleep architecture or respiratory events [42]. Fourth, dietary intake was based on caregiver-reported data using the SFFQ, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases [43]. Specifically, the classification of "savory snacks" encompassed a wide range of food items with varying nutritional profiles, limiting interpretation of this inverse association. Finally, residual confounding for dietary behavior and QOL from unmeasured lifestyle factors such as physical activity, socioeconomic status, or overall diet quality cannot be excluded [44]. Future prospective and interventional studies are warranted to examine whether targeted dietary modifications can effectively mitigate SDB severity and improve QOL in affected children.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this prospective observational study identified specific dietary behaviors that were independently associated with symptom severity and QOL in pediatric SDB. Higher intake of sugar-sweetened foods, processed meats, and refined carbohydrates was linked to worse symptom burden, while consumption of fish and certain savory snacks was associated with reduced risk. A novel, dietary-based msSDB prediction score demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance, underscoring the potential utility of diet as a practical, modifiable marker for risk stratification. These findings suggest that dietary screening may play a complementary role in identifying children at risk for clinically significant SDB and support future investigations into dietary interventions as part of integrated sleep health strategies.
