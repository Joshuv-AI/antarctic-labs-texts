Laughter-inducing Interventions for Workplace Stress Management: A Scoping Review
Authors: Leonie Matteau, Mahee Gilbert-Ouimet, Theo Stefan, Juliette Demers, Jenny Jin, Isabelle Toupin, Moises C Medrano, Annie Leblanc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health at work is a public health priority. Previous studies in the general population suggest that laughter-inducing interventions may support mental health. Their workplace potential warrants a scoping review. The aim of this study was to document, in the literature, the components and effectiveness of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace.MethodsA structured search strategy was applied to 6 databases. Studies assessing laughter-inducing interventions among workers using interventional designs were included. Two reviewers independently screened and extracted data and appraised studies using the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool. Thematic analysis was performed to answer the research questions.ResultsOut of the 613 records identified, twenty-five studies were included with samples of varying sizes (n = 7-200 participants) and professions. Laughter-inducing interventions included introduction (background, rationale) (n = 7), laughter exercises (n = 19), breathing (n = 15), warm-up (n = 10), chanting (n = 8), relaxation (n = 7), movements (n = 6), games (n = 3), and expression of feelings or feedback (n = 4). Intervention formats varied in sessions (1-28), duration (10-90 min), frequency (daily to monthly), and length (1-90 days). The most frequently investigated outcome was stress (n = 16) with results generally statistically significant (n = 12). There was a paucity of data informing the implementation process. The quality appraisal revealed poor quality with risks of selection and/or confounding bias (n = 17/17 among nonrandomized studies) and risks of information and/or selection bias (n = 5/6 in randomized studies).ConclusionWhile findings suggest that laughter-inducing interventions may be promising for reducing workplace stress, most included studies were of poor methodological quality, warranting cautious interpretation. Further research is needed due to methodological heterogeneity and limitations.Keywords: Implementation science, Laughter therapy, Mental health, Occupational health, Outcome and process assessment

Introduction

In 2022, 15% of working-age adults suffered from mental health problems [1]. According to the most recent estimates, 12 billion workdays are lost annually worldwide due to depression and anxiety, resulting in 1 trillion dollars per year in productivity losses [1]. Since 60% of the global population is employed, preventing mental health problems in the workplace has become a well-known public health priority [2]. Perception of a good organizational climate is significantly associated with lower levels of burnout, depression, and anxiety in workers [3]. Interventions aimed at simultaneously improving stress management and organizational climate have been recommended [4,5]. Among these, laughter-inducing interventions emerge as a potential solution [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

These interventions, rooted in the physical, social, emotional and mental benefits of laughter [10,11], involve unconditional and intentional laughter [12]. Literature reviews and meta-analyses have reported that laughter-inducing interventions reduced anxiety, stress, and depression in the general population [6,7,13]. A systematic review regarding laughter yoga, a laughter-inducing intervention using yogic breathing exercises [14], supports its benefits in reducing stress, burnout and cortisol levels among nurses and nursing students [15].

Recent studies have assessed the effectiveness of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace [8,9,16,17]. While benefits have been observed in physiological stress indicators including blood cortisol and blood pressure [8], as well as in self-reported stress levels [16], a lack of positive outcomes has also been reported [17]. A synthesis of this emerging literature is required to guide future research as the available studies seem to: i) be methodologically heterogeneous (e.g., therapeutic components and health indicators examined), ii) have significant limitations (e.g., small sample size and absence of a control group), and iii) present mixed results. The current scoping review sought to fulfill this need by examining the literature on laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace to document the components implemented and their effectiveness, and the extent to which implementation processes and outcomes were described. The following research questions were answered:

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol of this research was registered on Open Science Framework on March 11, 2024 [18].

Design

A scoping review of the literature following the Joanna Briggs Institute's recommendations was conducted [19]. The PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews checklist was used to report the study [20]. This review followed the five stages of the method described by Arksey and O'Malley [21].

Search strategy

The search strategy involved six databases: Academic Search Complete (EBSCO), PsychInfo (APA), MEDLINE (EBSCO), CINHAL (EBSCO), Web of Science (ISI), and Dissertations & Theses Global (ProQuest). The reference lists of the articles that met the eligibility criteria were manually screened to identify further studies. Also, for each selected study, the “Cited by” tool of Google Scholar was used to identify additional studies. Due to the novel aspect of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace, the eligibility criteria were not restrictive concerning the year of publication or language, and all published work on the subject, either scientific articles or theses, was eligible. The PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design) framework was used to establish the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1) and to develop the search strategy in collaboration with a university librarian. Table 2 presents the search strategy applied to MEDLINE, which was adapted to each database.

Selection of studies

Deduplication was performed in Endnote. Using DistillerSR, four reviewers (LM, TS, JD, JJ) conducted a pretest screening of 20 records and assessed eligibility on 10 full-text records to ensure consistent application of the criteria. Subsequently, pairs of reviewers screened titles/abstracts and evaluated full texts, with junior reviewers always paired with seniors. Inter-rater agreement was measured using the Kappa coefficient. Disagreements were discussed; unresolved cases were referred to a senior reviewer.

Data extraction process

On DistillerSR, the standardized data extraction form included: country, study design, total study duration (beginning to end) and length of follow-up, inclusion criteria for participation, total number of participants, participation rates at baseline and at follow-ups, type of work and organizations' size, mean age and standard deviation (by sex if available), definition of the control group, percentage of missing data and methods used to prevent and address these missing data, statistical analysis, outcomes along with their definition and data collection tools, and results including estimates of association with confidence intervals and/or p-values and subgroup analysis for each outcome.

Additionally, regarding the intervention, the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist guided data extraction [22]. This checklist contains: brief name of the intervention, objective(s), materials needed, procedure, where/how/by whom the intervention was delivered, number of times, frequency, length of each session and total duration, whether the intervention was tailored, changes brought to the intervention during the study, any strategy used to improve fidelity, and whether the intervention was delivered as planned.

Reviewers performed a pretest on three studies, ensuring mutual understanding of the data extraction grid. Data extraction was performed by junior reviewers and reviewed by senior reviewers.

Terminology handling

To ensure conceptual clarity and reproducibility, we used “laughter-inducing interventions” as an umbrella descriptor for all included interventions. Original intervention labels used by primary studies (e.g., “Laughter Therapy,” “Laughter Yoga,” or other author-specified terms) were extracted verbatim and reported as such. We did not harmonize or relabel interventions, and we did not treat distinct labels as interchangeable. Where appropriate, we mapped reported components to facilitate cross-study comparison without re-classifying the intervention type.

Quality appraisal

To better inform the scope of included studies, the methodological quality was appraised using the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool [23], which is adaptable to different study designs. A pretest was performed on one article to ensure consistency in interpretation of the criteria. Then, quality appraisal of the remaining studies followed the same process as data extraction. Following the tool creators' recommendation, results are reported by question rather than a less informative overall score.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics of the included studies' characteristics were performed. To answer the first review question “What are the components of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace?”, data from the TIDieR Checklist, namely materials, procedure, person who delivered the intervention, mode of delivery, place where the intervention was delivered, and number of times, frequency, length of each session, and total duration of the intervention, were descriptively analyzed. For the second review question, “How did the implementation unfold?,” the reported fidelity (extent to which an intervention was delivered as planned [24]), acceptability (extent to which stakeholders perceive the intervention as agreeable [25]), feasibility (practicality and viability of implementing the proposed intervention within a given context [26]), adoption (extent to which the target audience is willing to implement the intervention within their setting [25]), reach (extent to which the intervention is delivered to intended recipients [27]), sustainability (whether the intervention can be continued after initial implementation efforts cease [25]), appropriateness (whether the intervention aligns with the characteristics and needs of the setting [25]), cost-effectiveness (whether the intervention provides value for money [28]), and participant satisfaction with the intervention were assessed. To answer the third review question “What conditions facilitated or hindered the implementation?,” the strategies used to improve fidelity along with any reported barriers or facilitators of the intervention were analyzed thematically [29]. For the fourth question, “What were the reported outcomes?,” we conducted a narrative/descriptive synthesis due to the heterogeneity of designs, intervention formats (components, number and length of sessions, dose), outcomes, and measurement tools. Studies reporting association measures for the same outcome (e.g., stress, cortisol, burnout, anxiety, depression) were grouped. Within each study and outcome, when multiple time points or multiple measures were available, we prioritized the primary time point prespecified by the authors; if not prespecified, we used the earliest postintervention assessment to maintain comparability. The direction and statistical significance extracted for vote counting were based on pre-to post-intervention comparisons reported in each study when available, or on postintervention between-groups comparisons when pre-post data were not reported as defined by the study authors.

Vote counting based on statistical significance (p < 0.05) was then applied to summarize the direction of associations at the domain level (favorable vs. not favorable), acknowledging that this approach does not estimate the magnitude of associations. No quantitative pooling (meta-analysis) was attempted, in accordance with scoping review methodology guidance, which emphasizes mapping the breadth of evidence rather than generating pooled effect estimates in the presence of substantial methodological and clinical heterogeneity (e.g., JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [30]).

Results

A total of 613 records were obtained preliminarily through the databases searched, and 511 remained for title and abstract screening after deduplication. Eligibility of full texts was assessed for 48 studies. Finally, 12 eligible studies were included in the review, to which 13 were added based on references' lists of included articles and Google Scholar “cited by” tool. Results for each research question

Question 1: What were the components of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace?

Terminology: The name of the intervention varied between Laughter Yoga, Laughter Therapy, Laughter Yoga Therapy, Aerobic Laughter Therapy and Laughter Training, with some studies using various terminologies (n = 4) and similar interventions being referred to with different terminologies (e.g., an intervention described as “Aerobic Laughter Therapy” shared many similarities with another labeled “Laughter Yoga”). For consistency in this review, we retained the authors' original labels (e.g., “Laughter Therapy,” “Laughter Yoga”) and reported them verbatim. These labels are not treated as interchangeable in our synthesis; cross-study comparisons are based on reported components rather than on the nominal label.

Material: None of the studies reported using specific materials, which is consistent with the nature of laughter-inducing interventions and suggests that no particular materials were required.

Procedure: The following elements (Table 4) were reported as part of the interventions. Four studies did not describe the intervention procedure.

Introduction to laughter therapy: In 7 studies, the person who delivered the intervention introduced the laughter-inducing intervention's background, rationale, and benefits.

Who: In eight studies, the intervention was delivered by the researcher, half reporting either the training required (n = 3) or the validation of the procedure by experts (n = 1). Four studies had a third party with appropriate training perform the intervention, and one mentioned a “facilitator” without further information. Twelve studies did not report who delivered the intervention.

How and where: In six studies, the intervention was delivered face to face, five of which were conducted at the workplace and one in “a quiet place”. Three studies reported using Zoom. Sixteen studies did not report the mode of delivery nor the place where the intervention was delivered.

How much: The number of sessions varied between 1 and 28 with a median and mode of 8 (n = 4). The number of sessions was not mentioned in four studies. Duration varied between 10 and 90 minutes with a mode of 60 minutes (n = 6), and a median of 40 minutes. This information was not provided in five studies. The frequency of the sessions varied between once a day (n = 7) and once a month (n = 1). Twelve studies reported between 1 and 3 times a week. One study reported only 1 session, and data was missing for four studies. The total duration of the intervention varied between 1 and 90 days with a median and mode of 30 days (n = 9 reporting either 28 days, four weeks, or one month).

Question 2: How did the implementation unfold?

Acceptability: One study mentioned that some participants appreciated the intervention as it enabled them to use laughter as a coping mechanism [31].

Feasibility: Two studies mentioned that participants' attrition was due to difficulties to assist to all sessions [32,33].

Adoption: One study discussed poor participation. The organization was composed of 1800 employees, yet only 65 showed interest and 26 participated [31].

None of the included studies reported on fidelity, reach, appropriateness, cost-effectiveness or sustainability.

Question 3: What conditions facilitated or hindered the implementation?

Of the 25 studies included, only four documented conditions facilitating or hindering the implementation of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace.

Two studies documented facilitating conditions, namely strategies to enhance participation such as discussions with participants to choose the time of the intervention and the creation of a social media group to communicate with participants and send reminders regarding the following appointment [32,33].

Two other studies mentioned hindering conditions, which were time constraints and perceived expenses [34], a lack of understanding of the intervention, and participants finding fake laughter not enjoyable, hard and embarrassing to do (either because of the presence of unknown other participants or, to the contrary, very well-known ones) [31].

One study reported the online delivery as both facilitating and hindering. Some participants found it more accessible and appreciated the anonymity, while others would have preferred the sessions to be in-person, either for the contagious nature of laughter or for the desire for greater interpersonal connection [31].

Question 4: What were the reported outcomes?

The outcomes reported by each study are presented in Table 4, along with the statistical significance of the differences observed between groups or between measurements. The most frequently investigated outcome was self-reported stress (n = 16), followed by different measures of physiological stress (cortisol level (n = 5), blood pressure and heart rate (n = 2 each), nitrite level, IgA enzymes level, and respiratory rate (n = 1 each)). Most of the included studies used validated tools such as questionnaires with documented psychometric properties, to measure the reported outcomes (n = 20).

The studies either suggested improvements postintervention (n = 13) or when compared to a control group (n = 7) or showed no association with the outcome (n = 5). Studies examining self-reported stress predominantly observed significant reductions (n = 12/16). Mental health indicators, namely burnout (n = 4), depression (n = 3) and anxiety (n = 2), were also assessed, though improvements were inconsistently observed (n = 6/9). Anger was evaluated twice, yielding conflicting results. Finally, other outcomes, less frequently investigated in the included studies, yielded statistically significant improvements: resilience (n = 2), life satisfaction (n = 1), sleep quality (n = 1), sense of humor (n = 1), self-efficacy (n = 1), affect, and alexithymia (n = 1).

Some studies (n = 3) reported qualitative outcomes of laughter-inducing interventions including stress relief, socialization and improved interactions, enhanced work quality, diverse positive emotions, and new coping strategies.

Critical appraisal

The quality appraisal, reported in Supplementary Table 1, revealed that nearly all included studies were of poor quality, due to important risks of selection and/or confounding bias (n = 17/17 non-randomized studies) and risks of information and/or selection bias (n = 5/6 randomized studies). Notably, the two studies with the highest methodological quality [32,33] reported statistically significant outcomes. In contrast, four of the eight studies with the lowest methodological quality [17,31,34,35] reported non-significant results.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This scoping review identified 25 interventional studies published between 2007 and 2023 that evaluated the effectiveness of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace. In line with scoping review aims, we also mapped the extent to which implementation processes and outcomes were reported and found that such reporting was generally limited. Overall, laughter-inducing interventions appear promising for reducing stress at work; however, methodologically stronger studies are needed. There is a need for more thorough documentation of the implementation process. Specifically, future studies should prospectively define and report core implementation outcomes such as fidelity, reach, sustainability, appropriateness, acceptability, and costs, to clarify what works, for whom, under what circumstances, and in which contexts.

The current review showed considerable heterogeneity in the components of the interventions implemented to induce laughter among workers, along with a lack of precision and detail in reporting the interventions, as did previous reviews regarding laughter interventions among different populations [7,[36], [37], [38], [39]]. The terminology used to refer to the intervention varied, with some studies mentioning various names interchangeably, as noted previously in a scoping review regarding the use of laughter therapy among patients undergoing hemodialysis [39]. Moreover, many studies (n = 4) presented the intervention as described by the founder of Laughter Yoga [14], yet used the appellation of Laughter Therapy. Although Laughter Yoga is therapeutic, it does not qualify as a therapy as there is no previous diagnosis of the problem, and the intervention is not tailored to the specific needs of the recipient [14]. No specific materials were reported, likely because none were required. This aligns with the instructions for Laughter Yoga provided by its founder, Dr Madan Kataria [14], and is consistent with findings from a previous literature review on laughter interventions among dialysis patients [39].

Beyond listing procedural elements, their feasibility in workplace settings warrants consideration. Several commonly used components, such as playful movements, chanting, and intentional laughter, may be perceived as incongruent with professional norms in many workplaces, particularly in settings with strong expectations of composure, emotional restraint, or hierarchical formality [40,41]. These elements can evoke social awkwardness, which may reduce acceptability, discourage participation (reach), and diminish fidelity if participants inhibit laughter or hesitate to engage fully [40,41]. In contrast, components such as guided breathing, gentle warm-ups, brief relaxation, or structured laughter exercises could be more easily integrated into workplace routines because they require minimal space or disruption, and align more closely with existing wellness practices (e.g., mindfulness micro-breaks) [42,43]. As such, feasibility likely varies across components, and interventions combining low-awkwardness elements with opportunities for voluntary playfulness may achieve higher adherence and better fit within diverse organizational cultures [42,43]. Organizational or cultural context may also moderate feasibility and impact. For example, in organizational settings where teams are already accustomed to collective warm-ups or expressive group routines (e.g., certain clinical units or team-based workplaces), playfulness or chanting may feel energizing and naturally aligned with existing practices [44]. In contrast, in workplaces or cultural contexts where emotional restraint, formality, or low-disruption norms are emphasized such expressions may be perceived as incongruent, requiring adapted formats (e.g., quieter, shorter, or individual micro-break variants) [45,46]. At the same time, all elements are congruent with the guidelines for Laughter Yoga [14], which is the intervention used in most of the included studies and proposes including an introduction, clapping and warm-up exercises, chanting and moving, deep breathing, playfulness, laughter exercises, laughter meditation, and feedback. It is impossible to precisely answer the research question based on the data provided by the included studies regarding the person who delivered the intervention, the mode of delivery, and the place where the intervention unfolded, for which data were scarce. The duration and frequency of the intervention were also particularly heterogeneous as no studies presented the exact same number of sessions, duration of each session, frequency and total duration of the intervention.

In general, very few studies detailed the implementation process of laughter-inducing interventions at work. A previous scoping review regarding the use of laughter and humor interventions among dialysis patients came to similar conclusions, recommending an “ongoing reporting and evaluation of the implementation of interventions” to better understand the underlying mechanisms [39]. Likewise, very few studies provided data regarding conditions that facilitated or hindered the implementation of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace. The paucity of implementation data observed in this literature mirrors patterns reported across health research including occupational safety and health, where studies have historically prioritized demonstrating efficacy over specifying and evaluating implementation processes [47,48]. Structural factors likely contribute to this gap such as funding and publication incentives that emphasize outcomes over process, alongside the additional time and resource burden of collecting implementation indicators (e.g., attendance/dose, delivery fidelity, qualitative feedback, resource and cost tracking) [49,50]. As the study of implementation processes documents ways to bridge the gap between knowledge and action [[51], [52], [53]], it is important for further studies to appropriately document their indicators of success. From an evidence-synthesis perspective, this under-reporting limited our ability to compare implementation processes across settings or to link specific delivery strategies with outcomes. As such, documenting the paucity of implementation reporting is a central finding of this review and delineates clear priorities for future studies.

A total of 30 different outcomes were investigated in the included studies, the most frequently reported ones being stress, cortisol level, burnout, anxiety, and depression. While the included studies presented promising results for these outcomes, future studies of higher methodological quality are needed to replicate these findings. Because of the diversity in study designs, intervention formats, timing of assessments, and measurement tools, effectiveness outcomes were synthesized narratively with vote-counting based on significance to indicate the direction (not the magnitude) of associations, consistent with scoping review aims [30]. Previous literature reviews regarding laughter interventions came to similar conclusions in different populations: nursing students [54], older adults [38,55], patients undergoing dialysis [39], adults [7,36], and the general population [6,13,37,56,57]. However, transferability to workplaces is not straightforward. In clinical or residential contexts, participants often occupy a patient role, as recipients of care, so laughter-inducing interventions can be framed as part of care. By contrast, worker roles are tightly coupled with organizational goals, professional identity, and local display rules (e.g., expectations of composure and decorum), which may alter acceptability and contextual fit.

Most included studies were conducted in healthcare settings and in Asian countries. At the cultural level, in countries where group cohesion and collective practices are highly valued in professional life, group-based laughter sessions may be especially acceptable and logistically feasible [46]. Conversely, in cultural contexts where workplace norms emphasize formality, hierarchical distance, or restrained emotional expression during work hours, employees may feel less comfortable participating [45], which could influence reach, dose, and perceived appropriateness. At the healthcare workplace level, the organizational structure of clinical environments may further shape implementation. Healthcare settings can facilitate delivery (e.g., through routine team huddles or onsite wellness programming) [44], but they also introduce constraints such as shift work, time pressure, and staffing shortages that may limit attendance or fidelity [58]. These cultural and organizational features should be considered when interpreting the observed outcomes and when planning scale-up beyond the studied settings.

Intentional laughter was characterized as “embarrassing” by participants of some included studies. This perception likely reflects workplace display rules and professional identity norms that govern emotional expression at work [40,41]. In settings with high formality or clear hierarchies (e.g., clinical teams during work hours), overt playfulness, chanting, or simulated laughter can be experienced as incongruent with role expectations [40,59], potentially undermining appropriateness, acceptability, and willingness to participate. This incongruence may, in turn, affect reach and dose, thereby attenuating observed associations [40,41]. Conversely, teams with stronger norms of psychological safety and routine group activities (e.g., brief huddles, warm-ups) may find intentional laughter easier to integrate [60], preserving fidelity and amplifying perceived benefits. From an implementation standpoint, small adaptations such as offering quieter “micro-laughter” options, emphasizing controlled breathing and brief playful elements, or scheduling sessions off-unit, may mitigate embarrassment without abandoning the core mechanism (positive affect and social connection) [61,62].

The observation that higher-quality studies more often reported positive associations may appear counterintuitive (given concerns about publication bias), but it is consistent with several mechanisms. First, higher-quality designs typically favor better adherence and exposure (e.g., clear schedules, protected time, reminders), which increases dose and fidelity [63,64], increasing the chances that the active components (e.g., respiratory control, affective induction, social synchrony) operate as intended [65,66]. Second, in line with our Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool assessment, higher-quality studies more often prespecify primary outcomes, use validated instruments with adequate sensitivity and standardized timing, and minimize measurement error, which in turn yields more precise, reliable, and interpretable findings [64,65]. Third, improved allocation procedures and appropriate handling of missing data reduce selection and attrition biases, which could lead to underestimated effects if participants with poorer outcomes were more likely to drop out [67]. Finally, better reporting of co-interventions and contextual factors helps interpret heterogeneity and may facilitate targeting (e.g., focusing on teams likely to engage), which enhances observed effectiveness [42,43]. Together, these quality features make it more plausible to detect true associations when they exist, without inferring that weaker studies are necessarily negative; rather, their methodological limitations may have diluted or masked potential benefits.

Recommendations for research

Further studies are required to document the implementation process and effectiveness of laughter-inducing interventions in the workplace. For example, randomized or cluster-randomized studies with a thorough description of the methodology and intervention would provide more robust evidence and a better reproducibility of the intervention. In depth mixed-methods studies documenting the implementation process would also be beneficial to translate the results into action, informing eventual guidelines regarding the implementation of laughter-inducing interventions at work.

To enable cumulative learning, we recommend that future workplace laughter-inducing interventions: (1) pre-register implementation outcomes and process measures alongside effectiveness outcomes (2) use reporting tools (e.g., TIDieR); to fully specify intervention content, dose, mode and context of delivery; (3) track fidelity (planned vs. delivered components), dose/exposure (sessions, duration, attendance), and reach (numbers invited vs. participating), with thresholds defined a priori; (4) assess acceptability/appropriateness among workers and managers and report qualitative insights on facilitators and barriers; (5) plan for sustainability by reporting follow-up beyond immediate postintervention and describing any resource and cost implications; and (6) when feasible, adopt experimental or quasi-experimental designs with control groups and incorporate mixed-methods implementation evaluations, to strengthen both internal validity and real-world interpretability.

Given that most included interventions aligned with Laughter Yoga components, future studies should also examine whether specific component configurations (e.g., combinations of breathing, playfulness, and laughter meditation) are differentially associated with outcomes when delivered with high fidelity and adequate dose.

Future studies will be needed to prospectively capture contextual moderators including sector, team composition, leadership support, norms regarding emotional display, and work organization (e.g., shifts, workload), and report how these factors influence reach, attendance/dose, acceptability/appropriateness, and sustainability. Adaptation processes (e.g., tailoring session length, format, or privacy level; engaging supervisors and local champions) should be documented to support transferability across sectors and regions.

In summary, the key priorities for advancing research on laughter-inducing interventions in workplace settings include: (1) rigorous documentation of implementation processes through preregistered outcomes, structured reporting tools (e.g., TIDieR), and systematic tracking of fidelity, dose/exposure, and reach; (2) methodologically robust effectiveness studies using experimental or quasi-experimental designs with standardized outcome measures; (3) investigation of intervention components to determine which configurations and delivery conditions yield the strongest effects; and (4) prospective examination of cultural and organizational contextual moderators, together with thorough documentation of adaptation processes, to support transferability and sustainable implementation across sectors. Together, these priorities provide a coherent roadmap for strengthening both the scientific evidence and the practical applicability of workplace laughter-inducing interventions.

Limitations and strengths

This review presents some limitations. First, the inclusion of theses (n = 9) and a research report (n = 1) not considered peer-reviewed, contributed to the poor methodological quality observed. Second, our use of narrative synthesis with vote-counting summarizes the direction of associations but does not provide magnitude of associations; this choice reflects the heterogeneity of the evidence base and the scoping nature of the review. Third, the limited reporting of core implementation outcomes across primary studies restricted our ability to synthesize implementation processes; our review documents this gap to inform the design and reporting of future trials. Fourth, because most included studies were conducted in Asian healthcare settings, generalizability to non-healthcare sectors and to cultural contexts with different norms of emotional expression and workplace hierarchy remains uncertain; accordingly, we emphasize context-sensitive adaptation and reporting. Additionally, a limitation of the field is the inconsistent use of labels such as “Laughter Therapy” and “Laughter Yoga.” We mitigated this by retaining original labels verbatim and synthesizing at the component level.

This scoping review also has important strengths. The thorough search strategy allowed the conduct of an extensive literature review. No language criterion was used in studies selection, which allowed the authors to include studies of any language. This review mapped interventions across a range of cultural and professional contexts including studies from multiple countries and worker groups, thereby providing a broad descriptive overview of settings and participant characteristics. Kappa coefficients for the level of agreement between the reviewers were very strong, showing excellent consistency among reviewers [68]. A further strength is the explicit use of TIDieR to structure extraction, which can accelerate translation to practice by clarifying intervention replicability and highlighting implementation reporting gaps [22]. Although the scoping review guidelines do not enforce the use of quality appraisal [20], it was performed in the present study using a recommended grid.

Conclusion

Laughter-inducing interventions show potential as workplace strategies aimed at supporting workers' psychological well-being, particularly regarding stress reduction. A central contribution of this scoping review is to clarify the current state of the evidence by highlighting important limitations in how these interventions have been reported, especially with respect to implementation processes and core implementation outcomes (e.g., fidelity, reach, acceptability, appropriateness, sustainability, and resource requirements). These gaps constrain interpretation, reproducibility, and the ability to implement and scale these interventions in workplace settings. Also, the evidence base remains limited by substantial methodological heterogeneity, small sample sizes, inconsistent outcome measurement, and insufficient detail on intervention content, delivery, and contextual factors. Consequently, firm conclusions regarding effectiveness and underlying mechanisms cannot yet be drawn. Future research should therefore focus on stronger study designs and more systematic reporting of intervention content and implementation conditions to support interpretation, replication, and real-world use.
