Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma Presenting as a Thigh Mass: A Case Report
Authors: Sondos Badran, Karina Basmajian, Alexander T Phan, Romina Hashemizadeh, Sthefon Tran, Katherine Bourbeau-Medinilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and typically occurs later in life. Classic symptoms include lymphadenopathy, fevers, night sweats, and weight loss. Extranodal presentations are uncommon and may mimic other malignant processes. A 76-year-old female presented with right thigh swelling and pain. Imaging demonstrated a soft-tissue mass with radiologic features concerning for sarcoma. A biopsy was performed, and histopathology confirmed DLBCL. Primary involvement of the thigh by B-cell lymphoma is exceedingly rare and may be confused with sarcoma due to its clinical and radiographic appearance. Clinicians should be aware of this potential extranodal presentation of DLBCL. This case underscored the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in the evaluation of extremity masses, including lymphoma.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), accounting for about one-third of all NHL cases globally. 1 The incidence of DLBCL is estimated at 7.2 per 100,000 person-years and increases with age, with a median age of 66 years at diagnosis. 2 DLBCL occurs more frequently in males compared to females and has a higher incidence among White individuals in the United States. 2 However, it is noted that the International NHL Classification Project data were pooled from populations in Omaha, Nebraska, and Vancouver, Canada for North America, which may not account for the Hispanic population in North America. 2

The current gold standard treatment for DLBCL is rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP), which achieves cure in more than 60% of patients. 3 Standard therapy typically involves 6 cycles of R-CHOP for patients with bulky or advanced stage disease. 4

The authors have presented a case of a 76-year-old female who developed right thigh swelling and pain that was ongoing for 3 months. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a complex large mass involving the entire anterior compartment of the thigh with marrow involvement in the proximal right femur, suggestive of sarcoma. Histopathologic evaluation of a tissue biopsy confirmed high-grade DLBCL, supported by immunohistochemical stains.

Case Patient Presentation

A 76-year-old female with a past medical history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia presented with right thigh swelling and pain ongoing for approximately 3 months. When she first noticed these symptoms, she was evaluated by a physician who diagnosed her with a deep vein thrombosis and was treated with apixaban, which did not improve her symptoms. In the week leading up to hospital presentation, she had worsening pain, difficulty ambulating, and numbness over her right foot. Additional symptoms included a 25-pound weight loss over the past 3 months and poor appetite. She otherwise denied headaches, fevers, chills, nausea, vomiting, chest pain, shortness of breath, abdominal pain, or other neurologic symptoms.

In the emergency department, vital signs included a blood pressure of 124/80 mmHg, heart rate of 104 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 14, temperature of 36.7 °C, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Physical examination revealed marked right lower extremity swelling with 2+ pitting edema and a firm and diffusely tender anterior thigh compartment without a discrete palpable mass. Distal pulses were intact with regular motor and sensory function.

Initial imaging included a computed tomography (CT) angiogram of the right lower extremity and CT of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast. The studies demonstrated multiple complex enhancing masses predominantly within the quadriceps musculature. The largest cross-sectional region measured 12.3 × 9.4 cm (Figure 1). It was unclear whether it represented an abnormality of the muscle itself vs an infection or mass within the muscle. However, a mass was favored, given the well-delineated edges. Small areas of enhancement were also seen within the sartorius muscle and the vastus lateralis muscle as well. The spleen demonstrated a hypoattenuating lesion anteriorly, measuring 1.7 cm (Figure 2).

Laboratory studies were notable for hyponatremia, hypochloremia, anemia, hypercalcemia, and elevated lactate. Further workup, including parathyroid hormone, parathyroid hormone-related protein, vitamin D, iron, ferritin, and iron panel were ordered. Iron studies suggested anemia of chronic disease.

MRI of the right femur confirmed a complex large mass involving the entire anterior compartment of the thigh, with marrow involvement in the proximal femur, raising concern for sarcoma (Figure 3). Extensive subcutaneous edema and intramuscular extension throughout the right thigh was also noted (Figure 4).

Clinic Reasoning/Determining an Approach

Given these findings, a tissue biopsy was indicated to establish a diagnosis. A biopsy of the right thigh mass revealed DLBCL, which was confirmed by immunohistochemistry staining. The patient was staged as stage 4 DLBCL in the setting of splenic and extranodal involvement, bulky disease, and marrow infiltration.

Treatment Pathway

During hospitalization, she received opioids for pain control and participated in physical therapy. Hypercalcemia was treated with intravenous calcitonin. Oncology recommended initiation of R-CHOP. Cycle 1, day 1 was administered inpatient with monitoring for tumor lysis syndrome. She was ultimately discharged with plans to continue R-CHOP as an outpatient.

Outcomes

Due to insurance issues, outpatient R-CHOP therapy was postponed. One month after discharge, she was found to have a 32 mm right frontal lobe mass, consistent with brain metastasis, and underwent whole-brain radiation therapy. R-CHOP was eventually resumed. However, she encountered multiple hospital readmissions due to neutropenic fever and seizures. Given these complications, she and her family decided to transition to hospice care.

Discussion

DLBCL is the most common NHL, accounting for approximately 30% of cases, with around 27,000 new diagnoses annually in the United States. 5 Approximately 31.6% of patients present with primary extranodal involvement, with soft-tissue involvement occurring in only 3.3% of cases. 6 The pathogenesis of DLBCL is a complex and multifactorial, involving gene mutations, immune dysregulation, infections, chromosomal translocations, somatic mutations, deletions, and amplifications. 7 Common genetic alterations include translocations involving BCL2, BCL6, and MYC. 8 The patient demonstrated a MYC translocation, which accounts for 5% to 14% of DLBCL cases. 9

The patient did not fit the typical demographic profile for DLBCL, which usually occurs in the sixth to seventh decade of life and more commonly affects males who are not Hispanic. 10 By contrast, she was a Hispanic female in her seventh decade. Her tumor markers and presentation initially raised consideration for primary cutaneous large B-cell lymphoma, leg type (PCDLBCL-LT), a rare NHL subtype accounting for 1% to 4% of all cutaneous lymphomas. 11 PCDLBCL-LT typically presents with lower extremity pain or ulceration that may involve 1 or both legs. 12 However, in 28.3% of cases, patients present with pain alone without skin lesions. 12 Extracutaneous involvement occurs in 45% of cases and often affects the bone marrow, central nervous system, and lymph nodes. 13 Histologically, PCDLBCL-LT shows dense diffuse infiltrates of round nuclei with B-cell tumor marker expression including CD20, CD79a, BCL-2, BCL-6, MUM1, and c-MYC, while CD10 and CD138 are typically negative. 14

Although the patient’s presentation of lower extremity pain and swelling without cutaneous lesions could initially raise suspicion for PCDLBCL-LT, biopsy confirmed extranodal DLBCL of the thigh, with tumor markers positive for CD20, CD79a, BCL-6, Ki-67, MUM1, and c-MYC. This underscores that DLBCL can mimic PCDLBCL-LT clinically, demonstrating the importance of histopathologic evaluation. A complete staging workup, including CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, is essential in all suspected DLBCL cases to assess disease extent. In this patient, imaging revealed metastatic disease involving the spleen and liver.

A unique aspect of this case was the presentation of right lower extremity swelling and pain, initially concerning for soft-tissue sarcoma. CT imaging revealed multiple complex enhancing masses within the quadriceps, with additional involvement of the sartorius and vastus lateralis muscles. MRI further characterized a large anterior thigh mass with proximal femoral marrow involvement. This emphasized that DLBCL can occasionally mimic sarcoma, demonstrating the need to include lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of extremity masses. 6

This case underscored the importance of careful history-taking, physical examination, and imaging evaluation in patients with an extremity mass. Lymphoma should remain on the differential when imaging suggests sarcoma, and definitive diagnosis relies on biopsy, followed by timely initiation of systemic therapy with R-CHOP.

Conclusion

DLBCL is a common NHL. However, extranodal presentations are extremely rare. Treatment of DLBCL is with R-CHOP and can be curative. In this case, the patient presented with a large right-sided thigh mass, which on MRI appeared concerning for sarcoma. To the surprise of the authors, tissue biopsy revealed DLBCL. Clinicians should consider lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of extremity masses, as timely recognition and treatment can substantially improve outcomes.
