A Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Study (2009–2023): Exploring Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 Antibody Time Series in Humans and Swine and Vaccine Coverage in Two Target Groups
Authors: Solveig Jore, Ragnhild Tønnessen, Carl Andreas Grøntvedt, Kjersti Rydland, Anna Germundsson Hauge, Olav Hungnes, Anja Bråthen Kristoffersen, Even Fossum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSwine may act as ‘epidemiological bridges’ and reservoirs for the emergence of novel zoonotic influenza viruses with pandemic potential. While bidirectional exchange of influenza A viruses at the swine–human interface is well recognised, data on the extent of interspecies transmission are limited.MethodsWe analysed the post‐seasonal geometric mean titre (GMT) of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 antibodies in humans and the seasonal prevalence of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 antibodies in unvaccinated swine from 2009/2010–2022/2023 per county in Norway to search for evidence of interspecies transmission. We explored correlations at the national and individual county level and investigated possible associations by running a negative binomial regression model. Additionally, we distributed an influenza vaccination questionnaire to veterinarians and farmers working with swine to assess vaccination uptake and calculated total response rates per county and overall.ResultsThe time series of H1N1pdm09 antibodies from humans and swine show significant positive correlations both across (0.8 Pearson correlation coefficient) and within certain individual counties, with especially high correlations in Innlandet (0.9), Vestland (0.8) and Rogaland (0.7) Counties. Our regression analysis revealed a significant positive association between the annual GMT of H1N1pdm09 antibodies in humans, the annual seroprevalence of H1N1pdm09 antibodies in swine, and the density of swine farms in counties. Vaccination uptake was 39% and 50% in farmers and veterinarians, respectively.ConclusionsOur findings indicate a temporal relationship between the disease in humans and swine; suggesting spillover, environmental factors facilitating disease spread, and/or indirect relationships driven by unknown factors.Impacts The time series of H1N1pdm09 antibodies in humans and swine shows significant correlations. Regression analysis links GMT of H1N1pdm09 antibodies in humans to seroprevalence of H1N1pdm09 in swine and density of swine farms. This indicates a temporal relationship possibly due to spillover, shared risk factors or indirect relationships driven by unknown factors.The influenza vaccination survey showed a 39% uptake among farmers and 50% among swine veterinarians. Keywords: geometric mean titre, H1N1pdm09, Norway, seroprevalence, swine influenza, vaccine coverage

Introduction

Influenza in swine was first reported during the 1918 human influenza pandemic, suggesting a shared aetiology (Brown 2000; Zimmer and Burke 2009). By the 1930s, causative viruses from swine and humans were identified as highly similar influenza viruses (Shope 1931; Zimmer and Burke 2009). The Spanish (1918), Asian (1957) and Hong Kong (1968) pandemics were all caused by different influenza A viruses whose genetic components can be traced to viruses in birds, swine and humans (Anderson et al. 2016; Belshe 2005; Brown 2000).

The influenza A(H1N1) 2009–2010 pandemic marks the first pandemic influenza virus for which an immediate predecessor virus was identified in swine (Anderson et al. 2021; Belshe 2005; Garten et al. 2009; Mena et al. 2016; Zimmer and Burke 2009). Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 (H1N1pdm09) emerged in Mexico in March 2009 and arose from the reassortment of two swine influenza viruses (SIVs): a North American swine H1N2 triple reassortant lineage virus and a Eurasian avian‐like H1N1 swine lineage virus (Garten et al. 2009; van der Meer et al. 2010).

Swine have historically been regarded as the most likely ‘mixing vessel’ for influenza A viruses since they are susceptible to a wide range of avian and human influenza viruses and have receptors for both human and avian influenza A viruses in their respiratory tracts (Ito et al. 1998; Trebbien et al. 2011). However, many species of mammals and birds have both receptor types and may host reassortment events (Abdelwhab and Mettenleiter 2023). Swine and humans have similar tissue tropism for influenza A viruses in their respiratory tracts, suggesting that co‐infections and reassortments can also happen in humans (Trebbien et al. 2011; Van Poucke et al. 2010), and new hybrid strains with pandemic potential can emerge.

The Norwegian swine production sector produces approximately 1.5 million swine to slaughter annually, and there is negligible import of live swine (www.animalia.no). The population has a favourable health status with population‐wide absence of several important porcine pathogens. Movements of swine are recorded in the Norwegian Livestock Register, which is managed by the Norwegian Food Safety Authority. There has been a gradual decline in the number of swine herds in Norway, from approx. 3000 herds in 2009 to 1600 herds in 2023 (www.ssb.no/en/). The maximum herd size of swine holdings in Norway is regulated through concession limits. For fattening pig producers, the maximum annual production is 2100 pigs and for sow herds the concession limit is 105 breeding sows at any given time. The average herd size of farrow‐to‐feed or farrow‐to‐finish sow herds was 88 annual breeding sows in 2023 when sow pool systems are excluded (Norsvin 2023). The domestic trade and movement of swine is organised in a hierarchical pyramid, with downward live animal movement from closed genetic nucleus herds, multiplier breeding herds to commercial herds with farrow‐to‐finish or farrow‐to‐feed production and to specialised fattening pig herds that purchase all their pigs from herd categories higher in the pyramid. There are no regulations limiting the movement of pigs between counties and live animal transport is widespread in accordance with the pig production pyramid. Management practices in the sampled swine farms are quite homogenous across the country, as they are predominantly housed indoors with modern husbandry practices and required to comply with regulations prescribing mandatory biosecurity practices (Lovdata 2003).

Influenza A viruses are respiratory pathogens in swine that cause subclinical or mild disease, leading to economic losses (Boeters et al. 2023). SIVs are genetically highly diverse and distributed worldwide in swine. Results from more than a decade of active serosurveillance in Norway show a unique situation where influenza A was not present in swine prior to the introduction of H1N1pdm09 in 2009. Moreover, other SIVs have never been detected (Grøntvedt et al. 2022), indicating that H1N1pdm09 was introduced to swine by humans (Hofshagen et al. 2009). In addition, vaccination against influenza A virus is not practiced in the Norwegian swine population, eliminating the risk for vaccine‐induced seropositivity.

Transmission of human influenza viruses into swine occurs frequently and has had a major impact on the establishment and epidemiology of different influenza A virus lineages that circulate in swine, including H1N1pdm09 (Hennig et al. 2022; Rajao et al. 2019). Swine act as bridge hosts for these viruses. Swine‐to‐human transmissions are recognised as sporadic but regularly observed and have been caused by reassortant H3N2, Eurasian avian H1N1 and H1N2 subtype viruses. However, confirmed infections have had no or limited onward transmission between people. SIV was first isolated from a human in 1974 (Smith et al. 1976) and has since been reported worldwide (Anderson et al. 2021; Myers et al. 2007). No cases have been detected in Norway.

When SIVs that usually circulate in swine are detected in humans, they are presently termed variant viruses (WHO 2023) and typically occur in people exposed to swine. Two review studies about influenza at the human and animal interface (Freidl et al. 2014; Sikkema et al. 2016) show highly variable seroprevalences in humans. In populations occupationally exposed to swine, the prevalence of antibodies to H1N1v, H1N2v and to H3N2v ranged from 0%–80% (Sikkema et al. 2016). The other study identified 396 SIV‐confirmed patients who were naturally infected (Freidl et al. 2014). Of these, 347 were infected with subtype A(H3N2), 47 with A(H1N1) and 2 with A(H1N2). The reviews by Freidl et al. (2014) and Sikkema et al. (2016) concluded that swine‐to‐human transmission occurs sporadically. Recent studies have expanded our understanding of the genetic diversity and evolution of swine and human influenza A strains. Venkatesh et al. (2022) quantified the genetic diversity of A(H1) in North American swine and assessed the antigenic phenotypes. This study found that swine H1 lineages differ significantly from human vaccine strains, with this disparity increasing over time due to viral evolution in swine (Venkatesh et al. 2022). Meanwhile, Souza et al. (2022) reported that human A(H3N2) viruses crossed into swine populations in North America during the 1990s and again in the 2010s, leading to sustained circulation, viral evolution in swine, and reduced antigenic similarity between H3N2 viruses in swine and humans, and vaccine strains (Souza et al. 2022). Despite these findings, national surveillance systems and standardised serological surveillance for swine and at‐risk human populations remain limited. The influenza season in the northern hemisphere typically occurs in the winter months. Individuals occupationally exposed to live swine can represent a sentinel population for potential new pandemic influenza viruses, supporting yearly influenza vaccination for swine workers (Myers et al. 2007). Preventing infection in people working with swine will reduce risk of anthroponotic and zoonotic transmission and the further potential for virus reassortment in swine or humans (Gray and Baker 2007). Since 2009, the Norwegian Institute of Public Health (NIPH) has recommended annual vaccination for swine workers, farmers, and veterinarians practicing in swine farms. However, there has been no data on the vaccine uptake in this target group.

The aim of this study is to explore correlations in the H1N1pdm09 antibody time series from humans and swine in Norway, and to identify potential associations using a regression model. Additionally, we analyse and describe data from a survey on influenza vaccine uptake among swine farmers and veterinarians.

Methods

Datasets

H1N1pdm09 Antibody Testing in the Swine Population

In Norway, serosurveillance for influenza A virus in swine is an appropriate strategy due to a non‐vaccination policy. Antibodies against SIVs that are enzootic in most other swine producing countries have never been detected (Grøntvedt et al. 2022). After H1N1pdm09 was detected in swine holdings in 2009, the virus was included in the serosurveillance (Lium et al. 2010).

Each year, 3.500 to 5.000 swine sera from 500 to 760 herds (19%–35% of total herds) were included in Norway's surveillance program for swine viral infections. Sera were screened for antibodies against influenza A virus using a commercial competitive ELISA assay (ID Screen Influenza A antibody Competition, Multi‐species, IDvet, Grabels, France). For each farm testing positive or inconclusive for antibodies to influenza A in the ELISA test, antibodies to H1pdm09 were further confirmed using the hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) assays in accordance with the method described by WOAH (World Organisation for Animal Health 2018). The antigens used in the HAI assays were produced at the Norwegian Veterinary Institute (NVI). The antigens included A/Swine/California/07/09 (H1N1pdm09, 1A.3.3.2), A/Swine/Belgium/1/98 (H1N1, 1C.2.1), A/Swine/Gent/7623/99 (H1N2, 1B.1.2.1) and A/Swine/Flanders/1/98 (H3N2). Swine seroprevalence to H1N1pdm09 was based on farms testing positive for influenza A antibodies in the ELISA. For all these farms, antibodies specific to H1pdm09 were confirmed using the HAI assay.

We refer to swine data as seasonal since the samples are collected throughout the year. The season was set from July to June, aligning with the human influenza sample collection in July and August. Our dataset included the influenza season, county, number of swine farms, number of swine farms tested for H1N1pdm09 antibodies, number of positive swine farms and seasonal H1N1pdm09 seroprevalence in swine (Table 1).

H1N1pdm09 Antibody Testing in the Human Population

Since 1977, the National Influenza Centre (NIC Norway) at NIPH has annually collected anonymised residual sera (geographical and age‐representative selection of the population) between July and August from laboratories nationwide (Haaheim 1979). The serum samples are analysed in HAI assays against representative influenza strains, usually the ones included in the seasonal influenza vaccine (Table S1), to estimate levels of antibodies in the population (Fossum et al. 2024). Sera with titres < 10 (lowest measured dilution) were assigned an HAI titre of 5.

We compiled data on HAI titres against H1N1pdm09 from influenza season 2008/2009 to influenza season 2022/2023 for individuals aged 0–101 years. Data from the 2020/2021 influenza season are missing due to limited laboratory capacity for HAI assays during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Most serum samples included the donor's county; for the 0.3% missing this, we inferred it from the laboratory location. We utilise geometric mean titre (GMT) as an indirect measure of seroprevalence. GMT calculation involves logarithmic transformation of individual titre values, and the unit of GMT is expressed as the inverse dilution. We describe GMT as post‐seasonal since it reflects the level of the virus during the influenza season preceding the serum sampling. In this dataset we have the influenza season, age at serum sampling, county and titre (antibody response against H1N1pdm09 reference virus using HAI assay) for the patient tested (Table 1). We included only samples from individuals aged < 16 years (age at serum sampling). The final dataset consisted of 5431 individual samples. Children under 16 are more susceptible to seasonal influenza than adults (Ratre et al. 2020), have fewer past infections and are serologically more similar to swine which are born naïve and have short life spans (< 2 years of age). Additionally, vaccination is recommended only for at‐risk children, with a low uptake at 8% (NIPH 2023). The vaccination coverage for children aged 0–17 years is notably low at just 1.1% (FHI 2023). We have previously shown that there was very little preexisting cross‐reactive antibody against H1N1pdm09 in sera taken from children in 2008, indicating that sera from children born before the 2009 pandemic are unlikely to contain confounding reactivities (Waalen et al. 2010).

Before 2020, laboratories reported weekly influenza detections to NIC Norway; afterward, they switched to real‐time reporting via the Norwegian Surveillance System for Communicable Diseases (MSIS) lab database. We combined data from the two registries to obtain the total number of influenza infections from Week 18 of 2009 to Week 39 of 2023. Since not all laboratories subtype influenza A detections, the proportion of H1N1pdm09 infections was calculated based on samples tested for both H1N1pdm09 and Influenza A virus subtype H3N2. This proportion of H1N1pdm09 was then projected onto the total number of laboratory confirmed influenza A infections for each week (Figure 1) (Fossum et al. 2024).

Combined Swine and Human Dataset

Both the swine seasonal seroprevalence and the human post‐seasonal GMT were aggregated at the county level. The combined dataset encompassed influenza seasons from 2008/2009 to 2022/2023, originally including 152 seasonal seroprevalence and corresponding post‐seasonal GMT measurements at the county level. Data from the 2008/2009 influenza season were excluded since this season marks the initial introduction of H1N1pdm09 in Norway.

To ensure the accuracy of our analyses, we applied specific exclusion criteria: data from counties with fewer than 20 tested swine herds or 20 tested human samples analysed seasonally were omitted. This resulted in the exclusion of four counties: Oslo, Agder, Møre og Romsdal and Troms og Finnmark, and this reduced the dataset to 81 seasonal seroprevalence and GMT measurements.

For each county, we calculated ‘swine farm density’ and ‘human density’ using data from Statistics Norway (https://www.ssb.no). Swine farm density was determined by dividing the number of swine farms by the county area, while human density was calculated by dividing the number of inhabitants by the county area.

Influenza Vaccine Uptake in Farmers and Veterinarians Working With Swine

In May 2023, a web‐based questionnaire was mailed to 1196 members of the Norwegian Veterinary Association (SurveyXact) and to 1486 members of Norsvin (Norwegian swine breeding company and interest organisation for swine producers) (Nettskjema). Our questionnaire comprised two closed questions with multiple choices: ‘What is your residential county’ and ‘Did you take the influenza vaccine during the last 12 months’. In the introductory text of the questionnaire, it was stated that only veterinarians/farmers working with swine (according to population targeted) should answer the questions. We calculated the vaccine coverage for each of the survey populations, in total and per county, as well as the total response rates.

Data Analysis

This retrospective cross‐sectional study estimates seroprevalence based on the number of individual swine farms tested and offers a proxy for the human seroprevalence using GMT. The analysis employed descriptive summary statistics and included plots for each county to investigate and explore GMT and the seroprevalence of H1N1pdm09 in both humans and swine over time.

We conducted a correlation analysis employing the Pearson method to evaluate the linear relationship between GMT and swine seroprevalence across counties as well as in aggregate. The analysis involved calculating Pearson correlation coefficients for each county, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to quantify the strength and direction of these relationships.

To test the significance of the correlation coefficients, we transformed them into z‐scores and performed a one‐sample t‐test. To account for multiple comparisons, we adjusted the p‐values using the Holm (1979) method, which sequentially controls the family‐wise error rate.

To explore the impact of the virtually absent circulation of H1N1pdm09 in the human population during the 2020/2021 and 2021/2022 seasons on the seroprevalence in the swine population, we divided the seroprevalence in the swine time series into two timeslots: COVID‐19 influenza seasons (influenza season 2020/2021 and 2021/2022) and pre/post‐COVID‐19 influenza seasons.

Results

The H1N1pdm09 Antibody Testing of Swine

After H1N1pdm09 entered the Norwegian swine population in late 2009, herd seroprevalence against H1N1pdm09 remained stable at 41% to 49% until 2017. From 2018, a downward trend was noted, reaching around 12% by 2022 (Table 2, Figure 2). This decline was observed across all production types and across all counties (Grøntvedt et al. 2022). In 2022, when the national herd seroprevalence was at 12%, the densest swine farming area in Norway, Rogaland, remained higher at 36.6% herd seropositivity. This county also had a consistently higher seroprevalence in all preceding years (Table 2). No antibodies against other influenza A viruses besides H1N1pdm09 were detected during the period.

The H1N1pdm09 Antibody Testing of Humans

H1N1pdm09 viruses have circulated every winter in Norway since May 2009, with notable seasonal variations and sharp peaks roughly every 3 years (Figure 1). High levels of infections took place in the autumn of 2009, and in the 2012–13, 2015–16, 2018–19 and 2022–23 seasonal influenza peaks. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, despite extensive testing, influenza virus was virtually absent from April 2020 to March 2022 (Fossum et al. 2024).

We plotted H1N1pdm09 infections, and HAI GMT values from 2009 to 2023 to evaluate immunity towards H1N1pdm09 viruses in humans in relation to the seasonal variance in H1N1pdm09 infections (Figure 1). HAI titres towards H1N1pdm09 increased after the extensive wave of infections in the fall of 2009, although GMT values remained at a moderate level in 2010 (Waalen et al. 2010). HAI titres continued to increase until 2013 reaching a relatively high level of immunity in the population that remained until 2019 and then declined (Figure 1). After the COVID‐19 pandemic there was an increase in HAI titres from 2022 to 2023, following the H1N1pdm09 dominated 2022/2023 season.

The H1N1pdm09 time series from humans and swine exhibits a significant positive overall correlation (considering all the study counties) when aggregated by influenza season (Figure 2, national level; 0.8 Pearson correlation coefficient, p‐value = 0.0003 from the one‐sample t‐test and Figure 3). Three counties also show significant positive correlations individually (Innlandet (0.9 correlation coefficient, p‐value = 0.001), Vestland (0.8, p‐value = 0.0001) and Rogaland (0.7, p‐value = 0.01)); two counties show positive correlations at trend level (Nordland (0.5, p‐value: 0.06) and Viken (0.5, p‐value: 0.05)); and two counties (Vestfold and Telemark and Trøndelag) show non‐significant correlations (Figures 2 and 3).

Discussion

We found that the H1N1pdm09 time series from humans and swine is positively correlated both across and within counties. This can be a result of direct or indirect contact between the species, enabling the interspecies spread of the virus. A study based on sequencing of the hemagglutinin gene found that H1N1pdm09 circulating in Norwegian swine early in 2011 resembled the virus circulating in humans during this time (Forberg et al. 2013). Farm staff with influenza‐like illness were found to be the major risk factor for spillover to Norwegian swine in a population with negligible import of live pigs (Gjerset et al. 2011; Grøntvedt et al. 2013). Enhanced virological surveillance of SIVs, including whole genome sequencing, is needed to study the directionality of spillover since there is limited data available (Grøntvedt et al. 2013; Hofshagen et al. 2009). Anthroponotic events have received less attention so far compared to zoonotic host jumps. However, a comprehensive assessment analysing the directionality of viral host jumps found a much higher frequency of anthroponotic compared to zoonotic transmission events (64% vs. 36%) (Tan et al. 2024).

The absence of seasonal influenza during COVID‐19 enabled easier detection of zoonotic influenza spillover to humans (Dhanasekaran et al. 2022; Markin et al. 2023). In Norway, human influenza surveillance showed that H1N1pdm09 and other influenza viruses were virtually absent in the 2020/2021 season, and that there was little occurrence of H1N1pdm09 in the 2021/2022 season. The few viruses that were detected require further characterisation. Although influenza A infection in swine is notifiable in Norway, few samples are submitted for virological testing as clinical signs are often mild and unspecific, underlining the necessity of serological surveillance. The seroprevalence of H1N1pdm09 in swine, analysed across COVID‐19 and pre/post‐COVID‐19 seasons, remained nearly stable in farms within certain counties (Figure 4). This suggests that H1N1pdm09 circulation in swine in these areas is driven not only by spillovers from humans but also by sustained swine‐to‐swine transmission, as in other countries (George et al. 2025; Markin et al. 2023).

The results from our regression model reveal significant factors contributing to the seroprevalence of H1N1pdm09 in swine. The GMT of H1N1pdm09 antibodies in humans was a statistically significant predictor. Our findings indicate that when infection rates increase in humans, they also increase in swine. This is particularly evident in counties with high swine farm density, where significant positive correlations are found (Figure 2). Our model also identified swine farm density as a critical factor influencing H1N1pdm09 seroprevalence in swine (Table 3). The significant positive relationship suggests that areas with higher densities of swine farms are at greater risk for virus spread and persistence. This underscores the need for targeted farm management and biosecurity interventions to control H1N1pdm09 spread. However, previous research from Norway (Grøntvedt et al. 2013) did not find a significant protective effect of recommended biosecurity practices. Generally, the mandatory and recommended biosecurity measures in Norwegian swine farms are not specifically intended to reduce the risk of human to swine transmission of influenza A virus.

Environmental factors also play a role in the virus's persistence and transmission between herds (Torremorell et al. 2012). In Rogaland County where many swine farms are situated close to each other, this might enhance inter‐herd transmission through droplet/airborne routes or by the movement of farm staff between holdings. Our study highlights a characteristic that sets Rogaland County apart from other counties: the seroprevalence in swine is substantially higher compared to the rest (Figure 4). Additionally, we observe that the H1N1pdm09 seroprevalence level in swine relative to antibody GMT level in humans is higher in Rogaland than in other counties (Figure 2). A higher seroprevalence in swine in Rogaland might be explained by more continuous virus introductions from humans, or by the more extensive and sustained intra‐ and inter‐herd transmission among swine in this county than elsewhere due to the higher swine herd density, potentially leading to persistence and evolution of distinct genetic lineages. If an established lineage exists, interspecific transmission from swine to humans with viruses that differ from the contemporaneous human viruses could also occur. After the 2009 pandemic, the H1N1pdm09 virus was established as a seasonal influenza virus in humans. Both during the pandemic and afterwards, gene reassortment between H1N1pdm09 viruses and enzootic SIVs has been identified in other countries (Dhanasekaran et al. 2022; Rajão et al. 2017).

The number of documented zoonotic infections globally is relatively small compared to the number of people exposed, directly or indirectly, through swine farming. However, SIV infections in humans can resemble common influenza or be subclinical, potentially masking cases. In Norway, where swine carry only the H1N1pdm09 lineage also found in humans, a major challenge is differentiating zoonotic from human‐to‐human infections in serological and virological analyses.

Pandemic preparedness plans emphasise detection and response, but vaccinating swine workers is crucial to curb new virus strains' emergence and spread (Gray and Baker 2007). People working with animals are at risk of transmitting and contracting zoonotic diseases. Vaccinating swine farmers aims to protect swine from influenza infection and the development of viruses with new characteristics in swine. Second, it can also limit transmission back to humans.

Despite several countries recommending seasonal influenza vaccines for swine farmers, published research on vaccine uptake is scant. Since 2009, the NIPH has recommended annual influenza vaccination for those working with live swine, but data on the actual vaccine uptake have been lacking. In a recent study conducted on swine farms in the US Midwest (Moraes et al. 2024) only 29.2% of participants (14 out of 48) reported having received an influenza vaccine, whereas 66.7% (32 out of 48) indicated they had not received the human‐seasonal influenza vaccine. This vaccination rate among farm workers is considerably lower than the national average of 46.9% for the 2022–2023 influenza season in the United States, as reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. An earlier study found that just 5% of swine workers received the seasonal influenza vaccine in a particular year, with a slight increase to 7% in the subsequent year (Rabinowitz et al. 2013). A modelling study indicates that vaccinating at least 50% of farm workers can reduce the risk of pandemic virus introduction compared to naïve unvaccinated workers (Saenz et al. 2006).

We found higher vaccine uptake among veterinarians (50%) and swine farmers (39%) compared to the general population, based on the Norwegian Immunisation Registry SYSVAK (19.1%) and survey estimates from Statistics Norway (26.4%) for ages 18–64 (Table 2). Our survey results align with uptake data for another target group for influenza vaccination—people at risk of severe influenza within the 18–64 age group. In this other at‐risk group, coverage was reported as 36% by Beredt C19 registry data and 41% by Statistics Norway survey (Statistics Norway 2023). Different methods yield varying uptake rates. Self‐reported vaccination status aligns with registry estimates (Nowalk et al. 2021), though it may be influenced by recall and response biases (Archambault et al. 2023), while registry data could be impacted by incomplete entries.

We used SYSVAK registry data to compare our county‐level survey data on vaccine uptake among veterinarians and farmers with other groups, as Statistics Norway surveys lack county‐level information. Our findings showed higher uptake rates than the registry estimates. However, we faced a relatively low response rate and differences in recruitment and follow‐up processes between populations within our study, making direct comparisons difficult. Additionally, non‐responders likely had lower vaccine uptake than responders.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate a temporal relationship between H1N1pdm09 infection rates in humans and swine, pointing to potential spillover events, environmental factors or indirect relationships driven by unknown factors that facilitate virus spread. The model suggests that as infection rates increase in humans, they similarly increase in swine and emphasize the critical impact of swine farm density on seroprevalence. To gain deeper insight into the dynamics and direction of interspecies transmission, sequencing and phylogenetic analyses of the viruses from humans and swine are needed.
