A joint CNN-Bi-LSTM-transformer architecture with SHAP explanations for multi-label arrhythmia detection from 12-lead ECGs
Authors: Mohammed T Al-Bairmani, Mohammadreza Yazdchi, Fahimeh Nasimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterpretable, automated Artificial Intelligence (AI) solutions are essential for accurate 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) arrhythmia classification because they remove the time-consuming and inconsistent aspects of manual interpretation. Current models are limited in complexity, data variety, and validation. This paper proposes a novel Deep Learning (DL) architecture that combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTMs), and transformer layers to jointly extract morphological, temporal, and spatial patterns from ECG signals. The model was trained and evaluated on the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2020 dataset, comprising more than 43,000 multi-label ECG recordings across 27 arrhythmia classes. It achieved an accuracy of , a macro-F1 score of , and an Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) exceeding for life-threatening arrhythmias such as Ventricular Premature Beats (VPB) and Atrial Fibrillation (AF). To ensure clinical transparency, the model integrates SHAP (SHAPley Additive exPlanations), enabling case-by-case interpretability by attributing predictions to physiologically relevant waveform segments and ECG leads. This approach aligns with cardiologists’ diagnostic reasoning and supports real-world decision-making. Additionally, the model is computationally efficient, with a footprint of and inference latency of , enabling deployment in telemedicine, wearable monitoring systems, and critical care settings. The proposed framework achieves high diagnostic performance, robustness to class imbalance, and human-level interpretability simultaneously, providing a reliable, scalable solution for automated ECG analysis. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading global health concern, accounting for nearly one-third of all deaths worldwide1. Approximately 80% of sudden cardiac arrests, responsible for almost half of fatal cardiac events, are attributed to cardiac arrhythmias2. In cardiology, the electrocardiogram (ECG) is a simple, non-invasive tool for assessing cardiac rhythms. ECGs can detect pediatric cardiovascular conditions, including arrhythmias, conduction blocks, ventricular hypertrophy, and myocardial infarction3. Each cardiac cycle produces characteristic waveforms: the P wave (indicating atrial depolarization), the QRS complex (reflecting ventricular contraction), and the ST segment/T-U waves (representing ventricular repolarization). These waveforms collectively reveal the heart’s electrical activity and functional status4,5. For detailed assessment, the standard 12-lead ECG uses ten electrodes positioned to capture six limb leads (I, II, III, aVL, aVR, aVF) in the frontal plane and six precordial leads (V1–V6) in the transverse plane6. This high-resolution spatial data enables the localization and identification of cardiac abnormalities7,8.

Despite their clinical utility, ECGs require expert interpretation. Manual analysis is time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability9. Thus, automated, interpretable AI-powered ECG systems have become essential10. Recent advances in wearable technology further enable continuous ambulatory monitoring, allowing at-risk patients to be tracked outside clinical settings11. Continuous ECG monitoring enhances diagnostic accuracy and provides richer clinical insights into CVD, yet clinicians face significant challenges when interpreting long-term recordings or noisy, unreliable data12. Manual analysis under these conditions is often impractical and risks missing critical cases13. Consequently, deep learning (DL) has emerged as the dominant approach to ECG classification over the past decade, owing to its ability to automatically extract meaningful patterns from raw signals14–19. Researchers have extensively explored architectures including Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memories (Bi-LSTMs), Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), and attention mechanisms – often in hybrid combinations—seeking to improve both arrhythmia classification accuracy and model interpretability for single-label and multi-label data20,21.To provide a clear context for our work, we organize the related literature into five thematic clusters, highlighting the methodological progression in the field.

Most recently, DL architectures have been considered in the context of ECG-based arrhythmia-related analysis, such as lightweight CNNs with attention heads and CNN-Transformer systems. As an example, Elsheikhy et al.43 developed a computationally efficient CNN model with channel attention for detecting five-class arrhythmia based on multi-lead ECG signals, with an emphasis on real-time applicability rather than diagnostic coverage. Other studies have focused on single-lead ECG classification without QRS detection, or have used Transformer-based models such as ECGBERT to capture long-range correlations44,45. Although they exist, the majority of more recent methods treat arrhythmia recognition as a single-label task with few-mode classes and do not directly address the problem of co-occurring arrhythmias or severe label imbalance46.

Studies have recently discovered that using CNNs, Bi-LSTMs, GRUs, and attention-based models has greatly improved the accuracy (usually above ) of DL techniques for arrhythmia identification from ECG signals. They handled the task of finding temporal-spatial features, permitted explanation of predictions (through SHAP or attention), and worked in either real-time or environments with limited resources. Despite these advances, most existing approaches focus on limited subsets of arrhythmias, often overlooking rare or co-occurring cardiac conditions while demonstrating poor generalizability across diverse clinical classes. These limitations reveal a critical unmet need: no current DL framework can reliably detect and differentiate the full spectrum of cardiac arrhythmias in 12-lead ECGs. Even state-of-the-art models remain constrained to narrow arrhythmia classifications and exhibit limited clinical versatility. Consequently, an efficient diagnostic architecture is urgently needed—one that delivers consistent accuracy across complex cases, supports real-world deployment, and provides transparent decision-making to establish clinical trust.

To address this gap, we propose a novel architecture that integrates CNN, Bi-LSTM, and Transformer layers, enhanced with SHAP for interpretability. The model was validated on over 43,000 expert-annotated ECG recordings from the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2020 dataset47–49. Although CNNs, recurrent networks, and transformer-based attention have been adapted in isolation or in small-scale hybrid forms, the current work is the first to apply CNN, Bi-LSTM, and Transformer layers one after another, i.e., in an end-to-end architecture, explicitly to address the challenges of co-occurring pathology, class imbalance, and clinical interpretation together in a large-scale multi-label arrhythmia classification on 12-lead ECGs. Methods and materials

Dataset

This study utilized the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2020 dataset—the largest publicly available multi-institutional repository of 12-lead ECGs for automated arrhythmia classification. The dataset comprises 66,361 ECG records collected from hospitals across six countries on three continents, capturing significant diversity in patient demographics, recording conditions, and diagnostic profiles. From this collection, 43,101 expert-annotated recordings were publicly released, each annotated with one or more of 111 possible diagnostic labels. We focused on 27 clinically significant arrhythmia classes for assessment. The dataset integrates four key sources: China Physiological Signal Challenge (CPSC), PTB-XL Electrocardiography Database, St. Petersburg Institute of Cardiological Technics (INCART), and Georgia 12-lead ECG Challenge (G12EC). Demographic characteristics show broad patient representation with a mean age of approximately 60 years and balanced sex distribution (53% male, 47% female). Recordings exhibit substantial technical variation, with sampling rates ranging from 257 Hz to 1000 Hz and durations spanning 10 s to 30 min (1800 s). Crucially, the dataset enables authentic multi-label classification as many records contain co-occurring arrhythmias. Diagnosis distribution follows clinically realistic imbalance patterns, necessitating robust classifiers capable of handling skewed data. Noise filtering

ECG signals exhibited contamination from three persistent noise sources: baseline wander (low-frequency drift below ), powerline interference at, and Electromyographic (EMG) noise from muscle activity. To address these artifacts while preserving diagnostic waveform integrity, we implemented a sequential digital filtering cascade. First, a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency attenuated baseline drift without distorting critical ST-segment morphology. Next, a narrow-band notch filter centered at eliminated powerline interference while maintaining phase coherence across leads. Finally, median filtering suppressed high-frequency EMG and movement artifacts, with careful parameterization ensuring the PQRST complex morphology, including QRS slope and T-wave amplitude, remained clinically interpretable throughout processing.

Data integration

All signals from every dataset were put together after pre-processing all the sources. Here, all diagnoses got a common name through SNOMED CT, and all duplicated or damaged records were taken out. During this step, the labels used in the data from all institutions were made consistent53.

Proposed model architecture

This study introduces a novel interpretable DL model for automated classification of 27 clinically significant cardiac arrhythmias from raw 12-lead ECG signals. As depicted in Fig. 2, the architecture integrates CNNs for spatial feature extraction with bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) networks for temporal modeling. The processing pipeline begins with three sequential 1D convolutional blocks, each comprising a convolutional layer followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max-pooling operations. These blocks hierarchically identify morphological patterns in ECG waveforms while progressively reducing feature dimensionality and enhancing computational efficiency. The extracted spatial features are then processed by a Bi-LSTM layer that analyzes temporal dependencies in both forward and reverse directions. This bidirectional processing captures complex rhythm transitions and transient arrhythmic events while mitigating vanishing gradient problems through gated memory mechanisms. Following the recurrent layer, dropout regularization is applied to prevent overfitting. The temporal features subsequently pass through two fully connected layers that refine representations and reduce dimensionality before reaching the final classification layer. A sigmoid-activated dense layer outputs independent probabilities for each arrhythmia class, enabling multi-label classification. To handle overlapping conditions, the model was trained using binary cross-entropy loss with multi-hot encoded target vectors. Post-training, SHAP was integrated to quantify feature contributions57, allowing clinicians to identify physiologically significant waveform segments that influence predictions. All experiments were carried out on a rigorous patient-wise data partitioning policy in order to avoid subject-level information leaksage. Particularly, the training set was assigned to 80% of the patients, and the rest was chosen as an independent test set, 20%. Training The internal validation during training was only done on the training set, and was the basis of hyperparameter tuning and early stopping, but nothing was done with the test set.

Overall block diagram of the proposed approach to ECG analysis.

In the proposed architecture depicted in Fig. 2, three consecutive, mutually complementary modules are proposed: hierarchical spatial-morphological feature-extracting CNNs, local and long-range temporal modelling Bi-LSTM layers, and a global contextual reasoning Transformer encoder. A transformer module is added to the model to capture long-range, inter-beat, and inter-lead dependencies that are not fully represented by recurrent structures, thereby providing higher discrimination between arrhythmias observed to co-occur in the multi-label scenario. In particular, the Transformer block will include a single encoder block (with multi-head self-attention (four attention heads)) followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (dimensionality 128). On the one hand, positional encodings are used to maintain temporal order following Bi-LSTM processing. This design allows the model to selectively focus on diagnostically salient ECG segments within the entire window while remaining computationally efficient. The Transformer is then pooled to the output, which is then sent to the multi-label classification full layers. Finally, multi-head transformer attention mechanisms identify clinically significant waveform segments, such as aberrant P-waves or widened QRS complexes, by establishing long-range dependencies across the entire signal. This multi-scale feature fusion provides inherent robustness to lead-specific variations and acquisition noise. Post-training, SHAP analysis visualizes feature contributions via physiologically interpretable saliency maps, consistently highlighting clinically relevant indicators such as ST-segment deviations or T-wave inversions. The combined approach ensures model decisions align with cardiological domain knowledge while maintaining diagnostic fidelity across diverse ECG morphologies.

Transformer mechanism

The strategic placement of a Transformer encoder following the Bi-LSTM block substantially elevates model performance and generalization across diverse arrhythmia classes. While Bi-LSTM effectively captures bidirectional long-range dependencies, its inherent sequential processing limits multi-scale global attention. The Transformer overcomes this constraint through its multi-head self-attention mechanism, which dynamically weights diagnostically critical ECG segments, such as anomalous P-wave morphologies or variable RR intervals, to enhance discrimination between rhythm disorders. Positional encodings preserve temporal ordering, while feedforward layers nonlinearly transform representations, enriching the feature space with hierarchical abstractions58. This synergistic architecture leverages Bi-LSTM for temporal abstraction and Transformer for global contextual reasoning, creating a complementary framework that excels at disentangling co-occurring arrhythmias59. The resulting encoder output demonstrates: Enhanced robustness to acquisition noise and lead variations, improved feature disentanglement yielding clinically interpretable representations, and heightened reliability reflected in elevated macro-F1 scores.

Model explainability with SHAP

To resolve the “black-box” limitation and enhance clinical trust in multi-label arrhythmia detection, we integrated SHAP into our CNN-BiLSTM-Transformer architecture. Given the model’s complexity, SHAP analysis quantifies the contribution of individual ECG waveform segments to each prediction, providing both global and local interpretability. Using DeepExplainer, a variant optimized for deep neural networks, we computed two complementary perspectives: global feature importance across the entire dataset, and local explanation vectors for individual cases57. This approach consistently identified physiologically critical waveform characteristics—including P-wave morphology, QRS complex duration, and T-wave amplitude—as primary determinants of arrhythmia classifications. Crucially, SHAP interaction values revealed how these components cooperatively influence complex rhythm identification, such as coincident PR interval prolongation and ST depression, indicating concurrent conduction abnormalities and ischemic events. To ensure methodological rigor, we established a representative reference set of 100 background ECGs and validated SHAP value stability through cross-validation across demographic subgroups. By translating model decisions into cardiologist-aligned explanations grounded in electrophysiological principles, this framework bridges AI’s predictive capabilities with clinical reasoning. The resultant operationalization of trust represents a foundational advance toward transparent AI adoption in cardiovascular diagnostics60.

Results

Implementation details

The model was implemented using Python 3.8 and TensorFlow 2.9 on Google Colab’s free-tier CPU environment, accessed via Google Chrome on a Lenovo laptop (Intel i3-1215U, 16GB RAM, Windows 10 64-bit). The reported performance results in this section are only applicable to the independent test set, which constitutes 20% of patients and were not visible at all when training the model and performing internal optimization. This evaluation protocol will provide an impartial measurement of the generalization performance in practice clinical situations. Input data underwent per-lead standardization using StandardScaler (zero mean, unit variance). Training employed the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of and binary cross-entropy loss, appropriate for multi-label classification, over 100 epochs. Regularization included dropout (0.3) and early stopping (patience: 10 epochs), monitoring validation loss. Hardware constraints necessitated CPU-efficient configurations: Batch size of 64 and memory-mapped I/O for out-of-core processing. Post-training, SHAP analysis identified ECG segments influencing predictions, with computation optimized through kernel approximation and stratified background sampling (100 representative ECGs).

Evaluation criteria

The model’s performance in classifying 27 cardiac arrhythmias was comprehensively evaluated through six key metrics: accuracy (overall classification correctness), precision (minimizing false positives), recall (reducing false negatives), macro-averaged F1-score (robustness to class imbalance), ROC curves (visualizing specificity-sensitivity trade-offs), and confusion matrices (identifying class-specific error patterns). Given the significant imbalance in ECG class distribution, particular emphasis was placed on the macro-F1 metric due to its equitable weighting of all classes regardless of prevalence. Precision and recall provided complementary perspectives on diagnostic errors, while confusion matrices revealed systematic misclassifications between clinically similar arrhythmias. Beyond conventional metrics, SHAP values quantified feature-level contributions to predictions, enabling physiological interpretation of decision pathways. Collectively, these measures validated the model’s diagnostic reliability and clinical utility for real-world deployment.

An ablation study was conducted to quantify the contribution of the Transformer component by varying the proposed architecture while keeping training protocols, hyperparameters, and data partitions constant. Three of them were considered, namely: (i) the complete CNN-Bi-LSTM-Transformer model, (ii) the CNN-Bi-LSTM model without the Transformer encoder, and (iii) the CNN-Transformer model without recurrent layers. Accuracy, precision, recall, and macro-F1 score were used to measure the performance on the held-out test set. As can be seen in Table 8, eliminating the Transformer module led to a significant drop in macro-F1 score and recall, especially in low-prevalence and co-occurring arrhythmias. This validates that the global self-attention mechanism of the Transformer can represent long-range contextual dependencies that convolutional or recurrent layers cannot fully model. In turn, the entire architecture was consistently optimal in terms of accuracy, class robustness, and understandability, which confirms the decision to include the Transformer as a fundamental element of the proposed framework.

Discussion

The proposed study presents a clinically interpretable and computationally efficient DL model, based on the synergistic combination of CNN, Bi-LSTM, and Transformer blocks, for the multi-label classification of 27 cardiac arrhythmias using 12-lead ECG signals. The framework achieves impressive progress in accuracy, generalization, and explainability, establishing a new level in the field of ECG-based diagnostics. As shown in Fig. 2, the model architecture hierarchically decomposes the ECG signal into three different but complementary feature domains. The CNN blocks initially extract morphological features that are spatially localized, including P-QRS-T configurations, which is an important step to detect abnormalities such as bundle branch blocks or premature beats. After that, Bi-LSTM layers allow modeling the temporal dependency in both directions, including the rhythmic pattern and irregularities, such as sinus arrhythmia and AF. Finally, the transformer block also uses multi-head self-attention to contextualize ECG sequences on a global scale to detect less pronounced diagnostic patterns over longer time intervals. This three-modality learning cascade (CNN + Bi-LSTM+Transformer) allows a clinically coherent process of diagnosis that reflects the top-down thinking of cardiologists. On the imbalanced test data, the proposed model attained accuracy and macro-F1 (Table 4), which is % points higher than comparative approaches. The misclassifications corresponded to clinical similarity (kappa = 0.82; Fig. 4), as did cardiologist variability. The performance of life-threatening arrhythmias (PVC/VPB) was perfectly accurate (, ), and rare cases (NSIVCB/PAC) also showed a good performance (), with SHAP attributions to P-wave absence () and QRS widening (). sliding windows were fastest/accurate (5.6% faster/accurate than 2 s; Fig. 7), Training efficiency was demonstrated by a rapid 72% reduction in loss within the first 15 epochs, followed by continued optimization that achieved a final loss value three orders of magnitude smaller than the initial loss. The interpretability analysis indicates that the model puts greater weight on ECG sections and waveform parts commonly known in the cardiology literature to have diagnostic value in arrhythmia detection. Although these patterns can be qualitatively related to the current body of knowledge in electrophysiology, this paper does not purport to have been formally validated by cardiologists. In their place, the interpretability outcomes should enhance model transparency and ease clinical inspection. The further work will consist of the systematic review of such explanations by professional cardiologists to determine the degree of clinical concordance and diagnostic use quantitatively. Figure 8 shows the compact clustering of feature embeddings (high-dimensional) using t-SNE projection, where representations of Transformer-enhanced representations demonstrate better class separability compared to the baseline using Bi-LSTM alone. Such clusters not only confirm the discriminability of the latent space of the model but are also consistent with ECG waveform morphologies with diagnostic significance. As Table 6 indicates, comparative analysis with state-of-the-art architectures proves that the proposed model might be better than existing frameworks by in test accuracy and is trained in 35% shorter time. This is done at a significantly lower architectural footprint (Table 7), which makes the model suitable for being integrated into real-time applications such as edge devices, telemedicine, and ICU monitors. Its low size (below ) and inference latency make it suitable to be deployed in wearable ECG systems and mobile platforms, which will increase access to remote diagnostics. Additionally, SHAP-based analysis (Fig. 9) offers an interpretability framework that enables the model to match the prediction to acceptable clinical standards. Specifically, AF is marked by the lack of P-waves and irregular R-R intervals, with each of them providing >35% SHAP weight in the final prediction. In the same manner, abnormalities such as long PR intervals or Q-wave depressions have high SHAP relevance in the respective arrhythmias of LPR and QAb, respectively, proving the pathophysiological coherence of the internal representations of the model. Taken together, the model has a high AUROC mean of 0.98 in all classes of arrhythmia, narrow confidence intervals, and an even distribution of SHAP values in ECG leads. These measures not only confirm the reliability of the model but also show that it is transparent and safe to use in the real world. The framework solves the limitation of conventional DL techniques in cardiology, which is the black-box nature, by integrating interpretability into the classification pipeline. However, the research has a number of limitations. To begin with, performance on ultra-rare arrhythmia (e.g., TAb and LAnFB <100 samples) dropped to 81% in the recall. This is one of the limitations that can be addressed in future research by augmenting synthetic data using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). Second, lead-specific variability (e.g., lead III vs. V5) added some slight inconsistencies in the prediction accuracy. Lead weighting mechanisms that are based on attention can improve the consistency of the signal to diagnosis. Third, pre-processed data gave good results, but raw, noisy inputs led to a 5% decrease in classification accuracy, which demonstrates the importance of adaptive denoising modules in future iterations. To sum up, the suggested CNN-Bi-LSTM-Transformer model, along with the SHAP explainability, can be considered a major step in the direction of AI in cardiovascular disease. It also solves the issue of long-term diagnostic complexity, explainability of models, and data imbalance. This model can be a stepping stone toward the integration of trustworthy AI in cardiology because it will provide clinical-grade accuracy, interpretable predictions, and minimal computational overhead simultaneously. Future plans will be aimed at federated learning to continually optimize the models, multimodal ECG-echocardiography fusion, and prospective validation of the proposed models using randomized clinical trials to quantify their effect on patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study introduces a clinically informed and interpretable DL framework for the automated multi-label classification of cardiac arrhythmias in 12-lead ECGs. Our novel CNN-BiLSTM-Transformer architecture synergistically combines convolutional feature extraction, bidirectional temporal analysis, and global attention mechanisms to enable comprehensive ECG interpretation. Evaluated on 43,101 multi-center recordings from PhysioNet (spanning 27 arrhythmia classes), the model achieved state-of-the-art performance with 91.24% accuracy and an 83.01% macro F1-score, surpassing nine benchmarks by 3.04% absolute accuracy. Critically, the framework demonstrates exceptional capability in detecting life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias (PVC/VPB: AUC 0.99, 93.4% precision) while operating with unprecedented efficiency: 35% faster training convergence (3100 s total), <2 MB memory footprint, and real-time inference (< 10 ms latency). Hierarchical feature compression and attention mechanisms enable this deployable performance without sacrificing diagnostic precision, evidenced by strong agreement with cardiologists (Cohen’s κ = 0.82). The architecture’s robustness to class imbalance and expert-level error profiles (particularly for clinically ambiguous cases) positions it as a new standard for telemedicine platforms and next-generation wearables. By bridging high performance (91.24% accuracy), computational efficiency (< 10 ms inference), and physiological transparency, this work closes the translational gap between DL innovation and clinical ECG analysis. Future efforts will focus on cross-institutional domain adaptation, real-time motion artifact suppression, and multimodal signal fusion to further advance AI’s role in cardiovascular diagnostics. In addition to enhancing performance, the central innovation of this paper is its task-oriented approach to architectural synthesis. Instead of showing a disjointed set of network components, we show that a well-coordinated combination of CNN-based morphological encoding, Bi-LSTM-based bidirectional time modeling, and Transformer-based global attention can provide a clinically scalable solution to the task of multi-label ECG interpretation. The design allows reliable separation of co-existing arrhythmias, the electrophysiological interpretability of the design via SHAP-based explanations, and the computational efficiency of the design, making it reasonable to deploy in the real world. The presented framework, therefore, pushes the state of the art forward not only through architectural composition but also through a redefinition of how DL systems could be designed to satisfy the practical, interpretability, and scalability demands of current cardiovascular diagnostics.
