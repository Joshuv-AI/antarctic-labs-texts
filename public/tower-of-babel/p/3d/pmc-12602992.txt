Gender‐Affirming Hormone Therapy: Pharmacokinetic Considerations for Oncology in Transgender Patients
Authors: Anna Carollo, Lavinia Piazza, Alessio Provenzani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGender‐Affirming Hormone Therapy (GAHT) is a cornerstone of transgender care which induces profound physiological changes and significantly impacts pharmacokinetics and drug dosing strategies. This narrative review examines the clinical implications of GAHT, with a particular focus on renal function parameters, biomarkers, and challenges in the oncological setting. Evidence shows that GAHT considerably alters body composition, muscle mass and serum creatinine levels, resulting in fluctuations of standard calculations of creatinine clearance (CrCl) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Unfortunately, standardized dosing formulas remain unvalidated in transgender populations, although gender identity should guide renal function estimates and advocate for cautious interpretation and case‐by‐case assessment. Recent studies underscore the relevance of emerging biomarkers, such as cystatin C, as possible solutions for assessing renal function in transgender patients, since they are less influenced by body mass. Moreover, direct effects of exogenous hormones on renal physiology have also been highlighted, stressing the importance of thorough evaluation of all these factors, in order to ensure the accuracy of dosing algorithms based on gender‐affirming hormones. This issue becomes especially important in oncology, considering that improper use of antineoplastic agents may result in under or overdosing, especially for renally excreted or narrow therapeutic index drugs. Overall, this review aims to represent a resounding prompt to a multidisciplinary team of clinicians, pharmacists and pharmacologists that should play a crucial role in implementing safe, evidence‐based, and respectful individualized care pathways, particularly in complex clinical scenarios such as oncology.Keywords: cancer therapy, gender‐affirming hormone therapy, gender‐diverse care, oncology, transgender care

Introduction

Over the past decade, there has been a notable increase in the number and visibility of transgender and gender‐diverse (TGD) individuals seeking support and gender‐affirming medical treatments, alongside a significant increase in scientific literature in this field.

The disparities in healthcare faced by TGD individuals are a cause for serious concern, particularly with regard to medically necessary healthcare services. Despite these disparities in terms of equity, it is estimated that the TGD population will continue to become increasingly visible, likely due to greater social awareness and understanding.

Depending on the individual's clinical history with gender affirmation care, primary and preventive care may warrant special considerations. Transgender patients often suffer discrimination in healthcare settings and lack access to medical staff who should be knowledgeable about transgender medicine. This leads to a lack of access to preventive healthcare services and timely treatment for routine health issues.

Moreover, the global rise in the number of transgender and gender‐diverse individuals (including those with binary and nonbinary identities) undergoing GAHT has underscored the urgent need to integrate gender‐specific considerations into clinical practice, especially in pharmacology. GAHT induces physiological changes that substantially impact pharmacokinetic parameters essential for dosage calculation, such as creatinine clearance (CrCl) and ideal body weight (IBW) (Figure 1) [1, 2, 3].

Overview of the physiological transition and pharmacological implications of gender‐affirming hormone therapy (GAHT). Cisgender individuals differ in baseline body composition and renal function, with lower muscle mass and serum creatinine levels in cis females and higher values in cis males. After approximately 6 months of GAHT, represented by the central balance and capsule icon, transgender individuals experience physiological changes that align body composition and renal markers with gender identity. These modifications can influence pharmacokinetic parameters (ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) and may affect drug exposure in oncology, with potential risks of overdosing or underdosing.

Fluctuations in body fat distribution may increase the volume of distribution for lipophilic drugs and alter drug clearance rates, potentially leading to faster clearance in individuals assigned female at birth compared to those assigned male. Additionally, the systemic exposure of certain drugs, especially CYP3A substrates, may be higher in individuals assigned female, reflecting differences in bioavailability, clearance, or metabolic activity. Conversely, individuals assigned male generally exhibit faster renal processes (glomerular filtration, tubular secretion, and reabsorption), which can further modify drug metabolism.

All these issues become particularly relevant in oncology, where precise drug dosing is crucial to optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing toxicity. In this regard, understanding the variations in pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) in these populations for whom the drugs are intended becomes fundamental [4, 5].

This narrative review analyzes the current available evidence on dose adjustment in transgender patients, with a focus on physiological calculation parameters and challenges that may occur in cancer therapies.

Cultural Context

Gender expression is not necessarily related to the sex assigned at birth or gender identity; it can vary across geographical areas, cultures, and historical periods.

Cultural distinctions in gender concepts, the language used to describe gender, and attitudes toward TGD individuals can influence the expression of gender identity. Cultural norms, language, and societal attitudes toward TGD individuals profoundly shape how gender identity is expressed and perceived. Many Western societies consider gender as binary: male or female. This mindset sets an expectation that gender expression must comply with one or the other and can contribute to an increased susceptibility to poor mental health outcomes due to a lack of social acceptance and/or access to care, so specific challenges may arise making gender identity a delicate subject to deal with in counseling.

By contrast, some perspectives conceptualize gender as a continuum ranging from masculine to feminine, suggesting a spectrum of gender identities with variable proportions of masculinity and femininity and a broad “world” of opportunities to explore gender, including neither masculine nor feminine, or entirely distinct.

Societies that view gender from this more fluid or evolutionary perspective may be more tolerant of gender variations. Recognition and validation of the gender continuum by healthcare can play a transformative role, fostering acceptance within families and communities and reducing stigma [6, 7].

Barriers to Care

Many sexual and gender minority (SGM) individuals avoid seeking medical care due to judgmental or insensitive interactions with healthcare providers, often compounded by heteronormative assumptions. As a result, SGM individuals frequently report poorer health‐related quality of life compared with non‐SGM populations.

Several additional factors exacerbate these disparities. Exposure to discriminatory laws and policies is associated with elevated psychosocial stress. Within healthcare settings, discrimination often expresses through implicit or explicit biases, such as providers making presumptive judgments about patients or dismissing their concerns. Social and minority stressors, including victimization, stigmatization, and systemic inequities, further impair overall health outcomes, with Black SGM adults disproportionately affected when compared to their white counterparts. Financial barriers, including inadequate health insurance coverage and the inability to afford medications or timely care, further restrict access to essential services. Moreover, there is also a significant lack of standardized, validated outcome measures for evaluating gender‐affirming care. Most available data are retrospective and lack proper validation, limiting their reliability and clinical applicability. Developing patient‐centered, evidence‐based outcome metrics is urgently needed to evaluate the more appropriate pharmacokinetic parameters in patients undergoing GAHT, in order to improve transgender patients' care [6, 7, 8, 9].

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM‐5‐TR) defines a clinical diagnosis known as gender dysphoria, which encompasses a range of experiences commonly reported by TGD individuals. This diagnosis is characterized by psychological distress stemming from a misalignment between an individual's assigned sex at birth and their affirmed gender identity. Core diagnostic features include persistent discomfort with one's designated gender and significant interference in social, academic, or other functional domains.

While the DSM‐5‐TR criteria may offer valuable insight into the experiences of certain TGD individuals, it is important to acknowledge that many others do not meet the diagnostic threshold or do not find the diagnosis applicable or affirming. Conceptualizing gender diversity through a developmental, fluid, and evolving lens, rather than through a strictly diagnostic or pathological framework, encourages a broader and more inclusive understanding. This perspective affirms that gender exploration and diverse gender expression are common aspects of human development in both children and adults. Such a view can be instrumental in supporting families and professionals in recognizing gender exploration as a natural and non‐pathological process [8, 9].

Physiology and Pharmacokinetics in the Transgender Context

Absorption

Drug pharmacokinetics can differ substantially between biological sexes due to variations in body composition, hepatic metabolism, renal function, and fluid distribution. In transgender individuals undergoing GAHT, these physiological characteristics evolve progressively to align more closely with gender identity, introducing new complexity into pharmacokinetic modeling [10].

The factors influencing absorption are route‐specific (oral, dermal, rectal, vaginal, intramuscular, intravenous, intraarterial, intrathecal, intraperitoneal) and may also be sex‐specific. Chemicals or drugs cross body surfaces such as the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, or skin (different in males and females) to enter the systemic circulation [10].

Gastric enzymes, such as gastric alcohol dehydrogenase activity are higher in males than in females. As a result, women have a lower alcoholic toxic threshold than men and develop alcoholic liver injury more rapidly. In addition, estrogens have a major influence on the susceptibility of Kupffer cells to gut‐derived lipopolysaccharide differences in gastric first‐pass metabolism, elimination rate, or alcohol distribution volume. The enterocytes express significant levels of CYP3A isoenzymes which contribute significantly to the first‐pass metabolism of many orally administered drugs and have known sex differences [11].

Turning our attention to transporter proteins, multi‐specific transporters are involved in hepatobiliary and urinary excretion. Tissue distribution and elimination pathways of drugs are explained by the similarity and differences in substrate recognition by transporters expressed in the liver and kidney. Variability in intestinal expression of enzymes that modulate gut transport of drugs may result in sex‐based variability in plasma drug concentrations. For example, P‐glycoprotein (PGP), a membrane ATPase transporter protein found in high concentrations in the enterocytes of the small intestine, is encoded by the multi‐drug resistance transporter‐1 gene (MDR1) expressed in the human intestine, liver and other tissues. Complex relationships between intestinal transporters such as H+/di tripeptide transporter (PEPT1), organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) and other unidentified ones facilitate drug absorption, while efflux transporters such as PGP sometimes work as drug absorption barriers [12, 13].

Gastric fluids differ between men and women; gastric fluid is more acidic in males than females (pH = 1.92 vs. pH = 2.59), and the basal and maximal flow of gastric fluid and acid secretion is higher in men than women (reduced by 30% in pregnancy). Reduced pH results in decreased absorption of weak acids and increased absorption of weak bases. Drug absorption occurs at different sites along the gastrointestinal tract including the stomach, small and large intestines. The rate of absorption is influenced by multiple factors, including gut transit times, lipid solubility of the agent, pH at the site of absorption, ionization and molecular weight of the agent, and gut motility. Transit times differ significantly in men and women; mean transit times are shorter in men (44.8 h) than in women (91.7 h). While fiber ingestion decreases the transit time, female gut transit times are consistently longer [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

Distribution

Recent studies suggest that within the first 6 months of GAHT, there are marked changes in muscle mass, adipose tissue distribution, and serum creatinine levels, which can significantly impact drug distribution volumes and clearance rates [1, 2, 3]. Therefore, the conventional “male/female” dichotomy in pharmacokinetic calculations may be inappropriate for this population, considering that GAHT involves physiological changes that affect pharmacokinetics and therapeutic response to medications.

Also, transgender women (assigned male at birth and identifying as female), receiving estrogen and antiandrogen therapy typically experience muscle mass reduction, decreased endogenous creatinine production, and altered estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Conversely, transgender men (assigned female at birth) undergoing testosterone therapy usually see increased muscle mass and higher serum creatinine levels [2, 3]. Of note, these changes in body composition have direct implications on the tissue distribution of drugs, particularly for water‐soluble molecules, and on renal and hepatic metabolism. In addition, the duration of hormone therapy is also decisive: after at least 6 months of GAHT, physiological variations are generally considered stable and can be used as a reference for estimating renal function and for treatment planning. These alterations, which begin in the first three to 6 months of treatment, have an impact on key parameters, such as volume of distribution and renal clearance, and could preclude the use of standard pharmacokinetic algorithms based on sex assigned at birth [1, 2, 3].

Metabolism

Drug metabolism and biotransformation occur predominantly in the liver, as well as in extra hepatic sites of metabolism such as the lung, kidney, intestinal tract, and skin. Hepatic clearance of drugs is a function of liver blood flow and hepatic enzyme activity. Although cardiac output and hepatic blood flow are lower in women than in men normalized per m2/kg, sex differences in hepatic enzymes play a major role in determining sex‐related pharmacokinetic activity.

Hepatic drug metabolism is divided into two, usually sequential enzymatic reactions: Phase I and Phase II reactions. Some of the CYP450 enzymes show clear sex‐related differences. In general, lipophilic compounds have a tendency to pass through biological membranes and/or be stored, and are often susceptible to Phase I types of metabolism. Sex‐related differences have been shown in the pharmacokinetics of CYP450, with a higher activity in females for CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

Elimination

An important contribution arises from the multicenter perspective study KNIGHT, published in 2024, which examined the effects of GAHT on renal function using direct measures such as plasma clearance of iohexol for measured Glomerular Filtration Rate (mGFR) and para‐aminopuric clearance for Effective Renal Plasma Flow (ERPF). The adult transgender participants, between the ages of 17 and 40, were divided into transgender women and transgender men [16].

Estrogen and antiandrogen therapy in transgender women resulted in a slight but significant increase in mGFR (+3.6%) and ERPF (+9.1%), suggesting an estrogen‐mediated vasodilator effect. In contrast, testosterone therapy in transgender men did not significantly modify these parameters, but increased tubular biomarkers indicative of possible subclinical renal inflammatory stress [16]. In addition, fluctuations in serum creatinine levels should also be considered when evaluating renal elimination in transgender patients receiving GAHT, as they may influence pharmacokinetic assessments. Furthermore, it is emphasized that serum hormone levels must be interpreted in relation to the route of administration (oral, sublingual, transdermal, intramuscular) and the time of sampling, given the inter‐individual pharmacokinetic variability and different half‐lives of the available formulations [17].

Interestingly, these data indicate that exogenous sex hormones not only influence body composition, but also exert direct effects on renal physiology, potentially affecting the pharmacokinetics of renally excreted drugs. Obviously, these pharmacokinetic aspects become just as indispensable when developing highly personalized protocols in oncology.

Gender‐Affirming Hormone Therapy and Clinical Implications

GAHT substantially affects the physiology of the transgender patient, making the use of parameters such as IBW and CrCl critical, as both are influenced by lean mass and kidney function. Guidelines currently do not provide definitive, universally accepted indications, but there is a growing consensus that after 6 months of GAHT, CrCl and IBW should be calculated according to gender identity, whereas before 6 months it is more appropriate to refer to the sex assigned at birth [1, 2, 3].

In combination with these anthropometric aspects, it is essential to consider pharmacokinetic differences related to the route of administration of hormones, particularly estradiol (E2). Estradiol 17β administered sublingually in transgender women shows faster absorption, a greater bioavailability and increased plasma levels compared to the oral route. However, its levels decrease rapidly, requiring more frequent doses for effective testosterone suppression. The ratio of estradiol to estrone (E2:E1) is more favorable with the sublingual route. This route may impact hepatic metabolism (e.g., via CYP3A4) and drug interactions, and it is potentially preferable in patients with cardiovascular risk, although further studies are needed to define its safety and optimal dosage [4].

The absorption and metabolic pathways influenced by the route of estradiol administration should be carefully considered in transgender patients, particularly when interpreting hormone levels and adjusting therapy. Retrospective data from the Mayo Clinic reinforce these observations: transgender women exhibited significant reductions in serum creatinine within 3 months of estradiol therapy, while transgender men experienced rises corresponding to testosterone's anabolic effects on muscle mass. Importantly, these variations persisted at 6 and 12 months and were independent of BMI, supporting the notion that body composition rather than total body mass drives these changes [5].

Moreover, the estradiol formulation influenced the magnitude of the variation: the transdermal and injectable routes resulted in greater drops in creatinine than the oral formulation. These data reinforce the clinical need to reconsider the “baseline” creatinine value in transgender patients after at least 6 months of GAHT in order to appropriately calculate CrCl and eGFR. The retrospective study published in 2022, which longitudinally analyzed serum creatinine levels in a large cohort of transgender GAHT patients, showed that deviations occur as early as 3 months after the start of therapy. In particular, a reduction is observed in transgender women, while in transgender men there is a progressive and stable progression, independent of BMI. These results support the idea that the changes are mainly related to muscle mass or direct effects of the hormones on kidney function. This evidence confirms that, after 6 months of GAHT, it is clinically appropriate to adopt the new stabilized creatinine values as the basis for calculating renal function. Furthermore, the route of administration of estradiol (oral, transdermal, injectable) affects the magnitude of the observed changes, with more pronounced reductions associated with non‐oral routes [5].

Drug Dosage Calculation: Critical Issues and Equations

A systematic review and meta‐analysis confirmed that in the transgender population, after at least 6 months of hormone therapy, stable changes in serum creatinine are observed: a decrease in male‐to‐female patients and an increase in female‐to‐male patients, consistent with changes in muscle mass [3]. These data provide support for the use of the gender identified for the calculation of renal function in patients taking GAHT for more than 6 months, as also recommended by several scientific societies. In this regard, it is important to emphasize that classical tools for estimating CrCl or eGFR, such as the Cockcroft‐Gault, MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) or CKD‐EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) formulas, have not been formally validated in the transgender population, necessitating a critical and flexible clinical approach [3].

The Cockcroft‐Gault equations for estimating creatinine clearance and the Devine equation for calculating IBW are well‐established tools in clinical practice for dose adjustment. However, both are based on data derived from cisgender populations. In particular, the Cockcroft‐Gault formula is sensitive to serum creatinine and thus to changes in muscle mass, which are strongly influenced by gender‐affirming hormone therapy [1, 2, 3]. The Mayo Clinic retrospective study revealed that significant changes in creatinine can manifest themselves as early as 3 months after the start of GAHT, stabilizing within the first year [4, 5]. For this reason, it is recommended to periodically update creatinine reference values and adopt a gender‐identified CrCl or eGFR calculation when the duration of GAHT is longer than 6 months [4]. It has also been pointed out that serum creatinine levels may vary depending on the type and dosage of administered estradiol, underlining that GAHT may induce not only endogenous creatinine production through body remodeling, but also through direct effects on renal glomerular function [5]. In this context, a recent guideline recommends calculating CrCl with the Cockcroft‐Gault formula using both sex assigned at birth and gender identity. If both estimates are above 60 mL/min, chemotherapy dosing can be performed based on gender identity. If either estimate is below this threshold, a direct measurement with 24‐h urine collection is indicated to avoid under‐ or over‐estimation of renal function [17].

This concern for precision and tailoring treatment is essential, as many antineoplastic drugs have a narrow therapeutic index and potential elevated toxicity in case of overdose [17].

The KNIGHT study reported the first direct demonstration that GAHT induces measurable renal physiological modifications, even independently of changes in body mass. In especial, the increase in mGFR observed in male‐to‐female transgender patients can potentially alter drug clearance, thereby heightening the risk of underdosing in the case of renally excreted molecules. By contrast, the lack of improvement in renal function in female‐to‐male individuals, despite the increase in muscle mass, raises the issue of a possible unfavorable effect of testosterone at the tubulo‐interstitial level [18].

These data highlight that the approach to estimating renal function in transgender patients must go beyond simple anthropometric or serum creatinine assessment to include a broader understanding of the biological effects of GAHT on target organs. Hence, in complex clinical situations, such as oncology or advanced nephropathy, it is recommended that direct measurement methods, such as creatinine clearance on 24‐h urine or, when available, clearance with inulin, should be used in addition to CrCl estimation. The use of alternative markers such as cystatin C may be a valid approach to obtain a more accurate assessment of renal function in transgender patients [16]. Cystatin C represents a promising alternative, as it is less influenced by muscle mass, age or gender, although it may be altered by infection, obesity or smoking and is not yet widely available in clinical practice [16, 17, 18].

The Role of Cystatin C in Assessing Renal Function in Transgender Patients

Cystatin C, a low molecular weight protein produced by all nucleated cells, is freely filtered by the glomerulus and fully metabolized in the proximal tubules without reabsorption or secretion. Unlike creatinine, its levels are largely unaffected by muscle mass, sex, diet, or ethnicity, making it a more stable biomarker, particularly valuable for transgender patients experiencing rapid body composition shifts under GAHT [16, 19]. These features render it particularly suitable for transgender patients, in whom GAHT can result in rapid and significant changes in muscle mass, compromising the reliability of serum creatinine as an indicator of renal function. In such cases, measurement of cystatin C, alone or in combination with creatinine, can provide a more accurate estimate of eGFR [16].

More recent equations for calculating eGFR, such as the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration's combined formula (CKD‐EPI), which integrates both creatinine and cystatin C, are less influenced by demographic and anthropometric factors, thus representing a promising tool for assessing renal function in non‐traditional populations, including transgender patients [17].

Routine use of cystatin C could refine dose adjustments in complex fields like nephrology and oncology, supporting truly individualized, evidence‐based care [16, 17].

Oncological Implications: Challenges and Possible Solutions

In oncology setting, precise drug dosing is critical to ensure the efficacy of antineoplastic treatments while minimizing toxicity. Many oncologic drugs have a narrow therapeutic window and are dosed according to weight, body surface area or renal function. In transgender patients treated by GAHT, the inappropriate use of physiological parameters may lead to clinically relevant underdosing or overdosing [20].

Beyond systemic changes in serum creatinine, distribution volume and hepatic metabolism, recent evidence suggests that GAHT may also influence intracellular pharmacokinetics.

For example, renal function plays a critical role in calculating the dose of platinum coordination compounds, including carboplatin. The latter is prescribed in combination protocols with gemcitabine, pemetrexed or etoposide in cases of non‐small cell lung cancer, where the dosage is calculated based on AUC and creatinine clearance, in accordance with Calvert's formula. Therefore, changes in these parameters must be carefully evaluated in transgender patients [21].

Moreover, a notable study demonstrated that transgender women receiving estradiol (with or without antiandrogens) exhibited a 25%–35% reduction in intracellular concentrations of the active metabolites of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TFV‐DP) and emtricitabine (FTC‐TP), despite having comparable plasma levels to cisgender controls [21]. This effect is thought to be mediated by estrogen‐induced modulation of efflux transporters such as PGP and BCRP, which can influence intracellular drug accumulation. Comparable findings have been observed in cisfemales, who may display lower intracellular drug concentrations than cismales for certain substrates, and exogenous estrogen exposure (e.g., hormonal contraception or hormone replacement therapy) has been reported to further modulate these pathways [21]. Estradiol may modulate intracellular exposure by altering drug transport, phosphorylation, or accumulation. Although these effects have been primarily studied in the context of HIV PrEP, they underscore the potential need to re‐evaluate antineoplastic drug dosages in transgender patients [21].

Incorporating gender identity and GAHT as covariates into pharmacokinetic models is an important step toward fully personalized therapy. In the absence of direct studies, oncologists, clinical pharmacists and pharmacologists should consider GAHT when managing drugs with a low therapeutic index or in polypharmacy settings [22].

For transgender patients with hormone‐dependent neoplasms, such as HR+ (hormone receptor‐positive) breast tumors, the management of GAHT represents a clinical challenge. In some cases, temporary interruption of GAHT may be needed for receptor status assessment or to define optimal oncologic strategies.

However, systematic discontinuation of GAHT is not generally recommended; treatment decisions should be individualized, weighing oncologic risks against the psychological distress and increased gender dysphoria associated with discontinuation [23, 24, 25].

An innovative approach from the UK Cancer and Transition Service (UCATS), integrates oncology and gender‐affirming care, supporting therapeutic safety and respect for gender identity. For patients with cardiovascular or thrombotic risk, transdermal formulations such as estradiol patches are preferable due to their more favorable safety profile compared to the oral route. Additionally, a 2025 report from the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer highlights that GAHT can affect pain perception, fatigue and toxicity management, emphasizing the need for continuous and individualized symptom treatment assessment [23].

Finally, systematic discontinuation of GAHT in cancer patients is not recommended. It should be assessed on a case‐by‐case basis, taking into account the type of tumor and the patient's preferences. Forced discontinuation may generate psychological distress, including increased gender dysphoria and risk of depression [23].

From a good clinical practice perspective, the systematic documentation of gender identity and GAHT in electronic health records, where available, together with medication history, type of hormone therapy, and duration of treatment, is a strategic objective for ensuring therapeutic safety and advancing high‐quality, personalized care. At the same time, in certain legislative contexts (e.g., in the US), such documentation may carry social and safety risks for transgender patients, underscoring the importance of safeguarding patient privacy and confidentiality to prevent potential misuse of sensitive health information.

Conclusions

Hormone therapy in transgender patients involves significant physiological changes that directly impact the parameters used to calculate drug dosages. In the oncology setting, these variations can significantly affect both the efficacy and safety of pharmacological treatments. In the absence of formal guidelines, an individualized approach based on up‐to‐date data, the duration of GAHT and gender identity becomes essential [25].

Clinical pharmacists, pharmacologists and oncologists as part of a multidisciplinary team, play a pivotal role in ensuring equitable, evidence‐based, and safe care for transgender patients.

Modern oncology must evolve toward a truly personalized care model, where gender identity is recognized as a clinically meaningful variable. Integrating inclusive care pathways, respecting gender identity in routine practice, and adopting adapted dosing protocols are critical to improving treatment adherence, safety, and therapeutic outcomes for transgender and gender‐diverse populations.
