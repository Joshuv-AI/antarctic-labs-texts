Temperament and Insight in Offender and Non-Offender Patients with Schizophrenia in Türkiye
Authors: Bengisu Çatlı, Işık Batuhan Çakmak, Şahin Gürkan, Elif Tatlıdil Yaylacı, Hasan Kaya, Erol Göka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Offending behavior in individuals with schizophrenia reflects complex interactions extending beyond psychotic symptoms alone. Affective temperament and insight, particularly insight related to treatment compliance, may be relevant to offending-related outcomes and recidivism. This study examined the relationships among affective temperament, insight, and offense-related judicial involvement in individuals with schizophrenia.Methods:This single-center, cross-sectional study included 103 adults diagnosed with schizophrenia, classified as offenders (n = 63) or non-offenders (n = 40). Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected, and participants were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Schedule for Assessing the Three Components of Insight (SAI), and the Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris, and San Diego Autoquestionnaire (TEMPS-A). Statistical analyses were conducted employing t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests for group comparisons, chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, and Spearman correlations.Results:Affective temperament profiles did not differ between offenders and non-offenders. Within the offending group, lower insight into treatment compliance was associated with recidivism. Irritable temperament showed weak negative associations with multiple insight dimensions and was related to greater symptom severity. Substance use and frequent hospitalizations were more common among individuals with repeated offending and were also associated with self-harm and suicide attempts.Conclusion:Temperament traits and global insight did not distinguish offenders from non-offenders with schizophrenia. Instead, reduced treatment-compliance insight emerged as a clinically relevant correlate of recidivism within the offending subgroup. These findings suggest that repeated offending in schizophrenia may be more closely related to dynamic, treatment-related, and behavioral factors than to stable affective temperament traits. Although causal inferences cannot be drawn, the results highlight the potential importance of adherence-focused clinical strategies in efforts to reduce recidivism.

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a chronic, severe psychiatric disorder defined by disturbances in thought, perception, and affect. Roughly 1% of the global population is estimated to have schizophrenia. Beyond its clinical manifestations, schizophrenia has significant forensic implications.1 While individuals with schizophrenia are more frequently victims of violence, forensic evaluations and registry studies have demonstrated that offenses committed by individuals with schizophrenia are not rare, with these individuals having a 2- to 8-fold increased risk of offending, particularly in the context of deinstitutionalization and inadequate social support.2

Temperament is conceptualized as biologically based individual differences in emotional reactivity and behavioral tendencies.3 Temperament characteristics are relatively stable across the lifespan and have been implicated in the predisposition to various psychiatric and behavioral outcomes. Hyperthymic, depressive, irritable, cyclothymic, and anxious temperaments are the 5 primary affective temperaments identified by Akiskal et al.4 Individuals with schizophrenia are shown to have higher levels of harm avoidance and self-transcendence, accompanied by lower reward dependence and self-directedness.5,6 In forensic populations, higher levels of novelty seeking and lower levels of cooperativeness have been associated with violent offending.2,7,8 However, findings on the relationship between temperament and offending behavior in schizophrenia remain heterogeneous, necessitating further investigation.

Insight is the awareness of having a mental disorder, recognition of its emerging symptoms, and the need for seeking treatment. Therefore, it is a critical determinant in the management of schizophrenia. It is closely related to adherence to the treatment and social functioning, serving as a significant prognostic indicator.6 While poor insight has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes and impaired functioning, its specific relationship with offending behavior in individuals with schizophrenia has not been conclusively established, despite its potential role in mediating risk through pathways involving treatment nonadherence and social disintegration.

In this context, the aim is to investigate the temperament profiles and insight levels of individuals with schizophrenia who have and have not committed offenses. Understanding how temperament and insight relate to offending behavior may help clarify individual vulnerability factors in schizophrenia and contribute to improving risk assessment strategies and management approaches in forensic psychiatric settings.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Ankara Numune Training and Research Hospital between February and August 2016. Ethics approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ankara Numune Training and Research Hospital (03.02.2016, approval no: E16-728), and all participants provided written informed consent.

The non-offending group consisted of patients with schizophrenia who presented consecutively to the Adult Psychiatry Outpatient Clinics for routine psychiatric consultations. These patients were included if they met the study’s inclusion criteria and provided informed consent. The absence of a criminal history in this group was verified through 3 converging sources: i) the hospital electronic medical record system, which contained no forensic entries or legal involvement; ii) the absence of any prior presentation to the Forensic Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic; and iii) patients’ self-report confirming no history of offending during the structured clinical interview.

The offending group consisted of patients with schizophrenia who presented consecutively to the Forensic Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of the same hospital for court-ordered forensic evaluations (e.g., behavioral stability and public order evaluation) and who arrived with official judicial documents issued by the referring court. For this group, the presence and type of offending history were determined based on i) the court documents brought by the patient at the time of evaluation; ii) existing entries in the hospital’s forensic outpatient records; and iii) verbal confirmation obtained during the clinical interview.

In this manuscript, “offending” refers to documented offense-related judicial involvement prompting forensic psychiatric evaluation, rather than a legal determination of guilt or criminal responsibility. No additional data were requested from external institutions such as courts, prosecutors’ offices, prisons, police databases, or the Ministry of Justice. Offense information was based exclusively on court-certified judicial documents presented at the time of forensic evaluation and the hospital’s internal forensic records.

A total of 150 consecutive patients diagnosed with schizophrenia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were i) a confirmed diagnosis of schizophrenia, ii) age between 18 and 65 years, iii) willingness to participate, and iv) absence of intellectual disability or psychiatric disorders secondary to medical conditions. After excluding 47 individuals who did not meet these criteria, 103 participants were included in the study.

Eligible patients were invited to participate by the research team. After providing written and verbal informed consent, participants completed a case report form developed by the investigators to collect sociodemographic and clinical data. Subsequently, all participants were evaluated by a clinician using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Schedule for Assessing the Three Components of Insight (SAI), and the Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris, and San Diego Autoquestionnaire (TEMPS-A).

Participants were categorized into two groups: those with a history of offending (n = 63) and those without an offending history (n = 40). All collected data were de-identified under the supervision of the principal investigator. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code, and anonymized datasets were stored on a secure, access-restricted institutional server.

Data Collection Instruments

Sociodemographic and Clinical Data Form: Developed by the researchers, this form helps to collect sociodemographic information, such as age, sex, marital status, years of education, household structure, and employment status. It also gathered information on clinical and lifestyle variables such as illness duration, number of hospitalizations, tobacco use, history of alcohol and substance use, family history of psychiatric disorders, history of self-harm or suicide attempts, and forensic history. The form was administered by the interviewers.

Recording Forensic History and Offense Profiles: Participants’ forensic histories were recorded as none, initial (1), or multiple offenses. For those with a history of offending, the types of offenses were obtained from hospital electronic databases of forensic case reports as well as from the court documents brought by the patient at the time of evaluation. The names and legal definitions of the offenses were recorded exactly as they appeared in Turkish Penal Code subheadings. These included homicide, assault, simple assault, non-injurious attack, threat, insult, sexual offenses, property damage, theft/robbery/extortion, arson, fraud, damage to public property, and trespassing.

Based on these offense categories, the first and the fourth authors (B.Ç., E.T.Y.) grouped all offenses into 3 major categories: homicide and assault as Major Violent Offenses; simple assault, non-injurious attack, threat, insult, and sexual offenses as Minor Violent Offenses; and property damage, theft/robbery/extortion, arson, fraud, damage to public property, and trespassing as Property-Related Offenses. These groupings were applied when transferring each participant’s offense history to the study’s structured data collection form.

Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale: The PANSS is an interview scale used to assess positive, negative, and general psychopathology symptoms developed for patients with schizophrenia. The scale consists of 30 items: 7 items for positive symptoms, 7 for negative symptoms, and 16 for general psychopathology. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale.9 Kostakoğlu et al. (1999) completed its Turkish validation.10 Higher scores on the PANSS indicate greater severity of psychopathology. In this study, the PANSS was used to evaluate the current symptoms and illness severity in the participants.

Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris, and San Diego-Autoquestionnaire: Developed by Akiskal et al. (2005), the TEMPS-A is a 99-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess dominant affective temperament. Participants are asked to respond “yes” or “no” to each item based on their lifetime experiences, with “yes” responses scored as 1 and “no” responses as 0. The questionnaire evaluates 5 affective temperament dimensions—depressive (comprising 18 questions), cyclothymic (19 questions), hyperthymic (20 questions), irritable (18 questions), and anxious (24 questions).11 Higher scores on TEMPS-A subscales indicate a stronger expression of the corresponding affective temperament dimension. Vahip et al. (2005) completed the validity and reliability study.12 Although the scale has primarily been used in individuals with mood disorders, it has also been applied in studies involving individuals with schizophrenia.13,14,15,16 For temperament scores evaluated with the TEMPS-A, since only a small proportion of participants exceeded the cutoff scores for the temperament categories, the scores were treated as continuous variables in the analyses.

Schedule for Assessing the Three Components of Insight: The SAI, originally developed by David et al17 in 1990, is a clinician-administered, semi-structured interview consisting of 8 questions. The instrument evaluates insight by combining the patient’s verbal responses with the clinician’s behavioral observations during the interview. The first 7 items constitute the core of the scale and yield a maximum score of 14 points, and the 8th item is an optional hypothetical question and is not included within the 3 core components of insight. In this study, this optional 8th item was not administered. Higher total scores reflect greater levels of insight.

The SAI assesses insight across 3 distinct but related dimensions: Treatment Compliance (“Does the patient accept treatment? Does the patient ask for treatment unprompted?” 0-4 points); Recognition of Mental Illness (Does the patient think he/she has an illness? Does the patient think he/she has a mental/psychiatric illness? How does the patient explain the illness?” 0-6 points); and Relabeling of Psychotic Phenomena (Does the patient think this belief is not really true? Does the patient think this hallucination is not really there? How does the patient explain these phenomena? 0-4 points). The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Arslan et al18 (2001), confirming its psychometric suitability for Turkish clinical populations.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and visual inspection of histograms. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables are summarized as median and minimum-maximum values [median (min-max)]. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed variables. Mann–Whitney U-tests were used for non-normally distributed variables, including comparisons of TEMPS-A, SAI, and PANSS scores between offending and non-offending groups, as well as between initial and multiple offense subgroups. Pearson’s chi-square test was used for categorical data; however, when more than 20% of expected cell frequencies were < 5, Fisher’s exact test was performed for 2 × 2 tables, and the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was applied for larger contingency tables. Correlation analyses were performed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient due to the nonparametric distribution of variables. The type 1 error rate was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses.

RESULTS

The sample had 103 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia (91 males, 12 females), including 63 with a history of offending and 40 without. The mean age of all participants was 38.9 ± 9.3 years, and the mean years of education was 10.5 ± 4.3 years. Patients who had a history of offending were older than those who did not (P = .046).

Additionally, a history of multiple hospitalizations was more prevalent among patients with a history of offending, whereas patients with no history of hospitalizations were more frequently found in the non-offending group (P = .004). The groups were similar regarding other sociodemographic variables, age at illness onset, illness duration (months), family history of psychiatric disorders, history of suicide attempts and self-harm, or smoking and substance use (Table 1).

Among the 63 patients with a history of offending, 45 reported experiencing delusions and 27 reported hallucinations at the time of the offense. Regarding offense types, 19 patients (30.2%) were involved in major violent offenses, 29 (46.0%) in minor violent offenses, and 26 (41.3%) in property-related offenses. Of these, 24 patients (38.1%) had a history of reoffending, with minor violent offenses (n = 15) being the most frequently repeated offense type (Table 2).

Among patients with substance use, reoffending was significantly more frequent (n = 7, 29.2%) compared to those without reoffending (n = 1, 2.6%) (P = .002). Furthermore, self-harming behavior was more common in substance users (n = 9, 58.3%) than in non-users (n = 3, 28.2%) (P = .018). No significant associations were found between the presence of delusions or hallucinations during the offense and substance use, number of offenses, or offense types.

In terms of insight, the groups with and without a history of offending had similar characteristics across all subcomponents of insight (Table 3). Insight scores also demonstrated similar distributions regarding gender, employment status, substance use, history of suicide attempts, and number of hospitalizations. No significant correlations were found between insight scores and variables such as age and years of education.

A weak positive correlation was found between illness duration and the “Awareness of Illness” subscale of insight (r = 0.215, P = .029). Among the offending group, patients who reported experiencing hallucinations during the offense had significantly higher scores in both the “Relabeling of Psychotic Experiences” subscale and the total insight score compared to those without hallucinations (P = .003 and P = .017, respectively).

Regarding treatment compliance as a component of insight, patients with a history of reoffending had lower scores compared to those with an initial offense (P = .035, Table 4).

Regarding illness severity, the PANSS score distributions were similar between the offending and non-offending groups (Table 3). PANSS scores were also similar in individuals with an initial offense and those with multiple offenses (Table 4).

Both groups exhibited similar temperament profiles. Additionally, temperament traits did not differ between individuals with an initial offense and those with multiple offenses (Tables 3 and 4).

Irritable temperament scores were significantly higher among patients with substance use compared to non-users (P = .040). Regarding self-harming behavior, patients with a history of self-harm had higher scores in depressive, cyclothymic, irritable, and anxious temperament compared to those without such a history (P = .032; P < .001; P < .001; and P = .001, respectively). Similarly, patients with a history of suicide attempts had significantly higher depressive temperament scores than those without such a history (P = .030).

Correlation analysis revealed several significant associations among temperament dimensions. Depressive temperament showed moderate to high positive correlations with cyclothymic (r = 0.738, P < .001), irritable (r = 0.494, P < .001), and anxious traits (r = 0.621, P < .001), and a moderate negative correlation with hyperthymic temperament (r = −0.313, P = .013). Cyclothymic temperament was strongly associated with irritable (r = 0.700, P < .001) and anxious traits (r = 0.727, P < .001). Irritable and anxious temperaments were also strongly correlated (r = 0.642, P < .001).

Correlation analysis between temperament and insight revealed weak but statistically significant negative associations between irritable temperament and multiple insight dimensions, including treatment compliance (r = −0.255, P = .044), recognition of illness (r = −0.283, P = .024), and the total insight score (r = −0.257, P = .042). No other temperament traits were significantly associated with insight (Table 5).

Irritable temperament showed a moderate positive correlation with PANSS positive scores (r = 0.410, P = .001). Anxious temperament was weakly associated with positive symptoms (r = 0.295, P = .019). Depressive and anxious temperaments demonstrated moderate correlations with PANSS negative scores (depressive: r = 0.399, P = .001; anxious: r = 0.316, P = .012). Regarding general psychopathology, depressive (r = 0.393, P = .001), cyclothymic (r = 0.367, P = .003), irritable (r = 0.441, P < .001), and anxious (r = 0.456, P < .001) temperaments were all moderately and significantly related to higher general symptom severity. Similarly, each of these traits—depressive (r = 0.434, P < .001), cyclothymic (r = 0.347, P = .005), irritable (r = 0.395, P = .001), and anxious (r = 0.439, P < .001)—was moderately associated with higher PANSS total scores (Table 5). PANSS scores and SAI scores were not significantly correlated in the offending group.

DISCUSSION

Offending behavior in schizophrenia has been associated with comorbid substance use, self-harming behavior, and suicide attempts.19,20,21,22,23 In particular, substance use disorder has repeatedly been identified as a significant correlate of violent and criminal behavior in schizophrenia.19,24 In line with this literature, the present study found that substance use was significantly associated with repeated offending. However, substance use did not differentiate individuals with and without an offense history in the overall sample. Given the legal consequences of substance use under the Turkish Penal Code, this finding may reflect underreporting despite assurances of confidentiality. Alternatively, it may indicate that substance use is more closely related to recidivism than to the occurrence of a single offense, underscoring the importance of distinguishing between offending and repeated offending in forensic research.

Frequent hospitalizations were more common among individuals with offense histories, consistent with previous reports.19,21,22,25 This pattern may reflect recurrent symptom exacerbations related to treatment nonadherence, substance use, or psychosocial instability rather than illness severity per se. Consistent with the findings of comparable PANSS scores across groups, differences emerged primarily in dynamic clinical markers such as hospitalization frequency, supporting a process-oriented interpretation in which fluctuating clinical factors may be more relevant to offense-related outcomes than cross-sectional symptom severity.

Previous studies have shown that lower levels of social and occupational functioning are associated with higher rates of recidivism and that repeated criminal involvement is related to prior offense history, offense patterns, and violence severity.25,26 Consistent with these findings, the present study observed that recidivism was most commonly characterized by minor violent acts and property-related crimes. This pattern parallels trends in the general population, where recidivism more frequently involves minor violence and property-related offenses rather than severe violent acts.27 Taken together, these findings suggest that while schizophrenia may contribute to vulnerability for criminal involvement, the nature of recidivism may reflect broader social and functional determinants, underscoring the importance of interventions targeting socioeconomic disadvantage and functional impairment to reduce repeated offending.

In the present study, affective temperament profiles assessed with the TEMPS-A did not differ between offenders and non-offenders with schizophrenia. This finding suggests that stable affective temperament traits, on their own, may not be sufficient to distinguish individuals based on offense history. While temperament has been implicated in aggressive or impulsive behaviors in other psychiatric conditions,28 similar trait-based mechanisms may play a more limited role in psychosis-related offending. In line with this view, Preti et al. reported little or no association between schizotypal features and TEMPS-A temperament scores, suggesting that affective dispositions may have restricted relevance for psychosis-related behavioral outcomes.29 It therefore appears likely that offending behavior in schizophrenia reflects the interaction of multiple clinical, social, and environmental factors rather than temperament alone. In addition, the relatively small and predominantly male sample may have limited variability in temperament profiles, potentially reducing sensitivity to detect group differences. Taken together, these considerations point to the need for multidimensional approaches to better understand factors associated with recidivism in schizophrenia.

Although the TEMPS-A was initially developed for mood disorders, evidence suggests that affective temperament dimensions may relate to clinically relevant features in schizophrenia, including symptom expression and insight.30,31 In the present study, irritable temperament was negatively associated with treatment compliance, recognition of illness, and total insight scores within the offending group, while depressive and anxious temperaments were associated with greater negative symptom severity. In addition, higher depressive, cyclothymic, irritable, and anxious temperament scores were observed among individuals with histories of self-harm or suicide attempts,2,16,32,33 and substance use was associated with elevated irritable temperament. The intercorrelations among temperament dimensions followed patterns consistently reported in the literature, with hyperthymic temperament appearing relatively independent from other affective dimensions.34 These findings suggest that while affective temperament profiles may be meaningfully related to clinical and behavioral characteristics in schizophrenia, they may have limited discriminative value for distinguishing recidivism-related outcomes.

A weak positive association between illness duration and awareness of illness suggests that prolonged clinical exposure may be related to greater recognition of having a psychiatric condition. In addition, individuals who reported hallucinations at the time of the offense showed higher relabeling of psychotic experiences and total insight scores. This pattern may reflect that hallucinations are more readily recognized as pathological in retrospect, facilitating symptom relabeling during later clinical evaluations. It is also possible that the forensic assessment context influences how patients frame their symptoms and behavior, which may contribute to higher observed insight scores in this subgroup.

Reduced insight has been associated with treatment noncompliance and poorer clinical outcomes in schizophrenia and has been implicated in pathways linked to violent behavior.35,36 Although insight is a robust predictor of clinical course and quality of life,37,38 it may not function as a primary determinant of offending behavior. Offense-related outcomes appear to be more strongly associated with factors such as substance use, antisocial traits, social and environmental adversity, limited access to treatment, and fluctuations in illness course.39,40 These considerations may help explain why overall insight scores did not differ between offenders and non-offenders in the present sample. In addition, insight was assessed during clinical evaluations rather than at the time of the offense, which may have obscured state-dependent variations and contributed to the similarity observed between groups.

In contrast, when examined within the offending group, individuals with multiple offenses demonstrated lower treatment-compliance insight than those with a single offense. This pattern suggests that insight differences may be more relevant to recidivism than to the occurrence of an initial offense. While first-time offenses may occur in the context of acute psychotic symptoms or situational stressors, repeated offending may be more closely associated with persistent treatment nonadherence and related clinical vulnerabilities. In line with this interpretation, irritable temperament was negatively associated with treatment compliance, recognition of illness, and total insight scores among offenders.41,42 Together, these findings support a distinction between global insight and treatment-related insight, with the latter showing greater relevance for repeated offending.

In the present study, PANSS scores did not differ between offenders and non-offenders, nor between individuals with initial versus repeated offending, indicating that cross-sectional symptom severity alone may have limited value in distinguishing offense-related outcomes. In contrast, individuals with fewer offenses demonstrated higher treatment-compliance insight, despite similar symptom burden, illness duration, hospitalization history, and age at illness onset. This pattern suggests that factors beyond core psychopathology may be more relevant to recidivism-related outcomes. Importantly, insight is a multidimensional construct encompassing illness recognition, symptom relabeling, and treatment compliance, and differences emerged specifically within the treatment-compliance dimension. This finding may indicate that treatment-related insight, rather than global insight deficits, is more closely associated with repeated offending. Given the established role of treatment adherence in reducing relapse and aggression in schizophrenia,35,36,41,42 this association should be interpreted as clinically relevant but correlational, within the limits of the cross-sectional design.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it systematically examined affective temperament, insight, and clinical characteristics in relation to both offending and recidivism within a well-characterized clinical sample of individuals with schizophrenia. By including both offenders and non-offenders and further differentiating between individuals with initial and repeated offending, the study was able to move beyond simple between-group comparisons and identify clinically meaningful within-group patterns.

Second, the focus on treatment compliance insight as a correlate of recidivism represents a clinically relevant and underexplored perspective in forensic psychiatry. Rather than relying solely on static symptom severity or global insight measures, the study highlights a specific, potentially modifiable dimension of insight that may have implications for risk management and intervention planning.

Finally, the use of culturally validated assessment instruments, detailed clinical characterization, and judicially documented offense information strengthens the internal consistency and contextual validity of the findings. Together, these strengths support the robustness of the study’s conclusions within the limits of its design.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the relatively small and predominantly male sample may have limited statistical power and generalizability, particularly for detecting subtle differences in stable trait measures such as affective temperament. Second, the significant age difference between offender and non-offender patients represents a limitation, as age may act as a potential confounder influencing clinical presentation and behavioral outcomes.

Third, although participants were instructed to report lifetime temperament characteristics when completing the TEMPS-A, recall bias and the potential influence of residual symptoms cannot be fully excluded.

Fourth, offense types were categorized using groupings derived from the Turkish Penal Code based on clinical and forensic judgment. While alternative classification systems may offer greater standardization, the absence of such frameworks in the present study may limit comparability across forensic research settings. Finally, insight was assessed during clinical or forensic evaluations rather than at the time of the offense, and therefore, reflects a later stage of illness or treatment engagement. This temporal gap limits inferences regarding the relationship between insight, temperament, and offense-related behavior and underscores the need for longitudinal designs that can more precisely capture these dynamics over time.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that affective temperament profiles, global insight, and cross-sectional symptom severity do not distinguish individuals with schizophrenia who have offense-related judicial involvement from those who do not. Instead, clinically meaningful differences emerged within the offending group, where reduced insight into treatment compliance was associated with repeated offending despite comparable symptom burden. These findings suggest that recidivism in schizophrenia may be more closely related to dynamic, treatment-related processes than to stable temperament traits or overall psychopathology. Although causal inferences cannot be drawn from this cross-sectional design, the results highlight treatment-compliance insight as a potentially relevant clinical target in efforts to reduce repeated offending among individuals with schizophrenia.
