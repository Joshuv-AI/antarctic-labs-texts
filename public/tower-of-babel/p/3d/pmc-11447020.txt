Mistuning perception in music is asymmetric and relies on both beats and inharmonicity
Authors: Sara M K Madsen, Andrew J Oxenham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn out-of-tune singer or instrument can ruin the enjoyment of music. However, there is disagreement on how we perceive mistuning in natural music settings. To address this question, we presented listeners with in-tune and out-of-tune passages of two-part music and manipulated the two primary candidate acoustic cues: beats (fluctuations caused by interactions between nearby frequency components) and inharmonicity (non-integer harmonic frequency relationships) across seven experiments (Exp 1: N = 101; Exp 2: N = 63; Exp 3a: N = 87; Exp 3b: N = 28; Exp 3c: N = 69; Exp 4: N = 160; Exp 5: N = 105). Mistuning detection worsened markedly when removing either beating or inharmonicity cues, suggesting important contributions from both. The relative importance of the two cues varied reliably between listeners but was unaffected by musical experience. Finally, a general asymmetry in sensitivity to mistuning was discovered, with compressed pitch differences being more easily detected than stretched ones, thereby demonstrating a generalization of the previously found stretched-octave effect. Overall, the results reveal the acoustic underpinnings of the critical perceptual phenomenon of dissonance through mistuning in natural music.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Perception

Introduction

Many people have a visceral reaction to music that is out of tune, cringing in response to karaoke sung flat. But how do we tell if one voice is out of tune with another? Surprisingly, there is no consensus on this question. When tuning their instrument prior to playing, musicians often use slow beats – the acoustic fluctuations produced between two tones that are very close but not identical in frequency (Fig. 1a, e, f)1–3. For many years, more rapid beats, inducing the perception of roughness, were thought to underlie the distinction between consonance and dissonance (pleasantness/unpleasantness or resolution/tension) in music4,5, with neural correlates of beats having been observed in auditory cortical recordings from humans and non-human primates6. More recent work has shown instead that consonance judgments of isolated dyads and triads (two and three simultaneous tones, respectively) are based not on beats but on their inharmonicity – the extent to which the harmonics in the tone combination deviate from a single harmonic series (Fig. 1b–d) – although such judgments depend on both musical experience and enculturation7–9. These studies made use of the fact that the relationship between harmonics in some tone combinations (like the fifth, a frequency ratio of 3:2) are more harmonic (i.e., approximate integer multiples of a single fundamental frequency, F0) than others (like the augmented fourth, a frequency ratio of √2). A limiting factor of most previous studies of both beats and inharmonicity is that they have relied primarily on isolated tones, stripped of their musical context, making it unclear if those studies’ conclusions generalize to perception within a more realistic musical context.

It is also not clear whether the factors governing the relative consonance and dissonance of different musical intervals are the same as those governing the perception of mistuning between instruments. The beats and inharmonicity, generated when two instruments play a normally consonant dyad (e.g., octave or fifth) out of tune, could underlie our perception of mistuning in actual music, but it is unclear whether both contribute or whether our perception is dominated by one. Similarly, it is unclear whether the relative importance of these two cues varies with musical experience, as has been found for isolated tones in studies of consonance and dissonance7,8.

To address these questions, we presented online participants with excerpts from J. S. Bach’s Two-Part Inventions. These short keyboard pieces are written in two parts, with two notes usually present at any one time. We varied the degree of mistuning between the lower and upper parts and manipulated the tones to control the extent to which beats or inharmonicity cues were available. Our four main questions were: 1) What cues do we use to detect mistuning in musical passages? 2) Does musical experience influence how we detect mistuning? 3) Are we differentially adept at detecting mistuning that is flat (i.e., compressed musical intervals) or sharp (i.e., expanded musical intervals)? 4) Are some musical intervals (such as an octave or fifth) used more than others within musical passages to detect mistuning?

Methods

Participant recruitment and screening

The experiments were conducted online, with participants recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and the University of Minnesota (UMN). Only participants who reported having normal hearing and no neurological diagnoses or tinnitus were included. To test for the integrity of the audio equipment used by the online participants, we included at least one headphone check for all participants10, with an additional headphone check11 for experiments 2 and 4. A screening task using the training (experiment 1-4) or test (experiment 5) mistuning stimuli from the experiments was then run to ensure that participants were able to complete the task. To pass the mistuning screening prior to each experiment, the participants had to answer correctly on at least seven of ten trials. No feedback was provided during screening. To test for continued engagement during the main experiments, 10 catch trials were included in each of experiments 1-4 and 20 catch trials were included in experiment 5. In experiments 1-4, the catch trial stimuli were the same excerpts as those used for the main task but mistuned by a larger amount of −0.8 semitones (ST). The catch trial sequences for experiment 5 were the mistuned test stimuli sequences played in either octaves or fifths. Only participants judging at least 80% of the catch trials as being mistuned and performing above chance level (defined as the 95% confidence intervals based on Monte Carlo simulations) in at least one of the main conditions were included in the analysis. Participants who reported that bad sound quality affected their ability to do the experiment were also excluded from the analysis. All participants completed a questionnaire with demographic questions including age, years of musical experience, gender, ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino or not Hispanic or Latino), and race (American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, Black or African American, White, more than one) and an online consent form. They received extra course credit (UMN participants) or hourly compensation (MTurk) for their participation. All experiments were approved by the University of Minnesota’s Institutional Review Board.

General procedure

All experiments used a repeated-measures design. In each trial, we presented two-part musical segments with the parts either in tune or out of tune with each other, being stretched or compressed by 0.3 or 0.5 semitones (ST), equivalent to 30 or 50 cents or a mistuning of around 1.8% or 2.9%, respectively. After each trial, the participants were asked if the musical sequence was in-tune or out-of-tune. They had to pick one of the response options “sure in-tune”, “maybe in-tune”, “maybe out-of-tune”, or “sure out-of-tune” before they could continue to the next trial. No time restrictions were placed on listeners’ responses, and no feedback was provided for any of the experiments. The main mistuning experiments were divided into two blocks in experiments 1-4 and into three blocks in experiment 5, each containing one example of each musical excerpt in each condition. The order of the excerpts (trials) was randomized within each block, independently for each participant. The first ten trials of each experiment, which were treated as practice and not analyzed, included examples of any stimulus conditions that had not been included in the screening, to ensure that all participants had been exposed to all conditions before the actual test. We used the standard (A = 440 Hz) tuning with equal temperament, so that all tones in the in-tune conditions were separated from each other by a whole number of semitones, where 1 ST, or 100 cents, corresponds to a change in F0 by a factor of 2±1/12. Mistuning was always achieved by raising or lowering the pitch of the upper part. The stimuli were either different types of synthetic tones generated in Matlab (Mathworks, Natick, MA) or piano and marimba sounds produced using GarageBand (Apple, Cupertino, CA). The components of the synthetic tones were always added in sine phase and had an exponential temporal decay (i.e. the tones were multiplied with e−λt, where λ was a decay rate of 2.5 s−1). Finally, all tones generated in Matlab had 20-ms raised-cosine ramps imposed at the beginning and end of their nominal duration to avoid any temporal overlap between successive tones within the same part. To make the marimba and piano sequences, the entire sequence was generated separately for each part in GarageBand. Later, the high part was resampled if mistuned and the two parts were combined in Matlab. The beginning and end of each sequence (not each tone) was gated with 20 ms raised-cosine ramps. There was no preregistration of this study. The experiment was set up using HTML and JavaScript.

Musical sequences

Ten excerpts from J. S. Bach’s Two-part Inventions, each of 4-5 seconds in duration, were used as stimuli in experiments 1-4 (pages 2-4 in Supplementary Information and audio examples S1–S8). In experiment 5, the stimuli were ten 6-tone rising or falling sequences with two parts where all tones had a duration of 400 ms (page 5 in Supplementary Information and audio examples S9 and S10). All tones in a sequence belonged to the same major scale. In the in-tune conditions of experiment 5, the two parts were separated by a perfect fifth (7 ST) or by minor and major sixths (8 and 9 ST).

Statistics and data analysis

Performance was quantified as sensitivity or d’, a measure used in signal detection theory12 to estimate the ability to distinguish between signal and noise (here mistuned and in-tune music sequences). The value of d’ was calculated as: d′=2⋅qnormAUC, where qnorm is the inverse of the normal cumulative distribution function, and AUC is the area under the receiver operating curve (ROC). The AUC was estimated using the Matlab trapz function on false alarm and hit rates for the most liberal (all responses count as being mistuned) to the most conservative criteria (all responses count as being in-tune). To avoid infinite d’ values, the false alarm and hit rates were corrected using the loglinear approach13 before they were converted to d’. Statistical analysis was performed by fitting a mixed-effects linear model to the d’ values calculated from the mistuning ratings. Processing condition, mistuning direction (compressed or stretched), and mistuning size (experiment 5) were included as fixed effects and participants were treated as random intercept and slope. The data from the two groups of online participants (UMN and MTurk) were pooled, since the main experimental trends were the same (see Fig. S5 and Tables S3–S8 in Supplementary Information) and group was not included as a factor in the main statistical analysis. The models were implemented in R14 using the lme4 package15. Model selection was conducted with the lmerTest package16 following the backward selection approach based on stepwise deletion of model terms with high p-values. The models used can be seen in Table S2. All interactions of fixed terms were included in the final model to be able to also report on nonsignificant interactions. The p-values for the fixed effects were calculated from F tests (Satterthwaite’s approximation of denominator degrees of freedom) while the p-values for the random effects were based on likelihood ratio tests17. Multiple comparisons were conducted with the emmeans package18 and effect sizes were estimated with the effectsize package19. All paired comparisons, confidence intervals (CI), and correlations were two-tailed. In all studies, visual inspection suggested the distribution of the residuals to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Bayes factors (BF) were calculated for null results using the brms package20 for the main analysis with default priors, 4 chains each with 10,000 iterations, including 5000 warm up samples, and the BayesFactor package21 with a default prior for multiple comparisons and correlations. BF values less than 1 provide evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, with the evidence becoming stronger as the BF approaches zero.

Sample size determination

The sample size needed to detect an interaction between test condition and mistuning direction (and mistuning size for experiment 5) with a power of 0.8 was found using the powerCurve function in the R-package simr22 to compare mixed-effects models with and without interactions. The models were generated using pilot data from 10 participants for experiment 1, 4, and 5 and using data from 30, 41, and 28, participants from earlier versions of experiments 2, 3a, and 3b. The power was found as the proportion of simulations with a significant difference between the models with and without interactions and was based for each experiment on 10,000 simulations using the Kenward-Roger approximation. We found that we needed 39 participants for experiment 1, 21 participants for experiment 2, 7 participants for experiment 3a, 5 participants for experiment 3b, 7 participants for experiment 4, and 11 participants for experiment 5. The sample size for experiment 3c was based on the sample size calculation for experiment 3a. We used these numbers as a minimum sample size but generally tried to test more to strengthen the validity of our conclusions and counter the higher potential unpredictability of online studies.

Experiment 1

All tones were synthesized to contain the first 15 harmonic components except in the filtered conditions, where tones in the low part only contained frequency components below 1150 Hz and the tones in the high part only contained components above 1150 Hz. The harmonics in the pass band all had equal amplitude to maximize the amplitudes of any beats between components. All tones were scaled to have the same overall root-mean-squared (RMS) level before onset and offset ramps were applied. This level was 3 dB higher in the dichotic condition than in the other conditions to limit loudness differences between conditions. In the condition involving interaural time differences (ITDs), an ITD was introduced by delaying the first part in one ear by 600 μs and delaying the second part by 600 μs in the other ear. This led to the perception of one part in one ear and the other part in the other ear, even though the sound intensity of each part was equal in both ears.

The experiment consisted of two blocks, each consisting of 120 trials [3 mistunings (−0.5 ST, 0 ST, and 0.5 ST) x 4 processing conditions (unprocessed, dichotic, filtered, ITD) x 10 music sequences], excluding training and catch trials, and had an approximate duration of 40 minutes.

Data from 101 participants were included in the analysis. Experiment 4

This experiment used GarageBand piano sounds for the stimuli. Because mistuning detection for piano stimuli was near or at ceiling for many participants, we used a smaller mistuning size of ±0.3 ST, or ±30 cents. As in experiment 2, the stimuli in the dichotic ‘limited beats’ conditions were presented at a level 3 dB higher than that in the other conditions. In the chimera ‘limited inharmonicity’ condition, the mistuned temporal envelope from each of the 24 frequency sub-bands was combined with the in-tune temporal fine structure from the same sub-band to preserve envelope beating cues while limiting inharmonicity between frequency components23. The in-tune stimuli were also processed in the same manner by combining the original in-tune temporal fine structure within each sub-band with the temporal envelope of a transposed version of the in-tune stimuli, where both parts had been shifted upwards by 0.3 ST. By processing both the in-tune and out-of-tune stimuli in the same way, we were able to manipulate the availability of cues, while limiting the informativeness of any potential artifacts due to the chimera processing.

We used a 24-channel chimerizer23 spanning the frequency range from 80 Hz to 17,640 Hz. Twenty-four channels were selected to approximate the effective frequency selectivity found in humans with normal hearing24. To check the output of the chimerizer, we analyzed chimerized and unprocessed dyads with a 256-band gammatone filterbank, with overlapping bandpass regions and bandwidths taken from estimates of human auditory tuning25, to simulate the resolution of the human auditory periphery. Examples of the plots from such analysis are shown in Figs. S1−S4. The power spectra for each headphone channel were calculated as the sum of the power spectra of the output of each of the 256 analysis filters and then averaged across the two headphone channels. Similarly, the modulation envelope spectra for each headphone channel were calculated as the sum of the spectra of the temporal envelopes extracted from each of the 256 filters and then averaged across headphone channels.

This experiment consisted of two blocks, each consisting of 90 trials [3 mistunings (−0.3 ST, 0 ST, and 0.3 ST) x 3 processing (unprocessed, chimera, dichotic) x 10 music examples], excluding training and catch trials, and had an approximate duration of 30 min.

Of these, 16 UMN and 24 MTurk participants reported having no musical experience and seven reported having absolute pitch. Results

Experiment 1: Mistuning detection is asymmetric and dependent on beats

To determine the importance of beats for mistuning detection, we limited access to beats by minimizing the interactions between harmonic components from the two parts, either via filtering (low-pass and high-pass filtering the low and high parts, respectively) or via dichotic presentation (one part to one ear and the other part to the opposite ear) of the synthesized complex tones (Fig. 2a). Dichotic presentation greatly reduces perceived beats26, but leaves pitch and harmonicity judgments intact27,28. Similarly, presenting simultaneous harmonic information in different spectral regions has little effect on listeners’ ability to detect differences in pitch29,30. However, filtering and dichotic presentation make the two parts sound more different from each other, which might affect listeners’ ability to fuse them. To control for potential effects of perceptual fusion, we included a condition with opposite ITDs applied to the two parts. As ITDs are known to be the dominant cue for sound localization of broadband sounds31, differences in ITD between the parts should lead to similar loss of fusion as dichotic presentation, but without removing the beating cues, as both parts are presented to both ears.

Comparing the unprocessed, filtered, and dichotic conditions (Fig. 2b) revealed a significant main effect (F3,100 = 123.32, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.78, 95% CI = [0.70, 0.83]) with Bonferroni corrected post-hoc tests (α = 0.05/6 = 0.0083) confirming that performance was significantly better in the unprocessed condition than in either the dichotic condition (t100 = 12.82, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.28, 95% CI = [1.02, 1.54]), or filtered condition (t100 = 17.35, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.73, 95% CI = [1.42, 2.04]). This pattern of results is consistent with mistuning detection being strongly dependent on the presence of beats. There was no significant difference in mistuning detection scores between the unprocessed and the ITD conditions (t100 = −1.64, p = 0.104, Cohen’s d = −0.16, 95% CI = [−0.36, 0.03]; BF = 0.62). The results therefore provide no evidence that performance was affected by perceptual fusion, independent of access to beating cues. Performance was better in dichotic than the filtered condition, (t100 = 6.28, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.63, 95% CI = [0.41, 0.84]). The greater effect of filtering than dichotic presentation might be explained by access to more beating cues in the dichotic condition for these online participants, whose two headphone channels may not have been fully separated, or by timbre differences between the two parts caused by filtering perhaps leading to poorer relative pitch judgements and hence worse mistuning detection32,33.

The results also show a marked asymmetry in performance (Fig. 2b), with mistuning detection being significantly better for compressed (−0.5 ST) than for stretched (0.5 ST) pitch differences between the parts (F1,100 = 228.87, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.69, 95% CI = [0.59, 0.76]). One interpretation of this result is that the two parts are judged to be most in tune when the musical intervals between the two parts are somewhat stretched (somewhere between 0 and 0.5 ST), reminiscent of the ‘stretched octave’ phenomenon25,34–38. This asymmetry was most pronounced in conditions where beats were reduced, as confirmed by the significant interaction between processing condition and mistuning direction (F3,300 = 29.63, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.22, 95% CI = [0.14, 0.29]).

Experiment 2: Mistuning detection improves systematically with increasing beat strength

To investigate the relationship between beat strength and mistuning detection, we systematically varied the interaural level differences (ILDs) of the two parts in opposite directions, thereby introducing an equal but opposite difference in level between the parts in each ear (Fig. 2c). The strength of beats is greatest when the components producing the beats have the same level and decreases with increasing level difference between the components. Therefore, if mistuning detection depends on the detection of beats, then performance should worsen systematically with increasing ILD.

The results (Fig. 2d) show that mistuning detection performance indeed worsens systematically with increasing ILD (F3,62 = 56.97, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.72, 95% CI = [0.59, 0.79]), indicating a strong relationship between mistuning detecting ability and the strength of beats. Performance was again better for the compressed than for the stretched mistuning (F1,62 = 94.44, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.59, 95% CI = [0.44, 0.70]), an effect that increased with increasing level difference, as evidenced by the significant interaction between level difference and mistuning direction (F3,186 = 3.25, p = 0.023, ωp2 = 0.03, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.09]).

Experiment 3: Mistuning perception with more realistic instrumental sounds is also asymmetric and dependent on beats

This experiment tested the same musical excerpts, but now played with more natural musical sounds, namely marimba and grand piano. The marimba has a very sparse spectrum, with most energy at the F0 and little energy at upper harmonic frequencies (Fig. 3a), limiting access to beats between harmonics of the two parts. In contrast, the piano has many strong upper harmonics that can interact and generate beats if mistuned. If beats are important for detecting mistuning, then performance should be better for the piano than the marimba tones. Similarly, when the piano is in the low part, its upper harmonics could interact with the marimba, potentially producing audible beats, whereas when the marimba is in the low part, beats between it and the piano are less likely.

a Mean spectra across all tones in the range used in this study as a function of harmonic rank (frequency/F0 where F0 is an exact number of ST from A4 = 440). b Results from experiment 3a (N = 87) with combinations of the piano and marimba as the low and high part. c Results from experiment 3c (N = 69), showing that results are similar for pure tones when the marimba plays both parts. d Results from experiment 3b (N = 28), where the original conditions were compared with conditions in which the marimba tuning was corrected to better match that of the piano.

Experiment 3a investigated conditions in which the same instrument played both parts, as well as conditions in which the two parts were played by different instruments. Experiment 3b was a follow-up, designed to test an unexpected outcome in Experiment 3a. Experiment 3c provided a control condition using pure tones, as a less natural version of the sparse spectrum provided by the marimba.

As predicted, performance was markedly better when both parts were played by piano than when both parts were played by marimba (Fig. 3b). The effect of instrument type was significant (F3,86 = 377.61, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.93, 95% CI = [0.90, 0.94]), as was the difference between each combination of instrument type (Table S1).

As in earlier experiments, performance was better for the compressed than the stretched conditions, except for the combined marimba/piano condition, where the asymmetry appears to be reversed. Confirming this pattern was a significant effect of mistuning direction (F1,344 = 84.46, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.19, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.27]) and a significant interaction between mistuning direction and instrument type (F3,344 = 68.24, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.37, 95% CI = [0.29, 0.43]). A close analysis of the sound samples that we generated in GarageBand showed that the average F0 of the marimba was 0.13 ST higher than that of the piano (see Fig. S6), potentially explaining the apparent reversal of the asymmetry when the marimba was the higher part.

This explanation was supported by the results from experiment 3b, in which the original conditions were retested, along with conditions where the tuning of the two instruments was equalized by shifting the marimba downwards by 0.13 ST. The results showed that equalizing the tuning was sufficient to restore the expected direction of the asymmetry (Fig. 3d), as confirmed by a significant effect of tuning (F1,27 = 5.37, p = 0.028, ωp2 = 0.13, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.38]), and interactions between instruments, mistuning direction, and tuning (F1,135 = 59.35, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.30, 95% CI = [0.18, 0.41]). While the conditions with original tuning showed a significant interaction between instruments and mistuning direction (F1,54 = 91.53, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.62, 95% CI = [0.45, 0.73]), confirming replication of the data from experiment 3a, the conditions with the corrected tuning showed no interaction between mistuning direction and mix of instruments (F1,54 = 0.92, p = 0.34, ωp2 = 0.00, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.00]; BF = 0.45). Moreover, an analysis of the conditions with the corrected tuning showed that the slightly better performance when the piano played the low part than when the marimba played the low part is significant (F1,27 = 5.91, p = 0.022, ωp2 = 0.14, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.39]). This pattern would be expected based on the predicted effects of beats: the piano has more upper harmonics, which will interact with the marimba when the piano is the low part; in contrast, when the marimba is the low part, its lack of upper harmonics implies less interaction with the piano part.

The poorer performance with the marimba found in experiment 3a could be due to the reduction in beating cues, as initially hypothesized, but may also be influenced by the inharmonic nature of the (low-level) inharmonic transients at the onset of the sound. To test this alternative hypothesis, experiment 3c measured performance with synthetic pure tones, which maintain a sparse spectrum but have no inharmonic components. We found that results were slightly but not significantly worse for pure tones (Fig. 3c) than when the marimba played both parts (Fig. 3b) (−0.5 ST: t152 = 1.99, p =0.049, Cohen’s d = 0.32, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.64], BF = 1; 0.5 ST: t152 = 1.71, p = 0.089, Cohen’s d = 0.28, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.60], BF = 0.63; αBonferroni = 0.05/2 = 0.025). This outcome provides no evidence to support the hypothesis that the inharmonic nature of the marimba, rather than its sparse spectrum, affected performance.

Experiment 4: Beats and inharmonicity are both important for mistuning detection and their relative importance does not depend on musical experience

The previous experiments limited listeners’ access to beating cues. Here we attempted to limit access to inharmonicity cues. This was accomplished by creating auditory chimeras23, in which the temporal envelope from a mistuned passage was combined with the temporal fine structure from an in-tune passage. To the extent that the chimera successfully separates the envelope beating cues from the fine-structure inharmonicity cues (as verified in Figs. S1–S4), we should be able to identify any dependence of performance on inharmonicity. We tested a larger number of participants in this experiment to examine any potential relationships between musical experience and performance on these tasks.

The results show markedly better performance when preserving both cues (unprocessed) than when limiting cues from either inharmonicity (chimera condition) or beats (dichotic condition) (Fig. 4a). There was a significant effect of processing condition (F2,159 = 535.68, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.87, 95% CI = [0.83, 0.89]), and performance was significantly different between all conditions: (unprocessed vs chimera: t159 = 30.22, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.40, 95% CI = [2.09, 2.70]; unprocessed vs dichotic: t159 = 24.07, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.91, 95% CI = [1.65, 2.17]; chimera vs dichotic: t159 = −4.23, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.34, 95% CI = [−0.49, −0.17]; αBonferroni = 0.05/3 = 0.0167). The much better performance in the unprocessed than processed conditions shows that mistuning detection is influenced by both beats and inharmonicity cues. However, the above-chance performance of many participants in both the chimera and dichotic conditions suggests that it is still possible to detect mistuning when predominantly having access to just one of the cues.

a Results (N = 160) showing worsened performance when limiting inharmonicity cues (chimera) and beating cues (dichotic). b Correlation between the mean d’ value across the compressed (−0.3 ST) and stretched conditions (0.3 ST) and years of musical experience for the unprocessed (top), chimera (middle), dichotic conditions (bottom) are all highly significant. c Differences in d’ between the chimera and dichotic conditions for the compressed condition (x-axis) and stretched condition (y-axis) are significantly correlated indicating that the relative performance of the participants in these two conditions are consistent, i.e. some perform better with limited beating cues than with limited inharmonicity cues in both conditions and vice versa. d Differences in d’ are not correlated to musical experience. Bonferroni corrected α = 0.05/5 = 0.01.

The effect of mistuning direction (F1,477 = 40.70, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.08, 95% CI = [0.04, 0.13]) and the interaction between processing condition and mistuning direction (F2,477 = 111.88, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.32, 95% CI = [0.25, 0.38]) were also significant. As in the previous experiment, performance was better in the compressed than in the stretched conditions in the unprocessed and dichotic conditions. However, in the chimera condition, performance was not better in the compressed than in the stretched condition. This last finding is consistent with expectations, assuming the asymmetry is primarily observed in conditions where inharmonicity cues are used: the chimera condition was designed to reduce access to inharmonicity cues, and so may have led to an elimination of the compressed-interval advantage.

The number of years of musical experience was significantly correlated with performance in both the compressed and the stretched conditions for each of the processing conditions (Fig. 4b), showing that musical experience enhances the sensitivity to mistuning in music. This was true for the unprocessed condition (Fig. 4b, top), as well as for the conditions that reduced either inharmonicity or beating cues (Fig. 4b, middle and bottom, respectively).

To explore whether individual participants rely more on beating or inharmonicity cues and whether this is consistent across conditions, the estimates of performance, in terms of d’, for conditions with limited beats (dichotic) were subtracted from the d’ values for the conditions with limited inharmonicity cues (chimera) for each participant and compared for the stretched and compressed conditions. The significant correlation found for the difference scores between the stretched and compressed conditions (Fig. 4c) suggests a consistent tendency for some participants to place more weight on one cue than another. However, this weighting was not dependent on musical experience, as indicated by the lack of correlation between the d’ difference and years of musical experience (Fig. 4d; BF = 0.19).

Experiment 5: Mistuning detection and effect of mistuning direction vary across musical intervals

The asymmetry observed across experiments in detecting mistuning within musical passages is reminiscent of the stretched octave phenomenon, whereby an interval slightly larger than an octave is often judged to be most like an octave25,35–37,39. Attempts to extend this finding to musical intervals other than an octave have led to mixed results40–42. To determine whether our observed perceptual stretch was shared by musical intervals other than an octave, we tested mistuning perception for sequences in which the lower and upper parts were separated by an interval of either a fifth (7 ST) or a sixth (minor sixth: 8 ST; major sixth 9 ST). The fifth is a harmonic interval, close to a simple ratio of 3:2, whose aggregate components approximate a single harmonic series, whereas the major and minor sixth represent less simple ratios, with harmonic spacing that is less similar to a single harmonic series (Fig. 5a, b). Because the difference is greater between in-tune and out-of-tune fifths than minor and major sixths (both in terms of harmonicity and beats), it seemed likely that mistuning detection would be easier for fifths than for sixths. Our question was whether the greater sensitivity for compressed over stretched intervals would extend to either or both types of interval.

Stimuli and results for experiment 5.

a, b Frequency spectra (top) and time signals (bottom) of perfect fifths and major sixths. The harmonics of the fifth but not the sixths approximate a harmonics series. There is therefore a larger difference between the in-tune and mistuned fifths than between in-tune and mistuned sixths both in terms of regularity of spacing of spectral components (inharmonicity) and smoothness of temporal envelope (beats). c Examples of the tone sequences used as stimuli. d Results (N = 105) document better mistuning detection performance for the fifths and mistuning asymmetry for the fifths but not the sixths.

Mistuning detection performance was better for the fifths than for the sixths (Fig. 5d; F1,63 = 52.69, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.44, 95% CI = [0.26, 0.58]). Moreover, there was an interaction between interval type and mistuning direction (F1,671 = 22.99, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.03, 95% CI = [0.01, 0.06]), reflecting the observation that the difference between the compressed and stretched conditions was smaller for the sixths than for the fifths (Fig. 5d). In fact, Bonferroni-corrected multiple comparisons (α = 0.05/4 = 0.0125) showed that the effect of mistuning direction was significant for the fifths (0.3 ST: t684 = 6.14, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.23, 95% CI = [0.16, 0.31]; 0.5 ST: t684 = 3.92, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.15, 95% CI = [0.07, 0.23]), but not for the sixths (0.3 ST: t684 = −1.67, p = 0.095, Cohen’s d = −0.06, 95% CI = [−0.14, 0.01], BF = 0.03; 0.5 ST: t684 = 2.15, p = 0.032, Cohen’s d = 0.08, 95% CI = [0.01, 0.16], BF = 0.04). These results demonstrate that the ability to detect mistuning varies across intervals and that mistuning asymmetry generalizes to some, but not all, musical intervals beyond the octave. As expected, mistuning detection performance was better for the larger (0.5 ST) than the smaller (0.3 ST) mistuning size (F1,671 = 231.44, p < 0.001, ωp2 = 0.26, 95% CI = [0.20, 0.31]).

Discussion

Our study revealed that both beats and inharmonicity are important for detecting mistuning between parts in musical passages, but that the task can be achieved with just one of the cues fully present. The importance of beats was demonstrated by 1) filtering the low and high parts into different frequency regions, 2) presenting the low and high parts to opposite ears, 3) gradually changing the relative level of the parts in opposite directions in each ear, and 4) using pure tones or natural musical tones with little energy in the upper harmonics (marimba), all of which resulted in poorer mistuning detection. The possibility that the decrease in performance was due to a reduction in perceptual fusion between the two parts was ruled out by showing that 1) reducing fusion but maintaining beats, by lateralizing the low and high parts to opposite ears using ITDs, did not worsen performance, and 2) using pure tones or natural musical sounds with reduced beats (marimba) also resulted in poorer performance, despite full fusion. Thus, multiple lines of evidence support the hypothesis that beats are used to detect mistuning between parts in musical passages. The importance of inharmonicity was shown using a condition that attempted to maintain the cues from the beats while reducing inharmonicity cues; that condition also showed a decrease in performance, similar in size to the effect of loss of beats.

Although beats have been identified as being important for detecting mistuning between isolated tones1,3,5,6,43–45, their role in the context of ongoing music has not previously been fully examined. One early study used two parts but introduced mistuning within each part, concluding that melodic mistuning of successive intervals was more important than the harmonic mistuning between simultaneous tones40. However, because mistuning was present within each part, it was not possible to isolate the perceptual effects of mistuning perception between the two parts. Note that our definition of beats includes more rapid amplitude fluctuations than the slow beats typically associated with tuning a musical instrument. Humans are very sensitive to amplitude fluctuations up to at least 150 Hz46, meaning that even small changes in the depth or extent of these fluctuations, due to mistuning, may be used to detect mistuning, even if listeners are not actively aware of the phenomenon. Beats at rates beyond about 10–12 Hz are not heard as individual fluctuations, but rather as ‘roughness’47, which may be the cue listeners can use to detect mistuning in these cases.

As expected, musical experience was associated with better performance in the task48–51. Unexpectedly, however, the benefits of musical experience extended to sensitivity to both beats and inharmonicity, and there was no indication that musical experience led to more reliance on one over the other, despite significant individual differences in the weighting of the two cues. Taken at face value, the lack of association between musical experience and preferential use of inharmonicity cues seems to contradict work on consonance and dissonance perception of isolated in-tune chords, which found inharmonicity to be dominant and positively related to both musical experience7 and exposure to Western music more generally8. It may be that the difference between the subjective judgments of consonance and dissonance, in contrast to the more objective performance-based measures of mistuning detection, underlie this apparent discrepancy, along with the difference between the use of isolated tones in all previous studies and the more representative musical passages in the present one.

Another primary finding of this study is that mistuning detection performance is better when compressing the pitch difference between parts than when stretching it. Such mistuning asymmetry has only previously been documented for octave intervals presented in isolation34,36,38,52,53. The finding provides additional support for the hypothesis that mistuning perception is not based on beats alone, as beating cues are roughly symmetric for small mistunings. It also suggests that the pitch differences between two concurrent parts may need to be slightly stretched for them to be perceived as being completely in-tune with each other. Interestingly, when only one musical interval size was used in a passage, we found the asymmetry for sequences of musical fifths but not sixths. The finding of better mistuning detection and stronger asymmetry for the fifths than sixths may be explained by the fact that the harmonics of the fifth, like the octave, approximate a harmonic series leading to harmonic fusion when they are in tune. This is not the case for major or minor sixths, which are less harmonically related even in their in-tune state. The poorer mistuning detection of the sixths might also in part be explained by their definitions being slightly different in different tuning systems, perhaps making the template, or expected value, of an in-tune sixth less clear. The conclusion that only some interval types are perceptually stretched makes it noteworthy that we consistently found mistuning asymmetry for music excerpts containing many different intervals. It may therefore be that some intervals (such as the octave and fifth) receive more weight than others when judgments of mistuning within music are made.

Several theories have been proposed to explain the asymmetry in mistuning perception, but so far only for octaves. One family of theories uses physiological properties of the early auditory system, such as the refractory properties of auditory nerve fibers54,55, or different time constants in excitatory and inhibitory auditory brainstem circuits56. Another family of theories has focused on more central, place-based representations of harmonic complex tones, taking masking effects into account57. A third family of theories has postulated that the stretched octave may reflect the properties of some natural sounds, such as the stretched harmonics that are typically found in pianos58. It has also been proposed that the stretched octave can be explained by the principles of Pythagorean tuning59. Our finding that the stretch generalizes to some, but not all, intervals beyond the octave will help to constrain such models. For instance, the fact that listeners tend to perceive a stretched fifth as well as an octave seems to contradict the recent theory based on Pythagorean tuning59, as that theory would predict veridical perception of the fifth, upon which the entire tuning system is based (see reference56 for further objections to that Pythagorean theory).

Limitations

One limitation of this study is that our conclusions only apply to Western music and listeners, since the concept of dissonance and mistuning does not apply to all musical systems8,60,61. Another limitation of the study is the use of chimeric signal processing to reduce inharmoncity cues23. Although we analyzed the output to confirm limited inharmonicity cues and processed both the in-tune and mistuning passages to rule out the contribution of other processing artifacts, it is difficult to rule out all other potential cues that may have been generated by the processing.

Conclusion

Our study reveals important roles for both beats and inharmonicity when detecting mistuning between parts within ongoing music. Our findings extend to realistic instrumental sounds and demonstrate a close relationship between beat strength and mistuning detection abilities. We also provide evidence that perceptual asymmetries in mistuning extend from octaves in isolation to ongoing music containing a mixture of musical intervals, and that it is found for sequences of fifths but not sixths. Finally, our results show that musical experience is associated with an improved ability to use both beating and inharmonicity cues for mistuning detection but that it does not affect the relative perceptual importance of the two cues. Our methodology and results open opportunities to study such questions in more complex musical settings than have been studied in the past.
