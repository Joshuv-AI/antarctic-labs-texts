Long-term outcomes in medullary thyroid cancer patients treated with [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer therapy
Authors: Sanjana Ballal, G. B. Priyanka, Madhav Prasad Yadav, Kunal Ramesh Chandekar, Frank Roesch, Mohammad Sakir, Shipra Agarwal, Chandrasekhar Bal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibroblast activation protein (FAP)-targeted radionuclide therapy is a promising approach for patients with advanced or metastatic medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC). This study evaluates efficacy, safety, and survival outcomes of [177Lu]/[225Ac]-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ therapy in this population.ResultsNineteen patients with progressive MTC were treated with either [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ or [²²⁵Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂, receiving a median of 5 cycles. The median cumulative activity administered was 20.3 GBq (Lu) and 6.66 MBq (Ac) and the median follow-up was 21 months. Of the 17 evaluable patients, radiological response was assessed in 15 patients using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST 1.1). The analysis demonstrated a partial response (PR) in 33% of patients, stable disease (SD) in 47%, and progressive disease (PD) in 20%, resulting in a disease control rate (DCR) of 80%. Positron emission tomography response criteria in solid tumors (PERCIST) evaluation (n = 15) showed 47% PR, 40% SD, and 13% PD. Biochemical response was evaluable in 14 patients: 64.2% showed stable or decreasing tumor markers. Median calcitonin doubling time was 6.7 months. Clinical progression occurred in 2 patients. Median PFS was 26 months; median OS was 42 months. No statistically significant difference in OS was observed between progression and non-progression groups (log-rank p-0.758). No Grade 4 or life-threatening toxicities were observed. Only two patients experienced Grade 3 adverse events—one with elevated alkaline phosphatase and one with worsening anemia. All other hematologic and biochemical toxicities were limited to Grade 1–2.Conclusion[177Lu]/[225Ac]-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ therapy shows encouraging disease control, acceptable safety, and preliminary efficacy signals such as increased survival in the treated patients of advanced MTC.

Introduction

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare neuroendocrine malignancy originating from C-cells of the thyroid, accounting for approximately 1% to 5% of all thyroid cancers [1]. The 10-year-survival rate exceeds 95% for intrathyroidal disease but drops to ~ 40% in the presence of distant metastases. This stark contrast underscores the pivotal role of molecular imaging in accurately assessing disease extent, as it can significantly influence both clinical management and prognostication in MTC [2].

Traditionally used SPECT radiopharmaceuticals such as [99mTc]-DMSA(V), [111In]- or [99mTc]-labeled somatostatin analogues or anti-CEA antibodies, and Iodine-131 [131I]-MIBG have demonstrated limited sensitivity. However, bone scintigraphy with [99mTc]-labeled diphosphonates remains clinically useful for staging and restaging [3, 4]. PET imaging offers intrinsically higher sensitivity and spatial resolution compared to SPECT. Among novel PET radiopharmaceuticals, [18F]F-DOPA PET/CT shows the highest detection rate (> 70%), particularly in patients with serum calcitonin (Ctn) > 150 pg/mL and Calcitonin doubling times < 24 months [5]. Comparative studies confirm the superiority of [18F]F-DOPA over [18F]F-FDG and somatostatin receptor (SSTR) targeted [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-peptides in both lesion detection and impact on clinical management [6–8]. Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of patients with recurrent MTC continue to exhibit negative findings on PET/CT using all three tracers, highlighting the need for more specific molecular imaging agents and the development of novel theranostic approaches.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), a key component of the tumor microenvironment, contribute to tumor cell growth and aggressiveness via extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and immune-modulation. Targeting CAFs with radiolabeled fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPi) molecules has emerged as a promising theranostic avenue for various neoplasms including thyroid cancer [9]. In a recent study by Ballal et al. [10], [68Ga]Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT showed similar sensitivity to [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT for detecting primary tumors, lymph nodes, and brain and pleural metastases, and outperformed [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC in detecting lung nodules and liver and bone metastases of MTC (p-values < 0.0001). These findings highlight the potential of FAPi-based radiotracers in MTC, warranting further investigation within a theranostic framework for patients with limited treatment options.

For localized MTC, complete surgical resection of the tumor and affected lymph nodes remains the cornerstone of curative treatment. However, in patients with distant metastases, therapeutic options are limited. MTC is typically resistant to chemotherapy and radiotherapy and does not take up radioiodine, making treatment challenging. Currently, first-line systemic therapies include tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as vandetanib and cabozantinib, and selective RET inhibitors like selpercatinib. While these agents can lead to objective tumor reduction, complete responses are rare, and off-target toxicities often impair quality of life. Despite the availability of these systemic options, there is no clear consensus on the optimal treatment sequence or follow-up strategies, leading to considerable variability in clinical practice [11–13]. Research is ongoing into novel targeted agents, immunotherapy, and radionuclide therapies, especially for refractory or progressive disease.

Martin et al. [14] conducted the first proof-of-concept study to explore the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.(SA.FAPi)2 and [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)2 in a 40-year-old male with metastatic MTC. High tracer uptake and long tumor retention was observed with both radiopharmaceuticals. However, [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)2 demonstrated reduced uptake in non-target and critical organs (liver, colon). Ballal et al. [15] presented a case report evaluating the therapeutic potential of [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.(SA.FAPi)2 in a 56-year-old patient with relapsed, high-grade MTC that was refractory to standard treatments. This treatment resulted in a significant reduction in the size of the neck mass and a notable decline in serum chromogranin-A (CgA) levels, resulting in improved quality of life. However, subsequent imaging revealed a new liver lesion, indicating a mixed therapeutic response. In MTC, the role of FAPi-based therapy is currently limited to case reports, with no robust data on long-term outcomes. The current study aims to evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer in patients with locally advanced, inoperable or metastatic MTC, addressing this critical gap in knowledge.

Methodology

The study received ethical approval from the Institute Ethics Committee of the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi, India (Ref No: IEC 483). Patient enrolment occurred between November 2020 and March 2024. Follow-up duration was from November 2020 to June 2025. The research was carried out at the Department of Nuclear Medicine in collaboration with the Department of Medical Oncology at AIIMS and the Department of Chemistry at Johannes Gutenberg University, Mainz, Germany, which supplied the DOTA.SA.FAPi and DOTAGA.FAPi dimer compounds. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed consent prior to treatment. The local interdisciplinary tumor board assessed patient eligibility for [177Lu]Lu/[225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer therapy, and patients were informed of the investigational nature of the treatment before administration. The study is single-centre study reflecting real-world clinical practice. Adherence to STROBE guidelines was followed to ensure transparent and accurate reporting of the study’s observational data.

Study design

This was a retrospectively analysed single-centre interventional cohort study conducted at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi, India, reflecting real-world clinical practice.

Patient selection

Patients with histopathologically proven MTC were included in this study. Specifically, patients with locally advanced or metastatic stage disease who underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT scans were considered for this study. Exclusion criteria included known inflammatory conditions, dual malignancies, or pregnancy, impaired bone marrow function (defined as hemoglobin concentration < 7 g/dL, platelet count < 50 × 103 cells/L, or white blood cell count <2 × 109 cells/L), compromised renal function (glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), impaired liver function (albumin concentration < 2.5 g/L), or uptake lower than the physiological uptake in the normal liver parenchyma on [68Ga]Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT imaging.

All patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were consecutively included, thereby reducing selection bias. All treatments were administered at a single institution following standard institutional protocols and multidisciplinary decision-making. Treatment decisions were based on institutional protocols and tumor board recommendations. However, minor variations reflecting real-world clinical practice may have occurred. Nineteen patients were enrolled for [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA-FAPi dimer therapy at our institution based on the decision of multi-disciplinary tumor board.

[177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer treatment protocol

Following the [68Ga]Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT scan, the treatment protocol for [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer involved intravenous administration of the radiopharmaceutical solution and 30 mL of 0.9% normal saline over 10 min, followed by a saline flush. Patients were closely monitored for adverse reactions post-administration. The median activity per injection was 5.5 GBq per cycle, administered at 8-week intervals. All 19 patients received [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer therapy based on above protocol. Additionally, 4/19 patients received tandem [177Lu]Lu/[225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer therapy.

In the tandem [177Lu]Lu/[225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.FAPi dimer therapy regimen, treatment allocation (Lu-only vs. tandem therapy) was not protocol-driven and was influenced by availability and clinical considerations, reflecting real-world practice. The sequence of [225Ac]Ac and [177Lu]Lu was not fixed and was determined based on the availability of [225Ac]Ac at the time of the scheduled treatment cycle. Decisions regarding the need for treatment were personalized and based on interdepartmental consensus, unless treatment discontinuation was necessitated because of unacceptable toxicity, disease progression, death, or unwillingness of the patient.

[¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ /[²²⁵Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ radiolabelling

The preparation of [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ or [²²⁵Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ was performed as follows: ¹⁷⁷LuCl₃ (200–600 µL) and ²²⁵AcCl₃ (100 µL) in 0.1 M HCl, obtained under cGMP conditions, was used for radiolabeling. A freshly prepared 0.1 M sodium ascorbate buffer was utilized as the reaction medium. The calculated amount of peptide (˷30 µg/GBq) was added to the buffer and mixed thoroughly, ensuring that the pH was maintained at 4. The resulting reaction mixture was then transferred to the ¹⁷⁷Lu or ²²⁵Ac vial using a sterile needle.

The labeling reaction was carried out by heating the vial at 95 °C for 30 min. After completion, the reaction mixture was allowed to cool for 5 min, followed by venting. Where necessary, the radiolabeled peptide was purified using a preconditioned C18 light cartridge. The reaction mixture was then passed through the cartridge, allowing retention of the labeled compound, while unbound ¹⁷⁷Lu or ²²⁵Ac was discarded. The purified product was subsequently eluted using 2 mL of ethanol–water solution into the final product vial.

Follow-up

All patients remained on a replacement dose of thyroxine according to the 2015 ATA guidelines throughout the study, with no interruptions in thyroid hormone therapy. Clinical and biochemical data in the patients’ files were reviewed to assess for adverse events (AEs) during therapy. As per departmental protocol, all patients had complete blood counts (CBC), liver function tests (LFT), renal function tests (RFT) at 4 and 8 weeks after each therapy cycle. Interim [68Ga]Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT scans were typically conducted six to eight weeks after the first three cycles of therapy (or earlier if clinically or biochemically indicated). Follow-up imaging was usually performed every 3 to 6 months after completion of the treatment regimen. Follow-up assessments were performed at variable intervals as per clinical practice. Information regarding patients lost to follow-up or still alive at the time of the primary analysis was censored based on their latest known status date.

Efficacy, safety and survival outcomes

The best biochemical response was assessed using serial serum calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels. A biochemical complete response (CR) was defined as normalization of both serum calcitonin and CEA levels to within normal reference limits. Biochemical partial response (PR) was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction from baseline in either marker, while biochemical progression was defined as a ≥ 50% increase from baseline in either marker (each maintained for ≥ 4 weeks). Stable disease (SD) was assigned when changes in both markers did not meet any of the above criteria.

The best overall radiographic and molecular imaging response was assessed using [68Ga]Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT in accordance with the RECIST 1.1 [16] and PERCIST criteria, respectively [17].

Safety evaluations, including clinical assessment and laboratory monitoring, were conducted at baseline and prior to each treatment cycle. Adverse events were retrospectively analyzed and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [18]. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to the first documented clinical, morphological (according to RECIST 1.1), or molecular progression (according to PERCIST 1.0), or death [19]. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of treatment initiation to the date of death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean with 95% confidence intervals, standard deviation, and range, while skewed data were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). Pre- and post-therapy parameters were compared using either paired-sample t-tests (parametric) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (non-parametric). Survival analysis for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) was conducted, and Kaplan-Meier survival plots were generated. Statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc software, with significance set at a P-value less than 0.05.

Results

A total of 19 patients with MTC were included in this study. Baseline demographic, clinical, and prior treatment details have been summarized in Table 1. All patients underwent radionuclide therapy with either [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ or [225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂, receiving a median of 5 cycles (range: 1–9). The median cumulative administered activity was 20.3 GBq (range: 7.4–52.17 GBq) for [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ and 6.66 MBq (range: 5.55–9.25 MBq) for [225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂. The cohort was followed for a median duration of 21 months (range: 4–42 months).

Response assessment

Out of 19 patients included in the study, 17 (89.5%) were evaluable for treatment response based on at least one of the following: imaging or biochemical parameters. 12 patients had both radiological and biochemical follow-up; 3 had only radiological follow-up, and 2 had only biochemical follow-up. Two patients (10.5%) had insufficient data for response evaluation. An overview of various responses is depicted in Fig. 1.

Radiological and molecular imaging response

Radiological and molecular imaging response was assessed in 15 out of 19 patients, as four patients did not have follow-up imaging available for evaluation. Among the assessable patients, the best overall radiographic response based on RECIST 1.1 was seen as progressive disease (PD) in 3 patients (20%), stable disease (SD) in 7 patients (47%), and partial response (PR) in 5 patients (33%), with Disease Control rate of 80% (12/15) and Objective response rate of 33% (5/15). The mean time to progression was seen to be 22.5 months (in patients who showed best response as PR), 11.5 months (in patients who showed best response as SD patients) and 7.6months (in patients who showed best response as PD), indicating a radiological benefit in the treated individuals with clinically meaningful achievement of prolonged disease stabilisation. According to PERCIST criteria, the best overall response was seen as PD was noted in 2 patients (13%), SD in 6 patients (40%), and PR in 7 patients (47%) (Table 2). (Figures 2, 3, 4 and 5)

Biochemical and clinical response

Biochemical response assessment was feasible in 14 out of 19 patients. Four patients lacked follow-up biochemical data, and one patient had non-secretory disease at baseline, precluding further biochemical evaluation. Among the evaluable patients, biochemical progression was observed in 5 patients (35.7%), while 9 patients (64.2%) demonstrated either stable or decreasing tumor marker levels (Table 2). The calcitonin doubling times among these patients ranged from 2.3 to 18.0 months, with a median doubling time of 6.7 months, indicating a variable but measurable biological response to therapy. S. CEA progression was seen in 2 patients with a doubling time of 2.8 and 2.2 months each. Clinical progression was observed in two patients during the course of follow-up, while the remaining patients exhibited no overt clinical signs of disease progression. One patient developed new-onset hemoptysis, attributed to the progression of a locally invasive parapharyngeal mass. Another patient presented with abdominal distension and pain due to new-onset ascites, suggestive of disease progression despite ongoing therapy.

Subgroup analyses

Tandem [177Lu]/[225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ Vs [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ Monotherapy

Subgroup analysis based on treatment type was performed descriptively. Among patients receiving tandem (Lu/Ac) therapy (n = 4), 3 were evaluable for response. The best responses achieved: 1 patient demonstrated partial response (PR) and 2 had stable disease (SD). One patient did not have response assessment.

Among patients receiving Lu-only therapy (n = 15), 14 were evaluable (2 with biochemical responses and 12 with radiological responses): 5 patients had PR, 5 had SD, and 4 had progressive disease (PD), while one patient was not evaluable.

Prior exposure to Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (TKIs)

A descriptive subgroup analysis was conducted based on prior TKI exposure. Among patients without prior TKI therapy (n = 6), all were evaluable for response; four patients achieved a partial response (PR), while two demonstrated stable disease (SD). In contrast, among patients with prior TKI exposure (n = 13), 11 were evaluable, of whom two achieved PR, five had SD, and four exhibited progressive disease (PD); two patients were not evaluable for response. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and the non-comparative nature of the study design.

The OS analysis revealed that median survival was 42 months with five deaths (26.3%) reported.

Adverse events

Patients were evaluated for hematologic and biochemical toxicities at baseline and last follow-up. Toxicities were assessed and graded according to the CTCAE v5.0 criteria. At baseline, 2 patients (Patients 2 and 15) had Grade 2 anemia. At follow-up, most patients remained stable or improved, with only one patient (Patient 1) declining from Grade 1 to Grade 2 and one another patient showed worsening from grade 1 to grade 3 (patient 17). Platelet counts were normal (Grade 0) in nearly all patients except at both time points. One patient (Patient 5). No Grade 3 or higher thrombocytopenia was observed in any patient. WBC values remained within normal or near-normal ranges in most patients. Mild leukopenia (Grade 2) was seen in 1 patient at baseline and in 4 patients (Patients 13, 16,18 and 19) at follow-up, with no patient exhibiting Grade 3 or higher leukopenia. Bilirubin levels remained within Grade 0–1 range for all except patient 11, who developed Grade 1hyperbilirubinemia at follow-up. No Grade 2 or higher bilirubin toxicity was observed. Mild (Grade 1–2) transaminase elevations were observed in a few patients. One patient showed grade 3 increase in ALP (patient 11). Out of 19 patients, no Grade 4 or higher hematologic or biochemical toxicities were observed during the evaluation period. The overall hematologic and hepatic safety profile remained acceptable, with no life-threatening or dose-limiting adverse events detected. This supports the tolerability of the treatment or intervention administered during the study period. (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) is a serine protease selectively expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) within the tumor microenvironment. CAFs play an important role in tumor progression through extracellular matrix remodeling, promotion of angiogenesis, and facilitation of tumor invasion [20].

In thyroid malignancies, including medullary thyroid carcinoma, emerging evidence suggests a desmoplastic stromal component, which may show FAP-expressing fibroblasts [10, 15, 20–23]. Unlike conventional tumor-targeted tracers, FAP-targeted imaging reflects stromal activation rather than tumor cell density, offering a complementary biological perspective [20].

This pilot study represents one of the first clinical evaluations of [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ therapy in patients with advanced MTC, a population with limited treatment options beyond kinase inhibitors and conventional chemotherapy.

The results demonstrate that FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy is safe, well-tolerated, and capable of achieving disease stabilization or partial biochemical response in a significant subset of patients. These findings highlight the potential role of fibroblast activation protein (FAP) as a theranostic target in MTC, particularly in tumors with prominent stromal involvement and high FAP expression.

Best Radiological response assessed using RECIST 1.1 criteria revealed that approximately half the patients had stable disease (47%) and one third showed a partial response (33%) with a disease control rate of 80%, thus underscoring the ability to stabilize the disease. Approximately half of the evaluable patients eventually progressed but the time to progression was delayed in those who showed stable disease and partial response. Best Molecular response assessed using PERCIST criteria revealed that approximately half the patients had a partial response (47%) and 40% showed a stable disease with a Disease Control Rate of 87%. The overall disease control rate was similar with both radiological and molecular response assessments. These findings suggest that FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy likely exerts its effects through modulation of the tumor stroma, rather than direct tumor cell cytotoxicity, which could account for the above clinical and biochemical responses. These findings highlight the feasibility of FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy, particularly for those with limited alternatives, and indicate preliminary efficacy signals. The variable response to treatment is likely due to heterogeneous stromal activation across lesions. However, the relatively high proportion of progression (~ 42%) indicates that further optimization of patient selection, dosing, and combination strategies (e.g., with TKIs or immunotherapy) is warranted to enhance treatment efficacy.

Although FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy may not lead to significant tumor shrinkage in most cases, it could still offer radiological and molecular stabilization, potentially translating into clinical benefit. The minor discrepancy between RECIST and PERCIST evaluations supports the complementary role of functional imaging in response assessment, particularly in heterogeneous tumors like MTC. The results also raise the possibility that response heterogeneity may be linked to variable FAP expression within and between lesions, a hypothesis warranting further exploration through correlative imaging-pathology studies.

Biochemical response evaluation demonstrated that 64.3% of assessable patients had either biochemical stability or a decline in tumor markers (e.g., calcitonin or CEA), suggesting a favorable biological impact in a majority of treated patients. Although 35.7% experienced biochemical progression, the relative proportion achieving stabilization is encouraging, especially given the refractory or metastatic nature of their disease. Biochemical markers in MTC often precede radiological progression and are therefore critical indicators of early treatment response. The observed biochemical benefit in this cohort implies that FAP-targeted therapies may exert systemic antitumor effects even when morphological or molecular responses are modest. Additionally, the lack of data in some patient, either due to non-secretory status or missing follow-up highlights the importance of integrating biochemical surveillance into the post-treatment monitoring protocol. Going forward, combining biochemical trends with imaging and clinical outcomes may enhance response stratification and early prediction of therapy efficacy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design introduces inherent biases and precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Second, response assessment imaging was not conducted at standardized intervals; instead, follow-up scans were performed at the discretion of the treating clinician. This may introduce bias in the determination of “best response” and could impact time-to-event analyses due to informative censoring. Additionally, the survival analysis is constrained by the small number of events, resulting in imprecise estimates with wide confidence intervals; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory. Heterogeneity in prior systemic therapy exposure and tumor characteristics, including the presence of mixed histology, may have further influenced clinical outcomes. Finally, the absence of dedicated histopathological assessment of FAP expression in this cohort represents an important limitation.

Conclusion

Taken together, the results from radiological, molecular, and biochemical response assessments indicate that FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy using [177Lu]Lu/[225Ac]Ac-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPi)₂ demonstrates modest but clinically meaningful activity in a subset of patients with advanced or metastatic MTC. While nearly half of the patients progressed by RECIST or PERCIST criteria, a considerable proportion exhibited disease stabilization or partial response for a considerable duration of time. The heterogeneity in outcomes likely reflects inter-patient variability in FAP expression, tumor microenvironment, prior therapies, and disease kinetics. These findings demonstrate the feasibility and safety of FAP-targeted theranostics, with encouraging preliminary efficacy signals that warrant further validation in prospective studies in heavily pretreated MTC patients with limited therapeutic options.

Future directions

Future studies should aim to refine patient selection based on tumor FAP expression levels and stromal characteristics, potentially improving response rates and minimizing non-responders. Comparative studies between [¹⁷⁷Lu]- and [²²⁵Ac]-labeled FAP inhibitors will help delineate the optimal radioligand for different clinical scenarios, especially with regard to tumor burden and lesion size. Combining FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy with other modalities such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or redifferentiation therapies may enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome resistance mechanisms. Long-term follow-up is necessary to evaluate durability of response, overall survival, and late toxicity profiles, particularly for alpha-emitting agents. These directions will be critical in shaping the future role of FAP-targeted therapies in MTC and other FAP-expressing malignancies.
