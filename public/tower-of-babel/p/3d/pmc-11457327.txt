Clinic, CT radiomics, and deep learning combined model for the prediction of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis
Authors: Kaixiang Zhang, Guoxin Zhao, Yinghui Liu, Yongbin Huang, Jie Long, Ning Li, Huangze Yan, Xiuzhu Zhang, Jingzhi Ma, Yuming Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is a serious fungal infection. However, current diagnostic methods have limitations. The purpose of this study was to use artificial intelligence to achieve a more accurate diagnosis of IPA.MethodsTotally 263 patients (148 cases of IPA, 115 cases of non-IPA) were retrospectively enrolled from a single institution and randomly divided into training and test sets at a ratio of 7:3. Clinic-radiological independent risk factors for IPA were screened using univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis, after which a clinic-radiological model was constructed. The optimal radiomics features were extracted and screened based on CT images to construct the radiomics label score (Rad-score) and radiomics model. The optimal DL features were extracted and screened using four pre-trained convolutional neural networks, respectively, followed by the construction of the DL label score (DL-score) and DL model. Then, the radiomics-DL model was constructed. Finally, the combined model was constructed based on clinic-radiological independent risk factors, the Rad-score, and the DL-score. LR was adopted as the classifier. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn, and the areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated to evaluate the efficacy of each model in predicting IPA. Additionally, based on the best-performing model on the LR classifier, four other machine learning (ML) classifiers were constructed to evaluate the predictive value for IPA.ResultsThe AUC of the clinic-radiological model for predicting IPA in the training and test sets was 0.845 and 0.765, respectively. The AUC of the radiomics-DL and combined models in the training set was 0.871 and 0.932, while in the test set was 0.851 and 0.881, respectively. The combined model showed better predictive performance than all other models. DCA showed that taking 0.00–1.00 as the threshold, the clinical benefit of the combined model was higher than that of all other models. Then, the combined model was trained on four other machine learning classifiers, all of which achieved AUC values above 0.80 in the test set, showing good performance in predicting IPA.ConclusionClinic, CT radiomics, and DL combined model could be used to predict IPA effectively.Keywords: IPA, Deep learning, Radiomics, Predictive medicine, Retrospective study

Background

Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is a severe and potentially life-threatening fungal infection that predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals, especially those in hematology departments [1]. Patients undergoing treatments for hematologic malignancies, such as chemotherapy and stem cell transplantation, are particularly susceptible to IPA due to their compromised immune systems and prolonged neutropenia [2]. Delayed diagnosis or delayed antifungal therapy is associated with increased mortality in patients with IPA [3–5]. Early recognition of IPA represents an opportunity to improve clinical outcomes. However, current diagnostic methods, including clinical manifestations, imaging presentations, laboratory tests, and histopathological examinations, often suffer from delays and limited specificity and accuracy [6]. This underscores the critical need for new non-invasive diagnostic approaches that can provide faster and more accurate detection of IPA.

Studies have showed that the diagnostic accuracy for the prediction of IPA can be improved with the help of machine learning (ML) model-based image analysis and the analysis of clinical characteristics. Many studies have already been performed using ML models to predict or differentiate fungal infections, such as using readily available clinical parameters to predict IPA in acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) patients [7], constructing a prototype deep learning model with clinical characteristics for the early diagnosis of IPA [8], and utilizing a nomogram based on a radiomics model combined with clinical data to differentiate invasive fungal disease of the lung from bacterial pneumonia [9]. Combining deep learning, radiomics, and clinical data for disease diagnosis and treatment has been reported to be promising in many studies [10–12]. However, the combination of these three methods for predicting IPA has rarely been reported.

Briefly, radiomics, which refers to a high number of quantitative features extracted from medical images, has shown promise in capturing the heterogeneity of diseases through the analysis of texture, shape, intensity, and other features [13, 14]. Deep learning can learn and extract automatically generated features from pre-processed CT images through a convolutional neural network (CNN), and these features can be utilized to identify and analyze structures, lesions, or other crucial information within the images [15]. Then, radiomics or deep learning features are used as inputs into ML algorithms to perform regression or classification tasks.

In this study, we aim to assess the effectiveness of combining clinic-radiological data, CT radiomics, and deep learning techniques to predict IPA for rapid recognition and appropriate empirical antifungal treatment, especially in resource-limited hospitals. Furthermore, we aim to identify the clinic-radiological independent risk factors for IPA in hospitalized patients.

Methods

Patient enrolment and grouping

This project was conducted at a teaching hospital affiliated with Guangdong Medical University, China. The study was designed and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The institutional review board of the participating center approved this study (the Internal Registration Number is PJKT2024-144). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. We retrospectively reviewed the patients with IPA who were treated in our hospital between December 2018 and April 2024. The Inclusion criteria, based on guidelines and literature [2, 6, 16–20], were as follows: (a) positive serum Aspergillus antigen or antibody, (b) septate branching hyphae in tissue histopathology or positive culture from tissue obtained by an invasive procedure, (c) positive metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) results in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BLF) and isolation of Aspergillus in lower respiratory tract (LRT) samples, (d) high-risk patients with positive serum galactomannan (GM) and BLF GM tests, and (e) complete clinical data and radiographic findings typical of IPA. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Missing more than a fourth of clinical characteristics; (b) Poor image lesion segmentation; (c) Lung infection caused by other pathogens; and (d) Patients with pulmonary malignancies.

To ensure a robust assessment of the model's resolution and accuracy, a control set was established, comprising 46 cases of tuberculosis, 33 cases of lung cancer, and 36 cases of bacterial pneumonia, totaling 115 non-IPA cases, The Inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Time Frame: Patients diagnosed between December 2018 and April 2024, (b) For lung cancer: Pathological examination of tissue biopsies confirms the diagnosis of lung cancer [21]; For tuberculosis: patients with positive results on the Xpert MTB/RIF assay [22]; For bacterial pneumonia: Patients with positive blood culture results, (c) Cases included had typical clinical manifestations and radiographic findings consistent with tuberculosis, lung cancer, or bacterial pneumonia, and were similar to IPA in clinical and CT imaging presentations [6, 23, 24]. The exclusion Criteria were as follows: (a) Patients with multiple pulmonary infections or lesions, (b) Patients with insufficient clinical data or poor-quality CT images, (c) Patients who had already received etiological treatment.

Finally, data of 263 patients, including 148 cases with IPA and 115 cases with non-IPA, were retrospectively enrolled and were divided into a training set (104 cases of IPA, 80 cases of non-IPA) and a test set (44 cases of IPA, 35 cases of non-IPA) at a ratio of 7:3.

Data collection

Patients' information was obtained from the electronic medical records in the Hospital Information System (HIS). According to guidelines, literature, and clinical experience [2, 25], data regarding demographics, clinical and CT radiological characteristics of the patients with IPA and non-IPA were collected and entered into electronic case report forms by three recorders. Three independent radiologists sorted the CT image findings of lung lesions in both groups. To improve early diagnostic performance, initial CT images and clinic-radiological data from the hospitalization during which patients were first diagnosed with IPA or non-IPA were collected.

CT image acquisition

CT scans were performed using a GE64-row light-speed spiral CT scanner. All graphic raw data were transmitted to the ADW4.4 workstation for postprocessing. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 300 mA, rotation speed 0.7 r/s, screw pitch 0.8:1, collimator width 0.625 mm × 64 layers, and scanning layer thickness 1 mm. Lung windows were reconstructed using the bone algorithm with a layer thickness of 2 mm. Mediastinal windows were reconstructed using the soft tissue algorithm with a layer thickness of 4 mm, a reconstruction interval of 0 mm, and a matrix of 512 × 512. All patients were in a supine position with their hands on their heads, and the body was positioned at the center of the bed. The scanning range extends from the lung apices to the lung bases, with instructions for the patient to hold their breath after inhalation during the scan. Enhanced chest scan: Inject contrast agent intravenously at a volume of 60–100 ml, at a flow rate of 2–2.5 ml/s, with a scan delay of 30–35 s, and the scanning parameters are the same as those of plain scan.

Image segmentation

Manual segmentation of the lesion's three-dimensional region of interest (ROI) in CT images was performed by two experienced radiologists using ITKSNAP software (http://www.itksnap.org). The ROI covering the whole lesion volume was contoured. For patients with multiple lesions in the CT image, the largest lesion was segmented. For radiomics feature extraction, we assessed the interobserver reproducibility for ROI-based radiomics features using the CT images of 50 patients randomly chosen from the training cohort in a blinded manner. The segmentation procedure was repeated by two doctors after one week to assess the intraobserver reproducibility. Features with intraclass correlation coefficients > 0.8 were selected for further analysis.

Radiomic feature extraction

We utilized the Pyradiomics software (http://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io) [26] to extract radiomic features from the three-dimensional ROI of each lesion. In total, 851 features were obtained per lesion, comprising 107 original features and 744 wavelet-filtered features. The original features included 18 first-order features, 11 shape features (3D), 3 shape features (2D), 24 Gy level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, 16 Gy level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features, 16 Gy level run length matrix (GLRLM) features, 5 neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features, and 14 Gy level dependence matrix (GLDM) features.

Deep-learning feature extraction

In this study, we utilized four deep learning models—Xception [27], VGG16 [28], VGG19 [28], and InceptionV3 [29]—for feature extraction. All models were pre-trained on the ImageNet database, and the input layer dimensions were standardized to 224 × 224 × 3. Each layer employed the ReLU activation function, with convolutional kernel sizes of 3 × 3. The number of filters increased progressively, with VGG16 and VGG19 ranging from 64 to 512 filters, while Xception and InceptionV3 employed dynamic adjustments with various kernel sizes and depthwise separable convolutions. The padding strategy used was "same," with a stride value of 1. Pooling layers included max pooling and global average pooling, with the final output feature dimensions being 4096 for VGG16 and VGG19, and 2048 for Xception and InceptionV3. After removing the last fully connected layer, we applied global max pooling to the feature maps, and these extracted deep learning features were then input into a machine learning classifier to build the deep learning model.

Feature selection

The selection of radiomics and deep learning features began with normalization, ensuring consistent data scales and making different features comparable. This was followed by feature selection using the Mann–Whitney U test to remove irrelevant features. Next, recursive elimination was performed to further refine the feature set and reduce redundancy. Finally, the least absolute selection and shrinkage operator (LASSO) was applied to both radiomics and deep learning features for dimensionality reduction, identifying the most important predictive features. The optimal log(λ) value was determined using tenfold cross-validation, ensuring model stability and generalization ability, and the corresponding best features were selected.

Model construction and performance assessment

The radiomics and deep learning features were calculated by weighting the selected features with LASSO coefficients to construct the Rad-score and DL-score. Then, the radiomics model, DL model, and radiomics-DL model were constructed. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed on clinic-radiological characteristics that showed statistically significant differences in univariate analysis to select clinic-radiological independent risk factors for IPA and construct a clinic-radiological model. To ensure the reliability of the radiological features, we used the kappa values to evaluate observer consistency. The kappa values measures the consistency between multiple observers beyond what would be expected by chance. Finally, the combined model was constructed based on clinic-radiological independent risk factors, the Rad-score, and the DL-score. Logistic regression (LR) was adopted as the classifier. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn, and the areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated to evaluate the efficacy of each model for predicting IPA. The DeLong test was then performed to assess the statistical significance of the differences in AUCs between the combined model and other models (clinical model, deep learning model, and radiomics model). The calibration of the model was evaluated using calibration curves, and the application value of the model was assessed using decision curve analysis. Then, the accuracy of the best-performing model was validated using fivefold cross-validation based on LR.

In five-fold cross-validation, the whole data is divided into five mutually exclusive data partitions. There will be five iterations in the validation process, where in every iteration, one partition will be used as test data and remaining four partitions will be used as training data. The partition used as test data in one iteration will not be used as test data in another iteration.

Next, we evaluated the performance of various models. We then focused on the model that demonstrated the best performance with the LR classifier and used it to develop and train four additional machine learning classifiers, including XGBoost, RandomForest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB). This approach aimed to assess and compare their effectiveness in predicting IPA using the same set of features that proved most effective with the LR model.

Statistical analysis

The P values for differences in clinic-radiological characteristics between sets were calculated using the Chi-square test for categorical data and the student's t-test for numeric data. The AUC of the ROC curve was used to determine prediction performance. The calibration performance was graphically evaluated using calibration plots. For radiological features, Kappa analysis was also performed to assess inter-rater agreement. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. Statistical analysis and graphic production were performed using Python v3.7 and SPSS.

Prediction performance of the radiomics-DL model

A total of 851 radiomics features were extracted from the CT images of the training set, which were further reduced to 19 optimal features related to IPA using LASSO logistic regression (Fig. 2A-D). Then, these optimal features were used to construct the Rad-score and radiomics model for predicting IPA, yielding an AUC of 0.820 and 0.844 for the training and test sets, respectively (Table 4). More details regarding the radiomics study flowchart are described in Fig. 3. As for predicting IPA using DL features, we compared four models adopting different CNNs as feature extractors to optimize prediction performance (Fig. 4A-B). The AUC ranged from 0.795 to 0.888 for the training set and 0.632 to 0.737 for the test set (Table 5). The model using VGG-19 (LR model) contained 18 optimal features and achieved the best classification performance among the four algorithms. Then, these optimal features were used to construct the DL-score and DL model for predicting IPA, yielding an AUC of 0.836 and 0.737 for the training and test sets, respectively. Then, based on the Rad-score and DL-score, a radiomics-DL model was constructed, yielding an AUC of 0.871 and 0.851 for the training and test sets, respectively (Table 4).

Prediction performance of the combined model

The combined model for predicting IPA based on clinic-radiological independent risk factors, the Rad-score, and the DL-score was constructed, yielding an AUC of 0.932 and 0.881 for the training and test sets, respectively (Table 4 and Fig. 5A-B). The combined model showed superior predictive performance compared to all other models. The DeLong test results indicated that there was no statistically significant difference in diagnostic performance between the combined model and other models (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). However, DCA showed that taken 0.00–1.00 as the threshold, the clinical benefit of the combined model was higher than that of all different models (Fig. 5C-D), and the calibration curves showed that the combined model showed good calibration accuracy (Fig. 5E-F). The performance metrics for the combined model using five-fold cross-validation are list in Table 6. These results demonstrate that the combined model performs robustly across the different folds, with consistent performance metrics, highlighting its effectiveness in predicting IPA. We used four other machine learning classifiers to construct machine learning models with the combined model. The performance of different classifiers is shown in Table 7. We could find that in the test set, XGBoost, LR, RF, SVM, and GNB were all above 0.80, showing good performance for the prediction of IPA (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Using an LR classifier, we evaluated a model that combined clinic-radiological data, CT radiomics, and deep learning for the prediction of IPA. Our results showed that this combined model outperformed individual clinic-radiological, radiomics, and DL models, as well as any combination of radiomics and DL. It achieved AUC values of 0.932 in the training set and 0.881 in the test set. DCA showed that the clinical benefit of the combined model was higher than that of all other models. The combined model also demonstrated robust performance across four different machine learning classifiers for the prediction of IPA.

When comparing model performance, we should consider multiple aspects comprehensively. Firstly, model performance should be evaluated by considering both the training and test sets. In our study, the combined model demonstrated superior performance across both sets, with AUC values of 0.932 and 0.881 in the training and test sets, respectively, indicating consistent and reliable performance on different datasets. Secondly, we assessed the models using multiple performance metrics, including AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. In the training set, the combined model performed the best across all these metrics. Although the Rad model exhibited slightly higher precision and F1 score in the test set, the combined model showed superior accuracy, recall, and AUC, highlighting its overall effectiveness in predicting IPA. Additionally, through DCA, the combined model demonstrated higher clinical benefit across the entire range of threshold probabilities compared to other models, underscoring its greater practical utility in clinical applications. Finally, the combined model integrates clinic-radiological risk factors, radiomics features, and deep learning features, enhancing its robustness and generalizability, whereas other models, relying solely on single features, may have limited applicability. In conclusion, the combined model, by integrating diverse data sources, offers a more comprehensive and reliable tool for predicting IPA, outperforming other models across multiple evaluation metrics, and showcasing its significant potential for clinical application and decision support.

In our study, we employ a multi-algorithm approach to enhance the accuracy and robustness of IPA diagnosis. By leveraging a variety of machine learning algorithms, we capitalize on their unique strengths in handling different data characteristics and patterns. This strategy helps us identify the most accurate predictive model for IPA, improve model robustness by mitigating overfitting and bias associated with any single algorithm, and provide a suite of diagnostic tools tailored to diverse clinical needs. Ultimately, our goal is to develop a more reliable and precise diagnostic model for IPA that addresses the limitations of single-algorithm methods and enhances clinical outcomes.

In the diagnosis of IPA, the inadequacy of sputum samples often results in a low positive predictive value for sputum culture (approximately 72%), especially in non-hematological patients [30]. Serum galactomannan tests, while useful, have high false positive rates, and although CT signs of pulmonary fungal infections appear earlier than serum galactomannan positivity [31], they lack specificity. Due to the high risks associated with invasive procedures, diagnosing IPA through biopsy or bronchoscopy remains challenging. Consequently, early diagnosis of IPA is both challenging and crucial. To address this, our study employs binary logistic regression for univariate and multivariate analysis to identify significant factors and establish a clinical model. The study identifies CCI score, neutropenia, use of immunosuppressant, long-term use of two or more antibiotics, and transfusion history as independent risk factors for IPA. We found a significant association between the CCI score and IPA occurrence, where patients with higher CCI scores have a lower risk of developing IPA. This finding aligns with previous studies and provides new insights into IPA diagnosis [32]. The CCI score helps clinicians understand patient comorbidities and guide treatment decisions. When patients exhibit these clinical characteristics, the likelihood of IPA infection should be considered high. Although multivariate analysis of imaging features did not yield significant results, literature and guidelines suggest that CT signs such as cavity and crescent signs indicate IPA infection. Then by analyzing CT images through radiomics and deep learning, we can combine these models with the clinical model to build a robust combined model. Internal validation shows that this approach improves IPA diagnostic accuracy. And through these applications, our combined model enhances IPA diagnostic accuracy and provides clinicians with practical decision-support tools, offering significant clinical benefits.

To further illustrate the clinical application of the model, we consider a specific patient case. The patient is a 65-year-old male who was admitted due to persistent fever for two days, accompanied by cough and sputum. He has a history of acute myeloid leukemia and has recently undergone chemotherapy and immunosuppressive treatment, resulting in weakened immunity. Upon admission, the complete blood count indicated neutropenia, and a chest CT revealed cavitary lesions, leading to a preliminary suspicion of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA). Clinical parameters and chest CT images of the patient were input into our combined model, which processed the data and, based on model performance indicators (AUC 0.867, accuracy 0.875, and precision 0.667), showed a high-risk prediction for IPA. Based on this high-risk prediction, the clinician immediately initiated empirical antifungal therapy and performed bronchoscopy to obtain further diagnostic information, while also consulting an infectious disease specialist. During treatment, the clinician closely monitored the patient and arranged for follow-up imaging to assess the treatment response. Laboratory test results ultimately confirmed the presence of aspergillus, and the timely initiation of antifungal therapy significantly improved the patient’s condition, validating the model's practicality in guiding early treatment decisions.

As mentioned before, radiomics can convert medical images into extensive quantitative data. DL has also shown potential as another viable technique for predicting IPA. However, there are significant differences in feature extraction between radiomics and deep learning. Radiomics relies on manually defined features such as shape and texture, which are extracted from lower-level image features. In contrast, deep learning, driven by data, can automatically learn and extract higher-level features from image patches.

In this study, we collected non-IPA patients with tuberculosis, bacterial pneumonia, and lung cancer as control groups for non-IPA cases to enhance the practicality and reliability of the model. Collecting control groups from multiple diseases helps simulate clinical complexity, ensuring comprehensive and representative comparative results. Additionally, considering the impact of clinic-radiological data on the model, we integrated clinic-radiological data with radiomics and deep learning, thereby enhancing the model's performance [33].

When applying our combined model, we need to consider several key challenges and corresponding solutions. First, ensuring seamless integration with electronic health record (EHR) systems is crucial. This can be achieved by developing application programming interfaces (APIs) to enable the model to access patient clinical and imaging data in real time, thereby improving diagnostic efficiency. Second, to enhance the usability for clinicians, we recommend conducting targeted training to help them understand the model's outputs and effectively apply them in the diagnostic process. Additionally, it is necessary to conduct pilot studies in actual clinical environments to evaluate the model's performance and gather feedback, which will help optimize the model and workflows. Through these measures, we expect to achieve a smooth integration of the model into existing clinical workflows, thereby enhancing the early diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis and ultimately improving patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the observed results likely reflect the similarity in model performance across the training and test sets. Both the combined model and other models exhibited consistent accuracy and stability. This overall similarity in performance may have obscured any potential statistical significance, particularly in the DeLong test, where no significant differences were found. This suggests that while the models performed consistently across different datasets, their discriminatory power may be limited, underscoring the need for further refinement to enhance differentiation. Second, dataset limitations played a crucial role in the results. The study was based on single-center data with a relatively small sample size, which could not only affect the sensitivity of the DeLong test but also limit the generalizability of the models to other patient populations. Additionally, the manual segmentation of lesions, influenced by the radiologist's experience, introduces variability that may impact model outcomes. Automated segmentation methods should be explored to enhance objectivity and consistency. Third, the retrospective nature of the study resulted in significant missing clinical data, which may have reduced the reliability of the findings. Moreover, the study lacked additional clinical data and imaging from patients with conditions similar to IPA, such as Candida pneumonia or viral pneumonia, which could impact the models' clinical applicability. Finally, predicting IPA is inherently challenging due to the significant variability in patients' imaging and clinical characteristics. This variability may lead to differences in the models’ predictive capabilities across different scenarios. Furthermore, the deep learning network structure was not fully optimized due to limitations in data size and computational resources, which may have affected the models' performance in the DeLong test. Addressing these limitations could improve the sensitivity of the DeLong test, making its results more meaningful in future analyses, particularly in evaluating the discriminatory power of different models. Additionally, solving these issues will enhance the generalizability of the models, allowing for more accurate IPA predictions across diverse patient populations and clinical settings. By increasing the diversity and scale of the dataset, optimizing the deep learning network, and incorporating automated segmentation methods, the bias introduced by human factors could be reduced, leading to improved stability and consistency in prediction results. These improvements would further support the application of the model in clinical practice, providing a more reliable foundation for early diagnosis and personalized treatment.

In conclusion, this study confirmed that combining CT radiomics, deep learning, and clinic-radiological data effectively predicts IPA. This approach offers a promising, non-invasive method for IPA prediction, aiding personalized treatment strategies, and helps identify clinic-radiological independent risk factors for IPA in hospitalized patients through the analysis of clinical data. Furthermore, as future studies collect more patient data, the model's accuracy and usefulness are likely to improve gradually.
