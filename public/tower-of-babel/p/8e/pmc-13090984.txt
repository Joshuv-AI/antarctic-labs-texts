The association of multiple sclerosis with systemic inflammatory biomarkers: A case-control study exploring links with periodontal disease
Authors: Ola K. Abed, Raghad Fadhil Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple sclerosis is a chronic autoimmune disease that results in nerve demyelination. It has been theorized to be associated with other diseases, such as periodontal diseases, which comprise gingivitis and periodontitis. The current study investigates the effects of these two conditions on the inflammatory biomarkers MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-9, TIMP-1, and RgpA in the serum of individuals with periodontal diseases and multiple sclerosis. Revealing the bidirectional link between said conditions is key, as treatment must shift to a whole-body approach—managing periodontitis may help control neuroinflammation in MS.MethodsThe case-control study consisted of four groups: the first group consisted of 32 patients with MS and periodontal diseases, the second group encompassed 22 patients with MS and healthy periodontium, the third group included 20 patients with periodontal diseases, but they were systemically healthy, while the fourth group consisted of 15 subjects with healthy periodontium and systemically healthy. Serum was then collected from all participants, and ELISA kits were used to measure the inflammatory biomarkers.ResultsMultiple linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the independent effects of MS and periodontal disease on biomarker levels. Regression analysis revealed that MS was significantly associated with elevated MMP-9, RgpA, and MMP-1. While PDs appear to be less associated with MMP-9 and RgpA.ConclusionMS has a significantly greater independent effect on systemic levels of MMP-9, RgpA, and MMP-1 than periodontal disease. The association between MS, elevated RgpA, and worse periodontal parameters suggests a potential role for periodontal pathogens in the MS inflammatory milieu.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis is a disease that results in the degeneration and demyelination of nerve axons in the central nervous system, which affects cognitive and physical abilities.1 While periodontal diseases, including gingivitis and periodontitis, are chronic inflammatory conditions caused by dysbiotic biofilms, they can lead to the destruction of tooth-supporting tissues in untreated patients.2,3 As both diseases have immune and inflammatory components, researchers have tried to find an association between the two diseases, especially given the fact that periodontitis has been found to act as a reservoir for periodontal pathogens, which could be a potential source of systemic inflammation that plays a role in the etiology of neurological disorders with an inflammatory basis, such as multiple sclerosis.4

A key mechanistic link may involve P. gingivalis, a keystone pathogen that has been implicated in many pathological brain conditions associated with neurodegeneration.5 This pathogen produces proteases called gingipains, which cause dysregulation in cytokines like MMPs, host proteinases important in the remodeling of the extracellular matrix and tissue breakdown.6 In MS, there is also an increased expression of MMPs, and this expression leads to the destruction of the blood-brain barrier.7 Despite the recognized inflammatory nature of both PDs and MS, the specific systemic inflammatory interplay between them remains poorly understood. Crucially, the independent and combined effects of MS and PDs on a panel of shared serum biomarkers (MMPs, TIMP-1, RgpA) have not been comprehensively evaluated. Furthermore, the relationship between these systemic biomarkers and clinical periodontal parameters in MS patients is largely unexplored. This study, therefore, aimed to investigate these gaps. Since no previous study has quantitatively compared the independent effects of MS and PD on the specific panel of serum biomarkers. Accordingly, we hypothesized that patients with MS, PDs, or both conditions would exhibit distinct serum profiles of MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-9, TIMP-1, and RgpA compared to healthy controls. Our specific objectives were to: 1) quantify the independent associations of MS and PDs with these biomarker levels, and 2) evaluate the correlation between biomarker levels and clinical measures of periodontal disease severity.

Methods

Study design

The current study was a case-control (observational) study. Participants were recruited from The Medical City in Baghdadusing a convenience sampling method. Individuals who met the eligibility criteria during the recruitment period were invited to participate, and the purpose of the study was explained. Those who provided written informed consent were enrolled in the study consecutively. For participants, a case sheet was filled out with their names, ages, sex, and smoking status. Also, their BMI was calculated, then, after recording missing teeth, periodontal parameters were measured, which included: clinical attachment loss (CAL), probing pocket depth (PPD), and finally, bleeding on probing index percentage (BOP%).

Inclusion criteria

Participants selected were 18 years and older. And with at least ≥20 teeth. Participants with multiple sclerosis were included in the study, regardless of the date of diagnosis and the medications used for MS treatment. New multiple sclerosis patients were diagnosed according to the 2024 update of McDonald's criteria8.

As for periodontal status, the patients were either Healthy: manifested by BOP% less than 10%, i.e., without clinical attachment loss".2 Concerning periodontal disease, they were either Gingivitis, which entails a BOP percentage of 10% or more. The included gingivitis in the present study was generalized, with BOP% equal to or more than 30%.9 Or periodontitis: The patient is considered to suffer from periodontitis if there is a detectable interdental CAL at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth, or detectable buccal/labial or lingual CAL ≥3 mm with pocketing >3 mm at ≥2 teeth10.

Exclusion criteria

Subject who were not included in the study were: subjects who are alcohol drinkers, pregnant or nursing women, patients who have undergone extensive periodontal treatment in the last 3 months, patients with any oral disease of an inflammatory nature (other than periodontitis) that could influence the biomarkers under investigation, patients taking contraceptive pills, subjects who were not willing to participate in the study, physically compromised patients affecting OH measures, patients with symptoms of acute illness, e.g., COVID-19, and, subjects who were obese as indicated by the BMI index. Patients who wear any orthodontic or prosthetic appliances, patients who have fixed crowns and bridges, and patients who have any type of implants.

Clinical periodontal examination

All teeth (except for wisdom teeth) were thoroughly examined with the UNC-15 periodontal probe by a calibrated examiner at six locations to measure clinical periodontal parameters, including BOP, PPD, and CAL10, 11, 12, 13.

Blood samples collection

Blood samples were collected from all control group subjects and 50 of the MS patients. After placing the tourniquet around the upper left arm, venous blood was drawn with a 5 ml disposable syringe. It was then transferred to a 6 ml gel & clot activator tube, which, in turn, was kept in a cold box for a short while until it was transported to the centrifuge. The tubes with blood samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 rpm. Following that, the separated serum was placed in plain tubes and stored in a freezer at −20 °C until the time of analysis.

Laboratory procedures

After the sampling procedure was completed, the samples were brought to a specialized laboratory for analysis using ELISA kits. The sample tubes were thawed and left for a few minutes to reach the room temperature of 37 °C. ELISA kits from Elabscience® Biotechnology (Wuhan, China) for TIMP-1 (Catalog no.: E-EL-H0184), MMP-1 (Catalog no.: ELK2183), MMP-2 (Catalog no.: E-EL-H1445) and MMP-9 (Catalog no.: E-EL-H6075), and an ELISA kit from MyBioSource (San Diego, USA) for Gingipain R (Catalog no.: MBS2800343) were used to detect their corresponding biomarker. Manufacturer's instructions were followed for each kit to conduct the analysis.

Statistics analysis

The results were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS), version 25, while the figures were created with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1). In this research, the following statistics were used: Descriptive statistics, including standard deviations, means, maximum and minimum values, and statistical figures and tables. Also, Inferential statistics were done, including a one-way ANOVA test to compare the groups' measurable variables, a Games-Howell test to determine the statistical significance of the two groups, and an unpaired t-test to compare the measured variables between the two groups for CAL. Moreover, finally, Multiple Regression models are used to test the association among the measured variables in each group. In the statistical evaluation, significance levels were determined at the p-value ≤0.05.

The eighty-nine participants, including fifty-four with MS, underwent a periodontal examination. Twenty-two had healthy periodontium, and thirty-two had periodontal disease. Discussion

The present case-control study affords interesting evidence concerning the differential impression of Multiple Sclerosis and Periodontal Diseases on systemic inflammation represented by inflammatory biomarkers included in the current work. The present project aimed to evaluate whether periodontal disease and MS affect the serum levels of the studied biomarkers (TIMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-1, MMP-9, and RgpA). The principal finding of the current study was that MS exerts a significantly stronger independent effect on serum levels of key inflammatory biomarkers, in particular, MMP-9, RgpA, and MMP-1, than PD alone. Furthermore, a pronounced association between MS, elevated RgpA, and elevated clinical periodontal parameters as an indicator for periodontal disease suggests a possible intersection between oral dysbiosis and systemic neuroinflammation. In the current study, serum was analyzed, in contrast to most investigations of periodontal disease that primarily utilize saliva 14, 15, 16, 17. Saliva-based assessments remain limited in conditions associated with systemic disease, whereas analyses of blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are generally preferred, since multiple sclerosis is a systemic condition with a significant impact on specific serum biomarkers.18 The most significant observed result was concerning MMP-9, where MS was associated with a considerable increase in MMP-9 than the elevation associated with PD. This was in line with a well-known published literature stating that MMP-9 is a central player in MS pathogenesis, manipulating the blood-brain barrier disruption and the trafficking of immune cells into the central nervous system.7,19 Despite the fact that periodontal diseases were recognized as a source of systemic MMP-9,20 the regression analysis outcomes specify that the neuroinflammatory environment of MS was an added powerful driver for this protease's systemic presence. Consequently, this might reflect a comprehensive, systemic stimulation of immune cells (e.g., monocytes, neutrophils) in MS, which are key manufacturers of MMP-9, as opposed to the more localized gingival and connective tissue sources in periodontal disease. Similarly, RgpA levels were significantly elevated in MS patients, in comparison to the PD-only group. This finding was particularly interesting in that P. gingivalis and its gingipains were not only virulence factors in periodontitis but have also been implicated in neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.5,21 The higher level of RgpA in MS patients, including those without severe periodontitis, proposes either a discriminating susceptibility to oral colonization with pathobionts subsequently to MS-related immune dysregulation or as a result to immunomodulatory treatments, or a possible role of subclinical oral dysbiosis in driving systemic inflammation relevant to MS. Consequently, this upgrades the oral health from a local concern to a possible modulator of systemic disease activity such as MS.

Conflicting to the anticipated additive comorbid effect of both diseases, the PD + MS group did not reliably show the highest biomarker levels. As the median levels of MMP-9 were elevated in the MS group than in the PD + MS group, however, statistically it was non-significant. This apparent inconsistency may be clarified by the immunomodulatory therapies prescribed for the treatment of the majority of patients who participated in the current MS cohort. These medications probably attenuate the systemic inflammatory response to the dysbiotic periodontal pathogen, restricting the "spillover" of inflammatory mediators such as MMPs into the circulation. However, this would highlight a major consideration for those comorbid populations, as the profound influence of disease-modifying therapies may mask or alter typical inflammatory associations, making the isolated effect of PD harder to discern.

The role of other biomarkers, including MMP-1 and MMP-2, along with TIMP-1, additionally demonstrates the complicated interaction. Since MMP-1 was powerfully associated with both MS and periodontal tissue destruction,22 it probably contributes to both the destruction of connective tissue in the periodontium as well as nerve demyelination in the central nervous system. Yet, the protective role of MMP-2 and TIMP-1 on the clinical attachment loss outlined by the regression model highlights their dual impact in tissue remodeling. This protective elevation in MS may represent an insufficient attempt to compensate for the counterbalance tissue-destructive processes, similar to a dynamic observed in the MMP-9/TIMP-1 imbalance accompanying MS disease activity23.

The results of regression models solidify the association between systemic biomarkers and clinical parameters of periodontal disease. Meanwhile, the association of serum MMP-1, MMP-9, and RgpA with BOP% directly links systemic inflammation to the most objective cardinal sign of gingival inflammation. Furthermore, the ordinal regression identified MS as the robust predictor of periodontal disease severity. This suggests that MS, considered an issue, hypothetically through dysregulation of the systemic immune response and/or as a result of treatment side effects, institutes a significant risk factor for worse periodontal condition.

On the other hand, it is noteworthy to mention that the vast majority of the participants were females. This can be explained by what the Multiple Sclerosis International Federation (MSIF) stated in its 3rd Atlas of MS, that the female-to-male ratio in the Eastern Mediterranean sometimes reached 4:1.24

The contemporary findings of the present study must be interpreted with some limitations. The case-control design impedes causal inferences, as the result cannot illustrate whether periodontal disease contributes to increased MS inflammation and vice versa. The heterogeneousness of the MS and periodontal disease in terms of disease interval, phenotype, and different treatment modalities announces for confounding variability. Considering the level of RgpA as a substitution for P. gingivalis is questionable; therefore, future studies should consider microbial sequencing of subgingival biofilm for direct quantification of periodontal pathogens. Finally, the sample size used for this study was adequate for primary comparisons, but it was limited for robust subgroup analyses. Other limitations include the use of a convenience sampling strategy, which limits the generalizability of the findings. As participants were self-selected (i.e., those willing and available to participate), the sample may be subject to voluntary response bias and may not fully represent the broader population of individuals with MS. Second, the inclusion of smokers, while increasing the real-world applicability of the sample, introduces a substantial confounding variable. Even though smoking was statistically controlled for as a covariate, there is still a chance of residual confounding. Third, the study did not distinguish between gingivitis and periodontitis, combining all types of periodontal inflammation, which affects systemic inflammatory profiles in different ways and possibly changes the health of the periodontal status. The variability of disease-modifying therapies DMTs constitutes a significant confounding factor that could not be entirely controlled for, considering the sample size.

This study has several strengths. Primarily, it addresses an underexplored clinical research gap concerning oral-systemic connections in neurodegenerative disease, offering initial data to guide future prospective studies. Secondly, the presence of a control group and a well-defined sample facilitates a direct comparison of periodontal status between diseased and non-diseased populations. The addition of a control group enhances the validity of the results by offering a benchmark for assessing whether the prevalence of periodontal disease is genuinely increased in multiple sclerosis. Third, the distribution of periodontal disease within the MS group provides sufficient variability for meaningful analysis. Moreover, the inclusion of participants with both healthy and diseased periodontium in both groups enables a comparative assessment of systemic inflammatory burden, allowing differentiation between inflammation attributable to MS versus periodontal pathology.

In conclusion, the current work reveals that MS has a dominant association with systemic levels of inflammatory biomarkers related to tissue destruction, exceeding the association with periodontal disease. The strong association between MS with elevated RgpA and worse periodontal outcomes proposes a convincing link between oral and systemic inflammation that warrants longitudinal investigation. MS may be a significant risk factor for periodontal deterioration. These results advocate for a paradigm shift in MS management, where oral health is recognized as an integral component of comprehensive care. We recommend that neurologists and dental professionals collaborate to ensure rigorous oral hygiene and periodontal monitoring for MS patients. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are essential to determine whether effective periodontal therapy can modulate these systemic inflammatory biomarkers and, ultimately, influence MS disease progression.
