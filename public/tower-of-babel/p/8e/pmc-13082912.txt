Transmission Ratio Distortion Detection Using Microsatellite and Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Markers in Hanwoo of Gyeongnam Province
Authors: Ji‐Yeong Kim, Ho‐Chan Kang, Cheol‐Hyun Myung, Hyun‐Tae Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to identify and compare transmission ratio distortion (TRD) patterns using microsatellite (MS) markers and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers in a Hanwoo population from Gyeongnam Province. The objective was to construct parent‐offspring trios and investigate TRD patterns that may influence mortality and economic traits. MS marker data were obtained from 98,244 individuals, while SNP genotype data were collected from 392 individuals. TRD detection was performed using Bayesian statistical methods, with a threshold of log10(BF) ≥ 2 (BF ≥ 100) to indicate strong evidence. Using MS markers, allelic TRD was detected at ETH225, ETH3, TGLA227, and TGLA53, whereas genotypic TRD was observed across all 11 markers. Using SNP markers, five TRD regions met the strong‐evidence threshold. Functional enrichment analysis was performed on positional candidate genes, but no Gene Ontology terms remained significant after false discovery rate correction (≤ 0.05), likely due to limited statistical power from the small candidate gene set. TRD patterns observed with MS markers were not consistently replicated in the SNP markers, highlighting differences in signal detection across platforms and underscoring the need for refined, integrated analyses. Overall, our results identify genomic regions warranting follow‐up validation in larger datasets and functional assays, rather than supporting pathway‐level conclusions.

Hanwoo is an indigenous Bos taurus native to Korea. It is classified into four types based on coat color and has undergone continuous genetic improvement to enhance meat quality and yield (Lee et al. 2014). Since the implementation of the Livestock Traceability System in 2008, parentage verification in Hanwoo has been performed by extracting DNA from calf tail hair and comparing it to parental genotypes using 11 microsatellite (MS) markers. MS markers are characterized by short tandem repeats of 1–6 base pairs, with advantages such as high polymorphism and reproducibility. In addition, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are distributed throughout the genome and exhibit genetic variation among individuals. High‐density chip analysis enables rapid acquisition of large‐scale SNP information. Accordingly, various studies have utilized high‐density SNP data for linkage disequilibrium analysis, causal gene discovery, and identification of selection signatures (Singh et al. 2021; Lopdell 2023; Kim et al. 2025). Parentage verification chips have also been developed and applied. Parentage verification is based on Mendel's law of segregation, which states that offspring inherit one of two alleles for a given trait from each parent. In Hanwoo breeding, accurate pedigree information is obtained through parentage testing, which is then used to estimate genomic breeding values for selecting superior individuals. This process has led to the accumulation of a large volume of genomic data, enabling diverse research and industrial applications.

Transmission Ratio Distortion (TRD) refers to deviations from expected Mendelian inheritance patterns in the transmission of alleles from parents to offspring (Crow 1999). TRD can arise from various mechanisms during gametogenesis, and identifying the associated alleles, genes, and biological pathways may contribute to enhancing breeding programs (Abdalla et al. 2020; Id‐Lahoucine, Casellas, Suárez‐Vega, et al. 2023). Although deviations from Mendel's laws in the transmission of alleles from heterozygous parents have been reported in various livestock species (Eaves et al. 1999; Casellas et al. 2012; Friocourt et al. 2023), few studies have examined TRD in Hanwoo. Several Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) regions in the cattle QTL database overlap with TRD regions, particularly those associated with reproductive traits such as conception rate, calving ease, stillbirth, and birth index, suggesting that TRD regions may influence economically important traits (Id‐Lahoucine et al. 2019).

With the recent integration of genomic information into livestock improvement programs, it is now possible to construct parent‐offspring trios using individual genomic data. As a result, there is a growing demand in the livestock industry to analyze inheritance patterns through TRD analysis. In particular, genetic improvement in Hanwoo, which primarily relies on artificial insemination, leads to an increased proportion of homozygotes (Kim et al. 2018). The accumulation of lethal alleles during this process may result in reduced reproductive performance, making it essential to assess TRD patterns. Therefore, this study aims to analyze TRD patterns in Hanwoo using both MS and SNP markers to identify genetic patterns and compare them to determine candidate genes with potential biological significance.

Genomic DNA was extracted from tail hair samples of calves born between 2022 and 2024 from 10 regions in Gyeongnam Province. The following samples were excluded: (1) those with paternity or maternity mismatch; (2) cases that were indeterminable; (3) those with complete mismatch; (4) individuals with abnormal pedigree or animal identification numbers; (5) families unable to form parent‐offspring trios; (6) individuals with missing data; and (7) cases where the number of progeny per proven sire was less than 100. After applying these criteria, 127 proven sires, 41,058 dams, and 57,059 progenies were included in the analysis (Table S1). Allele genotypes were determined using 11 MS markers currently used for parentage verification: TGLA227, BM2113, TGLA53, ETH10, SPS115, TGLA126, TGLA122, INRA023, ETH3, ETH225, and BM1824 (Table S2).

For SNP genotyping, (1) individuals with available SNP genotype information for both sire and dam were selected; (2) individuals with call rates below 90% were excluded; and (3) families with five or more offspring were selected. As a result, 14 trios were formed comprising 4 sires and 14 dams, with a total of 374 offspring. SNP genotyping was conducted using the Bovine SNP50K BeadChip versions 2 and 3, as well as the Hanwoo SNP50K BeadChip version 1 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), each containing 54,609, 53,218, and 53,866 SNPs, respectively. For SNP marker filtering, Plink v1.9 software (Purcell et al. 2007) was used to remove markers on autosomes with a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 5%, missing genotype rate over 10%, or Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) less than 10−6. After excluding sex chromosomes, 34,468 markers remained for analysis. To account for the strong linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure in Hanwoo, haplotype blocks were subsequently defined using PLINK v1.9, resulting in a total of 1399 blocks. Blocks with fewer than three SNPs were excluded, and within each block, a tagSNP was selected as the SNP showing the highest mean pairwise LD (r2) with all other SNPs in the block (Table S3).

TRD analysis was conducted according to the biallelic procedure of the Bayesian framework using the TRDscan v2.0 software. A single Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) was run for 110,000 iterations with a sliding window of 20, discarding the initial 10,000 iterations as burn‐in (Id‐Lahoucine et al. 2019). The statistical significance of TRD was assessed using Bayes Factor (BF). To identify inheritance patterns within each region, both allelic parameterization (α, α s, α d) and genotypic parameterization (α g, δg) were analyzed.

In heterozygous parents (A/B), the transmission probability (P) of each allele to the offspring was parameterized in two ways: by using only the total TRD effect (α) in the parent‐unspecific model, or by separating paternal (α s) and maternal (α d) TRD effects in the parent‐specific model (Casellas et al. 2017). Here, α, α s, and α d represent TRD effect parameters, each of which is assumed to follow a uniform distribution (flat prior) between −0.5 and +0.5.

According to Casellas et al. (2014), genotype parameterization introduces additive (α g) and dominance (over‐ or under‐dominance, δg) effect parameters into the model. The probability (p off) of an offspring's genotype from a heterozygote × heterozygote mating is as follows: poffAA=1+αg+δg4 poffAB=1+δg2 poffBB=1−αg−δg4

In these equations, α g is the additive TRD parameter, and δg is the dominance (over‐ or under‐dominance) TRD parameter.

For heterozygote × homozygote matings (e.g., AA × AB), the sum of offspring genotype frequencies should be adjusted to equal 1, that is, poffAA+poffAB+poffBB=1. For example, the adjusted offspring genotype frequencies for AA × AB matings are as follows: poffAA=1+αg−δg2×1+δg2 poffAB=1+δg2×1+αg2 poffBB=0

Genes located within 100 kb upstream and downstream of SNP markers that exhibited significant TRD were selected from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) gene database (http://www.ncbi.nih.gov). The resulting candidate gene list was subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using PANTHER (Thomas et al. 2022) based on Bos taurus as a reference. Statistical significance was assessed at a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05, corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

TRD analysis was performed on offspring from heterozygous parents under the assumption that any observed deviations were not due to genotyping errors. TRD can arise from various biological mechanisms including meiotic drive, gametogenesis errors, embryonic or fetal lethality, imprinting resetting errors, and lethality. Such TRD patterns must be carefully considered in animal breeding programs as they may negatively affect genetic diversity or reduce genetic gain in reproductive traits (Perdomo‐González et al. 2023). Unlike SNP markers, MS markers used for parentage testing in Hanwoo are determined based on the length of repeat sequences. Thus, they may be recognized as having more than two alleles. Accordingly, to fit the TRDscan format, alleles inherited from parents were rearranged as separate markers and converted into a biallelic format. Significant evidence was determined with log10BF ≥ 1 (BF ≥ 10) as a reference (Jeffreys 1998). BF is a standard Bayesian tool for model comparison; according to the Jeffreys scale, BF ≥ 10 indicates significant evidence, while BF ≥ 100 indicates strong evidence. A condensed summary of MS‐marker TRD results in Hanwoo with strong evidence (log10(BF) ≥ 2; BF ≥ 100) is presented in Table 1. Table S4 provides the full MS‐marker results meeting the threshold of log10(BF) ≥ 1 (BF ≥ 10), including offspring genotype distributions by mating type, TRD patterns, and TRD effect estimates used for parentage testing. TRD calculates the over‐ or under‐transmission of specific alleles or genotypes from parents to offspring. In allele‐based TRD analysis, the α value ranges from −0.5 to 0.5, with α = 0 indicating equal transmission of both alleles. In genotype‐based TRD analysis, α g ranges from −1 to 1; α g < 0 implies a preference for A allele‐based genotypes, and α g > 0 implies a preference for B allele‐based genotypes. α g = 0 represents normal Mendelian segregation. The δg parameter indicates TRD of the heterozygous genotype (A/B), where δg > 0 signifies over‐dominance and δg < 0 signifies under‐dominance. In this analysis, allelic TRD was detected at ETH225 (α = 0.21, α s = 0.50), ETH3 (α = 0.44, α s = 0.49), and TGLA53 (α = 0.02). The coexistence of overall and sire‐ or dam‐specific TRD patterns suggests that transmission distortion at these loci may not be solely attributable to parental sex‐specific effects, but rather reflects a combination of general and parental biases in allele transmission. Genotypic TRD patterns were observed across the 11 MS makers, the range of genotypic‐based α values confirmed by MS markers was −0.87 to 0.42 with log10BF values from 2.00 to 25.20. These results suggest that multiple MS markers show strong evidence (log10BF ≥ 2) of TRD under both allelic and genotypic models. Within the analyzed population, certain alleles exhibited statistically significant transmission probabilities to offspring.

Cattle tend to exhibit relatively large linkage disequilibrium blocks, with haplotype blocks consisting of 3–4 SNPs averaging 10 kb (Villa‐Angulo et al. 2009). In this study, 698 haplotype blocks consisting of at least three SNPs were identified. Five SNP‐derived TRD regions met the strong‐evidence threshold (log10(BF) ≥ 2; BF ≥ 100) and are summarized in Table 2. The complete list of SNP‐derived TRD regions meeting log10(BF) ≥ 1 (BF ≥ 10), including those in Table 2, is provided in Table S5. The genome‐wide visualization using a sliding window size of 20 demonstrated that these TRD signals were not confined to particular chromosomes but were broadly distributed across the genome (Figure S1). This widespread distribution suggests that diverse genomic regions in Hanwoo may be involved in allele transmission distortion, highlighting potential targets for further investigation of candidate genes and their functional relevance to reproduction and selection. Initially, TRD analysis was conducted using MS markers, followed by SNP marker‐based TRD analysis in individuals where parent‐offspring trios could be reconstructed based on SNP genotype. However, TRD signals within ±100 kb of the MS marker locations were not significant. This suggests that the TRD observed in the MS marker analysis was not replicated in adjacent SNP markers, possibly due to SNP density, genetic discordance, or resolution differences between markers. MS markers are often located in or near repetitive or regulatory regions (Thalamuthu et al. 2005; Ellegren 2004), which may be subject to meiotic or postzygotic selection, whereas nearby SNPs might not tag the same functional variants. Commercial SNP arrays are typically biased toward common variants with high MAF (Zhang et al. 2018), which may limit their utility. Alternatively, biological factors may underlie the lack of replication. Thus, the observed TRD patterns could reflect locus‐specific biological phenomena that are not detectable by surrounding SNPs.

Only 392 individuals (14 trios and 374 offspring) were retained for SNP‐based TRD analysis due to stringent criteria, including MS‐confirmed parentage, complete parental genotypes, absence of outlier carcass phenotypes, and families with at least five offspring. Although this reduced sample size limits detection power, the retained trios represent multiple sires and dams across different families, maintaining genetic diversity and minimizing family‐specific bias. Haplotype‐based analyses were also performed to improve detection of TRD signals, providing complementary information to SNP‐by‐SNP analyses.

To validate the reproducibility of MS‐based TRD signals and their association with nearby SNP markers, high‐density SNP panels or sequencing‐based precision analyses may be required. Gene function analysis based on TRD characteristics in Hanwoo could help identify candidate lethal or production‐related genes.

Using MS marker locations, 40 candidate genes were identified; however, functional exploration was limited. Although no significant GO enrichment was observed, the candidate genes CATSPERD and PLCXD3 may still have biological relevance to TRD. CATSPERD encodes a subunit of the sperm‐specific CATSPER channel complex. CATSPER4 has been associated with male infertility and is expressed in the male reproductive tract, where it mediates sperm hyperactivation, sperm competition, and gamete interaction (Johnson et al. 2017). Variants in this gene could therefore influence gametic transmission through altered sperm function. PLCXD3 is expressed in germ cells of mouse and human testes and is involved in normal spermatogenesis (Li et al. 2016). PLCXD3 is a phospholipase‐related gene highly expressed in testicular tissue and may participate in phosphoinositide signaling during fertilization or early embryonic development, suggesting possible involvement in postzygotic selection. In contratst, only five SNP‐derived TRD regions met the strong‐evidence threshold (log10(BF) ≥ 2). GO enrichment yielded no significant terms after FDR correction (≤ 0.05), so we did not draw pathway‐level conclusions. Nevertheless, TRD patterns in beef cattle may be transmitted in a sire‐specific or dam‐specific manner (Casellas et al. 2017). In Holstein cattle, TRD was detected in regions containing recessive lethal alleles, validating the TRD approach (Id‐Lahoucine, Casellas, Suárez‐Vega, et al. 2023). In Nellore cattle, TRD analysis helped identify lethal alleles and emphasized its importance for enhancing production efficiency (Rodrigues et al. 2025). In Angus cattle, a region significantly affecting heifer pregnancy rate was discovered with TRD associated with up to a 15% reduction in conception rate, highlighting its biological relevance (Id‐Lahoucine, Casellas, Lu, et al. 2023). In a study across seven Spanish beef cattle breeds, dam‐specific TRD differed by breed, while common markers explaining sire‐specific TRD were identified (Casellas et al. 2017). TRD patterns were also identified on sex chromosomes, revealing opposite patterns between male and female offspring and suggesting their potential as indicators of X–Y recombination. A sex‐specific modeling approach helped identify candidate genes related to reproductive control (Id‐Lahoucine et al. 2022). Furthermore, if sufficient information is available from offspring of non‐distorted parents, the parental origin of TRD can be inferred. Thus, when exploring parent‐specific TRD, modeling should account for paternal and maternal inheritance paths (Id‐Lahoucine, Casellas, Suárez‐Vega, et al. 2023). In this study, TRD patterns differed between MS‐ and SNP‐based analyses. Genes located near the TRD regions include candidates previously associated with production traits, consistent with potential sire‐ or dam‐specific influences in Hanwoo. These immune‐ and reproduction‐related genes should be considered positional candidates for validation in larger datasets and functional assays.

Hanwoo selection programs have focused on enhancing meat quality to meet domestic market demands. Unlike many other cattle breeds, Hanwoo breeding heavily relies on the use of proven sires with superior genetic merit and widespread artificial insemination. Such intensive selection and extensive use of a limited number of male lines may have increased the frequency of specific haplotypes and reduced overall genetic diversity. Consequently, the rising level of homozygosity in Hanwoo warrants careful consideration, particularly regarding the expression of recessive alleles and fertility‐related traits. The TRD patterns identified in this study may serve as foundational data for investigating Hanwoo‐specific genetic factors related to reproductive fitness, disease susceptibility, or embryonic lethality. Further functional validation of genes located within TRD‐associated regions is required. Moreover, these results provide valuable insights into how artificial selection and demographic structure have influenced allele transmission patterns within the Hanwoo population.
