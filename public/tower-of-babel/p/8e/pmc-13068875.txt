Reconstituted phospholipid membrane-coated Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement nanoparticles block ferroptotic trigger waves
Authors: Runhan Zhao, Shanshan Zhang, Yanran Huang, Xiao Qu, Jingtao Xu, Jun Zhang, Wenguo Cui, Xiaoji Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerroptotic trigger waves (FTWs) mediate the long-distance propagation of transient cell-death signals across tissues, resulting in spatially correlated, large-scale cellular dysfunction and programmed demise. Blocking of FTWs represents a promising therapeutic strategy for iron accumulation-related pathologies. Based on spatial characteristics of FTWs, this study developed a nanocoupled system (Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs) integrating “intracellular-membrane-cell population” multi-physical level to block FTWs, aiming to efficiently block FTWs through precise cellular targeting, resistance of target cell membrane lipid peroxidation, and intracellular Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement. This study employed coordination chemistry synthesis combined with short-range electrostatic interactions to prepare Mg/Ce-MOF with toxic oxygen radical scavenging enzyme activity and Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement capability; subsequently, the composite nanoparticles were coated with monounsaturated fatty acid phospholipids (MUFA-PLs) to endow them with capabilities of precise targeting and cell membranes MUFA-PLs ratio enhancing. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs achieving near 100% equimolar Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement within 4 h, significantly increasing MUFA-PLs of target cell membrane and enabling efficient Mg/Ce-MOF delivery, over threefold improvement in ferroptosis resistance and redox systems compared to controls, and efficient bone mass enhancement with rapid defect healing in ovariectomized (OVX) mice/rats over 8 weeks. This FTWs-blocking strategy provides a paradigm for treating iron accumulation-related diseases.Keywords: Ferroptotic trigger waves, Iron accumulation, Nanoparticle, Monounsaturated fatty acid phospholipids, Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement

Introduction

The discovery of ferroptotic trigger waves (FTWs) provides a theoretical explanation for the ability of transient death signals to overcome spatial constraints and induce coordinated cellular demise under conditions of iron accumulation. The basic mechanism is a ferroptosis event in one cell or small cell cluster, released reactive oxygen species (ROS) mediated signal propagates through human cells over distances of more than 5 mm at constant speed of about 5.5 μm/min leading to large spatially correlated cell death [1]. The initiation of FTWs exhibits stochastic characteristics, which can be attributed to the heterogeneity in lipid peroxidation levels among different tissue regions and variations in cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis [[2], [3], [4]]. The propagation of FTWs is a cascade of spatially correlated cell death events, interventions designed to stop stochastic initiation and 3-dimensional propagation of FTWs could effectively prevent large-scale cell death under pathological iron accumulation, offering a new treatment for associated diseases.

The activation of FTWs typically originates from elevated intracellular Fe2+ levels that drive the Fenton reaction to generate ·OH, which rapidly oxidizes structurally unstable polyunsaturated fatty acid phospholipids (PUFA-PLs) and initiates a self-amplifying lipid peroxidation chain reaction, ultimately leading to irreversible cellular dysfunction and death [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. In contrast, structurally stable monounsaturated fatty acid phospholipids (MUFA-PLs) are resistant to lipid peroxidation [9,10]. Exogenous or endogenous supplementation of MUFAs can increase the proportion of MUFA-PLs in the cell membrane and confer a form of “ferroptosis-resistant armor” to cells [10,11]. However, even at the experimental cell level, supplementation with exogenous MUFAs may require more than 10 h to effectively suppress lipid peroxidation, largely due to the rate limitation of ACSL3-mediated MUFA-PL synthesis [9].

The propagation of FTWs depends on ROS-mediated spatial coupling between cells and the ROS bistability (low/high ROS steady states) of neighboring cells [1,12]. Ferroptotic cells release large amounts of ROS, which act on adjacent cell populations that exhibit ROS bistability. Due to abnormal ROS feedback loops, these cells function as relay amplifiers of death signals, thereby enabling the non-attenuated propagation of FTWs. In this process, the GSH-mediated double-negative feedback loop forms the molecular basis for the switching of ROS bistability [12]. Although supplementation with GSH or upregulation of its biosynthetic enzymes (GCL/GSS) can partially restore redox homeostasis [[13], [14], [15]], pharmacological or genetic interventions may introduce unpredictable off-target risks [[16], [17], [18]]. Recently, the concept of Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement has garnered increasing attention. As a highly biocompatible and low-toxicity divalent cation, Mg2+ can compete with Fe2+ for binding sites on key intracellular proteins or enzymes, thereby disrupting iron-dependent oxidative stress responses [19,20]. Simultaneously, Mg2+ serves as a crucial coenzyme factor that can efficiently promote GSH synthesis [16,21,22]. However, implementing Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement strategies also requires overcoming challenges such as low in vivo Mg2+ delivery efficiency, insufficient targeting, and potential ion equilibrium disruption. Overall, effective inhibition of FTW activation and propagation requires targeted strategies that address three core requirements: intracellular Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement, elevation of membrane MUFA-PLs, and suppression of ROS bistability in neighboring cells.

Extensive studies have demonstrated that abnormal iron accumulation occurs in various diseases [[23], [24], [25]], providing a basis for the activation and propagation of FTWs. Among these conditions, iron accumulation has been identified as an independent risk factor for postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) and has therefore received considerable attention [26,27]. Accordingly, PMOP was selected as the disease model in this study for mechanistic investigation and performance validation of the biomaterials. Here, from the novel perspective of inhibiting the activation and propagation of FTWs, this study, for the first time, designed and developed a multi-level nano-coupling system to block FTWs (Scheme 1). This system fulfills critical functions including precise cellular targeting, resistance of lipid peroxidation improvement, and intracellular Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement. Specifically, we precisely modulated the physicochemical properties of Ce-based nanocatalysts (Ce-MOF) and magnesium hexacyanoferrate (MgHCF) through coordination chemistry synthesis combined with density functional theory. Simultaneously, short-range electrostatic interactions induced nanoparticle interface coupling to form MgHCF/Ce-MOF. Then, using the self-assembled capability of phospholipids, we embedded the composite nanoparticles with bone marrow stromal cell (BMSCs) membranes enriched with MUFA-PLs and formed Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs. Based on homotactic recruitment and membrane fusion, Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs allows rapid increase of MUFA-PLs proportion in targeted cells and efficient delivery of Mg/Ce-MOF. The structural ordering of MgHCF enhances its affinity for Fe2+. Thus, Mg/Ce-MOF can effectively absorb intracellular Fe2+ and release Mg2+ which further promotes GSH production. Metal multivalency cycling of Ce-MOF possesses dual enzyme-like activity mimicking natural superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) to aid in clearing ROS.

Schematic illustration of the preparation of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs and promoting endogenous bone regeneration by blocking FTWs.

In vitro experiments show that nanoparticles reduce intracellular Fe2+ concentrations of targeted BMSCs, increase the MUFA-PLs proportion in membranes, and maintain GSH homeostasis, removing the activation of FTWs and its 3D propagation. In vivo experiments showed that nanoparticles restore total bone mass of ovariectomized (OVX) mice and complete healing of femoral condyle defects in OVX rats within 8 weeks. In conclusion, this nanoparticle system enables bone regeneration in the OVX model by effectively eliminating FTWs, which is a promising novel therapy that could be applied to other iron-accumulation related disorders.

Results

Ferroptotic trigger waves induced by iron accumulation constitute a critical risk factor for multiple disorders

Extensive studies in both humans and animal models have demonstrated that iron accumulation not only increases the incidence of various diseases but also serves as an independent risk factor for PMOP [28,29]. In this study, an OVX mouse model was established to mimic the postmenopausal state. H&E and Masson staining revealed a marked reduction in trabecular bone number in the femoral tissue of the OVX group compared with the Sham group (Fig. 1A). The 3D reconstruction and quantitative analysis of the distal femur by micro-CT further confirmed a significant decrease in bone mass in OVX mice (S1A-E). Meanwhile, the prussian blue staining, together with the measurements of iron levels in bone tissue and serum, demonstrated that mice in the OVX group were in a state of iron accumulation (Fig. 1A–C). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression levels of key iron metabolism–related genes in the bone tissue of OVX mice also differed significantly from those in Sham mice (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, based on the GTEx database, we constructed a human tissue expression atlas of key iron metabolism–related genes and found that these genes exhibited distinct expression patterns across different human tissues, with significant enrichment in female bone tissue (Fig. 1E). Finally, we analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data derived from bone marrow tissue of patients with PMOP [30]. Based on dimensionality reduction clustering analysis, we identified three distinct phenotypes of BMSCs from multiple cell types (Fig. 1F–H, S2). By analyzing the expression patterns and correlations of iron metabolism– and osteogenesis-related genes in BMSCs, we found that BMSCs characterized by a stable redox system and balanced iron metabolism exhibited enhanced osteogenic differentiation potential (Fig. 1I and J).

Under physiological conditions, organisms maintain systemic and cellular iron homeostasis by tightly regulating iron uptake, storage, and efflux, thereby enabling FTWs to function as a powerful tool for tissue remodeling [1,31,32]. In the PMOP bone microenvironment, aberrant iron accumulation provides a permissive condition for the activation and propagation of FTWs. Based on these findings, we propose a novel hypothesis: iron accumulation within the PMOP bone microenvironment induces the activation and propagation of FTWs, leading to impaired osteogenic differentiation and cell death of BMSCs, which may represent a previously unrecognized pathogenic mechanism underlying osteoporosis (Fig. 1K).

In summary, the development of nanomaterials specifically targeting FTWs is urgently needed, among which the ion-regulatory capacity and nanozyme-like activity of Metall–Organic Framework (MOF) merit particular attention [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]].

Preparation and Characterization of Mg/Ce-MOF

Leveraging the spatial propagation dynamics of FTWs across cellular populations, this investigation aims to suppress their activation and propagation within tissues through intracellular Fe2+ clearance, enhancement of ferroptosis resistance, and maintenance of GSH homeostasis in adjacent cells.

To this end, we engineered a nanoscale multilayer coupled system-Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs, integrating multi-physical level interventions at ‘intracellular, membrane, and cell population.’ In this study, we precisely controlled the structure and properties of nanoparticles by referencing published literature on molecular and coordination chemistry techniques combined with density functional theory (DFT) [[37], [38], [39]], which obtained synthesis of morphologically uniform MgHCF nanocatalysts (average particle diameter of 29 nm, Fig. 2A, S3A, D) and Ce-MOF nanozymes (average particle diameter of 338 nm, Fig. 2B–S3B, E). The MgHCF component, characterized by the inherent instability of its Prussian blue structure, exhibits pronounced affinity for Fe2+, thereby enabling specific adsorption of intracellular Fe2+ while concurrently releasing Mg2+. The Ce3+/Ce4+ valence state cycling within the Ce-MOF structure confers dual SOD/CAT enzyme activity, facilitating synergistic ROS scavenging; Subsequently, to achieve functional integration of the nanoparticles, we utilized short-range electrostatic coupling mediated by the cationic polymer polyethyleneimine (PEI) [40], resulting in the synthesis of Mg/Ce-MOF composite nanoparticles with an average particle diameter of 363 nm (Fig. 2C–S3C). We also performed dynamic light scattering (DLS), particle size, and thermogravimetric (TG) thermodynamic measurements that confirmed coupling of MgHCF with Ce-MOF to Mg/Ce-MOF (Fig. 2D and E). One key issue of inhibiting FTW activation is whether the cyclic potential energy of Fe2+ adsorption and Mg2+ release in the material fulfills thermodynamically spontaneous adsorption criteria. Analysis of X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and elemental mapping data for MgHCF, Ce-MOF, and Mg/Ce-MOF (Fig. 2F–H, S4) demonstrates that the Fe2+ adsorption energy is −2.31 eV, with a Mg2+ release barrier of 0.42 eV. These findings indicated that the internal structure of the functional nanoparticles provides sufficient energy for in situ Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement, thereby establishing a robust physicochemical foundation for effective Fe2+ adsorption and Mg2+ release.

Furthermore, the Ce3+/Ce4+ valence state cycling within the Ce-MOF structure, characterized by a redox energy differential of ΔE ∼0.18 eV (S4), indicates its robust redox capabilities, facilitating the effective elimination of cytotoxic ROS [41,42]. Subsequently, we corroborated through a suite of characterizations, including zeta potential analysis, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 2I–K), that the short-range mediation by PEI did not alter the characteristic spectral peaks or crystalline structure of the material [[43], [44], [45]]. To evaluate the ion displacement dynamics of Mg2+ and Fe2+ in Mg/Ce-MOF, we incubated the material in complete α-MEM medium (37 °C, incubated for 4 h at 110 r/min), and the inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis of metal ion concentrations at various time points demonstrated that the synthesized Mg/Ce-MOF possesses exceptional cation displacement capabilities, enabling rapid Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement, even in the presence of biomolecular interference from fetal bovine serum (Fig. 2L).

Finally, the stability of the nanoparticles was further evaluated. First, after the addition of other metal ions, including Mn2+, Zn2+, and Ca2+, the nanoparticles maintained their Fe2+ adsorption capacity (S5A). Moreover, when fresh nanoparticles were intermittently added at days 7 and 14, the Fe2+–Mg2+ displacement performance remained consistent and resulted in a sustained reduction in Fe2+ levels (S5B). During the 14-day observation period, the CAT- and SOD-like activities, average particle size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential of the nanoparticles showed no significant changes (S5C–F). These findings indicate that the functional nanoparticles developed in this study maintained stable physicochemical properties and biological activity under the tested conditions.

However, the development of a functional nanoscale system capable of multidimensional inhibition of the “activation-propagation” dynamics of FTWs requires two critical functionalities: precise delivery of Mg/Ce-MOF and rapid increment of MUFA-PLs at the cellular membrane.

Cellular membranes enriched with MUFA-PLs effectively counteract lipid peroxidation and facilitate the efficient delivery of mg/Ce-MOF

The abundance of PUFA-PLs in cellular membranes constitutes a key requirement for FTWs activation, with its content limiting the wave's propagation range [1]. Previous studies have demonstrated that exogenous supplementation of MUFA can competitively displace PUFA-PLs from cell membranes [9,11]. Our results further confirm that adding oleic acid (a type of MUFA) during cell culture could increase the proportion of MUFA-PLs on the target cell membranes and reach its peak value around 10 h (Fig. 3A, S6A-B). Due to the stable nature of the MUFA-PLs' hydrocarbon structure, cell populations with a high proportion of MUFA-PLs in their membranes could effectively resist lipid peroxidation [11]. The results of this study indicate that oleic acid treatment for 10 h effectively reverses lipid peroxidation induced by the iron-accumulating microenvironment (Fig. 3B and C) and prevents cell death events at both the subsequent 24-h and 48-h time points (Fig. 3D and E).

The suppression of lipid peroxidation by oleic acid correlates with the MUFA-PLs ratio. Previous studies confirmed that exogenous oleic acid supplementation has a time-dependent effect on the improvement of cell membrane MUFA-PLs, and its primary mechanism is the catalytic effect of Acyl-Coenzyme A synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3) on free MUFAs [46,47]. Therefore, this gene and a series of complex molecular mechanisms regulate the important process of free MUFAs incorporation into the cell membrane (Fig. 3F). Based on these findings, we propose an intriguing hypothesis: Could the use of oleic acid-treated cell membranes to encapsulate biomaterials enable precise cellular targeting and a rapid increase of the MUFA-PLs ratio in cell membranes (bypassing the ACSL3 catalytic pathway) through homotactic recruitment and membrane fusion mechanisms? To test this, we extracted MUFA-PL-rich cell membranes from BMSCs incubated with oleic acid for 10 h and employed a membrane extrusion strategy to coat them onto Mg/Ce-MOF, aiming to prepare Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results revealed that, compared to the encapsulated state (Fig. 2C), the surface of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs was covered by a membrane-like layer (Fig. 3G). Electrophoretic analysis demonstrated that the material retained membrane protein bands consistent with those of BMSCs (S7), thereby providing a structural basis for close-range recognition and adhesion between homologous cells. These results validate the successful preparation of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs.

Additionally, we evaluated the performance of targeting and the MUFA-PLs ratio increase from the encapsulation material. Through co-incubation of BMSCs with Mg/Ce-MOF and Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs, we observed that in the MUFA-PLs (+) group, Mg/Ce-MOF began entering the cytoplasm as early as 3 h post-incubation, whereas the MUFA-PLs (−) group showed only minimal biomaterial accumulation at 12 h, likely due to cellular phagocytosis (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, we observed a significant positive correlation between the proportion of MUFA-PLs on the BMSC cell membrane and the amount of Mg/Ce-MOF internalized by the cells (Fig. 3I). Notably, membrane MUFA-PLs reached a plateau at approximately 6 h, which was markedly faster than in the group treated with oleic acid alone (S6C). Overall, these results confirm that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs exhibit reliable targeting performance and rapidly increase the proportion of MUFA-PLs on the membrane of target cells by bypassing the ACSL3-catalyzed pathway.

Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs restore and maintain the redox system of BMSCs in iron-accumulated microenvironment to block the FTWs

Prior to conducting cellular experiments, we performed biocompatibility testing using BMSCs and rat neuronal cell lines (PC-12) as test models. Live/dead staining and CCK-8 assay results confirmed no significant differences in cell activity among groups on days 1 and 3 of incubation with different nanomaterials (S8). Notably, we observed a significant enhancement in the proliferative activity of BMSCs in the MgHCF and Mg/Ce-MOF groups (S8A, C), whereas no such effect was detected in the PC-12 cell line (S8B, D). These experiments successfully demonstrate that the bio-therapeutic strategy designed in this study induces no apparent cytotoxic effects and possesses proliferation-promoting capabilities for target cells.

Fe2+, as a key executor of ferroptosis, plays a pivotal role in the activation of FTWs. In contrast, Mg2+, serving as an essential cofactor, is crucial for restoring the cellular redox system [21,22]. Quantitative fluorescence analysis in the present study revealed that treatment with the nanomaterials markedly reduced intracellular Fe2+ levels while concomitantly increasing Mg2+ levels (Fig. 4A, B, G, H). Moreover, the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis represents a canonical anti-ferroptosis pathway: SLC7A11 promotes cystine uptake for GSH synthesis, and GSH serves as the substrate for GPX4 to eliminate lipid peroxides [4,48]. Immunofluorescence staining, GSH quantification, RT-qPCR, and Western blot analyses consistently demonstrated significant upregulation of SLC7A11, FTH1, GPX4, and GSH in the nanomaterial-treated group (Fig. 4C,I-M and S9A-B), thereby confirming the restoration of the redox system in BMSCs. Although iron metabolism is regulated by multiple proteins, and the loss of Fe2+ is regulated by complex mechanisms and is difficult to pinpoint precisely, our results still show that the intracellular Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement strategy is successful.

ROS act as key executors of ferroptosis and play a critical role in the propagation of FTWs [49]. In this study, fluorescence analyses using MitoSOX and general ROS probes demonstrated that nanomaterial treatment markedly reduced ROS levels in BMSCs within the iron accumulation microenvironment (Fig. 4D,N, O). In addition, we also used the Transwell-based coculture system to simulate the propagation process of FTWs (S10). The C11-BODIPY staining showed that BMSCs pretreated with the nanomaterials effectively resisted ROS transmission–induced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4D,P). Collectively, these findings indicate that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs induce a low-ROS steady state in BMSCs, thereby effectively suppressing the propagation of FTWs. In general, ferroptosis cell death manifests predominantly as mitochondrial injury. Based on TEM, we observed significantly fewer instances of mitochondrial atrophy and cristae reduction in the material group (Fig. 4E), indicating a gradual recovery of mitochondrial function. This result was also confirmed by JC-1 staining (Fig. 4F,Q). In summary, our study successfully demonstrated that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs effectively block the activation and propagation of BMSCs-FTWs.

Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs effectively restore the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs in iron-accumulated microenvironment

BMSCs are involved in bone regeneration process, directly formed into osteoblasts and synthesized bone matrix [50]. Research suggests iron accumulation can disrupt cell redox balance, leading to severe oxidative stress and inflammation, which inhibit osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [28,51]. In this work, we found that treatment with Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs could significantly increase the intensity of Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, which indicated that the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs is significantly restored (Fig. 5A, B, D, E). Meanwhile, the immunofluorescence, RT-qPCR, and WB showed that FAC-induced reduction of osteogenic markers (RUNX2, OPN, and OCN) was reversed after treatment with nanomaterials (Fig. 5C, F–J).

Accumulating evidence indicates that disruption of redox homeostasis in BMSCs is a key contributor to impaired osteogenic differentiation [52,53]. In our study, the reduction of the intracellular labile Fe2+ pool, together with an increase in Mg2+ with antioxidative potential, was associated with the restoration of redox balance in BMSCs. In parallel, Mg2+ could also exert its intrinsic bone-promoting effects by promoting the upregulation of osteogenic transcriptional programs and enhancing extracellular matrix mineralization. Leveraging these features, Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs not only suppressed the activation and propagation of FTWs but also shifted the intracellular ionic milieu toward a pro-osteogenic state through its Fe2+ – Mg2+ exchange property (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these findings support the potential utility of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs for treating PMOP.

Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs effectively restore the redox system in bone tissue of OVX mice and promote bone mass recovery

PMOP is a common metabolic bone disease of postmenopausal women, characterized by reduced bone mineral density, poor bone microstructure, and increased bone fragility leading to high fracture risk [54,55]. In order to assess systemic targeting and FTWs modulation of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs in vivo, we established an OVX mouse model and injected PBS via the tail vein as the blank group, and the other groups injected Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs, MgHCF@MUFA-PLs, and Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs once per week (Fig. 6A). At week 8, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were harvested for H&E staining. Histological examination revealed no detectable organ damage in any group (S11), confirming the favorable biocompatibility of our nanoparticles.

To assess the biological performance of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs, bone tissue was obtained after eight tail vein injections for micro-CT 3D reconstruction and histological analysis to assess the bone volume restoration and ferroptosis suppression. Based on micro-CT 3D reconstruction results, we found differences in the bone volume and microstructure between groups, Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs group showing the most significant bone recovery effect (Fig. 6B). We then quantitatively assessed micro-CT results for bone tissue with key metrics, such as bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular bone thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular bone separation (Tb.Sp). The results indicated that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs exhibited the most effective bone regeneration, with BMD values (0.2091 ± 0.0185 g/cm3), BV/TV values (23.1968 ± 3.0540 %), Tb.Th (88.8439 ± 3.3979 μm), and Tb.Sp (0.2121 ± 0.0314 mm) were 2.75, 2.908, 2.302, and 0.402 times higher than the control group, respectively (Fig. 6C).

Additionally, histopathological findings revealed that in the bone tissue of OVX mice, the regular trabecular network structure was disrupted, extensively infiltrated by adipose tissue, with minimal formation of new bone tissue. Surprisingly, in the treatment group, these adverse pathological changes gradually diminished. Among these, the bone tissue condition in the Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs group was significantly better than the other three groups under macroscopic examination and closely resembled normal bone tissue (Fig. 6D). Concurrently, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that both GPX4 and OPN expressions progressed in the desired direction (Fig. 6E, G, H), and MDA, SOD, and GSH levels gradually returned to normal ranges (Fig. 6F). Finally, we evaluated the bone-targeting capability of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs through in vivo imaging in small animals. Results demonstrated that encapsulated nanoparticles effectively accumulated in bone tissue regions 6 h after tail vein injection (Fig. 6I).

In summary, our in vivo data confirm that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs precisely and efficiently block FTWs activation and propagation, thereby effectively promoting bone tissue regeneration in iron-accumulated bone microenvironments.

Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs effectively restore the redox system in bone tissue of OVX rats and promote bone defect healing

Globally, numerous patients suffer from bone defects each year due to trauma, infection, tumors, and other causes [56]. In general, the bone tissue self-repairs minor bone defects by itself [57], but due to poor-quality bone and slow bone healing characteristic of PMOP patients, the bone tissue self-repair cannot repair larger defects alone [58]. Therefore, developing bone graft substitutes that meet clinical needs has become a key focus in bone repair research, and the performance of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs in repairing localized bone defects is worthy of anticipation.

To comprehensively evaluate the bone-promoting effect of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs on localized bone defects, a cylindrical bone defect (diameter: 2.5 mm; depth: 5 mm) was made in the femoral condyle of OVX rats according to the standard procedure [59]. In the treatment process, we utilized commercial gelatin sponges as carriers, with the PBS-loaded group serving as the control. The remaining treatment groups were loaded with Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs, MgHCF@MUFA-PLs, and Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs, respectively (Fig. 7A). At the 4th and 8th weeks post-implantation, femoral tissue samples were collected for micro-CT scanning and histopathological analysis, respectively. Based on the 3D reconstruction results from micro-CT, we observed a gradually incremental trend in bone defect repair across all groups at both 4th- and 8th-week (Fig. 7B). Simultaneously, the micro-CT results underwent quantitative analysis, with continued focus on indicators such as BV/TV, BMD, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp. Results indicate that the Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs group demonstrated the most effective bone repair. At the 4th week, this group exhibited a BMD value of 0.6652 ± 0.0256 g/cm3, a BV/TV ratio of 16.1102 ± 0.7110 %, Tb.Th (240.8069 ± 4.6471 μm), and Tb.Sp (1.2664 ± 0.0937 mm) were 1.308, 2.314, 1.667, and 0.559 times higher than those of the control group, respectively. At the 8th week, this group's BMD value (0.7532 ± 0.0183 g/cm3), BV/TV ratio (45.1984 ± 1.1861 %), Tb.Th (271.7385 ± 12.2096 μm), and Tb.Sp (0.6613 ± 0.0769 mm) were 1.338, 3.581, 1.766, and 0.327 times those of the control group, respectively (Fig. 7C). Concurrently, histopathological analysis of bone tissue revealed more abundant trabecular bone and newly formed bone tissue in the bone defect area of the Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs group (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a trend consistent with that observed in bone tissue of OVX mice, namely a significant increase in osteogenic genes and ferroptosis-inhibiting genes (Fig. 7E–G). Finally, the major organs were collected from the rats at week 8, and the results consistently confirmed the reliable biocompatibility of the biomaterials used in this study (S12).

In summary, the nanoparticles exhibited in this study not only promote the recovery of systemic bone mass but also exhibit outstanding biological performance in the repair of localized bone defects.

Transcriptome based pseudotime analysis confirms mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs possess excellent proliferation promoting and FTWs blocking capabilities

To study biological effects of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs in iron-accumulating BMSCs, we performed transcriptomic sequencing at 0, 2, and 4 days after treatment to discover possible mechanisms through dynamic analysis. PCA revealed different expression profiles among BMSCs at 3 time points (Fig. 8A), suggesting time-dependent biological effects of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs. This behavior was further confirmed by different expression honeycomb plots between treatment groups (Fig. 8B).

Transcriptome sequencing-based pseudo-time analysis confirms the outstanding proliferation-promoting and ferroptosis-inhibiting performance from Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs. A) PCA analysis of all samples. B) Differential expression analysis honeycomb volcano plot at different time points. C) GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes. D) Pseudo-time analysis heatmap of differentially expressed genes. E–G) GSEA analysis at different time points. H) Fluorescence images of EdU proliferation assays in Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs-treated and control BMSCs. I) WB images of AMPK and MAPK marker proteins in Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs-treated and control BMSCs. J) Schematic diagram of the molecular mechanism by which Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs activate AMPK and MAPK pathways. (n = 3).

Subsequently, we performed GO and KEGG analyses on differentially expressed genes to roughly explore which signaling pathways were involved in regulating the biological functions of BMSCs. The results indicate that these pathways are primarily concentrated in the cell cycle, osteogenesis-related pathways, GSH metabolism-related pathways, and ferroptosis-related pathways (Fig. 8C). Next, to delve deeper into the dynamic changes within these pathways, we employed a pseudo-temporal analysis algorithm to classify differentially expressed genes into three subgroups (C1, C2, and C3). The expression changes within these subgroups can be mapped to the dynamic alterations in their corresponding signaling pathways. Results indicate that during this 4-day period, the cell cycle and AMPK signaling pathway remained continuously activated, while the ferroptosis pathway was persistently suppressed (Fig. 8D). Combined with subsequent GSEA analysis, we appear to have unraveled the regulatory mysteries of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs on BMSCs: while continuously suppressing ferroptosis, it promotes cell proliferation via the MAPK pathway in the early stages and facilitates BMSC osteogenic differentiation under sustained AMPK pathway activation (Fig. 8E–G). Research indicates that the AMPK signaling pathway enhances cellular GSH synthesis capacity and maintains its reduced state through mechanisms such as upregulating the expression of glutathione synthase, increasing NADPH production, and promoting mitochondrial autophagy [60,61]. Meanwhile, this pathway can activate RUNX2 transcription, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [62]. Moreover, the MAPK pathway, which is the most classic osteogenic differentiation pathway, also plays a crucial role; it initially promotes BMSC proliferation during the early stages of differentiation to expand the pool of osteogenic precursor cells [63,64]. Finally, through EDU experiments, live-dead staining, and CCK-8 assays, we confirmed that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs promote BMSC proliferation (Fig. 8H–S8A, C). And the WB experiment demonstrated significant activation of AMPK and MAPK (Fig. 8I), with Mg2+ playing a key role in this biological function (Fig. 8J).

In summary, based on pseudo-temporal analysis, we successfully demonstrated that Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs effectively block FTWs activation and propagation, and through early pro-proliferation and late pro-osteogenesis significantly promoting osteogenic differentiation.

Conclusion

In this study, we designed and prepared a nanoparticle (Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs) that blocks FTWs activation and propagation. Based on homotargeted and membrane fusion mechanisms, this nanoparticle enables precise targeting of specific cells, rapid enhancement of membrane MUFA-PLs ratio, and efficient delivery of Mg/Ce-MOF; based on the ion displacement properties (Fe2+-Mg2+ displacement) and ROS scavenging activity of Me/Ce-MOF, it can effectively remove intracellular Fe2+ and maintain long-term stability of the redox system. Cell experiments have demonstrated that this nanoparticle can effectively block FTWs while restoring the biological functions of target cells. In vitro experiments demonstrated that these nanoparticles achieved precise targeting of bone tissue and significantly increased total bone mass in OVX mice. Furthermore, when used to fill femoral condyle defects in OVX rats via gelatin sponge carriers, the bone repair outcomes remained satisfactory. Finally, based on pseudo-temporal analysis of Bulk-seq data, we elucidated the molecular mechanism by which Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs blocks FTWs through sustained inhibition of ferroptosis and chronological activation of AMPK and MAPK pathways. In conclusion, we propose that nanoparticles blocking FTWs activation and propagation represent a novel therapeutic paradigm for treating iron accumulation-related diseases and theoretical basis.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the long-term in vivo fate of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs remains unclear and warrants extended tracking investigations. Although no acute immunogenicity was observed in rodent models, the immune response in humans may differ, necessitating further preclinical biosafety evaluation. In addition, direct real-time imaging evidence within bone tissue to substantiate the in vivo existence of FTWs is currently lacking; future studies employing spatiotemporal tracking techniques are required to verify the authentic dynamic behavior of FTWs in bone tissue. Finally, the generalizability of this FTW-blocking strategy to other iron accumulation-related diseases remains to be validated in additional disease models.

Experimental section

Establishment of BMSC-Related Cell Models: a) Establishment of MUFA-PLs-rich BMSC Model: BMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates (density 1 × 104 cells/cm2) and cultured in normal α-MEM medium. Upon reaching 70% confluence, cells were treated with 500 μM oleic acid for 3, 6, and 12 h to identify the optimal model. b) Establishment of the Iron-Accumulating BMSC Model: BMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates (at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2) and cultured using standard α-MEM medium. Upon reaching 70% confluence, the medium was replaced with α-MEM supplemented with 200 μmol/L ferric citrate (FAC). Cells were treated continuously for 24 h to establish the iron-accumulating BMSC model.

Preparation and Characterization of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs: a) Preparation of MgHCF: Under vigorous stirring, MgCl2·6H2O (0.8 mmol) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (Mw = 10000, 300 mg) were completely dissolved in 40 mL of aqueous solution (pH = 1, adjusted with HCl), yielding Solution A. Then, using a micro syringe pump, inject 40 mL of potassium ferricyanide solution (pH = 1, adjusted with HCl) containing 0.8 mmol of iron at a rate of 80 mL/h into Solution A. After mixing, react at room temperature for 2 h. Then, dialyze the mixture against deionized water (Mw = 50 kDa) for 6 h. Finally, concentrate the resulting dispersion by vacuum evaporation, freeze-dry it, and collect the MgHCF particles. b) Preparation of Ce-MOF: Ce-MOF nanoenzymes with varying particle sizes were synthesized by adjusting the ratio of 2-HMim to (NH4) 2Ce (NO3)6. Specifically, 1.416 g of 2-HMim was dissolved in 24 mL of DMF solution under magnetic stirring until complete dissolution. Then, 8 mL of an aqueous solution containing 4.64 g of (NH4) 2Ce (NO3)6 was added. After stirring for 10 min, the resulting mixture was transferred to an autoclave and sealed (maintained at 100 °C for 1 h). After natural cooling to room temperature, precipitate was dialyzed in room temperature to deionize water using a dialysis membrane (Mw = 14,000 Da) for 2 days. After dialysis, the solution was concentrated and freeze-dry. Ce-MOF powder collected. c) Preparation of MgHCF/Ce-MOF: First, PEI (Mw 10 K) modifies the charge of Ce-MOF (weakly positive charge, strongly positive after PEI modification): Add PEI solution (20 mg/mL) to the Ce-MOF nanoparticle suspension (5 mg/mL), then stir for 2 h to mix so that PEI grafts onto the surface of Ce-MOF nanoparticles. Wash five times with deionized water to remove excess reactants, obtaining Ce-MOF (+). Continue to add 1 mg/ml MgHCF and stir the Ce-MOF (+) suspension overnight at room temperature to obtain the MgHCF/Ce-MOF nanocomposite via electrostatic adsorption. d) Preparation of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs: First, according to the manufacturer's instructions, use the cell membrane extraction kit to extract the cell membrane rich in MUFA-PLs from BMSCs; subsequently, add the obtained MUFA-PLs-rich cell membrane to the Mg/Ce-MOF dispersion for sonication; then, use a mini-extruder to co-extrude sequentially through filters with pore sizes of 400 nm and 200 nm; then, centrifuge at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove excess cell membrane fragments to obtain Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs; finally, confirm the coating of the cell membrane by TEM, and determine the membrane protein retention rate by Coomassie Brilliant Blue. In this study, the nanoparticles were characterized and their physicochemical stability over 14 days was evaluated using zeta potential analysis, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), thermogravimetric analysis (TG), dynamic light scattering (DLS), inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), UV–Vis spectrophotometry, and chemical simulation methods.

Animal Model Establishment: The C57BL/6 mice (female) and SD rats (female) used in this study were obtained from the Medical University Experimental Animal Center. Mice and rats were housed in SPF-grade animal facilities with free access to sterile water and feed. All animal experiments were approved by the Medical University Animal Ethics Committee (IACUC-CQMU-2025-06021) and strictly adhered to the National Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in Research Institutions. a) OVX Model Establishment: Mice were anesthetized and positioned prone with the abdomen fixed. The surgical site was shaved and disinfected. Make longitudinal skin and dorsal muscle layer incision at the marked site. Bluntly dissect the peritoneal fat surrounding the ovaries to expose them. Ligate the ovarian arteries, veins, and ligaments using sterile silk sutures, then excise the ovaries entirely. After confirming no active bleeding, reposition the remaining fat tissue and genital tract stumps into the abdominal cavity. Close the muscle layer and skin incisions with layered sutures. After completing the ovariectomy on the opposite side using the same technique, the mouse was placed on a temperature-controlled recovery pad until fully awake, then transferred to a standard housing cage for routine care. Medications were administered for three consecutive days postoperatively for infection prevention and analgesia. The sham-operated group underwent removal of adipose tissue equivalent in volume to the ovaries. Except for drug dosages, the OVX rat model was established using the same methodology as mice. b) Construction of the Femoral Condyle Defect Model: Twelve weeks after establishing the OVX rat model, the femoral condyle defect model was constructed. OVX rats were anesthetized and positioned prone with the abdomen fixed. The surgical site was shaved and disinfected. The skin and muscle layers were incised to expose the medial condyle of the femur. Damage the periosteum at the drilling site and create a cylindrical bone defect (diameter: 2.5 mm; depth: 5 mm) using a pen-style dental burr. Fill the defect with saline solution and materials from different groups, then suture the subcutaneous soft tissue and skin. Place the rat on a temperature-controlled recovery pad until fully awake, then transfer to a standard housing cage for routine management. Administer medications for infection prevention and analgesia for three consecutive days postoperatively.

Immunofluorescence Staining of MUFA-PLs on Cell Membranes: a) Exogenous Oleic Acid Supplementation: Seed BMSCs into confocal dishes and culture until 50% confluence. Add 50 μM fluorescently labeled oleic acid. After incubation for 3, 6, 9, 10, 11, and 12 h, replace the culture medium. Staining was then performed using an Actin-Tracker Red-Rhodamine kit following the manufacturer's instructions. Finally, fluorescence images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss Ltd., Oberkochen, Germany).

Targeted Performance Evaluation of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs: a) Fluorescent Labeling of Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs: Add Cy5.5 fluorescent probes to the Mg/Ce-MOF dispersion and incubate for 24 h to complete fluorescent labeling of Mg/Ce-MOF. Subsequently, encapsulation of cell membranes is achieved using a microextrusion device. Subsequently, cell membranes were encapsulated using a micro-extrusion device. b) Cell Targeting Performance Evaluation: Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and subjected to corresponding treatments according to experimental groups. Immunofluorescence staining was performed at 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h post-treatment. Images were captured using confocal microscopy. c) Fluorescent Imaging of Small Animal Tissues: First, experimental mice were deeply anesthetized and secured to the operating table. Subsequently, Mg/Ce-MOF and Mg/Ce-MOF@MUFA-PLs were slowly injected sequentially via the tail vein using insulin needles. Mice were euthanized at 6 h post-injection, and key organs and femurs were harvested for fluorescence capture.

GSH Content Assay: a) Cellular GSH Content Assay: After washing cells with different treatments, add 3 vol of protein removal reagent to sediments and vortex of the cell and perform 2 fast freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen on the cell samples. After standing at 4 °C 5 min, centrifuge (10,000 g, 10 min) and collect supernatant for GSH content measurement. Following manufacturer instructions add GSH detection reagent and transfer samples to a 96-well plate. Measure optical density (405–414 nm) on each well with microplate reader. b) Tibia GSH Content Assay: Tibia tissue from mice group was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into powder. Powder was then dissolved in protein removal reagent and vortexed thoroughly. Tissue samples were then frozen for 2 quick freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen. After standing at 4 °C for 10 min centrifuge (10,000g, 10 min) collected supernatant for GSH content measurement. Proceed processing according to manufacturer's instructions, measure OD values in each well using microplate reader.

Determination of MDA and SOD in Bone Tissue: MDA levels in different tissues were measured using the MDA Assay Kit (S0131M, Beyotime). SOD levels in different tissues and cells were measured using the SOD Assay Kit (S0101S, Beyotime). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Bioinformatics Analysis: All bioinformatics analyses in this study were performed based on R (V 4.3.2). a) Data Collection: Bulk-seq data of human tissues were obtained from the GTEx database (http://www.huaplusmd.com); human bone marrow single-cell sequencing data were obtained from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). b) Transcriptome Sequencing: The iron accumulation BMSCs model was treated with materials, and total cellular RNA was extracted using TRIzol at different time points; samples were transferred to 1.5 mL enzyme-free EP tubes and stored at −80 °C; library construction was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq Reagent Kit (constant) method, and sequencing was completed; data quality control and quantification were performed. c) Single-Cell Analysis: This single-cell data analysis was mainly performed based on the ‘Seurat’ R package (V 5.1.0); data quality control thresholds: filter genes expressed in fewer than 5 cells, filter cells with fewer than 500 genes expressed, filter cells with mitochondrial gene expression greater than 20%; after completing cell annotation based on dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis, correlation analysis was performed. d) Bulk-seq Analysis: Gene annotation was completed based on the probe list; when duplicate probes exist, retain the probe with the larger average value. Gene expression atlas in the human body was drawn based on the ‘gganatogram’ R package (V 1.1.1); differential expression analysis was completed based on the ‘edgeR’ R package (V 4.0.16); GO, KEGG, and GSEA analyses were completed based on the ‘clusterProfiler’ R package (V 4.10.1); transcriptome pseudotime analysis was completed based on the ‘Mfuzz’ R package (V 2.62.0).

Statistical Analysis: Except for the bioinformatics analysis section, statistical analysis in this study was performed using GraphPad Prism (V 9.5.0). T-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was employed for comparisons among multiple groups. Unless otherwise specified, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
