ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis mediates prostate cancer progression and docetaxel resistance via m6A-stabilized CLSPN RNA
Authors: Yutong Chen, Yang Li, Zongzhu Liang, Zhao Yang, Haotian Ren, Wanqing Wei, Yuanjie Niu, Zhiqun Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryProstate cancer (PCa) often progresses to castration-resistant PCa (CRPC), where docetaxel (DTX) resistance is a major challenge. We investigated the role of the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 in this resistance. ALKBH5 expression was significantly reduced in CRPC clinical samples. Functionally, overexpressing ALKBH5 inhibited PCa cell proliferation and migration, while its knockdown enhanced these effects and increased DTX resistance. Conversely, restoring ALKBH5 or knocking down the m6A reader IGF2BP2 reversed resistance. Multi-omics analysis identified CLSPN, a DNA replication stress regulator, as a key downstream target. Mechanistically, ALKBH5-mediated m6A demethylation reduces CLSPN mRNA stability in an IGF2BP2-dependent manner. Low ALKBH5, therefore, stabilizes CLSPN via IGF2BP2, promoting resistance. These findings, validated in clinical samples and organoid models, demonstrate that the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis modulates DTX resistance in metastatic CRPC through m6A-dependent regulation of CLSPN. Targeting this pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome DTX resistance.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) has become the most significant malignancy of the urogenital system, posing a serious threat to men’s health.1 Currently, PCa is recognized as a highly heterogeneous cancer, and this heterogeneity complicates the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of its progression to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC).2 With the widespread use of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), an increasing number of patients undergoing ADT progress to the metastatic CRPC (mCRPC) stage.3 However, treatment options for mCRPC remain quite limited. Specifically, studies have shown that the combination of docetaxel with prednisone and enzalutamide provides greater clinical benefits in patients with mCRPC, although severe adverse events occur more frequently. This indicates that docetaxel continues to play a critical role in first-line treatment for mCRPC.4 Nevertheless, only about half of the patients treated with docetaxel exhibit a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response, and only a quarter experience an improvement in quality of life. Furthermore, most patients develop disease progression within one year of starting docetaxel treatment.5 In this context, gaining a better understanding of the mechanisms driving docetaxel resistance in patients with prostate cancer will be a crucial step toward improving patient outcomes.

In recent years, research on RNA methylation has spearheaded the third wave of epigenetics, becoming a crucial area in elucidating the mechanisms of disease development and progression.6 N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most common methylation modification in coding RNA and long non-coding RNA in eukaryotes, plays a pivotal role in various tumorigenic processes.7 Through diverse mechanisms, m6A influences RNA splicing, degradation, stability, and translation efficiency, mediating different molecular signaling pathways that promote tumor progression.8,9,10 Human AlkB homolog H5 (ALKBH5) is a well-known m6A demethylase, often referred to as an “m6A eraser.”11 In recent years, ALKBH5 has been implicated in multiple biological processes, including cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and osteogenesis.12,13,14 Additionally, ALKBH5 has been linked to a variety of cancers, such as glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, gastric cancer, lung cancer, and ovarian cancer.15,16,17,18,19,20 In urological tumors, our research team has previously demonstrated that METTL3 promotes the development of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) via the ERK signaling pathway in an m6A-dependent manner.21 However, in prostate cancer, particularly in the context of docetaxel resistance observed in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), the expression of ALKBH5, its impact on m6A RNA modification levels, and the resulting epigenetic and biological effects remain largely unexplored.

CLSPN is a nuclear protein associated with DNA replication stress and damage response, acting as a critical regulator of the S phase checkpoint.22,23 It has been identified in Xenopus laevis oocyte extracts that CLSPN is an essential factor for the phosphorylation of checkpoint kinase 1 (CHEK1) and for the activation of the upstream kinase ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related kinase (ATR).24 Through the ATR-CHEK1 pathway, CLSPN plays a crucial role in maintaining replication fork stability during the S phase of the cell cycle. In the presence of DNA damage, CLSPN promotes the activation of ATR-CHEK1, leading to cell-cycle arrest and DNA damage repair (DDR). Successful DDR facilitates checkpoint recovery, while failure to repair DNA damage results in cell death.25,26,27,28 Overexpression of CLSPN has been reported to promote the proliferation of several human solid tumors, including lung cancer, ovarian cancer, cervical cancer, gastric cancer, and renal cancer.29,30,31,32,33,34 Given the high rate of DDR-related mutations in mCRPC, CLSPN remains a key molecule in controlling DDR and cell-cycle progression, making it a promising target for anticancer therapy. In prostate cancer, Takashi Babasaki et al. have demonstrated the oncogenic role of CLSPN and its association with DDR and docetaxel resistance.35 Moreover, Stanislav Drápela et al. found that the CHK1 inhibitor MU380 increased docetaxel sensitivity by inducing mitotic defects and promoting the accumulation of DNA damage.36

In this study, we aim to further explore the role and mechanism of ALKBH5 in the demethylation of specific m6A sites in the CLSPN transcript through RNA m6A demethylation. We hypothesize that ALKBH5 “erases” m6A modifications at specific sites, leading to CLSPN degradation. Furthermore, we investigated the involvement of ALKBH5 and the m6A “reader” protein IGF2BP2 in CLSPN demethylation and their roles in CRPC progression and docetaxel resistance.

Results

ALKBH5 is downregulated in prostate cancer and decreases with tumor progression

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on the microarrays, followed by statistical analysis. We observed that ALKBH5 expression was significantly lower in castration-sensitive prostate cancer(CSPC) tissues compared with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) tissues. Next, we analyzed ALKBH5 expression levels across various stages of prostate cancer using multiple databases. Analysis of the GSE6919 dataset revealed that ALKBH5 expression was lower in CRPC compared with castration-sensitive prostate cancer (CSPC) (Figure 1C). Data from the PCTA database (www.thepcta.org) and the TCGA database further confirmed that ALKBH5 expression decreases as tumor Gleason scores increase (Figures 1D and 1E). Additionally, analysis of the SU2C database, which includes CRPC samples, demonstrated a positive correlation between ALKBH5 expression and androgen receptor (AR) score, and a negative correlation with neuroendocrine (NE) differentiation scores (Figures 1F and 1G). In summary, ALKBH5 is downregulated in prostate cancer, and its expression progressively decreases with tumor advancement.

ALKBH5 regulates CLSPN expression via m6A modifications, affecting DNA replication and cell division

To elucidate the tumor-suppressive role of ALKBH5 in prostate cancer, we first established stable ALKBH5 knockdown and overexpression cell lines in both C4-2 and PC3 cell models. Western blot analysis confirmed the efficient modulation of ALKBH5 expression in these engineered cell lines (Figures S1A and S1B). Systematic functional assessments through colony formation assays, CCK-8 proliferation assays, Transwell invasion assays, and scratch assay demonstrated that ALKBH5 overexpression significantly inhibited cellular proliferation and invasive capabilities in both cell lines, while ALKBH5 knockdown markedly enhanced these malignant phenotypes (Figures S1C–S1F).

To determine whether the tumor-suppressive effects were dependent on ALKBH5’s m6A demethylase activity, we generated cell lines overexpressing either wild-type ALKBH5 (with intact m6A demethylation activity) or the catalytically inactive ALKBH5-H204A mutant (Figure S1G). Functional assays revealed that wild-type ALKBH5 significantly suppressed colony formation and cell migration, whereas the ALKBH5-H204A mutant lost this inhibitory capacity (Figures S1H and S1I). Subsequent in vivo xenograft experiments corroborated these findings, showing that ALKBH5 overexpression significantly attenuated tumor growth while ALKBH5 knockdown promoted tumor progression (Figure S1J). Notably, the ALKBH5-H204A mutant failed to exhibit any tumor-suppressive effects in vivo (Figure S1K).

Given ALKBH5’s established role as an m6A demethylase, we performed genome-wide analyses using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and Methylated RNA Immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) in C4-2/NC (control) versus C4-2/LvALKBH5 (overexpression) cells (screening criteria: fold change >2, p ≤ 0.001). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that ALKBH5 overexpression significantly impacted DNA replication-related pathways: cellular component analysis showed significant enrichment of DNA replication fork-associated genes (Figure 2A); biological process analysis identified DNA replication and cell cycle regulation as the most significantly affected pathways (Figure 2B); molecular function and KEGG pathway analyses further supported these findings (Figures 2C and 2D).Figure 2ALKBH5 Regulates CLSPN Expression via m6A Modifications, Affecting DNA Replication and Cell Division(A) Enrichment analysis of cellular components for genes downregulated by ALKBH5 overexpression.(B) Enrichment analysis of biological processes for genes downregulated by ALKBH5 overexpression.(C) Pathway enrichment analysis for genes downregulated by ALKBH5 overexpression.(D) Enrichment analysis of molecular functions for genes downregulated by ALKBH5 overexpression.(E) Intersection of downregulated genes and m6A-modified genes after ALKBH5 overexpression.(F) Heatmap of intersecting genes between ALKBH5 downregulated genes and m6A-modification-reduced genes.(G) MeRIP-seq results showing changes in CLSPN m6A modification after ALKBH5 overexpression.

Through integrated analysis of mRNA expression and m6A modification changes, we identified 30 candidate genes regulated by ALKBH5 (Figure 2E). Among these, CLSPN, a key nuclear protein involved in DNA replication stress and S phase checkpoint regulation, emerged as a crucial downstream target due to its central role in DNA damage response (Figure 2F). MeRIP-seq visualization further confirmed significant reduction of m6A modifications at two specific sites within the CLSPN coding sequence (CDS) following ALKBH5 overexpression (Figure 2G).

ALKBH5 regulates CLSPN RNA stability via m6A modifications

It has been reported that the H204A mutation in ALKBH5 abolishes its demethylase activity.37 To investigate whether ALKBH5 regulates CLSPN through m6A modifications, we generated an H204A mutant of ALKBH5 (Figure 3A). Overexpression of both wild-type ALKBH5 and the H204A mutant in C4-2 and PC3 cells resulted in increased mRNA and protein levels (Figures 3B–3D); however, the H204A mutant failed to reduce m6A levels, unlike wild-type ALKBH5 (Figures 3C and 3D). To further investigate whether ALKBH5 influences CLSPN mRNA stability via m6A, actinomycin D (Act-D) assays were performed. Results showed that wild-type ALKBH5 reduced CLSPN mRNA stability, whereas the H204A mutant had no effect on CLSPN mRNA stability (Figures 3H and 3I). Consistently, MeRIP-qPCR experiments confirmed that wild-type ALKBH5 decreased m6A levels in CLSPN, while the H204A mutant did not alter m6A levels (Figures 3J–3K).

CLSPN is an essential factor for phosphorylation of checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) and activation of the upstream kinase ATR (ataxia-telangiectasia and Rad3-related protein).24 Through the ATR-CHK1 pathway, CLSPN plays a critical role in maintaining replication fork stability during the S phase of the cell cycle. Western blot analysis revealed that wild-type ALKBH5, by downregulating CLSPN expression, reduced the phosphorylation of CHK1 without affecting ATR or total CHK1 protein levels, whereas the H204A mutant failed to affect CHK1 phosphorylation (Figures 3L–3M).

To demonstrate that ALKBH5 exerts its tumor-suppressive function through its regulation of CLSPN, we overexpressed ALKBH5 and CLSPN simultaneously in different cell types. CCK8, colony formation, and Transwell assays revealed that overexpression of CLSPN after ALKBH5 overexpression reversed the tumor-suppressive phenotype of ALKBH5. These findings confirm that ALKBH5 exerts its tumor-suppressive function by regulating the levels of CLSPN (Figures 3N–3Q). In summary, wild-type ALKBH5, but not the H204A mutant, regulates CLSPN m6A levels, thus influencing its expression and modulating the ATR-CHK1 signaling pathway to exert its biological function.

M6A modification of CLSPN mRNA maintained its stability via IGF2BP2

It is well established that m6A modifications exert their functional effects primarily through reader proteins. To identify the specific m6A reader responsible for regulating CLSPN, we used small interfering RNA (siRNA) to knock down various m6A reader proteins (Figures S2A–S2J). Among them, IGF2BP2 was found to have the most significant impact on CLSPN mRNA expression (Figure 4A). Consistently, analysis of the TCGA database revealed a positive correlation between IGF2BP2 and CLSPN expression levels (Figure 4B). Given that our sequencing data identified two m6A sites in the CLSPN mRNA, we mutated these motifs to generate CLSPN-mut1 and CLSPN-mut2 constructs (Figure 4E). Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that IGF2BP2 binds to the m6A motifs in the CDS region of CLSPN mRNA, promoting its stability (Figures 4F–4H). We then overexpressed wild-type CLSPN and the two mutants in two cell lines and assessed their overexpression efficiency (Figure 4I). MeRIP-qPCR results showed that the m6A modification levels in CLSPN-wt were significantly higher than those in CLSPN-mut1 and CLSPN-mut2 (Figures 4J and 4K). Furthermore, RIP-qPCR revealed reduced binding of IGF2BP2 to CLSPN-mut1 and CLSPN-mut2 compared with CLSPN-wt (Figures 4L and 4M).

Actinomycin D (Act-D) assays further confirmed that IGF2BP2 knockdown decreased CLSPN mRNA stability (Figures 4N and 4O). CLSPN-wt mRNA was more stable than the CLSPN-mut1 and CLSPN-mut2 mutants (Figures 4P and 4Q).

ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 regulates docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer in vitro

Previous study demonstrates that CLSPN overexpression is associated with poorer prostate-specific antigen (PSA) relapse-free survival and that CLSPN knockdown enhances sensitivity to docetaxel.35 Our sequencing results revealed that ALKBH5 primarily affects DNA replication and cell division pathways during prostate cancer progression. Since docetaxel is a commonly used chemotherapeutic agent for prostate cancer, we hypothesized that the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis could regulate docetaxel resistance in an m6A-dependent manner.

We induced docetaxel resistance in C4-2 cells by gradually increasing the drug concentration (Figure 5A). With the development of resistance and increased passage number, ALKBH5 expression levels decreased, while CLSPN and m6A levels progressively increased. In docetaxel-resistant cells, there was no significant change in the expression levels of CHK1, but the levels of p-CHK1 were significantly upregulated (Figures 5B and 5C). In C4-2/DTXR (docetaxel-resistant) cells, overexpression of ALKBH5 (Figure S3A) led to downregulation of both CLSPN and CHK1 phosphorylation levels (Figure 5D), which enhanced docetaxel sensitivity as indicated by IC50 assays. Conversely, ALKBH5 knockdown in C4-2 cells increased docetaxel resistance (Figures 5E and 5F). To investigate the role of the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2-CLSPN axis in docetaxel resistance, we knocked down IGF2BP2 in C4-2/DTXR cells and overexpressed CLSPN-wt, CLSPN-mut1, or CLSPN-mut2 (Figures S3B and S3C). IC50 assays and clonogenic assays revealed that IGF2BP2 knockdown enhanced docetaxel sensitivity, while overexpression of CLSPN-wt restored docetaxel resistance in C4-2/DTXR cells. In contrast, CLSPN-mut1 or CLSPN-mut2 failed to restore resistance (Figures 5L–5N). Similarly, overexpression of ALKBH5 in C4-2/DTXR cells resulted in comparable outcomes (Figure 5K), further confirming the involvement of the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2-CLSPN axis. In summary, the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis regulates docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer through an m6A-dependent mechanism.

ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 regulates docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer in vivo

To evaluate the role of the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2-CLSPN axis in regulating prostate cancer growth and docetaxel resistance in vivo, we employed a subcutaneous xenograft mouse model. Tumor cells from different experimental groups were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice. After two weeks of tumor formation, the mice were treated with or without docetaxel (5 mg/kg body weight), administered intraperitoneally twice a week for two weeks. The mice were then sacrificed, and tumors were harvested for analysis (Figure 6A). Results indicated that both knockdown and overexpression of ALKBH5 were successful in tumor tissues (Figures S4A and S4B), and tumors in the C4-2 shALKBH5 group exhibited significantly larger volume and weight compared with controls, with reduced sensitivity to docetaxel treatment (Figures 6B–6D). To further validate the role of ALKBH5 and the m6A reader protein IGF2BP2, as well as its downstream target CLSPN, in docetaxel resistance in vivo, we first overexpressed CLSPN in stable ALKBH5-overexpressing C4-2/DTX cells and subsequently implanted these cells into BALB/c nude mice. The experimental groups included control, OE-ALKBH5, OE-ALKBH5+oeCLSPNwt, and OE-ALKBH5+oeCLSPNmut. Mice were then treated with DMSO or docetaxel. Overexpression of ALKBH5 significantly suppressed tumor growth and restored docetaxel sensitivity in resistant tumors. However, overexpression of CLSPN-wt reversed this effect, while CLSPN-mut overexpression had no effect (Figures 6H–6J).

Next, we generated stable IGF2BP2 knockdown C4-2/DTXR cells using shRNA (siIGF2BP2-1) and again overexpressed CLSPN. These cells were implanted into BALB/c nude mice, which were divided into control, shIGF2BP2, shIGF2BP2+oeCLSPNwt, and shIGF2BP2+oeCLSPNmut groups. Mice were treated with DMSO or docetaxel. Knockdown of IGF2BP2 significantly inhibited tumor growth and restored docetaxel sensitivity in resistant tumors. However, overexpression of CLSPN-wt reversed this effect, while overexpression of CLSPN-mut did not (Figures S4C–S4E).

Finally, immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of xenograft tumors revealed that ALKBH5 knockdown upregulated CLSPN expression and increased CHK1 phosphorylation (Figure 6K). These findings suggest that the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis regulates CLSPN expression in an m6A-dependent manner, thereby modulating docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer in vivo.

To confirm the role of ALKBH5 in modulating docetaxel sensitivity in patient-derived tissues, we developed three prostate cancer organoid models. For each model, we created shALKBH5 knockdown groups and corresponding control groups. Organoids were treated with docetaxel or DMSO, and their responses were assessed (Figure 7E). The results demonstrated that ALKBH5 knockdown markedly decreased the sensitivity of organoids to docetaxel, with only limited organoid disintegration observed following docetaxel treatment, likely due to transfection efficiency constraints (Figures 7F and 7G). Finally, we analyzed the correlation between ALKBH5 and CLSPN expression in prostate cancer using the PCTA database (http://www.thepcta.org/). The results revealed a negative correlation between ALKBH5 and CLSPN at different stages of prostate cancer (Figure 7H). These findings suggest that ALKBH5 modulates docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer.

Discussion

Current research indicates that m6A modification plays established roles in prostate cancer pathogenesis.38 In this study, we investigated the role of ALKBH5 in regulating docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer (PCa), particularly in the progression of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). We demonstrated that ALKBH5 exerts its function through an m6A-dependent mechanism and works in concert with the m6A reader protein IGF2BP2 to regulate the expression of CLSPN. Our findings reveal a novel epigenetic regulatory pathway that contributes to docetaxel resistance both in vitro and in vivo, offering new strategies to overcome chemotherapy resistance in PCa.

Our clinical sample and in vitro model data show that ALKBH5 expression is significantly downregulated in CRPC compared with castration-sensitive prostate cancer (CSPC) and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Consistent with the tumor-suppressive role of ALKBH5 observed in other cancers, we found that reduced ALKBH5 expression is associated with PCa progression. This is reflected by its downregulation in tumors with higher Gleason scores and its inverse correlation with CRPC progression. This downregulation of ALKBH5 likely contributes to the enhanced DNA replication and cell division activity in prostate cancer.

Additionally, we found that ALKBH5 overexpression suppresses tumor cell proliferation and migration, while its knockdown promotes these malignant behaviors. This further supports the role of ALKBH5 as a key tumor suppressor in prostate cancer, similar to its role in glioblastoma, breast cancer, and other solid tumors.12,15,17 The significant impact of ALKBH5 on tumor growth and metastatic potential underscores its clinical relevance as a marker of tumor aggressiveness.

We identified CLSPN as a direct downstream target of ALKBH5, linking it to the regulation of DNA damage response (DDR) and cell cycle progression. Our RNA-seq and MeRIP-seq analyses confirmed that ALKBH5 reduces m6A methylation in the CDS region of CLSPN mRNA, leading to decreased CLSPN expression. CLSPN plays a crucial role in maintaining replication fork stability during the S phase and activating the ATR-CHK1 pathway under DNA replication stress.22,24 By downregulating CLSPN through m6A demethylation, ALKBH5 directly influences DDR and cell cycle control, which are critical in PCa progression and chemotherapy response.

Our experiments showed that the H204A mutant of ALKBH5, which lacks demethylase activity, failed to reduce m6A levels or regulate CLSPN expression or downstream signaling pathways, such as CHK1 phosphorylation. These results support the hypothesis that ALKBH5 exerts its tumor-suppressive function through its m6A demethylase activity, particularly targeting CLSPN. This mechanism is consistent with the known roles of m6A modifications in regulating mRNA stability and translation, as observed in other tumor contexts.6,7

We also identified IGF2BP2 as the critical m6A reader that binds to methylated CLSPN mRNA and stabilizes its expression. Knockdown of IGF2BP2 resulted in a marked reduction in CLSPN levels, mimicking the effects of ALKBH5 overexpression, further confirming the importance of the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis in CLSPN regulation. Additionally, experiments using mutated CLSPN constructs demonstrated that the m6A motifs in CLSPN mRNA are essential for IGF2BP2 binding and mRNA stabilization.

The role of IGF2BP2 in stabilizing CLSPN mRNA is particularly important as it provides a mechanistic link between m6A modifications and the DDR pathway, which is crucial for cell survival under replicative stress. Given the common DDR-related mutations in mCRPC and their contribution to chemotherapy resistance, targeting IGF2BP2-regulated m6A-dependent CLSPN stability offers a promising therapeutic potential.23,26

One of the most significant findings of this study is the role of the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2-CLSPN axis in regulating docetaxel resistance. In docetaxel-resistant PCa cell lines and xenograft models, we observed decreased ALKBH5 expression, increased CLSPN expression, and elevated m6A methylation levels, all of which correlate with enhanced chemotherapy resistance. Overexpression of ALKBH5 restored docetaxel sensitivity, while IGF2BP2 knockdown increased drug sensitivity, further confirming the functional importance of this pathway.

Moreover, our clinical sample analysis showed that docetaxel-resistant prostate cancer tissues exhibit significantly lower ALKBH5 levels and higher CLSPN expression compared with docetaxel-sensitive tissues. Organoid models further validated this finding, where ALKBH5 knockdown significantly reduced organoid sensitivity to docetaxel, indicating the clinical relevance of this mechanism.

Our study highlights the potential of targeting the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis to overcome docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer. By modulating m6A levels and disrupting CLSPN-mediated DDR, it may be possible to enhance chemotherapy efficacy. Additionally, developing inhibitors targeting m6A-related proteins such as IGF2BP2 represents a promising therapeutic approach.

Future research should focus on further elucidating the broader epigenetic landscape involving m6A modifications in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and explore combination therapies targeting the ALKBH5-IGF2BP2 axis, including ALKBH5-overexpressing gene therapy, IGF2BP2-targeted small-molecule inhibitors, or monoclonal antibody-based treatments, to improve therapeutic outcomes for patients with mCRPC.

Limitations of the study

Our study has certain limitations. One limitation is that we used only one docetaxel-resistant cell line and did not validate the role of ALKBH5-IGF2BP2-CLSPN in docetaxel resistance in parallel cell lines. Additionally, in the animal experiments, we did not perform more detailed analyses of changes in the tumor microenvironment. We plan to address these aspects with more comprehensive analysis and validation in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

The human prostate cancer cell lines LNcap, 22RV1, C4-2, PC3, and DU145 were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). PC3 and 22RV1 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Docetaxel-resistant cell lines were developed from the original parental lines by continuous treatment with increasing concentrations of docetaxel. Prior to experiments, all cell lines were verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination using PCR. No mycoplasma contamination was detected throughout the study.

Clinical samples

Tissue microarrays comprising 4 benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) tissues, 31 castration-sensitive prostate cancer (CSPC) tissues, and 18 castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) tissues were designed and manufactured by OUTDO BIOTECH (Shanghai, China). The prostate tissue specimens used in this study were surgical samples obtained from patients with complete clinicopathological data. CSPC specimens (n = 31) were collected via radical prostatectomy, while CRPC specimens (n = 18) were acquired via transurethral resection of the prostate. This study included 5 docetaxel-resistant patients and 5 docetaxel-sensitive patients, with biopsy samples obtained from primary lesions. The docetaxel-resistant patients progressed to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and were treated with docetaxel, after which clinical resistance was defined by the following criteria: worsening clinical symptoms (e.g., increased difficulty in urination, hematuria, aggravated pain including bone pain, compression symptoms, and edema), a sustained increase in PSA levels (more than a 50% increase from baseline in two consecutive measurements during chemotherapy), and disease progression detected by imaging (tumor enlargement or new lesions on CT/MRI, and the appearance of new bone metastasis on whole-body bone scans).Patient-derived tissues for prostate cancer organoid culture are obtained from in situ needle biopsies of prostate cancer patients.All patient tissues were collected from 2018 to 2024.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from all patients(approval number:KY2025K321 [Jun 2025]).

Tumor xenografts in nude mice

All procedures involving mice were approved by the University Committee on Use and Care of Animals at Tianjin Medical University and the Institute of Radiation Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College and complied with all regulatory standards (approval number:IRM/2-IACUC-2506-021 [Jun 2025]). Male nude mice (7 weeks old) were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and held under specific pathogen-free conditions. The mice were kept in ventilated cages and allowed to acclimate for at least 5 days before any manipulation. Mice were housed in a temperature- (22 ± 1°C) and humidity-controlled vivarium with lights maintained on a 12:12 light/dark cycle.All animal experiments lasted for 40 days.

Method details

Bioinformatics analysis

To systematically delineate the expression dynamics of the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 in prostate carcinogenesis and progression, this study integrated analytical results from multiple public databases: first, based on the GEO dataset GSE6919 (accessed January 2007), ALKBH5 expression levels were compared between 126 patients with castration-sensitive prostate cancer (CSPC) and 25 patients with castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) to reveal alterations during the transition to acquired therapeutic resistance; second, utilizing the PCTA platform (http://www.thepcta.org/) (accessed August 2016), the ALKBH5 expression profile was evaluated across benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), localized prostate cancer (various stages), and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), characterizing its trajectory from benign tissue through early localized disease to advanced metastatic resistant states; third, the GEPIA platform (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) (accessed April 2017), employing The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) cohort data, compared differential ALKBH5 expression between benign prostate tissue and primary prostate tumor tissue, focusing on alterations during initial carcinogenesis; finally, for advanced mCRPC, data from the SU2C mCRPC cohort (accessed June 2019) analyzed via cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) investigated associations between ALKBH5 expression and key molecular features—namely Androgen Receptor (AR) activity scores and Neuroendocrine (NE) differentiation scores—wherein CRPC patients were stratified into ALKBH5-high (z-score ≥ +1) and ALKBH5-low (z-score ≤ -1) expression groups based on a predefined z-score threshold (±1), followed by comparison of established AR signaling pathway activity signature scores and NE transdifferentiation signature scores between groups to explore the potential significance of ALKBH5 expression levels in defining mCRPC molecular subtypes.

Immunohistochemistry, tissue microarray analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections, 5 μm in thickness, were prepared on charged glass slides. After deparaffinization and rehydration, heat-mediated antigen retrieval for ALKBH5 (ab195377) was performed using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH = 6). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated with diluted primary antibodies, followed by polymer-conjugated horseradish peroxidase in a humidified chamber. Standard DAB staining was then used for chromogenic detection of the immunohistochemistry (IHC) targets.21 Each tissue section was assigned a score based on staining intensity in the epithelial compartment (0, no staining; 1, low positive; 2, positive; 3, high positive). All stained specimens were reviewed, calibrated, and optimized following the protocols from our previous study.

Cell culture, cell lines and transfection

The human prostate cancer cell lines LNcap, 22RV1, C4-2, PC3, and DU145 were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). PC3 and 22RV1 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Docetaxel-resistant cell lines were developed from the original parental lines by continuous treatment with increasing concentrations of docetaxel. Transfection of C4-2, PC3, and C4-2/DTXR cells, at 50–70% confluency, with siRNA or plasmids was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and X-tremeGENE HP Transfection Reagent (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Stable cell lines were generated using lentiviruses purchased from Sheweisi Inc. (Tianjin, China). Prior to transfection, cells were passaged into T25 cell culture flasks. When cells were approximately 60% confluent, 20 μl of lentivirus solution was added to the medium. The sequences of the siRNAs and shRNAs are listed in Table S1. Docetaxel was purchased from MedChemExpress.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA concentration was measured by ultraviolet absorbance at 260 nm, and the RNA was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis with the Reverse Transcription System (Roche) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting cDNA was analyzed by PCR using the Applied Biosystems 7900 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific) and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. GAPDH was used as an internal control.Three independent replicates were conducted for both RNA extraction and RT-qPCR assays. The relative RNA expression was calculated using the comparative Ct method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

m6A dot blot assay

Total RNA was extracted from PCa cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA concentration was measured by ultraviolet absorbance at 260 nm. The extracted RNA was diluted to 100 ng/μl, and 2 μl of the diluted RNA was spotted onto a nitrocellulose filter membrane (Invitrogen). After air-drying, the RNA on the membrane was UV cross-linked using a Ultraviolet Crosslinker. The cross-linked membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 hour. After washing three times with TBST, the membrane was incubated with m6A antibody (Abcam, ab284130) overnight at 4°C. Following incubation, the membrane was washed three times and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution) for 1 hour at room temperature. After further washing with TBST, the membrane was prepared for exposure.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, 89,901). Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 hour. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ALKBH5 (Abcam, ab195377), IGF2BP2 (Abcam, ab124930), CLSPN (Abcam, ab3721), ATR (santa curz, sc-515173), CHK1 (Abcam, ab32531), and p-CHK1 (CST, #2348). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were finally detected using a Pierce ECL Western Blotting kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using an imaging system.Three biologically independent replicates were conducted for all Western blotting assays.

Cell proliferation, colony formation and invasion

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Sigma, USA) according to the protocols described in previous studies.39 For the colony formation assay, approximately 1,000 cells (treatment and control groups) were seeded into six-well plates in triplicate. After 2 weeks, the colonies were stained with 0.5% crystal violet, imaged, and counted. Cell invasion was evaluated using transwell assays and scratch assays, as previously described.40 Triplicate biological replicates were used for all proliferation and invasion assays.

RNA extraction, mRNA sequencing, and m6A-Seq

Total RNA was extracted from transfected C4-2 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Both mRNA sequencing and m6A sequencing were performed simultaneously by Cloud-Seq Biotech (Shanghai, China). Briefly, 500 ng of fragmented mRNA was reserved as input control for RNA-seq, while 5 μg of fragmented mRNA was incubated with 5 μg of anti-m6A polyclonal antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202003) in IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4) at 4°C for 2 hours. The mixture was then immunoprecipitated by incubation with protein-A beads (Thermo Fisher) at 4°C for an additional 2 hours. The bound mRNAs were eluted from the beads and extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified mRNA samples were used to generate RNA-Seq libraries with the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB). Both the input sample (without immunoprecipitation) and the m6A IP sample were subjected to 150 bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform.

Paired-end reads were obtained from the Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer, and quality control was performed with a Q30 threshold. After trimming 3' adaptors and removing low-quality reads using Cutadapt software (v1.9.3), the reads were aligned to the reference genome (UCSC MM10) using Hisat2 software (v2.0.4). Methylated RNA sites (peaks) were identified using MACS software, and differentially methylated sites were determined using diffReps. Peaks overlapping with mRNA exons were identified and selected using custom scripts.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments were conducted using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and an m6A antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells (1×10ˆ7) were lysed in ice-cold RIP Lysis Buffer, and the lysate was stored at -80°C. A magnetic bead-antibody complex was prepared by incubating 5 μg of the target antibody or control IgG with 50 μl of protein A/G magnetic beads at room temperature for 30 minutes with rotation. Equal volumes of cell lysate were then incubated with the magnetic bead-antibody complex overnight at 4°C to allow binding of RNA-protein complexes. Simultaneously, 10 μl of cell lysate was collected as input control. The next day, RNA was extracted and purified using proteinase K buffer. The isolated RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Reverse Transcription System (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA was analyzed by RT-qPCR.Three separate replicates were conducted for each experimental group.

Luciferase reporter assay

To assess the stability of CLSPN mRNA, the wild-type or mutant CDS of CLSPN was inserted into a reporter vector downstream of the firefly luciferase (F-luc) gene. The wild-type CDS vector was transfected into wild-type or ALKBH5-Mut prostate cancer (PCa) cells, and Renilla luciferase (R-luc) was co-transfected as an internal control reporter gene. After 24 hours of transfection, firefly luciferase (F-luc) and Renilla luciferase (R-luc) activities were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Similarly, to evaluate the effect of IGF2BP2 on the stability of CLSPN mRNA, the wild-type or mutant CLSPN CDS was inserted downstream of F-luc to generate the CLSPN-wt, CLSPN-mut1, and CLSPN-mut2 constructs. These three plasmids were transfected into wild-type or oeIGF2BP2 prostate cancer (PCa) cells, and Renilla luciferase (R-luc) was co-transfected as an internal control reporter gene. After 24 hours of transfection, luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega).

The detailed steps of the dual-luciferase assay followed the protocol provided by the manufacturer, and Renilla luciferase (R-luc) activity was used to normalize firefly luciferase (F-luc) activity. Each experiment was repeated three times to minimize experimental bias.Three separate replicates were conducted for each experimental group.

RNA stability

RNA stability was assessed using Actinomycin D (Act-D, Sigma, U.S.A.) treatment. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates, and when they reached 80% confluence, they were treated with 5 μg/mL of Act-D for 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 hours before RNA isolation. CLSPN mRNA levels were then measured using the qRT-PCR assay.Three separate biological replicates were conducted for each experimental group.

Animal studies

Seven-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and randomly assigned to multiple groups. For subcutaneous tumor growth assays, each mouse was injected subcutaneously with 1 × 10ˆ7 prostate cancer (PCa) cells from different groups. Tumor growth was monitored weekly by measuring tumor size through the skin. All measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group assignments. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 1/2 × larger diameter × (smaller diameter)ˆ2. After 6 weeks, tumor size and weight differences between the groups were recorded. For drug treatment, mice received intraperitoneal injections of docetaxel (5 mg/kg body weight) or vehicle control twice per week for 2 weeks. Tumor samples were collected according to standardized, institutionally approved protocols, fixed in paraffin, and processed for immunohistochemical analysis.

Organoid culture, transfection and proliferation

Prostate cancer tissue samples were collected from three patients and digested into 2∼10 cell pellets using 5 mg/ml collagenase type II and TrypLE with Y-27632 dihydrochloride. After washing with adDMEM/F12 +/+/+ (containing penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES and 2 mM GlutaMAX), count the cells and plate ∼50,000 cells in a 40-μl drop BME. Organoids were cultured in BME with Advanced DMEM/F12+/+/+ supplemented with N21-MAX, N-acetylcysteine, EGF, Noggin, R-spondin 1, Wnt3a, HGF, A83-01, FGF10, FGF2, Prostaglandin E2, Nicotinamide, SB202190, Plasmocin treatment, DHT, and Y-27632 dihydrochloride (only one week after passaging). Lentiviral transduction was performed with two constructs: non-targeting shRNA (NC group) and ALKBH5-targeting shRNA (shALKBH5 group). After transduction, cells were re-embedded in BME at a density of 30,000 cells/40μL. After 11 days of organoid culture, organoids were allocated into four groups: NC + DMSO, shALKBH5 + DMSO, NC + docetaxel (10 nM), and shALKBH5 + docetaxel (10 nM). On day 14, organoid growth, morphology, and response to docetaxel were assessed via microscopy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA to compare differences between experimental and control groups. All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA) or SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed p-value of ∗p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗p ≤ 0.01, and ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments.
