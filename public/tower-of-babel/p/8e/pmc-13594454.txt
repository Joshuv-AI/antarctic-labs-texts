Epidemiologic shift and future projections of atrial fibrillation and flutter in North Africa and the Middle East region: A comprehensive Global Burden of Disease analysis
Authors: Ibrahim Ghayada, Basma Hammad, Mohammad Shahin, Saeed Abdel Kamel, Rand Al-Huneidy, Yahya Almazaraa, Abdalrahman Al-slaimieh, Hussien Al-Somadi, Adnan Alkayal, Muhammad Hammouri, Yasser Lafi, Adees Vartan Bedrosian, Abdallah Al-Ani, Mohammad Hajjiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOwing to the limited availability of data pertaining to atrial fibrillation and flutter (AFF) in the North Africa and the Middle East (NAME) region, a systematic analysis of epidemiologic trends is crucial.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the epidemiologic burden of AFF across the NAME using the Global Burden of Disease 2021 and project trends over the next 10 years.MethodsData on age-standardized incidence (ASIR), prevalence (ASPR), mortality (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life years (ASDRs) were retrieved from the Global Burden of Disease study. Temporal trends between 1990 and 2021 were explored using estimated average percentage change (EAPC) and joinpoint regression. Autoregressive integrated moving average forecasting was applied to project future trends until 2031.ResultsAcross the NAME region, ASIR (EAPC, 0.03) and ASPR (EAPC, 0.10) demonstrated significant upward trends, exclusively among males. ASMR (EAPC, 0.49) and ASDR (EAPC, 0.23) demonstrated a significant upward trend in both sexes. Joinpoint regression revealed higher ASMR and ASDR burden among females. The burden of disability-adjusted life years was lower for the NAME nation than the global disability-adjusted life year rate across all age groups for both 1990 and 2021. The sociodemographic index was positively correlated with ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR. Autoregressive integrated moving average projections indicate a probable rise in ASIR, ASMR, and ASPR, but not in ASDR, by 2031.ConclusionOur study demonstrated the growing burden of AFF across the NAME region with clear disparities by sex, geography, and economic status. Compared with males, females continue to have a disproportionate share of AFF-related mortality and disability.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Atrial flutter, Global Burden of Disease, Epidemiology, Burden, Projections

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation and flutter (AFF) are the most sustained arrhythmias worldwide.1 These conditions often lead to severe systemic complications affecting both patients’ mortality and morbidity.2 In addition, they result in significant direct and indirect familial and societal costs.3 The etiology and severity of these conditions are complex, given that they are influenced by multiple overlapping genetic, biological, and environmental risk factors.4 Despite the advancements and innovations in the treatment of AFF, the global prevalence, incidence, and mortality of these conditions have markedly increased during the past 3 decades.5,6 Recent reports showcase a sharp rise in AFF-related hospitalizations, which only fortifies its impact on patients and health care systems.7 Interestingly, even on optimal therapy, patients with AFF are at a high risk of stroke, heart failure, and death.8

The rising burden of AFF at the global, national, and local levels is poorly understood.9,10 The epidemiology of AFF-related burden varies per geographic region, age, sex, socioeconomic level, and education.6,11 It is believed that such disparities are augmented by economic globalization, given that higher disease burden is often associated with less developed regions and poor socioeconomic circumstances.12

Although the epidemiology, treatment, and clinical outcomes among patients with AFF have been thoroughly studied in Europe and North America, evidence is scarce for other regions, particularly the North Africa and the Middle East (NAME) region.13 The NAME region encompasses a wide range of nations with unique cultural and economic characteristics; nonetheless, there are relatively few epidemiologic studies in this region.13,14 Syntheses of evidence within the NAME region demonstrate a relatively earlier disease onset with higher prevalence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors.15 In addition, risk stratification tools (eg, CHADSVASC or HASBLED) carried lower predictive accuracies when applied to NAME-based cohorts.16

Considering the dearth of evidence on AFF within the region, a comprehensive and systematic analysis of the epidemiologic trends and patterns of AFF within the NAME region is imperative for the development of targeted interventions, earlier prevention, and optimized resource allocation. Therefore, this study aimed to provide an updated evaluation of the trends in AFF epidemiology at the level of the NAME region stratified by sex, age, and country using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. The new GBD 2021 study improved its methodology by including more data sources, re-estimating demographic inputs, and adopting the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision, for outcome consistency. Moreover, this analysis provides projections of the burden of AFF over the next 10 years.

Methodology

Overview

We conducted a secondary analysis of the GBD 2021 estimates on AFF across the NAME region. The GBD is a comprehensive international effort aimed at measuring the epidemiologic burden attributed to diseases and injuries by age, sex, and location from 1980 to 2021 across 204 countries, 21 regions, and 7 super-regions. Its metrics of burden include incidence, prevalence, deaths, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) measured for 369 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors. The methods for GBD estimation are published elsewhere.17 The NAME region is 1 of the 7 super-regions in the GBD study and comprises the following countries: Afghanistan, Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Yemen.

This study adheres to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting.18

Data sources

All GBD 2021 data concerning AFF was extracted from the Global Health Data Exchange query tool (VizHub – GBD Results [healthdata.org]). Incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALY estimations were extracted alongside their 95% uncertainty intervals. Incidence rate represents the number of individuals diagnosed as having a disease in a particular calendar year per 100,000 persons. Prevalence rate represents the total number of cases of a given disease in a specified population at a designated time. Death rate (ie, mortality) represents the number of individuals dead because of a disease in a particular calendar year per 100,000 persons. YLDs could be described as years lived in less-than-ideal health and are measured by taking the prevalence of the disease multiplied by its disability weight. YLLs are years lost to premature mortality and are calculated by subtracting the age at death from the longest possible life expectancy for a person at that age. DALY is the sum of YLLs and YLDs, and its rate represents the total number of healthy years lost owing to a specific disease or risk factor at the country, regional, or global level.

Metrics were extracted for the entire NAME cohort, by country, sex, and age group. A total of 15 age groups were determined at 5-year intervals (ie, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94). In addition, we included 2 age groups that comprised age ranges higher or lower than 5 years (<20 years and >95 years). Also included was the sociodemographic index (SDI), which is a composite indicator for the social and economic conditions of countries. It is the geometric mean of multiple indices, including the total fertility rate for those younger than 25 years, mean education for those aged 15 years and older, and lag-distributed income per capita.19

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive analyses of incidence, death, DALY, and prevalence rates per region, sex, year, and country. All estimates were standardized by age to population using the direct standardization method. Age-standardized rates per 100,000 persons were estimated using the GBD age-standardized world population, updated to the 2021 population age structure.20 The estimated average percentage change (EAPC) was computed to describe the temporal trends in the studied metrics of burden among individuals with AFF. The computation of EAPC and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was based on 2 regression models as follows: (1) Y = α + ßX + ε, where X represents the calendar year, Y refers to the natural algorithm of metric, and ε indicates the error term. (2) EAPC = 100 × (exp(ß) − 1). The trend in the metric of burden is determined to trend upward when its EAPC and the corresponding 95% CI are >0. They were determined to trend downward when EAPC and the corresponding 95% CI are <0. Otherwise, 95% CIs encompassing 0 are considered steady. The correlation between different metrics of burden and SDI was measured using the Pearson r correlation coefficient.

Joinpoint regression was applied to analyze the temporal trends in age-standardized incidence (ASIR), death, and DALY over the period between 1980 and 2021. Joinpoint regression was conducted using the National Cancer Institute’s Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.0.2. This model uses the least squares method to estimate the changing patterns of illness rates, thus mitigating the subjectivity inherent in conventional trend analyses based on linear trends. By calculating the sum of squared residuals between the estimated and actual values, the joinpoint regression model identifies the turning point where the trend shifts.21 Trends were expressed as annual percentage change (APC), and the Z-test was used to assess whether APCs were statistically significant from 0. For statistically significant APCs, “upward” or “downward” were used to describe the trend. Nonstatistically significant APCs were considered “stable.”

Population estimates for the GBD population from 1980 to 2021 were extracted from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation database. Estimated population estimates for the years between 2022 and 2040 were extracted from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation reference forecasted population.22 Population standardization rates were adapted from the World Health Organization–adjusted world population percentages. Moreover, forecasting of other metrics of burden was constructed using an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) (p,d,q) model from 2021 to 2030.23 The letters p, d, and q represent the order of autoregression, degree of difference, and order of moving average, respectively. The best ARIMA model was selected by minimizing Akaike and Bayesian information criteria. Normal distribution of residuals was examined using the Ljung-Box Q test. Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) values were used to assess the ARIMA model’s accuracy. Model validation was conducted by fitting ARIMA models on different data time frames and comparing model metrics (eg, 1990–2000, 2000–2010, 2010–2021).

All analyses and data visualizations were conducted on R, version 4.3.3, using the appropriate and relevant packages. For all conducted analyses, P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Burden of AFF at the regional level

The all-ages number of AFF cases among individuals in the NAME region was 49,893 (38,439–65,909) in 1990 and 145,164 (114,584–186,085) in 2021, reflecting an increase of 190.9%. These increases were mirrored by both males and females (203.8% and 175.6%, respectively). ASIR for the entire cohort demonstrated a significant upward trend (EAPC, 0.03; 95% CI, 0.01–0.05). This trend was true for the male-only cohort (EAPC, 0.2; 95% CI, 0.19–0.20) but reversed in the female-only counterpart (EAPC, −0.19; 95% CI, −0.23 to −0.14). The all-ages prevalence of AFF in the NAME region was 474,265 (369,341–613,686) in 1990 and 1,417,367 (1,140,557–1,788,911) in 2021, showcasing an increase of 198.8%. The male-only cohort experienced a higher increase in prevalence than the female-only cohort (214.0% vs 180.7%, respectively). Age-standardized prevalence (ASPR) demonstrated a significant upward trend for the entire NAME cohort (EAPC, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.07–0.14). Similar to ASIR, ASPR demonstrated a significant upward trend for males (EAPC, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.27–0.29) but not females (EAPC, −0.15; 95% CI, −0.20 to −0.09).

The number of deaths attributed to AFF increased from 3373 (2613–4241) in 1990 to 11,181 (9279–12,593) in 2021, representing an increase of 231.4%. Age-standardized mortality (ASMR) showed a significant upward trend across the entire NAME cohort (EAPC, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.37–0.61); a trend that was mirrored across both the sex-stratified cohorts. Moreover, AFF was estimated to have resulted in 88,829 DALYs (69,528–110,178) in 1990 and 265,649 DALYs (218,558–309,832) in 2021, reflecting an increase of 199.1%. Age-standardized DALY (ASDR) demonstrated a significant upward trend for the NAME cohort (EAPC, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.12–0.33), male-only cohort (EAPC, 0.05; 95% CI, 0.01–0.09), and female-only cohort (EAPC, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.21–0.54). Trends for ASMR and ASDR were significantly higher for females than for males. Burden of AFF at the national level

The burden and trends of AFF in terms of incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALY across NAME countries are presented in Supplemental Tables 1–4. At the national level, all NAME countries experienced a positive increase in the number of incidences of AFF. The greatest percentage of increases in all-ages numbers between 1990 and 2021 was observed in the UAE (971.5%), Qatar (907.4%), Jordan (540.8%), Bahrain (456.2%), and Kuwait (438.6%). Similarly, ASIR demonstrated an increase from 1990 to 2021 for all NAME nations except Turkey. The highest margins of increase were noted in Oman (18.6%), Saudi Arabia (12.8%), Sudan (12.2%), and Egypt (11.8%). Across both 1990 and 2021, Iran, the UAE, and Iraq had the highest ASIR rates (Supplemental Figures 1–4). In terms of prevalence, all NAME nations showcased a positive increase in ASPR between 1990 and 2021 except Turkey. The highest margins of ASPR increase were observed in Oman (24.7%), Saudi Arabia (18.5%), Egypt (17.7%), and Sudan (17.3%). Across 1990 and 2021, Iran, the UAE, Kuwait, and Iraq had the highest ASPR (Supplemental Figures 5–8).

The greater majority of NAME nations experienced increases in ASMR between 1990 and 2021. However, Qatar (44.6%), Lebanon (32.0%), Bahrain (18.6%), Jordan (12.9%), and Egypt (12.2%) were associated with decreased ASMR percentage changes. Interestingly, Iran’s ASMR remained consistent between 1990 and 2021. In 1990, Qatar (ASMR, 7.37; 95% CI, 3.17–10.02), Bahrain (ASMR, 6.97; 95% CI, 3.4–9.27), Algeria (ASMR, 5.32; 95% CI, 3.83–6.82), and Palestine (ASMR, 5.11; 95% CI, 3.74–6.68) were the leading nations in terms of ASMR attributed to AFF. In 2021, Qatar’s lead was markedly lowered, whereas Morocco’s and Iraq’s ASMR rates demonstrated the highest margins of increase (43.3% and 48.5%, respectively) (Supplemental Figures 9–12).

In terms of DALYs attributed to AFF, similar trends were noted to ASMR. Qatar (32.4%), Lebanon (25.8%), Bahrain (16.1%), Jordan (8.2%), Turkey (5.8%), and Egypt (0.7%) were the only nations with negative percentage changes in DALY from 1990 to 2021. Qatar (ASDR, 116.2; 95% CI, 65.7–150.1), Bahrain (ASDR, 113.1; 95% CI, 66.9–143.1), Lebanon (ASDR, 92.9; 95% CI, 60.7–132.4), and Algeria (ASDR, 90.0; 95% CI, 68.6–112.9) had the highest ASDR in 1990. In contrast, Algeria (ASDR, 95.9; 95% CI, 76.7–116.4) ranked first in 2021, followed by Bahrain (ASDR, 94.9; 95% CI, 57.6–112.8), Iraq (ASDR, 93.6; 95% CI, 74.2–114.0), and Oman (ASDR, 90.9; 95% CI, 74.6–109.2) (Supplemental Figures 13–16).

The burden of DALYs attributed to AFF was lower for the NAME nation than the global DALY rate across all age groups for both 1990 and 2021 (Figure 2). This was mirrored across the male-only cohort. However, among females, the burden of DALYs was higher for the 30–34, 85–89, 90–94, and 95+ age brackets in 2021.

Burden of AFF by age

When stratified by age, age-specific incidence demonstrated a consistent rise across age groups in 2021 (Supplemental Figure 17). The age-specific incidence rate peaks for the 95+ age bracket. Throughout its rise across incrementing age brackets, the burden of age-specific incidence is markedly higher in males than in females. These trends were mirrored for age-specific prevalence (Supplemental Figure 18). However, age-specific prevalence rate peaks for the 90–94 age bracket, then decreases slightly for the 95+ bracket.

A similar rise through increasing age brackets is noted for age-specific mortality and DALY rates in 2021 (Supplemental Figures 19 and 20). However, for both of the aforementioned rates, the burden is significantly higher in females than in males across all relevant age groups.

Joinpoint regression and trend analysis

Overall, the NAME cohort exhibited a significant upward trend in terms of ASIR across the entire period between 1990 and 2021 (average APC [AAPC], 0.10; 95% CI, 0.07–0.12) (Figure 4A). Only the period between 1996 and 2005 exhibited significant decreases in ASIR (APC, −0.18; 95% CI, −0.19 to −0.16). Males demonstrated a significant upward trend (AAPC, 0.21; 95% CI, 0.20–0.22) whereas females demonstrated a significant negative trend (AAPC, −0.04; 95% CI, −0.09 to −0.004) (Supplemental Figure 21). Differences between sexes were statistically significant (AAPC difference, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.21–0.30).

In terms of ASPR, the NAME cohort demonstrated a significant upward trend between 1990 and 2021 (AAPC, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.16–0.21) (Figure 4B). The period between 1996 and 2004 was associated with a significant decrease in ASPR (APC, −0.22; 95% CI, −0.24 to −0.20). The male-only cohort demonstrated a significant upward trend (AAPC, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.29–0.32). In contrast, the ASPR for the female-only cohort was neutral. Differences between sexes were statistically significant (AAPC difference, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.25–0.35) (Supplemental Figure 22).

Similarly, ASMR showed a significant upward trend from 1990 to 2021 for the included NAME cohort (AAPC, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.57–0.95) (Figure 4C). Only the period between 1989 and 2005 demonstrated significant decreases in ASMR (APC, −0.53; 95% CI, −0.63 to −0.44). Both the male-only (AAPC, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.17–0.33) and female-only cohorts (AAPC, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.90–1.34) demonstrated significant upward trends (Supplemental Figure 23). Differences between sexes were statistically significant (AAPC difference, −0.86; 95% CI, −1.10 to −0.63).

ASDR attributed to AFF across the NAME region demonstrated a significant upward trend from 1990 to 2021 (AAPC, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.11–0.35) (Figure 4D). The period between 1996 and 2005 was associated with a significant decrease in ASPR (APC, −0.52; 95% CI, −0.63 to −0.41). These trends were consistent among males (AAPC, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.01–0.20) and females (AAPC, 3.18; 95% CI, 0.15–0.48), in which the latter had a significantly higher margin of ASMR decrease (AAPC difference, −0.21; 95% CI, −0.39 to −0.02) (Supplemental Figure 24).

Projections to 2031

Based on the ARIMA forecasts, ASIR for the entire NAME cohort is expected to fluctuate, reaching an estimated 35.33 per 100,000 persons in 2031 (ARIMA1,1,0; RMSE 0.061; MAPE 0.368) (Figure 5A). When stratified by sex, ASIR for males is estimated to increase, reaching 40.53 per 100,000 persons in 2031 (Supplemental Figure 25A). Conversely, female-specific ASIR is forecasted to decrease by 2031, reaching 29.34 per 100,000 persons (Supplemental Figure 26A).

Prediction of age-standardized rates in the next 10 years (2021–2031) for (A) incidence, (B) mortality, (C) DALY, and (D) prevalence. DALY = disability-adjusted life year; MENA = Middle East and North Africa.

ASMR is expected to increase, reaching an estimated 4.13 per 100,000 persons by 2031 (ARIMA1,1,2; RMSE 0.022; MAPE 0.453) (Figure 5B). ASMR for the male-only cohort is forecasted to stabilize, reaching 2.85 per 100,000 persons by 2021 (Supplemental Figure 25B). Conversely, ASMR for the female-only cohort is expected to increase by 2031, reaching an estimated 5.30 per 100,000 persons (Supplemental Figure 26B).

ASDR attributed to AFF demonstrated downward trends from 2021 to 2031, reaching a rate of 72.36 per 100,000 persons (ARIMA1,1,0; RMSE 0.132; MAPE 0.285) (Figure 5C). Among males and females, ASDR is projected to decrease, reaching 67.27 and 73.08 per 100,000 persons, respectively (Supplemental Figures 25 and 26C).

ASPR is expected to increase, reaching an estimated 385.5 per 100,000 persons by 2031 (ARIMA1,1,0; RMSE 0.201; MAPE 0.441) (Figure 5D). These trends were mirrored for males but not females, reaching 433.45 and 300.91 per 100,000 persons by 2031, respectively (Supplemental Figures 25 and 26D).

Discussion

This comprehensive analysis showcases a substantial increase in the burden of AFF across the NAME region in the period from 1990 to 2021. We note a significant rise in the all-ages incidence of AFF, with numbers nearly tripling from 49,893 to 145,164 cases, whereas prevalence surged from 474,265 to 1,417,367 cases. Correspondingly, AFF-related mortality increased by more than 2-fold, and the DALYs nearly tripled. These results highlight a growing public health challenge in the region. Our results contrast with a previous report by Yaghoubi and colleagues.24 They observed minimal change in ASIR from 1990 to 2019 and a slight increase in both ASMR and ASDR. Although both studies highlight the ever-increasing burden of AFF in the NAME region, our findings display a steeper and more dynamic trend. This is likely caused by the inclusion of the more recent data, the expansion of the analysis to include the years from 2019 to 2021, and the future projections included. This comparison suggests that the burden of AFF may have drastically increased in recent years beyond previous estimations.

AFF is the most widespread type of chronic arrhythmia worldwide and poses a significant public health concern. Data from the global GBD reports on the subject indicate a steady annual increase in the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs associated with AFF alongside a notable difference between both sexes.3,25 In terms of gender disparities, our results contrast those of global reports, which indicate a slightly higher overall burden for males, albeit consistent with previous studies in the region.24,26,27 Our findings showcase that females experience a disproportionately higher burden in terms of mortality and DALYs despite lower incidence and prevalence rates than males.

Such contrast may be attributed to biological, environmental, and social factors. From a physiological standpoint, females are more likely to have higher AFF recurrence and worse ablation outcomes. This phenomenon is attributed to lower repolarizing ion currents, prolonged action potential duration in female atria, greater frequency of nonpulmonary triggered activity, and more pronounced fibrotic remodeling.28, 29, 30 Other baseline pathophysiological differences include hormonal differences before and after menopause, variable expression of calcium-related ion channel subunits, and higher degrees of parasympathetic activation,31 the latter of which is associated with a higher risk of AFF owing to the extensive vagal innervation of the atrial muscle sleeves up to the pulmonary veins.32

Disparities between sexes were also demonstrated at the level of AFF treatment. The ORBIT-AF, EORP-AF, and ROCKET-AF registries demonstrated lower beta-blocker and higher digoxin usage among females.33, 34, 35 The latter proved in poor tolerability in females and a significant association with cardiac complications.36 Similarly, the literature shows that rhythm control is less likely to be used for females even when symptomatic. Females were more likely to receive pharmacologic rather than electric cardioversion than their male counterparts and are more likely to experience AFF recurrence after cardioversion.

Interestingly, females were also less likely to be referred for ablation and are often on excessive regimens of antiarrhythmic drugs, increasing their risk of higher permanent pacemaker placement.37 In their meta-analysis of 151,370 patients, Cheng et al38 demonstrated that females are significantly more likely to experience lower efficacy of AFF ablation procedures and a higher risk of complications. Interestingly, women who undergo ablation are often older than their male counterparts.30 Champsi et al39 found that older age in women is a key driver of thromboembolic risk and is the likely culprit offsetting the greater vascular comorbidity burden in men. The authors suggest that the association between sex and AFF outcomes is heavily confounded by age, comorbidity profiles, and access to therapy, which might be the case for the unadjusted trends of the GBD. In fact, Zheng et al40 recommended investigating sex differences after balancing for age to avoid biased interpretations.

From a social perspective, females face a variety of challenges. Under-recognition of AFF symptoms in women is more likely to occur because they often have atypical presentations that may be misattributed to anxiety or other noncardiac illnesses.41 Owing to the lower incidence of AFF in females, clinicians may be primed with a lower index of suspicion for AFF. Furthermore, given that females often present with delayed late AFF within the context of multiple comorbidities, they are more likely to receive fragmented care from an already exhausted health care infrastructure.42 Other notable factors include self-neglect imposed by unique gender roles (eg, exclusive caregiver roles), financial constraints, limited awareness of AFF, and lack of autonomy (eg, need for a male chaperone to access health care).43,44 Interestingly, the behavioral components of seeking health care for cardiovascular disease or related symptoms have not yet been investigated within an Arab, collectivist context.

Nonetheless, analysis from the UK Biobank registry demonstrated that, despite a poorer social determinant of health profile, females demonstrated a lower risk of cardiac adverse events and mortality.45 In their analysis of a large-scale multicentric registry involving more than 27 European countries, Houle et al46 showcased that females are associated with a lower risk of adverse events after adjustment for social determinants of health. Such a phenomenon could only be attributed to the hypothesized greater social resilience and adaptive coping mechanisms of females, which could buffer the impact of poor social characteristics on health outcomes.47 To note, these analyses are limited to the European context given that current NAME AFF registries are lacking in terms of reporting of social variables.48

The surge in the incidence of AFF in the Gulf states, most notably in Qatar and the UAE, closely mirrors the region’s rapid socioeconomic developments (eg, urbanization) and associated lifestyle shifts.3,5,49 Although the UAE showed a near-11-fold increase in both incidence and prevalence between 1990 and 2021, Turkey emerged as a regional outlier despite facing similar demographic and health challenges. These divergent trends might be linked to the latter’s adoption of proactive preventive measures, a multidisciplinary approach to management, and investment in evolving the public’s awareness.50 Nonetheless, the reliance on GBD trends in isolation necessitates a cautious interpretation of these policy-based correlations.

Similarly, the observed mortality trends reveal a widening gap in regional cardiovascular outcomes. ASMR significantly declined in Lebanon and Qatar but sharply increased in Morocco and Iraq. Such disparities are potentially reflective of variance in health care infrastructure. For example, Qatar’s investment in specialized cardiac units and stroke databases may facilitate earlier detection, intervention, and expanded access to care. These correlations were previously highlighted by Imam et al51 in their analysis of the Qatar Stroke Database. In contrast, the upward mortality trends in Iraq or Morocco may underscore the impact of resource instability, unequal insurance coverage, and, in the case of Iraq, the persistent impact of prolonged conflict.52, 53, 54, 55 The impact of these factors was demonstrated for oncology health care in Iraq.56 Although these systemic factors provide a plausible context for the GBD findings, they remain hypothetical explanations that require targeted empirical study to confirm.

Understanding the risk factors associated with mortality from AFF is central to guiding prevention efforts and optimizing clinical care. This is especially important because modifiable factors play an important role in the progression and development of AFF. Active management of such factors, such as hypertension, obesity, and smoking, has been shown to prevent and improve AFF’s prognosis.57,58 Among these factors, high systolic blood pressure reflects the broad influence of hypertension on cardiovascular health in the region. The negative impact of high systolic blood pressure on AFF is well established given that it is known to increase the risk of thromboembolic events, worsen myocardial ischemia, and accelerate cardiac remodeling.59 As for thromboembolic events, previous reports from the 2019 GBD database show a 0.5% reduction in the age-standardized point prevalence of stroke with a 27.8% decline in mortality compared with 1990. Furthermore, over the same period, the DALY rate decreased by 32% in the region. Lebanon had the lowest DALY for stroke, whereas Afghanistan scored the highest. Regionally, women were more likely than men across all age groups to have a stroke, with the highest incidence rate occurring in the age group 60–64 years. Furthermore, the report noted a negative correlation between the stroke burden and SDI between 1990 and 2019.60

Recent evidence analyzing metabolic risk factors across the NAME region further underscores the significance of these trends. Although age-standardized death and DALY rates attributable to high systolic blood pressure and high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol have declined over the past 3 decades, other risk factors have shown a drastic increase, particularly high fasting plasma glucose and body mass index (BMI). Elevated BMI had an increase of 76%, whereas high fasting plasma glucose increased by more than 92% between 1990 and 2019.61 Adult obesity rates increased from 15.1% to 20.7% by 2015. In the same year, 6.3% of all DALYs across all age groups and approximately 10% of all mortalities in the Eastern Mediterranean Region were attributed to elevated BMI.62 Obesity-related structural and functional changes, such as excess adiposity, fibrosis, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, ion channel alterations, and autonomic nervous system dysfunction, all contribute to the development and progression of AFF. The growth of epicardial adipose tissue stimulates arrhythmogenesis through paracrine signaling and direct tissue infiltration and is speculated to be a crucial factor leading to AFF in obese individuals.61,63, 64, 65 Poor dietary practices, through a low intake of whole grains, nuts, and seafood, along with sedentary behavior, are key elements that further increase this risk. Such factors are particularly pronounced among women.63

Considering these findings, a number of large-scale, multisectoral interventions are recommended and urgently needed. In high SDI regions, early detection systems should be integrated within cardiovascular risk assessments of adults, especially for asymptomatic and paroxysmal episodes.66 In the case of low SDI regions, increased resource allocation through global partnerships, twinning programs, digitized health infrastructure, and volume guarantees is necessary to establish standardized treatment protocols, catheter ablation infrastructure, and prevention programs. Health care hubs within the region should strive to promote affordable services to affected patients in areas in distress and war-torn regions. At the population level, concerned authorities and policy makers must undertake serious measures to investigate the public’s understanding of AFF, raise awareness if necessary, and curb the burden of modifiable risk factors, particularly smoking, unhealthy diet, and lack of exercise.67 Proactive measurement of AFF’s attributable risk factors, such as hypertension, obesity, and diabetes, could enhance prevention of disease and improve both clinical and economic outcomes.3 In addition, it is critical to further investigate the sex disparities in AFF burden in women by developing personalized treatment strategies, encouraging early screening, and enhancing sex-responsive education.27 Finally, it is recommended to further broaden the depth and quality of research conducted on AFF in the NAME region through the establishment of a unified registry that not only tracks clinical characteristics and outcomes longitudinally but also provides sufficient documentation on the social determinants of health and economic costs.

Despite methodological improvements in the latest iterations of the GBD database, the limited availability of source data remains the most significant constraint in our study. Insufficient data entry and reporting may cast doubt on the accuracy of the collected data. Furthermore, the absence of high-quality data could introduce bias. Such challenges are likely to be more evident in lower SDI countries or those affected by conflict in the region, given that fewer health care facilities are available to conduct epidemiologic studies. In addition, ARIMA model predictions offer short-term projections that do not account for changes in interventions or policies, as well as emerging risk factors that may alter disease trajectories. Finally, all of the conducted analyses are 1-dimensional because they are not adjusted to risk factors attributable to AFF (eg, obesity) nor concomitant comorbid diseases (eg, diabetes). Lack of adjustment to these clinical profiles may invalidate the comparisons between nations, overestimate the true burden of disease while masking true drivers, and increase the risk of ecological fallacies when interpreting trends.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the growing burden of AFF across the NAME region from 1990 to 2021 with clear disparities by sex, socioeconomic development, and geography. Although incidence and prevalence rates have generally increased, particularly among males, females continue to have a disproportionate share of AFF-related mortality and disability, highlighting the urgent need for attention in those regards. The steep rise in AFF burden in Gulf countries is closely related to rapid urbanization and shifting lifestyle patterns, whereas countries like Turkey are prime examples of how strategic health care interventions are crucial to mitigating disease trends. The noted contrast between trajectories of countries such as Qatar and Lebanon, where mortality rates significantly declined, and those of Iraq and Morocco, where it continues to rise, further accentuates the influence of health care infrastructure and political instability. Given that projections suggest persistent increases in incidence and mortality, focused efforts and targeted interventions on prevention, early detection, and equitable access to proper health care services are elemental to reducing the burden of AFF and improving cardiovascular outcomes across the region.
