Comprehensive study of Maillard glycated hydrolysate of ‘Fengdan’ peony (Paeonia ostii) seed meal proteins: characterization, antioxidant properties and aroma profile
Authors: Tianrong Zhang, Wanyu Yang, Yijia Liang, Jiameng Wang, Ming Zhao, Qiongmin Li, Yuan Zhao, Jinling Fan, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peony seed meal (PSM) is an abundant byproduct after oil extraction with high protein content. To enhance the antioxidant and aroma properties of PSM hydrolysate (PSMH), Maillard reaction-mediated glycation was performed using ribose, xylose, fructose, or glucose. Among all treatments, ribose-derived Maillard reaction products (MRPs-ribose) exhibited the most pronounced Maillard reaction progression and the strongest antioxidant activity, increasing DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging rates by 45.50% and 46.30% versus PSMH. FTIR analysis indicated higher β-sheet and lower random-coil contents, reflecting a more ordered conformation. MRPs-ribose also showed a more negative ζ-potential and reduced polydispersity index, suggesting improved dispersion stability. HS-SPME–GC–MS analysis identified 22 volatile compounds, demonstrating enrichment of aldehydes and pyrazines and a more complex aroma profile. Cellular assays confirmed MRPs-ribose restored HepG2 viability to 91.9% after H2O2 injury, outperforming PSMH. Collectively, these results support ribose-mediated Maillard glycation as an effective strategy to upgrade PSMH for functional food and nutraceutical applications.

Introduction

Oxidative stress is a physiological state resulting from an imbalance between the generation and elimination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under internal or external stress. Excessive ROS can damage biological macromolecules such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby disrupting normal physiological functions and contributing to the development of various diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and cancer (Zhu et al., 2024). With the increasing prevalence of oxidative stress-related diseases, there is a growing demand for safe and effective antioxidants. However, the limited efficacy and potential safety concerns associated with synthetic antioxidants have stimulated increasing interest in natural, food-derived antioxidant peptides as promising alternatives (Han et al., 2021).

Food-derived antioxidant peptides are initially inactive within parent proteins and are released through enzymatic hydrolysis, after which they can exert biological activity. These peptides are capable of scavenging free radicals and inhibiting lipid peroxidation, thereby helping to maintain redox homeostasis and alleviate oxidative stress (Lv et al., 2022). Typically composed of short amino acid sequences, antioxidant peptides exhibit favorable properties such as low molecular weight, high activity, and good bioavailability (Xu et al., 2024). To date, antioxidant peptides have been widely identified from diverse sources, including animals, plants, and microorganisms, and several have demonstrated protective effects against oxidative damage at the cellular level (Li et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024; Zhao & Liu, 2023).

Among the many protein-rich plant sources, peony seed meal (PSM) is produced in large quantities as a major byproduct of peony seed oil extraction, exceeding 100,000 tons annually in China. In addition, PSM has been reported to contain up to 32.44% protein and is rich in essential amino acids, making it highly suitable for enzymatic hydrolysis to release peptides with diverse biological activities (Gao et al., 2018). This abundance and nutritional quality render the utilization of PSM highly valuable and will broaden plant-protein resources for functional food ingredient development. For example, peptides derived from PSM hydrolysates produced by bromelain have been reported to significantly enhance immune organ indices and cytokine secretion, thereby improving immune function in mice (Gong et al., 2020). Additionally, PSM protein hydrolysate (PSMH) exhibited notable inhibitory effects on α-glucosidase and α-amylase activities, indicating their promising potential for blood glucose regulation (Qiao et al., 2020).

Furthermore, the antioxidant properties of PSMH-derived peptides have also been extensively investigated. For instance, a previous study compared the antioxidant activities of PSMH prepared using various proteases, including alcalase, neutrase, papain, protamex, and flavourzyme. Among them, the alcalase hydrolysate displayed the highest radical-scavenging activity (DPPH IC50 = 0.18 mg/mL; ABTS IC50 = 1.57 mg/mL), Fe2+ chelating activity (IC50 = 0.99 mg/mL), and reducing power (0.594) (Wang et al., 2021). In another study, antioxidant peptides were identified from the <3 kDa fraction after ultrafiltration of PSMH obtained using alkaline protease, based on peptidomics and virtual screening. Among these, LPSHPPR and LHPHFR exerted protective effects against oxidative stress by activating the Keap1–Nrf2 pathway, highlighting their potential application in functional food development (Wang et al., 2025). Despite these promising findings, the application of PSMH as a natural antioxidant remains limited by factors such as poor stability, low bioactivity under certain conditions, and undesirable sensory attributes. Consequently, chemical modification strategies, such as the Maillard reaction (MR), have emerged as promising approaches to overcome these limitations and enhance peptide functionality.

MR is a non-enzymatic process that occurs between the carbonyl groups of reducing sugars and the amino groups of amino acids, peptides, or proteins. This reaction leads to the formation of a wide range of compounds known as Maillard reaction products (MRPs), which include intermediate products, dark brown pigments, and volatile compounds. In addition to significantly enhancing the aroma and color of many food products, MRPs also exhibit notable biological activities, such as antioxidant and antibacterial effects, thereby conferring additional health benefits (Wang et al., 2023). For example, glycated crab shell-derived bioactive peptides prepared with fructose were reported to exhibit enhanced antioxidant and antibacterial activities, which increased with fructose concentration and reaction pH, highlighting the effectiveness of Maillard modification in improving peptide bioactivity (Jiang et al., 2018). Similarly, Maillard-type glycosylation of pea protein hydrolysates with D-xylose has been shown to generate glycopeptides with enhanced antioxidant capacity and pronounced hepatoprotective effects in an alcohol-induced liver injury zebrafish model(Li et al., 2025). In another study, the MR markedly enhanced both the antioxidant activity and umami taste of peanut protein hydrolysates (Xing et al., 2024). Moreover, a recent study also reported the glycation of PSMH via the MR (Shang et al., 2020). However, the resulting MRPs from Shang et al. (2020) were primarily investigated in terms of their aroma profiles and corresponding structural features, without assessing changes in antioxidant activity, and demonstrated that MRPs prepared using ribose and xylose exhibited stronger meaty and umami attributes. Therefore, MRPs have gained increasing attention for their potential applications in the food industry, where they are used not only to enhance the aroma profile but also to improve the nutritional and functional properties of food products. However, prolonged exposure to high temperatures during the MR can lead to the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which have been linked to cytotoxicity and inflammation (Wu et al., 2021). Accordingly, systematic evaluation of the cytotoxicity and potential adverse effects of MRPs is essential to ensure their safety in food applications.

Given the promising yet limited antioxidant activity and stability of PSMH-derived peptides, the MR is believed to offer a viable strategy to enhance their antioxidant activity, physicochemical properties, and aroma profile. To test this hypothesis, the present study systematically investigated the impact of MR on enhancing the antioxidant capacity and modulating the structural and aroma profile of PSMH. MRPs were generated by reacting PSMH with reducing sugars, including ribose, xylose, fructose, and glucose, followed by comprehensive analyses of structural changes and antioxidant activities. Additionally, the cytoprotective effects and potential cytotoxicity of ribose-derived MRPs were assessed in H2O2-stimulated HepG2 cells. These findings provide evidence for the effects of MR-induced peptide modification and support the development of PSMH-based functional ingredients with improved antioxidant functionality.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The “FengDan” peony seed meal (Paeonia ostii) used in this study was provided by Guohuafang Peony Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Luoyang, China). The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose) was obtained from Cytiva (Logan, USA). Penicillin-streptomycin solution, trypsin-EDTA solution, and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased from Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was acquired from En Moasai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China).

Glycation via Maillard reaction

Initially, proteins were extracted from PSM using alkali extraction followed by acid precipitation, according to a previously described method (Zhao et al., 2023). The extracted PSM proteins were then hydrolyzed with neutrase under conditions optimized in our previous work, specifically at a temperature of 39 °C, pH 7.1, a substrate concentration of 1.0% (w/v), an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 6.4% (w/w), and a reaction time of 4.9 h. After hydrolysis, the reaction was terminated by heating the mixture at 100 °C in a water bath for 10 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was freeze-dried to obtain PSMH.

Separately, ribose, xylose, fructose, or glucose were individually dissolved in deionized water to a final concentration of 20 mg/mL, and then mixed with the 10 mg/mL PSMH solution at a 1:1 volume ratio. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 7.0 using 1 mol/L aqueous HCl and NaOH, and the reaction was carried out at 95 °C for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h as longer reaction times led to excessive browning and undesirable thickening of the reaction mixture in preliminary trials. Following the reaction, the samples were immediately cooled in an ice-water bath, freeze-dried, and stored at −20 °C for further analysis. As a result, four distinct MRPs were obtained: MRPs-ribose, MRPs-xylose, MRPs-fructose, and MRPs-glucose.

Intermediate products and browning intensity

The formation of intermediate products and browning intensity of the MRPs (MRPs-ribose, MRPs-xylose, MRPs-fructose, and MRPs-glucose heated for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h) were evaluated by measuring absorbance at 294 nm and 420 nm, respectively, using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (L5S, INESA Analytical Instrument Co., LTD, Shanghai, China). Prior to analysis, samples were diluted 30-fold with deionized water for the detection of intermediate products and 5-fold for browning intensity measurement.

Fluorescent intensity

Fluorescence intensity of the MRPs were measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Spark 10 M, Tecan Co., Männedorf, Switzerland) at an excitation wavelength of 347 nm and an emission wavelength of 415 nm. MRPs were diluted 30-fold with deionized water.

In vitro chemical radical-scavenging assays

DPPH radical-scavenging activity

All samples were initially diluted 200-fold with deionized water, based on a previously reported method with slight modifications (Delgado-Andrade et al., 2005). Briefly, the diluted MRPs were mixed with 40 μg/mL DPPH ethanol solution at a volume ratio of 1:2 (v/v). The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and then the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The DPPH radical-scavenging rate was calculated using the following formula:(1)DPPH radical−scavenging rate%=1−A1−A2/A0×100where A0 represents the absorbance of the blank containing DPPH solution and deionized water without sample, A1 represents the absorbance of the sample solution reacting with DPPH solution, and A2 represents the absorbance of sample solution mixed with ethanol without DPPH.

ABTS radical-scavenging activity

The ABTS radical-scavenging activity was evaluated according to a previously reported method with slight modifications (Ni et al., 2021). To prepare the ABTS working solution, the absorbance was adjusted to 0.70 ± 0.01 at 734 nm. The diluted samples were then mixed with ABTS working solution at a volume ratio of 1:3 (v/v). The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 6 min, and then the absorbance was measured at 734 nm. The ABTS radical-scavenging rate was calculated as follows:(2)ABTS radical−scavenging rate%=1−A1−A2/A0×100where A0 represents the absorbance of a blank containing ABTS solution and deionized water without sample, A1 represents the absorbance of the sample solution reacting with ABTS solution, and A2 represents the absorbance of sample solution mixed with ethanol without ABTS.

Amino acids composition

The amino acid content of freeze-dried PSMH and MRPs (10 h) was determined according to the Chinese National Food Safety Standards (GB 5009.124-2016, 2016). Briefly, samples were hydrolyzed in 10 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid, at 110 °C for 22 h. After hydrolysis, the samples were cooled and evaporated to dryness. The residue was then completely dissolved in 1 mL of pH 2.2 citrate buffer and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Subsequently, separation was performed with an ion exchange column using an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Results were expressed in mg/g.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Freeze-dried PSMH and MRPs (10 h) were individually mixed with KBr and compressed into pellets. FTIR spectra were subsequently recorded in transmission mode over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Shanghai, China). The spectra in the amide I region were deconvoluted using PeakFit 4.12 software to analyze secondary structure components.

Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI) and ζ-potential

Freeze-dried PSMH and MRPs (10 h) were dispersed in deionized water to a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. The dispersion was continuously stirred on a magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm overnight to ensure complete hydration. Particle size, PDI, and ζ-potential were then measured using a laser particle size analyzer (BeNano 90 Zeta, Dandong Bettersize Instruments Ltd., Dandong, China).

Volatile compound analysis

Volatile compounds were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction–gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME–GC–MS), which was previously optimized and internally validated for protein/peptide-based food matrices based on a reported protocol (Han et al., 2018). Briefly, 30 mg of freeze-dried PSMH and MRPs (10 h) were dissolved in 20 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution, and 50 μL of 2,4,6-trimethylpyridine (50 μg/mL) was added as an internal standard. The headspace vials containing the mixture were sealed and incubated at 60 °C for 20 min. Volatile compounds were then extracted using a DVB/CAR/PDMS SPME fiber (Sigma Aldrich Inc., St. Louis, USA) exposed to the headspace for 30 min. After extraction, the fiber was desorbed in the GC injection port at 250 °C for 5 min prior to GC–MS analysis. All samples were analyzed under identical HS-SPME conditions to ensure comparability.

Volatile compounds were analyzed using an Agilent 6890 N gas chromatograph coupled with a 5973 A mass selective detector (Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, USA). Separation was carried out on a highly polar capillary column (HP-INNOWax 60 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, USA). The GC oven temperature was initially set at 40 °C for 5 min, then increased to 250 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and held for 10 min. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing the obtained mass spectra with those in the NIST17.L library, and only those compounds with a matching score greater than 80% were considered valid volatile components. The concentrations of volatiles were quantified by calculating the peak area ratios of the analytes to the internal standard (2,4,6-trimethylpyridine).

Cellular antioxidant activity assay

Cytotoxicity evaluation

The HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. After reaching approximately 80% confluence, HepG2 cells were digested by trypsinization and inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL (200 μL/well), followed by incubation for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with different concentrations of freeze-dried PSMH (200, 250, 300, 350, and 400 μg/mL) or MRPs-ribose (50, 75, 100, 125, and 150 μg/mL) for another 24 h, based on preliminary MTT screening. Following treatment, cell viability was immediately assessed using the MTT assay. Cell viability was calculated according to the following formula:(3)Cell viability%=As−Ab/Ac−Ab×100where As represents the absorbance of the experimental group containing cells, MTT, and the test substance; Ac represents the absorbance of the control group containing cells and MTT without the test substance; and Ab represents the absorbance of the blank group containing MTT without cells and the test substance.

Cytoprotective effects of PSMH and MRPs-ribose

The cytoprotective effect of PSMH and MRPs-ribose was evaluated using an H2O2-induced oxidative stress HepG2 cell model. In the control group, cells were cultured in complete medium for 36 h without any treatment. For the sample groups, cells were incubated with PSMH at concentrations ranging from 200 to 400 μg/mL, or MRPs-ribose at concentrations ranging from 50 to 150 μg/mL for 24 h, and subsequently treated with H2O2 (200 μL, 500 μmol/L) for an additional 12 h. For the model group, cells were treated in the same manner as the sample groups, except that no sample was added before H2O2 exposure. After all treatments, cell viability was determined using the MTT assay. Protective effect was calculated using the following equation:(4)Cell viability%=As−Ab/Ac−Ab×100where As represents the absorbance of the experimental group containing cells, MTT, and the test substance; Ac represents the absorbance of the control group containing cells and MTT without the test substance; and Ab represents the absorbance of the blank group containing MTT without cells and the test substance.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Pearson's correlation analysis and heatmap visualization were conducted using Origin 2022 software with the “Correlation Plot” plug-in.

Results and discussion

Maillard intermediates and browning development

The formation of brown pigments and the corresponding color changes in samples are key characteristics of the MR system between proteins and reducing sugars (Shen et al., 2021). In the early stages of the MR, carbonyl and amino groups undergo condensation and rearrangement to form Amadori products, which subsequently generate various highly reactive compounds through multiple reaction pathways. These intermediates exhibit UV absorption at 294 nm. As the reaction proceeds, the intermediates further polymerize into nitrogen-containing brown pigments that show visible absorption at 420 nm. Therefore, absorbance values at 294 nm and 420 nm are commonly used as indicators of the concentrations of intermediate and advanced MRPs, respectively.

As the reaction time increased, both the concentration of intermediate MRPs and browning intensity steadily rose (Fig. 1A–B). Among all sugar types, MRPs-ribose exhibited the highest absorbance at 294 nm (A294 = 1.36 ± 0.03) at 10 h, followed by MRPs-xylose, MRPs-fructose, and MRPs-glucose, which were 31.51%, 35.48%, and 37.89% lower, respectively, compared to MRPs-ribose at the same time. Similarly, browning intensity, as measured by absorbance at 420 nm, followed a comparable trend, with MRPs-ribose showing the highest value (A420 = 0.74 ± 0.01), followed by MRPs-xylose, MRPs-fructose, and MRPs-glucose. These results indicate that the type of reducing sugar significantly affects the extent and kinetics of the MR. The notably higher intermediate formation and browning intensity in MRPs-ribose and MRPs-xylose compared to MRPs-fructose and MRPs-glucose can be attributed to the structural differences between pentoses and hexoses. Due to their smaller molecular size and higher proportion of open-chain forms, pentoses offer more reactive carbonyl groups, facilitating a faster MR under thermal conditions (Han et al., 2018). Among the pentoses, ribose is recognized as more reactive than xylose. For example, MR between ribose and fish scale gelatin hydrolysates proceeds more rapidly and extensively than with xylose or glucose under identical conditions (Chen et al., 2019). This reactivity is consistent with the rapid increases in A294 and A420 for MRPs-ribose, supporting the accelerated formation of both intermediate and advanced MRPs.Fig. 1Maillard reaction progression of PSMH and reducing sugars (ribose, xylose, fructose, or glucose): (A) Absorbance at 294 nm (intermediate products); (B) Absorbance at 420 nm (browning intensity); (C) Fluorescence intensity; (D) DPPH radical-scavenging activity; (E) ABTS radical-scavenging activity; and (F) Pearson's correlation between radical-scavenging activities and MR indicators.Fig. 1

Formation of fluorescent Maillard intermediates

During the MR, Amadori products may undergo dehydration and cleavage, leading to the formation of fluorescent compounds and colorless reductones (Bai et al., 2022). Therefore, fluorescence intensity often serves as a reliable early-stage indicator of the MR process. As shown in Fig. 1C, the fluorescence intensity of MRPs increased progressively with reaction time. Among all MRPs, MRPs-ribose exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity at 10 h (49,337.67 ± 210.38), which was 1.15 to 1.33 times higher than those of other MRPs (p < 0.05). Previous studies have indicated that fluorescent compounds formed during the MR can act as hydrogen donors, suggesting their potential contribution to the antioxidant capacity of MRPs (Han et al., 2019). Therefore, to verify this functional implication, the antioxidant activities of different MRPs were subsequently evaluated.

In vitro chemical radical-scavenging activity

DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging assays were employed to evaluate the antioxidant properties of the MRPs prepared in this study. As shown in Fig. 1D–E, the radical-scavenging abilities of MRPs increased steadily with reaction time, indicating that the accumulation of Maillard-derived products contributes to antioxidant capacity. Among all samples, MRPs-ribose exhibited the highest activity at 10 h, with a DPPH scavenging rate of 20.34 ± 0.08%, representing a 45.50% increase compared to unreacted PSMH. Similarly, its ABTS scavenging rate reached 63.42 ± 0.08%, demonstrating a 46.30% improvement. These results are consistent with previous findings. For instance, Chen et al. (2019) reported that MRPs derived from gelatin hydrolysates of grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idellus) scales exhibited significantly higher DPPH and ABTS scavenging capacities than their unreacted counterparts. Additionally, Jin et al. (2018) found that MRPs from scallop (Patinopecten yessoensis) gonad hydrolysates showed stronger DPPH scavenging activity for ribose-derived MRPs than those from xylose, glucose, arabinose, or rhamnose with a trend consistent with the present study. Furthermore, MR-mediated glycosylation of pea protein hydrolysates with D-xylose was reported to markedly improve DPPH scavenging activity, resulting in an enhancement of 50.37% relative to the unglycated hydrolysates(Li et al., 2025), which is comparable to the improvement observed in this study. Collectively, these findings suggest that MR effectively enhances the antioxidant properties of protein hydrolysates, and ribose is a particularly effective modifier in generating high-activity MRPs.

Generally, an increased fluorescence intensity indicates the formation of reductones and cross-linked structures, while increases in A294 and A420 correspond to the accumulation of Amadori rearrangement products and the formation of high-molecular-weight melanoidins, respectively, all of which contribute significantly to the overall antioxidant potential. In this study, Pearson's correlation analysis demonstrated strong associations between radical-scavenging activities and MR indicators (Fig. 1F). Specifically, DPPH scavenging activity showed strong positive correlations with all three indicators (A294, A420, and fluorescence intensity), with correlation coefficients (r) all exceeding 0.95, implying contributions from both early-stage fluorescent intermediates and late-stage melanoidins. In contrast, ABTS activity was more strongly and positively correlated with fluorescence intensity (r = 0.93), suggesting a greater influence from early-stage products. Consistent with these findings, a previous study on MRPs generated in the chicken bone hydrolysate–galactose system also reported that DPPH scavenging activity was positively related to all three indicators, albeit with lower correlation coefficients (0.729–0.760) (Nie et al., 2017). Similarly, another study demonstrated that fluorescence is more effective than browning for monitoring the formation of MRPs with free radical-scavenging activities (Morales & Jiménez-Pérez, 2001). Taken together, these results indicate that the enhanced antioxidant activity of MRPs arises from the combined effects of structurally diverse Maillard-derived compounds and can be indirectly monitored using the above indicators.

Amino acids composition

In this study, the total amino acid content was significantly reduced to approximately one-third after the MR compared to PSMH (Supplementary Table 1), which can be attributed to multiple concurrent pathways, including Strecker degradation and thermal degradation of amino acids to form volatile compounds, and the cross-linking between reducing sugars and free amino groups to form non-volatile compounds. The proportions of amino acid loss were comparable for MRPs-ribose (67.52%), MRPs-xylose (68.44%), and MRPs-fructose (69.24%), all of which were higher than that observed for MRPs-glucose (64.43%). This result partially reflects the differences in sugar reactivity, where glucose, as previously discussed, exhibits the lowest reactivity in MR. However, among the three more reactive sugars, despite the highest reactivity, ribose did not cause the greatest amino acid loss, suggesting that amino acid consumption is not solely dependent on sugar reactivity, but rather the combined effects of Strecker degradation, thermal degradation, and sugar-amino cross-linking all play important roles in the progression of MR.

For individual amino acids, all decreased significantly, with arginine, alanine, histidine, glycine, and lysine ranking among the top five most depleted in MR for all four MRPs, except that lysine was replaced by aspartic acid in MRPs-glucose (Fig. 2), indicating that these amino acids are the predominant amino reactants of PSMH in MR. The pronounced losses of arginine, histidine, and lysine can be attributed to their basic nature, which renders them more reactive in the initial condensation steps of the MR (Qi et al., 2024). Mechanistically, the MR begins with a nucleophilic addition step, where a Lewis base (nucleophile, Nü) reacts with the carbonyl group of reducing sugars to initiate subsequent cascades. In food systems, reactive nucleophiles typically include amino acid side chains such as the guanidino group of arginine (pKa ≈ 12.5), the ε-amino group of lysine (pKa ≈ 10), and the imidazole group of histidine (pKa ≈ 6.5), explaining their high reactivity during MR (El Hosry et al., 2025). In contrast, alanine and glycine, although generally less reactive than basic amino acids in the early MR stages, still showed substantial depletion, likely through pathways such as Strecker degradation or thermal breakdown. Furthermore, the observed reductions in arginine, lysine, and histidine could also have a positive impact on the aroma profiles of the MRPs, as these basic amino acids are known to impart pronounced bitterness. Their depletion has been linked to improved aroma profiles in previous studies (Li & Liu, 2022). Taken together, the MR-induced alterations in amino acid composition, characterized by the formation of MRPs and the reduction of bitter-taste–associated amino acids, are likely to synergistically enhance antioxidant potential while contributing to a more desirable aroma profile.

FTIR spectroscopy analysis

FTIR spectroscopy serves as a valuable tool for elucidating vibrational characteristics of functional groups and conformational changes in protein secondary structures. As shown in Fig. 3A, the MRPs derived from different sugars exhibited similar transmittance peak positions but varied markedly in peak intensities, reflecting different degrees of reaction. The broad transmittance peak around 3400 cm−1 is attributed to O—H stretching vibrations, while the band near 2930 cm−1 corresponds to C—H stretching. After the MR, the absorbance at 2930 cm−1 decreased, likely due to the consumption or structural transformation of aliphatic C—H groups during condensation and dehydration reactions, as well as the formation of new covalent linkages that modified their vibrational environment. Both peaks also exhibited a slight blue shift toward higher wavenumbers, reflecting alterations in hydrogen-bonding interactions and molecular packing. Similarly, the amide II band at 1556 cm−1 also underwent a red shift, which further supports backbone modifications induced by glycation. According to Wnorowski and Yaylayan (2003), such red shifts in the amide I and II regions are indicative of Schiff base (CN) formation, an early-stage MR product formed via condensation between amino and carbonyl groups. Alterations were also observed in the amide III region (1450–1230 cm−1), which are attributed to the out-of-plane bending vibrations of C—H bonds in aromatic rings (Chen et al., 2020). Besides, an enhanced absorption intensity was observed in the range of 1260–1000 cm−1 after MR, which can be attributed to the increased number of hydroxyl groups and carbon‑oxygen bonds formed in the MRPs, consistent with previous findings reported for MRPs derived from rice protein hydrolysates and glucose (Xiao et al., 2021). Collectively, these spectral changes suggest extensive covalent interactions between reducing sugars and PSMH during the MR, especially involving structural modifications to the amide I, II, and III bands.

Beyond these covalent interactions, the FTIR spectra revealed conformational rearrangements of the protein backbone. To elucidate these changes in secondary structure, the amide I region was deconvoluted using second derivative fitting to resolve overlapping peaks (Fig. 3B–F), and the relative content of each secondary structure component was quantified by integrating the corresponding peak areas (Table 1). Generally, PSMH was primarily composed of β-turns (52.67%). After the MR, pronounced structural reorganization was observed, with β-turns and β-sheets becoming predominant, accounting for 39.02–40.53% and 24.22–26.45%, respectively, accompanied by a significant increase in β-sheet content, a marked decrease in β-turns, and slight reductions in both α-helices and random coils. Notably, peptides enriched in ordered secondary structures, such as α-helices, β-sheets, and β-turns, have been reported to exhibit stronger antioxidant activity than those dominated by disordered random coils (Zhang et al., 2021). Beyond the influence of amino acid composition and sequence on antioxidant behavior, the conformational architecture of peptides also plays a critical role in modulating radical-scavenging capacity (Xu et al., 2024). This may be due to the enhanced spatial orientation and accessibility of antioxidant functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl, amino) in ordered conformations, which can promote electron transfer or hydrogen donation. In addition, such conformations may stabilize the peptide framework and reduce steric hindrance, thereby enhancing intermolecular interactions and overall radical-scavenging efficiency. Overall, the enhanced antioxidant activity observed in MRPs in this study is likely attributable to both the formation of new covalent linkages and alterations in secondary structure induced by the MR.

Analysis of particle size, PDI and ζ-potential

Particle size and PDI are critical indicators of the dispersion state and aggregation behavior of molecules in aqueous systems, with particle size reflecting the extent of molecular aggregation or dissociation, and PDI assessing the uniformity of particle size distribution. A lower PDI indicates a more homogeneous system, whereas a higher value suggests poor dispersibility. Together, these parameters provide important insights into how glycation influences the conformation and solution behavior of the molecules in PSMH.

As shown in Table 2, native PSMH exhibited the smallest particle size (133.03 nm). After MR modification, all MRPs displayed increased particle sizes, with MRPs-ribose reaching the largest value (141.07 nm, a 6.0% increase). This enlargement is likely attributable to partial molecular unfolding and subsequent intermolecular aggregation during glycation. The PDI of PSMH was 0.318, indicating a relatively uniform size distribution. Following MR, only minor variations in PDI values were observed among samples, with a significant reduction found exclusively for MRPs-ribose (0.277), suggesting enhanced homogeneity relative to the other MRPs. In addition to particle size and distribution, ζ-potential provides insight into the electrostatic properties and dispersion stability of protein systems. A higher absolute ζ-potential implies stronger electrostatic repulsion between particles, which can inhibit aggregation and enhance dispersion stability (Zhao et al., 2024). Native PSMH had a ζ-potential of −16.637 mV, indicating modest surface charge. After glycation, all MRPs exhibited increased negative surface charges, with MRPs-ribose showing the most negative value (−34.147 mV) markedly lower than that of PSMH, indicating enhanced electrostatic repulsion and stability. The absolute ζ-potentials of other MRPs followed the order: MRPs-xylose (−23.013 mV) > MRPs-fructose (−20.597 mV) > MRPs-glucose (−19.480 mV). These changes align with the extent of MR and may result from the introduction of sugar-derived hydroxyl groups, which increase surface electronegativity. Similar trends have been reported in soy protein isolate–galacto-oligosaccharide systems, where glycation increased negative ζ-potential (Xie et al., 2022). However, despite higher electrostatic repulsion, MR-induced partial molecular unfolding and covalent crosslinking can promote aggregation, leading to larger hydrodynamic diameters even when the absolute ζ-potential increases (Yan et al., 2022). Thus, the final particle size of MRPs in this study reflects the combined effects of electrostatic repulsion, molecular unfolding, and covalent crosslinking.

In this study, the observed trends in particle size, PDI, and ζ-potential closely align with glycation-induced structural rearrangements revealed by FTIR spectroscopy and secondary structure analysis, promoting the formation of more ordered and compact molecular architectures. These conformational changes likely account for the controlled aggregation reflected in increased particle size, the improved dispersion uniformity indicated by lower PDI, and the enhanced dispersion stability associated with higher absolute ζ-potential, particularly in MRPs-ribose, thereby facilitating the formation of stable MRPs with potent antioxidant activity.

These compounds were considered to represent the major volatile components relevant to the formation and differentiation of aroma characteristics in PSMH and its glycated derivatives.

UpSet plot analysis (Fig. 4B) further illustrated the distribution of these 22 sample-derived volatiles across PSMH and the four MRPs. Only five volatiles were detected in native PSMH, with n-eicosane and tetramethylpyrazine being the only two compounds present in all samples before and after the MR, suggesting baseline volatiles that were little affected by glycation. In contrast, the remaining 17 volatiles occurred exclusively in the MRPs, mainly comprising Maillard-related compounds such as furfural, pyrazines, benzaldehydes and tetrahydrothiazole, together with several lipid-derived volatiles. This quantitative comparison highlights the sugar-dependent nature of volatile formation during glycation.

The Maillard-related group was mainly composed of tetramethylpyrazine, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, furfural, benzaldehyde and its dimethyl-substituted derivatives, and tetrahydrothiazole (Fig. 4C). Among these, dimethyl-substituted benzaldehydes emerged as key Maillard-related aldehydes, with 3,5-dimethylbenzaldehyde being particularly abundant in MRPs-ribose, MRPs-fructose and MRPs-glucose, and 3,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde predominating in MRPs-xylose, contributing benzaldehyde-like, cherry-type aroma notes (Chen et al., 2019). Furfural, a typical furan aldehyde produced via pentose degradation, was detected in MRPs-ribose and MRPs-xylose, imparting caramel- and baked-like notes and contributing to a more layered aroma profile (Shang et al., 2020). Tetrahydrothiazole was specifically found in MRPs-glucose. Tetramethylpyrazine was already present at a relatively high level in PSMH and further increased in MRPs-ribose, whereas 2,5-dimethylpyrazine was newly formed and detected only in MRPs-fructose. Together, these Maillard-related volatiles are generally associated with roasted, nutty, cocoa-like and caramel-like notes, and their marked enrichment and diversification in MRPs indicate that Maillard glycation plays a central role in building the characteristic aroma of glycated PSMH (Han et al., 2018).

Lipid-derived volatiles comprised long-chain alkanes (e.g., n-eicosane, n-octadecane, n-triacontane), fatty acid methyl esters (methyl palmitate and methyl 14-methylpentadecanoate), unsaturated ketones (isophorone and (E,E)-3,5-octadien-2-one) and 1-dodecanol (Fig. 4D). In PSMH, this group was dominated by n-triacontane, n-eicosane and n-octadecane, which together formed a substantial lipid-derived background. After the MR, the absolute concentrations of lipid-derived volatiles increased in MRPs-ribose, MRPs-xylose and MRPs-glucose (428.04, 324.56 and 230.17 μg/g, respectively), but decreased in MRPs-fructose (93.33 μg/g), while their relative proportions generally declined due to the strong growth of Maillard-related volatiles. Therefore, the contribution of lipid-derived volatiles became less predominant in glycated samples. Isophorone was the most abundant lipid-derived volatile in all MRPs, especially in MRPs-ribose and MRPs-xylose, while the other lipid-derived volatiles (fatty acid methyl esters, long-chain alkanes and 1-dodecanol) mainly contributed to the background matrix of the aroma profile. Although these compounds typically provide mild fruity, fatty or solvent-like notes and are less character-defining than Maillard-related volatiles, they contribute to the underlying body and complexity of the aroma profile.

Overall, the volatile composition and aroma profile of PSMH were significantly altered after MR, yielding distinct MRPs with sugar-dependent volatile profiles. Among the four MRPs investigated, MRPs-ribose exhibited the most pronounced transformation, with the highest overall volatile content and the highest abundance of Maillard-related volatiles. These results suggest that ribose may be particularly effective in promoting the formation of volatile compounds associated with desirable aroma characteristics during the MR. Therefore, MRPs-ribose may represent a promising candidate for aroma enhancement and potential functional food applications, although further sensory evaluation is needed to confirm its sensory impact.

Cellular antioxidant activity

In this study, MRPs-ribose demonstrated higher antioxidant activity in terms of both DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging capacities, and favorable structural characteristics and an improved aroma profile, suggesting promising application potential in the food and nutraceutical product industries. To further confirm their safety and efficacy, a cellular antioxidant evaluation was conducted using the HepG2 cell model.

HepG2 cells are commonly used to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of bioactive peptides, because the liver is a main target for antioxidant compounds absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract (Abdo et al., 2024). Importantly, no significant cytotoxicity was observed for either PSMH or MRPs-ribose, as cell viability remained above 95% relative to the untreated control in all treatment groups, as detected by MTT assay (Fig. 5A–B), indicating that heat-processed MRPs did not induce cellular toxicity at the tested concentrations. This highlights the potential safety of MRPs-ribose for applications in the food and nutraceutical industries, and helps alleviate concerns over adverse effects potentially associated with MR–induced structural modifications.Fig. 5Evaluation of cytotoxicity and cytoprotective effects of PSMH and MRPs-ribose on HepG2 cells: (A–B) Cell viability after treatment with PSMH (A) and MRPs-ribose (B) at various concentrations; and (C–D) Protective effects of PSMH (C) and MRPs-ribose (D) against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in HepG2 cells.Fig. 5

To establish an oxidative damage model, HepG2 cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of H2O2 (200–600 μmol/L), and 500 μmol/L was chosen as the optimal dose, as it induced an approximate cell viability to 52% (Supplementary Fig. 1). Both PSMH (200–400 μg/mL) and MRPs-ribose (50–150 μg/mL) significantly mitigated H2O2-induced cytotoxicity across the tested concentration ranges (Fig. 5C–D). Specifically, PSMH at 300 μg/mL increased HepG2 cell viability to 82.31%, whereas MRPs-ribose at only 100 μg/mL restored cell viability to 91.90%, as determined by the MTT assay. These results indicate that MRPs-ribose exhibit superior cytoprotective effects compared to unmodified PSMH, likely due to enhanced antioxidant activity and potentially improved cellular uptake conferred by MR-mediated glycosylation. Previous studies have shown that glycosylated sea cucumber peptides exert stronger protective effects against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in HepG2 cells than their unmodified counterparts (Abdo et al., 2024). Similarly, MRPs generated from scallop mantle hydrolysate and ribose completely eliminated H2O2-induced cytotoxicity at 100 μg/mL (Han et al., 2018), which is comparable to our findings. Overall, these observations suggest that MR-mediated glycosylation represents a promising strategy to improve the cytoprotective potential of protein hydrolysates under oxidative stress conditions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that Maillard glycation is a practical route to enhance both the functional properties and aroma profile of PSMH. Among the four reducing sugars evaluated, ribose consistently produced the most desirable outcomes, including the greatest accumulation of intermediate and advanced Maillard products, superior antioxidant performance in chemical assays, beneficial conformational remodeling aligned with stronger radical-scavenging potential, and enhanced dispersion behavior. Volatile profiling further showed selective enrichment of aldehydes and pyrazines, conferring a richer, more layered aroma. Importantly, cell studies confirmed no cytotoxicity within the tested ranges and robust cytoprotection against H2O2-induced damage, with MRPs-ribose achieving meaningful protection at 3-fold lower concentration than PSMH. Collectively, these results identify MRPs-ribose as the lead candidate and support ribose-mediated Maillard glycation as a targeted strategy to valorize PSMH for functional foods and nutraceuticals, for instance as a dry powder ingredient for beverage and bar formulations or as a concentrated preparation for capsules and sachets.
