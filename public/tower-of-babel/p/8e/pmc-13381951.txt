Mix proportion optimization of red mud manufactured sand foam concrete and its sulfate resistance
Authors: Junnan Wu, Chuandong Ren, Yingli Zhang, Fuchao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study optimized the mix proportion of red mud manufactured sand foam concrete (RMMSFC) using orthogonal tests and evaluated its sulfate resistance. Results showed that the sand-cement ratio most significantly influenced dry density and porosity, while foam content predominantly governed strength. Through range analysis, variance analysis, and comprehensive scoring, the optimal mix was determined as water-cement ratio 0.27, red mud content 30%, sand-cement ratio 0.9, and foam content 8%, achieving a 28-day compressive strength of 18.79 MPa and dry density of 1724.50 kg/m³. Microstructural analysis indicated that active components in red mud participated in secondary hydration, producing C-S-H gel and ettringite, thereby refining pore structure. Sulfate resistance tests revealed that specimens with ≤ 30% red mud retained good integrity after 50 wet-dry cycles. When the content of red mud is over 30%, the comprehensive performance decreases significantly due to the accelerated decomposition of hydration products and the expansion and accumulation of erosion products in the high alkali environment.

Introduction

Cement and concrete are the most basic and most used materials in the global construction industry. Their production scale ranks among the highest in the industrial field, but they are also accompanied by huge environmental costs. According to statistics, each ton of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) produces about 0.85 tons of CO2 and a large number of sulfur oxides and nitrogen oxides, which makes the cement industry one of the main sources of global carbon emissions1–3. In the context of addressing climate change and promoting the sustainable development of the construction industry, the development of green and low-carbon cementitious materials to replace traditional cement has become the focus of current research4–6. Among them, the use of industrial solid waste as an alternative or auxiliary cementitious material can not only reduce the consumption of natural resources, but also effectively consume bulk solid waste.

Among many industrial solid wastes, red mud is a kind of strong alkaline solid waste produced in the process of alumina smelting. For each ton of alumina produced, about 1.25 tons of red mud was produced. The cumulative stock of red mud in the world has exceeded 4 billion tons, China accounts for 28.2%, the inventory is about 790 million tons, and the comprehensive utilization rate is only about 4%7,8. A large amount of red mud not only encroaches on land resources, but also poses a serious threat to the surrounding soil, groundwater and ecosystem, and even endangers human health9–12. Therefore, it has become an urgent and significant issue to promote the large-scale, high value-added resource utilization and safe disposal of red mud. Nowadays, many scholars have tried to use red mud as an alternative or supplementary material for the development of different types of red mud-based building materials. Studies have shown that the incorporation of red mud has a significant effect on the mechanical properties and microstructure of cementitious materials13–16. Li et al17. used red mud and steel slag, coal gangue and other multi-source solid waste to prepare cement materials, and their compressive strength reached 8.8 MPa and 13.5 MPa under one-dimensional and three-dimensional conditions, respectively, reflecting the synergistic effect of red mud and other solid wastes. The research on the application of red mud in concrete also shows that it has certain feasibility. Bayat et al18. incorporated the original and heat-treated red mud into the alkali-activated slag system, and found that the compressive strength was the highest when the red mud content was 20%. Liu et al19. pointed out that replacing cement with 40% red mud will reduce the 28-day compressive strength of concrete by 20.9%, but basalt fiber can effectively improve its mechanical properties. Nikbin et al20. also found that the compressive strength, flexural strength and elastic modulus of red mud lightweight concrete decreased with the increase of red mud content. However, some studies21,22 have shown that the appropriate amount of red mud can have a positive impact on the performance of concrete, indicating that the rational use of red mud still has engineering value.

Foamed concrete is a kind of lightweight porous concrete. Its density is usually between 400 and 1850 kg/m3, and it is rich in a large number of randomly distributed pores23–25. Compared with ordinary concrete, foamed concrete has multiple advantages, including light weight, good sound insulation and thermal insulation performance, low elastic modulus and excellent seismic capacity, and has good durability, fire resistance, fluidity and construction plasticity. It is an environmentally friendly building material26–28. In addition, due to its simple production process and low cost, foamed concrete is widely used in engineering fields such as light building construction, road subbase filling and house partition wall29–32. According to whether the fine aggregate is mixed, foam concrete can be divided into two categories : sand-free foam concrete and sand-containing foam concrete. Sand-free foamed concrete is based on pure cement (or cementitious material) slurry without fine aggregate, and its density is usually low. It mainly uses a large number of closed pores introduced by foam to achieve lightweight and thermal insulation functions33. In recent years, the use of recycled solid waste for the preparation of sand-free foam concrete has become an important direction. Scholars have carried out many studies : Xiao et al34. used waste concrete recycled powder to prepare new foam concrete, and found that recycled powder can effectively reduce the thermal conductivity. Yao et al35. used recycled waste concrete powder as the main raw material to prepare foamed concrete, and found that its low cementitious activity mainly played a physical filling role; jiang et al36. used the Yellow River silt to prepare alkali-activated fly ash concrete, and pointed out that the addition of silt weakened the mechanical properties but still met the standard strength requirements. Sand-containing foam concrete introduces fine aggregate (such as natural sand, machine-made sand or solid waste fine aggregate) into the cementitious material slurry, and its density usually corresponds to the medium and high density grade (900 ~ 1200 kg/m3 and above). The skeleton filling effect of fine aggregate makes it have better mechanical properties and volume stability37. Driven by the concept of sustainable development, the use of solid waste materials to replace or partially replace sand is increasing : Gencel et al38. studied the effect of rice husk ash instead of cement and waste marble powder instead of sand on the performance of foam concrete; studies have shown that the use of quarry waste instead of manufactured sand to prepare foamed concrete can improve the compressive strength and reduce the thermal conductivity39; some studies have also confirmed that casting waste sand and treated plastic waste as alternative materials for sand can significantly improve the mechanical strength and durability of lightweight foam concrete40. At present, the research on sand-containing foam concrete mainly focuses on the substitution of solid waste fine aggregate, while the research on the application of machine-made sand, as the mainstream alternative material of natural sand, in foam concrete is still very limited. However, with the increasing depletion of natural river sand resources, manufactured sand has gradually become the main variety of construction sand with its advantages of sufficient raw materials and green environmental protection.

In addition, in the actual use environment, building materials often face the test of various complex climatic conditions and environmental factors. Dry-wet cycle is one of the common and significant environmental effects41. In the natural environment, when the foam concrete structure is in the area where the humidity changes greatly, such as near the water source, the area with high groundwater level or the area often subject to rainwater erosion, the dry-wet cycle phenomenon occurs frequently. The repeated absorption and evaporation of water will lead to complex physical and chemical changes inside the material, which will affect its performance. Therefore, it is very important to study the performance evolution of foamed concrete under dry-wet cycles for accurately evaluating its long-term durability and service life and ensuring the safety and stability of building structures.

Based on the above background, it is of great engineering value and environmental significance to develop red mud machine-made sand foam concrete (RMMSFC) by combining the demand for bulk consumption of red mud, the sustainable trend of replacing natural sand with machine-made sand and the lightweight and porous characteristics of foam concrete. This composite system can not only realize the synergistic resource utilization of industrial solid waste (red mud) and sustainable aggregate (manufactured sand), but also improve the mechanical properties and durability of foamed concrete through the skeleton effect of manufactured sand, and expand its application range in medium and high strength engineering scenarios. However, at present, the research on red mud-based foam concrete is mostly focused on the sand-free system. The research on the sand-containing system combining red mud, machine-made sand and foam concrete is still blank, and there is a lack of systematic optimization method of mix ratio and in-depth understanding of durability in sulfate attack environment.

This study was built upon previous single-factor tests investigating the performance of RMMSFC42. The orthogonal experimental design method was employed to select representative mix proportion combinations from the comprehensive experiments, thereby improving the experimental efficiency while systematically analyzing multi-factor interactions. Through the regulation of key parameters including water-binder ratio, red mud content, sand-binder ratio and foaming agent content, the influences of various factors on the physical and mechanical properties of RMMSFC were evaluated by means of intuitive analysis, range analysis and variance analysis. The mix proportion was subsequently optimized through a comprehensive scoring method. Subsequently, the sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄) solution was employed as the erosion medium to conduct sulfate dry-wet cycle tests under different red mud contents. The performance response and deterioration mechanisms of RMMSFCs with different red mud contents under various numbers of cycles were systematically investigated. The microstructural evolution was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

This study is guided by the bulk consumption of red mud industrial solid waste, using machine-made sand instead of natural river sand as fine aggregate to improve the performance of foam concrete, focusing on its application in industrial and civil building roofs, partition walls and retaining structures. The influence of various factors on the physical and mechanical properties of the material and the optimization of the mix ratio were determined by orthogonal test. At the same time, the durability of sulfate resistance in different service environments was investigated, and the mechanism of action was revealed by microscopic hydration mechanism analysis. The research results aim to improve the comprehensive performance of foam concrete, form a new type of solid waste-based wall material, and provide experimental reference and basis for the large-scale utilization of red mud.

It should be noted that this study focuses on the post-hardening properties (physical and mechanical properties and sulfate resistance durability) of RMMSFC, and the working performance of fresh slurry (such as fluidity, slump, time loss, foam stability, etc.) will be carried out separately as the focus of subsequent research due to the involvement of different test systems and evaluation indicators.

Raw materials and test methods

Raw materials

The materials in this study were from the same batch as those employed in the preliminary single-factor experiments. The red mud (RM) was sourced from Qingzhen City, Guiyang, Guizhou Province. The raw material was naturally air-dried, sieved through a 0.6 mm mesh, and subsequently subjected to low-temperature calcination at 105 °C for 4 h. Table 1 lists the main chemical composition of red mud and cement, and the data are from the previous single factor test42. The distribution of red mud particles was relatively uniform, with D90 of 63.325 μm, D50 of 5.657 μm and Dmax of 3.2417 μm. The main mineral composition of red mud was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), including calcite (CaCO3), boehmite (AlO(OH)), hematite (Fe2O3), quartz (SiO2), cancrinite ((Na, Ca)3−4[(Si, Al)6O12](SO4,CO3,Cl)·nH2O), Ca3Al2(SiO4)(OH)8), muscovite (KAl2(AlSi3O10)(OH)2)42. The cement used was Conch brand P·O 42.5 ordinary Portland cement, obtained from the Panjiang Cement Plant in Guizhou Province; its chemical composition is also provided in Table 1. The foaming agent was a liquid polymer composite cement foaming agent produced by Hefei Bailuo Energy Equipment Co., Ltd., with a solid content of 16.5% and a foaming ratio of 30. The aggregate was manufactured sand produced from limestone parent rock, having an apparent density of 2650 kg/m³ and a fineness modulus of 3.6. Furthermore, a poly­carboxylic acid high-performance water reducer (liquid) supplied by Shanxi Feike New Material Technology Co., Ltd., was incorporated; it had a solid content of 39% and a water reduction rate of 28%.

Specimen preparation

The preparation of the specimens started with weighing each dry component according to the mix proportion, while the water and water reducer were weighed based on the proportion of the total mass of the cementitious materials. The weighed red mud, cement, and manufactured sand were placed into a mixing barrel. A hand-held mixer was used to dry-mix the materials for 2 min until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. Meanwhile, the foaming agent and water were mixed at a ratio of 1:30, and foam was generated using an air-compression foaming machine. The required amount of foam was weighed and set aside. After dry mixing, the pre-mixed water and water reducer solution were added to the dry materials according to the specified water-cement ratio, and mixing continued for another 2 min to form uniform slurry. The prepared foam was then incorporated into the slurry and mixed for 1 min, finally yielding a foamed concrete mixture with good fluidity. The mixture was cast into 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm and 40 mm × 40 mm × 160 mm test molds in accordance with the requirements specified in the Chinese standards JG/T 266–2011 Foamed concrete and GB/T 17,671 − 2021 Test method of cement mortar strength (ISO Method), respectively. After curing for 24 h under natural conditions, the specimens were demolded and transferred to a standard curing chamber (temperature 20 ± 2 °C, relative humidity ≥ 95%) for continued curing until the designated testing ages, after which various performance tests were conducted.

Performance test methods

The performance indexes involved in the orthogonal test and sulfate resistance test in this study were obtained by independent test. Among them, the test methods of dry density, porosity, 28 d compressive strength and 28 d flexural strength are consistent with the previous single factor study42. In addition, two durability indexes of mass loss rate and strength loss rate were tested for sulfate resistance test. The specific test steps of each index are briefly described as follows.

Mass loss rate

According to the standard GB/T 11,969 − 2020 The methods of autoclaved aerated concrete, the dry-wet cycle test was carried out. The mass of three specimens in each group was weighed, and then the mass loss rate of each specimen was calculated. Sulfate dry-wet cycle system

According to GB/T 50,082 − 2024 Standard for Long-term Performance and Durability of Ordinary Concrete, the concentration of sodium sulfate solution used in the sulfate resistance test was 5% (mass fraction). The specimens were dried and weighed, and then put into an automatic dry-wet cycle machine. After soaking in sodium sulfate solution at 20℃ for 5 min and air-drying for 30 min, and drying for 7 h (60 ± 5℃), air-cooled for 10 min and cooled for 20 min (20℃) as a cycle, a total of 50 cycles. In the dry-wet cycle test, three specimens were observed, weighed and recorded every 10 cycles.

Orthogonal experimental results and discussion

Intuitive analysis

Table 5 lists the test results of dry density, porosity, 28 d compressive strength and flexural strength of RMMSFC under 16 different mix ratios of orthogonal test. Through the preliminary intuitive analysis of each evaluation index, it can be found that the combination of different factor levels has a significant impact on the performance of RMMSFC, and the data distribution range is wide, which provides a good data basis for the subsequent range and variance analysis.

From the perspective of physical properties, the highest and lowest dry density are group 16 (1873.83 kg/m3) and group 13 (1119.17 kg/m3), respectively. The maximum and minimum porosity are group 13 (9.19%) and group 16 (3.22%), respectively. There is a significant negative correlation between dry density and porosity. On the whole, in the combination of high red mud content or high sand-ash ratio, the dry density of the specimens is generally higher, while the porosity is relatively lower. During the test, it was also observed that when the content of machine-made sand in the ratio was high, the fresh slurry was relatively viscous, which may have a certain impact on the stability of the foam and the formation of the pore structure, which was initially related to its physical and mechanical properties. In terms of mechanical properties, the maximum values of 28 d compressive strength and flexural strength appear in group 11 (18.79 MPa) and group 6 (7.81 MPa), respectively, and the minimum values appear in group 7 (8.47 MPa) and group 13 (3.21 MPa), respectively. Preliminary observations show that groups with higher strength values usually correspond to lower foam content and higher sand-cement ratio, but this is only a surface phenomenon based on a single test point. Because the levels of each factor in the orthogonal test are interlaced, the optimal value of a single test group cannot be directly equal to the optimal level of the factor. Therefore, this section only gives a general overview of the test results. The following will focus on the physical and mechanical properties, respectively, using range analysis and variance analysis, in-depth exploration of water-cement ratio, red mud content, sand-cement ratio and foam content of these four factors on the RMMSFC indicators of the impact of the law and significance.

Physical properties

Range analysis

The range analysis results for the physical properties of RMMSFC are presented in Table 6. When dry density was used as the evaluation index, the order of factor influence based on the range values was: RC> RD> RB> RA. This indicates that the sand-cement ratio was the most significant factor affecting dry density, followed by the foam content, red mud content, and finally the water-cement ratio. Based on the level average values ki, the optimal mix proportion for maximizing dry density was determined to be A1B1C4D1. When porosity was used as the evaluation index, the order of factor influence was also: RC> RD> RB> RA. The sand-cement ratio remained the primary factor influencing porosity. The optimal mix proportion for minimizing porosity was identified as A4B4C1D3. As a high-density admixture, the incorporation of manufactured sand increased the solid content within the system. Concurrently, it disrupted the pore structure and reduced compactness, thereby exerting a significant influence on both the dry density and porosity.

To further elucidate the influence of different factors on the physical properties of RMMSFC, the mean effect plots for the four factors concerning dry density and porosity were constructed based on the range analysis results, as shown in Fig. 1. Thus, a higher sand-cement ratio led to lower porosity, while the increased solid content contributed to a higher dry density.

When the content is reduced to 4%, the porosity decreases to 5.58% and the dry density rises to 1614.79 kg/m³. Mix proportion optimization via comprehensive scoring method

In the mix proportion optimization of foam concrete, 28 d compressive strength is the primary mechanical index to measure the bearing capacity and structural safety of materials. Dry density is the core physical parameter that determines the degree of material lightweight, thermal conductivity and structural weight. Therefore, this study selects the above two indicators as the core variables of the comprehensive evaluation. For the two indexes of porosity and flexural strength, considering that there is a strong negative correlation between porosity and dry density (as shown in Sect. 3.2), one of them can effectively reflect the macroscopic physical state of the material; at the same time, although the flexural strength is an important mechanical parameter, its change trend in foam concrete is usually positively correlated with the compressive strength, and the attention to compressive strength is higher in general filling and retaining engineering. In order to simplify the scoring model and highlight the main contradiction, porosity and flexural strength were not included in the final comprehensive scoring calculation in this study, but they have been fully discussed as important reference indexes in the previous single factor and orthogonal test analysis. Membership degree and reverse membership degree indices are introduced to quantify the two evaluation indicators, enabling weighted scoring. Regarding the weight coefficient, this study gives the compressive strength weight a = 0.8 and the dry density weight b = 0.2, mainly based on the following considerations: (1) The goal of foam concrete mix optimization is to achieve a multi-objective balance of light weight and high strength45. The ideal foam concrete should have both higher strength and lower density. (2) There is an inherent positive correlation between dry density and compressive strength46. If the two are given equal weight, the scoring model will be excessively biased towards the direction of low density, resulting in too low strength of the optimization results and loss of engineering value. (3) Because the foam content is the most critical factor affecting the strength, it is necessary to assign a higher weight to the strength in the comprehensive score, so that the score results can sensitively reflect the performance difference caused by the change of foam content, so as to effectively screen out the optimal ratio. (4) The dry density of each ratio in this orthogonal test is in the range of medium and high density, and the optimization of compressive strength is more urgent. The weight distribution is designed to ensure that the material maximizes its mechanical bearing potential under the premise of meeting the lightweight requirements.

Analysis combining range, variance, and comprehensive scoring methods clearly revealed the primary and secondary order of influence of each factor. The final optimal mix proportion was determined as A₃B₃C₁D₂, corresponding to a water-cement ratio of 0.27, a red mud content of 30%, a sand-cement ratio of 0.9, and a foam content of 8%.

Pore distribution and microscopic mechanism analysis

pore size distribution

In order to explore the improvement mechanism of red mud on the pore structure and performance of foamed concrete, the first group with the lowest comprehensive score in the orthogonal test and the 11 th group with the optimal ratio (red mud content 30%) were selected. The high-definition cross-section of the specimen was taken by a digital camera, and then the image processing was performed using Imagepro plus software to obtain a binary image of the cross-section of the specimen, and the pore size and distribution were calculated, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4. The binary image of Fig. 3 shows that the pore distribution of the optimized ratio specimen (red mud 30%) is more uniform, and the proportion of large-sized pores is significantly reduced. The proportion of pore size distribution in Fig. 4 further confirms this trend statistically. Compared with the first group (red mud 10%), the proportion of small pores with a diameter of less than 200 μm in the optimized group increased from 43.6% to 59.6%, while the proportion of large pores with a diameter of more than 800 μm decreased from 2.3% to 0.7%. This shows that the increase of red mud content promotes the refinement and homogenization of pore structure. The reason is that the red mud particle size is small, which can be filled in the internal voids of the slurry, play a physical filling effect, and reduce the large pore formation space; secondly, the addition of appropriate amount of red mud adjusts the cohesion of the slurry, which is helpful for the uniform dispersion and stability of the foam and inhibits the merger and rupture of the bubble. It should be pointed out that the quantitative analysis of pore structure in this study is limited to the two-dimensional cross-sectional image method. For the accurate characterization of three-dimensional pore connectivity and nano-scale pores, it still needs to be further studied by mercury intrusion method (MIP) or X-ray CT technology.

Micro-mechanism analysis

The microstructure of the two groups of specimens was observed by SEM, and the hydration products and the mechanism of action were analyzed. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and (b), the first group exhibited poor mechanical properties, with a compressive strength of only 8.91 MPa. The microstructure appeared generally loose, with non-uniform pore size distribution, irregular pore morphology, evident pore connectivity, and microcracks, indicating poor structural integrity. Although some red mud particles and incompletely hydrated products were observed within the pores, their filling effect was limited, providing minimal improvement to the pore structure. Furthermore, voids were present in the pore walls due to crack propagation, further deteriorating pore quality. Such pore structure defects will have a significant adverse effect on the properties of RMMSFC materials characterized by porosity.

In contrast, the Group 11 specimens incorporating 30% red mud exhibited a superior microstructure, as shown in Figs. 5(c) and (d). The pore distribution was uniform throughout the matrix. Although interconnected pores occasionally appeared due to the inherent randomness of foam incorporation, the overall combination of large, medium, and small pores was well-balanced, resulting in high compactness. The high-magnification image reveals that the pores were filled with substantial hydration products, including ettringite (AFt), C-S-H gel, and Ca(OH)₂. With increased red mud content, the total number of pores in RMMSFC increased; however, both the average pore size and total pore area decreased, indicating effective optimization of the pore structure. The influence of red mud on the microstructure of foamed concrete has been confirmed in related studies. Some studies have found that47, red mud can significantly increase the yield stress and plastic viscosity of geopolymer foam concrete slurry, thereby enhancing foam stability and improving pore size distribution. When the red mud content is 30%, the pore size distribution reaches the best state. The study of Wang et al48. also showed that an appropriate amount of red mud (30%) could optimize the proportion of small and medium pore size (86.46%), but when the content exceeded 40%, the pore size distribution was obviously deteriorated and the proportion of large pores increased significantly. In addition, the previous single factor test42 has confirmed by image analysis that the pore size distribution of RMMSFC tends to be refined with the increase of red mud content. The uniform pore structure presented by the optimized ratio (red mud 30%) in this study is consistent with the laws revealed in the above literature.

Red mud particles work synergistically in foamed concrete through both physical filling effects and chemical reactions to enhance material performance. Physically, the particles function as micro-aggregates that directly fill pores, thereby increasing compactness. Chemically, the porous structure of red mud absorbs free water, while its main reactive components (SiO₂, Al₂O₃, and Fe₂O₃) participate in pozzolanic reactions and contribute to secondary hydration. The secondary hydration process involves four principal reactions: First, SiO₂ dissolves in the alkaline environment to form silicate ions, which combine with Ca²⁺ to produce amorphous C-S-H gel. As the primary strength-giving phase in cementitious systems, C-S-H gel fills hydration pores and enhances density. Second, Al₂O₃ dissolves under alkaline conditions and reacts with Ca²⁺ and sulfate ions to form needle-like AFt. The expansion associated with AFt formation compensates for shrinkage, rapidly develops a skeletal framework, and improves compressive strength49. Third, Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃ collectively contribute to the formation of calcium aluminoferrite hydrate (C-A-F-H). Although its binding capability is inferior to that of C-S-H, C-A-F-H helps reduce the proportion of large pores and refines the pore structure. Finally, SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ can react together to form hydrogennet (C₅SAH₉), a phase that combines the advantages of both C-S-H and AFt, significantly enhancing long-term strength50. Through the aforementioned reactions, red mud effectively transitions from an industrial solid waste to a functional cementitious material. To balance its reactivity with the potential risks of alkali-silica reaction, it is recommended to maintain the red mud content within the range of 20–30% in practical applications.

In addition to the physical filling effect and pozzolanic reaction, the porous characteristics of red mud particles may further optimize the pore structure through water regulation mechanism. Studies have shown that porous aggregate can be used as an internal curing medium in cement-based materials : Recycled ceramic aggregate (RCA) can adsorb water in the mixing stage through its rich pore structure, and gradually release the stored water when the internal relative humidity decreases due to cement hydration, thereby reducing the peak temperature of hydration heat, prolonging the hydration process, and effectively reducing autogenous shrinkage51,52. Similarly, in the 3D printed ECC system, the internal curing effect of recycled ceramic tile fine aggregate (RTFA) has been proved to significantly improve the degree of hydration, improve the interlayer bonding performance and reduce shrinkage cracking53. For the red mud machine-made sand foam concrete in this study, the high water absorption and porous structure of red mud particles make it have similar internal curing potential : during the curing process of foam concrete, the water adsorbed by red mud can be gradually released in the later stage, which promotes the continuous hydration of unhydrated cement particles and active components in red mud, increases the production of C-S-H gel and AFt, and then refines the pore structure and improves the compactness of the matrix. However, when the content of red mud is too high (> 30%), excessive water adsorption may lead to excessive viscosity of the slurry, decrease of foam stability, and deterioration of pore structure. Therefore, there is a balance range between the internal curing effect of red mud and the pozzolanic activity, which is consistent with the result of 30% red mud content in the above optimized ratio.

Sulfate resistance of RMMSFC

Apparent morphology change

Figures 7 and 8 illustrate the apparent morphological changes of RMMSFC specimens with different red mud contents after being subjected to 20 and 50 dry-wet cycles in Na₂SO₄ solution, respectively. As shown in Fig. 7, the degree of deterioration of the specimens gradually intensified with increasing red mud content. At red mud contents of 0%, 10%, and 20%, the specimens remained largely intact after 20 cycles, with no obvious damage observed. When the red mud content reached 30%, the surface of RMMSFC showed signs of erosion by the Na₂SO₄ solution, accompanied by the appearance of a small number of micro-cracks and slight spalling. As the content increased to 40%, spalling occurred at the corners of the specimen, and multiple interconnected micro-cracks developed in the central region. Surface roughness increased, local pores became exposed, and minor material loss was observed. At 50% content, damage was further exacerbated, manifesting as more pronounced material loss, extensive pore exposure, and aggregate revelation, but the overall structural integrity of the specimen was maintained.

After 50 dry-wet cycles, the extent of damage to RMMSFC was significantly greater than that after 20 cycles. Specimens without red mud retained a smooth and intact surface, whereas increasing the red mud content led to progressively more severe surface damage. Higher red mud content resulted in a looser structure and more pronounced penetration of the sodium sulfate solution. At red mud contents of 0%, 10%, and 20%, the specimen surfaces remained smooth, demonstrating good resistance to dry-wet cycling. When the content reached 30%, numerous micro-cracks were observed along the edges and in the middle of the specimens, though the overall structure remained intact. At 40% content, noticeable spalling occurred at the edges and corners, micro-cracks increased in number, and pores became exposed. At 50% content, the specimen surfaces appeared rough, with severe local damage and considerable shedding of fragments and sand particles. These results indicate that RMMSFC exhibits strong resistance to sulfate attack under dry-wet cycling when the red mud content does not exceed 30%. Beyond this threshold, corrosion resistance decreases markedly, and specimen damage increases significantly.

Mass loss rate

Figures 9(a) and (b) show the mass change and mass loss rate of RMMSFC under different red mud content in 10–50 dry-wet cycles. With the increase of the number of cycles, the mass of all groups increased first and then decreased, and the mass loss rate decreased first and then increased. At 30 dry-wet cycles, the mass of each group reached the peak. Corresponding to 1719.48 g, 1793.69 g, 1851.95 g, 1900.94 g, 1973 g and 2003.67 g, the mass loss rates were − 0.18%, −0.22%, −0.29%, −0.35%, −0.40% and − 0.52%, respectively. It shows that within 30 cycles, the higher the red mud content, the more significant the mass increase, and the lower the mass loss rate. This early mass increase phenomenon can be explained from two aspects : the high water absorption characteristics of red mud and the continuous filling effect of incomplete hydration products. Firstly, red mud particles have high specific surface area and abundant micropore structure, which endow them with significant water absorption capacity47. Secondly, the Na2SO4 solution in the inhaled pores reacts with the unreacted cementitious material components in the matrix by sulfate attack. According to the classical theory of sulfate attack of cement-based materials, this process usually produces expansive products ettringite (AFt) and gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O)54, and AFt can effectively fill the internal pore structure of the matrix and refine the pore size, thereby improving the mechanical properties and compactness55. Therefore, in the range of 10 to 30 dry-wet cycles, although the surface of the specimen has slight erosion marks, the continuous water absorption of red mud and the pore filling effect of expansion products such as internal AFt contribute to the net increase of mass, and the formation effect of internal hydration products temporarily offsets the mass loss caused by surface erosion.

After more than 30 cycles, the mass began to decline continuously, and was lower than the initial value after 50 cycles. The loss rates were 0.33%, 0.38%, 0.46%, 0.50%, 0.73% and 0.87%, respectively. The mass loss of high content red mud specimens is more significant. As the cycle progresses, Na2SO4 crystallizes in the form of mirabilite (Na2SO4·10H2O), and the volume expansion is about 4 times, resulting in huge crystallization pressure and microcrack propagation56. The continuous chemical formation and physical crystallization process in the dry-wet cycle continuously destroy the pore structure, which promotes the development of microcracks into macroscopic damage and spalling. Therefore, the mass change is controlled by the water absorption characteristics and chemical expansion effect of red mud.

Strength loss rate

Figures 10(a) and (b) show the compressive strength change and strength loss rate of RMMSFC with different red mud contents after being subjected to 10–50 sulfate dry-wet cycles. From Fig. 10(a), it can be seen that when the red mud content is less than 40%, the compressive strength of the specimen in 50 dry-wet cycles increases first and then decreases, reaching a peak at 10 cycles. When the red mud content is ≥ 40%, the compressive strength decreases continuously with the increase of the number of cycles, and there is no early strength growth. The peak strength of the six groups of specimens (red mud content of 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%) was 19.41 MPa, 19.44 MPa, 19.95 MPa, 20.27 MPa, 17.84 MPa and 16.75 MPa, respectively. From the strength loss rate diagram 10(b), the strength loss rate of the specimens with red mud content less than 40% decreased first and then increased, and the strength loss rate was the lowest at 10 cycles, while the strength loss rate always increased when the red mud content was more than 40%. After 50 cycles, the strength loss rates of each dosage group (from low to high) were 19.26%, 21.98%, 25.42%, 28.18%, 30.41% and 37.29%, respectively.

This phenomenon is closely related to the long-term hydration characteristics of foamed concrete and the role of red mud. The early hydration products, such as C-S-H and AFt, contributed to strength development. However, water evaporation at later stages may have led to insufficient hydration. During the sodium sulfate dry-wet cycles, water and ions from the Na₂SO₄ solution promoted continued reaction of incompletely hydrated particles, including active components in red mud. At low dosage levels, the high water absorption capacity of red mud provided additional moisture to the system, supporting further hydration and thereby enhancing strength. However, when the red mud content was too high, although the pozzolanic reaction was more substantial, it could not compensate for the reduction in hydration products due to decreased cement content. Consequently, RMMSFC exhibited a higher strength loss rate under these conditions57.

Micro-mechanism analysis

Under sulfate dry-wet cycling, damage in RMMSFC progressively accumulated with increasing cycle numbers. To investigate the micro-damage mechanisms, this study employed SEM to examine the micro-morphology of specimens subjected to 20 and 50 cycles, with particular focus on comparing specimens with 20% and 50% red mud content. The damage mechanisms were analyzed in conjunction with physicochemical reaction processes. Figures 11(a) and (b) show the microstructure of specimens with 20% red mud content after 50 cycles. Multiple cracks of varying widths are visible on the surface, with some cracks penetrating through pores, leading to severe collapse of the pore structure. In addition, the high alkali environment also aggravates the repeated dissolution-recrystallization process of sulfate erosion products (AFt, gypsum), which accelerates the destruction of pore structure64.

In the sulfate dry-wet cycle testing, the deterioration mechanism primarily evolves through three distinct stages. The first stage is the wetting stage: Na₂SO₄ solution penetrates into the pores and reacts with cement hydration products including Ca(OH)₂ and calcium aluminate hydrate to form gypsum and AFt. Concurrently, sulfate ions accumulate within the pores of the foamed concrete, providing reactants for subsequent reactions. The second stage is the drying stage: Water evaporation induces supersaturation of Na₂SO₄ in the pores, leading to crystallization as mirabilite (Na₂SO₄·10 H₂O). This crystallization generates substantial pressure that initiates and propagates microcracks56. Simultaneously, gypsum and AFt undergo dehydration and shrinkage, which subsequently reabsorb water and expand during rewetting, thereby intensifying internal stresses. The third stage is the cyclic accumulation stage: Chemical expansion products (AFt) and physical crystallization products (mirabilite) are repeatedly generated and accumulate within the pores. The continuous propagation and interconnection of microcracks form macroscopic cracks, which accelerate ionic penetration and establish a positive feedback loop of damage progression. In summary, the deterioration of RMMSFC exposed to Na₂SO₄ solution under dry-wet cycling results from the combined effects of physical crystallization pressure and chemical expansion. Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the mix proportion optimization and sulfate resistance of red mud manufactured sand foam concrete through orthogonal experiments and performance testing. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Range and variance analyses revealed that the sand-cement ratio is the most significant factor influencing dry density and porosity, while foam content plays the dominant role in governing compressive and flexural strength. The water-cement ratio and red mud content exhibit secondary but non-negligible effects. This hierarchy provides clear guidance for RMMSFC mix design prioritization.

(2) Through a comprehensive scoring method integrating compressive strength (weight 0.8) and dry density (weight 0.2), the optimal mix proportion was determined as water-cement ratio 0.27, red mud content 30%, sand-cement ratio 0.9, and foam content 8%. This formulation achieved a 28-day compressive strength of 18.79 MPa with a dry density of 1724.50 kg/m3.

(3) The sulfate resistance is significantly affected by the content of red mud. When the content of red mud is less than 30%, the ettringite and gypsum formed by sulfate reaction fill the pores, and the structure of the specimen remains intact after 50 dry-wet cycles. When the content of red mud is more than 30%, the high alkalinity introduced by excessive Na2O accelerates the decalcification of C-S-H, aggravates the expansion of cumulative erosion products, leads to serious cracking, and the strength loss exceeds 30%.

(4) The optimized RMMSFC is suitable for the application of medium and high density foam concrete, including road subbase filling, mine backfilling, load-bearing lightweight partition wall, etc. Its compressive strength far exceeds the standard requirements of these scenarios. In practical application, it is recommended to adopt the optimal mix ratio, and the red mud content should be kept at ≤ 30% to reduce the risk of durability.

This study has two main limitations. First, fresh slurry workability—including flowability, slump, slump retention, and foam stability—was not systematically evaluated. Such properties are critical for assessing pumpability and self-compacting performance in field applications like road subbase filling and mine backfilling. Second, sulfate attack products were identified primarily through SEM morphology and supporting literature, without direct phase characterization by XRD or TG-DSC. Consequently, the specific phase composition and quantitative evolution of these products during wet-dry cycling remain to be confirmed. Future investigations should systematically address both fresh-state workability and direct phase identification to fully establish the engineering applicability and durability mechanisms of RMMSFC.
