Perspectives on harm reduction kit implementation in heterogeneous outpatient clinics
Authors: Raagini Jawa, Margaret Shang, Austen Markus, Megan Hamm, José Luiggi-Hernández, Flor de Abril Cameron, Gary McMurtrie, Olivia Studnicki, Mary Hawk, Devon Check, Jessica Merlin, Jane Liebschutz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe evolving U.S. drug market has fueled a public health crisis with rising drug use-associated morbidity and mortality, revealing a mismatch between current abstinence-based addiction care and the needs of people who use drugs (PWUD) to access evidence-based harm reduction services (HRS). Co-locating HRS into outpatient clinics could reduce mortality and improve clinical outcomes. We investigated barriers and facilitators of HRS implementation through kit distribution at three heterogenous outpatient addiction clinics using pre- and post-implementation focus groups.MethodsUsing a qualitative description approach, we conducted 1-hour virtual focus groups and individual interviews with clinic staff and providers both pre- and post-implementation of kit distribution. Interview guides were based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research to assess anticipated and actual implementation barriers and facilitators. Interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsFive providers and six staff participated in pre-implementation data collection. Dominant themes pre-implementation included participant enthusiasm for HRS integration and anticipated barriers of personal knowledge and external stigma against PWUD. Six providers and five staff participated post-implementation. Participants reported successful kit implementation with few actual barriers, though external stigma at the individual, health system, and community levels, along with issues related to patient use and interactions with child protective services, law enforcement, and pharmacies, occasionally disrupted the process. The lack of sustainable funding for supplies and labor emerged as a primary concern for long-term sustainability, with clinics actively seeking alternative funding sources.ConclusionsImplementation of HRS in outpatient addiction clinics was well-received by providers and staff and supported by pre-implementation trainings, site champions, and favorable implementation environments. Further efforts are needed to reduce stigma in the greater community and achieve sustainable funding for HRS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13011-026-00746-1.Keywords: Harm reduction, Outpatient clinics, Implementation science

Introduction

Rapid and dynamic changes within the U.S. drug market have taken place in the past several years. Potent adulterants with sedating properties like fentanyl, xylazine, and medetomidine dominate the unregulated opioid supply, which has contributed to an ever evolving overdose crisis [1]. In parallel, rates of infectious complications like hepatitis C, human immunodeficiency virus, and serious bacterial infections have risen at an alarming pace [2]. This has resulted in increased hospitalizations and higher mortality rates among people who use drugs (PWUD), placing significant financial strain on healthcare systems [3, 4].

Harm reduction is a set of strategies and principles aimed at minimizing health risks associated with drug use without necessitating cessation [5]. The most visible harm reduction services (HRS) include syringe service programs (SSPs), naloxone distribution, and overdose prevention hotlines like SafeSpot [6]. The community organizations and mutual aid groups that deliver these services are often siloed from healthcare settings and limited by staff and funding to provide necessary coverage to an exponentially growing population in need [7].

Outpatient clinics including primary care and Federally Qualified Health Centers are ideal for integrating HRS. While historically seen as conflicting with abstinence-based treatment goals, HRS integration supports a patient-centered approach for PWUD who wish to use substances more safely or are not ready to stop [8]. Unlike SSPs, which remain illegal in some states, outpatient clinics offer broader geographic coverage, extended hours, and access to medical services like addiction treatment, vaccinations, and viral infection screenings [9, 10]. Modeling studies show that integrating HRS in outpatient clinics is cost-effective, reducing drug-related mortality by 30%, strengthening provider-patient relationships, encouraging treatment initiation, and decreasing acute healthcare use [9, 11, 12]. This aligns with national efforts including the 2023 Health Resources and Services Administration guidance to expand HRS access in outpatient care [13].

Despite growing support for incorporating HRS into outpatient clinics, practical implementation outside hospitals remains limited [9, 14–16]. Research from isolated clinics shows integration is challenging due to stigma towards PWUD, legal and political opposition, and prioritization of abstinence-only treatment modalities over other evidence-based treatment strategies [17–19]. As comprehensive addiction treatment and harm reduction can better address evolving drug market needs, it is critical to examine effective implementation. To address this gap, we explored barriers and facilitators to implementing HRS at three outpatient clinics that provide addiction treatment.

Methods

This qualitative study evaluated an initiative to implement harm reduction kits within outpatient clinics that offer addiction treatment. Using a qualitative description approach guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research 2.0 (CFIR), we aimed to accurately describe participants’ thoughts and experiences [20–22]. Pre- and post-implementation data collection allowed us to assess both anticipated and actual barriers and facilitators, providing insights to guide future implementation efforts.

Settings and participants

Since 2016, the Pennsylvania Department of Human Services has certified over 200 behavioral and physical health entities as Centers of Excellence for Opioid Use Disorder (COEs), outpatient clinics that include primary care practices and office-based addiction treatment programs aimed at providing evidence-based treatment for PWUD. Core components include providing comprehensive case management services and Certified Recovery Specialist support to address social determinants of health and increasing access to medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) to increase patient retention [23]. This outpatient structure positions the COE network as an ideal venue for integrating and scaling evidence-based interventions such as HRS while supporting patient-centered care. Our study occurs at three heterogenous urban COEs in Western Pennsylvania: a dual-diagnosis clinic, a pregnancy and women’s recovery clinic, and a family medicine primary care clinic. All sites expressed interest in implementing HRS and identified faculty leads and staff champions (site champions hereafter) for their respective COE [24].

In August 2023, site champions to helped to recruit for pre-implementation focus groups via clinic listservs and regularly scheduled clinic meetings. Using purposive sampling to ensure adequate representation from each COE, we targeted recruiting at least two staff members and at least two providers (e.g., MDs, Advanced Practice Practitioners, RNs) from each COE to participate in separate staff and provider focus groups. Four months after our initiative began in February 2024, we recruited participants for our post-implementation focus groups using the same strategies; participants were not required to be the same individuals as those who participated in the pre-implementation groups.

Implementation process

We used standard implementation strategies with all sites including trainings on HRS evidence and practices, designated on-site champions, and COE-specific workflows [24]. All clinics adopted a model in which pre-packaged harm reduction kits were universally offered or provided to patients via menus listing available kits and staff or providers distributing the selected items to patients. Kits included safer injection, safer smoking, safer snorting, safer boofing (per-rectal administration of drugs), fentanyl and xylazine test strips, and wound care kits (Supplement, Table 1). Staff-led education about kit use occurred during distribution so patients understood safe practices. Personnel considerations were made to ensure that certain staff such as Certified Recovery Specialists were not placed in positions in the workflow that could compromise or jeopardize their wellbeing. The study team covered the cost of the kits during the implementation period.

Data collection

The pre- and post-implementation focus group interview guide (see Appendix) was based on CFIR 2.0, which is a widely used determinants framework to explore barriers and facilitators in implementation of interventions. It includes five domains—intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, individual characteristics, and process—with 26 constructs in total [25]. Questions included current experiences with HRS, anticipated (pre-implementation) and actual (post-implementation) barriers and facilitators to implementing kits within each clinic, and anticipated workflow for integration.

In September 2023, an experienced qualitative methodologist (MH) conducted two pre-implementation hour-long focus groups via Zoom. Given challenges associated with recruiting busy front-line providers, our approach was flexible and allowed for the option of one-on-one interviews and focus groups with fewer than 5 participants, which are regarded as group interviews. Participants provided verbal consent prior to focus groups/group interviews. In May-June 2024, the same methodologist (MH) conducted two post-implementation focus groups via Zoom. Due to a scheduling conflict, two providers were interviewed together. Participants were compensated $25 for the pre-implementation and $30 for the post-implementation focus groups.

Data analysis

All focus groups and interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified. A codebook was developed deductively based on the CFIR 2.0 framework, and inductively, based on recurring topics identified during the initial review of the transcripts. The codebook was then applied to all transcripts by two experienced coders (MH, FC), and the primary coder (MH) compared and adjudicated any discrepancies to ensure consistency across all transcripts. Following coding, the primary coder first conducted a conventional content analysis and secondarily a thematic analysis with the study team to further refine themes [26–28]. This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh institutional review board.

Pre-implementation

Participants expressed enthusiasm for providing HRS and viewed it as aligned with clinic values and therapeutic goals.

Anticipated support and positive prior experiences

Participants were receptive to providing HRS and anticipated enthusiasm from staff, providers, and patients. Some clinics were already distributing naloxone and/or drug checking supplies and reported success with these efforts:

I think it really helps to create therapeutic relationships when patients know you’re honoring their safety first and foremost. I feel like the care we’re giving is a lot less judgmental. And through conversations about harm reduction, I find that patients are able to open up more.

Another provider noted feeling “more comfortable sending our patients back out into the community after the visits, knowing that they may be a little safer if they do end up using again.” Overall, HRS provision aligned with clinic and federal priorities, and participants anticipated a positive response, with many describing themselves as “excited.”

Perceived benefits for patients and communities

Harm reduction was viewed as a valuable service for patients and their communities. One participant described:

I think the more harm reduction we can offer our community, not just our patients, the better we all are as a community. I like to make the parallel with education: the more educated a population is, the better everybody is going to do regardless of how educated or not educated the specific person is. So, the more harm reduction we can put into our communities, the better every person in our community will do, whether they want it, need it, seek it out, or they don’t.

Participants also emphasized that HRS aligned with a broader shift toward patient-centered, non-punitive care for PWUD, and several described HRS as an extension of existing clinical conversations on safety and overdose prevention.

Anticipated logistical considerations

While participants anticipated needing to work out the logistics of kit distribution (i.e., distribution workflows, kit storage, staffing, inventory management, etc.), those issues were not viewed as insurmountable:

I don’t see it being much of an increase or a burden. I feel like we’re already educating on many of these things, we just don’t have the physical supplies. It’s gonna be such a great tool for us to actually have them there, so we can use them while we’re educating. I don’t think it’s going to take much more time or anything like that.

Need for training and comfort with harm reduction practices

When describing barriers, participants noted a need for competency training on safer use strategies and kit contents, particularly for less-familiar drug use methods like boofing (per rectal administration) and snorting. Many providers explained that while they supported the harm reduction philosophy, some lacked technical knowledge or language to confidently discuss safer use practices with patients.

External stigma and structural barriers

Providers at some COEs also anticipated external, most notably public and healthcare-related stigma and a broader focus on abstinence-based care in health system(s):

I think stigma is a huge [barrier]… especially when you’re talking about women who are pregnant or potentially becoming pregnant, I think this idea is not well-received by the community at large. That it’s still seen, very unfortunately, as sort of enabling patients to have ongoing use.

Other providers described institutional and legal barriers, including pharmacy leadership preventing naloxone distribution at an Overdose Awareness event, the discontinuation of sterile syringe distribution following leadership changes at one site, and concerns about negative interactions with law enforcement if patients were found in possession of drug use equipment:

I know at the needle exchange, people are given cards that say, ‘I’m a member of [Name of SSP],’ and they’re told that if they have a negative interaction with law enforcement, they can show the card to indicate where you obtained this equipment. I don’t know how common it is, but even though it’s legal for them to be receiving these supplies, it can still cause a problem for them. Speaking of harm reduction, I would certainly hate to be part of something that actually caused harm or caused problems for people.

Participants also noted uncertainty around local and institutional policies surrounding HRS. Potential legal risks were perceived as particularly significant for patients with past incarceration, living in mandated abstinence-based recovery housing, and/or with Child Protective Services (CPS, also referred to as CYF) involvement. Even when legal concerns were not present, providers emphasized the importance of discussing safe storage of kits with patients to reduce potential conflicts or stigmatizing interactions with family, partners, and/or roommates.

Implementation was feasible with minimal workflow disruption

Participants primarily viewed kit distribution as successful, encountering few actual barriers, which many attributed to the implementation strategies that facilitated integration into routine practice. One clinic modified its workflow for kit storage to maintain routine access for all staff and providers. COEs embedded within larger practices serving other patient populations faced additional workflow challenges, such as omitting kit-specific messaging in shared waiting rooms (i.e.; with pediatric patients) and relying on staff or providers to provide menus to the appropriate patients in lieu of universal distribution of menus upon check-in.

Harm reduction kits were a means for patient engagement and continuity of care

Participants reported that patients viewed kits as a sign of the clinic’s commitment for their overall health and continued care regardless of substance use, and providers and staff noted strong patient interest and engagement in discussing and receiving the kits. Providers emphasized that HRS helped engage patients ambivalent about abstinence, supporting safety while reinforcing that they remain welcome in care. Several participants noted that this approach helped preserve trust and encouraged patients to continue attending clinic visits:

I think the other piece that’s been really great with harm reduction is just for patients that are inconsistent with their buprenorphine use, or have returned to use, instead of discharging them from the clinic, or saying, “let’s take a break and have the social worker reach out to you in a month,” we’re able to just still see them and offer harm reduction instead. And that’s been pivotal at keeping people engaged.

Participants repeatedly framed this as a key strength, highlighting that HRS allowed clinics to offer meaningful services to patients irrespective of their treatment and substance use status.

Structural stigma and external challenges

Challenges with kit distribution largely reflected stigma at various levels: individual (internalized stigma and fear of disclosing active use), health system, and community (CYF, law enforcement, and pharmacies). While rare, some cases posed the potential for serious negative impacts. For example, one staff member recalled writing a letter to address a CYF caseworker’s concern about a patient’s kit:

[We had one] patient who was searched and had testing strips and was being pushed by CYF about paraphernalia. That was the only bump that I’ve really had with it. I told the CYF worker, ‘it’s all labeled’. And the CYF worker was like, ‘Anyone can print out stickers. There’s no doctor’s name signed to that.’ So that’s why the onus of needing to have a letter [had] to happen. CYF will always have a reaction. that was certainly a bump, and it only happened one time.

Another clinic described a community pharmacy incident that highlighted the need for partnerships with local pharmacies and patient education on risks of negative interactions for informed decision-making:

Our provider had written a prescription for syringes to a rural pharmacy where the staff was very judgmental. They told the patient that if they picked them up, they would notify the police that ‘you’re getting syringes and you don’t have a medical disorder like diabetes.’ So, the patient left at that point. It helped us be able to say we’ll fill at our pharmacy here, and you can take them with you. That type of situation, trying to mitigate those risks for the patient, and being upfront and honest with them about it was a key component making sure that they were understanding of the risks.

These experiences underscore that the main challenges in HRS implementation were structural stigma such as unsupportive policies, not internal clinic resistance.

Financial and logistical sustainability concerns

Participants raised concerns about sustaining funding for supplies and labor to assemble and distribute the kits after the study period, particularly since HRS are not billable services. One provider noted:

At our clinic, we would need some time [to figure out sustainability], because we’ve benefited from the study [team] taking care of that [kit packing]. The study has taken care of the ordering, kit packing, and the delivery to our locations. So, we would need to figure out logistically some of those things, especially concerning who’s going to pack the kits and how are we going to compensate them?

Some clinics planned to use internal funds post-implementation, while others sought external grants. Participants emphasized that operational barriers were manageable, but the lack of reimbursement mechanisms for HRS posed the greatest threat to long-term sustainability. Despite this uncertainty, all participants wanted to continue and expand kit distribution within their health systems, noting strong ideological alignment with clinic culture and goals:

For us, I have been speaking with our medical director who would like to expand this to the other clinics. This feels like a bigger swing, and I think there’s a lot of support for it. Unfortunately, it just kind of goes back down to funding of where is it going to come from. But overall, I think we’ve received a lot of support. It’s more just how do we make it work.

Overall, participants indicated that institutional, provider, and staff support were strong, and financial mechanisms, rather than acceptability or feasibility, remained the primary barrier for expansion.

Discussion

We found that implementation of office-based HRS in three heterogenous COEs was accepted among staff and providers and aligned well with their respective clinic priorities. The integration of HRS was perceived as a valuable addition to the treatment model, supporting a patient-centered approach. Several anticipated barriers to kit distribution were addressed effectively through targeted implementation strategies, such as having on-site champions and developing tailored, site-specific workflows, both of which facilitated smoother integration of the kits into routine clinical practice. Remaining challenges, such as substance use stigma from government agencies and community pharmacies at the outer setting level, underscore the difficulty of HRS implementation amid societal attitudes that prioritize abstinence and criminalization over treatment. Consistent with prior literature, pervasive societal and healthcare stigma against PWUD embedded in federal and state laws and institutional policies serves as a compelling implementation barrier [29]. Despite this, advocacy at the individual and inner setting clinic level helped mitigate some obstacles: site champions acted as change agents within the broader ecosystem, educating colleagues and streamlining kit distribution, while providers and staff intervened to support patients facing stigmatizing interactions [30, 31]. Future efforts should proactively include community partners throughout implementation and contingency plans for legal challenges to protect patients, providers, and staff [32].

While COEs are well-positioned to adopt HRS as a part of the addiction care continuum, low self-efficacy in harm reduction practices posed a barrier, which we addressed through pre-implementation trainings and site champions [33]. This reflects a broader curricular gap in health sciences education, which often emphasizes abstinence-based measures and medications over harm reduction [34–37]. Although isolated trainings on naloxone and safer use can increase provider and staff comfort and adoption, future efforts should integrate harm reduction education early in medical education, including within mandated Drug Enforcement Administration prescriber courses, asynchronous trainings, local technical assistance programs, learning collaboratives, and partnerships with harm reduction organizations and individuals with lived or living experience [38–40].

Financial sustainability of implementing office-based HRS was a concern both pre- and post-implementation, reflecting the long-standing funding challenges faced by community-based harm reduction organizations [7]. Clinics worried about bearing ongoing costs, despite evidence that HRS are cost-effective for health systems by promoting patient engagement and reducing substance use-associated acute visits and mortality [9]. Sustainable funding is critical for scaling HRS into outpatient clinics, yet proportional governmental investment in harm reduction programming remains limited [7]. Strategies to cover HRS under Medicare and Medicaid, similar to expansions for addiction treatment, patient navigation services, and contingency management, should be explored [41]. In the interim, given the affordability of kits, health systems could partner with mutual aid groups to procure kits and use existing staff for kit assembly and inventory management through task shifting as a low-cost solution [24, 42–44].

This study was limited by a small sample of three outpatient sites in Pennsylvania and thus, our results may not be transferable to other settings. Our study also did not include patient perspectives, which would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of HRS acceptability and impact. HRS implementation initially occurred at no cost to the clinics, and while post-study self-funding was found to be achievable and comparable to other clinic-based medical supplies, these findings may not generalize to all self-funded programs or to clinics with different resource constraints [42].

Despite these limitations, the implementation of office-based HRS in COEs was generally well-received due to a favorable environment, pre-implementation trainings, site champions, and site-specific workflows. External stigma from abstinence-focused treatment communities and pharmacies remained a barrier but was addressed through advocacy and education from clinic providers and staff. More importantly, financial sustainability for HRS remains a major challenge, and while low-cost strategies like utilizing mutual aid networks and task-shifting can support these services in the interim, advocacy for broader policy change and federal investment in harm reduction programs is needed to facilitate widespread implementation and ensure long-term success.
