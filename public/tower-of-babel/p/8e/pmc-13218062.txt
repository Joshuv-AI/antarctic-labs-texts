Managing transitions: a qualitative study of undergraduate international nursing students’ experience of a mentoring program
Authors: Tosin Popoola, Elizabeth Bembridge, Natalie Russell-Hurst, Aung Phone Myint, Ginger Chu, Ritin Fernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectivesInternational nursing students (INS) enhance the diversity of the professional nursing workforce and offer universities substantial cultural, academic, economic, and social advantages. However, INS frequently face challenges in their educational experiences, particularly when targeted support is lacking. Guided by Bridges’ Transition Framework, this study examines the roles of a mentoring program on the academic, social, and personal development of undergraduate INS.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with seven participants (four mentors and three mentees) and analysed data using a hybrid deductive-inductive approach adapted from deductive qualitative analysis, and guided by Bridges’ Transition Framework.ResultsData analysis revealed three themes. The first theme captures emotional responses to studying nursing in a new country, such as uncertainty and uneasiness stemming from differences in academic systems, culture, and language. The second theme explores the process of accepting a new reality, marked by ongoing confusion that prompted INS to seek mentoring support. The third theme demonstrates the emergence of mutual psychological, academic, and social benefits for both mentors and mentees, including increased belonging, academic confidence, and a sense of purpose.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the significance of structured mentoring programs in facilitating INS's transition from uncertainty and confusion to integration and academic confidence.Keywords: International nursing students, Nursing education, Peer mentoring, Peer mentee, Peer mentor

Background

International education plays an important role in supporting global academic mobility and in developing a skilled, internationally competitive workforce [1]. Australia is the fourth most popular destination for international students, following the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States [2]. Approximately 9.6% of international students in Australia are enrolled in health-related programs [3], and given the ongoing demand for qualified healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, many of these students, especially international nursing students (INS), are expected to transition into the Australian nursing workforce after graduation. As a result, we argue that improving the educational experience of INS may contribute to retaining them in the Australian healthcare system, which continues to face a shortage of nurses.

International nursing students constitute a distinct subgroup within the broader international student population, but their experiences often mirror those of other international students. Research indicates that INS frequently experience social isolation, rejection, and discrimination from peers and clinical staff, which diminishes their confidence and sense of belonging [4]. Furthermore, research also reveals that INS often encounter limited learning opportunities due to hostile environments marked by discrimination [4–6]. Beyond social and cultural challenges, INS often encounter transitional difficulties at multiple levels. For example, a scoping review found that INS face the need to manage complex curricula with overlapping deadlines, clinical placements, and online submissions, all of which may not be in their native language [5]. The breadth and intensity of nursing curricula, which require mastery of extensive content across multiple courses in a condensed timeframe, often leave many students feeling overwhelmed and at risk of attrition [4, 5, 7].

Another area where INS has been documented to struggle is the clinical placement aspect of their study. In Australia, a standard three-year program requires a minimum of 800 placement hours, accounting for roughly one-quarter of the total study period [8]. Participation in clinical placements necessitates completion of health and police checks, mandatory training modules, and relevant vaccinations and immunizations. Fulfilling these requirements can be challenging for INS due to language barriers, difficulties obtaining necessary documentation, bureaucratic obstacles in their home countries, differing vaccination requirements, and complex verification processes in Australia [8].

In light of the challenges INS face, there is a growing interest in strategies to improve their learning experiences. One of these strategies is peer mentoring. For example, it was found that INS actively seek peer mentors to assist with navigating the complexities of transitioning to a new academic environment [4]. Similarly, it was reported that INS in Australia value support from peer mentors because they believe that more experienced INS understand their specific needs and cultural backgrounds [7].

Peer mentoring is based on the premise that individuals entering new environments benefit from guidance and support provided by more experienced peers [9]. Extensive evidence in higher education indicates that peer-mentoring programs enhance social and academic integration, foster a stronger sense of belonging, improve emotional resilience, support academic performance, and increase retention [10–14]. Research on mentoring and peer support for INS demonstrates that peer mentoring not only assists students but also builds confidence in managing educational challenges, thereby promoting a sense of belonging [4, 7]. However, most peer-mentoring programs for INS have concentrated on clinical settings [10–12, 15] or on postgraduate INS [16].

Considering the demand for nurses and the unique challenges that INS face, supporting them to have a sense of community can contribute to their retention in Australia [17], which is crucial to addressing the workforce deficit that continues to plague the nursing profession. As such, the School of Nursing and Midwifery at the University of Newcastle in Australia developed a mentoring program (called the Australian Mateship Program) to support undergraduate INS at the University of Newcastle.

The Australian Mateship Program (AMP)

The Australian Mateship Program (AMP) was co-designed in 2023 and piloted in 2024. It continues as an ongoing program at the School of Nursing and Midwifery, University of Newcastle, to support INS. A multidisciplinary team comprising international students, academics who were formerly INS, and faculty involved in supporting INS co-designed the program following a comprehensive review of the literature and stakeholder consultations. The program integrates a suite of tailored orientation resources, including a handbook that addresses common academic and clinical issues, a structured peer-mentoring model with regular check-ins, a dedicated communication channel, and ongoing social and cultural integration activities. Mentors, that is, INS in Years 2 and 3, were recruited via the University’s learning management system (Canvas) and trained in peer support and cultural sensitivity. The AMP was promoted to the commencing INS (mentees) during orientation and early tutorials. The program had 16 mentors and 32 mentees when it first ran in 2024. Each mentor was assigned two mentees.

During the co-design of the AMP, it was established that transitioning into the Australian university system and undertaking clinical placements involves multiple concurrent changes, such as geographical relocation, academic expectations, practical challenges, and sociocultural adjustments. This required the selection of a change theory that not only explicates and normalises the psychological processes that accompany such changes but that also provides a trajectory for how support can lead to a successful transition. We selected Bridges Transition Framework as the change theory because of its theoretical and empirically grounded knowledge about how transition occurs.

Bridges’ Transitions Framework (TF) was developed in the 1970 s to help people and organisations manage change [18]. One key distinction in Bridges’ TF is the distinction between change- the external event (such as relocating, commencing university, starting a new clinical placement)- and transition, the inner psychological process by which people come to terms with that change. From Bridges' perspective, whether change is voluntary or imposed, everyone would undergo emotional work to adjust to a new reality. While transition is not a linear process, Bridges outlines three phases.

Endings – According to Bridges, every transition begins with an ending, such as the end of familiar roles, relationships, routines, or identities. Understandably, difficult emotions such as uncertainty, fear, confusion, anxiety, sadness, and frustration are commonly experienced in this phase of transition. This knowledge informed the development of a program handbook that addresses common academic and clinical issues, including the reality of studying and living in Australia as an international nursing student.

By addressing the most salient challenges and needs in the Endings and Exploration phases, the logic behind the AMP is that it will facilitate the progression of INS toward New Beginnings. As such, the AMP and, by extension, this study were informed by Bridges’ Transition Framework.

Aim of the study

This study aimed to describe the role of the Australian Mateship Program in the academic adaptation, social integration, and personal development of INS.

Methodology

Design

We used a qualitative descriptive design to provide an in-depth view of mentors and mentees' experiences and perceptions of the AMP. Qualitative descriptive design is appropriate for seeking straightforward accounts of phenomena [19], and in this study, it helped us provide formative insights into the role of the AMP in the academic adaptation, social integration, and personal development of INS.

Participants, sampling, and data collection

We used a purposive sampling approach to invite all AMP mentors and mentees to participate in interviews about their experiences. An author-developed, semi-structured interview guide (see supplementary file) was used to collect perspectives on the AMP, including motivation for joining the program, expectations, perceived benefits, and overall experiences with mentoring. The interview guide was designed to serve two purposes. First, we collected data to help us evaluate the program for areas that can be improved, and secondly, we used questions such as ‘what motivated you to join the AMP and how have you experienced the AMP’ to capture their transition experience. However, because we wanted to elicit participants’ experiences inductively and minimise confirmation bias that can occur when questions are tightly mapped to pre-specific theoretical categories, we did not impose the Transitions Framework at data collection. Rather, we applied the framework during analysis and interpretation to enhance the explanatory power and the program's practical implications. The validity of the semi-structured interview guide was established through content review by the fourth author (APM), who was an INS at the time the program was designed, and by TP and GC, who were once INS. Minor suggestions to the order of the questions and to the wording improved clarity and flow. The interviews were conducted via Zoom in English between October and December 2024 and ranged from 30 to 45 min. All interviews were conducted by the last author (RF) to ensure consistency.

Data analysis and rigor

We used a hybrid deductive-inductive approach adapted from Fife and Gossner's deductive qualitative analytic approach [20], as follows. The recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and cross-checked for accuracy by the first and last authors. Thereafter, the first and last authors independently coded the data, met to compare and reconcile codes, and developed themes through consensus. Following inductive coding, we mapped the resulting themes onto Bridges’ phases to enhance interpretation and situate patterns within an established, coherent change theory. Rigour was achieved through investigator triangulation, an audit trail of coding decisions, and transparent reporting with exemplar quotations, which ensures credibility, dependability, and confirmability.

Results

Seven participants (four mentors and three mentees) took part in the study. In terms of gender, there were five females and two males, with ages ranging from 19 to 32 years (median age = 25). Countries of origin included India (n = 4), China (n = 2), and the Philippines (n = 1). Mentees were first-year students who had recently arrived in Australia. Mentor–mentee interactions ranged widely, typically involving 5–8 in-person meetings. Three themes that mirrored the Transition Framework: Endings, Explorations, and New Beginnings, are presented below.

Themes

Theme 1: endings

This theme encapsulates the emotional and social upheaval experienced by INS as they transition from dependence on family to independence in Australia. The participants described a complex mix of emotions, including fear, excitement, and confusion.

I came to Australia when I was 19 years old… straight from high school. It was nerve-racking and exciting, leaving my family and everything behind at such a young age (Mentor 1)

Although the students had mixed emotions, they spent little time dwelling on the lives they left behind. Instead, their attention was on the change they were experiencing. Upon resuming university, the participants quickly realised that the educational systems and academic expectations differed from those in their home countries, which created a sense of unease, uncertainty, and being overwhelmed. The participants’ sources of unease were not confined to the classroom; rather, they extended to discipline-specific and social issues, as described below:

… you have many questions, like about nursing placements, like which documentation you need, how to deal with the placements, and when we are researching for assignments, how should we do it, … how to get a job, you know. That was my situation, because I didn’t know how to (Mentee 1).

… for a 1 st year student and coming from a different culture, I struggled with finding my way through uni in my first year… it was really tough for me to navigate my way and to make friends (Mentor 4)

The participants used the word ‘culture’ to loosely illustrate everything that is different between Australia and their home countries. These perceived differences varied across the nationalities represented in the study and contributed to unique perceptions of disorientation. For example, Chinese participants reported that their transition was more difficult due to language barriers.

It was my first time in Australia. I didn’t know Australian culture and my English is not good, but I needed to do a lot of things for my family, my daughter and my study (Mentee 3)

In contrast, Indian participants expressed greater concern about the gap in academic practices between India and Australia. As an illustration, the dominance of assignments over exams as the primary means of assessing learning was a source of culture shock for students who felt they lacked the academic writing skills needed to thrive in the new context.

So first of all, there is no assignment in my country where I come from… we used to be assessed mainly with exams (Mentee 2)

In alignment with Bridges’ framework, the participants identified what they were losing in terms of language, social networks, culture, and academic systems, and began to recognize the discomfort associated with this new reality. In this stage, participants struggled to make friends, felt academically lost, and had many questions. Although Bridges (2003) argued that people often show resistance at this stage, this was not apparent among the participants, possibly because they chose the change voluntarily.

Theme 2: explorations

This theme describes the awareness of a new reality and new ways of doing things. However, the participants remained uncomfortable with their new reality. The participants continued to struggle with the new skills they needed to develop, and many experienced confusions.

I remember how confused I was… the 1 st semester is the scariest because you don’t know what is expected from the assignments, what kind of writing is expected, how to do your nursing verification… what is expected of you at placements… it was a very daunting experience for someone who is not used to the culture or know how things are usually done (Mentor 2)

The main drivers of this state of confusion are the assignment-based academic system, discipline-specific requirements such as nursing placement verifications, and practical issues, including finding employment. These issues led the participants to seek out mentoring through the Australian Mateship Program.

The nature of the issues faced by the INS required a holistic, but balanced approach from mentors. For example, mentees often approached mentors for help with assignments and proofreading. Taking this on would have increased the burden on mentors who are also students. So, the mentors had to navigate boundaries and roles when supporting mentees. For example, rather than proofreading assignments, mentors provided information about university services that can provide more structured guidance. These clear role expectations, aligned with the structured mentoring approach of the AMP, which helped the students achieve independence earlier.

Yeah, all of their questions were about assignments, like wanting to know whether they were on the right track in their assignments and how to navigate Canvas… I told them I can give ideas about your assignment, but you should go to Academic Learning Support for your specific needs. With Canvas, I referred them to the help button in Canvas and demonstrated how they can use it… For those struggling to find jobs, I provided them with recommendations and showed them how to obtain necessary checks (Mentor 1).

Mentees find this approach helpful, as one participant described below:

My mentor really helped me get familiar with our uni environment. She showed me how to use the Canvas system and the school library to support my studies. She gave me useful tips for my assessment and also showed me how to use Facebook market placement to get uniforms and books, which is cheaper (Mentee 3)

However, tensions existed as mentees tried to navigate and make sense of the confusion inherent in this phase of transition. For example, some mentees were said to be “a bit shy to ask questions and very hesitant to engage with the new system” (Mentor 1). This hesitancy sometimes places emotional labour on mentors as they try to assist mentees through this phase.

Scheduling a meeting is the most difficult part of being a mentor since everyone works these days… Some of my mentees who were initially responding stopped after a while and would not respond to all my attempts to keep in touch (Mentor 2)

The declining motivation reflects the disorientation and disconnection typical of this phase of transition, where students feel overwhelmed by the need to juggle academic, placement, and practical responsibilities.

Theme 3: new beginnings

This theme is characterised by growing confidence in social and academic domains, as students adapt to their new environment. During this phase, mutual psychological and emotional benefits emerged. Mentors found joy, purpose, and a sense of identity through mentorship, while mentees described academic gains and an increased sense of confidence and belonging. At this stage, the confusion and fear had waned, and the participants were much calmer, with a positive attitude about the remaining time at the university. Unlike before, when mentees were reluctant to ask questions, mentors noticed transformative growth in their mentees.

I felt like they initially came with lots of questions and concerns, but now I feel like we have progressed to the stage where they are confident and managing most things on their own. They only reach out occasionally if they really need help… Based on the feedback I received from my mentees, I can say that they have actually adapted quickly and are now more willing to ask questions. They have made friends, have their own lives, and work part-time jobs. So they are actually doing quite well and not as reliant on me as they were when I started mentoring them (Mentor 3)

After the first semester, like in the second semester, I became really confident. I knew what to do, which way to go (Mentee 2)

The AMP also fostered social integration and relationship building. All the participants reported developing a friendship with their partners.

… I became good friends with her [mentee] over time. We always see each other over the weekends or whenever one of us is free. And yeah, I think, based on her feedback, I’d say that this program has been helpful to her, especially when it comes to adjusting and navigating through the courses, placements, and university life. (Mentor 4)

These newly developed relationships signify a new beginning, where students developed positive outlooks, new understandings, new identities, and a sense of belonging. The AMP’s attention to culturally sensitive mentor–mentee matching, when possible, enhanced this social integration. For example, Mentee 3, who said, “… I speak the same language – Mandarin – as my mentor, it’s easy to understand and it lessened my anxiety about my lack of confidence in English.” Not only did the students derive a sense of satisfaction from the mentoring relationship, but they were also motivated to continue participating in the program. For example, all the mentees said they want to repay the benefits they derived from the AMP by volunteering as a mentor in the future.

The participants also provided suggestions about the program that can facilitate the journey of INS towards New Beginnings. First, all participants agreed that the program should start even before INS arrives in Australia to mitigate the negative emotions associated with the Endings of previous roles. For example, Mentee 1 said.

…there were many things I wished I had known before I got here and if, let’s say, we had been told about this program, even before we left home, it could have been better… better because there was a lot of unknowns, so, in my opinion, if there is a way the program can start before we arrived, it would make a big difference… For example, during the university orientation week, you get too much information, and you feel lost and more scared about the whole thing (Mentor 4)

Second, while the mentors said the training they received prepared them for mentorship, they also identified that other information, such as the university grading system and more details about the verification system for clinical placements, should be included in the program, as these are some of the main sources of uncertainty and anxiety for mentees.

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of INS to understand the role of the Australian Mateship Program in their academic adaptation, social integration, and personal development. Our findings revealed that INS were not prepared for differences in language, culture, and educational systems, which resulted in various transitional challenges. As such, the AMP was a valuable resource that enabled participants to manage the emotional reactions of confusion, uncertainty, and fear that dominated their early transition experience, while also helping them become more confident in their new environment.

Although there is increasing attention to the experiences of INS, our study is the first to describe the role that a mentoring program can play in helping them transition from fear and confusion associated with starting in a new environment towards adaptation. The participants started off relying heavily on mentors in multiple domains of learning, such as social, academic, clinical, and personal, but by the end of the program, they had become self-reliant. Through the AMP, INS became more aware and familiar with the university support systems, leading to the dissipation of the confusion, fear, and anxiety that had dominated their earlier experiences. With the support of mentors, mentees gained confidence in the Australian academic system, developed life skills, developed friendships, adjusted to cultural differences, and felt better prepared for their studies. This finding is consistent with other studies [21, 22] on peer mentoring for international students. This suggests that mentoring programs like the AMP can yield significant benefits for the emotional, personal, professional, and social well-being of INS.

Our study confirms earlier studies that reported on the communication, cultural, social, and academic challenges experienced by INS [4, 5] and aligns with earlier studies that reported that INS did not understand or prepare for how the host education system operates. However, the present study differs from earlier studies [4–6, 23] in that the participants did not report experiencing racism, bias, or discrimination. This is an encouraging finding because it provides supporting evidence that mentoring programs can alleviate the challenges of social isolation, a lack of belonging, and discrimination that often accompany the normal transition experiences of INS. However, this finding could also be explained by the lack of specific questions about racism or discrimination. Discrimination can compound the transition experience of INS and lead to a sense of despair and feelings of being overwhelmed [5], which can negatively impact INS’s well-being, especially in the absence of adequate support [4].

Implications

By interpreting the findings through the lens of Bridges’ Transition Framework, we identified that fear, confusion, and uncertainty were common experiences for INS during the early stages of arrival (Endings) and continued even after INS had started their studies (Exploration). These transition challenges prompted INS to seek support and suggest that mentoring programs such as the AMP are poised to meet INS’ need for support in managing transitions. Based on the issues raised by INS in our study, mentoring programs should consider including specific support for the academic, practical, social, and cultural aspects of transition, which are characteristic of the Endings and Exploration phases, and which, when addressed, could accelerate INS’ progress towards New Beginnings. Furthermore, those planning mentoring programs for INS should consider starting the program even before they arrive in the new country, as this can lessen many of the challenges associated with the early phases of transition. Furthermore, contextual information that can ease the transition to INS, such as the grading system and verification information, should be used to improve program resources.

Limitations

Our study provides early insights into the development and implementation of a mentoring program for INS and highlights the academic, social, and personal support the program offers. While previous studies have broadly explored INS experiences, this study contributes new knowledge by examining them specifically within a structured mentoring program. Nevertheless, we recognise that the limited sample reduces the extent to which the full diversity of INS perspectives is represented. Since the AMP is offered each year to new INS, we have an opportunity to address this limitation in future phases of the research by intentionally recruiting a larger, more diverse cohort of participants. This will enable comparisons between the current findings and those from subsequent cohorts, strengthening the robustness and transferability of the conclusions. Additional work will also explore how the program evolves over time and whether its benefits are sustained across different student groups.

A second limitation concerns the interview guide, which was designed primarily to evaluate the program through participants' experiences and did not include specific prompts on sensitive issues such as racism or discrimination. Although such experiences did not emerge organically in the interviews, we acknowledge that they may have surfaced if we had explored them more directly. Future studies on the program will therefore include targeted questions on these topics to better understand whether and how a program like the AMP supports students who may encounter these challenges.

We also recognise the value of complementing qualitative insights with quantitative measures. Future research will incorporate pre- and post-program surveys to assess changes in confidence, academic performance, social connectedness, and transition experiences. This mixed-methods approach will allow us to more clearly establish program impact and strengthen the evidence base for mentoring interventions in international nursing education.

Finally, we acknowledge limitations in the way the Bridges’ TF was applied. Bridges TF was used as an analytical lens to interpret participants’ narratives, rather than shape the interview questions. Although this provided strong conceptual grounding and supported analytic rigour, we recognise that presenting the participants' experiences in a phased manner may inadvertently imply linearity. Thus, we may not have captured the dynamic nature of transition processes, which future studies should consider.

Conclusion

The AMP program played a pivotal role in supporting INS through their transition into Australian academic and social life. Mentors helped bridge cultural, academic, and language gaps by offering practical guidance and sharing personal experiences, making the navigation of unfamiliar systems more manageable. These relationships fostered not only competence but also confidence, as mentees gained insight and reassurance from those who had faced similar challenges. Beyond academic support, the friendships formed through the program provided emotional grounding and a sense of belonging. The shared cultural and transitional experiences between mentors and mentees created a cycle of empowerment, where empathy and encouragement helped students move from uncertainty and isolation toward integration and self-assurance. Most importantly, mentees' desire to become mentors themselves suggests the program has the potential to have an enduring impact, reflecting a successful transition in which students not only adapt to their new environment but also feel inspired to give back and contribute to the community.
