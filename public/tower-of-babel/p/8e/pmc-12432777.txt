Influential factors affecting the survival benefit of combined neoadjuvant therapy and esophagectomy in patients with esophageal cancer: an analysis based on the SEER database
Authors: Mi Chen, Li Jia, Zhou Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal cancer (EC) is a major contributor to cancer-related deaths globally, with significant mortality rates. Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT), involving preoperative chemotherapy or radiation, aims to enhance outcomes in EC when combined with esophagectomy. However, its efficacy varies, necessitating identification of factors influencing survival benefits. This study aims to identify the factors influencing the survival benefit of NAT in patients with EC.MethodsThe current retrospective cohort study collected data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database between 2004 and 2015. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance baseline characteristics between the NAT and No NAT groups. A Cox model-based residual approach was applied to estimate the likelihood of survival benefit. Logistic regression was used to explore associated factors in the training cohort, and a nomogram was subsequently developed and validated.ResultsIn total, 2,755 patients were included in this study. Before PSM, the NAT group had a significantly longer median overall survival (mOS) than the No NAT group (31 vs. 21 months, P<0.001). After PSM, the survival advantage of the NAT group persisted (24 vs. 21 months, P=0.03). In the training cohort, tumor (T) stage, metastasis (M) stage, tumor grade, and lymph node ratio (LNR) appeared to be associated with survival benefit among patients receiving NAT. A nomogram was subsequently constructed based on these factors to estimate the likelihood of deriving benefit from NAT. In the validation cohort, the model exhibited reasonable discriminatory performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.782.ConclusionsThis study identified clinical features potentially associated with survival benefit from NAT in patients with EC. The nomogram may serve as a reference tool to support treatment planning and patient selection in clinical practice.Keywords: Survival benefit; neoadjuvant therapy (NAT); esophagectomy; esophageal cancer (EC); Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results database (SEER database)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Tumor (T) stage, metastasis (M) stage, tumor grade, and lymph node ratio (LNR) are factors affecting survival benefit in esophageal cancer undergoing esophagectomy after neoadjuvant therapy. A nomogram based on these variables was developed to estimate the likelihood of deriving survival benefit from the treatment.

What is known and what is new?

• Patients who received the combination of neoadjuvant therapy and esophagectomy showed significantly higher survival rates compared to those who underwent esophagectomy alone.

• Patients with higher T stage, M1 stage, poorly differentiated tumors, and a LNR of 0 appeared more likely to benefit from neoadjuvant therapy followed by esophagectomy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• It may be very important to select appropriate patients for neoadjuvant therapy, and the subsequent clinical practice should be careful to select treatment methods according to the actual situation of patients.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) ranks as the seventh most prevalent cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with an estimated 544,000 fatalities reported in 2020 (1-3). The prognosis for patients with EC remains poor (4). Surgery remains the primary treatment modality for EC (5). However, the 5-year survival rate for patients who undergo surgery without additional treatments is relatively low (6). Therefore, it may be necessary to improve the esophagectomy procedure to enhance the survival benefits for patients.

Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT), also known as preoperative treatment, has emerged as one of the focal points in the research for the treatment of locally advanced cancer (7). The results of previous studies have demonstrated that esophagectomy following neoadjuvant chemoradiation therapy yields better outcomes, with higher survival rates and a greater R0 resection rate, compared to esophagectomy alone for EC (8,9). Current treatment guidelines recommend that all resectable EC patients, except for those with Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) stage I disease, should undergo NAT before surgery (10-12). However, in recent years, this “one-size-fits-all” approach has increasingly come under scrutiny (13). A single-center study has shown that after propensity score matching (PSM), there was no difference in the 5-year survival rate between the group that underwent direct esophagectomy and the group that received NAT (14). However, the group treated with NAT experienced a higher rate of postoperative complications and a longer average stay in the intensive care unit (ICU) (14). Another multicenter study has indicated that, compared to younger patients, elderly patients derive fewer benefits from NAT and are more likely to experience a greater number of side effects (15). These findings suggest that not all patients with EC are suitable for NAT, highlighting the need for a comprehensive consideration of individual patient characteristics in treatment planning.

Therefore, this study aims to explore which clinical factors are associated with the survival benefit of NAT. The goal is to help identify patients with EC who may benefit more from this treatment. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-543/rc).

Methods

Study design and patients

The current study employs a retrospective cohort design. Data were extracted from the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database between 2004 and 2015. The SEER is recognized as the most extensive publicly accessible database on cancer, encompassing data from 18 cancer registries that collectively represent around 28% of the total population of the United States (https://seer.cancer.gov/data/). Based on the Third Edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) and the SEER historical staging system, a thorough screening of patients with EC was conducted. The tool of SEER*Stat 8.4.1 was adopted to choose the qualified subjects. Patient records were extracted from the SEER database for this study if they met the following criteria: (I) diagnosed as EC; (II) undergoing esophagectomy; and (III) age at diagnosis ≥18 years old. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients who did not undergo esophageal surgery; (II) patients with non-primary EC; (III) patients diagnosed only through autopsy, death certificate, or clinical diagnosis without pathological confirmation; (IV) patients with a follow-up time of 0 months; and (V) patients with incomplete or missing data.

The SEER database maintains stringent confidentiality protocols, ensuring that no personally identifiable information is disclosed. Consequently, the data analysis conducted for this study was exempt from medical ethical review, and it was not required to obtain informed consent from the participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Data collection

The data were collected including (I) demographic characteristics: age (<70 and ≥70 years), gender (female, and male), and income (<$70,000 and ≥$70,000); (II) clinicopathological features: histologic type (adenocarcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma), primary tumor site (upper third of the esophagus, middle third of the esophagus, and distal third of the esophagus), tumor size (≤4.5 and ≥4.5 cm), AJCC tumor (T: T1, T2, T3, and T4), AJCC node (N: N0, N1, N2, and N3), AJCC metastasis (M: M0 and M1), Grade (I, II, III, and IV), number of examined lymph nodes (<12 and ≥12), and lymph node ratio (LNR, 0, < median, and ≥ median); (III) treatment: NAT (yes or no), and post operation radiotherapy (yes or no); and (IV) overall survival (OS), and follow-up (months).

Within the SEER database, histological types were identified based on the ICD-O-3 codes. Adenocarcinomas were categorized with ICD-O-3 codes ranging from 8140 to 8573. Squamous cell carcinomas were designated with ICD-O-3 codes ranging from 8050 to 8082. In the SEER database, the primary tumor site for EC was specified using the ICD-O-3 topography codes. The specific codes for different segments of the esophagus are as follows—C150: cervical esophagus; C151: thoracic esophagus; C152: abdominal esophagus; C153: upper third of the esophagus; C154: middle third of the esophagus; C155: lower third of the esophagus; C158: overlapping lesion of the esophagus; C158: esophagus, unspecified. For cases from 2004 to 2010, the “Clinical Stage (CS) Extension” variable was used to assess T stage. For cases from 2011 to 2015, the “DERIVED AJCC T, 7th Ed” variable was used to determine the T stage. For cases diagnosed between 2004 and 2010, the “CS Lymph Nodes” variable was used to assess N stage. The codes were interpreted as follows—N0: 000; N1: 560+800; N2: 610; N3: 710. For cases with codes 100-255 and 265-280, the number of positive lymph nodes was used to determine the N stage—N0: 0 positive lymph nodes; N1: 1–2 positive lymph nodes; N2: 3–6 positive lymph nodes; N3: 7 or more positive lymph nodes. For cases diagnosed between 2011 and 2015, the “DERIVED AJCC N, 7th Ed” variable was used to determine the N stage. For cases from 2004 to 2010, the “CS Mets at DX” (Clinical Mets at Diagnosis) variable was used to assess the M stage (https://web2.facs.org/cstage0205/esophagus/Esophagus_hbg.html). For cases from 2011 to 2015, the “DERIVED AJCC M, 7th Ed” variable was used to determine the M stage.

Definition of survival benefit

To evaluate patient-level differences in survival outcomes, the Cox model-based residual method was applied. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was first constructed based on the No NAT group to estimate expected OS from baseline characteristics. This model was then used to predict the expected OS for patients in the NAT group. Patients whose observed OS exceeded the predicted values (i.e., positive residuals) were classified as potentially associated with improved survival outcomes following NAT.

Statistical analysis

The categorical data were described by the number of cases and component ratio [n (%)], and the Chi-squared test was used for comparison. To achieve balance in the baseline characteristics between the two groups (NAT group, and No NAT group), PSM was utilized, employing a matching ratio of 1:1. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate OS before and after PSM, and differences between the two groups were compared using the log-rank test. The NAT group was randomly divided into a training cohort and a validation cohort in a 6:4 ratio. In the training cohort, clinical variables potentially associated with survival benefit were explored using univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Variables with P values less than 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. Based on the results, a nomogram was developed to estimate the likelihood of survival benefit. In the validation cohort, the model’s predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The confidence level was set to 0.05 to determine statistical significance. Utilizing SEER*stat version 8.4.1 software, a case list was generated, from which the corresponding data were extracted and subsequently exported for further analysis. The data manipulation, cleansing, statistical analysis, and tabulation were conducted employing R software (version 4.4.3).

Comparison of OS between EC patients who underwent either esophagectomy alone or esophagectomy following NAT

Before PSM, the median OS (mOS) was significantly longer in the NAT group than in the No NAT group (31 vs. 21 months, P<0.001, Figure 2A). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates in the NAT group were 79.4%, 45.4%, and 33.9%, respectively, compared to 67.0%, 34.3%, and 24.7% in the No NAT group. After PSM, the mOS remained longer in the NAT group than in the No NAT group (24 vs. 21 months, P=0.03, Figure 2B).

Nomogram construction for predicting survival benefit

In the training cohort, a nomogram was developed based on T, M, grade, and LNR to evaluate which patients may derive a survival benefit from NAT (Figure 4). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study utilizing data from the SEER database, we explored factors associated with the likelihood of deriving survival benefit from NAT in patients with EC who underwent esophagectomy. Our findings indicate that patients who received the NAT showed higher OS compared to those who underwent esophagectomy alone. The nomogram developed to estimate the likelihood of survival benefit among patients receiving NAT followed by esophagectomy demonstrated good discriminatory ability, indicating a relatively strong predictive performance.

The survival benefit associated with NAT observed in our study has also been reported in previous studies. A meta-analysis, including 27 studies with 4,188 EC patients, has corroborated the survival benefits of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, showing a significant advantage over surgery alone (16). A PSM analysis has identified a significant association between neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery and improved OS in patients with N3 EC (17). NAT not only diminishes tumor volume and surgical complexity but also eradicates micrometastases, mitigates the risk of distant metastasis, and restricts postoperative tumor recurrence (18).

Our findings further suggested that patients with more advanced tumor features—including higher T stage, M1 disease, and poorly differentiated histology (Grade III/IV)—were more likely to be associated with improved survival outcomes following NAT. This showed patients with more aggressive disease burden possess a greater margin for clinical improvement when multimodal strategies are implemented.

Higher T stage, indicative of deeper tumor invasion and larger tumor volume, may allow for more meaningful downstaging following NAT and facilitate a higher rate of complete (R0) resections (19,20). Likewise, patients with M1 disease—typically excluded from curative treatment—may still achieve systemic control through NAT, particularly in the presence of limited or occult metastases (21). Furthermore, poorly differentiated tumors, often exhibiting high proliferative activity, may be more responsive to cytotoxic agents, contributing to better treatment responses in this subset (22).

Importantly, these observed associations must be interpreted with caution. As an observational, non-randomized study, our analysis cannot infer causality. It is possible that the apparent lack of benefit in certain subgroups reflects underlying differences in patient fitness, comorbidity burden, or clinician treatment selection criteria—leading to inherent selection bias (23). For instance, patients deemed unfit for NAT may have had a poorer baseline prognosis regardless of treatment strategy. As such, the associations reported in this study may be confounded by unmeasured clinical variables, even after statistical adjustment and PSM (24-27).

Beyond conventional staging parameters, the LNR, which is the ratio of pathologically metastatic to total harvested lymph nodes, has been identified as an evaluation of prognosis in EC (28-30). Based on the National Cancer Database (NCDB), a study has demonstrated that the LNR is a more effective predictor of OS in patients with EC who are undergoing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (29). A retrospective cohort study has identified that the LNR serves as a precise predictor for determining the potential benefits of postoperative radiotherapy in patients with EC (30). The study by Yukawa et al. found that LNR is recognized as a prognostic factor that can influence OS in patients undergoing curative surgery for EC (31). We observed that a higher LNR was associated with reduced benefits from NAT with esophagectomy in EC patients. In a retrospective analysis of 199 patients with locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) who underwent radical resection after NAC between January 2011 and December 2019, LNR was a significant independent risk factor for OS (32). A study analyzing patients with ESCC who underwent radical esophagectomy after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy found that high LNR was associated with an increased risk of death (33). Our findings highlight the importance of using LNR as a prognostic tool and treatment decision aid in the management of EC patients.

To translate these findings into clinical practice, we constructed a Nomogram incorporating T stage, M stage, tumor grade, and LNR to estimate the probability of association with survival benefit from NAT. Our predictive model demonstrated good performance, with an AUC of 0.782, indicating good discriminatory ability in identifying patients who may benefit from NAT. This model provides a practical tool for estimating the probability of being associated with improved survival outcomes among patients with EC. In clinical practice, patients with higher predicted probabilities may be considered more suitable candidates for NAT, while those with lower predicted scores may warrant more cautious evaluation. This risk-based stratification could support individualized treatment planning and help optimize therapeutic decisions in a real-world setting.

Nevertheless, for patients predicted to derive limited benefit from NAT, it is important to emphasize that this should not be interpreted as evidence of ineffectiveness. Rather, it highlights the necessity of considering alternative or adjunctive treatment strategies tailored to individual risk profiles. For instance, patients with low-risk disease or poor tolerance to multimodal therapy may benefit more from upfront surgery followed by adjuvant treatment. Additionally, definitive chemoradiotherapy remains a guideline-endorsed option for medically inoperable patients or those who are poor surgical candidates (34). With the advent of immunotherapy, ongoing clinical trials are also exploring neoadjuvant and adjuvant checkpoint inhibitors as novel strategies, particularly in high-risk or treatment-resistant populations (35). Incorporating these options into shared decision-making can help ensure that all patients receive the most appropriate and personalized care based on their unique clinical context.

The clinical implications of this study are as follows. First, understanding which patients are less likely to benefit from treatments allows for the development of personalized therapeutic strategies. Second, the study aids in categorizing patients into distinct risk groups, which can guide treatment decisions and more accurately predict outcomes. This stratification can lead to more targeted and potentially effective treatment plans tailored to the risk profile of each group. Third, knowledge of the likelihood of treatment success can inform healthcare systems on how to effectively allocate resources. This information can help prioritize treatments with the highest success rates for each patient group, leading to more efficient and cost-effective healthcare delivery.

While the nomogram provides an accessible and clinically relevant tool for estimating potential benefit from NAT, several methodological considerations should be acknowledged to enhance its clinical interpretability and predictive precision. To this end, continuous variables such as age and LNR were categorized using practical cutoff values to facilitate ease of application in clinical settings. However, this simplification may lead to information loss and reduced model accuracy. Future studies should consider adopting more advanced statistical approaches, such as machine learning algorithms, to enhance the model’s predictive accuracy, capture complex nonlinear relationships, and improve generalizability across heterogeneous patient populations (36,37).

The present study is subject to several limitations primarily related to the data available within the SEER database. Firstly, the collection of data is constrained, precluding the acquisition of potential confounding factors such as the specifics of chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens, dosages, and any modifications to treatment protocols that may have occurred during the follow-up period. Secondly, essential prognostic variables—including patient performance status, comorbidities, nutritional and inflammatory markers, and molecular characteristics—were not captured in the database, potentially contributing to residual confounding even after PSM. Thirdly, the use of logistic regression modeling, while interpretable and clinically practical, may not fully capture nonlinear interactions between predictors. Lastly, the retrospective and observational nature of this study precludes causal inference, and selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Future prospective studies with more comprehensive data and advanced modeling approaches are warranted to validate and improve the predictive utility of our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified key clinicopathological factors—including T stage, M stage, tumor grade, and LNR—that were associated with the likelihood of survival benefit from NAT in EC patients. The developed nomogram demonstrated good predictive performance and may assist in refining patient selection for neoadjuvant treatment.
