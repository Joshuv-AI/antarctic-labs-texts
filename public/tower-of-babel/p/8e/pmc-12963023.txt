Temporal Characteristics and Management of Prolonged Sinus Pause Following Atrial Fibrillation Termination After Catheter Ablation—A Preliminary Study
Authors: Yi Guo, Cheng Cai, Hongwu Chen, Kai Gu, Weizhu Ju, Gang Yang, Jiaojiao Shi, Zidun Wang, Hailei Liu, Xiaohong Jiang, Chang Cui, Mingfang Li, Minglong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionProlonged sinus pause (PSP) after atrial fibrillation (AF) termination shortly after catheter ablation seems to be a reversible phenomenon without further need for pacemaker implantation (PMI). However, no mature management strategy has been established.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, we included consecutive patients who developed PSP ≥ 3 s upon AF termination during hospitalization following AF ablation at our center between October 2020 and October 2022. Regular follow‐up was conducted.ResultsTwelve patients (mean age 66.3 ± 8.5 years; 7 females) were included. PSP ≥ 3 s upon AF termination initiated at a median of 2.0 days (IQR: 1.3–3.8) post‐ablation and resolved by 5.0 days (IQR: 3.5–7.5). Three patients received temporary PMI, all of which were removed before discharge without further pacing. One patient underwent permanent PMI during hospitalization due to Adams‐Stokes syndrome. After discharge, one patient received permanent pacing for symptomatic sinus arrest at 3 months, and another underwent atrioventricular node ablation followed by permanent pacing due to recurrent arrhythmia. The remaining patients had no related symptoms or need for pacing during follow‐up.ConclusionPSP ≥ 3 s upon AF termination shortly after catheter ablation appears to be reversible. Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most prevalent cardiac arrhythmia in adults, is clinically significant due to its association with impaired quality of life, increased risk of ischemic stroke, new‐onset and exacerbating heart failure, and elevated mortality (Hindricks et al. 2020). Catheter ablation incorporating pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) as the procedural cornerstone is an established therapeutic strategy for rhythm control of AF. The current guidelines recommend it as the first‐line treatment in symptomatic patients who are refractory or intolerant to antiarrhythmics.

Nevertheless, even after successful restoration of sinus rhythm during the procedure, some patients may develop new‐onset sinus node dysfunction (SND), occasionally requiring temporary or permanent pacemaker implantation. The underlying mechanisms can be attributed to the following factors: (1) Unmasking of preexisting SND—AF termination may reveal latent sinus node dysfunction (SND) previously concealed by the arrhythmia (Semmler et al. 2019). (2) Pharmacological effects—rate‐controlling and antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) can suppress sinus node function. (3) Ablation‐related injury—direct damage to the sinoatrial node complex, sinoatrial nodal artery (SNA), or autonomic plexus may occur (Choi et al. 2013). (4) Post‐procedural inflammatory response—prolonged sinus pauses (PSPs) following arrhythmia termination may result from an acute inflammatory storm.

Our previous retrospective study (Guo et al. 2023) identified PSP following AF termination as the primary indication for pacemaker implantation (PMI) in patients undergoing AF ablation. However, emerging evidence (Hocini et al. 2003; Inada et al. 2014) suggests that successful arrhythmia elimination via ablation may lead to PSP resolution, potentially obviating the need for permanent pacing. Additionally, it is well established that early atrial arrhythmia recurrence during the blanking period (the first 3 months post‐ablation) is frequently attributed to acute inflammatory responses (Mccabe et al. 2008; Wei et al. 2022). Importantly, these early recurrences do not necessarily predict long‐term procedural failure (Calkins et al. 2021).

Based on these observations, we hypothesize that new‐onset PSP following atrial arrhythmia termination post‐ablation may represent a transient phenomenon, potentially obviating the need for permanent PMI. In such cases, close monitoring with temporary cardiac pacing support during the recovery period may constitute a reasonable management strategy. The present study aims to describe the natural process of PSP following AF termination and to explore the optimal surveillance duration and temporary pacing bridging period for this patient population.

Methods

Study Population

This prospective observational study included consecutive patients who underwent catheter ablation for AF and exhibited PSP ≥ 3 s following AF termination during the acute phase during hospitalization at our center between October 2020 and October 2022. The exclusion criteria were (1) pre‐existing indications for PMI prior to the ablation; (2) previous PMI history; (3) current amiodarone therapy for arrhythmia management; (4) significant valvular heart diseases. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. All enrolled patients provided written informed consent.

Ablation Procedures

All AADs were discontinued for ≥ 5 half‐lives prior to the ablation procedure. Patients received oral anticoagulation for a minimum of 3 weeks before the procedure. Preprocedural evaluation for intracardiac thrombi was performed using transesophageal echocardiography, computed tomography angiography, or intra‐cardiac echocardiography. Ablation procedures were conducted under conscious sedation with intravenous midazolam and fentanyl. Following transseptal puncture, intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time between 300 and 350 s throughout the procedure. The procedural details have been previously published in the STABLE‐SR series studies (Yang et al. 2017). In brief, circumferential pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) served as the foundational ablation strategy for all patients. When AF persisted following circumferential PVI, electrical cardioversion was performed to restore sinus rhythm. Subsequent to rhythm restoration, we conducted high‐density voltage mapping of the left atrium using a multipolar mapping catheter. Low‐voltage areas were defined as regions exhibiting bipolar electrogram amplitudes < 0.5 mV. When identified, these regions underwent targeted substrate modification according to our previously published methodology. If non‐pulmonary vein triggers, sustained atrial tachycardias or supraventricular tachycardias were identified during the procedure, further ablation was performed.

Post‐Procedural Management

All patients received antiarrhythmic agents unless contraindicated. Propafenone was used orally in patients with paroxysmal AF and amiodarone in patients with persistent AF. For recurrent sustained atrial arrhythmias, intravenous amiodarone was administered for 48–72 h and electrical cardioversion was performed when atrial arrhythmia persisted despite medical therapy. Upon detection of PSP ≥ 3 s on termination of AF, AADs were immediately discontinued and cardiac monitoring would be extended. Intravenous atropine or isoprenaline was used for sinus bradycardia < 45 bpm. When patients with post‐ablation PSP had related symptoms of amaurosis or syncope, they would have temporary pacemaker via subclavian vein access and remained hospitalized for continuous monitoring until rhythm stabilization. A “watch‐and‐wait strategy” was adopted in patients without related symptoms for 7 days (Guo et al. 2023). Patients with no related symptoms would consider discharge when PSP vanished over 24 h with sinus rhythm.

Implantation and Removal of Pacemaker

Temporary PMI was performed using an activation‐fixation permanent pacing lead (Medtronic 5076, Minneapolis, MN, USA) positioned in the right ventricle apex. The lead was connected to an externalized pacing generator (Medtronic X3, Minneapolis, MN, USA) programmed to VVI mode with a backup rate of 40 bpm. Patients underwent daily evaluation for sinus node function recovery. Temporary pacemaker would be removed unless absence of PSP ≥ 3 s for 24 consecutive hours and stable intrinsic heart rate > 40 bpm without symptoms. Otherwise, if PSP ≥ 3 s persisted, permanent PMI would be considered. Following permanent pacemaker implantation, AADs were reinstated as needed for rhythm control. Follow‐Up Protocol

Patients underwent scheduled outpatient clinic visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months following the ablation procedure. Cardiac arrhythmia monitoring was performed using 24‐h Holter recordings at 1 and 3 months, followed by 7‐day ambulatory Holter monitoring at 6–12 months post‐ablation.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was defined as the requirement for permanent PMI within the 12‐month post‐procedural period. The secondary endpoint was AF recurrence, documented by electrocardiographic evidence within 12 months after ablation.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables. Skewed distribution data are expressed as median (Interquartile range, IQR). All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 22.0.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between October 2020 and October 2022, a total of 1330 patients underwent atrial fibrillation catheter ablation (AFCA) at our center. Among these, 14 patients (1.1%) exhibited PSP ≥ 3 s during post‐ablation hospitalization. Two patients were excluded from further analysis. One had pre‐existing sick sinus syndrome and was under consideration for PMI prior to ablation. The other had a history of rheumatoid heart valve disease and had undergone both mitral and aortic valve replacement. The remaining 12 (12/1330, 0.9%) patients were included in the study. Their mean age was 66.3 ± 8.5 years, and 7 (58.3%) were female. Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Among the 12 included patients, 11 (91.7%) had paroxysmal AF, while one had non‐paroxysmal AF. Notably, none of the patients had a documented history of symptomatic sinus pause or atrioventricular block before the ablation procedure. One patient (No. 4) exhibited an asymptomatic PSP of 5 s upon AF termination prior to ablation. Two patients (No. 2 and No. 10) had undergone prior catheter ablation for AF. No patients reported symptoms of heart failure, and none had impaired left ventricular ejection fraction.

Post‐Procedural Sinus Pauses and Pacemaker Characteristics

The PSP ≥ 3 s following AF termination first appeared at a median of 2.0 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 1.3–3.8 days) after ablation and resolved by a median of 5.0 days (IQR: 3.5–7.5 days). The longest PSP ≥ 3 s occurred at a median of 2.5 days post‐procedure (IQR: 1.5–4.0 days), with the maximum pause duration measuring 7.3 ± 1.9 s. Among the cohort, three patients (No. 1, 3, and 11) received temporary PMI during hospitalization, all of which were successfully removed prior to discharge. Notably, Patient No. 11 experienced temporary pacing electrode dislocation necessitating reimplantation. Patients No. 4 and 6 with symptomatic PSP were recommended for temporary pacing but declined intervention. Patient No. 9 experienced PSPs precipitating ventricular fibrillation following electrical cardioversion for early AF recurrence and received permanent PMI (VVI mode) on day 10 post procedure. The other 6 patients did not receive temporary or permanent pacing, as they did not present with severe symptoms. Discussion

This single‐center prospective study provides novel insights into the incidence, time course, and clinical implications of PSP following AFCA. We found that the incidence of PSP ≥ 3 s shortly after AFCA was less than 1%. Notably, most cases occurred within 2 days post‐procedure and resolved within 1 week, suggesting a transient and reversible phenomenon. Only a small proportion of patients (3/12, 25%) needed permanent pacing therapy. These findings support a conservative management approach in most cases, avoiding premature permanent PMI.

Our results differ significantly from previous reports. For instance, Barra et al. (2018) reported a 0.67% incidence of PSP, but in their study, most patients developed symptomatic PSP approximately 1 week after ablation, and the majority ultimately required permanent pacing. In contrast, the patients in our cohort typically recovered sinus node function within 1–2 weeks without the need for permanent devices. These discrepancies may reflect differences in the underlying mechanisms of PSP. Subacute PSP (occurring after 1 week) may be more likely due to irreversible ablation‐related injury to the SNA, while acute PSP (within 1 week) may be driven by reversible factors such as peri‐ablation edema, transient SNA ischemia, or AAD usage post‐ablation. As these factors resolve, sinus node function may recover, often within 2 weeks. Our observations are consistent with findings from Semmler et al. (2019) who reported that although some patients with SND required temporary pacing, approximately one‐third eventually recovered, indicating that the dysfunction was transient in a substantial proportion of cases.

Several mechanisms may contribute to transient SND following ablation. AF and SND had a common underlying electrophysiologic substrate, which is known as atrial myopathy (John and Kumar 2016). However, most of the subjects had paroxysmal AF, and none had pre‐existing SND may argue against the notion that atrial remodeling is solely responsible for PSP. Thermal injury to the SNA with resultant ischemia is a plausible cause. The fact that PSP predominantly occurred after reversion from tachyarrhythmias, rather than as isolated bradycardia or sinoatrial arrest, supports a hypothesis of ischemia superimposed on a damaged SNA, possibly exacerbated by overdrive suppression (Barra et al. 2018). Moreover, pharmacological factors may play a role. AADs could have contributed to PSPs as they were routinely used in most patients. It is evident that discontinuation of AADs would attenuate sinus arrest in many cases.

From a clinical perspective, the available evidence suggests that a “watch‐and‐wait” strategy is both safe and reasonable in many cases. Killu et al. (2016) suggested a 2–6 day observation period to identify patients who truly require permanent pacing. Kitamura et al. (2016) reported recovery of sinus node function in 1–2 weeks post‐ablation, a timeline consistent with our findings. Based on these data, a stepwise, symptom‐driven approach during a 2‐week watch‐and‐wait period appears practical. This approach should include daily monitoring of heart rate and rhythm, temporary discontinuation of rate‐limiting medications, and the use of isoproterenol infusion in symptomatic patients. For those with refractory symptoms, temporary pacing may be appropriate. Management decisions, however, should be tailored to the individual. In some cases, prolonged hospitalization may be required until stable intrinsic heart rate is restored. Looking forward, the development of semi‐permanent pacing systems that are easily removable after several weeks could offer a useful interim solution for patients with transient but symptomatic SND.

Anatomical considerations may further explain the variability in post‐ablation SND. Although the anatomical location of the sinoatrial node is well known, variation in its arterial supply is often overlooked (Ho and Sánchez‐Quintana 2016). The SNA originates from the left circumflex artery in about 40% of individuals (Pejković et al. 2008), making it susceptible to injury during left atrial ablation procedures such as PVI or mitral isthmus ablation (Sohns et al. 2016; Hai et al. 2014). Additionally, the SVC, derived embryologically from the same origin as the sinoatrial node, often contains myocardial sleeves that may trigger AF (Mirzoyev et al. 2010; Chen et al. 2022). SVC isolation is therefore sometimes performed, but our findings suggest an increased risk of acute SND with peri‐SVC ablation—7 of 12 affected patients developed PSP. The variable course of the SNA—entering the node precavally, postcavally, or both—renders it vulnerable during SVC ablation (Killu et al. 2016). Even endocardial ablation may result in subepicardial damage via thermal conduction or microvascular injury.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, post‐ablation cardiac computed tomography angiography (CTA) and pathological analysis was not performed. Thus, variations in the course of the SNA and the presence of ablation‐related injury could not be assessed. The mechanism of PSP was conjectural. Second, asymptomatic PSP ≥ 3 s during follow‐up may have been underdetected. Third, sinoatrial node recovery time was not routinely evaluated before or during ablation. Lastly, due to the small sample size (N = 12) in our study, the clinical applicability of the proposed management strategy requires confirmation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Conclusion

PSP ≥ 3 s observed at the termination of AF following AFCA may be a reversible condition. In most cases, it can be managed with the discontinuation of AADs or temporary pacing support. A small proportion of patients may ultimately require permanent PMI.
