The functional dynamics of FicD’s TPR domain are modulated by the interaction with ATP and BiP
Authors: Svenja Runge, Ecenaz Bilgen, Andrea Magni, Giorgio Bonollo, Giorgio Colombo, Aymelt Itzen, Don C. Lamb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe heat shock protein 70 family, including the ER-specific homolog BiP, is vital for protein homeostasis. BiP is regulated posttranslationally via AMPylation by FicD, whose crystal structures reveal conformational variability and suggest dynamic interdomain motions of the tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domain. Using two-color, smFRET experiments, three TPR conformations were identified. By comparing intensity and lifetime-based FRET efficiency values, one conformation was found to be a static TPR-in conformation whereas other molecules exhibited dynamic fluctuations between TPR-in and TPR-out states. The dynamics observed for the monomeric FicD are conserved for the dimeric enzyme, are modulated by ATP and BiP, and correlate with FicD’s AMPylation and deAMPylation activities. This research uncovers real-time dynamics of FicD, advancing the understanding of its functional mechanisms.

The heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) family of proteins is essential for protein homeostasis from bacteria to humans. Despite their various functions, which include assisting in protein folding, targeting, and transportation of newly synthesized proteins, they share common structural and mechanistic features (1). BiP is the Hsp70 homolog found in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of mammalian cells where it participates in translocation of proteins across the ER, targets misfolded proteins for degradation and aids in the folding of nascent proteins (2). Like all Hsp70 proteins, BiP shows an adenosine-triphosphate (ATP) dependent conformational cycle that regulates its chaperoning activity: In the ATP-bound state of the nucleotide binding domain (NBD), the substrate binding domain (SBD) loosely associates with unfolded client proteins (3). However, after ATP hydrolysis to ADP, the SBD gains high affinity to the client, thereby stabilizing it until subsequent replacement of ADP with ATP releases the client. This nucleotide-dependent cycle is accompanied with structural changes in the Hsp70: In the ATP-state, the SBD docks to the NBD whereas, in the ADP-bound form, the SBD dislodges from the NBD (4–6).

Being involved in ER homeostasis, BiP is susceptible to changes in the protein folding load and contributes to regulation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) (7). BiP’s ability to bind client proteins is regulated by the ER-resident “filamentation induced by cyclic-AMP” (Fic) domain containing protein FicD (8, 9). FicD is the only representative of the Fic enzyme family within the human genome and is highly conserved among eukaryotic species (10). Using ATP as a cosubstrate, the bifunctional enzyme can reversibly transfer an adenosine-monophosphate (AMP) to the hydroxyl group of amino acid residue T518 in the SBD of BiP, a process termed AMPylation (11–14). AMPylation traps BiP in a conformation where the NBD and SBD are docked, establishing a pool of ATP hydrolysis deficient BiP with low client-binding affinity (15). While AMPylation-mediated BiP inactivation occurs during low client protein load, the deAMPylation of BiP by FicD reactivates BiP during protein-folding stress (16). This posttranslational regulation of BiP has been proven to be essential in stress resilience of cells and tissues (17). Furthermore, mutations in FicD that deregulate its AMPylation/deAMPylation activity were linked to motor neuron and pancreatic diseases due to elevated levels of AMPylated, and thus inactive, BiP (18, 19).

The N-terminus of FicD (amino acid residues 1-105) contains a single transmembrane domain and is structurally uncharacterized. In contrast, the ER luminal domains are well studied: Two tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) motifs, each composed of two α-helices, are connected to the Fic domain by a helical linker (20, 21) (Fig. 1). The Fic domain contains sequence motifs conserved in the Fic family, i.e. the catalytic HcatPFx(D/E)GN(G/K)R motif and the inhibitory (S/T)XXXEinh(G/N) motif (22, 23). Mutation of the catalytic H363 (Hcat) to alanine abrogates the enzymatic activity of Fic enzymes. The inhibitory E234 (Einh) residue instead inhibits AMPylation and is required for deAMPylation; mutation of E234 to glycine results in enhanced AMPylation activity of FicD (22). In the ER, functional regulation is conferred by the dimerization state of FicD: The dimer interface between the Fic domains is connected to the catalytic center via a hydrogen bond network, favoring deAMPylation by imposing rigidity to Einh. In monomerized FicD [e.g. by the L258D mutation (20)], a higher flexibility within this network allows deflection of the repressive residue and leads to the AMPylation competent binding of a divalent magnesium ion (Mg2+) and ATP. In addition, the ATP binding is proposed to allosterically regulate FicD’s dimerization equilibrium and affinity to BiP (21). The TPR motifs of FicD are essential for target recognition and binding during AMPylation and deAMPylation reactions (24). Moreover, the intrinsic flexibility of this domain is linked to both catalytic activities of FicD, as reaction rates are significantly reduced upon fixation to the interdomain linker helix via an engineered disulfide bond (25).

A comparison of the crystal structures of monomeric nucleotide-free (apo-state) versus ATP-bound FicDL258D hints at a potential rearrangement between the Fic and TPR domain in the nucleotide-bound state (21). In the apo-state, the TPR domain crystallized in a commonly observed TPR-in conformation (Fig. 1B, light blue) that is structurally similar to its conformation in the dimeric enzyme when bound to BiP (Fig. 1B, green) (20, 21, 24, 25). In contrast, the TPR domain of the ATP-bound FicDL258D crystallized in a TPR-out conformation, being flipped outward by ca. 180° (21) (Fig. 1B, yellow). Although the crystal structures are informative, details of FicD’s dynamics and of its structural transitions in solution are missing. Such information would provide insights into how the conformational dynamics of FicD are necessary for enzyme regulation and enzyme–target interactions. More specifically, it enhances our understanding of how the flexibility of the TPR motifs regulates functionality and its regulatory interaction with BiP, where disturbances can cause severe phenotypes. Therefore, we applied single-molecule Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (smFRET) spectroscopy to probe the interdomain rearrangements of FicD in dependence of its interactions with BiP and the cosubstrate ATP. Our experiments reveal that both the TPR-in and TPR-out conformations coexist in equilibrium in-solution with transitions occurring on the microsecond to millisecond timescales. We demonstrate that ATP and BiP modulate the transitions of the TPR domain. Furthermore, the conformational dynamics of FicD are conserved in both the monomeric and dimeric states but are altered upon its interaction with BiP and is dependent on BiP’s AMPylation state. With the aid of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we derive ensembles of structures for the different states that are consistent with the smFRET data.

Results

The Conformation of the TPR-Domain in FicD Undergoes Dynamic Interconversion.

To investigate the conformational flexibility of the TPR-motifs relative to the Fic domain in solution, we fluorescently labeled monomeric FicDL258D (Fig. 1B) for smFRET experiments. The monomeric mutant was chosen since domain rearrangements were observed for monomeric FicD in crystal structures. Cysteine substitutions were implemented for labeling of the protein with maleimide-functionalized fluorophores ATTO532 as the donor dye (D) and ATTO643 as the acceptor dye (A). We chose S288C as a labeling position on the surface of the Fic domain, which has been previously implemented for fluorescent labeling (21), and combined it with R118C as a second position located on the 1st TPR motif. The integrity of these constructs was validated via thermal stability analysis using nano differential scanning fluorimetry (nDSF) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). After fluorescent labeling of FicDL258D S288C R118C (hereafter referred to as FicDR118C), the presence of hetero, double-labeled species was confirmed via intact liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). Note that the endogenous cysteine (C421) of FicD was not removed by mutation in our smFRET samples as FicDC421S exhibits a substantially reduced thermal stability (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) and only a negligible amount (≤ 5 %) of triple labeled species were detected by LC-MS (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Since the TPR domain is essential for the interaction with BiP (24, 25), BiP-AMPylation assays were used to ensure that the mutations and fluorescent dyes do not affect FicD’s activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E).

For the dual-color smFRET experiments, we used multiparameter fluorescence detection (MFD) with pulsed interleaved excitation (PIE) (26). This technique measures the FRET efficiency of single, freely diffusing FicDR118C molecules in solution as they transverse the observation volume. By gathering statistics of single molecule events, we can resolve the various conformational states of FicD with respect to the relative orientation of the TPR motifs to the Fic domain. The FRET efficiency histogram for FicDR118C in the apo-state reveals two distinct populations with mean FRET efficiency values of E=0.31 (low-FRET) and E=0.71 (high-FRET) (Fig. 2A, light blue, SI Appendix, Table S1), indicating at least two conformational states of FicD. Next, we investigated the conformation of FicD in the presence of 5 mM ATP. The affinity of ATP binding to FicD was determined to be ca. 60 µM and no relevant hydrolysis was observed during the measurement duration of 2 h (see SI Appendix, Supplementary note 1, Fig. S2 A and B). Hence, FicD is saturated with its co-substrate at 5 mM ATP. Two highly similar states are still observed with FRET efficiencies of E=0.33 and E=0.73, but the fraction of the high-FRET state is increased compared to FicD in the absence of ATP (Fig. 2A, yellow, SI Appendix, Table S1). To rule out that the presence of the low-FRET peak is not due to incomplete saturation of ATP binding, we also investigated the conformation of FicD in the presence of the ATP analog, AMP-PNP. Here again, both populations were observed, indicating that both states are present in the ATP bound conformation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). We attribute the difference in amplitude of the high FRET state to less effective mimicking of the ATP bound conformation. To evaluate whether the observed conformations interconvert dynamically on the millisecond timescale, we made use of the fluorescence lifetime information available from the MFD-PIE measurements (26). In a two-dimensional plot of the fluorescence lifetime of the donor dye in the presence of acceptor (τD(A)) vs the intensity-based FRET efficiency (E) (E-τD(A) plot), the static FRET line (black line in Fig. 2 B and C) represents the theoretical E-τD(A) correlation for static molecules (SI Appendix, Eq. S1). Populations that undergo conformational transitions during their diffusion through the observation volume appear shifted to the right from the static FRET line and can be analyzed using a dynamic FRET line (red line in Fig. 2 B and C and SI Appendix, Eq. S1) (27, 28). For systems that show fluctuations on the µs to ms timescale between two conformational states, fitting the fluorescence lifetime distribution of τD(A) to a biexponential function determines the two endpoints for the dynamic FRET line (SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 2).

In the E-τD(A) plots for FicDR118C in the absence and presence of ATP (Fig. 2 B and C), the low-FRET populations lie on the static FRET line (black) of the E-τD(A) plot, indicating that these populations represent a conformation that is static on the ms timescale. The corresponding lifetime of τD(A)=2.8ns translates into a fluorophore distance (R) of 72 Å (SI Appendix, Table S2). In contrast, the high-FRET populations at E of ca. 0.72 in the histograms reveal a substantial shift to the right of the static FRET line (Fig. 2 B and C). The lifetime analysis of the high-FRET population of FicDR118C in the absence of ATP can be fit with a biexponential with fluorescence lifetimes of τD(A)=2.4ns and τD(A)=0.5ns, which corresponds to the endpoints of the dynamic FRET line (red). These lifetimes correspond to FRET efficiencies of 0.35 and 0.87 and fluorophore distances of 65 and 43 Å, respectively (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S2). Thus, there is a dynamic flipping of the TPR domain on the µs to ms timescale between a low-FRET (R = 65 Å) and a high-FRET (R = 43 Å) conformation. The 2D E-τD(A) plot in the presence of ATP exhibits similar static and dynamic states with an increased fraction of the dynamically interconverting FicDR118C population (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Table S2).

To check the correlation between the experimentally detected states and the aforementioned crystal structures, we performed accessible volume (AV) calculations (29) based on the apo [PDB 6I7J (21)] and ATP-bound [PDB 6I7K (21)] FicD crystal structures. The theoretically accessible positions of the fluorescent dyes (ATTO532 and ATTO643) attached at positions R118C and S288C (surface clouds in Fig. 2 D and E) allow us to predict the expected mean distance between the dye pair (RDA, SI Appendix, Table S3), which can be directly compared with the experimental data (SI Appendix, Table S2). The RDA of 74 Å for the apo-state crystal structure nicely corresponds to the static low-FRET conformation of FicDR118C in the presence or absence of ATP (RDA = 71 to 72 Å). For the ATP-bound structure of FicD, the AV calculations predicted a RDA of 42 Å, which again is very similar to the distance measured for the high-FRET endpoints of the dynamic switching of FicDR118C (RDA = 43 to 44 Å). As this population was also enhanced in the presence of ATP (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S1), we attribute the dynamic high-FRET population to a TPR-out conformation as represented by the FicD:ATP crystal structure (Fig. 2D, yellow). Similarly, the static low-FRET (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S1) likely corresponds to a TPR-in conformation as represented by the apo-state FicD crystal structure (Fig. 2E, light blue).

In conclusion, the smFRET experiments clearly reveal three distinguishable conformational states: the static, low-FRET TPR-in conformation (state 1) versus the dynamically interconverting low-FRET TPR-in (state 2) and high-FRET TPR-out (state 3) conformations. The FRET distances for the TPR-in correspond with that of the apo crystal structure whereas the TPR-out distance is consistent with that of the ATP-bound FicD crystal structure. Upon the addition of ATP, the dynamic population is enhanced.

BiP Affects the Conformational Distribution of FicD.

Non-AMPylated BiP.

Next, we investigated the influence of BiP on the conformational equilibrium of FicD. These smFRET experiments were performed using near full-length (amino acids 19-654), ATP-hydrolysis deficient BiPT229A (5, 30). In addition to the reduction of BiP-dependent ATP hydrolysis, this mutant is a more efficient AMPylation substrate for FicD (12). The affinity for the interaction between BiP and FicD is approximately 2 to 20 µM (16, 25, 31). Hence, we used 100 µM of unlabeled BiP (5 to 50-fold KD) to facilitate enzyme–substrate complex formation. In comparison to the apo- and ATP-bound states, the FRET efficiency histogram of FicDR118C in the presence of BiP shows shifts for both the static and the dynamically averaged FRET populations to lower FRET efficiencies of 0.24 and 0.61, respectively. (Fig. 3A, light blue, SI Appendix, Table S1). The addition of ATP to FicDR118C and BiP further shifted the low-FRET population to E = 0.19 (Fig. 3B, orange, SI Appendix, Table S1).

The 2D E-τD(A) plot of FicD:BiP (Fig. 3C) confirms the presence of three similar conformations with a static, low FRET state (E = 0.23, R = 72 Å) and a dynamic population fluctuating between the dynamic TPR-in (E = 0.29, R = 68 Å) and TPR-out (E = 0.86, R=43 Å) conformations (SI Appendix, Table S2). The AV calculation based on the crystal structure of a covalently linked complex (clc) between FicD and BiP (PDB 6ZMD) (24) predicts a shift to a slightly larger dye distance (RDA = 78 Å compared to RDA = 74 Å) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and Table S3). We observe no significant changes in the measured values for the three conformations. However, the FRET efficiency histograms and E-τD(A) plots indicate an increase in the contribution of the static TPR-in population upon addition of BiP (which has the furthest separation of the three conformations). This fraction returns to a similar value of FicD:ATP when all three are present (FicD:ATP:BiP, Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Table S1).

This raises the question of whether BiP is bound by FicD despite the large contribution of the dynamic TPR-out conformation. To investigate this question, we performed a fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) analysis of the smFRET measurements and calculated the average diffusion coefficients (D) for the labeled protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S4). Due to the inverse correlation with the hydrodynamic radius, binding events between FicDR118C and BiP will reduce the diffusion coefficient. Compared to the apo-state of FicD, the addition of ATP caused a minor (ca. 5 %) decrease in the diffusion coefficient, whereas the addition of BiP and BiP with ATP resulted in a reduction by almost 20 and 35 %, respectively. Due to the many factors that can influence the diffusion coefficient determined from an FCS analysis of smFRET data, we limit the discussion to a qualitative discussion of the change in diffusion coefficients.

The decrease in the diffusion coefficient indicates that binding between FicD and BiP is enhanced in the presence of its cosubstrate ATP (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S4). This observation is not unexpected since apo-state BiP mainly adopts the domain-undocked conformation, which cannot be bound by FicD, while BiP:ATP favors the domain-docked conformation that presents the interface for binding by FicD (15, 24, 25, 32). To further verify this observation, we performed a species FCS analysis on the single-molecule bursts that correspond to the static TPR-in state (E < 0.4) and the dynamic population fluctuating between the TPR-in and TPR-out conformations (E > 0.4). We analyzed the acceptor channel after acceptor excitation to avoid complications from the FRET signal. The results show that BiP interacts with both the static and dynamic populations both in the absence and presence of ATP (SI Appendix, Table S5). Our results overall suggest that BiP-binding, especially in the presence of the cosubstrate ATP, induces moderate structural changes in the TPR conformations of FicD and that the dynamic characteristics of the TPR domain, including the effect of ATP, are not restricted by the interaction with BiP (SI Appendix, Tables S1, S2 and S4).

AMPylated BiP.

Since FicD can deAMPylate BiPAMP, we additionally investigated the interaction of the monomeric FicDR118C with AMPylated BiP. In the presence of BiPAMP, FicDR118C shows a major peak for the static, low-FRET TPR-in conformation and the corresponding decrease in the dynamic, high-FRET population (Fig. 3A, black, Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Table S1). The conformation of the major static, TPR-in population remains similar with a dye separation of 75 Å (SI Appendix, Table S2). Previous research established a high-affinity interaction between catalytically inactive FicD and AMPylated BiP (21). As monomerized FicDL258D is defective in deAMPylation (25), it is likely that the interaction between BiPAMP and monomeric FicD results in a stabilized enzyme–target complex. This hypothesis is supported by a significant decrease (ca. 40 %) in the diffusion coefficient of FicDR118C compared to the apo-state protein or the presence of unmodified BiP (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S4). We also performed FCS experiments on a titration series of FicDR118C in the presence of increasing BiP or BiPAMP concentrations confirming the substantially higher affinity of FicD for AMPylated BiP (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and Table S6). Based on these results, we assume that FicDR118C is saturated with BiPAMP during the smFRET measurements, while the addition of 100 µM unmodified BiP without ATP may result in less than 50 % complex formation. Taking into account the decreased conformational flexibility of FicD on the observable timescales (Fig. 3E), we conclude that monomeric FicD binds to BiPAMP more strongly in the static, TPR-in conformation. From a subspecies FCS analysis, the diffusion coefficients determined for the low-FRET (static, TPR-in conformation) and high-FRET (dynamic) subpopulations are similar, indicating that also the small fraction of the dynamic population binds to BiPAMP (SI Appendix, Table S5).

Interestingly, the simultaneous addition of ATP with BiPAMP restored the dynamic behavior of FicDR118C (Fig. 3B, brown, Fig. 3F) with population distributions similar to that observed for ATP:FicD in the absence of BiP (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). The species FCS analysis indicated strong binding of FicD to BiPAMP in the dynamic conformation and an increased diffusion coefficient for the static, TPR-in conformation (SI Appendix, Table S5). The diffusion coefficient of the static, TPR-in conformation is still significantly lower than for FicD alone, suggesting that binding of FicD to BiP in this conformation is reduced but not abolished. In summary, the interaction of monomeric FicDR118C with AMPylated BiP leads to a loss of conformational dynamics, which can be restored by the addition of ATP. Furthermore, the ATP-enhanced dynamic flipping of the TPR domain does not interfere with the binding to BiP or BiPAMP, as both subpopulations of FicD are bound to BiP.

Conformational Dynamics Involve the 1st and 2nd TPR Motifs.

To further investigate the conformation of the TPR motifs, we extended the analysis of FicD’s conformational dynamics to alternative labeling positions within the TPR domain, each in combination with S288C as a consistent second labeling position on the Fic domain (Fig. 4A). Residue L104C, which is N-terminal to the first α-helix of the TPR domain, was introduced to investigate the position and dynamics of the 1st TPR motif at a different location (FicDL104C, Fig. 4A). Despite the similar AV-predicted distances (RDA) of 60 and 59 Å for apo- and ATP-bound states, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S3), we observe three states, a static, low FRET state (E = 0.31) and dynamic fluctuations between two distinct FRET populations at E = 0.35 and E = 0.79 (Fig. 4B, light blue, SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and Table S7). Similar to FicDR118C, the addition of ATP altered the population distribution in favor of the dynamic FRET states (Fig. 4C, yellow, SI Appendix, Table S8), whereas the addition of BiP shifted the static population to a lower FRET efficiency value (Fig. 4B, blue, SI Appendix, Table S7). The pronounced dominance of the low-FRET population in the presence of BiPAMP is conserved for FicDL104C as well (Fig. 4B, black, SI Appendix, Table S8). The fluorescence lifetime analysis revealed significant sub-ms dynamics for the high-FRET populations, although the amplitude is very low in the presence of BiPAMP. Analogous to FicDR118C, these were attributed to conformational switching between the dynamic TPR-in (65 Å) and TPR-out (41 to 47 Å) conformations (SI Appendix, Table S7 and Fig. S6). Overall, BiP and ATP induced similar effects on the distance population distribution of both 1st TPR motif mutants, which validates the proposed conformational equilibrium of FicD in solution.

Next, we investigated the conformational flexibility of the 2nd TPR domain. We fluorescently labeled the 2nd TPR motif at the loop residue K154C in addition to S288C on the Fic domain (FicDK154C, Fig. 4A). Here, only a single population at E = 0.53 was observed for FicDK154C in the apo-state (Fig. 4D, light blue, SI Appendix, Table S9). In the E-τD(A) plot, this population appears on the static FRET line (Fig. 4F), indicating a single static conformation. The fluorescence lifetime analysis reveals a dye distance of 61 Å for this static conformation (Fig. 4D, light blue, SI Appendix, Table S10), which corresponds well with the expected distance of 62 Å from the AV simulations (SI Appendix, Table S3). In the presence of ATP, however, a high-FRET population emerges in addition to the slightly shifted low-FRET population (Fig. 4E, yellow, SI Appendix, Table S9). The observation of a high-FRET state is consistent with the AV simulations for the ATP-bound enzyme, where the predicted distance between the dyes decreases to 54 Å (SI Appendix, Table S3). The high-FRET efficiency state is also observed for FicDK154C in the presence of BiP or BiPAMP together with ATP, (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Table S9). The E-τD(A) plots for FicDK154C in the presence of ATP indicate that the emerging high-FRET states are dynamic (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). The endpoints of the dynamic fluctuations were determined to be 41 Å and 61 Å (SI Appendix, Table S10) and the lifetime analysis revealed contributions from an additional static state at 71 Å with a low population (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Table S10). The static TPR-in state that was detected in the absence of ATP (R = 61 Å, SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 2 and Table S10) is similar to the dynamic, TPR-in state of the 1st TPR motif mutants. Therefore, FicDK154C showed three distinct conformational states as detected for FicDR118C or FicDL104C. However, the apparent nucleotide dependence of the dynamics of FicDK154C stands in stark contrast to the smFRET probes labeled on the 1st TPR motif and the correlation to the previously described conformational states is not as clear.

To further explore this difference, we labeled FicD at two additional TPR domain residues, each in combination with S288C: residue D138C located in the loop region between the 1st and 2nd TPR motif, and S170C at the loop between the TPR domain and the helical domain linker of FicD (FicDD138C and FicDS170C, Fig. 4A). Comparable to FicDK154C, smFRET measurements of these mutants revealed a single FRET population in the apo-state (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B, light blue histograms). In contrast to the previous FRET mutants, the AV simulations in both cases predicted the distance between the labeling dyes to increase from the apo- to the ATP-bound state (SI Appendix, Table S3). A decrease in FRET efficiency was indeed observed for the D138C construct. Here, a single, high-FRET population is detected in the apo-state and the corresponding E-τD(A) plot indicates only occasional, fast fluctuations away from this state (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). In the presence of ATP and BiP or BiPAMP, however, the high-FRET population peak broadens to lower FRET-efficiency values (SI Appendix, Figs. S8C and Fig. S9 D and F). The respective E-τD(A) plots indicate dynamic fluctuations between 49 Å and 71 to 79 Å with the high-FRET population of the apo-state representing one of the endpoints (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Table S11). Interestingly, the addition of ATP did not change the observed behavior unless BiP was present, in contrast to other labeling positions. For FicDS170C, the single distance population of the apo-state is consequently broadened in the presence of ATP, irrespective of the addition of BiP or BiPAMP (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B and D). The E-τD(A) plots for these conditions indicate an increased dynamic averaging of this population due to fluctuations between conformations with donor-acceptor dye separations of 49 Å and 68 to 72 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 and Table S12). These values differ from the expected distances given by the AV simulations, suggesting that the crystal structures do not fully describe the structure of the dynamic protein.

To verify that the conformational dynamics and deviations in structure are real and not artifacts of the FRET measurements, we inspected the anisotropy of the donor and acceptor fluorophores (representative results for FicDR118C are shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S11–S16). No indication of significant sticking of the dyes was observed. We also repeated the measurements using an alternative dye pair (ATTO488-Alexa Fluor 647, SI Appendix, Figs. S17 and S18). The alternative dye pair corroborated the measured results with the exception for FicDL104C. Here, we observed a broad distribution rather than two distinct FRET populations, which did not change much upon the addition of ATP (SI Appendix, Figs. S17A and S18A compared to Fig. 4 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). We attribute this deviation to potential dye influences from the differently charged structure of Alexa Fluor 647 but note that the results obtained for the FicDL104C mutant may not be fully conclusive.

A comparison between the experimentally measured (ATTO532–ATTO643) and AV simulated dye-to-dye distances for the five FRET mutants of FicD are summarized in a correlation plot for each conformational state (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Overall, the 2nd TPR motif labeling positions are less consistent with the AV calculations as those within the 1st TPR motif, especially FicDR118C. Furthermore, the TPR-in states (apo-state and BiP-bound) are more comparable to the respective crystal structures than the TPR-out state (FicD:ATP). Here, also FicDL104C shows a higher deviation, as no distinct conformations were predicted by the AVs. In summary, both TPR motifs of FicD undergo conformational dynamics. The low-FRET and high-FRET populations observed for constructs labeled on the 1st TPR motif (L104C, R118C) can be roughly approximated by the TPR-in and TPR-out conformations of FicD observed in crystallography (Fig. 1B). However, when taking all the measurements together, there are differences from what is expected from the crystal structures.

To get an impression of what the actual structures of the different FicD conformations may look like, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. As we detected emerging dynamics only in the presence of ATP for mutants on the 2nd TPR motif (K154C, D138C, S170C), we performed simulations using the crystal structure of ATP-bound FicD (PDB 6I7K (21); see SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 4 and Movie S1). We then filtered the trajectory from the MD simulations using the distances obtained from the photon distribution analyses (PDA, (33) see section below) for all mutants (SI Appendix, Tables S13–S17). We generated an ensemble of structures for each conformation that match all five measured distances for the given conformational state at the same time (SI Appendix, Fig. S20 A–C). The ensembles of structures for the dynamic TPR-out and TPR-in, static conformations resemble the corresponding crystal structures whereas the distances of the dynamic TPR-in state are fulfilled by a more diverse set of structures. These ensembles indicate that the same set of distance restraints can be fulfilled by multiple conformations of the TPR domain. Furthermore, the experimentally observed differences in dynamics of the 1st and 2nd TPR motif are qualitatively reflected in the 2D kernel density plots, which report on the MD-based predicted FRET distance distributions associated with each TPR motif. For the 1st TPR motif label pair (R118C and L104C), the simulated conformations exhibit multiple distance populations (SI Appendix, Fig. S20D). In contrast, all simulated conformations result in a single dominant distance population for the 2nd TPR motif (K154C and D138C) label pairs (SI Appendix, Fig. S20E). Thus, the majority of simulated conformations exhibit similar dye distances with respect to the 2nd TPR motif labeling positions, which correlates with the lower experimental occurrence of multiple distance populations for these constructs.

Quantification of the Conformational Dynamics.

ATP influences the dynamics of the TPR-in–TPR-out transitions.

As several FicD constructs exhibit conformational dynamics, we performed a dynamic photon distribution analysis (PDA) (33) to quantify the timescales of the TPR motions of FicD. By analyzing the smFRET histograms with different time binnings, PDA can extract the contribution of each state to the FRET distribution and the rate constants of the dynamic switching between different conformations. Based on the conformational states obtained from the fluorescence lifetime analysis, the dynamic TPR-in and TPR-out conformations for FicDR118C were fixed globally for all conditions with fluorophore distances of 65 and 43 Å, respectively (Fig. 5A, blue and orange, respectively). We define the transition rates between these two conformations as kout for the switch from TPR-in to TPR-out and kin for the reverse transition (Fig. 5A, yellow). A third, static TPR-in conformation, with a distance between the fluorophores of 71 Å in the PDA model, was used to describe the additional, static low-FRET population (Fig. 5A, green). To account for acceptor blinking or impurities, we included another static, low-FRET population at 92 Å (Fig. 5A, purple) into the model. We globally applied this two-state dynamic model with two-additional static states to all datasets to extract the transition rates of the TPR motion under each condition (Fig. 5 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S21 andTable S13). In the absence of ATP or BiP, the transition rates of kin= 1.08 ms−1 and kout = 0.88 ms−1 (SI Appendix, Table S13) indicate a faster transition from the TPR-out to the TPR-in conformation, which suggests a preference of the apo-state of FicDR118C to adopt the dynamic TPR-in conformation. In addition, a higher fraction of molecules is in the static TPR-in population under these conditions (SI Appendix, Table S1). Substantially faster rates were obtained for FicDR118C:ATP with kin = 1.78 ms−1 and kout = 2.57 ms−1. Here, the increase of kout over kin suggests a shift in equilibrium towards the dynamic TPR-out state, which correlates with the increase in the dynamically interconverting, high-FRET population observed in the E-τD(A) plots of FicDR118C:ATP (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Table S1). In the presence of BiP, the ratio between kout and kin decreases (Fig. 5D), highlighting a preference for the dynamic TPR-in conformation as well as an increase in the fraction of TPR-in static molecules. The lowest ratio of kout to kin was obtained for BiPAMP (Fig. 5D), where the dynamically interconverting population is depleted significantly (Fig. 3 A and E). Due to the correlation between the ratio of kout to kin and the fraction of dynamic molecules, we interpret the ratio of kout to kin as a measure for FicD’s tendency to adopt the dynamic TPR-out conformation. We also quantified the kinetics of FicDL104C using the same two-state dynamic PDA model with two static states globally applied to all measurement conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S22 and Table S14 and Supplementary Note 3). Similar trends for the transition rates are observed as for FicDR118C for the interaction with BiP (SI Appendix, Fig. S26 and Table S14): The presence of BiP and BiPAMP reduces the kout to kin ratio. Again, this effect is most evident for BiPAMP and can be reverted by the addition of ATP. The addition of ATP alone did not increase the ratio of kout to kin for FicDL104C.

The 2nd TPR motif becomes dynamic in the presence of ATP.

For FicDK154C, dynamics were observed only for the measurements in the presence of ATP. One dominant population is observed with a FRET efficiency of ca. 0.5. However, to describe the conformation of FicD for all conditions with a similar model, we performed a global fit to the conditions without ATP using a 3-state static PDA model. For the measurements in the presence of ATP, we included dynamic transitions between the TPR-out (41 Å) and TPR-in (57 Å) populations along with the static population at 74 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S23 and Table S15 and Supplementary Note 3). The results from the global PDA describe both the smFRET histograms and the fluorescence lifetime distributions well (Fig. 4 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Tables S9 and S10). Overall, the dynamic rates for switching between the TPR-in and TPR-out conformations are reduced compared to FicDR118C or FicDL104C in the presence of ATP, indicating slower fluctuations (SI Appendix, Table S15). However, also for FicDK154C, the addition of BiP reduced the ratio of kout to kin, highlighting the bias toward the TPR-in conformation (SI Appendix, Fig. S26) and the fraction of the static, TPR-in state increases (SI Appendix, Table S10).

We also applied PDA to the FicDD138C and FicDS170C constructs, revealing similar trends regarding the ratio of kout to kin in the presence of ATP and BiP or BiPAMP (SI Appendix, Figs. S24 and S25 and Tables S16 and S17 and Supplementary Note 3). Consistent across all labeling positions, the addition of BiP and ATP results in a lower ratio of kout to kin than the addition of BiPAMP and ATP (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). The lowest ratio was observed upon the addition of BiPAMP in the absence of ATP (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S26). This preference for the TPR-in conformation presumably is a result of enzyme–substrate complex formation, where FicD binds BiPAMP in the TPR-in conformation (24, 25). Except for FicDL104C, the addition of ATP either increases the absolute transition rates of FicD compared to the apo-state or triggers dynamics in the first place, suggesting overall faster dynamics of the enzyme in the presence of its cosubstrate. These results indicate that the conformational shifts between TPR-in and TPR-out states of FicD extend into the 2nd TPR motif. However, in contrast to the 1st TPR motif, the fluctuations first become prominent in the presence of ATP.

The impact of ATP binding on dynamics is further supported by additional MD simulations that were performed based on the different crystal structures of FicD (see SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 4 and Movies S1–S5). The apo-state [PDB 6I7J (21)], the ATP-bound state [PDB 6I7K (21)] and the BiP-bound structure of FicD [PDB 6ZMD (24)] (modeled both in the absence and presence of BiP) were all simulated for 4 µs (4 replicas of 1 µs each). The MD simulations of the apo and BiP-bound structures show rare or no large fluctuations/reorientations of the TPR domain, which is reflected in low backbone RMSD values compared to the reference structure (Fig. 5E). Here, the models explore the conformational space close to the starting structure, except for one replica of the apo-state simulation, in which the TPR domain packs onto the Fic domain and remains docked causing a higher RMSD. In contrast, large RMSD values, visited recursively multiple times during the MD simulation, were observed in the presence of ATP. They result from the dynamic sampling of the TPR domain over a diverse conformational space. The observation of this tendency was further corroborated by extending the simulation time from 4 µs to 17 µs (SI Appendix, Fig. S20F).

FicD’s Conformational Dynamics Are Conserved for the Dimeric Protein.

In contrast to the monomerized FicDL258D mutant, no rearrangements of the TPR domain were observed for the dimeric wildtype FicD in crystal structures of the apo- or ATP-bound states (20, 21). This observation raises the question of whether the dynamics for the monomeric FicDL258D are conserved for the dimeric protein. Since monomeric and dimeric FicD fulfill antagonizing catalytic functions (AMPylation versus deAMPylation, respectively) (21), a change in the enzyme’s dynamics is conceivable. Therefore, we extended our investigation to the dimeric enzyme. To ensure dimer formation in the smFRET experiments, we mixed a high excess of unlabeled wild type FicD (50 nM unlabeled vs ~ 100 pM labeled protein) with the fluorescently labeled proteins, which is well above the KD for dimerization (1 nM for FicD) (21). Dimer exchange between labeled and unlabeled FicD then ensures a maximum of one labeled subunit per detectable dimer. Dimer formation of FicDR118C with unlabeled FicD (resulting in the FicD:FicDR118C complex, referred to as dFicDR118C) was validated using FCS, where slower diffusion coefficients are detected under all measurements conditions when compared to monomeric protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Tables S4 and S5). The addition of unlabeled monomeric FicDL258D to the smFRET measurements of the labeled, monomeric FicDR118C did not significantly alter the smFRET histograms, as expected (SI Appendix, Fig. S27).

In the absence and presence of ATP, the FRET efficiency histograms and corresponding E-τD(A) plots obtained for dimeric FicD labeled at residues R288C and R118C (dFicDR118C) display a similar conformational landscape as obtained for the monomeric FicDR118C (Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Fig. S28). Consistent with the previous results, the apo-state of dFicDR118C reveals a static, low-FRET population, which is assigned to the static TPR-in conformation and a dynamic, high-FRET population, which is attributed to a flipping motion of the TPR domain between dynamic TPR-in and TPR-out conformations (SI Appendix, Table S18). The FRET distances are identical with the monomeric protein but the conformational equilibrium is shifted toward the dynamic, high-FRET population in the dimeric enzyme (compare SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S18). The addition of ATP further shifted the conformational equilibrium toward the dynamic, high-FRET population in all measurements (SI Appendix, Table S18 and S19). From the fluorescence lifetime analysis, we concluded that the dimeric FicDR118C shows similar dynamic interconversions between the TPR-in (R = 66 Å) and TPR-out (R = 46 Å) states (SI Appendix, Fig. S28 and Table S19) as observed for the monomeric enzyme.

Most importantly, the dynamic, high-FRET population of dFicDR118C is also observed in the presence of AMPylated BiP (Fig. 6 A and B). Although the static TPR-in conformation is favored for this condition (SI Appendix, Table S18), dynamic fluctuations were observed between the dynamic TPR-in (R = 69 Å) and TPR-out (R = 46 Å) conformations (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Table S19). This single condition stands in stark contrast to monomeric FicDR118C where the dynamic, high-FRET population is depleted in the presence of BiPAMP (Fig. 3 A and E). Due to the high excess of BiPAMP (100 µM) to enzyme (50 nM unlabeled dFicD) and the slow catalyzation rate of kcat (10–2 s–1) for deAMPylation of BiP by dFicD (25), no significant deAMPylation of the sample is expected during the 2 h measurement. Therefore, we can rule out that the dynamics are caused by increasing amounts of unmodified BiP during the measurement. This assumption is confirmed by the 2D mean macrotime versus FRET efficiency plots of dFicDR118C:BiPAMP, as the dynamic high-FRET population does not increase during the measurement time (SI Appendix, Fig. S29).

As deAMPylation active dFicD stays dynamic when bound to BiPAMP, the catalytic process may be coupled to the dynamics. To investigate this possibility, we measured a fluorescently labeled, catalytically inactive dFicDR118C H363A mutant in the presence of BiPAMP. Here, the dynamic behavior was almost completely depleted, similar to the results for monomeric FicDR118C (Figs. 3 A and E and 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S30), supporting the hypothesis that dynamic switching of the TPR domain of dFicDR118C may correlate with its deAMPylation activity. Upon addition of ATP to the BiPAMP:FicD complex, the monomeric FicDR118C, inactive dFicDR118C, and the active dFicDR118C all exhibit dynamics (Figs. 3F and 6 E and F and SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S19).

To see whether the dynamics of FicD correlate with its interactions with BiP, we analyzed the smFRET data using species FCS on the static and dynamic subpopulations. Similar diffusion coefficients were observed for the static TPR-in and the dynamic conformation with the exception of monomeric FicD in the presence of BiPAMP and ATP, and dimeric FicD in the presence of BiPAMP. We then analyzed FicD and dFicD interactions with BiPAMP in the absence and presence of ATP using a 2-component fit to the ACFs where the diffusion coefficients were fixed to that of enzyme only and the static population of the enzyme bound to BiPAMP. The fraction of bound enzyme for each subpopulation is given in SI Appendix, Table S20. For the dynamic subpopulations, the fraction of enzymes bound to BiPAMP does not substantially change upon the addition of ATP. In contrast, the fraction of bound molecules in the static, TPR-in state decreases for both FicD and dFicD upon the addition of ATP. However, there is still a significant fraction of bound molecules interacting with BiPAMP indicating a mixture of bound and unbound molecules in this conformation (SI Appendix, Table S20). These results rule out the interpretation that only the static, TPR-in conformation of FicD is bound to BiP and the dynamic conformation is only present for FicD alone. Instead, ATP likely has an allosteric effect on FicD bound to BiPAMP, allowing for dynamics irrespective of the binding status. Nevertheless, a substantially smaller fraction of the dynamic subpopulation of dFicD is bound to BiPAMP compared to monomeric FicD, which again may be linked to the difference in their deAMPylation activity.

Other differences between monomeric and dimeric FicD were identified in the PDA (SI Appendix, Fig. S31 and Tables S13 and S21). The absolute rates of the fluctuations between the dynamic TPR-in and TPR-out states are overall lower for dFicDR118C compared to monomeric FicDR118C, indicating slower conformational switching. However, the ratio of kout to kin is increased for the dimeric enzyme, in line with the higher preference of dFicDR118C to adopt the dynamic TPR-out conformation for all measured conditions (Fig. 5D). The effect of ATP, in general, is less pronounced for the dimeric enzyme. Furthermore, ATP does not increase but decrease the kout to kin ratio for dFicDR118C:BiP in contrast to monomeric FicDR118C (Fig. 5D). Here, the enhancing effect of ATP might be reduced by the formation of stabilized dFicDR118C:ATP:BiP ternary complexes, as dimeric FicD is AMPylation defective (21). We conclude that the dFicD deAMPylase undergoes dynamic conformational fluctuations in solution similar to those observed for the monomeric AMPylase, but that the distribution between these conformations differs in favor of the dynamic TPR-out conformation.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the conformational interdomain dynamics of the human FicD enzyme using smFRET. The resulting data revealed dynamic conformational rearrangements of FicD’s TPR domain in solution, leading to the definition of an equilibrium between three distinguishable conformational states: the static TPR-in conformation, which corresponds to the crystal structure of FicD bound to BiP [PDB 6ZMD (24)] and a dynamic, interconverting population fluctuating between a TPR-in and a TPR-out conformation (Fig. 7A). The annotation of conformational states in this study are based on AV calculations using crystal structures of FicD in the apo-state [TPR-in, PDB 6I7J (21)], of FicD bound to ATP [TPR-out, PDB 6I7K (21)], or of FicD complexed with BiP [TPR-in, PBD: 6ZMD (24)] (Fig. 2 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and Table S3) and the measured FRET efficiencies. Especially the experimental dye separations obtained for monomeric and dimeric FicDR118C are comparable to the distances predicted by AV simulations (SI Appendix, Tables S2, S3 and S19). All three states are consistently detected for monomeric and dimeric FicD (dFicD) labeled at residue R118C and confirmed by the labeling of additional positions (L104C, K154C) on the 1st and 2nd TPR motifs.

(A) The static TPR-in conformation (green) as well as the dynamically interconverting TPR-in (light blue) and TPR-out (yellow) states of FicD exist in equilibrium for monomeric and dimeric FicD. The full structure of dimeric FicD is shown in the inset (one protein in green, the other in wheat). While the presence of BiP promotes the static TPR-in state, binding to ATP favors the dynamically interconverting states and furthermore enhances the transition to the TPR-out state (kout). Furthermore, dimeric FicD showed larger fractions of dynamic molecules compared to monomeric FicD. No transitions between the dynamic and static TPR-in conformation have been observed in the smFRET burst experiments, indicating a timescale much larger than the burst duration (transition rates << 0.1 ms−1). The interconversions between the dynamic states (kout and kin) have been observed in a range of 0.75 to 2.84 ms−1 for FicDR118C. (B and C) The dynamics of FicD are linked to catalysis. While noncatalytic enzyme–target complexes (e.g. monomeric FicD or inactive FicDH363A interacting with BiPAMP) are mainly static and kin is predominant (B), catalytic complexes (e.g. during deAMPylation of BiPAMP by dimeric FicD) exist in all three conformational states of FicD with similar likelihood (C). Note that binding of ATP to FicD enhances dynamics irrespective of catalysis. Transparency of the TPR domain in B and C indicates the relative population of the conformational states (lighter colors are less populated). FicD is shown as colored cartoon representation [PDB 6I7J, 6I7K (21), BiP as a gray surface (PDB 6ZMD (24)].

In comparison to the crystal structures, only minor differences are observed for the ensembles of solution structures extracted from the MD simulations for the static TPR-in and dynamic TPR-out states. With respect to the TPR-out conformation, it is interesting to note that the TPR domain of each FicD molecule in the FicD:ATP crystal structure (21) is intimately intertwined with a neighboring one (SI Appendix, Fig. S32). Our in-solution smFRET data may provide an explanation of this observation: Fast conformational switching allows for the constant sampling of different TPR positions, thereby presumably creating the possibility for additional intermolecular interfaces that produce crystal contacts different from other FicD structures. This sampling is reflected in the ensemble of the dynamic TPR-in state, which covers a range of conformational states bridging between the TPR-in, static and TPR-out conformations.

The transitions between the TPR-in and TPR-out conformations are observed on the millisecond timescale indicating that the presence of the TPR-out conformation is transient in nature. ATP shifts the conformational equilibrium toward the dynamic state whereas BiPAMP binding promotes the static state. However, FicD is still dynamic when bound to BiP. Consistent for all the measured mutants, the fluorescence lifetime and photon distribution analyses (PDA) confirmed that the presence of ATP enhances the dynamic flipping motion of FicD (Figs. 5D and 7 and SI Appendix, Fig. S26). More specifically, the static TPR-in population is decreased and the increased ratio of kout over kin for the dynamic population results in a shift of the equilibrium in favor of the dynamic interconverting TPR-out population of FicD. In contrast, binding to BiP causes the opposite effect and reduces the contribution of the dynamic population in favor of the static, TPR-in population (Fig. 7B). This effect was the strongest for AMPylated BiP and correlates with a higher affinity of monomeric FicD to the modified target (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Most interestingly, although binding to BiP reduced the dynamic flipping of FicD, the dynamic subpopulations of FicD in the presence of ATP bind BiP to a similar or higher extent as the static populations (SI Appendix, Tables S5 and S20). We postulate that this observation is caused by an allosteric rather than a competitive effect of ATP on FicD that allows for dynamics irrespective of BiP-binding and catalysis. We further compared the conformational dynamics upon interaction with BiP between the monomeric FicDL258D mutant and the dimeric protein. Here, we observed diverging trends between monomeric and dimeric FicD in the presence of BiP and BiPAMP: While ATP enhances the dynamic flipping motion (kout/kin ratio) in all other cases, it is reduced exclusively for dimeric and AMPylation deficient FicD in the presence of BiP and ATP (Fig. 5D). Even more pronounced is the depletion of the conformational dynamics of deAMPylation deficient enzymes (i.e. the monomeric L258D or inactivated H363A mutants) in the presence of BiPAMP. While the dimeric and deAMPylation active enzyme retains its dynamics, the deAMPylation inactive mutants persist in the static, TPR-in conformation (Figs. 3E, 6 B and C, and 7B). This observation suggests that the flipping motion of the TPR domain may be linked to the catalytic activity of FicD. Hypothetically, the dynamic switching of the TPR domain correlates with the AMPylation or deAMPylation activities. Since the TPR domain is required for binding to BiP (24, 25), the conformational rearrangement would contribute to dissociation of the postcatalytic complex and thereby enhance catalytic turnover (Fig. 7C). Consistent with this hypothesis, the artificial fixing of the TPR domain to the connecting helix of the Fic domain reduces the deAMPylation activity of FicD (25). This model can be extended by the observation that ATP binding recovers the dynamics of monomeric FicD in the presence of BiPAMP (Fig. 3 E and F). Binding of ATP has been proposed to allosterically weaken the affinity between FicD and BiP (21). Similarly, our FCS analysis showed a decrease in the FicDR118C:BiPAMP complex formation upon addition of ATP for both monomeric and dimeric FicD (SI Appendix, Table S20). This effect may be explained by the increased flipping motion of FicD’s TPR domain upon nucleotide binding (Figs. 5D and 7C), which might stimulate complex dissociation irrespective of catalysis. ATP might thus prevent the accumulation of high-affinity enzyme–substrate complexes and contribute to efficient activity cycling of FicD (21).

In summary, using smFRET, we could show that FicD undergoes conformational fluctuations in solution and while bound to BiP. These conformational fluctuations persist in the active dimer, even when bound to AMPylated BiP, although it was not observed for noncatalytic monomeric FicDL258D nor the inactive dimer FicDH363A. This suggests that not only is FicD dynamic, but that the dynamics are essential for regulating FicD’s function. These measurements also lay the groundwork for future studies that investigate the allosteric signaling between the nucleotide binding site and the TPR domain.

Materials and Methods

For complete Materials and Methods, see the SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods. All chemicals used in this study and their suppliers are listed in SI Appendix, Table S23.

Fluorescent Labeling of Proteins.

Purified FicD proteins were reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT) prior to labeling. After buffer exchange using repeated cycles of centrifugation in centrifugal filter units, FicD was labeled semisequentially with the maleimide-coupled acceptor and donor dye (ATTO-TEC and Invitrogen). Excess unbound dye was removed by centrifugation as above. In addition to SDS-PAGE, labeling was controlled by intact LC-MS.

Accessible Volume Calculations.

The accessible volume calculations for the FicD FRET constructs were done with the FRET-restrained positioning and screening (FPS) software (29). For FicD:apo (PDB 6I7J), FicD:ATP (PDB 6I7K), and FicD:BiP (PDB 6ZMD), the donor fluorophore ATTO532 is placed on R118, L104, K154, D138, and S170 residues in combination with the acceptor fluorophore ATTO643 on the S288 residue. For the labeling dyes ATTO532 and ATTO643, the three radii AV model was used with a Förster radius of 59 Å, dye linker-length of 21 Å and linker width of 4.5 Å. The dye parameters used for AV simulations were R532(1): 5.5 Å, R532(2): 4.5 Å, R532(3): 1.5 Å for ATTO532 and R643(1): 7.15 Å, R643(2): 4.5 Å, R643(3): 1.5 Å for ATTO643.

smFRET Measurements and Data Analysis.

Fluorescently labeled FicD monomers labeled with ATTO532 and ATTO643 were diluted to a final concentration of 100 pM in the smFRET assay buffer containing 25 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4), 100 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2. The sample was measured in an 8-well Nunc Lab-Tek slide (Thermo-Fischer Scientific), which was passivated with 1 mg/mL Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA, New England Biolabs) prior to the measurement. For the experiments containing BiP, the labeled FicD mutants were incubated and measured in the presence of 100 µM unlabeled BiP concentration. The ATP concentration was kept at 5 mM for all the measurements containing the nucleotide. The dimeric FicD experiments were conducted by preincubating 1 nM of double labeled and 50 nM unlabeled FicD dimers at 37 °C for the formation of the dimeric protein containing a double labeled monomer. A custom-built confocal microscope equipped with MFD and PIE (34) was used to perform the smFRET experiments as previously described (26). The labeled FRET samples were excited with 532 nm and 640 nm laser lines with 70 and 25 µW of powers measured on the objective, respectively. All the analyses of the collected data were done with the open-source PIE analysis with MATLAB (PAM) software (35). Detailed description of the fluorescence lifetime analysis and the dynamic photon distribution analysis (PDA) (33) are provided in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Notes 2 and 3.
