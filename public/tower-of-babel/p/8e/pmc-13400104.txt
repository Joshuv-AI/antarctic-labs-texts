Heterogeneity of Treatment Effect of DOACs vs Warfarin in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Samer Al Said, Andrea Bellavia, Eugene Braunwald, Elaine M Hylek, Michael G Palazzolo, Hwanhee Hong, Elliott M Antman, Anthony P Carnicelli, John W Eikelboom, Christopher B Granger, Manesh R Patel, Lars Wallentin, Christian T Ruff, Robert P Giugliano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDirect oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are recommended over vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation (AF). However, limited accessibility in resource-constrained settings makes it important to identify patient subgroups in whom VKAs may achieve comparable net clinical outcomes (NCOs).ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to identify AF patient subgroups and explore potential predictors of differential treatment benefit to inform anticoagulant selection where DOAC access is limited.MethodsIndividual patient data from the COMBINE-AF data set pooling 4 pivotal randomized trials of DOACs vs VKAs in AF were analyzed. Model-based clustering identified patient subgroups. Heterogeneity of treatment effects for the NCO—a composite of all-cause death, disabling or fatal stroke, and intracranial or fatal bleeding— was explored using Cox regression and gradient boosting analyses adapted for time-to-event data.ResultsA total of 58,634 patients were included (29,272 warfarin; 29,362 standard dose DOACs). Two subgroups were identified: a high-risk cluster (mean creatinine clearance 55.1 mL/min, age 75.6 years, body mass index [BMI] 26.6 kg/m2) and a low-risk cluster (mean creatinine clearance 101 mL/min, age 64.2 years, BMI 32.3 kg/m2). DOACs significantly reduced NCOs in the high-risk cluster (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81-0.92; P < 0.001), but not in the low-risk cluster (HR: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.85-1.03; P = 0.154, p-interaction = 0.115). Primary predictors of treatment heterogeneity were renal function, age, and BMI; no statistically significant treatment-by-subgroup interactions were identified.ConclusionsRenal function, age, and BMI are key determinants of anticoagulant treatment benefit in AF. In regions with limited access to DOACs, these findings suggest that some lower-risk patient profiles may derive similar outcomes with VKAs, although differences were not statistically significant.Key Words: atrial fibrillation, direct oral anticoagulants, heterogeneity of treatment effect, machine learning, risk stratification, warfarin

Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), such as warfarin, were the cornerstone of stroke prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) for several decades. Despite their proven efficacy in preventing thromboembolism,1 VKAs have significant limitations, including a narrow therapeutic window, frequent drug-drug interactions, and increased bleeding risks.2, 3, 4, 5 Therefore, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) were developed to address these limitations and have demonstrated at least comparable efficacy in preventing ischemic stroke compared to VKAs, with reduced risks of intracranial bleeding and modestly lower overall mortality.6,7 VKAs remain the preferred choice for patients with mechanical heart valves or moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis, in whom DOACs are contraindicated.2,3,8,9 While DOACs are recommended for most patients with AF,2,3 warfarin is still widely used globally, particularly in resource-limited settings in which DOACs may be less accessible due to cost or availability.10, 11, 12 In regions with restricted access to DOACs, it is important to identify patient subgroups in whom warfarin may achieve similar net clinical outcomes (NCOs) to DOACs for more targeted and efficient use of anticoagulants.

Understanding which patients might derive the most benefit from DOACs and who could safely continue warfarin is critical for improving care in resource-limited settings. We aimed to identify patient profiles in whom warfarin may achieve NCOs comparable to standard-dose (SD) DOACs, thereby informing anticoagulant treatment selection in settings where DOAC access is limited. To this end, we used individual-patient data from pivotal randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing DOACs and VKAs, leveraging classical and modern machine learning approaches for multivariable heterogeneity of treatment effects (HTE) assessment.13,14 Our objectives include identifying distinct patient subgroups through cluster analyses, assessing HTE within these subgroups, and evaluating individual treatment benefits based on clinical variables. Of relevance for translating results into potential clinical guidance, we explored whether HTE of DOACs vs warfarin was mostly driven by a true differential effect of DOACs or by population heterogeneity. Additionally, results were implemented into a simplified risk model and an interactive online tool to help health care providers select patients who would gain the most benefit from DOACs, thus enhancing patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and data source

We used individual participant data from a collaboration between multiple institutions to better investigate non -vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant use in atrial fibrillation (COMBINE AF),7,15 a data set that included 4 pivotal phase 3 international RCTs comparing the efficacy and safety of DOACs vs warfarin for stroke prevention in patients with AF: RE-LY (Randomized Evaluation of Long-Term Anticoagulation Therapy; dabigatran),16 ROCKET AF (Rivaroxaban Once Daily Oral Direct Factor Xa Inhibition Compared with Vitamin K Antagonism for Prevention of Stroke and Embolism Trial in Atrial Fibrillation),17 ARISTOTLE (Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation),18 and ENGAGE AF–TIMI 48 (Effective Anticoagulation With Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation–Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48; edoxaban).19 Each trial was approved by the governing Institutional Review Board or ethics committee at each site, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Because of data privacy restrictions, data from COMBINE AF are unable to be shared outside of the members of the COMBINE AF Executive Committee and their institutions. The primary analysis was restricted to SD DOAC treatment, which was defined as any of the following: 150 mg of dabigatran twice daily; 20 mg daily of rivaroxaban (or 15 mg if meeting dose-reduction criteria) daily, 5 mg of apixaban (or 2.5 mg if meeting dose-reduction criteria) twice daily, or 60 mg daily of edoxaban (or 30 mg if meeting dose-reduction criteria) daily. Because lower-dose DOACs (110 mg of dabigatran twice daily or 30/15 mg daily of edoxaban daily) are not approved globally for clinical use in patients with AF, patients randomized to these doses were excluded from this analysis. Warfarin was the sole VKA used across all 4 trials in the COMBINE-AF data set. For detailed results in the warfarin arm overall and in subgroups, please see our companion paper in this issue [reference forthcoming].

Clinical outcome

The outcome of interest for this analysis was a NCO defined as a composite of all-cause death, disabling or fatal stroke, intracranial or fatal bleeding. This endpoint was a prespecified NCO in ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 and was the chosen endpoint of interest in the analysis since it captures events in AF most specific for highest severity and irreversibility. In cases of fatal events of prespecified endpoints (eg, intracranial hemorrhage), the event was attributed to the initial event (eg, intracranial hemorrhage) rather than death. Each patient contributed a maximum of 1 NCO event, with the earliest qualifying event used for time-to-event analysis.

Baseline characteristics for patients’ stratification

Assessing HTE based on multiple characteristics (multivariable HTE) requires the a priori identification of specific baseline characteristics that can contribute to effect heterogeneity.13 Based on clinical relevance and availability in the clinical trial record of participants treated in the cohort, the following baseline characteristics were considered: age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), creatinine clearance (CrCl), baseline AF, prior ischemic stroke, prior myocardial infarction, diabetes mellitus, and prior VKA experience. Race was classified as Asian vs non-Asian regardless country, which included patients from East, Northeast, Southeast, and South Asia, as well as patients of Asian race from other continents.20

Statistical analysis

To provide a robust assessment of HTE and to identify specific population phenotypes for whom warfarin might work as well as DOACs, several approaches for multivariable HTE assessment were employed including unsupervised techniques based on model-based clustering for phenotype-based stratification and supervised techniques based on gradient boosting for effect-based stratification.13 To improve HTE results interpretation and application as a treatment decision rule, cross-validated results from the different approaches were used to develop a personalized treatment benefit calculator.

Patients phenotyping

First, model-based clustering was used to identify distinct phenotypes among patients.21,22 By grouping patients based on their clinical and demographic characteristics, the comparative effectiveness of DOACs and warfarin within these phenotypic subgroups was assessed. This approach allowed for a nuanced analysis focusing on group-level outcomes rather than individual characteristics. Two tools to characterize the derived clusters and improve their interpretation in terms of patients’ phenotyping were used. First, the discriminative power of each variable, which indicates those characteristics that mostly contribute to defining heterogeneity in the study population, was calculated. Second, the baseline characteristics of the identified subgroups were presented. The two-cluster solution was selected based on Akaike information criteria and Bayesian information criteria criteria and yielded the most clinically interpretable patient groupings. Solutions with 3 or more clusters produced overlapping profiles without meaningful additional clinical differentiation (Supplemental Figure 2).

Multivariable HTE with phenotype-based and effect-based modeling

To explore multivariable HTE, both phenotype-based and effect-based modeling approaches were employed.13 First, we evaluated treatment effects with Cox regression models across the phenotypes identified through the clustering approach previously described as well as presented Kaplan-Meier estimates of cumulative incidence function of NCO across clusters. Next, generalized random forests were used to assess effect-based stratification of the treatment effect.14 This latter nonparametric stratification approach enabled an assessment of treatment effects across population subgroups and the identification of potential interactions between treatment and clinical variables in predicting the NCOs.

Interactions through gradient boosting and Cox models

To further investigate whether the treatment effect heterogeneity is due to treatment-by-clinical variable interactions or population heterogeneity, gradient boosting techniques were applied.23 Gradient boosting models adapted for time-to-event data were used, implemented using a Cox proportional hazards loss function (gbm R package),24 which directly models the hazard function and appropriately accounts for censoring through the partial likelihood. Gradient boosting was used as an exploratory tool to identify variables potentially associated with variation in treatment effect. This approach does not provide formal statistical tests for interaction terms; variables identified through this method were subsequently evaluated in Cox regression models including explicit interaction terms. All machine learning approaches implemented (model-based clustering, generalized random forests, and gradient boosting for interaction assessment) were cross-validated in a training set (70%) and evaluated in a validation set (30%). Model tuning was performed using internal cross-validation within the training data set. An independent validation set (30% of patients) was used to evaluate out-of-sample model performance. Sensitivity analyses using bootstrap resampling yielded consistent results (Supplemental Methods 5).

Prediction model for DOAC relative benefit

A flexible prediction model was utilized to comprehensively assess the treatment benefits of DOACs vs warfarin for individual patients based on the evaluated characteristics. This model incorporated identified interactions and was complemented by an interactive online tool to aid clinicians in identifying patients who may derive the most benefit from DOAC therapy compared to warfarin (https://timi.org/timi-calculators/).

When required, statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Cross-validation techniques were used to enhance the reliability of the machine learning models and mitigate overfitting. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and baseline characteristics, while treatment effects were quantified using effect-size measures for comparative analysis across subgroups. Missing data were minimal across all analysis variables (<0.3% for any variable).15 Model-based clustering, gradient boosting, and random forest analyses accommodate missing values natively; Cox regression analyses were conducted on complete cases, with the negligible missingness rates having no material impact on results. All Cox proportional hazards models were stratified by trial to account for potential differences in baseline hazard, consistent with the standard analytic approach used in prior COMBINE-AF publications.

Results

Patient characteristics and clustering

A total of 58,634 patients with AF were included in the analysis, with 29,272 patients receiving warfarin and 29,362 patients treated with SD DOACs. Cluster analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in population phenotypes, with the main drivers for this heterogeneity being renal function estimated by CrCl, age, and BMI (Figure 1). Two distinct patient groups based on CrCl, age, and BMI were identified. High-risk cluster comprised older patients with lower BMI and lower CrCl, while low-risk cluster included younger patients with higher BMI and higher CrCl. The baseline characteristics for these clusters are summarized in Table 1. The cumulative incidence of NCO differed significantly between risk clusters (Figure 2). Patients in the high-risk cluster demonstrated consistently higher cumulative incidence function for NCO over the follow-up period compared to those in the low-risk cluster. By 2 years, the cumulative incidence reached approximately 15% in the high-risk group vs 12% in the low-risk group. The separation between the curves was evident early and widened progressively over time, indicating a sustained divergence in event rates between the 2 groups. A log-rank test comparing the 2 curves showed a P value <0.001. The identified clusters demonstrated good discriminative performance and a low probability of misclassification (Supplemental Figure 1). Heterogeneity of Treatment Effect of Direct Oral Anticoagulants vs Warfarin in Atrial Fibrillation

Model-based clustering of 58,634 patients from COMBINE-AF identified two phenotypic clusters—high-risk (older age, lower BMI, reduced CrCl) and low-risk (younger age, higher BMI, preserved CrCl)—with clinically meaningfully different absolute treatment benefits (ARR 2.4% vs 0.5%, respectively). DOACs significantly reduced net clinical outcome in the high-risk cluster (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81-0.92; P < 0.001) but not in the low-risk cluster (HR: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.85-1.03; P = 0.154; P interaction = 0.115), with renal function as the dominant predictor of treatment benefit variation (52.8% relative importance). These findings support risk-guided anticoagulant selection, particularly in resource-limited settings where DOAC access is restricted. ARR = absolute risk reduction; CrCl = creatinine clearance; NCO = net clinical outcome (all-cause death, disabling/fatal stroke, intracranial/fatal bleeding); other abbreviations as in Figure 1.

Heterogeneity of treatment effect on net clinical outcome

The comparative analysis of SD DOACs vs warfarin revealed heterogeneity in treatment effects on NCO across the identified clusters. In the high-risk cluster, DOACs significantly reduced NCO events compared to warfarin (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81-0.92; P < 0.001) (Figure 3A). In the low-risk cluster, the benefit of DOACs was smaller and nonsignificant (HR: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.85-1.03; P = 0.154, P interaction = 0.115) (Figure 3B). Effect-based heterogeneity assessment, based on a random forest–trained model, confirmed CrCl, age, and BMI as the most relevant predictors of heterogeneity in treatment effects.

Interaction assessment

Phenotype-based and effect-based stratification suggest the presence of treatment effect heterogeneity with respect to the advantages of DOACs vs warfarin. Gradient boosting analyses were used to further investigate whether such heterogeneity is driven by treatment-interactions or population heterogeneity. The gradient boosting analysis confirmed that the main independent predictors of the NCO were renal function, BMI, and age. The ranking of these predictors remained consistent even when restricting the analysis to either warfarin or SD DOACs. The relative importance of each predictor is summarized in Supplemental Table 1. Importantly, no significant treatment interactions were observed (Table 2), indicating that the heterogeneity in treatment effects is driven by the weight of specific risk factors rather than differences in the effectiveness of DOACs or warfarin among different patients. Further analysis using a Cox model confirmed these findings (Table 2), showing a larger coefficient for CrCl compared to treatment (DOACs), suggesting renal function has a greater impact on outcomes. The overall effect of DOACs was significant (HR: 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85-0.96); P < 0.001). Other significant predictors included age (HR: 1.17 (95% CI: 1.11-1.23); P < 0.001), BMI (HR: 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90-0.99); P = 0.0174), and baseline CrCl (HR: 0.67 (95% CI: 0.63-0.73); P < 0.001). These findings underscore the importance of individual characteristics—especially renal function, age, and BMI—in predicting treatment outcomes, highlighting that the treatment response is relatively uniform across various patient subgroups, and that the observed differences in treatment effect is more likely due to population heterogeneity.

Risk predictions

We developed an interactive risk prediction model to assess individual patient outcomes based on the identified covariates, including renal function, age, and BMI, which is implemented as an interactive tool at www.timi.org/timi-calculators. The model utilizes data from both treatment groups and allows for dynamic input of the patient characteristics, yielding individualized predictions for the likelihood of experiencing NCO. For example, the estimated absolute risk reduction (ARR) with DOACs as compared to warfarin is 2.4% (95% CI: 1.7%-3.1%) for an individual with age 80, CrCl 30 mL/min, and BMI 20 kg/m2, but only 0.5% (95% CI: 0%-1.2%) for an individual with age 60, CrCl 70 mL/min, and BMI 30 kg/m2.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated HTE of DOACs vs warfarin by leveraging several modern methods for HTE. Potential heterogeneity in the treatment effects of DOACs vs warfarin in patients with AF was observed, including a low-risk group in which warfarin might provide similar benefit as compared to DOACs. Key baseline predictors associated with variation in absolute treatment benefit included renal function, age, and BMI. These findings underline the importance of personalized anticoagulant therapy in patients with AF, particularly in resource-limited settings in which access to DOACs may be restricted.

One of our main goals was to identify patients’ phenotypes for assessing HTE over multiple baseline characteristics, thus improving the understanding of treatment effects at the individual level. Through applications of cluster analysis techniques, clearly distinct low-risk and high-risk groups were identified. In the high-risk cluster, characterized by older age, lower BMI, and impaired renal function, SDs of DOACs were associated with a significant reduction in NCO compared to warfarin, suggesting that these patients derive a greater benefit from DOAC therapy. In contrast, the low-risk cluster, composed of younger patients with better renal function and higher BMI, exhibited only a nominal, nonsignificant benefit from DOACs, indicating that warfarin may remain a reasonable alternative for these patients. This heterogeneity, as well as its key drivers, were confirmed by applying alternative approaches for multivariable HTE. This differential treatment effect emphasizes the need for clinicians to tailor anticoagulant therapy based on individual risk factors.

DOACs are generally considered superior to VKAs in terms of NCO, primarily due to their more favorable safety profile.6,16,17,19,25 However, results from randomized clinical trials provide evidence of the treatment’s benefits in the overall studied population, while subgroup analyses in these trials often offer limited insights into how treatment effects vary across different patient groups.13 Previous studies demonstrated consistent relative benefits of SD DOACs over warfarin for stroke prevention across the entire age spectrum, with no evidence of interaction by sex.7 However, the benefits of DOAC over VKAs in preventing stroke tended to be greater in patients with lower CrCl.26 Regarding major bleeding, younger patients and those with lower body weight may derive greater benefits from SD DOACs compared to warfarin.7,27 Traditional subgroup analyses, which evaluate single covariates, often fail to capture the complexity of patient interactions and may lead to false positives or negatives due to multiple testing and limited power.13 Moreover, interpreting individual characteristics in isolation can be misleading, as treatment effects are more dependent on combinations of factors.13 In contrast, our multivariable approach considers interactions among multiple clinical variables, identifying patient subgroups with distinct risk-benefit profiles. This methodology not only corroborates prior findings but also offers a more personalized understanding of treatment effects, overcoming the limitations of conventional analyses.28

Our findings are consistent with those from individual DOAC trials and prior COMBINE-AF analyses, which demonstrated generally consistent relative treatment benefits across prespecified subgroups, with some signals of attenuated benefit in lower-risk patients—particularly those with better renal function, younger age, and higher body weight.7,26,27 The present analysis extends these observations by demonstrating that the combined effect of these patient characteristics, identified through an unsupervised clustering approach, can define discrete patient phenotypes with meaningfully different absolute treatment benefits. In the low-risk cluster—characterized by younger age, higher BMI, and preserved renal function—the ARR with DOACs over warfarin was substantially smaller (0.5% vs 2.4% in the high-risk cluster), providing quantitative support for the clinical plausibility of warfarin as a reasonable alternative in this population when DOACs are unavailable or inaccessible.

The absence of significant treatment interactions, as confirmed by the gradient boosting and Cox models, suggests that the observed heterogeneity is driven primarily by patient characteristics—renal function, age, and BMI—rather than inherent differences in the efficacy of DOACs vs warfarin across various patient subgroups. This finding aligns with prior research highlighting the influence of renal function and age on anticoagulant effectiveness and underscores the need to carefully assess these factors when selecting therapy.7,26 Taken together, we did not identify statistically significant treatment-by-subgroup interactions, indicating that the relative efficacy and safety of DOACs compared with warfarin are broadly consistent across patient subgroups. However, substantial differences in baseline risk resulted in meaningful variation in the absolute treatment benefit. Thus, our findings are best interpreted as risk stratification informing treatment benefit, rather than definitive evidence of heterogeneity of treatment effect. This distinction is clinically important, as treatment decisions are often driven by ARR rather than relative effects alone.

Finally, we developed a simplified risk prediction model, coupled with an interactive online tool, to provide clinicians with a dynamic method for estimating individual patient outcomes based on key clinical variables. This tool integrates baseline characteristics such as renal function, age, and BMI to predict NCO, offering a more comprehensive view of the overall benefit-risk balance of treatment. By using NCO as an endpoint, the model helps guide treatment decisions, enabling health care providers, particularly in low-resource settings, to identify patients who would benefit most from DOACs and those for whom warfarin remains a viable option. This approach facilitates personalized optimization of anticoagulation therapy, ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

Clinical implications

The study’s findings have important implications for clinical practice. Although DOACs remain the preferred anticoagulant strategy for most patients with AF, access and cost barriers limit their use in many regions worldwide. In such settings, identifying patient subgroups that may achieve similar NCOs with warfarin can help optimize resource utilization while ensuring that higher-risk patients are prioritized for treatment with DOACs. While our trial-based findings suggest a smaller net benefit of DOACs over warfarin in lower-risk patients, warfarin’s risks in clinical practice—including higher intracranial hemorrhage rates, more drug-food interactions, and the burden of international normalized ratio monitoring—must be considered when applying these results clinically. Our findings identify a population in whom the net clinical benefit of DOACs is small, not one in whom warfarin is equivalent or preferred.

Study Limitations

Despite the robust analysis using individual patient-level data from the COMBINE-AF data set, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the study included a large and diverse patient population, it is based on data from 4 RCTs, which may not fully represent clinical practice settings, particularly in regions with limited health care access. The treatment regimens in these trials were standardized, and the findings may not fully capture variations in practice patterns, adherence, or patient characteristics outside of the trial settings.

Second, while our study identified key predictors of heterogeneity, such as renal function, age, and BMI, other factors—such as comorbid conditions, genetic differences, or detailed clinical data—were not incorporated into the model. These factors may influence treatment outcomes and should be explored in future studies. Third, the observational nature of the clustering analysis may limit the ability to draw definitive causal conclusions about the effect of these predictors on treatment outcomes. Although statistical models suggest associations, the potential for residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Fourth, warfarin time-in-therapeutic range and DOAC adherence/discontinuation were not included, as these are post-randomization variables that would introduce bias into treatment comparisons. While important for the assessment of clinical effectiveness, our analysis focused on baseline characteristics relevant to treatment selection. A registry-based analysis incorporating treatment patterns in clinical practice represents a valuable avenue for future investigation. Lastly, while the developed prediction model and online tool offer a practical approach for selecting patients who would benefit from DOACs, further validation in external, independent cohorts is necessary to confirm the generalizability and accuracy of these findings, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Conclusions

Although DOACs remain the preferred anticoagulant therapy for most patients with AF, access and cost barriers continue to limit their availability in many regions worldwide. Our findings suggest that a personalized approach to anticoagulation, guided by patient-specific factors such as renal function, age, and BMI, may help identify subgroups in whom warfarin achieves comparable NCOs. These insights are particularly relevant in resource-limited settings, where thoughtful selection of anticoagulant therapy may optimize clinical outcomes and promote more equitable use of health care resources.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with atrial fibrillation, renal function, age, and body mass index identify phenotypic subgroups with differing magnitudes of net clinical benefit from direct oral anticoagulants versus warfarin. In lower-risk phenotypes, differences between treatments were smaller and not statistically significant.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Prospective validation is needed to determine whether phenotype-based treatment selection can guide anticoagulant choice in routine practice, particularly in settings where access to direct oral anticoagulants is limited and where warfarin management quality may vary.
