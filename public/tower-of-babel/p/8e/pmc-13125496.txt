Conducting polymer-stabilized nanozymes alleviate sepsis-induced myocardial injury by inhibiting iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation
Authors: Tingting Wu, Ying Liu, Wei Wang, Rong Sun, Yanyan Wang, Jiangpeng Pan, Jingfei Zhu, Kelong Fan, Qiuran Xu, Wei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Septic cardiomyopathy (SC) is a severe complication of sepsis that significantly increases patient mortality. The onset of SC is associated with excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to increased oxidative stress and iron accumulation. Herein, we develop a library of conductive polypyrrole-polythiophene copolymer (PPy-co-PTh)-stabilized nanozymes by coordinating different metal ions within the copolymer framework. From this library, we identify a PPy-co-PTh stabilized ruthenium-based nanozyme (Ruzyme), which exhibits significant catalase- and superoxide dismutase-like activity, as well as nitrogen radical scavenging capability. Modifying the conductive interface significantly improves the catalytic activity and stability of the Ruzyme by lowering the catalytic energy barrier, reducing excessive oxidation caused by ROS, and facilitating the continuous decomposition of ROS. To achieve precise targeted delivery to myocardial mitochondria, (5-carboxypentyl) triphenyl phosphonium bromide (TPP-COOH) and a cardiac-targeting peptide (CTP) are conjugated onto the surface of the Ruzyme. In vitro, the stabilized nanozymes effectively scavenge ROS, reduce iron accumulation, and inhibit iron-dependent cell death, thereby lowering lipid peroxidation. In male SC mice, they improve cardiac function, demonstrating therapeutic benefits. Given the growing interest in nanozymes for cardiovascular diseases, this study provides a potential foundation for the development of therapeutic agents for severe cardiomyopathy and related conditions.

Introduction

Sepsis is a syndrome characterized by a systemic inflammatory response and organ dysfunction resulting from the body’s uncontrolled reaction to infection1. This dysregulated response can lead to damage to and failure of the heart, kidneys, lungs, and other organs2,3. Sepsis is one of the leading causes of death worldwide4. According to statistics, the number of people diagnosed with sepsis in 2017 was close to 49 million, and the number of related deaths was approximately 11 million, accounting for about 20% of the deaths in that year5. Septic cardiomyopathy (SC) is a common complication of sepsis2 and presents as an acute but reversible heart disease induced by sepsis6,7. SC is reversible in the early stages, affecting up to 40% of sepsis patients8,9, with reported incidence rates ranging from 13.8% to 40%7. The main pathological features are left and/or right ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction10,11, reduced left ventricular output12, and decreased ejection fraction (EF)1,8. Without timely intervention, the patient’s condition progressively worsens, leading to septic shock, which causes systemic organ dysfunction and failure. This can result in mortality rates as high as 70%–90%13. Currently, early clinical diagnosis of SC primarily involves assessing hemodynamics14, electrocardiogram, echocardiography8, and monitoring afterload-related cardiac function (ACP)11. Certain serum biomarkers, such as procalcitonin (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and N-terminal forebrain natriuretic peptide (NT-pro-BNP)11,15, are also detected to aid in diagnosis. Furthermore, clinical treatment primarily involves supportive therapies, such as fluid resuscitation and antibiotics, in the early stages of the disease16; however, targeted interventions are still lacking3. Early diagnosis of SC, along with a thorough understanding of its pathogenesis and the development of targeted treatments, is essential for improving patient outcomes and achieving a favorable prognosis.

The pathophysiological mechanism of SC is complex and has not been fully elucidated6. The contributing factors include systemic cytokine storms (such as IL-1β and TNF-α)17, imbalance of nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species (ROS)18, endotoxin release8,19, mitochondrial structural damage and dysfunction20, high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) dysregulation21, ferroptosis22, calcium overload in cardiomyocytes23, endoplasmic reticulum stress3, and pyroptosis24. Mitochondria play a central role, as they are the primary site of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. In the adult heart, the ATP needed for cardiac contraction is produced mainly through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, with oxidative phosphorylation accounting for 95% of ATP generation25. Damage to mitochondria disrupts oxidative phosphorylation, resulting in excessive free radical production and subsequent cardiomyocyte apoptosis, as demonstrated in numerous animal studies6,26. Several clinical studies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction can directly contribute to the negative outcomes of sepsis20. Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death (RCD) that depends on iron and ROS22. It differs from other forms of cell death, such as necrosis, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy27. Intracellular iron overload and an abnormal redox system are the main features of ferroptosis28. Fe2+ accumulates in cells, promotes the production of ROS, and exacerbates oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thereby leading to cell damage29. Iron chelators or lipid peroxidation inhibitors can prevent the occurrence of ferroptosis30. Iron-mediated cell death has been implicated in the development of various cardiomyopathies, including SC, cardiac hypertrophy, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and Adriamycin-induced cardiotoxicity22,27,31. For example, Shen et al. were the first to demonstrate the therapeutic potential of melanin nanoparticles as cardioprotective agents, showing that they alleviate sepsis-induced myocardial injury by scavenging ROS and preventing iron accumulation, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis32. Wang et al. confirmed that microRNA-130b-3p (miR-130b-3p) effectively reduces sepsis-induced myocardial injury by inhibiting ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes10. Thus, previous studies have confirmed that targeting mitochondria and inhibiting ferroptosis may be effective approaches for alleviating SC.

Oxidative stress is the cornerstone of sepsis pathogenesis, mainly caused by ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS). ROS typically include superoxide anion (O2•−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radical (•OH). RNS mainly include nitric oxide (NO) in free radical form and peroxynitrite (ONOO−) in nonfree radical form33,34. Antioxidant therapy is a pharmacological treatment in which antioxidants help reduce inflammation by scavenging free radicals in the inflammatory microenvironment35. The body contains various antioxidants, including small molecules such as reduced glutathione, vitamin A, vitamin E, and trace elements, as well as enzymatic antioxidants such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)36. Under normal physiological conditions, small molecule antioxidants and enzymatic antioxidants work to scavenge reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), maintaining redox homeostasis. However, during sepsis, the production of RONS exceeds the scavenging capacity of these antioxidants, disrupting redox balance. This leads to oxidative stress, which in turn produces even more RONS, creating a vicious cycle37. It is therefore necessary to introduce exogenous antioxidants to scavenge excess RONS to mitigate oxidative damage in tissues and cells.

Nanozymes are nanomaterials with inherent enzymatic properties38. Compared with natural enzymes, they not only mimic the activity of natural enzymes but also offer advantages such as lower production costs, greater stability, more diverse functions, and more easily regulated catalytic activity39. Antioxidant nanozymes may exhibit activities similar to those of SOD, CAT, or GPx33. Among these, SOD-like nanozymes can convert O2•− into H2O2 with relatively low reactivity, CAT-like nanozymes can catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into O2 and H2O, and GPx-like nanozymes can decompose H2O2 into H2O33,36. By clearing ROS in the inflammatory microenvironment, antioxidant nanozymes can help alleviate oxidative stress. Currently, various nanozymes have been extensively studied in the context of ROS-related diseases, such as the Fe-Curcumin@Tannin acid (Fe-Cur@TA) nanozyme for targeted myocardial infarction therapy40, the Prussian blue nanozyme for alleviating Parkinson’s disease41, the Pt-NC single-atom antioxidant nanozyme for alleviating myocardial ischemia‒reperfusion injury42, and the multifunctional selenium nanozyme for the treatment of acute lung injury43. However, some nanozymes present several limitations, including overoxidation, low catalytic specificity, and inadequate stability in complex physiological environments44. Interfacial modification can effectively enable the precise regulation of the catalytic activity of nanozymes45. Therefore, a suitably modified interfacial nanozyme has the potential to represent a clinically applicable category of antioxidants. These nanozymes can mimic the structure and function of natural enzymes, effectively scavenging RONS while restoring redox balance.

In this study, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), pyrrole, and thiophene were used as the frameworks to coordinate various metal ions, resulting in a conductive polypyrrole-polythiophene copolymer (PPy-co-PTh) stabilized nanozyme library containing eleven polymers. Following a series of enzymatic activity screenings and characterizations, the PPy-co-PTh stabilized ruthenium-based nanozyme (Ruzyme) was selected for its improved CAT-like, SOD-like, and nitrogen radical scavenging abilities. To enable precise mitochondrial targeting, (5-carboxypentyl) triphenyl phosphonium bromide (TPP-COOH) was modified onto the surface of the Ruzyme, creating the ICT-Ruzyme. Subsequently, ICT-Ruzyme was further modified with a cardiac-targeting peptide (CTP) to achieve cascade targeting to the myocardial mitochondria, resulting in CICT-Ruzyme (Fig. 1A). A series of studies revealed that, compared with the nonpolymeric nanozyme (NCT-Ruzyme), the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme can better prevent itself from overoxidation in the environment of excessive H2O2 and has better conductivity and antioxidant properties. Moreover, electrostatic forces between conductive polymer nanoparticles can inhibit the aggregation of nanozymes (including Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme), thereby enhancing their solubility and dispersion in aqueous solutions. This property allows nanozymes targeted at the mitochondria and myocardium to disperse uniformly within the heart, forming an efficient conductive pathway. Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes

Using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), pyrrole, and thiophene as the basic framework, various metal ions were coordinated to synthesize 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes (Fig. S1, Supplementary Information). A series of structural characterizations were subsequently performed on the 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that the nanozymes exhibited good dispersion (Fig. S2, Supplementary Information). UV‒vis spectroscopy revealed a distinct absorption peak at 378 nm for all 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes (Fig. S3, Supplementary Information). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to characterize the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes. The results showed that their sizes were predominantly in the range of 50–400 nm, with particle size distribution indices (PDI) all below 0.7, indicating good dispersion of the nanozyme solutions (Figs. S4, S5 and S6, Supplementary Information). All the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes showed positive potentials (Fig. S7, Supplementary Information). Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. S8, the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes containing Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Mn, Ce, Ru, Rh, Dy, and Pt were found to have an amorphous structure. However, the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Au-based nanozyme exhibited a distinct crystalline structure (Fig. S8, Supplementary Information). Overall, a library of 11 types of polypyrrole nanobiocatalysts with different metal ion ligands was successfully constructed.

Characterization and screening of the enzyme-like activities of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes

The scavenging of RONS is a key property of nanozymes that enables their antioxidant mechanisms. The enzyme-like activities of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes were systematically evaluated to identify one with superior antioxidant properties. Peroxidase (POD) activity catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 into toxic •OH, while oxidase (OXD) activity triggers the conversion of O2 into H2O2 or O2•− (Fig. 2A). The POD-like and OXD-like activities of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes were evaluated by the 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) probe assay. Almost none of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes produced •OH or H2O2 under the neutral pH conditions typically required for antioxidant effects (Fig. 2B, C). Additionally, given the sensitivity of the TMB chromogenic substrate to pH variations, we further validated the POD-like and OXD-like activities of 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes at pH 7.4 using o-phenylenediamine (OPD) and 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) as alternative substrates. The results demonstrated that, regardless of the substrate (OPD or ABTS), all 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes exhibited minimal, even negligible, POD-like and OXD-like activities (Figs. Nanozymes with CAT-like activity catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into O2 and H2O (Fig. 2D). Since H2O2 has a characteristic absorption peak at 240 nm, the CAT-like activity of the nanozymes can be assessed by measuring the decrease in the absorbance of H2O2 at this wavelength during the reaction with the nanozymes. Among the 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes, Ruzyme demonstrated the most significant reduction in H2O2 content over time, followed by Ptzyme (Fig. 2E). Moreover, O2 generation during the reaction was monitored to assess the CAT-like activity of nanozymes. Compared with the other PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes, Ruzyme significantly increased the O2 concentration in the system and produced more bubbles (Fig. S11, Supplementary Information). These findings indicate that Ruzyme has a strong ability to catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into H2O and O2. Although Ptzyme exhibited CAT-like activity, its catalytic performance was inferior to that of Ruzyme (Fig. 2E, F; Fig. S11, Supplementary Information).

SOD is an important cellular antioxidant enzyme that can convert O2•− to H2O246 (Fig. 2D). The SOD-like activity of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes was assessed using the cytochrome C assay. In this reaction, O2•− interacts with oxidized cytochrome C, generating reduced cytochrome C, which has a specific absorption peak at 550 nm. In the presence of SOD, a portion of O2•− is consumed, reducing the absorption of reduced cytochrome C at 550 nm. Thus, the SOD-like activity of the nanozymes was indirectly evaluated by measuring the inhibition rate. When the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes were compared, Ruzyme effectively scavenged O2•− even at lower concentrations, with an IC50 of 0.009368 mg/mL (Fig. 2G; Fig. S12, Supplementary Information).

Both ROS and RNS have the potential to damage DNA in tissue cells, which can subsequently trigger the development of inflammation47. Therefore, the ability of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes to scavenge nitrogen-free radicals was examined next, using 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical cation(ABTS•+) and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazine radical (DPPH•) as substrates (Fig. 2D). The results showed that Ruzyme was relatively more effective at clearing RNS in a concentration-dependent manner, with its ability to scavenge RNS nearly matching that of the positive control, vitamin C (Vit C) (Fig. 2H, I).

Based on a series of enzyme-like activity characterizations of all the PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes in the library, Ruzyme was selected for its greater ability to scavenge both ROS and RNS (Fig. 2J, K). Therefore, we chose PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme for subsequent experimental studies.

Synthesis and characterization of Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme

Ruzyme was identified from 11 types of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes through screening of enzyme-like activities for further research. ICT-Ruzyme and CICT-Ruzyme were subsequently synthesized by sequentially modifying the surface of Ruzyme with TPP-COOH and CTP, as outlined in Fig. 1A. Moreover, the nonpolymer nanozyme synthesized using BSA as a ligand (NCT-Ruzyme) and the Ru-nanozyme with the polyvinylpyrrolidone framework (PVP@Ru) were selected as controls (Fig. S13, Supplementary Information). The EDS mapping diagram displayed the distributions of Ru, C, N, O, and S elements within the Ruzyme, confirming the presence of Ru signals in the polymer and providing direct evidence of the successful synthesis of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme (Figs. S14 and S15B, Supplementary Information). The distribution of each element in the Ruzyme, along with the specific Ru content, was subsequently analyzed via inductively coupled plasma‒mass spectrometry (ICP‒MS), revealing a Ru content of about 16.42% (Fig. S15A, Supplementary Information). Additionally, the contents of phosphorus (P) in Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme were detected by ICP‒MS, respectively. The content of P in ICT-Ruzyme was approximately 0.0057%, but it was not detected in Ruzyme. The addition of P further indicated the successful ligation of TPP-COOH on the surface of the Ruzyme, and the amount of grafted TPP-COOH accounted for approximately 0.0844% of the total nanozyme. We subsequently measured the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the two nanozymes, ICT-Ruzyme and CICT-Ruzyme. After CTP modification, distinct stretching vibrations were observed at 1644 and 1343 cm−1 in the spectrum of the CICT-Ruzyme, corresponding to the C=O bonds of CTP, confirming the successful conjugation of CTP (Fig. S16, Supplementary Information). The Ru contents of the Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme nanozymes detected by ICP–MS were 16.42, 9.83, and 8.69%, respectively (Fig. S17, Supplementary Information). EDS elemental mapping also revealed the distributions of Ru in the ICT-Ruzyme and CICT-Ruzyme, respectively (Fig. S18, Supplementary Information). The TEM micrographs demonstrate that the nanoparticles exhibit a relatively uniform distribution, as illustrated in Fig. 3A and Fig. S19A of the Supplementary Information. Additionally, the amorphous nature of the nanozyme complex is consistent with the findings from the high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) image, which are presented in Fig. S19B in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S19B, Supplementary Information). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements demonstrated that the thickness of the Ruzyme was approximately 6–15 nm, the NCT-Ruzyme had a thickness of about 23–35 nm, and the ICT-Ruzyme exhibited a thickness of around 20 nm (Fig. 3B, C; Fig. S20, Supplementary Information). The hydrated particle sizes of the NCT-Ruzyme, Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme ranged from 50 to 100 nm (Fig. 3D). The smaller PDI values of Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme indicated better dispersion of their aqueous solutions than that of the NCT-Ruzyme (Fig. 3E). NCT-Ruzyme exhibited a negative zeta potential of −34.08 mV, whereas Ruzyme had a zeta potential of 5.272 mV. The presence of lipophilic cations (TPP-COOH) and myocardial targeting peptide (CTP) in the ICT-Ruzyme and CICT-Ruzyme resulted in increased zeta potentials for both (Fig. 3F). Figure 3G displays the stability of each nanozyme stored in different biological media (DI water, saline, PBS, FBS, and DMEM) on days 1 and 7. After 1 mg/mL nanozyme aqueous solutions were prepared, the NCT-Ruzyme rapidly precipitated in several biological media, whereas the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes maintained better dispersibility over 7 days with negligible changes in particle size (Fig. 3G; Fig. S21, Table S1, Supplementary Information). These results further indicate that electrostatic forces between conductive polymer nanoparticles can inhibit the aggregation of nanozymes and that the modification of TPP-COOH and CTP effectively increases the dispersibility of Ruzyme. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis further demonstrated that both Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme exhibit amorphous structures, a finding that aligns with the results presented in Fig. S19B (Fig. 3H; Fig. S19B, Supplementary Information). Both polypyrrole and polythiophene are commonly used conductive polymers. Among them, polypyrrole offers advantages such as easy preparation, high conductivity, stability, and solubility. To assess the electrical conductivity of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted, with the nonpolymer NCT-Ruzyme used as a control. The size of the semicircle diameter in the high-frequency region of the EIS data directly reflects the electrical impedance of the material, providing insight into the electron transfer dynamics on the electrode surface. A smaller semicircle diameter indicates lower electron transfer resistance and better conductivity, whereas a larger diameter suggests higher impedance and poorer conductivity. As shown in Fig. 3I, compared with the NCT-Ruzyme, the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme exhibited a smaller semicircle diameter in the high-frequency region (Fig. 3I). This indicates that the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme, with polypyrrole and polythiophene as the framework, has a lower charge transfer resistance. This suggests that the nanozyme facilitates faster charge transfer during the catalytic reaction, resulting in better electrical conductivity. In conclusion, the incorporation of polypyrrole and polythiophene effectively enhanced the electrochemical properties of the nanozyme, linking the electrochemical behavior to the catalytic activity. H2O2 decomposition was effectively catalyzed by Ruzyme in the buffer solution, leading to bubble formation, followed by ICT-Ruzyme and CICT-Ruzyme (Fig. S22, Supplementary Information). This difference in activity was likely attributed to the presence of TPP-COOH and CTP, which may have partially shielded the active site. The results show that the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes have significant CAT-like activity. In addition, the ability of the nanozymes to decompose H2O2 was evaluated using classical UV‒Vis absorption spectroscopy with the H2O2 indicator Ti(SO4)2. The H2O2 concentration in the system decreased significantly over time when it was incubated with PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes, accompanied by a noticeable color change. NCT-Ruzyme showed almost no ability to decompose H2O2 (Fig. 4B; Fig. S23A–D, Supplementary Information). Among them, CICT-Ruzyme was able to deplete H2O2 in a time- and dose-dependent manner, gradually changing the color of the system from yellow to colorless (Fig. 4B, C; Fig. S23D, Supplementary Information). To further examine the catalytic kinetics of the nanozymes, the time-dependent decomposition of H2O2 was monitored for each nanozyme. The results revealed that Ruzyme and the single-targeted ICT-Ruzyme were able to decompose nearly 80% of H2O2 within the first 10 min, reaching a plateau by 20 min. In comparison, the dual-targeted CICT-Ruzyme depleted almost 40% of H2O2 within the first 10 min and reached a plateau at 60 min. The control group, however, showed little to no change in H2O2 concentration throughout the incubation period (Fig. 4D). Consistent results were obtained based on the color change observed in each system during the detection process (Fig. S23E, Supplementary Information). The catalytic properties of Ruzyme were further evaluated using PVP@Ru, RuO2@BSA, and several other previously reported nanozymes as controls. As illustrated in Fig. S24A, Ruzyme exhibited the ability to eliminate nearly 70% of H2O2 within the first 5 min, accompanied by a significant color change. This was followed by Ru@MOF, PVP@Ru, and Ru@PAM. In contrast, RuO2@BSA did not scavenge H2O2 (Fig. S24A, J; Supplementary Information). We analyzed the decomposition of H2O2 at the same concentration and different incubation times. As anticipated, Ruzyme demonstrated superior catalytic ability for the decomposition of H2O2, resulting in a more pronounced color change in the system (Fig. S24B–G, K, Supplementary Information). Subsequently, we assessed the sustained catalytic properties and kinetics of this series of nanozymes. After four cycles, Ruzyme maintained strong catalytic activity, whereas the performance of the other nanozymes progressively decreased. NCT-Ruzyme and RuO2@BSA displayed negligible sustained catalytic activity (Fig. S24H, Supplementary Information). Additionally, we investigated the kinetics of their CAT-like activities and summarized the Michaelis‒Menten constant (KM) and maximum initial velocity (Vmax) for several previously reported nanozymes. With respect to H2O2 scavenging kinetics, Ru@MOF and PVP@Ru demonstrated slightly higher H2O2 affinities (1/KM) than Ruzyme; however, Ruzyme achieved a greater catalytic rate (Vmax) compared with the other 11 nanozymes (Fig. 4E; Fig. S24I; Table S2, Supplementary Information). Additionally, the calculated turnover number (TON) value of Ruzyme for CAT-like activity is 6.053 s−1, which is significantly greater than that of Ru@CoSe (2.004 s−1) (Table S2, Supplementary Information). Overall, Ruzyme exhibited a relatively high catalytic rate and favorable affinity for H2O2, demonstrating optimal CAT-like kinetic properties for H2O2 scavenging. Furthermore, both the single-targeted ICT-Ruzyme and the dual-targeted CICT-Ruzyme effectively decomposed H2O2 repetitively (Fig. S25, Supplementary Information).

To further investigate the role of Ruzyme in scavenging H2O2 and its catalytic mechanism, in situ Fourier transform infrared (in situ FTIR) spectra was performed. As depicted in Fig. 4F, H, the signal intensity at 3214 cm−1 gradually decreases with an increasing applied voltage in the presence of H2O2, which is attributed to the continuous consumption of H2O2 in the reaction system. Simultaneously, the peak intensities at 1600 cm−1 and 1100 cm−1 significantly increase as the voltage rises, corresponding to the formation of the key intermediate *OOH during the catalytic decomposition of H2O2. Meanwhile, the gradually strengthening peak at 1166 cm−1 is ascribed to the surface-adsorbed *O2 intermediate. The direct monitoring of the *OOH and *O2 intermediates strongly confirms that Ruzyme can effectively catalyze the decomposition of H2O2, with its catalytic driving force enhancing synergistically with higher voltages (Fig. 4F, H). Furthermore, a comparison of Fig. 4G, I reveals that, under a constant voltage and in the presence of H2O2, the evolution trend of these key species over prolonged reaction times is highly consistent with the aforementioned voltage-responsive behavior. This further corroborates that Ruzyme possesses remarkable CAT-like activity, enabling the highly efficient catalytic decomposition of H2O2 into H2O and O2. Crucially, this catalytic conversion process exhibits pronounced voltage dependence and a clear time-cumulative effect (Fig. 4G, I). These results not only demonstrated the excellent CAT-like activity of the Ruzyme but also highlighted its strong catalytic stability, providing a solid foundation for potential biotherapeutic applications.

Investigating the mechanism of PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme to prevent excessive oxidation and enhance catalytic stability

To investigate the specific mechanisms regulating the activity of the conductive PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme, the valence changes of both the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme and NCT-Ruzyme were analyzed before and after the catalytic reaction via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). XPS analysis of the NCT-Ruzyme and PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme before and after the reaction with H2O2 demonstrated that the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme exhibited greater valence stability under identical catalytic conditions (Fig. 5A, B; Fig. S26, Supplementary Information). In contrast, the NCT-Ruzyme shows larger shifts, suggesting greater susceptibility to changes in the valence state. The conductive nature of PPy-co-PTh likely stabilizes the electronic structure of the Ruzyme, preventing excessive oxidation or reduction. This stability ensures consistent catalytic activity over multiple cycles, which is crucial for applications such as RONS removal in sepsis-induced cardiac injury. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms underlying the SOD- and CAT-like activities of the NCT-Ruzyme and PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme. The dynamic equilibrium structures of the NCT-Ruzyme and PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme, derived from ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations, were used as the initial models. These structures, which involve nonpolymer nanozymes and PPy-co-PTh molecules adsorbed on their surfaces, provide the foundation for investigating the catalytic mechanisms (Fig. 5C). Figures 5D–G, S27A, B and S28A, B illustrate the proposed mechanisms underlying the SOD- and CAT-like activities of the NCT-Ruzyme and PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme. In the SOD pathway, the rate-determining step for the NCT-Ruzyme is *H2O-OOH → *OH + H2O2, with an energy barrier of 1.465 eV. For the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme, where an electron-conductive polymer is grafted onto the surface, this energy barrier decreases to 0.702 eV. The rate-determining step then shifts to the final O2 desorption step, with an energy barrier of 1.032 eV (Fig. 5D, E; Figs. S27A and S28A Supplementary Information). The catalytic performance significantly improved after grafting with PPy-co-PTh. In the CAT pathway, the rate-determining step for the NCT-Ruzyme is the final oxygen desorption, which has an energy barrier of 1.907 eV. For the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme, where an electron-conductive polymer is grafted onto the surface, this energy barrier decreases to 1.154 eV (Fig. 5F, G; Figs. S27B and S28B, Supplementary Information). This reduction in the energy barrier is attributed to the PPy-co-PTh modification, which weakens O2 adsorption and thereby promotes a more efficient catalytic effect. Iron is a transition metal that readily alternates between Fe2+ and Fe3+ states under certain conditions. This property gives it a strong pro-oxidative effect, which plays a significant role in its biological activity29. Studies have shown that excess iron in the body can lead to the accumulation of highly reactive nontransferrin-bound iron (NTBI) in cardiac tissues. This NTBI forms intracellular iron pools, which promote the generation and accumulation of RONS, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation. Over time, these processes contribute to cellular damage48,49. Testing the iron-chelating ability of nanozymes is therefore well justified. The compound 1,10-phenanthroline reacts with Fe2+ to form an orange‒red complex that exhibits a characteristic absorption peak at 510 nm. The absorbance of this complex at 510 nm correlates linearly with Fe2+ concentrations ranging from 0–100 μM, accompanied by a noticeable color change (Fig. S31A, B, Supplementary Information). This property makes 1,10-phenanthroline an effective and sensitive indicator for detecting free Fe²⁺ in the system. The results demonstrated that the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes could chelate free Fe2+ in a concentration-dependent manner, achieving a chelation efficiency of up to 95% at 500 mg/L. The nonpolymer NCT-Ruzyme, however, exhibited a much lower chelation efficiency of only 30% (Fig. 6H; Fig. S31C–F, Supplementary Information). This conclusion was more clearly illustrated in the scanning graph of the system at the same concentration, which demonstrated the strong iron chelation ability of the nanozymes (Fig. 6I).

Fe2+ can undergo the Fenton-like reaction with H2O2 to generate highly toxic •OH50. Therefore, we further investigated whether the iron-enriched nanozyme (PPy-co-PTh stabilized Fezyme, referred to as Fezyme) itself could initiate the Fenton-like reaction under neutral conditions where antioxidant activity is typically needed. The results showed that in a pH 7.4 buffer system, regardless of whether TMB or ABTS was used as the substrate, Fezyme did not exhibit Fenton-like reaction activity; that is, it failed to effectively catalyze the production of •OH (Fig. S32, Supplementary Information). This finding indicates that although the nanozyme contains abundant iron ions, it does not initiate a typical Fenton-like reaction at neutral pH, where antioxidant activity is typically needed.

After confirming that Ruzyme could effectively chelate Fe2+, we evaluated the potential leaching risk of metal ions in the relevant biological environment (including the presence of Fe2+). To evaluate ion leaching, the Ruzyme was incubated for 72 h in a simulated biological environment (PBS) under both Fe²⁺-supplemented and iron-free conditions. We employed a dialysis method to separate the leached metal ions and used ICP‒MS for highly sensitive quantitative detection of Ru ions in the dialysate. The results showed that the relative leaching amount of Ru ions was very low (<0.0002%) in both groups. Although the leaching rate in the iron-containing group showed a slight increasing trend compared to the iron-free group (P = 0.0503), the absolute values remained negligible (Fig. S33, Supplementary Information). This suggests that the risk of Ru ions leaching from the material is negligible, demonstrating its good stability in biological environments. Moreover, this finding also indicates from another perspective that the ruthenium sites remain highly stable.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated H9C2 cells were used to establish an in vitro model of SC to evaluate the potential application of PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes for cardiomyocyte protection (Fig. 7B). The DCFH-DA probe was used to stain cardiomyocytes treated with nanozymes and/or LPS, and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to observe the intracellular ROS levels. As shown in Fig. 7C, compared with that in the untreated group, the green fluorescence signal was significantly enhanced following LPS treatment, indicating that LPS stimulated H9C2 cells to produce and accumulate ROS. However, after treatment with Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme, the green fluorescence intensity was markedly reduced, suggesting that the nanozymes effectively scavenged intracellular ROS. However, the NCT-Ruzyme showed no significant effect on the ROS levels (Fig. 7C, Fig. S35, Supplementary Information). Consistent results were obtained when O2•− was used as an inducer to examine the role of nanozymes in scavenging ROS (Fig. S36, Supplementary Information).

Previous results have demonstrated that PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme exhibits good biocompatibility and effective ROS scavenging ability. TPP, a commonly used lipophilic cation, can target mitochondria by enhancing the electrostatic interaction between the delivery vector and the mitochondria, enabling efficient drug accumulation within the mitochondria51. Mitochondria are crucial organelles involved in energy production within the cell52. Therefore, to further clarify the targeting mechanism of the as-prepared nanozyme, we first determined its suborgan localization in H9C2 cells. However, owing to the low electron density of the polymeric nanozyme, we were unable to directly observe the accumulation of ICT-Ruzyme in the mitochondria or lysosomes at the ultrastructural level (Fig. S37, Supplementary Information). Therefore, we further verified the ability of ICT-Ruzyme to target mitochondria at the cellular level via fluorescence confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 7D, the nuclei labeled with the dye DAPI displayed blue fluorescence, whereas the nanozymes labeled with the dye Cy5.5 emitted red fluorescence. Mitochondrial-targeting fluorescent probes, which can penetrate cell membranes, have been used for specific fluorescence staining of mitochondria in cells53. After 1 h of coincubation with the nanozymes, a small amount began to enter the cells. As the coincubation time increased, the red fluorescence signal gradually intensified, indicating progressive cellular uptake across all systems. However, different nanozyme systems at the same concentration exhibited distinct uptake behaviors. Specifically, throughout the incubation period, the Cy5.5 fluorescence signal of the ICT-Ruzyme group was significantly stronger than that of the Ruzyme group (Fig. 7D). This finding indicates that the incorporation of TPP-COOH enables the nanozyme to target mitochondria, thereby increasing the cellular uptake. To further confirm that TPP-COOH modification enhances the cellular uptake of ICT-Ruzyme by H9C2 cells, we compared the ruthenium (Ru) content in cells treated with either Ruzyme or ICT-Ruzyme at equivalent Ru contents. The ICP–MS results showed that the Ru content in the ICT-Ruzyme group was approximately 1.2 times higher than that in the Ruzyme group (Fig. 7E). Moreover, TEM revealed that all the nanozymes maintained mitochondrial structural integrity, demonstrating their biosafety, which is consistent with the cytotoxicity results from the CCK-8 assay (Fig. S38, Supplementary Information). Mitochondria are the primary organelles responsible for generating O2•− and inducing cellular oxidative damage, whereas lysosomes serve as the main organelles for phagocytosing exogenous materials54. Therefore, we next investigated the behavior of Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme entry into lysosomes. After H9C2 cells were incubated with the nanozymes for 24 h, the lysosomes were stained with LysoTracker. The results, as shown in Fig. S39, indicate that both Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme accumulated in the lysosomal compartments, with Pearson’s correlation coefficients of 0.69 and 0.65, respectively (Fig. S39, Supplementary Information). In summary, ICT-Ruzyme significantly accumulated in both lysosomes and mitochondria.

Mitochondria are the main site of intracellular ROS production55. Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) were detected by staining with the mitochondrial superoxide indicator MitoSOX to assess oxidative damage. After LPS-induced oxidative damage, a strong red fluorescence signal was observed in the mitochondria, which decreased following treatment with Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme (Fig. 7F). Quantitative analysis further revealed that both Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme treatments significantly reduced mtROS levels compared to the model group, whereas the NCT-Ruzyme had no significant effect (Fig. 7G). These results indicate that ICT-Ruzyme effectively targets the mitochondria of cardiomyocytes, scavenges ROS in the mitochondrial region, and prevents the generation of intracellular free radicals.

To subsequently verify the ability of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes to alleviate LPS-induced cardiac injury, this study first evaluated their cardiac targeting efficacy via in vivo imaging. For this purpose, Cy5.5-labeled Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme nanoparticles were prepared. Studies have shown that the CTP with the amino acid sequence CSTSMLKAC exhibits significant selectivity toward the pathological myocardium56. Therefore, this experiment was performed using an LPS-induced SC mouse model. Specifically, SC mice were intravenously injected with equal doses of three types of Cy5.5-labeled nanozyme dispersions (Cy5.5-Ruzyme, Cy5.5-ICT-Ruzyme, and Cy5.5-CICT-Ruzyme). The average fluorescence intensity of each nanozyme in the organs at different time points (1 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h) was detected via an in vivo imaging system. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that the accumulation of nanoparticles in myocardial tissue increased over time. Moreover, in the SC mouse model, compared with that in the non-targeted group, the group treated with CTP-modified nanozyme exhibited the highest myocardial fluorescence intensity at all time points, indicating that CTP modification significantly enhanced the targeted accumulation of nanozymes in the pathological myocardium (Fig. 8F and Fig. S41, Supplementary Information). To further validate the targeting efficacy of CTP-modified nanozyme toward pathological myocardium, LPS-induced SC mice were intravenously administered three types of nanozymes with equivalent Ru contents. Heart tissues were collected 12 h post-injection to compare the Ru content. The ICP‒MS results demonstrated that the Ru level in the cardiac tissue of the CTP-modified nanozyme (CICT-Ruzyme) group was approximately 2.3 times greater than that in the non-targeted group (ICT-Ruzyme) (Fig. 8G). This result further confirms the ability of CTP to target pathological myocardium and facilitates subsequent in vivo animal experiments. Moreover, the accumulation of the three nanozymes in the liver, spleen, lung, and kidney was monitored over time to reflect their metabolic profiles. The results indicated that all three nanozymes were distributed to varying degrees in each organ, with the most significant accumulation observed in the liver and kidney, which is consistent with conventional drug metabolic pathways (Fig. S42, Supplementary Information).

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes

Studies have reported that once nanoparticles enter the bloodstream, proteins and other biomolecules in the blood may interact with them, forming a so-called “biomolecular corona” on their surface, which alters the intrinsic bioactivity and therapeutic efficacy of the drugs57,58. Therefore, prior to conducting in vivo experiments, the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes were incubated with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C for 0, 3, 6, and 18 h, respectively, to evaluate the effect of the formed biomolecular corona on the activity of the nanozyme. The dissolved oxygen content produced by the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 by nanozymes after incubation with 10% FBS for varying durations was measured via a dissolved oxygen meter. The results indicated that as the incubation time with 10% FBS increased, the biomolecular corona on the surface of the nanoparticles gradually formed, leading to a corresponding decrease in their CAT-like activity to varying degrees (Fig. S43A–C, Supplementary Information). This may be attributed to the partial obscuration of the nanozymes’ active sites by the formed biomolecular corona. Subsequently, their capacity to scavenge RNS was evaluated using ABTS•+ and DPPH• as substrates. The results demonstrated that when ABTS•+ was used as the detection substrate, the biomolecular corona formed on each nanozyme after varying incubation periods with 10% FBS had a negligible effect on their ability to scavenge ABTS•+ radicals. The fact that the color of the reaction system did not change also accurately confirmed this conclusion (Fig. S44A–E, Supplementary Information). Meanwhile, when DPPH• was used as the detection substrate, the RNS scavenging capacity of all the nanozymes slightly decreased with prolonged incubation time, accompanied by varying degrees of color change. Among them, CICT-Ruzyme exhibited the most significant reduction. Nevertheless, even after 18 h of coincubation, CICT-Ruzyme retained 60% of its RNS scavenging activity (Fig. S45A–E, Supplementary Information). We further evaluated and compared the cellular uptake of ICT-Ruzyme by H9C2 cells, with and without prior incubation with 10% FBS. As the coincubation time of the nanozymes with the cells increased, the red fluorescence signal within the cells tended to increase. However, the cellular uptake of the nanozyme with a “biomolecular corona” was slightly weaker than that of the nanozyme without incubation (Fig. S46, Supplementary Information). This finding indicates, from another perspective, that the “biomolecular corona” formed on the surface of the nanozyme does indeed affect the efficiency of its cellular uptake. Therefore, PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes with a surface biomolecular corona still exhibit considerable ROS and RNS scavenging capacities. Meanwhile, ICT-Ruzyme can still be effectively internalized by H9C2 cells after incubation with 10% FBS, demonstrating its efficient cellular uptake. These results lay a foundation for subsequent validation of the therapeutic efficacy of these nanozymes in vivo.

To better assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes, an SC mouse model was established using LPS, and the effect of induction time on cardiac function was first explored. After intravenous injection of LPS for 12 or 24 h, echocardiography and histological examination were performed to evaluate changes in cardiac function and histology, to determine the optimal induction time (Fig. S47A, Supplementary Information). Cardiac echocardiography evaluation showed that, compared with the control group, a 12-h induction time significantly altered the cardiac function of the mice, as evidenced by a more pronounced reduction in the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) (Fig. S47B–D, Supplementary Information). Furthermore, histological analysis revealed inflammatory cell infiltration, red blood cells accumulation, and increased ROS production when the induction time was 12 h (Fig. S47E, F, Supplementary Information). Since SC is reversible in the early stages, the cardiac function of the mice may have entered a compensatory phase after 24 h of induction, leading to the restoration of cardiac function. Therefore, the induction time of 12 h was ultimately selected for the subsequent animal experiments.

Next, the SC mouse model was established by intravenously injecting LPS for an induction time of 12 h. We then used NCT-Ruzyme, Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, and CICT-Ruzyme for treatment (Fig. 9A). According to the results of echocardiography, compared with the control, LPS damaged the cardiac function of the mice, which significantly reduced the LVEF and LVFS. After PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes treatment, the LVEF and LVFS values significantly increased, indicating that the cardiac function of the mice improved, but the effect of the nonpolymer nanozyme, NCT-Ruzyme, was not obvious (Fig. 9B–D). In addition, there was no difference in the ratio of heart weight to body weight among the groups (Fig. S48, Supplementary Information). Serological and histological assays were then conducted at 12 h to assess the serum concentrations of CK-MB and LDH, two markers of myocardial injury. The results revealed a significant increase in the serum levels of these markers in the LPS group compared with those in the control group. However, in the mice treated with Ruzyme, ICT-Ruzyme, or CICT-Ruzyme, the levels of both myocardial injury markers were effectively reduced (Fig. 9E, F). As shown in Fig. 9G, histological analysis demonstrated that, compared with those in the control group, the myocardial tissue of the mice treated with LPS exhibited noticeable pathological changes. These included infiltration of inflammatory cells, disordered arrangement of myocardial cells, cell rupture and dissolution, significantly widened intercellular spaces, and accumulation of red blood cells, indicating successful induction of myocardial injury by LPS. After treatment with the NCT-Ruzyme, the intercellular space was partially restored; however, the infiltration of inflammatory cells and red blood cell accumulation persisted. However, treatment with the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes effectively improved the tissue structure. The myocardial morphology became clearer, the cells were more neatly arranged, and pathological damage was markedly reduced (Fig. 9G). As depicted in Figs. 9H, S49 and S50, LPS-induced myocardial injury resulted in the generation of ROS, which increased the level of oxidative stress in myocardial tissue and promoted the apoptosis of myocardial cells. The CICT-Ruzyme with myocardial targeting ability effectively reduced oxidative stress levels and the number of apoptotic myocardial cells (Fig. 9H; Figs. S49 and S50, Supplementary Information). Additionally, the RNS scavenging capacity of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes was verified at the tissue level via the fluorescent probe O52. The results showed that LPS significantly increased RNS levels in myocardial tissue, whereas treatment with PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes effectively reduced RNS levels. In contrast, the NCT-Ruzyme exhibited no alleviating effect (Fig. S51, Supplementary Information). Subsequently, PVP@Ru, RuO2@BSA, and three previously reported nanozymes were employed as controls to further examine the mitigating effect of the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzyme on SC (Fig. S52A, Supplementary Information). The results indicated that, compared with the control, LPS significantly decreased both LVEF and LVFS in mice, leading to a reduction in cardiac function. Following treatment with nanozymes, cardiac function improved across all groups; however, CTP-Ruzyme treatment resulted in the most pronounced effects (Fig. S52B–D, Supplementary Information). Mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of CICT-Ruzyme in alleviating SC

To further investigate the mechanisms through which PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes alleviate LPS-induced SC, high-throughput transcriptomics sequencing analysis was conducted on mouse heart tissues. DESeq2 analysis revealed 2919 differentially expressed mRNAs between the model group and the control group, indicating that the SC model was successfully established (Fig. S53, Supplementary Information). A total of 2196 mRNAs were differentially expressed following CICT-Ruzyme treatment compared with the model group, with 1120 mRNAs upregulated and 1076 mRNAs downregulated. These findings indicate significant transcriptomic differences between the model group and the CICT-Ruzyme treatment group (Fig. 10A–C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the differentially expressed mRNAs indicated that CICT-Ruzyme treatment was involved in signaling pathways related to the inflammatory response, receptor complex, and receptor ligand activity (Fig. 10D; Table S3, Supplementary Information). To gain a deeper understanding of the biological functions and pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) induced by CICT-Ruzyme, KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted. When the intracellular Fe2+ concentration becomes excessive, the Fenton reaction can generate a substantial amount of ROS, leading to oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, which in turn triggers ferroptosis in cells59. In previous characterization studies, the Fe2+ chelating ability of PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes was demonstrated. Building upon these findings, the role of the CICT-Ruzyme in the ferroptosis pathway was further investigated in cardiomyocytes, guided by previous characterization and transcriptome analysis results. An iron overload model was established in vitro using LPS on cardiomyocytes. Iron accumulation in the cells was assessed using FerroOrange, a fluorescent probe for Fe2+. Excess Fe2+ accumulated in LPS-treated cells, but preincubation with Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme significantly inhibited this process. However, the effect of the nonpolymer nanozyme, NCT-Ruzyme, was minimal (Fig. 10F; Fig. S54A, Supplementary Information). Intracellular iron accumulation is a hallmark of ferroptosis, promoting the generation of ROS and lipid ROS (LOS), which specifically increases oxidative stress within the inflammatory microenvironment60. To further investigate this, intracellular lipid peroxide (LPO) levels were measured using the lipid peroxidation fluorescent probe (Liperfluo) after incubating cells with LPS in the presence or absence of nanozymes. Coincubation with LPS significantly elevated LPO accumulation. However, pretreatment with Ruzyme and ICT-Ruzyme led to a significant reduction in LPO levels, whereas the effect of the NCT-Ruzyme was comparatively weaker (Fig. 10G). The relative quantitative results were consistent with the above results (Fig. S54B, Supplementary Information). Prussian blue staining of cardiac tissue showed that iron levels increased in the myocardial tissue after LPS induction. Treatment with CICT-Ruzyme effectively reduced the cardiac iron content (Fig. S55, Supplementary Information). These results indicated that the PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes effectively reduced ferroptosis-related characteristics, including Fe2+ accumulation and lipid peroxidation, in LPS-induced cardiomyocytes.

RSL3, a GPX4 inhibitor, has been widely used to induce ferroptosis61. Therefore, we employed RSL3 as an inducer to further validate the inhibitory effect of PPy-co-PTh-stabilized Ruzymes on ferroptosis. We first evaluated the cytotoxicity of RSL3 to H9C2 cells to determine the concentration that induced approximately 50% cell death, which was identified as 12 μM (Fig. S56A, Supplementary Information). Subsequently, H9C2 cells were treated with 12 μM RSL3 to induce ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes, allowing observation of the rescue effects of various nanozymes (Fig. 10H). The results demonstrated that treatment with PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes restored cell viability to over 75%, whereas the NCT-Ruzyme showed no significant protective effect (Fig. 10I). Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), a small molecule that protects cells from death caused by inactivation of ferroptosis inhibitors62, was used as a positive control. Malondialdehyde (MDA), an aldehyde produced by the decomposition of lipid peroxides, causes functional impairment of proteins and DNA in cells and tissues, and serves as a biomarker of lipid oxidation to evaluate ferroptosis levels63. After confirming the safety of Lip-1 (Fig. S56B, Supplementary Information), MDA levels were subsequently measured in cells subjected to the various treatments. As expected, MDA levels were significantly higher in the RSL3-induced cardiomyocytes than in the control cardiomyocytes. Nanozymes treatment markedly reduced the elevated MDA levels induced by RSL3, providing evidence that the nanozymes inhibited lipid peroxidation (Fig. 10J).

Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a small-molecule inhibitor that specifically suppresses ferroptosis64. The cytotoxicity of Fer-1 in H9C2 cardiomyocytes was evaluated, and 2 μM was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments (Fig. S56C, Supplementary Information). The inhibitory effect of the nanozyme on ferroptosis was subsequently validated by Western blotting. Acyl-coenzyme A synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), a key promoter of ferroptosis, facilitates the incorporation of free polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) into cell membranes, thereby accelerating lipid peroxidation65. Following RSL3 induction, ACSL4 expression increased in cardiomyocytes. Pre-protection with both ICT-Ruzyme and Fer-1 suppressed ferroptosis, with Fer-1 exhibiting more pronounced effects than the ICT-Ruzyme (Fig. 10K, Fig. S57, Supplementary Information). Conversely, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a specific biomarker associated with ferroptosis, protects cells against lipid peroxidation damage and maintains the intracellular redox balance66. RSL3 directly inactivates GPX4 through binding, leading to intracellular lipid peroxide accumulation and ferroptosis initiation61,66. After treatment with ICT-Ruzyme or Fer-1, GPX4 expression level was significantly increased (Fig. 10K, Fig. S57, Supplementary Information). GPX4 enzymatic activity was then measured in drug-treated cells. The results demonstrated that RSL3 induction significantly reduced GPX4 activity in cardiomyocytes, whereas both ICT-Ruzyme and Fer-1 effectively ameliorated the RSL3-induced reduction in GPX4 activity (Fig. 10L). These findings indicate that the ROS-scavenging and antioxidant properties of nanozymes effectively mitigate lipid peroxidation in RSL3-induced cardiomyocytes. At the tissue level, GPX4 activity was assessed in LPS-induced myocardial tissues. Compared with that in the control group, GPX4 activity decreased in the model group but was effectively restored by PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes (Fig. 10M). 4-Hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE), a lipid peroxidation metabolite and critical ferroptosis marker66,67. The expression and distribution of 4-HNE in different treatment groups were evaluated by performing microstructural and quantitative analyses on four discontinuous sections from each cardiac tissue sample. The results showed that LPS induction effectively elevated 4-HNE expression, whereas treatment with the PPy‑co‑PTh stabilized Ruzymes suppressed 4‑HNE levels in multiple cardiac sections, although the extent of suppression varied among sections and treatment groups, with statistically significant reductions observed in selected sections (Fig. S58, Supplementary Information). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes effectively alleviate ferroptosis-induced cardiomyocyte damage by significantly inhibiting lipid peroxidation, restoring GPX4 activity, and modulating key ferroptosis regulators.

Overall, this study further demonstrated the efficacy of conducting polymer-stabilized Ruzyme in alleviating LPS-induced cardiac ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation. The conducting polymer-stabilized Ruzymes not only exhibit excellent electrical conductivity but also show enhanced enzyme-like activity and a strong ability to scavenge nitrogen-free radicals, thereby effectively preventing excessive oxidation and improving nanozyme stability. Their pronounced antioxidant and iron-chelation properties contribute to the improvement of cardiac function, highlighting their potential for the treatment of sepsis-related cardiac injury. Moreover, PPy-co-PTh stabilized Ruzymes exhibit good biocompatibility and safety, providing a solid basis for potential clinical applications. Beyond the present system, the modification of nanozymes with a conductive polymer interface offers a general strategy to enhanced catalytic efficiency, stability, and biocompatibility. This approach may be particularly relevant for therapeutic applications in diseases associated with electrical conductivity, especially those affecting the heart and nervous system. Nevertheless, further studies are required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and to evaluate the translational potential of these nanozymes in clinical settings.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Central China Subcenter of National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases (Approval No. CCSNCCD-IACUC-2024000).

Construction of the conductive PPy-co-PTh stabilized nanozymes library

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, grade 2099, MW 31,000–50,000, 87–89% hydrolyzed, Bidepharm, 100 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized (DI) water by heating at 90 °C, and ammonium persulfate (APS, ≥98%, Bidepharm) solutions (40 μmol) containing different metal ions were prepared. The APS solutions containing different metal ions were slowly added to PVA solutions that had cooled to room temperature (RT) and stirred vigorously for 1 h at RT. Pyrrole (≥98%, Macklin, 100 μL) and thiophene (≥99%, J&K Scientific, 100 μL) were added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred thoroughly for 4 h at 4 °C. The molar ratio of the metal ions to thiophene was fixed at 1:4. The pH of the system was subsequently adjusted to 5.5–6.0 with 0.1 M NaOH/NaBH4 (analytical grade, Bidepharm), and the resulting mixture was dialyzed (MWCO 8–14 kDa) to remove unbound metal ions and then lyophilized to obtain a black cotton-like solid. Among them, the selected metal ions are FeCl3·6H2O (≥99%, Macklin), CoCl2·6H2O (≥99%, Macklin), NiCl2·6H2O (≥99%, Adamas-beta), CeCl3·7H2O (≥99%, Bidepharm), MnCl2·4H2O (≥98%, Macklin), CuSO4·5H2O (≥99%, Aladdin), HAuCl4·3H2O (≥99.9%, Adamas-beta), H2PtCl6·6H2O (≥99.9%, Adamas-beta), RuCl3·xH2O (≥99%, Adamas-beta), RhCl3·3H2O (≥99%, Macklin), and DyCl3·6H2O (≥99.9%, Macklin). All experimental procedures were conducted in a well-ventilated fume hood using appropriate personal protective equipment due to the involvement of strong oxidizing agents, reducing agents, corrosive alkaline reagents, and volatile organic monomers.

Synthesis of ICT-Ruzyme

(5-carboxypentyl) triphenyl phosphonium bromide (TPP-COOH, ≥98%, Adamas-beta, 3.7 mg), 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCL, TCI, 2.4 mg) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, ≥98%, Aladdin, 1.2 mg) were dissolved in DI water (10 mL) and stirred at room temperature for 30 min to activate the carboxyl group. Subsequently, Ruzyme (50 mg) was added to the above mixture, and the reaction was allowed to proceed under continuous stirring at RT for 1 h. After completion, the reaction mixture was dialyzed (MWCO 8–14 kDa) for 24 h to remove unreacted small molecules. The resulting product was collected by lyophilization to afford ICT-Ruzyme as a black cotton-like solid. All experimental procedures were conducted in a well-ventilated fume hood using appropriate personal protective equipment due to the use of reactive coupling reagents.

Synthesis of CICT-Ruzyme

DSPE-PEG2000-MAL (≥95%, Ponsure, 5 mg) was dissolved in dichloromethane (CH2Cl2, 500 μL). ICT-Ruzyme (25 mg) was then added, and the biphasic mixture was stirred overnight at RT to allow conjugation and removal of the organic solvent. Subsequently, cardiac-targeting peptide (CTP, ≥99%, MCE, 2.5 mg) was added, and the reaction mixture was further stirred at RT for 2 h. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was subjected to vacuum freeze-drying to afford CICT-Ruzyme as a black cotton-like solid. All experimental procedures were conducted in a well-ventilated fume hood using appropriate personal protective equipment due to the use of volatile organic solvents and reactive functional groups.

Synthesis of the nonpolymer nanozyme NCT-Ruzyme

Ruthenium(III) chloride aqueous solution (RuCl3·xH2O, ≥99%, Adamas-beta, 0.1 M, 5.0 mL, 0.50 mmol) was mixed with bovine serum albumin solution (BSA Albumin Fraction V, ≥98%, Neofroxx; 25 mg/mL, 45 mL, 1.125 g) under vigorous stirring at room temperature for 5 min to ensure complete homogenization. Separately, sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH, 2.0 M, 1.5 mL, 3.0 mmol) and sodium borohydride solution (NaBH4, 2.0 M, 1.5 mL, 3.0 mmol) were prepared and mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1 to obtain a NaOH/NaBH4 mixture (total volume: 3.0 mL). Subsequently, 1.5 mL of this mixture (containing NaOH and NaBH4) was added to the system to reduce Ru3+. The reaction mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under continuous stirring. After completion, the mixture was dialyzed (MWCO 8–14 kDa) for 24 h to remove unbound metal ions and then lyophilized to afford NCT-Ruzyme as a cotton-like solid. All experimental procedures were conducted in a well-ventilated fume hood using appropriate personal protective equipment due to the involvement of strong reducing agents and corrosive alkaline reagents.

Synthesis of PVP@Ru

Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, K30, MW ≈ 30 kDa, Bidepharm, 133 mg) was dissolved in anhydrous methanol (MeOH, analytical grade, 180 mL) under continuous stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. Separately, ruthenium (III) chloride hydrate (RuCl3·xH2O, 19.2 mg) was dissolved in DI water (20 mL) to form an aqueous RuCl3 solution. The RuCl3 aqueous solution was added dropwise to the PVP methanolic solution, followed by vortex mixing to ensure thorough homogenization. The resulting mixture was then reacted at 70 °C for 3 h under continuous stirring. After completion of the reaction, most of the methanol was removed by rotary evaporator. The remaining small amount of methanol was used to resuspend the solid, which was transferred into a clean centrifuge tube. Subsequently, chloroform (CHCl3, analytical grade, equal volume to the residual methanol) and n-hexane (analytical grade; five times the volume of the residual methanol) were added. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and centrifuged at 7000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was retained. This washing process was repeated three times. Finally, the precipitate was dried in a vacuum drying oven to afford PVP@Ru as a solid product. All experimental procedures were conducted in a well-ventilated fume hood using appropriate personal protective equipment due to the use of volatile and flammable organic solvents and toxic halogenated solvents.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons of all data were performed by GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. P values were determined by unpaired two-tailed t tests between two groups. One-way ANOVA was performed for data from three or more groups. Two-way ANOVA was performed for data with two factors. When performing enrichment analysis on transcriptomic results, the Fisher’s exact test was used, and the BH method was applied for P value adjustment.
