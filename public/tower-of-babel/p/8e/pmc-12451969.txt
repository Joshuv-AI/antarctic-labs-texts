Oral microbial signatures underlying recurrence via PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway modulation in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Da-Woon Kwack, Zeba Praveen, Yeon-Hee Kim, Chong Woo Yoo, Jae Hee Ko, Suk Min Youn, Joo Yong Park, Jong-Ho Lee, Sung Weon Choi, Mi Kyung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) often recurs locally, reducing survival. The oral microbiome may influence tumor recurrence, but its prognostic role is unclear. This study investigated oral microbiomes associated with OSCC recurrence and their prognostic merit.Materials and methodsSaliva samples were collected from 133 patients with OSCC. 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed, and microbial signatures were predicted via XGBoost. Functional metagenomic prediction was conducted using PICRUSt2.ResultsXGBoost identified Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, Kingella, Paludibacter, Parvimonas, Staphylococcus, and Veillonella as predictive for OSCC recurrence. Eubacterium and Lactobacillus were significantly enriched in recurrent disease and associated with poor survival. Staphylococcus and Veillonella were abundant in non-recurrent disease, correlating with a favorable prognosis. The microbiome-based model achieved superior predictive performance (AUC = 0.741) compared with the clinical N-stage model (AUC = 0.66). Eubacterium and Lactobacillus showed positive correlations with key genes, such as protein kinase B (AKT), fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 and guanine nucleotide-binding protein G subunit beta-2, within the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. In contrast, Veillonella was negatively correlated with these genes.ConclusionsOral saliva microbiome profiling reveals distinct microbial patterns associated with OSCC recurrence. Our correlation-based functional predictions indicated that the enrichment of Eubacterium and Lactobacillus along with a lower abundance of Veillonella may influence recurrence through oncogenic PI3K/AKT/mTOR, underscoring the prognostic potential of saliva-based microbial biomarkers.KEYWORDS: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, oral microbiome, recurrence, machine learning, prognosis, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a malignant tumor that account for approximately 90–95% of all oral cancers. In 2022, oral cancer ranked as the 16th most common malignancy globally, with an estimated annual incidence of 389,846 new cases and 188,438 deaths [1]. The five-year overall survival (OS) rate for OSCC ranges from 48 to 70% [2,3]. Key prognostic factors include lymph node metastasis, perineural invasion, and tumor differentiation [3]. In particular, patients without metastasis have an 80.5% five-year OS rate compared to 54.7% in those with metastasis. Despite advances in surgical interventions and chemoradiotherapy, recurrence rates for OSCC remain high at 28–33.9% [2,3]. Given that tumor recurrence is considered a critical factor that negatively influences OS in patients with OSCC, developing effective strategies to prevent and manage recurrence is a central research focus.

Early detection of OSCC recurrence is crucial for improving patient prognosis. Saliva-based diagnostics have been explored as a non-invasive and accessible approach for assessing recurrence risk and monitoring disease progression in OSCC patients [4]. Additionally, saliva is a key component of the oral cancer tumor microenvironment. Indeed, the oral microbiome plays a significant role in the development, progression, and prognosis of OSCC [5–13]. More specifically, pathogens, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Treponema denticola, promote the epithelial–mesenchymal transition, increase matrix metalloproteinase expression, and modulate immune responses within the tumor microenvironment, enhancing invasion and metastatic potential [9]. Furthermore, microbiomes may uniquely influence cancer recurrence in different organ systems. For example, the relative abundances of Acidovorax, Clostridioides, Succinimonas, and Shewanella spp. are increased in patients with recurrent lung cancer [14]. Meanwhile, Fusobacterium nucleatum and Bacteroides fragilis are more abundant in patients without recurrence than in those with recurrence in colorectal cancer [15].

Recent studies have demonstrated that alterations in the oral microbiome are associated with the recurrence of OSCC. In particular, Granato et al. reported that the relative abundances of Staphylococcus, Stenotrophomonas, Acinetobacter, Centipeda, Selenomonas, and Alloscordovia were significantly increased in the saliva of patients with recurrent or metastatic OSCC, which correlated with lower overall survival rates [16]. Furthermore, another recent study identified potential microbial biomarkers for OSCC recurrence from oral swab samples, including Leptotrichia trevisanii, Capnocytophaga sputigena, Capnocytophaga, Cardiobacterium, and Olsenella [17]. Despite these findings, the specific microbial signatures associated with OSCC recurrence remain unclear. Therefore, further investigation is warranted to elucidate the role of the oral microbiome in OSCC recurrence and to evaluate its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker.

This study aimed to characterize the oral microbiome associated with OSCC recurrence using machine learning analysis of unstimulated whole saliva samples, and to identify predictive genes, pathways, and key interactions underlying the mechanisms driving recurrence.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment and clinical characteristics

Adult patients (aged > 19 years) newly diagnosed with OSCC were recruited between September 2018 and November 2022 at the National Cancer Center, Korea. The study population comprised 133 participants: 35 with recurrent OSCC and 98 with non-recurrent OSCC. The mean follow-up duration after treatment was 42 months. Recurrence of OSCC was defined as either locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis occurring after initial curative treatment, with a mean recurrence interval of 11.87 months (range: 1−37 months).

The key clinical variables analyzed were age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, 8th edition of the American Joint Committee of Cancer (AJCC) tumor, nodes, and metastasis (TNM) stage, primary tumor extent (T-stage), and lymph node involvement (N-stage). The pathologic differentiation status was categorized as poorly differentiated (PD), moderately differentiated (MD), and well-differentiated (WD).

This study was approved by the National Cancer Center, Korea (institutional review board [IRB] approval numbers NCC2019−0050 and NCC2019−0116), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Saliva sample collection

During the initial visit for OSCC diagnosis, unstimulated whole saliva samples were collected after a 1-h fasting period to minimize variability in saliva composition due to recent food or drink intake [18]. To further reduce confounding factors, participants were also instructed to refrain from smoking for at least 1 h prior to sample collection. Samples were stored in 1.5-mL tubes at −80 °C. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects.

Characterization of the oral microbiome through 16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing

Microbial DNA (70 ng/mL) was extracted from 500-µL saliva samples using the Fast DNA Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA) per the manufacturer's instructions; 20 ng/mL was used for subsequent analyses. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a Qubit dsDNA BR Kit and a fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). PCR amplification was performed using fusion primers targeting the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. For bacterial amplification, fusion primers 515F (5′-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACAC-XXXXXXXX-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGAT-XXXXXXXXGTCTCGTGGGCTCGG-AGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) were constructed in the following order: P5 (P7) graft binding, i5 (i7) index, Nextera consensus, sequencing adaptor, and target region sequencing [19].

Amplification was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were confirmed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized using a Gel Doc system (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). Amplified products were purified using CleanPCR (CleanNA). Equal concentrations of purified products were pooled, and short fragments (non-target products) were removed using CleanPCR (CleanNA). The quality and product size were assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA) with a DNA 7500 chip. Mixed amplicons were pooled, and sequencing was performed by CJ Bioscience, Inc. (Seoul, Korea) using an Illumina iSeq Sequencing system (Illumina, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Bacteria were classified based on taxonomic data provided by EzBioCloud 19. Poor-quality reads < 80 base pairs (bp) or > 2,000 bp were excluded. Taxonomic analyses were performed using USEARCH. The UPARSE algorithm was used to classify reads into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with 97% similarity. Single-end reads were clustered into OTUs using UCLUST and cut-off numbers.

Machine learning model for predicting of OSCC recurrence

Python software (version 3.7.15) and the H2O Python module (version 3.38.0.2) (https://github.com/h2oai/h2o−3) were used to predict OSCC recurrence and identify significant microbial taxa. A gradient-based one-sided sampling method was utilized within the light gradient boosting machine (XGBoost) model. The data were randomly divided into discovery (recur: n = 20, non-recur: n = 70) and validation (recur: n = 15, non-recur: n = 28) datasets. The discovery dataset was used to generate the prediction model, which was estimated through five-fold cross-validation. Optimization was performed based on accuracy, AUC, F1 score, precision, and recall.

The top-feature importance analysis of the model revealed significant contributors to cancer prediction performance via ROC analysis. The utilization of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values revealed the output generated by the machine learning model. This method offers a localized interpretation of individual predictors by assigning proportional contributions of the features to the prediction outcome.

Functional homology inference: predicting functional genes and pathways

A functional profile of the oral microbiome was constructed using the PICRUSt algorithm and EzBioCloud microbiome taxonomic profiling (MTP). Sequencing reads were obtained using the EzBioCloud 16S MTP pipeline and matched to the reference database entries. Functional profiles were annotated by multiplying the gene counts/OTU by the OTU abundance per sample, using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. The accuracy of each functional profile was analyzed using the nearest sequenced taxon index.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using the R software (version 4.1.1). Student's t-tests and chi-square test were used to compare clinical characteristics between the recur and non-recur groups. Alpha diversity was assessed using Shannon and Simpson indices; beta diversity was evaluated using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on weighted UniFrac distances.

Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier curves. Spearman's rank correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the association between microbial taxa and functional genes. Statistical significance was determined using Wilcoxon's rank-sum test, quartile-based comparisons, fold change (FC) analysis, and logistic regression, as appropriate. A flowchart of the study is provided in Supplementary Figure 1.

In the discovery set, no significant differences were detected between the recur and non-recur groups regarding sex, age, alcohol consumption, T stage, TNM stage, or histological grade. However, the prevalence of lymph node metastasis (N+) was significantly higher in the recur group than the non-recur group (70.0% vs. 32.9%, P = 0.007). A similar trend was observed in the validation set, with markedly higher proportions of N+ stage disease in the recur group (93.3% vs. 32.1%, P = 0.001). No statistically significant differences were identified in other clinical or demographic variables.

A total of 48 phyla and 1,714 genera were identified across all saliva samples. The dominant phyla (relative abundance > 1%) included Actinobacterium, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Fusobacteria, comprising > 90% of the bacterial population in both groups (Figure 1A). The average number of reads per sample was 40,000, with a minimum threshold of 20,000 reads per sample. Patients in the recur and non-recur groups did not exhibit significant alpha diversity, as demonstrated by the OUTs, PD-whole tree, and Simpson and Shannon indices (Figure 1B–E). In contrast, beta diversity analysis using PCoA based on weighted UniFrac distances showed a significant difference between recur and non-recur groups (P = 0.0396), with the recur group exhibiting greater microbial diversity (Figure 1F). Lefse cladogram analysis was performed to identify key microbiome differences between the recur and non-recur patient groups. We found significantly upregulated and downregulated microbiomes in the recur groups (Figure 1G).

Prediction model of microbial biomarkers for OSCC recurrence based on machine learning

Utilizing the XGBoost model with the discovery dataset, a high accuracy level was achieved in predicting OSCC recurrence (F1 score: 0.59, sensitivity = 0.80, specificity = 0.93, accuracy = 0.84, AUC = 0.88; Figure 2A). The top-feature importance analysis and SHAP values of the model revealed 20 significant microbiotas associated with recurrence prediction (Figure 2B, C). The model was further evaluated using the validation dataset, exhibiting a higher AUC of 0.94 (Figure 2F).

Among the 20 significant microbial taxa, seven were selected based on the highest FC, AUC, and Wilcoxon test results. Higher abundances of Eubacterium (FC = 7.23) and Lactobacillus (FC = 8.00) were associated with an increased risk of recurrence in patients with OSCC, whereas Staphylococcus (FC = 0.71) and Veillonella (FC = 0.84) were associated with a reduced risk of recurrence (Supplementary Table 1).

An odds ratio plot was constructed using logistic regression analysis (P < 0.005) to evaluate the association between individual bacterial genera and recurrence status (Supplementary Table 2). The recur group had significantly higher relative abundances of Eubacterium (univariable OR [95% CI] = 10.5 [4.1–26.6]; multivariable OR = 10.3 [4.1–25.8]) and Lactobacillus (univariable OR = 18.8 [7.0–67.8]; multivariable OR = 17.1 [7.6–80.4]). Conversely, the recur group showed lower relative abundances of Staphylococcus (univariable OR = 0.50 [0.2–2.63]; multivariable OR = 0.5 [0.36–2.1]) and Veillonella (univariable OR = 0.8 [0.78–1.5]; multivariable OR = 0.8 [0.77–1.3]) compared to the non-recur group.

Impact of microbiome abundance and clinical N-stage on survival in OSCC

Kaplan–Meier survival curves depicted the association between the relative abundance of seven specific bacterial genera in the oral microbiome and the disease-free survival (DFS) of patients with OSCC over a five-year follow-up period. High proportions of Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, Kingella, Paludibacter, and Parvimonas were significantly associated with poor survival outcomes. In contrast, high Staphylococcus and Veillonella proportions were significantly linked to improved survival (Figure 3A–G). These findings highlight the potential prognostic value of the oral microbiome composition in determining the survival of patients with oral cancer.

Prognostic significance of 7 key oral microbiota in relation to disease-free survival in OSCC patients (A–G).

The univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses, adjusted for clinical N-stage as a confounder, revealed seven microbiota factors significantly associated with disease-free survival (DFS) in the recur group (Table 2). Eubacterium (Hazard ratio (HR) [95% CI] = 4.17[1.94–8.98]) and Lactobacillus (HR[95% CI] = 3.54[1.64–7.64]) was strongly associated with an increased risk of recurrence. Conversely, Veillonella (HR[95% CI] = 0.44[0.22–0.89]) and Staphylococcus (HR[95% CI] = 0.48[0.28–0.83]) were associated with a reduced risk of recurrence. These findings highlight the potential role of specific microbiome factors in influencing disease progression, independent of the N stage.

Predictive performance of microbiome and clinical N-stage models

ROC analysis demonstrated the predictive capabilities of the microbiome-based and clinical N-stage models for OSCC recurrence. The microbiome-based model, incorporating seven key genera (Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, Kingella, Paludibacter, Parvimonas, Staphylococcus, and Veillonella), exhibited the strongest predictive performance (AUC: 0.741; Figure 4A). In comparison, the clinical N-stage model showed moderate predictive ability (AUC: 0.66; Figure 4B).

Correlation between key microbial genera and functional genes in OSCC

Functional analysis revealed significant changes in the phosphoinositide 3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway in patients with OSCC. Within the PI3K/AKT pathway, AKT and fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) expression was significantly associated with recurrence. Similarly, within the mTOR pathway, AKT and mTOR were highlighted as key contributors (Supplementary Table 3). These results emphasize the potential role of these pathways and genera in the recurrence patterns and disease progression of OSCC.

The Spearman's correlation analysis revealed distinct associations of seven key microbial genera with six functional genes and two pathways in the recur and non-recur groups (Figure 5A, B). In the recur group, Eubacterium was significantly positively correlated with AKT, guanine nucleotide-binding protein G subunit beta-2 (GNB2), and FGFR1. Similarly, Lactobacillus demonstrated strong positive correlations with mTOR, AIP3, AKT, GNB2, FGFR1, and thrombospondin 1 (Thbs1). Conversely, Veillonella negatively correlated with AKT, GNB2, and FGFR1 in the recur group, suggesting a potential protective role against tumor progression. These results indicate that Eubacterium and Lactobacillus may contribute to tumor progression and recurrence by activating oncogenic PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways. In contrast, Veillonella may exert a protective effect by reducing the activation of these key pathways in OSCC.

Discussion

This study identified specific oral microbiota associated with OSCC recurrence. Eubacterium and Lactobacillus were positively linked to oncogenic PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, whereas Veillonella showed potential tumor-suppressive effects.

The dominant phyla in the recur and non-recur groups were Actinobacterium, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Fusobacteria, and Proteobacteria. A systematic review [6] reported similar phyla compositions in saliva samples collected via oral rinse from patients with OSCC compared to healthy controls. In the current study, alpha diversity showed no significant difference between groups, whereas beta diversity differed markedly, with the Recur group demonstrating greater microbial community variation. This observation is consistent with previous studies [16,17,20], suggesting that intergroup ecological shifts, rather than overall diversity metrics, may play a more critical role in influencing OSCC recurrence. However, it is important to acknowledge that not all studies have shown such associations. For example, some reported no statistically significant differences in OSCC-associated microbiome composition [21]. These contrasting findings underscore the complexity of host–microbe interactions in OSCC and highlight the necessity for larger, well-controlled studies to establish the reproducibility and clinical relevance of microbial alterations.

The XGBoost model achieved high predictive performance for OSCC recurrence, identifying seven key genera, such as Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, Kingella, Paludibacter, Parvimonas, Staphylococcus, and Veillonella, as significant microbial biomarkers associated with recurrence risk.

Notably, Eubacterium may be integral to microbial communities associated with OSCC recurrence. Wang et al. [22] reported that a lower salivary abundance of E. infirmum is significantly associated with no response to immunotherapy in patients with OSCC. As a failure to respond to immunotherapy can lead to decreased efficacy of subsequent treatments and an increased risk of recurrence, these findings suggest that E. infirmum is closely associated with recurrence in patients with OSCC.

Chattopadhyay et al. [5] reported a significant association between higher TNM stages in OSCC patients and an increased abundance of Lactobacillus species. More specifically, L. vaginalis, L. gasseri, L. johnsonii, L. fermentum, L. salivarius, and L. rhamnosus were significantly more abundant in patients at advanced stages (T3, 4) compared to those at early stages (T1, 2). Given that higher TNM stages are typically associated with increased recurrence rates and reduced OS, these findings support the results of the present study.

Similar to the observed potential protective role of Veillonella in our study, V. parvula exhibited tumor-suppressive activity in OSCC and breast cancer by inhibiting PI3K/AKT pathway [23,24]. However, it has also demonstrated tumor-promoting effect in lung cancer [25]. These divergent findings highlight the complex and dynamic interactions between the microbiome and tumor development, emphasizing the necessity for cancer-specific evaluation when considering microbiome-based therapeutic strategies.

ROC analysis demonstrated that a microbiome-based model incorporating seven key genera outperformed the traditional clinical N-stage model in predicting recurrence, achieving a higher AUC (0.741 vs. 0.66). This suggests that microbiome profiling offers a valuable adjunctive tool to traditional staging systems to improve the prognostic accuracy in patients with OSCC.

Functional pathway analysis suggested a correlation of the microbiome with the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in the recur group. Accordingly, Spearman's correlation analyses showed positive associations with Eubacterium and Lactobacillus as well as a negative association of Veillonella with genes related to this pathway, such as AKT, FGFR1, and GNB2. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is an established driver of tumorigenesis. AKT activates mTOR via PIP3, which is formed following PIP2 phosphorylation by PI3K. Subsequent signaling promotes cell growth and proliferation, ultimately leading to tumor growth [26]. FGFR1 overexpression can co-activate AKT signaling through FRS2α-mediated stimulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade [27–29]. Moreover, experimental and clinical studies in lung and breast cancer have demonstrated that dual inhibition of these pathways enhances antitumor efficacy, highlighting their cooperative role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [27,28]. Similarly, mechanistic studies of GNB2 have revealed that Gβ protein, such as GNB2, activates downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [30]. Taken together, these correlative findings highlight AKT, FGFR1, and GNB2 as potential key mediators linking microbiome dysbiosis to tumor recurrence.

We next reviewed the literature on the three identified genera and their relationship with the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Although research specifically focusing on Eubacterium species is limited, Lactobacillus iners has been suggested to promote resistance to chemoradiation therapy by activating PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway via lactate accumulation in the tumor microenvironment [31]. In contrast, Asoudeh-Fard et al. [32] demonstrated L. plantarium induced apoptosis in KB oral cancer cells by upregulating PTEN, an inhibitor of the AKT pathway, and suppressing MAPK signaling, suggesting a tumor-suppressive effect that contradicts our findings. Similarly, Chang et al. [23] reported that Veillonella parvula inhibited OSCC progression by suppressing the TROP2/PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby decreasing tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis.

Clinical trials have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway inhibitors, such as alpelisib, copanlisib, and idelalisib, in the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [33]. Based on these findings, we propose that further in-depth research on Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, and Veillonella is warranted to facilitate the development of anticancer probiotics that target these pathways, offering potential insights for developing therapeutic strategies to reduce the risk of recurrence in patients with OSCC.

This study had some limitations. First, functional pathway analysis was correlative, and causality cannot be inferred. Multi-omics would provide deeper mechanistic insights into host-microbiome interactions and their role in recurrence biology. Second, the number of patients in the recur group (n = 35) was significantly smaller than that in the non-recur group (n = 98), which limited our ability to control potential confounding variables such as smoking, clinical N-stage, TNM stage, and grade. This imbalance might have contributed to the overfitting or underfitting of the machine learning model. Third, analysis was conducted at the genus level without a species-level resolution. Given that different species within the same genus may exhibit opposing biological effects, for instance, L. iners vs. L. salivarius, species-level characterization through whole metagenome sequencing would provide deeper insights. Finally, salivary-based sampling may not fully reflect the microbial composition at the tumor site, warranting future studies incorporating tumor tissue or swab-based sampling.

Despite these limitations, our findings offer novel insights into the microbiome's role in OSCC recurrence and suggest its potential as a source of prognostic biomarkers for detecting early recurrence and selecting targeted therapy. To date, clinical evidence supporting microbiome-targeted interventions, such as probiotic administration, in head and neck cancers remains limited, with most data derived from other malignancies, including melanoma and gastrointestinal cancers related to the gut microbiome [34,35]. In OSCC, future research should focus on functional validation of the key taxa identified in this study – Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, and Veillonella – through in vitro co-culture and pathway analyses. Preclinical and clinical studies are warranted to determine whether probiotic-based strategies can enhance antitumor efficacy and reduce recurrence. Notably, probiotic-induced enrichment of Lactobacillus spp. has been shown to activate NK cell-mediated immunity and suppress tumor growth in pancreatic cancer, underscoring the potential of microbiome modulation to reprogram the tumor microenvironment in OSCC [34]. Although the impact of probiotics on the salivary microbiome remains insufficiently characterized, short-term supplementation has been reported to shift commensal abundance, suggesting responsiveness to external modulation [36].

Taken together, these findings support further exploration of microbiome-based interventions as adjunctive strategies in OSCC management, with the potential to refine recurrence prediction and improve therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, future research should explore diverse probiotic strains and alternative delivery modalities, such as oral rinses and sprays, to identify optimal strategies for sustained modulation of the oral microbiome.

Conclusions

This study identified specific oral microbiota associated with OSCC disease-free survival. Eubacterium and Lactobacillus may correlate with recurrence through activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, while Staphylococcus and Veillonella appear protective, being associated with lower recurrence risk and improved survival. The microbiome-based predictive model outperformed conventional N-stage in discriminating recurrence risk, underscoring its potential as a non-invasive prognostic tool for detecting early recurrence. These findings suggest that saliva-based microbial profiling may improve prognosis for early detection of recurrence and therapeutic outcomes in OSCC.
