Network reorganization of gaming disorder, depression, and anxiety across the transition to emerging adulthood: a longitudinal symptom-level analysis
Authors: Yinan Ji, Hui Hu, Xinfeng Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood is a critical period of psychological and neurobiological change. Gaming disorder (GD), depression, and anxiety often co-occur, yet little is known about how their comorbidity structures reorganize across developmental stages.MethodsA two-wave longitudinal study was conducted with 663 Chinese adolescents assessed during late adolescence (T₀, mean age = 17.52) and 620 participants reassessed three years later in emerging adulthood (T₁, mean age = 20.53). Validated self-report scales measured symptoms of GD, depression, and anxiety. Symptom networks were estimated separately at each wave using regularized Gaussian graphical models. Expected influence (EI) and bridge expected influence (BEI) were computed to assess central and bridging symptoms, network stability was evaluated using bootstrap procedures, and Network Comparison Tests were conducted to compare global strength and network structure between waves.ResultsFrom T₀ to T₁, network sparsity decreased from 0.80 to 0.75, density increased from 0.20 to 0.25, and global strength increased from 19.55 to 22.50. At T₀, the highest EI nodes were GAD1 (nervousness, 1.23) and GD6 (persistent gaming, 1.11), and the highest BEI nodes were GAD1 (0.50) and PHQ6 (worthlessness, 0.41). At T₁, the highest EI nodes were GAD5 (restlessness, 1.22) and GAD3 (excessive worry, 1.19), and the highest BEI nodes were GAD5 (0.64) and PHQ9 (suicidal thoughts, 0.62). Network Comparison Tests indicated that global EI increased by 1.28 (p = 0.02). Centrality invariance testing revealed significant EI increases for GAD3 (0.58, p < 0.01) and GAD5 (0.57, p < 0.01). For BEI, GAD7 (catastrophizing, 0.42, p = 0.02) and GAD3 (0.40, p < 0.01) showed significant increases.ConclusionsThe longitudinal network analysis showed that the comorbidity of GD, depression, and anxiety reorganized from late adolescence to emerging adulthood. Cognitive–affective symptoms became central and bridging, whereas gaming-related emotional and behavioral links weakened, reflecting a possible developmental shift from reactive, behavior-based coping in adolescence to internally sustained, cognitively driven symptom networks in emerging adulthood. Interventions should focus on strengthening adaptive coping and targeting core cognitive–affective symptoms to reduce long-term psychopathological risk.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08410-5.Keywords: Gaming disorder, Depression, Anxiety, Symptom network, Emerging adulthood

Introduction

The transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood marks a critical period of psychological and neurobiological development. During this stage, individuals undergo accelerated maturation in executive functioning, increasing autonomy, and heightened demands related to identity formation, academic achievement, and social role navigation [1, 2]. As individuals exit the structured environments of adolescence, they often face new academic, relational, and existential stressors with limited guidance. The loss of familiar routines and the sudden demand for self-directed coping may place increased strain on previously adaptive emotion regulation strategies, potentially increasing vulnerability to the consolidation of psychiatric symptoms [3]. Furthermore, mental health trajectories established during this phase have been shown to predict long-term psychosocial outcomes into adulthood, underscoring the urgency of early detection and a deeper understanding of the development of psychological symptoms [4].

Depression and anxiety are among the most prevalent psychiatric conditions during this life stage, and they frequently co-occur [5–7]. In addition, gaming disorder (GD) has emerged as a salient psychosocial concern, especially among adolescents and emerging adults. Recognized by the ICD-11 as a clinical condition characterized by impaired control over gaming, prioritization of gaming over other activities, and continuation despite negative consequences [8], GD has gained increasing attention as a developmentally embedded, emotion-related psychopathology [9, 10]. Recent epidemiological studies estimate that GD affects approximately 3–4% of adolescents worldwide, with higher prevalence rates reported in East Asia [11, 12].

A growing body of research has documented the frequent co-occurrence of GD, depression, and anxiety across adolescence and young adulthood [13–15]. Shared mechanisms such as escapist motivation, emotion dysregulation, and social withdrawal are believed to underlie these associations [16, 17]. These mechanisms operate in a bidirectional and mutually reinforcing manner: emotional distress may precipitate excessive gaming as a maladaptive coping strategy, while sustained gaming can further exacerbate affective dysregulation, impair executive control, disrupt circadian rhythms, and reduce offline social engagement, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle of comorbidity. Over time, this cycle may undergo reorganization in its structure and intensity as individuals progress through key developmental milestones, potentially altering the relative influence of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral symptoms. However, how the structure of this comorbidity reorganizes across key developmental transitions remains insufficiently understood.

Among the available approaches to studying comorbidity, symptom network analysis offers distinct advantages. Rather than conceptualizing disorders as discrete entities, network theory posits that psychopathology emerges from direct interactions among individual symptoms, which can reinforce one another and generate self-sustaining patterns of distress [18, 19]. This framework enables the identification of central symptoms that maintain a disorder, as well as bridging symptoms that transmit distress between conditions—offering potential leverage points for targeted intervention. Building on this framework, the developmental network psychopathology perspective further emphasizes that the structure and organization of symptom networks are not static, but may reorganize across key developmental transitions. Periods such as the shift from late adolescence to emerging adulthood are therefore viewed as developmental windows during which changes in network connectivity, centrality, and cross-domain bridging may reflect evolving regulatory demands and vulnerabilities [20–22].

Existing network studies have yielded valuable insights into the GD–depression–anxiety relationship in youth populations. Sit et al. identified “gaming as escape” as a key bridge between GD and depression in adolescents, while Yang et al. reported strong GD–anxiety connections centered on restlessness and worry [23, 24]. Research in Chinese samples has similarly highlighted cognitive-affective bridging symptoms, with Liu et al. identifying “worthlessness” and Xie and Tang noting “nervousness” as central links across symptom domains [25, 26]. Despite these contributions, all of these studies have employed cross-sectional designs, limiting their ability to capture how comorbidity structures reorganize over developmental time.

Some longitudinal studies have begun to address these limitations. For example, Gao et al. examined symptom network changes in early and middle adolescence and documented age-related shifts in symptom centrality and bridge connectivity; however, the sample excluded individuals over 18 years of age and the follow-up period was insufficient to capture large-scale network reorganization across the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood [27]. Other studies have relied on short follow-up intervals, typically less than one year, or restricted developmental coverage. This includes adolescent-only samples with one-year follow-ups (e.g., Peng et al. [28]; Liang et al. [29]), which primarily capture within-adolescence change, as well as young adult gamer samples with limited temporal spacing (Ye et al. [30]), where observed associations may reflect short-term symptom fluctuation rather than developmental reconfiguration.

Longitudinal research conducted in college populations has likewise remained largely confined to a single developmental stage. For instance, Tang et al. followed college students to examine the longitudinal interplay among loneliness, internet use, and depressive symptoms; however, the study did not explicitly model the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood, nor did it focus on gaming-related symptom systems [31]. As a result, the findings primarily inform within-stage dynamics among college students rather than network reorganization across a developmental boundary. Similarly, Zhao et al. employed a three-month follow-up design among college students [32], and Jiang et al. examined psychological distress and general internet addiction within a relatively narrow age range, both of which limit inferences about longer-term developmental change [33]. Still other longitudinal studies, such as Tullett-Prado et al., focused exclusively on adult samples, thereby bypassing the transitional dynamics unique to late adolescence [34].

Although longitudinal symptom network research may entail methodological challenges (e.g., measurement non-invariance), it remains suited to capturing developmentally anchored reorganization of symptom associations across transitions. Collectively, existing longitudinal research suffers from restricted age coverage and limited follow-up durations, leaving the developmental reorganization of comorbidity networks across the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood largely unexplored. Moreover, studies simultaneously modeling GD, depression, and anxiety within a single network remain rare.

This study employs a longitudinal symptom-level network approach to examine changes in the comorbidity structure of GD, depression, and anxiety from late adolescence to emerging adulthood. By tracking shifts in central and bridging symptoms over a three-year interval, it provides novel insights into the developmental dynamics of these symptom networks and identifies potential targets for preventive and clinical interventions. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) compare the global network structures of late adolescence and emerging adulthood to assess changes in overall symptom interconnectivity; (2) track shifts in central and bridge symptoms to evaluate changes in node-level importance and cross-domain connectivity; and (3) identify differences in specific symptom-to-symptom associations to determine how pairwise relationships reorganize over time.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cohort studies.

Participants and procedure

Participants were recruited from a cohort of Grade 11 students at a public secondary school located in an urban district of Shandong Province, China. The school is a non-selective, general public institution that enrolls students from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and is broadly representative of urban high schools in the region. It was selected based on accessibility and an established collaboration with the research team. Inclusion criteria were self-reported regular engagement in gaming activities, enabling a targeted investigation into the interplay between GD, depression, and anxiety. Exclusion criteria included a self-reported history of psychiatric diagnoses, implemented to avoid confounding from pre-existing psychiatric conditions.

This two-wave longitudinal design assessed participants at two distinct developmental stages: late adolescence (T₀, May 2020), when they were in their second year of senior high school, and emerging adulthood (T₁, April 2023), after transitioning to college, employment, or other adult roles. The same set of validated psychological measures was administered at both time points.

All participants provided informed consent before completing the survey. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology, Renmin University of China (IRB Approval No. 23–035). Participants were informed about the voluntary nature of the study, the confidentiality of their responses, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Descriptive and attrition analyses

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize participant characteristics, and psychological symptoms at both time points. As the survey was administered via an online platform that required completion of all items prior to submission, there were no item-level missing data within each wave. Attrition was defined as failure to participate in the second wave of data collection. To evaluate potential attrition bias, t-tests and chi-square analyses compared baseline demographic and psychological variables between participants retained at T₁ and those lost to follow-up. In addition, network stability analysis was conducted to assess the potential influence of random attrition on centrality indices.

Measures

Gaming disorder

GD was assessed using the Chinese version of the Gaming Disorder Scale–Short-Form (GD-9) [35]. The GD-9 is a 9-item self-report scale based on the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for gaming disorder. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “never” to 5 = “very often”), with higher scores indicating greater GD severity. In this study, the internal consistency of the IGDS9-SF was α = 0.86 at T₀ and α = 0.89 at T₁, indicating high reliability across both time points.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Chinese version of the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [36], a widely used self-report instrument for assessing major depressive disorder symptoms based on the DSM-IV diagnostic criteria. Each of the 9 items corresponds to one criterion and is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. In this study, the internal consistency of PHQ-9 was α = 0.88 at T₀ and α = 0.90 at T₁, confirming high reliability over time.

Anxiety

Generalized anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) [37]. The GAD-7 is a brief self-report measure based on the DSM-IV criteria for generalized anxiety disorder. Each of the 7 items is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. In this study, the internal consistency of GAD-7 was α = 0.89 at T₀ and α = 0.91 at T₁, confirming strong reliability across both time points.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R using the qgraph, bootnet, NetworkComparisonTest, and networktools packages [38–41]. The R code is available in Supplement 1.

Network estimation

Prior to network estimation, longitudinal measurement invariance of the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and GD-9 across T₀ and T₁ was examined using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis with the WLSMV estimator. Configural and metric invariance were supported for all scales, as indicated by acceptable model fit and negligible changes in fit indices (ΔCFI ≤ 0.001; ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.013; ΔSRMR ≤ 0.007; see Supplementary Table S1). These results supported the comparability of symptom indicators across time. Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated approximate univariate normality for all symptom indicators at both time points (all p > 0.05), and node redundancy was examined using the goldbricker function. Together with measurement invariance testing, adequate sample size, and bootstrap-based assessments of edge accuracy and centrality stability, these procedures supported the appropriateness of Gaussian Graphical Model estimation [18]. Gaussian Graphical Models were then estimated separately for T₀ and T₁ to model the network structure of psychological symptoms during late adolescence and emerging adulthood, respectively. To ensure a sparse yet interpretable network, we used the EBICglasso regularization method [42] with a default hyperparameter of γ = 0.5 for both T₀ and T₁ networks. Only edges with nonzero weights were retained in the final network. Following Epskamp et al. [39], networks with 25 nodes typically require a sample size of more than 500 to ensure reliable estimation of edge weights and centrality indices.

Centrality analysis

To assess changes in symptom importance, we computed Expected Influence (EI) and Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) to capture overall symptom impact and cross-cluster symptom connectivity. EI represents the sum of all direct connections a symptom has with others, incorporating both positive and negative associations [43]. In psychopathology networks, where symptoms can mutually reinforce or inhibit each other, higher EI values indicate greater centrality and potential as intervention targets [44]. BEI extends this concept by quantifying the extent to which a symptom connects distinct symptom communities, thereby facilitating the transmission of distress across domains [45]. BEI was computed as the sum of each symptom’s edges with nodes outside its own community. Symptoms with high BEI are critical cross-cluster connectors and may represent leverage points for disrupting comorbidity pathways. To compare EI and BEI between time points, we applied a non-parametric bootstrapped difference test.

Accuracy and stability estimation

To assess the precision of edge weight estimates, we applied nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 resamples) to generate 95% confidence intervals for both T₀ and T₁ networks. Centrality stability was evaluated using case-dropping bootstrapping to examine the robustness of EI and BEI across time points. The centrality stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) quantifies the proportion of the sample that can be dropped while retaining a correlation of 0.7 or higher with the original centrality rankings; values above 0.25 indicate acceptable stability [39].

Network comparison

The Network Comparison Test was used to statistically compare the T₀ and T₁ networks. This permutation-based approach (1,000 iterations) evaluates differences in global connectivity, individual edge weights, and node-level centrality while accounting for sampling variability. Changes in overall symptom interconnectivity were examined via the Global Expected Influence test, where higher EI values at T₁ indicate stronger overall connectivity. Bootstrapped difference tests were applied to compare EI and BEI across time points, and edge weight difference tests identified symptom pairs with significantly altered associations. Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Centrality analysis

In the T₀ network, the most influential symptom based on EI was GAD1 (nervousness, EI = 1.23, 95% CI [0.99, 1.47]), followed by GD6 (persistent gaming; EI = 1.11, [0.84, 1.37]) and PHQ6 (worthlessness; EI = 0.98, [0.72, 1.23]). By T₁, the ranking had shifted, with GAD5 (restlessness, EI = 1.22, [1.04, 1.40]) ranking highest, followed by GAD3 (excessive worry, EI = 1.19, [0.99, 1.40]) and GD3 (tolerance; EI = 1.08, [0.91, 1.24]) (Fig. 2).

For BEI, the top bridging symptom in the T₀ network was GAD1 (0.50, 95% CI [0.25, 0.74]), followed by PHQ6 (0.41, [0.21, 0.62]) and PHQ9 (suicidal thoughts; 0.35, [0.10, 0.61]). In the T₁ network, GAD5 (0.64, [0.38, 0.89]) emerged as the strongest bridge, followed by PHQ9 (0.62, [0.37, 0.87]) and PHQ6 (0.50, [0.25, 0.75]) (Fig. 2).

Difference tests indicated that GAD1 had significantly higher EI at T₀, whereas GAD3 and GAD5 dominated at T₁. For BEI, GAD1, GAD5, PHQ6, and PHQ9 consistently exhibited greater cross-cluster influence across both time points (Supplementary Figures S1–S2).

Accuracy and stability test

Edge weight accuracy analysis indicated that most symptom-to-symptom connections remained stable across bootstrapped samples, suggesting low estimation variability. Both the T₀ and T₁ networks exhibited a narrow range of edge weight fluctuations (− 0.2 to 0.4), indicating high certainty in symptom associations (Supplementary Figure S3). Centrality stability analysis (Supplementary Figure S4) showed that EI and BEI demonstrated acceptable reliability. Case-dropping bootstrapping revealed that the centrality stability coefficient was 0.36 for EI and 0.36 for BEI at T₀, and 0.36 for EI and 0.44 for BEI at T₁. All coefficients exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.25 (Epskamp et al., 2018), indicating moderate stability across time points.

Network comparison test

The global network invariance test indicated no significant overall structural difference between T₀ and T₁ (M = 0.28, p = 0.37). The global strength invariance test also revealed no significant difference in overall connectivity between time points (ΔS = 2.95, p = 0.18). In contrast, the global EI test showed a significant change across waves (increase = 1.28, p = 0.02). Centrality invariance testing identified notable shifts in symptom importance: GAD3 and GAD5 demonstrated significant EI increases of 0.58 and 0.57, respectively (both p < 0.01), while GD3 increased by 0.26 (p = 0.02) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study examined structural changes in the comorbidity network of GD, depression, and anxiety across the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood using network analysis. Grounded in a developmental network psychopathology perspective, this transition is conceptualized as a developmentally sensitive window during which symptom networks are expected to reorganize rather than remain structurally static. At the global network level, while overall network strength remained stable across time, global EI showed a significant longitudinal increase. This pattern suggests that developmental changes were driven less by changes in the overall magnitude of symptom connectivity and more by a reorganization of the functional structure of symptom interactions. The results offer insights into how emotional and behavioral symptoms become more intertwined during this pivotal developmental stage.

Convergent findings on stable emotional–cognitive vulnerabilities

Our results align with previous adolescent network studies in identifying consistent transdiagnostic vulnerabilities across developmental stages. In particular, worthlessness (PHQ6) and suicidal ideation (PHQ9) consistently exhibited high BEI values, both in our study and in prior literature [46, 47]. These symptoms represent enduring cognitive–emotional vulnerabilities that persist into emerging adulthood and are especially potent in linking anxiety and depression domains. Their repeated emergence across culturally diverse samples (UK, US, China) supports the existence of a core transdiagnostic architecture that consolidates during mid-to-late adolescence [48–50].

Our T₁ network also revealed increasing centrality of cognitive anxiety symptoms, such as excessive worry (GAD3) and restlessness (GAD5). This pattern is consistent with findings from Gao et al. and Konac et al. [27, 47], which documented a developmental shift from somatic to cognitive anxiety expressions. Such a shift likely reflects normative developmental processes during this sensitive period, including maturation of abstract reasoning and metacognitive capacity, which foster more internalized and persistent worry patterns [51, 52]. Concurrently, behavioral coping strategies such as gaming may lose some of their regulatory utility as individuals face greater cognitive and social demands, further contributing to the relative decline in the centrality of behavioral symptoms.

From coupled emotion–gaming links to strengthened internal emotional networks

Our longitudinal analysis revealed marked reorganization in the integration between gaming-related and emotional symptom domains from late adolescence to emerging adulthood. A defining feature was the strengthening of intra-cluster connections among depression and anxiety symptoms alongside a weakening of inter-cluster links between gaming disorder nodes and emotional symptoms. For instance, the edges PHQ6–GAD3 and PHQ9–GAD7 became stronger, and these nodes simultaneously exhibited increases in EI and BEI, indicating that they became more central and more influential across symptom clusters. In contrast, gaming-related nodes GD5 (interest loss) and GD6 (persistent gaming) showed decreased BEI, reflecting a reduced role in bridging across depression and anxiety symptoms. It indicates a reorganization in the coupling of emotional and behavioral symptom domains [53, 54]. This reorganization is consistent with neurodevelopmental changes during this life stage, which have been associated with enhanced top-down control and metacognitive capacity, potentially shifting symptom dynamics away from externally anchored behavioral regulation toward internally sustained cognitive–affective processes [2, 51].

From physiological reactivity to internalized dysregulation in cognitive–affective symptoms

We found that from late adolescence to emerging adulthood, anxiety and depression symptoms shifted from outward physiological and reactive expressions to more internally focused, cognitively elaborated, and self-reinforcing patterns.

In the anxiety subnetwork, GAD1 (nervousness) had relatively high centrality at T₀, reflecting diffuse somatic arousal triggered by immediate stressors [55]. By T₁, GAD1 declined in prominence, while GAD3 (excessive worry) and GAD5 (restlessness) became dominant anxiety nodes, both increasing in EI and serving as major bridges to depression. GAD3 reflects the internalization of anxiety through ruminative anticipation of negative events, whereas GAD5 represents the somatic manifestation of cognitive overload [55]. The emergence of GAD7 (catastrophizing) as a bridging symptom further supports a shift toward heightened cognitive threat appraisal. As individuals mature, stressors increasingly stem from identity formation, decision-making under uncertainty, and long-term planning [56], fostering anxiety that is less reactive and more anticipatory, maintained by uncontrollable thought loops rather than immediate environmental triggers [57, 58].

In the depression subnetwork, a comparable transition was observed. PHQ3 (sleep disturbance) and PHQ4 (fatigue), previously peripheral in adolescent networks, emerged as bridge symptoms at T₁. Fatigue and low energy reflect both somatic and cognitive–affective signals [59, 60]. Sleep disturbance indicates hyperarousal impairing emotion regulation driven by intrusive thoughts and worry loops [61]. Beyond within-domain shifts, the emergence of a PHQ1–GAD5 edge illustrates the shift from physiological reactivity to internalized dysregulation. While restlessness reflects sustained internal activation, anhedonia captures motivational exhaustion. Their coupling suggests a self-maintaining cognitive–affective state characterized by low reward sensitivity and persistent internal tension.

Taken together, these changes suggest a developmental reorientation in emotion regulation—from externally reactive and somatic strategies in adolescence to internally recursive and cognitively burdensome processes in emerging adulthood.

From emotion regulation to compensation in emotion–gaming networks

The gaming symptom network exhibited a shift toward compensatory patterns in emerging adulthood. Within the gaming domain, centrality moved from GD6 (persistent gaming) in adolescence to GD3 (tolerance) in emerging adulthood, accompanied by stronger connections between GD3, GD4 (loss of control), and GD5 (loss of interest). Beyond the general weakening of links between gaming and emotional symptoms, a negative edge appeared between GD5 and GAD6 (irritability). This pattern suggests that decreased gaming interest was less closely associated with irritability and may reflect reduced affective reactivity, pointing to a more compensatory pattern of gaming behavior during emerging adulthood. This pattern aligns with the I-PACE model, which predicts that persistent digital engagement despite diminished emotional rewards reflects the replacement of adaptive regulation by habitual behaviors [53, 62]. Emerging adulthood involves stage-specific shifts in cognitive, emotional, and behavioral systems, necessitating more sophisticated self-regulation strategies such as cognitive reappraisal, self-directed motivation, and tolerance of uncertainty [63]. Continued reliance on external, stimulus-driven strategies like gaming, particularly when compulsive or avoidant, may be ineffective and instead amplify frustration, guilt, and self-criticism [64]. This pattern may reflect a transitional rupture in emotion regulation development, in which lower-level behavioral avoidance strategies lose adaptive value.

Together, these changes reflect a developmental transition in gaming behaviors—from primarily serving as emotion regulation in adolescence to being characterized by habitual or tolerance-driven use in emerging adulthood.

A developmental pattern of external coping transitioning to internal entrapment

Building on the above findings, these network-level shifts reflect not isolated symptom fluctuations but a coherent reorganization of comorbidity architecture across the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood. The network appears to shift from a behaviorally anchored, emotionally reactive configuration to a cognitively dominated, internally entrapped structure.

In late adolescence, comorbidity patterns are shaped by externalized regulation: emotional distress readily links to behavioral coping (e.g., gaming), anxiety is expressed in diffuse physiological terms, and fatigue appears as a secondary outcome of mood disturbance. These patterns fit a developmental context in which external feedback, immediate relief, and low-effort strategies are often sufficient and adaptive.

By emerging adulthood, the network undergoes reorganization. Gaming functions as a compensatory or maladaptive strategy; anxiety becomes anticipatory and cognitively mediated; fatigue shifts from consequence to cause. These changes converge into a triadic cognitive–affective–behavioral entrapment: cognitive overload (e.g., excessive worry, catastrophizing) sustains emotional loops; affective depletion (e.g., self-criticism, fatigue) undermines resilience; and maladaptive behaviors (e.g., compulsive gaming) persist despite diminished reward, reinforcing dysfunction.

This pattern marks a developmental inflection point where earlier coping scripts collapse before more advanced regulatory capacities are fully established—a vulnerable period in which the symptom network intensifies and reorganizes into rigid, self-perpetuating loops.

Implications for developmentally informed intervention

This study underscores the need for developmentally sensitive mental health interventions during the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood.

First, emotion regulation interventions should target the intensifying anxiety–depression loops observed in this transition. Consistent with our network findings, GAD3 and GAD5 emerged as EI-central symptoms linking anxiety and depression, indicating strong connectivity with other symptoms in the network. These findings suggest that interventions targeting processes reflected by these symptoms—such as cognitive hyperarousal and persistent worry—could potentially have cascading effects across the symptom network. The escalation of cognitive hyperarousal is consistent with reduced regulatory flexibility during this developmental transition. School-based programs integrating cognitive restructuring, worry postponement, and mindfulness-based training can equip students to manage intrusive thoughts and restore emotional balance [65, 66]. These can be embedded into academic stress workshops, orientation programs, or general education psychology curricula.

Second, digital behavior interventions may benefit from framing problematic gaming as an attempted form of emotion regulation that becomes increasingly ineffective at providing emotional relief. Given the modest EI increase for GD3, tolerance may be better understood as a secondary indicator of changing gaming-related symptom organization, rather than as a stand-alone intervention target. Structured approaches such as digital hygiene education, behavioral activation toward offline engagement (e.g., sports, volunteering, peer mentorship), and goal-setting may reorient reward systems and reduce reliance on gaming for mood regulation [67].

Third, suicide prevention efforts should strengthen self-compassion to counteract affective vulnerabilities. Symptoms reflecting PHQ6 and PHQ9, which showed high EI/BEI in our network, represent critical affective hubs. Interventions such as compassion-focused therapy and mindful self-compassion could be adapted into psychoeducational group formats or counseling services [68, 69]. These approaches target shame-based schemas and foster internal safety, particularly for students under high-performance pressure.

Fourth, campus mental health services may benefit from complementing symptom-based diagnosis with structural risk profiling informed by network analysis. Periodic mental health monitoring using validated tools (e.g., DASS-21, IGDS9-SF, GAD-7), paired with basic network analytics, can identify high-impact bridge symptoms and guide tiered interventions—ranging from universal education to targeted workshops and individual referrals.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s key strength lies in its three-year longitudinal symptom network design, enabling the examination of dynamic changes in comorbidity structures during the transition from late adolescence to emerging adulthood. By moving beyond categorical diagnoses or mean-level changes, our approach reveals shifts in the architecture of psychopathology, including the reorganization of central and bridge symptoms that sustain emotional–behavioral comorbidity. The use of Gaussian Graphical Models and Network Comparison Tests adds methodological rigor, allowing detection of subtle yet clinically relevant alterations in symptom connectivity.

Conceptually, the study bridges developmental theory and network psychopathology, illustrating how normative life transitions such as increasing autonomy, identity formation, and academic pressure may not only elicit distress but also reorganize symptom interrelations. This integration offers a theoretically grounded account for the emergence, consolidation, or attenuation of specific comorbidity patterns in emerging adulthood.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the two-wave design captures broad developmental shifts but provides a coarse temporal resolution, and the three-year assessment interval may have introduced unmeasured environmental or contextual changes—such as life transitions, academic, or social factors—potentially affecting the interpretability of observed network reorganization. The developmental interpretations proposed in this study are inferential. Although the longitudinal network design can describe changes in symptom connectivity across developmental periods, it cannot directly test the psychological mechanisms underlying these changes. Second, the reliance on self-report measures may introduce reporting bias and symptom overlap, and observed changes could partly reflect shared method variance or unmeasured confounders. Third, the sample comprised Chinese adolescents and young adults, which may limit generalizability. In addition, network models are inherently sample-dependent, potentially affecting reproducibility. Further cross-cultural replication and robustness testing are needed to confirm the reliability of central and bridging symptoms. Despite these limitations, our findings underscore that psychological comorbidity is structurally dynamic and highlight the importance of both developmental and intervention timing.

Conclusion

This three-year longitudinal network analysis revealed reorganization in the comorbidity structure of GD, depression, and anxiety from late adolescence to emerging adulthood. Cognitive–affective symptoms, including excessive worry, restlessness, self-criticism, and suicidal ideation, emerged as central and bridging nodes, while links between gaming-related emotional and behavioral symptoms weakened. These patterns suggest a developmental shift from externally reactive, behavior-based coping in adolescence to internally sustained, cognitively driven symptom networks in emerging adulthood. The findings highlight core cognitive–affective symptoms as potential targets for preventive or early interventions, emphasizing the importance of addressing maladaptive symptom loops during this transitional period to mitigate long-term psychopathological risk.
