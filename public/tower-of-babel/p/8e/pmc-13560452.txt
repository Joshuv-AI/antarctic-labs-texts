Bulk and single-cell transcriptome-based integrative analysis of an rRNA processing-related prognostic signature with functional validation of DDX47 in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Jianlin Liu, Hengxing Tan, Jiaqing Yang, Jialei Zhong, Qiang Zhou, Ju Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) exhibits pronounced molecular heterogeneity and variable clinical outcomes, complicating prognosis and treatment. While many prognostic models exist, signatures centered on ribosomal RNA (rRNA) processing remain underexplored. This study aimed to establish a prognostic signature associated with rRNA processing and to investigate the functional role of DDX47 in ccRCC.MethodsrRNA processing-related genes were curated from Gene Ontology. Prognostic candidates were identified via differential expression and Cox regression in the TCGA-KIRC cohort. A total of 101 machine-learning model combinations were evaluated, and the optimal approach was used to develop the RPPS, with its predictive performance further confirmed in the E-MTAB-1980 and GSE167573 datasets. Prognostic accuracy was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier, ROC, Cox regression, calibration, and decision curve analyses. Immune features, drug sensitivity, and immune escape potential were assessed. Single-cell datasets were integrated to compute an rRNA processing activity score (RPAS), followed by cell–cell communication and pseudotime analyses. DDX47 was knocked out in B cells via scTenifoldKnk, and the resulting perturbed pathways were investigated. DDX47 function was examined in 786-O and A498 cells via siRNA knockdown, Western blotting, EdU, and Transwell assays.ResultsThe eight-gene RPPS effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, yielding significantly distinct survival outcomes across all cohorts. The Riskscore independently predicted overall survival and enhanced a nomogram incorporating Age and Stage. High-risk patients showed increased immune infiltration, higher TIDE scores, distinct mutation patterns, and lower predicted IC50 values for selected TKIs. RPAS-low tumor cells displayed enhanced SPP1-mediated communication. Given its high B-cell expression, virtual knockout of DDX47 via scTenifoldKnk revealed enrichment of perturbed genes in immune-related processes, suggesting that DDX47 may be involved in immune-related regulatory programs. High DDX47 expression predicted poor prognosis, and its knockdown suppressed ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ConclusionThe RPPS provides a potential tool for individualized prognostic assessment and molecular stratification in ccRCC. RPAS-defined tumor-cell states may contribute to ccRCC progression through distinct mechanisms, while DDX47 may serve as a candidate prognostic biomarker and warrants further mechanistic investigation in ccRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16562-6.

Introduction

CcRCC is the most common histological subtype of renal cell carcinoma and accounts for the majority of kidney malignancies [1]. Although advances in surgery, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved treatment options, the heterogeneity, metastatic potential, and therapeutic resistance of ccRCC continue to complicate clinical management [2–4]. Many patients present with advanced or metastatic disease, and therapeutic resistance frequently limits durable efficacy. Therefore, identifying biomarkers that reflect ccRCC biology and inform prognosis remains an important translational objective.

Ribosome biogenesis is among the most energetically demanding processes in eukaryotic cells [5]. In the nucleolus, this process involves precursor rRNA (pre-rRNA) transcription, chemical modification, cleavage, folding, ribosomal protein incorporation, and export of mature ribosomal subunits [6, 7]. Post-transcriptional rRNA processing and modification are essential steps in this workflow [8–10]. During rapid proliferation, cancer cells often increase rRNA maturation and ribosome assembly to meet elevated protein-synthesis demands [11]. Accordingly, dysregulation of rRNA processing-related genes may contribute to malignant growth, stress adaptation, and genomic instability [12].

Abnormal expression of rRNA processing-related genes has been linked to tumor development, progression, and prognosis in several solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. DEAD-box RNA helicases, nucleolar proteins, and small nucleolar RNA-binding proteins participate in rRNA precursor processing and may acquire oncogenic functions in cancer [13–15]. Although ribosome biogenesis has been preliminarily investigated in ccRCC [16], the prognostic and biological relevance of rRNA processing-related genes in ccRCC remains insufficiently characterized.

DDX47 is a DEAD-box RNA helicase that mainly localizes to the nucleolus and participates in pre-rRNA processing and small ribosomal subunit assembly [17]. Recent studies have associated DDX47 with several malignancies, including lung adenocarcinoma and prostate cancer [18, 19]. However, its prognostic value and functional role in ccRCC remain unclear.

In this study, we evaluated rRNA processing-related genes in ccRCC at the bulk transcriptomic, single-cell transcriptomic, and functional levels. We integrated multiple cohorts and compared 101 machine-learning algorithm combinations to construct and evaluate an RPPS for ccRCC. We further used single-cell RNA sequencing to quantify RPAS and characterize cellular heterogeneity related to rRNA processing activity and tumor microenvironmental remodeling. Finally, DDX47 was selected for in vitro validation to examine its involvement in ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Materials and methods

Data retrieval and preprocessing

Transcriptome profiles from 529 tumor samples and 72 normal samples, together with clinical survival information for 525 tumor samples, were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma cohort (TCGA-KIRC). Gene expression and matched clinical data for 55 tumor samples were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession GSE167573 [20], and transcriptomic data and matched clinical data for 101 tumor samples were retrieved from the ArrayExpress dataset E-MTAB-1980 [21].

Single-cell RNA sequencing datasets GSE210038 and GSE156632, each containing seven patients with ccRCC, were downloaded for downstream analyses [22, 23]. Quality control and normalization of raw expression matrices were conducted with the Seurat package. Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 4000 detected genes, or with a mitochondrial gene fraction exceeding 10% in GSE156632 and 20% in GSE210038, were excluded. Potential doublets were removed with scDblFinder. The data were normalized, highly variable genes were extracted, and principal component analysis was then conducted. Batch effects among datasets were mitigated with Harmony, after which the merged single-cell profiles were clustered and displayed using UMAP. Cell populations were defined using canonical markers.

Acquisition of RPRGs, differential expression analysis, and screening of candidate genes for RPPS construction

All genes annotated to rRNA processing were collected from the Gene Ontology term “rRNA processing” (GO:0006364). Raw count data from the TCGA-KIRC cohort were used for differential expression analysis with DESeq2 [24]. Before statistical testing, low-expression genes were filtered out by retaining genes with a mean raw count greater than 5 across all samples. Differentially expressed genes between ccRCC tumor samples and normal tissues were then identified using DESeq2, with thresholds set at |log₂ fold change| > 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05. The intersection between the differentially expressed genes and the collected RPRGs was used as the initial gene set for construction of the rRNA processing-related prognostic signature.

Development of a prognostic signature and its validation

The TCGA-KIRC cohort was used as the training set. Univariate Cox regression analysis was initially performed to screen differentially expressed rRNA processing-related genes (RPRGs) that were significantly associated with overall survival (OS) (P < 0.05). To reduce data-platform effects, expression profiles from multiple cohorts were adjusted with the removeBatchEffect function in the limma package and then standardized by Z-score transformation [25, 26].

Model training and feature selection were performed with 101 combinations of 10 machine-learning algorithms. A total of ten machine learning algorithms were employed in this study: Random survival forest (RSF), CoxBoost, Ridge regression, elastic net (Enet), LASSO, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), and stepwise Cox regression, generalized boosted regression modeling (GBM), survival support vector machine (survivalSVM), and supervised principal components (SuperPC). The optimal signature was selected by comparing the mean C-index across training and validation cohorts. Genes retained by the final model were defined as the RPPS genes, and each patient received a Riskscore derived from the model.

The cutoff value derived from the training cohort by the surv_cutpoint function was used as the risk-score threshold for patient stratification across all cohorts. Accordingly, patients were assigned to either high- or low-risk groups. Survival differences between groups were compared using Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests. The prognostic accuracy of the model was further evaluated by time-dependent ROC analysis with the timeROC package, with AUCs calculated at 1, 3, and 5 years. Finally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were applied to investigate whether the risk score was associated with clinical factors such as age, sex, stage, and grade.

Establishment and assessment of the nomogram

To improve clinical usability, variables that remained statistically significant in Cox regression analyses were incorporated into a nomogram with the rms package. To provide individualized survival prediction, a nomogram was constructed for estimating OS at 1, 3, and 5 years. Calibration curves were used to examine the agreement between model-based predictions and observed survival outcomes. In addition, decision curve analysis was applied to compare the clinical utility of the nomogram with that of individual clinical factors over a range of threshold probabilities.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune-cell infiltration was inferred by seven complementary algorithms, including EPIC, XCell, MCPCOUNTER, QUANTISEQ, CIBERSORT-ABS, CIBERSORT, and TIMER. The relative abundance and composition of immune-cell subsets were contrasted between patients classified as high‑risk versus low‑risk. Associations between RPPS Riskscore and immune-cell abundance were also analyzed. Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were computed from bulk transcriptomic data using the ESTIMATE package [27]. Differences in these scores between the high- and low-risk groups were subsequently evaluated. We further applied the tidepy package in Python to perform TIDE analysis on the high- and low-risk groups, assessing the potential immune escape levels in each group [28].

Characterization of the somatic mutational landscape

The TCGA database was used as the source of somatic mutation data for ccRCC [29]. Maftools was employed to depict and compare somatic mutation patterns between the high- and low-risk groups. Mutated genes were displayed in waterfall plots, and genes showing mutual exclusivity or co-occurrence were explored within each risk group. By integrating the median TMB level with RPPS risk stratification, patients were assigned to four combined groups. Kaplan-Meier curves were subsequently generated, and log-rank tests were applied to compare survival outcomes among the different categories.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The oncoPredict package was applied to estimate drug response. Gene expression values in transcripts per million and corresponding half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values from cancer cell lines in the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP v2) were used as the training resource [30, 31]. Drug response in transcriptomic samples was predicted after TPM transformation and empirical Bayes correction for batch effects. Representative TKIs were selected for comparison, and differences in predicted sensitivity between the high- and low-risk groups were evaluated. Molecular docking simulations of DDX47 with sunitinib and cabozantinib were performed using AutoDock Vina to explore potential binding affinity [32].

Single-cell transcriptomic RPPS scoring and functional analysis

For each individual cell, the AddModuleScore function from the Seurat package was applied to calculate RPAS scores based on the RPPS gene signature. The resulting RPAS values were visualized across cells with violin plots, and the distribution of RPPS genes was also depicted. Cells were then stratified into high- and low-RPAS groups according to the median RPAS score. CellChat was utilized to investigate the ligand–receptor interactions between tumor cells with distinct RPAS levels and other cell types [33]. Next, tumor cells were extracted using Monocle2 and subjected to pseudotime analysis to reconstruct differentiation trajectories [34]. Finally, ClusterGVis was employed to identify dynamic gene modules along the pseudotime trajectory, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted for each branch. In addition, we utilized scTenifoldKnk to knock out DDX47 in B cells, and GO enrichment analysis was performed to reveal the perturbed pathways after knockout [35].

Cell culture

The human clear cell renal cell carcinoma cell lines 786-O and A498 were supplied by Zhengzhou Weisai Biotechnology. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. RPMI-1640 medium was used for 786-O cells, whereas MEM was used for A498 cells, with both culture media containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were passaged regularly during culture.

Cell transfection

Three specific small interfering RNAs (si-DDX47-1, si-DDX47-2, and si-DDX47-3) targeting the coding region of DDX47, together with a negative control scrambled sequence (si-Control), were synthesized by Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology. The siRNA sequences are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Table 1). 786-O and A498 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into six‑well plates and upon reaching 60–70% confluency, were transiently transfected with the indicated siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h, knockdown efficiency at the protein level was determined by Western blotting, and the siRNA exhibiting the most pronounced suppression was selected for subsequent functional experiments. The primary antibodies applied were rabbit anti‑human DDX47 polyclonal antibody (17795-1-AP, 1:1000) and mouse anti‑human GAPDH monoclonal antibody (60004-1-Ig, 1:5000).

EdU proliferation assay

The influence of DDX47 silencing on ccRCC cell proliferation was evaluated using EdU incorporation. Transfected 786-O and A498 cells were plated in 96-well plates. After attachment and growth to an appropriate density, EdU working solution was added according to the kit instructions and cells were incubated at 37 degrees C with 5% CO2. Cells were subsequently fixed, permeabilized, and subjected to the Click reaction. After DAPI counterstaining, fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and cell proliferative capacity following DDX47 knockdown was assessed by calculating the proportion of EdU-positive cells.

Assessment of cell migration and invasion

Transwell assays were performed to assess cell migration and invasion capacities. For invasion assays, Matrigel was diluted in serum-free medium, applied to the upper chamber, and allowed to solidify at 37 degrees C. Transfected cells were suspended in serum-free medium and placed in the upper chamber, whereas complete medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation, cells remaining on the upper side of the membrane were removed, and cells that migrated or invaded to the lower surface were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted in randomly selected microscopic fields. Migration assays were conducted using the same procedure except that the membrane was not coated with Matrigel.

Statistical analysis

Statistical computing and data visualization were carried out using R (v4.5.2) and Python (v3.9), with GraphPad Prism 10 employed as an additional tool for analysis and figure preparation. Each in vitro experiment was repeated independently three times. P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. Significance is denoted by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

Identification of differentially expressed RPRGs and selection of candidate genes for RPPS development

A total of 236 RPRGs were obtained from the Gene Ontology resource using the rRNA processing term. A total of 9,503 DEGs were detected between ccRCC and normal tissues in the TCGA-KIRC dataset based on the established screening thresholds. Intersecting these genes with the RPRG set yielded 12 differentially expressed RPRGs for subsequent prognostic screening. A heatmap depicted the expression profiles of the top 200 DEGs, while a Venn diagram illustrated the overlap between these DEGs and RPRGs (Fig. 1A‑B). Batch effects were effectively removed, as shown by principal component analysis (PCA) with improved alignment between the training and validation datasets (Fig. 1D).

Construction and evaluation of the RPPS prognostic risk model

Univariate Cox regression analysis of the 12 differentially expressed RPRGs identified eight candidate genes with significant associations with OS (Fig. 1C). Following batch adjustment and data normalization, we evaluated 101 machine-learning algorithm combinations. Among them, the RSF model attained the highest mean C-index of 0.736 in the training and validation cohorts, and was consequently chosen as the best-performing method (Fig. 2A). The RSF model was constructed with 20 nodesize and 3000 trees. The final RPPS contained eight genes: RPS17, DDX47, ISG20, CDKN2A, NOP2, RPS19, RPS28, and RPS14. Variable importance (VIMP) scores derived from the RSF model were used to rank the genes comprising the RPPS (Fig. 2B). Although RPS17, RPS19, RPS28, and RPS14 encode structural proteins of the 40 S small ribosomal subunit, previous studies have shown that small-subunit ribosomal proteins are functionally involved in pre-rRNA processing, 18 S rRNA maturation, and 40 S ribosomal subunit formation; therefore, their inclusion is consistent with the GO-defined rRNA processing-related gene set used in this study [36, 37].

The RPPS model contained both rRNA processing-related regulatory factors and ribosomal structural proteins, suggesting that defects in rRNA maturation and ribosome assembly may jointly shape prognostic biology in ccRCC. Based on the optimal risk-score threshold determined in the TCGA training cohort, patients from all datasets were classified into high- and low-risk groups. In the TCGA-KIRC, E-MTAB-1980, and GSE167573 cohorts, the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer overall survival than the low-risk group (Fig. 2 CEG). Time-dependent ROC curves indicated favorable predictive performance, with AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS of 0.830, 0.841, and 0.877 in TCGA, 0.624, 0.713, and 0.710 in E-MTAB-1980, and 0.608, 0.703, and 0.648 in GSE167573 (Fig. 2 DFH). These results indicate that the RPPS retained prognostic discriminatory ability in independent cohorts, although its predictive performance was attenuated compared with the training cohort and varied across datasets and time points.

Construction of the nomogram and evaluation of its clinical applicability

In both univariable and multivariable Cox regression models, the Riskscore was independently associated with patient outcome in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (Fig. 3A-B). Together with selected clinicopathological variables, the Riskscore was incorporated into a nomogram for individualized prognostic estimation (Fig. 3C). By integrating risk score, age, and tumor stage, the nomogram was developed to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities. The corresponding AUCs were 0.909, 0.868, and 0.891 (Fig. 3D), suggesting improved predictive accuracy over single clinical characteristics (Fig. 3E). The calibration curves showed favorable agreement between model-predicted survival and observed outcomes (Fig. 3F). In addition, decision curve analysis confirmed the higher clinical utility of the nomogram compared with individual clinical variables across broad risk-threshold ranges (Fig. 3G).

Immune infiltration and tumor microenvironment characteristics associated with RPPS model risk stratification

The immune landscape differed substantially between high- and low-risk patients. High-risk tumors were characterized by greater regulatory T-cell infiltration and lower M2 macrophage levels, indicating heterogeneous alterations in immune cell composition (Fig. 4A–B). Consistently, ESTIMATE results demonstrated increased immune and ESTIMATE scores in the high-risk group, suggesting more pronounced immune and stromal infiltration (Fig. 4C). Moreover, higher TIDE scores were detected in high-risk patients, supporting a greater likelihood of tumor immune escape (Fig. 4D).

Fig. 4A, B Immune-cell infiltration profiles estimated by multiple algorithms. C Differences in stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores across risk groups. D TIDE score comparison between risk groups. E Differential expression of immune checkpoint genes according to risk stratification

A, B Immune-cell infiltration profiles estimated by multiple algorithms. C Differences in stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores across risk groups. D TIDE score comparison between risk groups. E Differential expression of immune checkpoint genes according to risk stratification

Immune-checkpoint analysis indicated that CTLA4, PDCD1, and LAG3 were upregulated in the high-risk groups, whereas HAVCR2 expression was lower (Fig. 4E). These genes did not change in a uniform direction, implying that the immune regulatory landscape in the high-risk group is heterogeneous rather than globally activated or suppressed. The high-risk groups were characterized by enhanced immune infiltration, altered immune regulation, and a stronger tendency toward immune evasion.

Somatic mutation landscape analysis

Mutation profiling showed that both risk groups were dominated by alterations in classical ccRCC driver genes, including VHL and PBRM1 (Fig. 5A-B) [29]. However, several differences in mutated genes were evident between groups. Compared with the low-risk groups, high-risk groups showed significantly higher mutation frequencies of BAP1 and SETD2. Because BAP1 and SETD2 are involved in chromatin remodeling, epigenetic regulation, and genome stability, their enrichment in high-risk groups suggests a mutation landscape associated with aggressive disease biology and poor prognosis [38].

Co-mutation analysis indicated a stronger co-occurrence pattern between VHL and PBRM1 in the high-risk groups, suggesting greater mutational complexity (Fig. 5C-D). Survival analysis revealed that patients with elevated TMB experienced significantly poorer OS compared with those exhibiting low TMB levels (Fig. 5E). Combined analysis of RPPS risk and TMB level showed that the high-risk/high-TMB group had the poorest OS, while the low-risk/low-TMB group achieved the best survival outcome (Fig. 5F).

Drug sensitivity prediction

Drug response prediction using the CTRP v2-derived reference dataset identified distinct sensitivity profiles in the two risk groups. The high-risk groups showed lower predicted IC50 values for several clinically relevant TKIs, including sunitinib, sorafenib, cabozantinib, and pazopanib. Because lower predicted IC50 values generally suggest greater computationally inferred drug sensitivity, these findings indicate that patients assigned to the high-risk category by the RPPS may be more likely to respond to selected targeted agents. However, these results should be interpreted as drug-sensitivity predictions rather than direct evidence of therapeutic response. Molecular docking analysis further suggested potential interactions between DDX47 and sunitinib or cabozantinib, with predicted binding energies of -7.7 kcal/mol and − 9.0 kcal/mol, respectively. These docking results are hypothesis-generating and require biochemical validation.

Single-cell RPAS score analysis

After integration of GSE156632 and GSE210038,14 ccRCC samples were analyzed at single-cell resolution. Following quality control, Seurat-based integration, and Harmony batch-effect correction, 70,978 high-quality cells were retained (Fig. 6A). On the basis of known cell‑type‑specific markers and InferCNV analysis (Fig. 6C-D), cells were annotated into nine major populations: T cells, B cells, NK cells, monocytes, macrophages, mast cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and tumor cells(Fig. 6B).

We used violin plots to show the distribution of RPAS in each cell type. RPAS was relatively high in a subset of tumor cells, indicating intratumoral heterogeneity in rRNA processing activity (Fig. 6E). Expression of RPPS genes was heterogeneous across cell populations (Fig. 6F). DDX47 was predominantly detected in NK cells, T cells, B cells, endothelial cells, and tumor cells. NOP2 exhibited a similar distribution pattern but was confined to NK cells, T cells, and endothelial cells. ISG20 was mainly present in NK cells, T cells, and monocytes, whereas CDKN2A expression was largely restricted to T cells. Among the ribosomal protein-encoding genes, RPS14, RPS19, and RPS28 were broadly expressed across diverse cell types. In contrast, RPS17 showed negligible expression in NK cells, T cells, and B cells. These observations indicate that RPPS genes are not restricted to tumor cells and may reflect both tumor-intrinsic states and microenvironmental features.

To investigate whether RPAS-defined tumor cell states were associated with reprogrammed tumor–microenvironment communication, tumor cells were stratified into RPAS-high and RPAS-low groups based on the median RPAS score, and CellChat analysis was conducted (Fig. 7A). The global communication network revealed extensive interactions between RPAS-classified tumor cells and neighboring immune and stromal populations (Fig. 7B). Notably, the SPP1 signaling network was more prominent in RPAS-low tumor cells than in RPAS-high tumor cells, with stronger outgoing signals directed toward multiple recipient cell types, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, monocytes, and mast cells (Fig. 7C). Ligand–receptor analysis further demonstrated that this difference involved several SPP1-mediated interactions, including SPP1‑CD44 and multiple SPP1–integrin pairs, such as SPP1‑ITGAV/ITGB1, SPP1‑ITGAV/ITGB5, SPP1‑ITGA5/ITGB1, SPP1‑ITGA8/ITGB1, and SPP1‑ITGA4/ITGB1 (Fig. 7D).

From a biological perspective, SPP1 is a multifunctional secreted glycoprotein that engages CD44 and a variety of integrin receptors to regulate cell adhesion, migration, immune modulation, and angiogenesis. In ccRCC, SPP1 overexpression has been convincingly linked to enhanced tumor aggressiveness and poor clinical outcome [39, 40]. Our observation that RPAS-low tumor cells exhibit heightened SPP1 signaling output suggests that this subpopulation may represent a communication‑active malignant cell state that establishes extensive paracrine crosstalk with diverse stromal and immune components of the tumor microenvironment through the SPP1‑CD44 and SPP1–integrin axes. SPP1‑CD44 engagement has been reported to foster an immunosuppressive niche by driving M2‑like macrophage polarization, promoting T cell exhaustion, and facilitating the recruitment of myeloid‑derived suppressor cells [41]. In parallel, SPP1‑mediated signaling via integrin receptors such as ITGAV/ITGB1 and ITGAV/ITGB5 can stimulate angiogenesis and endothelial cell activation, thereby providing structural support for tumor progression [42, 43]. Collectively, these findings indicate that RPAS-low tumor cells may contribute fundamentally to ccRCC progression by coordinating immune evasion, vascular remodeling, and stromal activation through enhanced SPP1 signaling output.

We then performed pseudotime analysis to characterize the dynamic relationship between RPAS-defined tumor-cell states. Tumor cells were ordered along a continuous trajectory, with RPAS-low tumor cells mainly distributed toward the early pseudotime region and RPAS-high tumor cells enriched toward later pseudotime stages (Fig. 7E). Dynamic gene-module analysis identified two major transcriptional programs along this trajectory (Fig. 7F). The early C2 module was enriched in focal adhesion, proteoglycans in cancer, and TNF signaling pathway, suggesting active extracellular matrix interaction, inflammatory signaling, and microenvironmental communication. In contrast, the later C1 module was enriched in ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, and reactive oxygen species-related pathways, indicating increased translational activity and energy metabolism during pseudotime progression.

Preliminary functional investigation of DDX47

DDX47 had the highest integrated priority because it ranked second in both univariate Cox effect size and RSF-derived VIMP, and was therefore selected as the candidate gene for subsequent investigation. Survival analysis revealed poorer OS in patients with increased DDX47 expression than in those with lower expression levels (Fig. 8A). In addition, drug response prediction focused on DDX47 suggested that high-DDX47 tumors may be more responsive to sunitinib and cabozantinib (Fig. 8B). Docking simulations indicated potential binding between DDX47 and both agents, although these computational findings require direct experimental validation (Fig. 8C). Given that DDX47 is highly expressed in B cells, we knocked out DDX47 in B cells using scTenifoldKnk and found that the perturbed genes were enriched in immune-related biological processes, suggesting that DDX47 may play a critical role in the cellular regulatory network of the immune response (Fig. 8D-E).

Western blotting and densitometric quantification confirmed that si-DDX47-1 and si-DDX47-3 achieved the most efficient DDX47 knockdown in both 786-O and A498 cells, and these two siRNAs were therefore selected for subsequent functional assays (Fig. 9A). Raw Western blot images of DDX47 and GAPDH after DDX47 knockdown in 786-O and A498 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). EdU assays demonstrated that DDX47 knockdown significantly reduced proliferation in both 786-O and A498 cells (Fig. 9B). Transwell migration and invasion assays further showed that depletion of DDX47 markedly impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of these cells (Fig. 9C-D). Collectively, these results support an oncogenic role for DDX47 in ccRCC, indicating that high DDX47 expression may promote proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Discussion

Accurate prognostic assessment and individualized therapy remain major challenges in ccRCC. Although VEGF‑targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors have yielded clinical benefits in select patients, marked heterogeneity in both therapeutic response and long‑term survival persists. This study focused on rRNA processing, a central rate-limiting step in ribosome biogenesis, and integrated multi-cohort transcriptomic data, single-cell RNA sequencing, and functional experiments. We developed and validated an RPPS composed of eight RPRGs and demonstrated that this signature can stratify survival risk, capture features of the tumor microenvironment, and highlight DDX47 as a candidate oncogenic factor.

Aberrant ribosome biogenesis is a defining feature of many tumor cells, and disruption of post-transcriptional rRNA maturation may alter both translational capacity and nucleolar stress responses [44, 45]. The final RPPS included genes involved in rRNA processing and ribosomal structure. This combination suggests that ccRCC progression may be influenced not only by the abundance of mature ribosomes but also by the fidelity of rRNA maturation and assembly. Mechanistically, impaired rRNA processing can compromise the quantity and quality of mature ribosomes, whereas altered ribosomal proteins may disturb pathway balance through ribosome-associated feedback loops. These effects may influence cell-cycle regulation, proliferation, survival, and adaptation to metabolic stress.

The RPPS showed prognostic stratification ability in the TCGA training cohort and in two independent validation cohorts. Patients classified as high risk generally had shorter OS than those classified as low risk, suggesting that the RPPS captured survival-related molecular information. However, the time-dependent ROC results also showed that predictive accuracy was attenuated in the external validation cohorts and varied across follow-up time points. This attenuation may be partly related to the relatively small sample sizes of the external validation cohorts, as E-MTAB-1980 included 101 patients, whereas GSE167573 included 55 patients. Therefore, the RPPS should be interpreted primarily as a prognostic stratification signature rather than a fully optimized clinical prediction model. Further validation in larger, independent, and preferably prospective cohorts is required before its clinical utility can be established. Multivariate analysis confirmed that the Riskscore was independent of conventional clinicopathological parameters such as Age, Gender, Grade, and Stage. Moreover, a nomogram incorporating the RPPS Riskscore improved individualized prediction and showed good calibration and net clinical benefit. These findings suggest that the RPPS captures biological information beyond routine clinical features and may serve as a useful molecular tool for risk assessment.

The single-cell analysis provided further insight into the cellular heterogeneity underlying the RPPS. RPAS values varied across tumor and microenvironmental cell populations, indicating that rRNA processing activity is not restricted to malignant cells but may also reflect broader tumor ecosystem states. Within tumor cells, RPAS-low and RPAS-high populations appeared to represent distinct malignant programs rather than a simple low- versus high-aggressiveness gradient. RPAS-low tumor cells displayed stronger SPP1-mediated communication with stromal and immune cells, involving ligand-receptor interactions such as SPP1-CD44 and multiple SPP1-integrin pairs. Because SPP1 has been implicated in tumor invasion, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and poor outcome in ccRCC and other malignancies [46–48]. RPAS-low tumor cells may represent a communication-active state that promotes microenvironmental remodeling through paracrine interactions with endothelial cells, fibroblasts, monocytes, T cells, and other cell types.

The pseudotime trajectory further suggested that RPAS-high tumor cells were enriched at later stages of tumor-cell progression. Early tumor-cell states were associated with adhesion, extracellular matrix interaction, and inflammatory pathways, whereas later states were enriched in ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, and reactive oxygen species-related pathways. These findings suggest a potential transition from a microenvironment-interactive state to a translationally and metabolically active state. Therefore, RPAS-low and RPAS-high tumor cells may contribute to ccRCC progression through different mechanisms: RPAS-low cells mainly through SPP1-mediated microenvironmental communication, and RPAS-high cells through enhanced ribosome-related and metabolic activity. The present data do not directly determine which state is clinically more aggressive, and further studies linking these cell states to patient outcomes are needed.

DDX47 emerged as a particularly relevant gene within the RPPS. It is a nucleolar DEAD-box RNA helicase involved in pre-rRNA processing and small ribosomal subunit assembly. Bioinformatic analysis showed that high DDX47 expression was linked to unfavorable prognosis in ccRCC. Furthermore, knockdown of DDX47 led to significant reductions in the growth, motility, and invasive capacity of both 786-O and A498 cells. These observations suggest that DDX47 may contribute to malignant behavior in ccRCC. Nevertheless, the current data do not establish that these phenotypic effects are mediated by impaired rRNA processing. Therefore, DDX47 should be interpreted as a candidate oncogenic factor that may influence ribosome-related or nucleolar pathways, and further mechanistic studies are required to clarify its precise role in ccRCC progression.

The B-cell virtual knockout and tumor-cell knockdown experiments should be interpreted as complementary analyses. The scTenifoldKnk analysis suggested that DDX47 perturbation in B cells may influence immune-related regulatory programs, whereas the in vitro knockdown experiments showed that DDX47 depletion suppressed malignant phenotypes in ccRCC tumor cells. These findings suggest that DDX47 may be relevant to both immune-cell-associated regulation and tumor-intrinsic behavior. However, the present data do not establish a direct mechanistic link between B-cell DDX47 perturbation and tumor-cell proliferation, migration, or invasion. Future studies using co-culture systems or cell-type-specific models are needed to clarify whether DDX47-expressing B cells functionally affect ccRCC tumor cells.

Drug sensitivity prediction indicated that high-risk or DDX47-high tumors may be more sensitive to selected TKIs, including sunitinib and cabozantinib. Docking simulations also suggested possible interactions between DDX47 and these agents. However, these findings are computational and hypothesis-generating. Biochemical and pharmacological experiments are required to determine whether these compounds directly interact with DDX47 and whether such interactions influence drug response.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the RPPS showed prognostic stratification ability in independent cohorts, the external validation cohorts were relatively small, and predictive performance varied across datasets and follow-up time points. Larger multicenter and prospective cohorts are therefore needed to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility. Second, the mechanistic role of DDX47 remains incompletely defined, and this study lacked in vivo validation and independent protein-level validation in clinical specimens. Third, the single-cell datasets were obtained from public repositories, and differences in sampling design, sequencing depth, and cell-type annotation may affect generalizability. In addition, the interpretation of RPAS should be cautious. Because RPAS was calculated from the final RPPS gene set, which includes several ribosomal protein genes broadly expressed across cell types, this score may partly reflect general transcriptional or translational activity rather than rRNA processing-specific activity alone. Therefore, RPAS should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of rRNA maturation, but rather as a signature-based score related to rRNA processing and ribosome-associated cellular states. Although two public ccRCC single-cell datasets were integrated in the present study, they were analyzed as a combined cohort rather than as independent discovery and validation datasets. Future studies using additional independent single-cell datasets are needed to confirm the reproducibility and biological specificity of RPAS stratification. Finally, the drug sensitivity and molecular docking analyses were computational predictions, and experimental validation is required before therapeutic implications can be drawn.

Conclusions

This study constructed and validated an RPPS for ccRCC based on rRNA processing-related genes. By integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic data, single-cell RNA sequencing, and functional assays, we showed that the RPPS provides prognostic risk stratification, improves prognostic prediction when combined with clinical features, and reflects biologically meaningful differences in immune infiltration, mutation status, predicted drug sensitivity, and tumor-cell state. Single-cell analysis suggested that rRNA processing activity is heterogeneous across tumor and microenvironmental populations and may be related to tumor progression. Functional experiments further indicated that DDX47 promotes ccRCC-cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Overall, the RPPS may serve as a candidate tool for molecular classification and individualized prognostic assessment in ccRCC. DDX47 may represent a biomarker of aggressive disease and a potential therapeutic target linked to nucleolar function and rRNA processing. Future studies integrating larger clinical cohorts, mechanistic experiments, and treatment-response data are needed to confirm the clinical utility of the RPPS and to clarify whether DDX47 influences ccRCC progression through ribosome-related pathways.
