Macrophage derived VEGF regulates macrophage senescence to inhibit radiation-induced dermatitis
Authors: Rishi Man Chugh, Pooja Gupta-Saraf, Payel Bhanja, Subhrajit Saha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMacrophages are essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and accelerating the repair processes; however, their functionality can be severely compromised in pathological conditions such as radiation-induced dermatitis. In this study we analyzed the role of macrophage derived Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) on regulation of macrophage senescence and its role on radiation-induced skin damage.MethodsWe used bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMɸ) isolated from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl (VEGF-null) and wild-type (WT) mice. Macrophages were exposed to oxidative and genotoxic stress using H₂O₂, doxorubicin, and radiation exposure to evaluate senescence. Senescence was assessed via SA-β-Gal staining and expression of senescence-related genes. Additionally, VEGF receptor inhibition in WT macrophages was performed to determine the role of VEGF/VEGFR signaling in senescence regulation. Phagocytosis and migration assays were conducted to evaluate functional differences. For in vivo analysis, WT and Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice were exposed to radiation, and skin toxicity, histological changes, and senescence markers in skin macrophages were assessed.ResultsVEGF-null macrophages showed increased sensitivity to senescence, with elevated SA-β-Gal staining and upregulated senescence-associated gene expression. WT macrophages treated with a VEGF receptor inhibitor displayed increased senescence-associated markers expression, highlighting the importance of VEGF/VEGF-R signaling in preventing macrophage senescence-like phenotypes. Additionally, VEGF-null macrophages have reduced phagocytic and migratory abilities. Our in vivo study using Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice showed more severe radiation-induced dermatitis, including increased skin toxicity, hyperkeratosis, and elevated senescence-associated markers in skin macrophages compared to WT controls.ConclusionsAbsence of macrophage-derived VEGF leads to heightened macrophage dysfunction and exacerbates radiation-induced dermatitis. Targeting VEGF signaling may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy to mitigate radiation-related skin toxicity and improve patient outcomes during radiation therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07016-x.

Background

Radiation induced skin injury is a major side effect for external beam radiotherapy in cancer patients. Cutaneous radiation injury can occur after total or partial body exposure to radiation, which penetrates deep into underlying tissue [1]. This concern is also pertinent for individuals exposed during a radiological accident or terrorist attack. Experience following the Chernobyl nuclear plant accident highlighted the impact of skin injury on patient prognosis. Radiation affects the skin, leading to a range of conditions including erythema, epilation, desquamation, hyperpigmentation, and erosion, collectively known as radiation dermatitis [2]. Early side effects of radiation exposure, including erythema, edema, inflammation, and moist desquamation which arise over a period of weeks to months, while late complications including atrophy, pigmentation changes, telangiectasia, induration, fibrosis, and ultimately ulceration and necrosis, develops after months, years or even decades [3–5].

Radiation-induced dermatitis is a common and devastating condition that significantly impairs quality of life. Hickok et al. (2005) reported the occurrence and severity of skin toxicity among patients undergoing radiotherapy, identifying skin reactions as one of the most frequent and persistent issues [6]. McQuestion (2011) emphasized the need for evidence-based skin care management to minimize the adverse effects of radiation induced dermatitis [7]. In breast cancer patients, Yee et al. (2018) systematically reviewed the prevalence and severity of radiation-induced skin toxicity, highlighting the growing need for effective management strategies [8]. Similarly, Salvo et al. (2010) reported the prophylaxis and treatment options for acute radiation-induced dermatitis, emphasizing the importance of understanding the biological mechanisms underlying skin injury to improve clinical outcomes [9].

Several factors, such as impaired skin barrier function and modifications of the immune system influence the progression of radiation dermatitis. In cutaneous injury macrophages are known to be involved in inflammation, repair, fibrosis and wound healing. Macrophages have been described to have many functions in skin injury, including host defense, the promotion and resolution of inflammation, the removal of apoptotic cells, and the support of cell proliferation and tissue restoration following injury [10]. Recent studies have demonstrated that macrophages exist in several different phenotypic states within the site of injury, and that influence of these cells on each stage of repair varies with the specific phenotypes [10, 11]. In addition to classical M1/M2 polarization, emerging evidence suggests that senescent macrophages characterized by the acquisition of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), may play critical roles in modulating inflammation and tissue remodeling [12]. However, the role and significance of senescence-associated markers in macrophages in radiation-induced skin injury remain poorly understood.

Macrophages play essential roles in both acute and chronic phases of skin inflammation. In the initial inflammatory response, macrophages contribute to host defense by presenting antigens and releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, thereby promoting the resolution of inflammation [13, 14]. However, prolonged activation or dysregulation of macrophage function can lead to the development of chronic inflammatory conditions, including dermatitis [13, 15, 16]. The exact mechanism of macrophage activation in these processes are not yet fully understood. Further research is essential to clarify the dysregulated mechanism of macrophages activation in Dermatitis. Understanding these mechanisms could allow us to target macrophages with diverse functions for therapeutic purposes, thereby improving disease management and control. Additionally, the phagocytic activity of macrophages is crucial for the clearance of bacteria and other pathogens in inflamed tissue, involving the ingestion and digestion of pathogens and the subsequent elimination of their inactivated products [17]. It has been reported that patients with dermatitis frequently demonstrate deficiency in phagocytic activity in macrophages [18]. This indicates a reduction in chemotactic response and phagocytic activity in in-vitro assay by mononuclear phagocytes in the majority of patients with moderate to severe Atopical Dermatitis [17, 19]. These studies were coherent with the clinical data concerning the higher incidence of infections by pyogenic bacteria and fungi in patients with AD, which are microorganisms that require defense by the phagocytes [20].

Increased expression of senescence-associated markers has been associated with reduced macrophage function including phagocytosis [21–24]. VEGF has been reported to play a significant role in cellular senescence in various diseases, such as retinal pericytes and colorectal cancer [25, 26]. Similarly, the role of senescence in macrophages has been explored in several pathologies, including kidney disease, cancer, and inflammation. It has been demonstrated that cellular senescence in macrophages may drive age-related inflammation in kidney disease [27]. Additionally, macrophage immunosenescence has been linked to various age-related diseases, and recent studies have highlighted its potential as a therapeutic target [28]. Moreover, senescent-like macrophages have been shown to mediate angiogenesis and contribute to endplate sclerosis in male mice via IL-10 secretion [29], and the clearance of these senescent macrophages has been linked to amelioration of tumorigenesis in KRAS-driven lung cancer [30]. Based on these findings, we aimed to investigate the role of VEGF-mediated regulation of macrophage senescence and function in the radiation-induced skin injury.

In the present manuscript we have demonstrated that deletion of VEGF, a key regulator of senescence [25, 31, 32] promotes the expression of senescence-associated markers in irradiated macrophages and also modulates the phagocytic activity of macrophages. Importantly, macrophage-specific deletion of VEGF, using Csf1r-iCre; Vegffl/fl mice model, leads to increased sensitivity to radiation induced dermatitis in-vivo, which is accompanied by a significant reduction in macrophage phagocytic activity. This implicates the involvement of macrophages-specific VEGF signaling in the regulation of radiation-induced dermatitis.

Materials and methods

Mice

To study the role of macrophage-derived VEGF in regulating macrophage function and senescence-associated markers, macrophage-specific VEGF knockout mice was generated by crossing VEGFflox/flox mice with Tg(Csf1r-Mer-iCre-Mer)^1Jwp transgenic mice, which express tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase under the control of the colony-stimulating factor-1 receptor (Csf1r) promoter [33]. This resulted in the generation of Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice, where VEGF was specifically deleted in macrophages. VEGF knockout was induced by subcutaneous tamoxifen injections, which activated the Cre recombinase to excise the floxed Vegfa allele in macrophages. After generating the knockout mice, they were interbred and compared with littermate Cre-negative controls (wild-type) to evaluate the effects of macrophage-specific VEGF deletion on macrophage senescence. All the animals were maintained ad libitum Ad libitum with food and water, and all experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and protocols established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Kansas Medical Center. The animal studies were performed under the animal protocol. (ACUP number 2019–2487).

Isolation and culture of bone-marrow macrophage

Bone-marrow (BM) was isolated from both femurs and tibias of adult females Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice and WT mice. The bones were flushed with Bone-marrow macrophage (BMMɸ) culture media containing αMEM (Gibco) with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% Glutamax as described earlier [34]. The isolated BM cells were plated into 100 mm tissue culture plates with culture media for 24 h. To generate fully differentiated monocyte-derived macrophages non-adherent cells were transferred to petri dishes and cultured for 6–7 days at 37 °C with 5% CO2 with culture media supplemented with 100ng/ml of M-CSF (Cat no. 315-02-250, Peprotech Inc., NJ, USA). Culture medium was changed with the fresh addition of M-CSF twice a week. The cultured BM cells from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice were treated with 4-Hydroxytamoxifen (5 µM) [35] every 2–3 days, to activate the tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase which induces ablation of the macrophage specific VEGF.

Bone-marrow macrophages senescence induction

Cultured fully differentiated BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice were plated in 24 or 6 well plates at a density of 15 × 103 or 1 × 105 cells per well respectively. Cells were allowed to adhere and grow overnight in the incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For induction of senescence, on the following day cells were treated with H202 (100 and 200 µM) [36, 37] or doxorubicin (0.1 µM) [38] in fresh BMMɸ culture media with incubation at 37 °C for three hours. Thereafter, the media was removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS to remove all traces of H202 or doxorubicin. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to senescence assays.

Radiation exposure

All mice used for radiation exposure were shaved on the back, covering an area of approximately 2 × 2.5 cm, one day prior to radiation treatment. Radiation-induced dermatitis in mice was induced using fractionated radiation of 8 Gy per dose for six alternate days using a small animal radiation research platform (XENX; XStrahl) (0.67 mm Cu HVL) at a dose rate of (2.26 Gy/min) at 220 kVp and 13 mA. After the end of radiation doses, mice were under observation to see the development of radiation-induced dermatitis which usually started from 4 to 5 weeks of post-irradiation. N = 6 mice per group was used for radiation induced dermatitis. Radiation-induced senescence in BMMɸ was preformed using 4 Gy and 6 Gy of radiation dose at a dose rate of 2.26 Gy/min. After radiation cells were returned to cell culture incubator for 2 h incubation. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to senescence assays.

Senescence-associated galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay

SA-β-galactosidase activity was detected using a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat no #9860) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, H202 or doxorubicin or either radiation treated BMMɸ were washed with PBS to remove the cell culture media. Cells were then fixed in glutaraldehyde fixative solution for 15–20 min at room-temperature. The fixed cells were then incubated with β-galactosidase staining solution containing X-gal at 37 °C overnight in a dry incubator without CO2. The development of a distinct blue color is an indication of senescence. Cells images were taken using Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope. All the experiments were repeated at least three times with three replicates each time.

Cell proliferation assay

The BMMɸ proliferation assay was performed using a BrdU Cell Proliferation ELISA Kit (Abcam, USA, Cat no. ab126556) according to manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice were plated on 96-well plates and treated with H202 (200 µM) with incubation at 37 °C for three hours before adding BrdU labelled solution. Thereafter, cells were fixed and incubated with pre-diluted anti-BrdU antibody (100 µl) per well for 60 min at room temperature according to the manufacturer protocol. The cells were washed, and peroxidase-labeled antibody was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were washed, and the substrate solution was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min in dark. Absorbance was taken using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite® 200 PRO). The experiments were repeated at least two times with three replicates each time.

Wound-healing assay

BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice were plated in 6 well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well. Cells were allowed to grow until reaching a confluent monolayer culture at 37 °C with 5% CO2, which usually takes 3 days. Upon reaching confluence cells were treated with H202 (200 µM) or doxorubicin (0.1 µM) with incubation at 37 °C for three hours. Parallel cultures were exposed to 4 Gy and 6 Gy irradiation. Thereafter, cells were washed with PBS and the center of cell monolayers were scraped with a sterile 1000 µL pipette tip. Cells were washed three times with PBS to remove dislodged cells, then cultured with medium as described above. Incubate the culture at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and allow them to migrate and close the scratch/wound over time. Photographs were taken to the scratch/wound area at 0, 24 and 48 h using the phase-contrast microscope. Images were analyzed using image analysis software (ImageJ software) to calculate the percentage of wound closure relative to the initial scratch width wound and compared among the different groups as described previously [39]. Scratch assay was performed at least two times with minimum three to five replicates each time.

Phagocytosis assay

A phagocytosis assay was conducted to investigate the effect of senescence on phagocytic activity of BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice. In this experiment we used conjugate particles: pHrodo™ Deep Red E. coli BioParticles™ (Thermofisher Scientific, USA, Cat no. P35360) Conjugate (for flow cytometric analysis). Cells were either treated with H2O2 (200 µM) for 3 h [37] or 4 Gy of irradiation (2.26 Gy/min) [40] before performing the staining for analyzing the phagocytic activity of the macrophages. In brief, after the treatment BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice were incubated with fluorescently conjugated particles to assess their phagocytic activity. The cells were exposed to the particles at a final concentration of 0.1 mg/ml and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C to allow the particles to interact and internalize by the macrophages. The assay was performed according to manufacturer protocol to ensure optimal interaction between the particles and the macrophages. After the incubation period, the cells were washed once with PBS to remove any unbound or non-phagocytosed particles. Flowcytometry data was acquired using BD LSR II flow cytometer instrument with a minimum of 10,000 events were acquired per sample, and flowcytometric analysis was performed using FlowJo software. The experiments were repeated at least two times with replicates each time.

VEGF-VEGFR interaction assay

To analyze the role of VEGF-VEGFR interaction in BMMɸ senescence, WT BMMɸ were treated with VEGF receptor inhibitor BAW2881 (1 µM) (Selleckchem, Cat no. S8189) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C followed by treatment with H202 (200 µM) for three hours at 37 °C incubation. In a separate experiment where Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl BMMɸ were treated with WT BMMɸ conditioned media (CM) for 24 h at 37 °C incubation. After treatment all the different experimental groups were analyzed for senescent-associated maker p16, p21, p53 expression by qPCR (Quant Studio 7) and SA-β-gal-positive cells were captured and counted using EVOS Phase contrast Microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), flowcytometry was performed using BD LSR II flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Quantitative real time PCR

qPCR was performed to determine the senescence-associated marker expression (p16, p21 and p53) in BMMɸ pre-treated with H2O2 (200 µM) or dermatitis skin sorted macrophages from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice. Total RNA was isolated from BMMɸ pre-treated with H2O2 using RNeasy micro/mini kit from Qiagen (Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer instructions. Briefly, cells were suspended in lysis buffer followed by homogenization was performed for complete disruption of the cells. RNA was purified through a series of binding, washing, and elution steps using spin columns. The RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed with the RNA to cDNA EcoDry™ Premix (Double Primed) (Takara Bio USA Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The reaction was carried out for 1 h at 42 °C; followed by a 10 min. incubation at 70 °C to end the reaction cycle.

cDNA was prepared directly from sorted macrophages from dermatitis skin tissue using the Cells-to-cDNA™ CellAmp™ Whole Transcriptome Amplification Kit for RT-PCR (TaKaRa, Cat# 3734), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sorted cells were first lysed to release total RNA, and the lysates were directly subjected to reverse transcription without the need for prior RNA purification. This process allowed efficient synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) from the sorted macrophage population for downstream analysis. qPCR was carried out using the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™, New York, NY, USA) and SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). β-Actin was used as the internal control for sample normalization. Relative gene expression was calculated using ΔΔCt method. A complete list of primers used in this study are mentioned in Supplementary Table 1.

Radiation-Induced skin toxicity scoring

Radiation-induced skin damage was assessed using a scoring system based on the severity of tissue lesions. Lesions were evaluated and scored according to the following criteria: 0 = no lesion, 1 = erythema, 2 = dry desquamation, 3 = wet desquamation, and 4 = erosion or ulceration. Each animal was monitored at regular intervals following radiation exposure, and scores were assigned based on the most severe lesion observed in the irradiated area. Skin toxicity scoring provides a quantitative comparison of radiation-induced dermatitis severity across experimental groups.

Skin histology

At the end of experimental timeline for dermatitis development, mice were euthanized, and the dermatitis skin of each animal was dissected carefully, washed in PBS and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h at room temperature prior to paraffin embedding. The tissue Sect. (5-µm) were placed on glass slides for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and IHC staining. All H&E staining was performed at the Pathology Core Facility at the University of Kansas Cancer Center (Kansas City, KS). Briefly, sections for H&E were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded series of alcohols, and stained with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei, followed by eosin staining to highlight cytoplasmic and extracellular matrix components. Slides were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic evaluation using EVOS Phase contrast Microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Immunohistochemistry analysis

For Ki67 and Caspase-3 staining, deparaffinized and rehydrated with graded series of alcohols, skin sections were incubated overnight with a monoclonal anti-Ki67 antibody (M7240 mib1; Dako) and anti-Caspase antibody (43-7800, Invitrogen). Nuclear staining was detected using streptavidin-peroxidase with diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, followed by counterstained with hematoxylin. Quantification of Ki67 and Caspase-3 positivity was performed by manually counting of Ki67 and Caspase-3 positive cells in at least ten randomly selected high-power fields per section, using image captured with Nikon NIS element 80i microscope. The positive cells were expressed as a percentage of total counted cells per field.

For immunohistochemistry of mouse dermatitis skin to co-stain CD68 with p16 or p21, deparaffinized and rehydrated skin sections were treated for antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling, sections are blocked with 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature followed by incubation with Primary antibodies anti-CD68, anti-p16 and anti-p21 overnight at 4 °C. After washing 3 times with 0.1% TBST, secondary anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 for CD68 and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 for p16 or p21 were added for 1 h and incubated at room temperature in a humidified chamber. After washing, sections were counterstained with DAPI containing mounting media (Vectashield, Cat no. H-1200-10) and imaged using a confocal (Nikon, A1RMP) microscopy. Quantification was performed by manually counting double positive cells in randomly selected ten fields and expressing them as a percentage of the total number of DAPI stained nuclei.

Cell cycle analysis

To confirm the role of senescence in cell-cycle impairment, H202 (200 µM) or doxorubicin (0.1 µM) treated BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl and WT mice were used for Propidium Iodide (PI) cell cycle analysis. After treatment cells were fixed with ice-cold 70% ethanol for 1 h at 4 °C followed by staining with PI solution (final concentration 50 µg/ml) for 30 min at 4 °C in dark. After staining, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer to determine the distribution of cells in different phases of the cell cycle (G0/G1, S, G2/M). The experiments were repeated at least two times with three replicates each time.

Statistics

For comparisons between two groups, 2-sided Student’s unpaired 2-tailed t test was used using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The difference between groups with∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.005, ∗∗∗p < 0.0005, and, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.00005 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Absence of VEGF makes bone marrow derived macrophages more sensitive to H2O2 and doxorubicin induced senescence

Macrophages can acquire the senescence-like phenotype characterized by increased expression of p16 (Ink4a) and elevated β-galactosidase (β-gal) activity at pH 6.0, which impairs the essential macrophage function including phagocytosis. VEGF has been shown to inhibit cellular senescence, suggesting a potential protective role against stress-induced dysfunction [25, 41]. VEGF-null bone marrow macrophages isolated from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice showed significantly higher SA-β-Gal positive staining compared to macrophages from WT mice or Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice without activation of Cre recombinase (without 4-Hydroxytamoxifen treatment) (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) suggesting that absence of VEGF promote senescence. These macrophages, when treated with H2O2 (100 µM and 200 µM) a senescence inducer, show 200 µM of H2O2 induces higher SA β-galactosidase positivity compared to 100 µM of H2O2. (Fig. 1A, B, and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Flow cytometry analysis of SA-β-Gal activity was also performed in 100 µM and 200 µM of H₂O₂ treated WT and Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl BMMɸ. Cells were stained with a fluorescent SA-β-Gal substrate and analyzed using the FITC channel. Fluorescence intensity profiles were normalized to mode for comparative analysis. Treatment with 200 µM H₂O₂ showed higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI: WT, 10991 vs. VEGF null, 14773) resulted in increased SA-β-Gal activity compared to 100 µM (MFI: WT 10583 vs. VEGF null 13945) of H₂O₂ (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Therefore, all the experiments in this study that used H2O2 were all performed with a concentration of 200 µM of H2O2. The BrdU cell proliferation assay shows BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice untreated and treated with H2O2 have significantly reduced proliferation compared to the untreated and treated WT BMMɸ (Fig. 1D). Which suggested that absence of VEGF makes macrophages prone to the expression of senescence-associated markers accompanied by a reduced proliferative capacity.

Next, we analyzed senescence associated gene expression in BMMɸ from Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl or WT mice treated with H2O2 (200 µM) or doxorubicin (0.1 µM). VEGF-null macrophages showed an increased expression of cell cycle arrest associated genes p16, p21 and p53 in response to H2O2 and doxorubicin-induced senescence compared to WT macrophages (Fig. 1E, F, G). The senescent cells are in permanent cell cycle arrest, established and maintained by the expression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs) [42, 43]. qPCR analysis demonstrated that expression of CKIs p21, p53 and p16INK4a were upregulated in H2O2 and doxorubicin treated VEGF-null macrophages compared to WT macrophages (Fig. 1E, F, G). We also analyzed the BMMɸ for cell cycle arrest using PI staining and flow cytometry. The cell cycle analysis revealed the shorter S phase in Untreated VEGF null BMMɸ, The S phase was further decrease after H2O2 or doxorubicin treatment. Which suggested that VEGF null BMMɸ have impaired cell cycle progression compared to the WT BMMɸ (Fig. 1H). Altogether, these data strongly demonstrate that absence of VEGF makes bone marrow derived macrophages more sensitive to H2O2 and doxorubicin induced senescence.

Supplementation of VEGF inhibits senescence in VEGF null macrophages

To further understand the involvement of VEGF in mitigation of macrophage senescence, VEGF null BMMɸ exposed to H2O2 was supplemented with WT BMMɸ conditioned media (CM) for 48 h with media change every 24 h (Fig. 2A). SA-β-Gal staining in these cells shows WT BMMɸ CM significantly reduce senescence (Fig. 2B, C, Supplementary Fig. 4A-C). This result suggests that presence of VEGF in WT BMMɸ CM compensate for the absence of VEGF in the VEGF null BMMɸ, thereby rescuing the normal macrophage functions and characteristics. Flow cytometry results demonstrated that VEGF null BMMɸ, when supplemented with WT CM, exhibited down-regulation of SA-β-Gal FITC signal similar to those of WT BMMɸ (Fig. 2D). qPCR analysis also revealed reduced cell cycle arrest gene expression (p16, p21 and p53) in VEGF null BMMɸ treated with WT BMMɸ CM (Fig. 2E, F). All these results indicates that WT BMMɸ CM effectively restores VEGF null BMMɸ to a WT phenotype, as evidenced by reduced SA-β-Gal staining and confirmed by flow cytometry and qPCR analyses of key senescence-associated markers.

Consistent with the H2O2 and doxorubicin induced senescence, VEGF-null BMMɸ showed higher sensitivity to radiation induced senescence (Fig. 3A, B, C, D, Supplementary 6 A, B and 7 A, B). Expression of p16, p21 and p53 was also upregulated in VEGF-null BMMɸ compared to WT BMMɸ at day 5–10 post-irradiation (Fig. 3E, F, G and Supplementary 7 C-E).

Macrophages expressing increased senescence-associated markers show reduced phagocytotic and migration activity

Phagocytosis of pathogens, apoptotic cells and debris is a key feature of scavenging function of macrophages. To assess whether macrophages expressing increased levels of senescence-associated markers retain their phagocytic activity, we used pHrodo Red E. coli BioParticles in vitro. Macrophages were treated with 6 Gy of radiation dose. After treatment, phagocytic activity was measured using pHrodo Red E. coli BioParticles, which fluoresce upon ingestion by macrophages, enabling the comparison of phagocytic function between senescent and control macrophages. VEGF-null BMMɸ showed reduced phagocytic activity when compared to WT BMMɸ in radiation-induced senescence conditions (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). The migratory properties of cells were evaluated by a wound scratch assay. While WT macrophages almost closed the original scratch width within 24 h (30% gap remaining), the VEGF-null BMMɸ displayed a higher percentage of unclosed wound scratch (60% gap remaining) in an untreated condition (Fig. 3I- and iv). Senescent WT BMMɸ with radiation dose of 4 Gy and 6 Gy migrated significantly slower than the untreated WT macrophages, but faster than the senescent VEGF-null BMMɸ (Fig. 3I-ii and v, I iii and vi). These results suggest that absence of VEGF and increased expression of senescence-associated markers lead to decreased phagocytotic and migration capacities in BM-derived macrophages.

Mice with VEGF null macrophages are more sensitive to radiation-induced dermatitis

Radiation induced dermatitis is one of the major side effects for external beam radiation therapy. Dermatitis is caused mainly due to tissue damage and inflammation and leads to discomfort and pain. Macrophages play a major role in the clearance of antigens by phagocytic activity and resolution of inflammation during dermatitis [13, 44, 45]. It has been reported that patients with dermatitis frequently demonstrate deficiency in phagocytic activity in macrophages. The expression of senescence-associated markers has been implicated in the regulation of macrophage function including phagocytosis [46, 47]. Since our in-vitro studies showed that BMMɸ from the Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl displayed increased expression of senescence-associated markers and demonstrated less migration and phagocytic activity compared to BMMɸ from WT mice, we wanted to investigate the effect of macrophage derived VEGF in radiation induced dermatitis. The Dorsal part in WT and Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice were exposed to irradiation (48 Gy, 6 fractions (8 Gy/fraction/day) [48] (Fig. 4A). At 4 weeks post irradiation Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl mouse developed more severe erythema and desquamation as compared to WT mice (Fig. 4B). A separate study was performed using Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice without activation of Cre recombinase (without Tamoxifen treatment), which did not lead to deletion of VEGF in macrophages. The mice Csf1r-iCre; VEGFfl/fl with no tamoxifen treatment leading to a phenotype comparable to WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 9A).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that absence of macrophage-derived VEGF promotes the expression of senescence-associated markers in macrophages. Using the Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice model we have shown that cutaneous macrophages deficient in VEGF are more sensitive to the expression of senescence-associated markers following radiation exposure. Importantly, this is accompanied by an increased sensitivity to radiation-induced dermatitis in Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice when compared to WT littermate. It has been reported that macrophages play key role in cutaneous inflammation [10]. The scavenging action of macrophages are crucial to resolve acute inflammation in skin [16]. We have observed that, in the absence of VEGF, both bone marrow and cutaneous macrophages display increased expression of senescence-associated markers and have impaired function. In specific, VEGF null macrophages demonstrated significant loss of phagocytic activity along with decreased mobility and responsiveness to chemotactic stimuli. This impaired chemotactic response hinders their ability to migrate toward inflammatory signals, compromising their scavenging function and resolution of acute inflammation in radiation-induced dermatitis. The lack of effective inflammation resolution exacerbates the severity of dermatitis, leading to aggravated clinical scores [52]. In vivo experiments clearly demonstrated that VEGF null macrophages have decreased expression of phagocytic markers when compared to wild type littermates following cutaneous radiation exposure. VEGF is known to modulate cellular senescence [25]. We therefore hypothesized that the presence of VEGF is critical to maintain macrophage function and inhibit the expression of senescence-associated markers in these cells.

Radiation-induced dermatitis is a common dose-limiting side effect of radiation therapy with limited effective treatments available [9, 53]. The oxidative stress and inflammation are key factors, while recent studies also suggest that impaired phagocytic clearance also promote atopic dermatitis [19, 54–57].

Patients with atopic dermatitis experience repeated infections caused by pyogenic bacteria or from intracellular pathogens. The innate immune response depends on the rapid action of mononuclear cells and polymorphonuclear neutrophils which are essential for early defense against infectious agents [58, 59]. These immune cells first exhibit chemotactic activity, migrating towards inflammatory signals, where they then carry out phagocytosis by engulfing, digesting and eliminating pathogenic organisms and their byproducts [60, 61]. The high frequency of infections in individuals with AD suggests immune disorders, possibly involving the alterations in phagocytosis. Macrophages play a crucial role in clearing apoptotic neutrophils through a process known as efferocytosis, which is essential for resolving inflammation and maintaining tissue homeostasis [62]. In dermatitis, alteration in macrophage-mediated efferocytosis may result in the accumulation of apoptotic neutrophils, prolonging inflammation and contributing to disease severity [63].

Macrophages dysfunction contributes significantly to increased risk of skin infections and plays a pivotal role in the cellular mechanism underlying dermatitis [13]. In atopic dermatitis, macrophages accumulate in both acute and chronic lesion [13, 64]. where they initially aid in inflammation resolution through antigen presentation, phagocytosis, and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and growth factors [65–67]. However, persistence of pro-inflammatory activity and altered function of macrophages result in the development of chronic inflammatory diseases such as dermatitis. The exact mechanism behind dysregulated macrophage function in these processes is not yet completely understood. In the current study, increased expression of senescence-associated markers in skin macrophages post-irradiation were accompanied by impaired mobility and phagocytic activity. This implicates the expression of senescence-associated markers and/or cell cycle arrest in macrophage dysfunction and sensitivity to in radiation-induced dermatitis.

In this study, we have observed the lack of change in senescence-associated markers p16 and p21 in WT cells despite H₂O₂ or Doxorubicin treatment which is interesting, particularly given the observed reduction in S-phase progression. This could suggest that WT cells may be employing alternative mechanisms of cell cycle regulation that do not involve classical senescence-associated pathways. One potential explanation is that WT cells may undergo a transient cell cycle arrest rather than a stable senescent state. Other possible explanation might be that oxidative stress and Doxorubicin trigger a transient cell cycle arrest in WT cells via non-canonical pathways [68], such as activation of the G1/S checkpoint through p53-independent mechanisms or modulation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors that are not as prominently regulated by p16/p21 [41]. The presence of VEGF in WT cells can promote rapid repair of DNA damage or bypass cell cycle checkpoints, preventing the cells from entering a prolonged senescent state [69]. VEGF supports cellular repair mechanisms and maintains tissue integrity by activating pathways that enhance cell survival and proliferation [69]. As a result, even though the S-phase progression is reduced due to initial stress, VEGF allows the cells to recover quickly and re-enter the cell cycle, which may explain why there is no sustained increase in the expression of senescence-associated markers like p16 or p21 in WT cells. This ability to effectively repair damage or bypass checkpoints helps WT cells to avoid a stable senescent phenotype. Further study is needed to investigate other markers of DNA damage response and transient cell cycle arrest to fully understand this phenomenon.

VEGF is one of the key regulators in cell senescence [25]. VEGF promotes cell cycle with an increase in p42/p44 MAP kinase, cyclin A and cyclin D1 expressions and with a decrease in p21 waf1/cip1 and p27 kip1 expressions [70, 71]. p16, p21 and p53 are well known markers to analyze the cellular senescence [72, 73]. p53 is a well-known senescence-associated marker that regulates cellular responses to stress, including radiation-induced DNA damage [43]. However, in radiation-induced dermatitis, the p53-independent expression of p16 in macrophages might suggest alternative regulatory mechanisms driving senescence, possibly through inflammatory signals or oxidative stress [41], which further need to be explored. In the present study both deletion of the Vegfa gene in macrophages and pharmacological inhibition of VEGF receptor lead to increased expression of senescence-associated markers in macrophages, suggesting that VEGF-VEGFR autocrine signal regulates these markers in macrophages. The elevated VEGF levels are typically associated with worsening skin diseases like atopic dermatitis [74], VEGF receptor inhibition can help alleviate these conditions. In contrast, our findings show increased radiation-induced skin damage in VEGF-deficient mice, which is more similar to cancer patients receiving VEGFRi treatments during radiotherapy [75]. This suggests that VEGF plays a protective role in mitigating radiation damage, and its absence may exacerbate skin injury during radiation exposure. In specific, mice with VEGF null macrophages displayed increased p21 and p16 expression in response to radiation when compared to wild type mice controls. While this study offers valuable insights into VEGF signaling in the regulation of macrophage senescence and phagocytic activity under stressors like H₂O₂, doxorubicin, and radiation, we did not analyze the effect of these stressors on macrophage polarization (M1 vs. M2). This limitation highlights the need for further studies to analyze the impact of macrophage specific VEGF deletion on macrophage polarization.

The absence of VEGF in Csf1r.iCre; VEGFfl/fl mice displayed higher senescence-associated marker expression in macrophages, when compared to WT mice. The senescent macrophages, characterized by permanent cell cycle arrest in response to stressors like DNA damage or radiation exposure [76]. These senescent macrophages might secrete a variety of inflammatory and growth-promoting factors known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) in response to radiation responses [77]. In radiation-induced dermatitis, senescent macrophages in the dermis influences skin architecture by promoting keratinocytes proliferation through SASP [77], resulting in increased Ki67 expression possibly leading to hyperplasia. On the other hand, higher Caspase-3 levels indicate increased apoptosis, likely due to the absence of macrophage derived VEGF, which normally supports tissue repair and cell survival [78]. The concurrent rise in both proliferation and cell death suggests that the absence of macrophage derived VEGF tissue is struggling to maintain homeostasis after radiation, resulting in an imbalance between cell loss and regeneration, which contributes to aberrant tissue architecture and hyperplasia.

We therefore propose that macrophage derived VEGF is an important factor in regulation of macrophage senescence-like phenotype and dysfunction which contribute to increased sensitivity to radiation-induced dermatitis. The VEGF pathway could therefore be a useful therapeutic target to mitigate radiation-induced dermatitis.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the critical role of macrophage-derived VEGF in maintaining macrophage function and preventing the expression of senescence-associated markers following radiation exposure. The absence of VEGF results in impaired macrophage phagocytic activity, reduced chemotactic response, and increased radiation-induced dermatitis severity. Furthermore, VEGF-null macrophages showed increased expression of senescence-associated markers, suggesting that VEGF signaling is essential for preventing macrophage senescence and promoting inflammation resolution. These results provide new insights into the mechanisms of radiation-induced dermatitis and skin toxicity while suggesting that targeting the VEGF pathway could offer a potential therapeutic approach to mitigate radiation-induced dermatitis and enhance skin recovery following radiotherapy.
