Anti-PD-1 Combinations: Triplets and Beyond
Authors: Claudia Trojaniello, Antonella Lucia Marretta, Bianca Arianna Facchini, Paolo Antonio Ascierto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewThe advent of anti-PD-1 therapy has transformed the treatment paradigm of advanced melanoma, enabling durable responses in a proportion of patients. However, the impact of both primary and acquired resistance remains a significant barrier to sustained disease control. This review explores the biological rationale and clinical progress of anti-PD-1 based combination strategies, with a particular emphasis on emerging triplet regimens and next-generation immunotherapeutic approaches designed to extend survival and overcome resistance.Recent FindingsWhile dual checkpoint inhibition (anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4 or anti-LAG-3) has improved clinical outcomes, further therapeutic advances are needed to expand the depth and durability of responses. Triplet combinations have shown promising activity in early-phase trials, with acceptable safety profiles. Promising triplet strategies currently under investigation include combinations of BRAF and MEK inhibitors with anti-PD-1 agents in BRAF-mutant melanoma, as well as regimens pairing anti-PD-1 therapy with emerging immunomodulatory agents. Biomarkers and disease control prior to infusion appear to be key determinants of outcome.SummaryAnti-PD-1-based triplet therapies represent a promising frontier in melanoma treatment. Future trials should define optimal combinations, patient selection, and therapeutic sequencing to maximize benefit and minimize toxicity.Keywords: Melanoma, Anti-PD-1, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Combination therapy, Immunotherapy resistance

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have dramatically changed the therapeutic landscape of advanced melanoma. The pivotal phase III CheckMate 066 trial established nivolumab as a highly effective first-line treatment in patients with BRAF wild-type metastatic melanoma, demonstrating a significant overall survival (OS) benefit over dacarbazine, with a 5-year OS of 39% versus 17% in the chemotherapy arm [1].

Subsequently, the KEYNOTE-006 trial confirmed the superiority of pembrolizumab over ipilimumab in both progression-free survival (PFS) and OS, leading to anti-PD-1 monotherapy becoming the standard first-line treatment in both BRAF-mutated and wild-type populations. At a 10-year follow-up, pembrolizumab achieved an OS rate of 34.0% versus 23.6% with ipilimumab; median OS was 32.7 months [2].

To further improve outcomes, dual immune checkpoint blockade with nivolumab and ipilimumab was investigated in CheckMate 067. This landmark phase III trial, with a minimum 10-year follow-up, showed a median OS of 71.9 months for patients receiving the combination, compared to 36.9 months with nivolumab alone and 19.9 months with ipilimumab monotherapy, 10-year OS rates were 43%, 37% and 19% respectively. [3]

Despite its efficacy, the high incidence of grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events (irAEs) (~ 60%) remains a major concern.

More recently, the phase II/III RELATIVITY‑047 trial evaluated nivolumab plus relatlimab (anti‑LAG‑3) versus nivolumab monotherapy in first‑line metastatic melanoma. The combination significantly improved median PFS (10.1 vs. 4.6 months; HR 0.75) and increased 4‑year PFS rates (30.6% vs. 23.6%) and 4-year OS rates (52.0% vs. 42.8%). [4]. Importantly, toxicity remained modest relative to nivolumab plus ipilimumab; at 3-year follow-up, grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse events (trAEs) occurred in 22.0% with the combination versus 12.0% with nivolumab alone, substantially lower than the 55–60% reported with nivolumab plus ipilimumab [5].

Despite substantial advances, a significant proportion of patients either fail to respond or eventually relapse after an initial response. Long-term follow-up from pivotal trials confirms that durable benefit is limited to a subset of individuals, and resistance, primary or acquired, remains a major barrier. Moreover, while dual ICIs combinations such as nivolumab plus ipilimumab have extended survival outcomes, their widespread use is constrained by high rates of irAEs, which can be life-altering or treatment-limiting.

These limitations underscore the critical need for novel combination strategies that can enhance anti-tumor efficacy while minimizing toxicity. Adding targeted agents, immune modulators, anti-angiogenics, or co-stimulatory agonists to anti–PD-1 therapy represents a promising approach to deepen immune responses, overcome resistance mechanisms, and ultimately expand the therapeutic window.

Triplet combinations, designed to work through complementary mechanisms and supported by biomarker research, offer a promising way to improve treatment outcomes and reduce toxicity in patients with melanoma.

Triplet Immunotherapy Strategies: Integrating Checkpoint Inhibition with Microenvironmental Modulation

Despite the success of dual checkpoint blockade in advanced melanoma, many patients still experience resistance or relapse. This has led to growing interest in triplet immunotherapy combinations that target multiple immune regulatory pathways simultaneously, aiming to overcome adaptive immune resistance and reinvigorate exhausted T cells through complementary mechanisms [Table 1].

IL-6R modulation strategies in clinical trials

In parallel, strategies targeting the tumor microenvironment (TME), such as IL-6R inhibitors (e.g., tocilizumab, sarilumab) are being explored to reduce inflammation-driven immunosuppression and improve tolerability of ICIs. Other emerging approaches, including combinations with therapeutic cancer vaccines or adoptive cell therapies, are under investigation for their potential to enhance antigen presentation and T cell infiltration, thereby complementing the effects of checkpoint inhibition.

In the phase 1/2 RELATIVITY-048 trial (NCT03459222), the triplet combination of nivolumab (anti–PD-1), relatlimab (anti–LAG-3), and ipilimumab (anti–CTLA-4) was evaluated in patients with advanced melanoma: among 46 participants, confirmed overall response rate (ORR) was 58.7%, 48-month OS rate reached 71.1% and 48-months PFS was 51.6%. The safety profile was consistent with known ICIs toxicities: 39.1% experienced grade 3–4 trAEs, and 41.3% discontinued treatment due to toxicity. There were two trAE-related deaths [6]. Despite the small sample size, these early findings support the potential of this triple combination to achieve durable responses, warranting further investigation in larger trials.

Given the toxicity of combination ICIs regimens, modulating inflammatory pathways is an attractive strategy.

One promising strategy involves targeting the interleukin-6 (IL-6) pathway, a key mediator of systemic inflammation, T cell differentiation, and immune-related toxicity. IL-6 plays a central role in the activation of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells and has been implicated in the development of several irAEs, such as colitis and hepatitis, commonly observed with ICIs. By blocking the IL-6, it may be possible to attenuate the inflammatory cascade that underlies irAEs, while preserving or even enhancing antitumor immune responses.

A single center phase II study (NCT04940299) investigates the addition of tocilizumab, an anti–IL-6 receptor antibody, to ipilimumab and nivolumab in treatment-naïve unresectable stage III/IV melanoma. Grade 3 irAEs occurred in 35% of patients, mostly diarrhea and elevated enzymes, with an ORR of 58% (disease control rate 75%). The treatment was generally well tolerated, with only one discontinuation due to toxicity and no treatment-related deaths [7].

In a separate phase II study (NCT03999749) with combination of ipilimumab, nivolumab and tocilizumab in treatment-naïve patients with advanced melanoma, early data in the first 28 patients showed an ORR of 70% with 25% experienced grade 3–4 irAEs, lower than historical ipilimumab/nivolumab, toxicities included colitis, nephritis, enteritis, and transaminitis. Updated results in 70 patients reported best ORR 57%, clinical benefit rate 65%, and median PFS 13 months. Importantly, the addition of tocilizumab significantly reduced severe irAES (grade 3–4 irAEs occurred in 22% vs. 34% in historical controls), all reversible, with no treatment-related deaths. In addition to clinical outcomes, the study provided compelling translational insights. Biomarker analyses revealed that the presence of osteopontin⁺ IL-6⁺ CD45RA⁻ classical monocytes correlated with clinical response, suggesting a potential immunologic signature of benefit. Conversely, higher levels of naïve CD8 + T cells were associated with disease progression, highlighting a possible marker of resistance. Moreover, patients who developed severe irAEs had significantly lower levels of mature NK cells (CD16⁺CD56⁺CD57⁺), indicating a potential predictive biomarker for toxicity [8].

The phase II trial (NCT04940299), single center, is evaluating subcutaneous dosing strategies (weekly vs. biweekly) of tocilizumab plus ipilimumab + nivolumab in patients with advanced first line melanoma, aiming to reduce severe irAEs while preserving efficacy [9]. Two subtuaneous dosing strategies of tocilizumab were tested: a regular regimen (RR) (biweekly) and a dose-dense regimen (DD) (weekly). In early results, both regimens showed encouraging response rates (56% in RR and 70% in DD) and disease control (for both regimens) of 80%. Approximately 40% of pts developed grade 3/4 irAEs and 20–30% of patients required hospitalization due to irAE, 16% in DD and 30% in RR discontinued ICIs due to irAEs with some reduction in grade 3–4 irAEs compared to historical data, particularly in the dose-dense group [10].

A phase 2 study (NCT05428007) investigated the addition of sarilumab (fully human monoclonal antibody that targets the interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R), functioning as an IL-6R antagonist) to the combination of ipilimumab, nivolumab, and relatlimab in patients with melanoma. The regimen demonstrated a 24-week ORR of 63.6%, which is notably higher than the ORRs reported in prior trials with ICIs (58.7% in Relativity-048). At a median follow-up of 9.8 months, the 24-week clinical benefit rate reached 78.8% (95% CI, 61.1%–91%). Importantly, the safety profile appeared favorable. Grade 3/4 irAEs at week 24 occurred in 12.1% of patients, while irAEs of any grade were observed in 93.9%. The 24-week period was selected as the benchmark for evaluating grade 3–5 irAEs, corresponding to the treatment duration of sarilumab. Compared to historical data from Relativity-048, where grade 3–5 irAEs were reported in 43% of patients, the sarilumab-based regimen showed a lower incidence of high-grade toxicity [11].

Another relevant immune-modulatory triplet is being evaluated in the ECOG-ACRIN EA6141 phase II/III trial (NCT02339571), which compares nivolumab plus ipilimumab with or without sargramostim, a recombinant GM-CSF, in patients with unresectable stage III or IV melanoma. The rationale derives from the immunostimulatory role of GM-CSF in promoting antigen-presenting cell differentiation and function, potentially enhancing antitumor immune priming when combined with dual checkpoint blockade. Enrollment of 600 patients has been completed, and follow-up for the primary OS analysis is ongoing. This study is particularly relevant because it evaluates whether immune stimulation through GM-CSF can improve the efficacy–toxicity balance of nivolumab plus ipilimumab rather than adding another checkpoint inhibitor.

Triplet Combinations with Targeted Therapies: Integrating BRAFi/MEKi with PD-1 Blockade

The integration of targeted therapies with ICIs has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of melanoma treatment [Table 2]. Combining BRAF and MEK inhibitors (BRAFi/MEKi) with PD-1 blockade seeks to leverage rapid tumor regression from targeted therapy with the durable immune activation from immunotherapy. This “triplet” approach aims to overcome intrinsic resistance mechanisms and improve outcomes. In addition to their direct anti-proliferative effects on melanoma cells, BRAFi/MEKi also have important immunomodulatory actions that influence the TME. BRAF-mutant melanomas typically display an immunosuppressive TME, characterized by IL-6/IL-10 secretion, accumulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), reduction in antigen presentation due to downregulation of MHC class I molecules and inhibition of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), contributing to poor T-cell activation [12, 13, 14].

During the early phase of BRAFi/MEKi treatment, the TME undergoes favorable changes: MHC-I upregulation [15], improved antigen presentation [16], increased CD8 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) with concurrent reduction in Tregs/MDSCs and immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, VEGF), shifting towards an IFN-γ-driven profile [17, 18].

Despite these early benefits, the TME may revert to an immunosuppressive state as resistance to therapy develops, marked by increased PD-L1 expression, a lower CD8+/Treg ratio, and T-cells exhaustion, even without new mutations in the MAPK pathway, suggesting a broader immune dysfunction induced by prolonged MAPKi exposure [19].

These insights provide a strong biological rationale for combining MAPK inhibitors with ICIs, early in the treatment course. The goal is to capitalize on the window of immune activation induced by BRAFi/MEKi before the onset of an immunosuppressive, therapy-resistant TME that can undermine the effectiveness of subsequent immunotherapy.

Early clinical experience to explore the safety and clinical activity of triplet therapy in advanced melanoma came from GP28384 (NCT01656642) and KEYNOTE-022 (NCT02130466).

GP28384 is a phase Ib dose-escalation and cohort-expansion study, evaluated the combination of atezolizumab (anti PD-L1) with vemurafenib and cobimetinib in BRAF V600 mutated metastatic melanoma, this study provided foundational support for later trials like IMspire150, which further validated the feasibility of combining anti–PD-L1 with MAPK-targeted therapies.

The KEYNOTE-022, a phase I/II trial (NCT02130466) evaluated the addition of pembrolizumab to dabrafenib and trametinib (DT) in 120 patients with advanced BRAF-mutated melanoma. Initial results showed a numerical improvement in mPFS for the triplet arm (16.0 vs. 10.3 months; HR 0.66, p = 0.043), though the study did not meet its pre-specified significance threshold at the primary analysis (median follow-up: 9.6 months). Interestingly, the ORR was slightly lower in the triplet group (63% vs. 72%), likely due to a higher proportion of patients with poor prognostic factors. Nevertheless, the triplet therapy led to a higher rate of complete responses (18% vs. 13%) and significantly prolonged duration of response (18.7 vs. 12.5 months) across all subgroups, including those with aggressive disease [20, 21].

Biomarker analyses from early cohorts (n = 15) suggested that pre-treatment CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression might correlate with clinical benefit, supporting their potential as predictive markers for triplet immunotherapy.

The safety profile revealed a higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 trAEs (70% vs. 45%), including fever, transaminase elevations, rash, and pneumonitis. Although most adverse events were manageable through dose modifications, treatment discontinuation was more frequent in the triplet arm (25% vs. 15%). Importantly, extended follow-up (median: 36.6 months) showed further gains in PFS, OS, and duration of response, suggesting that the full benefits of the triplet regimen may become more apparent over time [22].

The IMspire150, a phase III trial (NCT02908672), enrolled 514 patients with unresectable stage IIIC/IV BRAF-mutant melanoma, demonstrated a significant improvement in PFS with the triplet of atezolizumab, vemurafenib, and cobimetinib compared to the targeted therapy doublet (15.1 vs. 10.6 months; HR 0.78, p = 0.025). This benefit was attributed to a longer duration of response (21.0 vs. 12.6 months) and a delay in the development of central nervous system metastases. Based on these findings, the regimen received FDA approval for advanced BRAF-mutant melanoma.

Despite the PFS benefit, OS and ORR were comparable between groups (OS: 28.8 vs. 25.1 months; ORR: 66.3% vs. 65.0%). However, the OS curves showed divergence after two years, hinting at a possible late benefit. The combination was generally well tolerated, with similar discontinuation rates (12.6% vs. 15.7%), although the incidence of grade ≥ 3 trAEs events was slightly higher in the triplet arm (79% vs. 73%), including muscle enzyme elevations, rash, diarrhea, fever, joint pain, and liver function abnormalities [23].

The COMBI-I is a phase III trial (NCT02967692) randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study designed to assess the efficacy and safety of adding spartalizumab (anti PD-1) to the standard combination of dabrafenib and trametinib (DT) in patients with advanced BRAF-mutated melanoma. The trial followed an encouraging safety run-in phase involving 36 patients, which demonstrated a remarkable ORR of 78%, including a complete response rate of 44% and a median PFS of 23 months. Moreover, baseline tumor mutational burden (TMB) and T cell–inflamed gene expression signatures were associated with longer PFS, highlighting the potential role of predictive biomarkers in guiding therapy [24].

The final results from the randomized portion of the study (n = 532), presented after a median follow-up of 27.2 months, confirmed improvements in PFS (16.2 vs. 12.0 months; HR 0.82; p = 0.042) and duration of response (not reached vs. 20.7 months) with the triplet regimen. However, the trial did not meet its primary endpoint, and the observed clinical benefit was not deemed sufficient to support routine first-line use of the triplet therapy in this setting [25].

The ORR was comparable between treatment arms (69% with the triplet vs. 64% with DT), but the incidence of grade ≥ 3 trAEs was notably higher in the triplet group (55% vs. 33%), leading to a greater rate of treatment discontinuation (12% vs. 8%) [26].

The TRIDeNT study (NCT02910700) is a phase II trial evaluating nivolumab in combination with dabrafenib and trametinib in patients with BRAF-mutated metastatic melanoma, including both PD-1–naïve and PD-1–refractory individuals, with or without active brain metastases. Among 26 evaluable patients, the ORR was 92%, with 3 complete responses and 21 partial responses. Notably, all evaluable PD-1–naïve patients (n = 10) achieved a response, and even among the PD-1–refractory subgroup, the ORR remained high at 88%. Importantly, the regimen also showed intracranial activity, with 4 out of 7 evaluable patients with brain metastases achieving a response (57%), including 2 CRs. Median PFS was 8.5 months across all subgroups, with no significant difference based on prior PD-1 exposure or presence of brain metastases. Median OS was not reached at a median follow-up of 18.4 months. TrAes G3-4 occurred in 78% of patients, and 22% discontinued all three agents due to toxicity, highlighting the need for close monitoring and improved tolerability in future strategies [27].

The STARBOARD is a phase III study (NCT04657991) investigating the efficacy and safety of combining encorafenib (BRAFi) and binimetinib (MEKi) with pembrolizumab in patients with advanced BRAF V600-mutant melanoma in first line. The study includes an open-label safety lead-in (SLI) phase to determine the recommended phase 3 dose of the drug combination. Results from the SLI phase indicate that the combination therapy is generally safe and well-tolerated without teatment-related deaths in either treatment arm. The ORR was 65.0% in the COMBO450 plus pembrolizumab arm and 47.1% in the COMBO300 plus pembrolizumab arm. Based on these results, the standard COMBO450 regimen in combination with pembrolizumab was selected as the recommended phase 3 dose (RP3D). The phase 3 portion of the study is ongoing, comparing the combination therapy to pembrolizumab alone [28].

The IMMU-TARGET trial was initially designed as an open-label phase I/II trial in patients with unresectable or metastatic BRAF V600–mutated melanoma [29]. In this phase I, 15 patients were treated with the triplet combination of pembrolizumab, encorafenib, and binimetinib. The safety profile was generally manageable, with dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) observed only in the initial cohort and not confirmed in subsequent patients. Overall, 87% of patients experienced trAEs, and 53% had grade 3 or higher toxicities (most commonly liver enzyme elevations and creatine phosphokinase increases). Despite this, no treatment-related deaths occurred, and the adverse events were considered acceptable. The clinical activity was encouraging: among the 14 patients evaluable for efficacy, the ORR was 64%, and PFS at 12 months was 41%. The planned phase II of IMMU-TARGET included a 6-month triplet induction, followed by randomization to continued triplet therapy or pembrolizumab alone (with reintroduction of targeted therapy upon progression). However, the study was halted after phase I, as the combination is now being tested in the ongoing phase III STARBOARD trial.

These findings underscore the ongoing challenge of balancing efficacy with tolerability in triplet strategies and highlight the need for refined patient selection and biomarker-driven approaches in future trials.

Among these studies, IMspire150 was the only one to achieve a statistically significant improvement in investigator-assessed PFS. This result may be partly attributed to variations in trial design, such as the run-in period with BRAF/MEK inhibition used in IMspire150, as well as differences in patient populations and treatment schedules.

Despite the strong biological rationale and encouraging signals observed in selected studies, BRAF/MEK/PD-(L)1 triplets are rarely used in routine clinical practice. Several factors explain this limited uptake. First, the magnitude of benefit over BRAF/MEK inhibition alone has generally been modest, with improvements mainly in PFS or duration of response rather than consistent gains in ORR or OS. Second, toxicity is substantially increased, with frequent pyrexia, rash, hepatic enzyme elevations, diarrhea, pneumonitis, and CPK elevation, often requiring dose interruptions, corticosteroids, or treatment discontinuation in 12–25% of patients [Table 3]. Third, the therapeutic landscape has evolved, and dual immune checkpoint blockade, particularly nivolumab plus ipilimumab or nivolumab plus relatlimab, offers durable benefit without continuous exposure to targeted therapy. Finally, optimal sequencing remains unresolved: many clinicians prefer starting with immunotherapy in fit patients with BRAF-mutant melanoma, reserving BRAF/MEK inhibitors for rapid disease control, symptomatic disease, or later-line use. Therefore, current use of BRAF/MEK/PD-(L)1 triplets is generally limited to selected patients or clinical trials rather than broad first-line adoption.

Nonetheless, subgroup analyses suggest that patients with high tumor burden or poor prognostic features may derive greater benefit. Further biomarker-driven research is essential to better define which patients are most likely to respond, supporting a more personalized treatment approach based on patient risk.

Conclusion

Over the past decade, anti-PD-1 therapies have revolutionized the treatment landscape of advanced melanoma. However, durable responses remain confined to a subset of patients, and resistance continues to limit long-term disease control. In this context, rationally designed combination strategies are critical to deepen and extend responses while mitigating toxicity. Triplet regimens combining anti-PD-1 agents with either additional checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies represent a promising evolution in immuno-oncology.

Emerging data from early-phase trials suggest that these combinations can enhance efficacy and, in some cases, improve tolerability when integrated with microenvironmental modulators such as IL-6R inhibitors. The integration of BRAFi/MEKi with PD-1 blockade has also yielded encouraging results, particularly in patients with high-risk BRAF-mutant melanoma. However, despite a strong biological rationale, the clinical adoption of these triplet strategies remains limited. This is largely due to the modest incremental benefit observed over standard doublet therapies, the lack of consistent overall survival advantage, and a substantially increased toxicity burden, often leading to treatment interruptions or discontinuation. In addition, the evolving therapeutic landscape where highly effective dual immune checkpoint combinations are available and the unresolved question of optimal treatment sequencing further constrain their routine use in clinical practice.

Looking ahead, the development of next-generation triplet strategies will likely depend on a shift from empiric intensification toward biologically informed combinations. Novel approaches include the integration of anti-PD-1 therapy with emerging checkpoint targets (such as TIGIT or TIM-3), as well as agents that modulate the tumor microenvironment, including cytokine blockade, anti-angiogenic therapies, and myeloid-targeting strategies. In parallel, combinations incorporating cancer vaccines, oncolytic viruses, or adoptive cellular therapies may further enhance antigen presentation and T-cell priming. In this evolving landscape, biomarker-driven patient selection will be essential to identify individuals most likely to benefit from specific triplet regimens.

Ultimately, future research must prioritize personalized approaches to combination therapy, optimizing the balance between efficacy and toxicity through refined patient selection, improved dosing strategies, and rational sequencing, with the goal of tailoring treatment to individual tumor biology.
