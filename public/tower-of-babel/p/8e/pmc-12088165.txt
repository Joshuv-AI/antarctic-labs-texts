The Role of Psychology in Emergent Pediatric Limb Salvage and Reconstruction
Authors: Whitney M Herge, Mikhail Samchukov, Emily Elerson, Alexander Cherkashin, Elizabeth Hubbard, David Podeszwa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimilar to elective pediatric limb lengthening and reconstruction (LLR) cases, treatment of pediatric patients undergoing emergent limb salvage and reconstruction (LSR) is approached as a multidisciplinary process. Unlike elective pediatric LLR, however, where the patient's individual interest in and motivation for LLR drives treatment decisions, emergent LSR treatment must be approached differently. In particular, the timing of emergent LSR treatment is driven by the risks associated with the patient's medical diagnosis and the potential consequences of nonintervention. Multidisciplinary treatment shifts from optimizing the patient's preoperative physical and psychological health to triaging and stabilizing the patient's physical and psychological health concurrent with LSR treatment. Without the luxury of preoperative time, the team must instead focus on physical and psychological risk mitigation, as well as the implementation of trauma-informed care, as appropriate.This work reviews the application of pediatric LLR preparation principles to emergent LSR scenarios. Our team's established six-step preparation workflow is adapted such that the timing, location, and focus of each step are individualized to the LSR patient's care needs. The team psychologist plays a particularly important role in this process in educating the multidisciplinary care team regarding the patient's specific psychological trauma symptoms, as appropriate, as well as ensuring the medical team takes a trauma-informed approach in their treatment provision and planning.Key Concepts(1)Pediatric patients undergoing emergent LSR rarely have time for preoperative treatment preparation and, instead, typically must be evaluated after the index surgery.(2)Rather than focusing on preoperative physical and psychological optimization, the multidisciplinary team focuses on physical and psychological triage and stabilization concurrent with LSR intervention.(3)Treatment should be provided through a trauma-informed lens in recognition of the enormous stress that emergent LSR treatment often places on pediatric patients and families.Keywords: External fixator, Pediatrics, Trauma, Psychology

Introduction

Preparing pediatric patients considering limb lengthening and reconstruction (LLR) is a complex, demanding process that requires coordinated multidisciplinary assessment, education, and communication. We have previously proposed a staged, developmentally informed approach to elective treatment preparation, as it allows the patient's needs to dictate the pace at which treatment is pursued [1]. Specifically, we recommended a six-stage preparation process [1], beginning with an introduction to the multidisciplinary team in the clinic, followed by work with nursing, psychology, physical/occupational therapy, and other specialists, as appropriate. This six-step process represents our ideal preparation approach to patients undergoing LLR. Yet, we recognize that extended time preparing for such a treatment is a luxury not available to all patients due to the pressing nature of a particular medical diagnosis or a traumatic or infectious event. The latter patients are also challenged with an acute sense of functional loss and reduced self-efficacy, which is quite different from the experience of a patient with a congenital or developmental condition that affords years of adaptation, medical and social support, and gradual acceptance.

Traumatic events in childhood, including receiving a diagnosis of a life- or limb-threatening problem, may impact a child's functioning in a pervasive manner. Prevalence estimates suggest that between 15% and 43% of children assigned female sex at birth and 14%–43% of children assigned male sex at birth experience at least one physical, emotional, and/or psychological trauma in their childhoods [2]. The symptoms of traumatic stress exposure tend to vary by developmental stage, such that young children may display separation anxiety, emotional dysregulation, changes in sleep or appetite, or difficulty concentrating, while older children may display clearer symptoms of depression or engage in high-risk behaviors, such as alcohol use or misuse, engagement in risky sexual behavior, and/or engagement in self-harm behavior [3]. Additionally, trauma exposure in childhood has been associated with a variety of long-term negative outcomes, including behavioral health symptoms, academic difficulties, involvement with child welfare and juvenile justice systems, substance misuse, and the development of chronic health problems (e.g. heart disease, diabetes) [3]. The provision of appropriate psychological intervention to youth affected by traumatic stress is critical in reducing the likelihood of such ubiquitous long-term sequela and may include treatments such as psychological first aid and trauma-informed cognitive behavioral therapy [4].

Our approach for emergent limb salvage and reconstruction (LSR) is then defined as treatment wherein comprehensive preoperative preparation is not logistically possible or prudent, such as in the setting of trauma, fracture, or infection, and carriers a high risk of traumatic stress in patients. The multidisciplinary team focuses on triaging and stabilizing the LSR patient's physical and psychological health, and engagement in the six-step multidisciplinary process occurs in a truncated manner concurrent with LSR treatment and molded to the patient's individual functioning level. Appreciating this reality, the purpose of this study is to review the role of psychology within the multidisciplinary treatment of pediatric patients within the setting of emergent LSR. Our recommendations regarding the role of psychology in emergent LSR are outlined below.

As the patient and family's mental health is of particular concern within the setting of emergent LSR, the psychologist will play an integral role in the introductory visit. A traumatic event that precipitates the need for emergent LSR, as well as trauma associated with emergent medical care itself has the potential to strongly influence the patient and family's ability to cope with LSR treatment. In particular, a patient may experience symptoms of acute stress disorder associated with an accident or injury that precipitates the need for emergent LSR treatment (DMS-5-TR, American Psychiatric Association) [5]. This may manifest as negative or low mood, irritability, dissociation, avoidance behaviors, intrusive thoughts or memories, sleeping difficulties, difficulty with concentration, and/or hyperarousal [5]. A patient may also experience grief or feelings of guilt associated with an accident or injury, particularly when their limb (and/or other organs or systems) has been irrevocably altered, and especially when other individuals were harmed or died. Certainly, these symptoms may impact a patient's ability to tolerate LSR physically and emotionally; for example, a patient may experience consistent negative cognitions regarding treatment, display a low tolerance for the new treatment team members/environments/activities, overestimate threat and/or expected pain associated with treatment tasks, display difficulty with motivation, experience complicated grief/guilt/loss that severely limits their ability to engage with treatment, appear hypersensitive to sensory stimulation, and struggle with ADLs and other hygiene activities. Further, it is possible that emergent LSR treatment may represent a traumatic stressor unto itself, based on the patient's perceived level of danger associated with treatment and concurrent lack of control. As such, providing the patient and family with education regarding the psychological impact of trauma, as well as typical recovery trajectories and beneficial coping strategies (e.g. relaxation strategies, discussing the traumatic event, incorporating the traumatic event into one's life narrative, meaning making), is imperative starting from the patient's first visit. For particularly complex cases, especially those involving fatality and loss, more intensive psychological intervention both within the medical team and within the community will likely be necessary. Similarly, ensuring that the medical team's case conceptualization, treatment planning, and treatment execution occur through a trauma-informed lens is equally critical [6,7].

Step 2. Education visits

In the case of emergent pediatric LSR, the team nurse and psychologist build upon the introductory visit and spend significant time with the patient and family on the inpatient unit and/or at each clinic visit. This time is spent providing ongoing education regarding current and future treatment expectations, gathering information regarding the patient and family's functioning, and assessing and bolstering the patient and family's coping (Fig. 1).

The team nurse and psychologist are responsible for educating the patient and family about the patient's medical device(s) and equipment in a developmentally appropriate manner (e.g. external fixator, splints, heating blanket, etc.). This may include tasks such as helping the patient to unwrap their external fixator and clean their limb, which can be both physically and emotionally demanding, or coping with suture removal. The team nurse and psychologist also provide education on care tasks, such as pin site care, completing turns on an external fixator device, etc. The psychologist, in particular, helps to ensure that the patient feels as physically and emotionally safe as possible throughout these activities and supports the patient in collaborating in their medical care to the maximum extent possible.

The team nurse and psychologist are also responsible for troubleshooting the extensive logistical support typically required for emergent LSR patients. This may include helping to coordinate appointments with other specialists, enrolling the patient in hospital homebound education if they have extensive injuries that prohibit school attendance, assisting the family in requesting Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) assistance and/or paid time off (PTO), and procuring a parking placard if the family desires.

Ongoing assessment of the patient's potential psychological trauma symptoms is also necessary, particularly if the patient displays consistent and prolonged or increasing symptoms over time. In this scenario, it will be important to assist the patient in getting connected to additional mental health support outside the LSR team. This may take the form of an outpatient counselor, an intensive outpatient programming (e.g. a partial hospitalization program), and/or psychotropic medication. Rarely, this may also include inpatient admission at a behavioral health hospital in the setting of imminent safety concerns.

Step 3. Physical assessment visits

In contrast to elective LLR, where the goal of the physical assessment is to optimize the patient's strength, endurance, and ROM before surgery, the goal of the physical assessment visits within the setting of emergent LSR treatment is physical recovery and risk mitigation. This work occurs either on the inpatient unit and/or as part of regular multidisciplinary clinic visits throughout treatment. In particular, the team focuses on stabilizing the patient's physical health, including coordinating and following up with other specialists providing concurrent care. Within the context of trauma, a patient will likely require a robust team, possibly including plastic surgery, vascular surgery, nephrology, neurology, etc. Additionally, the medical team evaluates nutrition labs, begins a vitamin regimen to correct for any deficits, prescribes antibiotics in the setting of infection, and counsels on immediately discontinuing nonprescribed substances, as needed.

As a course of prehabilitation is not possible for emergent LSR patients, physical therapy (PT) and/or occupational therapy (OT) work with the patient concurrent with LSR treatment and within the confines of the patient's global medical restrictions (Fig. 2). This may include a combination of inpatient vs outpatient therapy visits as well as an intensive home exercise program to support a patient's acclimation to and use of medical equipment (e.g. crutches, walker, and wheelchair) and to slowly rebuild strength, range of motion, and endurance. The psychologist may be particularly beneficial in helping the emergent LSR patient understand the purpose of their home exercises and the risks of nonadherence. The psychologist also plays an important role for any patient who experiences posttraumatic kinesiophobia (fear of pain due to movement) and requires coaching through progressive exposure to an approximation of desired activity.

Step 4. Establishing peer support

Establishing a peer support relationship should be considered case-by-case with LSR patients. The psychologist plays an important role in assessing the coping of an emergent LSR patient, as well as the possible risks vs benefits of introducing an emergent LSR patient to a peer. Peer support is indicated when the emergent LSR patient's treatment trajectory is known, the patient is coping, at least, moderately well, and they would benefit from treatment normalization and active support (e.g. pairing with peer support in PT). Peer support is contraindicated when the emergent LSR patient's medical trajectory is unclear and/or when they are having an extremely difficult time coping. In these cases, pairing the emergent LSR patient with peer support may be harmful as it may create unrealistic treatment expectations and/or prompt the emergent LSR patient to compare themselves in unfavorable or unhelpful ways to the peer.

Step 5. Multidisciplinary team staffing

When treating a patient with emergent LSR, the multidisciplinary team meeting occurs after the index surgery and continuously thereafter periodically. The purpose of these meetings is to ensure that all team members are knowledgeable regarding the patient's treatment to date and to disseminate information regarding the anticipated treatment plan moving forward. The team psychologist plays a valuable role in these discussions, helping to educate the team on the patient's trauma experience as well as their individual psychological trauma symptoms (e.g. reexperiencing the traumatic event, feeling constantly ‘on edge,’ sensory integration challenges, etc.). The psychologist also helps to ensure that the medical team takes a trauma-informed approach to the patient's care to minimize the risk of retraumatization [6,7].

Step 6. Active treatment support

For patients undergoing emergent LSR, ongoing active support across the inpatient and clinic settings is critical for the patient and family. For these patients, regular and consistent assessment by the team psychologist is imperative to monitor for the development and/or worsening of psychological symptoms (e.g. mood, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, acute stress disorder, etc.). Education should be provided regarding beneficial cognitive, physiological, and behavioral coping strategies (Fig. 3), and as noted above, referrals to outside mental health providers should be made as appropriate.

Case example

The patient (12-year-old male) was involved in a bicycle versus motor vehicle crash. He sustained an open type IIIC left distal femoral fracture with significant bone loss and left distal tibia fracture. The patient initially underwent irrigation debridement (I&D) and provisional stabilization of his fractures using a uniplanar external fixator in conjunction with reconstruction of his popliteal artery. He subsequently underwent multiple I&D's and application of a definitive multiplanar circular external fixator, in addition to placement of an antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer (Fig. 5). Three months later, the spacer was removed, the fixator was revised, and a proximal femoral corticotomy was completed to accommodate approximately six weeks of femoral bone transport. After bone grafting the docking site, the patient subsequently continued femoral lengthening to complete 8 ​cm of bone transport/lengthening. After treating an indolent infection and delayed healing at the docking site, the external fixator was unlocked to begin knee range of motion. The external fixator was removed after 17 months, and the patient was transitioned into a knee-ankle-foot-orthosis. The patient also underwent a contralateral epiphysiodesis. Presently, the patient utilizes his knee-ankle-foot-orthosis and a cane to ambulate. He participates in both regular physical therapy and aquatic therapy.

Patient who sustained femoral and tibial fractures following a bicycle vs motor vehicle crash undergoing LSR treatment. LSR, limb salvage and reconstruction.

Emotionally, the patient displayed significant psychological trauma symptoms following his accident. In particular, the patient displayed substantial emotional dysregulation, heightened tactile sensory sensitivity, catastrophization in his thought processes, and a heightened alert system. The six-step approach was followed, as outlined above, to address the patient's mental health symptoms. Specifically, the team psychologist worked closely with the patient and his family both on the inpatient unit as well as in the clinic, providing psychoeducation related to psychological trauma, typical recovery trajectories, and targeted coping strategies grounded in trauma-informed cognitive-behavioral therapy. In particular, the psychologist taught the patient relaxation and cognitive reframing strategies, encouraged the patient to participate in his own medical decision-making and procedures to the maximum extent possible, engaged the patient in discussing the logistical and emotional details of his accident, and guided the patient in incorporating the accident into his larger life narrative and making meaning from the accident.

Throughout treatment, the patient's mood improved, his anxiety stabilized, and his sensory sensitivity returned to baseline. By the end of treatment, the patient displayed symptoms of posttraumatic growth, and he appeared quite comfortable and confident within the medical setting, even removing his own stitches and sutures with supervision. He also invested time and effort in his passion for art, including making an art piece out of his external fixator after it was removed, and he entered and won numerous awards for the art he created throughout his treatment.

Summary

A psychologist plays a particularly important role in helping pediatric patients and families to cope with the complex, demanding medical needs associated with LSR, as well as assisting the medical team in the provision of trauma-informed care to minimize risks of complications and retraumatization. For patients with emergent LSR needs, preoperative treatment preparation is rarely possible, and instead, the focus shifts to stabilizing the patient's physical and emotional health following the index surgery, coordinating multiple medical specialists (as needed), and attending to/treating the potential psychological trauma, stress, and other mental health symptoms evidenced by the patient and family.
