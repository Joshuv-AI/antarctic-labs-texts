Deviations from the standard of care in the treatment of pregnant people with opioid use disorder in jails; a mixed methods study
Authors: Sonia Hamilton, Erin Sommers, Camille Kramer, Carolyn Sufrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) with methadone or buprenorphine are the standard of care for opioid use disorder (OUD) during pregnancy and postpartum. MOUD is not routinely available in US jails. Little is known about OUD management practices and patient experiences when MOUD is unavailable in jails. This study aims to assess deviations from standard pregnancy OUD care in jail and demonstrate patients’ experiences of such deviations.MethodsMixed-methods, secondary analysis from two prior studies: (1) a survey of US jails (n = 836) regarding availability MOUD in pregnancy and (2) interviews with pregnant and postpartum people with OUD (n = 32) who were in jail while pregnant. We analyzed survey results using descriptive statistics and interview transcripts using directed content analysis. We triangulated practices across both studies and grouped them thematically.ResultsPractices deviating from the standard of care included: use of naltrexone in pregnancy (n = 111), MOUD discontinuation postpartum (n = 489), and use of acetaminophen/codeine as maintenance therapy (n = 5). Interviewees described a lack of counseling and choice in MOUD, frequent opioid withdrawal, and inadequate withdrawal management.ConclusionsDeviations from standard pregnancy OUD care are common in jail. Since the lack of MOUD and opioid withdrawal increases overdose risk and other adverse perinatal outcomes, we must address substandard care in jail for this population.Keywords: Opioid use disorder, Pregnancy, Jails, Incarceration, Medications for opioid use disorder

Introduction

Between 1980 and 2022, the number of incarcerated women in the United States (US) skyrocketed by over 525%, disproportionately affecting Black and Brown women [1, 2]. Roughly three-quarters of the females who were incarcerated by year-end of 2022 were between 18 and 44 years old, reproductive years [1]. It is estimated that 3% of women in jails are pregnant upon intake [3].

Simultaneously, opioid use disorder (OUD) incidence in pregnancy has drastically increased in the US, from 3.5 per 1000 delivery hospitalizations in 2010 to 8.2 per 1000 delivery hospitalizations in 2017 [4]. Punitive responses to substance use have driven the growing incarceration of people with substance use disorders in the US, and pregnant people are no exception [5]. Many states have laws that criminalize substance use in pregnancy, with research showing disproportionate targeting of Black women [6, 7]. The intersection of criminalization rather than treatment of substance use in pregnancy, racial discrimination, and the opioid epidemic manifests in a significant number of pregnant people with OUD cycling through jails, with one study estimating that 14% of pregnant people admitted to jails had OUD, roughly 7,700 each year [8].

Opioid use in pregnancy is a major contributor to maternal mortality, accounting for 10% of all pregnancy-associated deaths in 2016 [9, 10]. After release from carceral facilities, the risk of fatal overdose is significantly elevated for all individuals, but particularly for pregnant and postpartum individuals; and incarcerated pregnant people with OUD have a four times greater odds of postpartum overdose [11, 12]. Access to medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) in custody decreases postpartum overdose risk, signaling that jail is a critical point of access in the cascade of care for pregnant and postpartum people with OUD [12].

The long-established, evidence-based treatment for OUD in pregnancy is MOUD with either methadone or buprenorphine and avoiding withdrawal [13]. MOUD in pregnancy improves adherence to addiction treatment and prenatal care while reducing risks of return to use, overdose, HIV, and hepatitis, and improves overall maternal and neonatal outcomes [14–16]. Medically supervised withdrawal is not recommended, as it is associated with high rates of return to use (59%-90%), increasing risks of overdose, communicable disease transmission, and obstetric complications [14, 17]. Instead, buprenorphine or methadone should be initiated for pregnant patients experiencing withdrawal [14]. Naltrexone, a full opioid antagonist, and other prescription opioids such as hydromorphone or codeine are not recommended as maintenance therapy in pregnancy due high return to use rates with associated risks for maternal and fetal harm [14, 18]. Guidelines recommend MOUD continuation in the postpartum period, when individuals are particularly vulnerable to return to use, opioid overdose, and psychiatric disorders [13, 14]. MOUD has been shown to reduce the risk of overdose death in the postpartum period by as much as 60% [19]. Care guidelines also emphasize the importance of pregnant people with OUD having autonomy in treatment decisions and non-pharmacologic resources [14, 20].

Despite being the standard of care, MOUD is not routinely available in carceral facilities, even in pregnancy. Our national survey of 826 jails found that while 60% of jails continued at least one MOUD medication for pregnant individuals, only 32% initiated MOUD in pregnancy [21]. Of the MOUD-providing jails, only 24% allowed individuals to continue MOUD postpartum, forcing a substantial number of people to go through withdrawal during the high-risk postpartum period [21]. Similar findings of MOUD non-availability have been reported in other studies, with a 2023 Bureau of Justice Statistics report finding that, independent of pregnancy status, only 19% of jails initiate MOUD [22–24]. Furthermore, for pregnant people with OUD who go from jail to prison without existing access to MOUD, prisons are unlikely to initiate treatment, despite the overall benefits of being on treatment. A cross-sectional study of 22 state prisons found that, while most sites continued MOUD in pregnancy if it had been started before arrival, very few initiated it [8].

The inconsistent availability of MOUD in pregnancy and postpartum raises concerns about numerous deviations from the standard of care for this population who are at high-risk for adverse outcomes. To investigate and describe jail practices that deviated from the standard of care for treating pregnant people with OUD, we conducted a mixed-methods, secondary data analysis of a national jail survey on MOUD availability for pregnant people in custody [21]. We complemented this with a secondary analysis of interviews with women with OUD who were in jail while pregnant to examine how they experienced these deviations from the standard of care [25]. The current study aims to illuminate gaps that exist in appropriate MOUD implementation in jail for pregnant individuals and identify opportunities for improvement while centering the voices of those with lived experience.

Methods

This study is a secondary analysis of two prior studies: (1) a national jail survey on the availability of MOUD for pregnant people in jail and (2) qualitative interviews with pregnant and postpartum people with lived experience of being in jail while pregnant with OUD [21, 25]. We conducted the current analysis with the goal of identifying patterns of treatment practices for pregnant people with OUD in jails that deviate from the standard of care. By combining quantitative and qualitative data, we aimed to understand what substandard practices occur in jails and how pregnant individuals experienced them. The methods for each parent study are briefly described below, with further details published elsewhere [21, 25]. Both studies were approved by the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (National Jail Survey: IRB00174954, Qualitative Interviews: IRB00197843).

National jail survey on MOUD availability for pregnant people in jail

From August-November 2019, we sent electronic REDCap and paper surveys to medical and custody leaders at the 2885 US jails verified by the National Jails Compendium [26]. We received 836 analyzable responses from jails across 48 states, with half being from rural counties [21]. The survey included 49 questions about the availability of MOUD for pregnant people in jail, withdrawal practices in pregnancy, and postpartum MOUD access. Each respondent’s completion of the survey served as their consent to participate in the study.

Interviews with pregnant and postpartum people with OUD in jail

Between October 2020-November 2021, we conducted semi-structured, qualitative interviews with 32 pregnant and postpartum individuals with OUD who were currently or had recently been in jail while pregnant across four states in the eastern U.S [25]. Interview questions focused on the individual’s experiences of OUD treatment and withdrawal while pregnant in jail from the beginning of their incarceration to their release. We obtained oral consent from all interview participants.

Outcomes and data analysis

Our secondary analysis involved a convergent parallel mixed-methods analysis using the descriptive quantitative data and open-ended responses from the jail survey which we triangulated with the qualitative data from interviews with people with lived experience of pregnancy, opioid use, and jail incarceration [27]. Although the studies collected data from different participant groups—jail staff and patients, respectively—the studies provided data that aligned, connecting MOUD provision during pregnancy (jail staff) with experiences accessing MOUD in jail while pregnant (pregnant/postpartum participants).

Because we aimed to identify deviations from the standard of care in treating pregnant people with OUD in jail settings, we first developed a framework to organize the established standards of care for OUD in pregnancy, referencing guidelines by three national organizations: the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), the Bureau of Justice Assistance (BJA), and the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) [13, 14, 28]. By compiling these guidelines, we considered the pregnancy OUD standard of care as:

Timely access to continue and initiate methadone and buprenorphine in custody [28].

Avoidance of forced switching between medications, especially from methadone to buprenorphine, given risks of precipitated withdrawal [13].

Medically supervised withdrawal with safe medications in pregnancy for those who, after thorough counseling, decline treatment [14].

We organized these standards into categories: MOUD availability, specific medication availability, withdrawal management, postpartum care, and non-pharmacologic treatment and resources. Specific behaviors or treatments contraindicated in care guidelines were organized within these categories and considered deviations from the standard of care.

We used this framework to identify specific questions in the jail survey that were relevant to this study. We used descriptive statistics to analyze variables and identify the occurrence and frequency of deviations from care guidelines. When analyzing open-ended responses from these survey questions, we developed a codebook based on the domains of the jail survey and the framework we used to categorize standard care. Additional codes were added from survey responses as we encountered them, including codes for specific deviations from care guidelines. For our secondary analysis of interviews of people with OUD who were pregnant in jail, we recoded all interview transcripts using a new codebook that we developed, similar to the one used in the jail survey, based on the standard of care framework. Additional codes were later added from emergent themes across patient responses.

We triangulated the data by identifying common themes and patterns between them and categorizing them based on the aspects of care guidelines they related to.

Results

The parent jail survey found that of the 836 analyzable responses, 60% of jails (n = 504) continued at least one MOUD medication that pregnant individuals were taking before jail [21]. 32% (n = 267) of jails both initiated and continued MOUD treatment during pregnancy, while 28% (n = 237) only continued MOUD if the pregnant patient was on treatment before arrival [21].

From the parent qualitative study with 32 participants, whom all identified as cisgender females, 23 were pregnant and nine were postpartum at the time of the interview [25]. Sixteen participants were interviewed in jail and 16 were back in the community. Twenty-five (78%) of the individuals received MOUD while pregnant in jail, and 10 (31%), including some who also received MOUD, described experiencing withdrawal while pregnant in jail [25]. Among those who received MOUD in jail, many recounted experiencing stigma and discrimination from jail staff.

In the following sections, we detail practices that deviated from the standard of care for OUD in pregnancy. Our analyses identified six categories of deviations from the standard of care: (1) limited choice in MOUD and forced switching between medications; (2) use of non-evidence-based MOUD in pregnancy or non-Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA)-approved uses of opioids as substitutes for MOUD; (3) frequent experiences of withdrawal from treatment delays and lack of MOUD; (4) substandard opioid withdrawal management; (5) postpartum MOUD discontinuation; and (6) lack of counseling around OUD and MOUD in pregnancy.

Limited choice in MOUD and forced switching between medications

The standard of care for OUD in pregnancy is that both methadone and buprenorphine should be available [13]. In jail settings, this includes both continuation of the same pre-jail MOUD from the community and initiation of MOUD of the person’s choice at intake. Individuals should be able to choose between methadone and buprenorphine since they differ in dispensing methods, medication interactions, and risk and duration of Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS) [14]. Most pregnant people with OUD have experience with these medications and have a preference for one or the other [13]. Of the 836 jails surveyed, only 18% (n = 152) offered MOUD initiation and continuation with both. While 60% of jails continued MOUD, 21% (n = 174) only offered one medication, resulting in some individuals being forced to switch their treatment regimen.

Interviews with pregnant and postpartum women with OUD illuminated this lack of choice in which treatment individuals received—if they were offered treatment at all. One woman, when describing her preference for methadone over buprenorphine in pregnancy, expressed:

I don’t know how long Subutex has been around. And I don’t really know like what all studies and what, necessarily, the birth, you know, what birth defects or what side effe- you know, I’m not really – like I don’t know all what could happen from taking that medication while pregnant. Whereas, with like methadone, I feel like it’s been around for a lot longer. It seems like it’s been more like tested, tried and true, type deal. So, when I asked them, it was like, “Hey, well, maybe, methadone?” […] And they said, “Well, no. Because we only do Subutex here.”

This lack of choice between methadone and buprenorphine in jails demonstrated by the survey results and experienced by the participant meant that many people may be forced to switch from one form of MOUD to another, which is not recommended, particularly when switching from methadone to buprenorphine due to the risk of precipitated withdrawal [13, 28]. Two interviewees described being forced to switch from methadone to buprenorphine, with a third describing having to switch from buprenorphine to methadone and experiencing opioid withdrawal during the process:

So I stayed in [jail A] on Subutex for about 72 h until [jail B] came to pick me up. When [jail B] came to pick me up, I guess they do not do Subutex. [.] So what they did was I had to wait 48 h before I could get methadone on the Subutex. So when I got to [jail B] I sat there for 48 h, detoxing, and then, they gave me the methadone.

Furthermore, interviewees recounted experiences of their MOUD doses being increased or decreased without their consent. While dose increases are often required in pregnancy, patients should be counseled about dose adjustments and be a part of such treatment decisions. Across our analyses, we identified jails limiting the options available for patients, forcing switching, and overall limiting their autonomy in treatment.

Use of non-evidence-based MOUD in pregnancy or Non-DEA- approved opioids as substitutes for MOUD

Of the existing FDA-approved MOUD, only methadone and buprenorphine are the established evidence-based treatments during pregnancy [13, 14]. Naltrexone, which is commonly used for non-pregnant individuals with OUD, is not evidence-based for use in pregnancy due to high return to use rates and limited data on pregnancy efficacy [14, 18, 29]. Other opioid agonists are not approved as OUD treatment for either pregnant or non-pregnant individuals, and therefore we classified their use as deviations from the standard of care. National correctional guidelines recommend that jails that cannot provide methadone or buprenorphine should rapidly transfer patients to a setting that can [28].

In our jail survey, 3% (n = 21) of respondents indicated that their facility initiated treatment with naltrexone during pregnancy for individuals who were not previously on it. 12% (n = 100) of surveyed jails continued naltrexone for individuals on it prior to entry. While naltrexone may be used in pregnancy with shared decision-making if an individual was stable on it before pregnancy, it is not recommended for initiation during pregnancy.

Additionally, two facilities reported using codeine/paracetamol (also known as Tylenol #3) as maintenance treatment instead of methadone or buprenorphine for the duration of the pregnant individual’s stay in jail, with one respondent explaining they chose this to avoid having to transport patients offsite to the nearest methadone clinic.

Other jails described using non-MOUD opioids for temporary management for OUD in pregnancy, with three respondents noting their institution used hydrocodone or codeine/paracetamol as bridge therapy while individuals awaited access to methadone or buprenorphine. This was also described by one of the pregnant individuals we interviewed:

Interviewer: How long until you got that first dose of Methadone? […]

Respondent: I would say about a week.

Interviewer: Oh, wow. So you went through detox for a week?

Respondent: Well, it wasn’t straight detox. What they were doing, they had gave me a Tylenol Three, Tylenol with codeine. […] To help with the withdrawal until I got to the Methadone treatment center.

Interviewer: And were you comfortable on the Tylenol Threes?

Respondent: I say just until about 20–25 min of me taking it and then after that, no.

The use of non-approved opioids as alternatives to methadone or buprenorphine was commonly reported for both short-term bridging and long-term use in jails, violating clinical guidelines of pregnancy OUD management.

Frequent experiences of withdrawal from treatment delays and lack of MOUD

BJA guidelines state that all patients at risk of opioid withdrawal should have same-day access to treatment with buprenorphine and methadone, and treatment should be started immediately [28]. In pregnancy, withdrawal should be avoided, and patients should be immediately transferred to a facility with access to methadone or buprenorphine if the jail is unable to provide appropriate care [28].

Twenty-three percent (n = 190) of jail survey respondents indicated that their facility did not provide any form of MOUD in pregnancy, and pregnant people in custody had to go through withdrawal. This was true regardless of whether they were receiving treatment prior to entry. Many of the interviewed women experienced this, with one describing:

When I first came in and I was withdrawing, if they would have told me there was medical treatment, like if I could have been on methadone or Suboxone or something, I would have jumped on that just to feel better. I felt like I was dying.

We learned from the qualitative interviews that even women who received MOUD often had to wait hours to days between jail intake and receiving medication, delays that frequently led to opioid withdrawal:

I didn’t get it while I was in there because they took too long to reach out to my clinic. So, I had to go two days without it. […] I was pissed off. I was sick. You know what I mean? Because I hadn’t dosed that day. So, I went two whole days. I was sick. I was scared to death because I don’t know how - you know? I was told that, if you go through withdrawal on methadone, that the baby can self-abort.

Overall, experiences and descriptions of opioid withdrawal were common even in jails where MOUD was available. Interviewees emphasized their profound distress while experiencing withdrawal in jail due to physical discomfort and concern for the health of their fetuses.

Substandard opioid withdrawal management

While opioid withdrawal is not recommended in pregnancy, if a pregnant individual chooses to undergo withdrawal, it should be done under the supervision of an experienced provider with symptom management medications that are safe in pregnancy [14]. The most effective medications to manage withdrawal symptoms are methadone and buprenorphine, administered in a tapered fashion to treat symptoms [13]. Additional non-opioid medications may be used, such as alpha-2 adrenergic agonists, antihistamines, antiemetics, acetaminophen, and anti-diarrhea medications; ibuprofen is to be avoided in pregnancy [13, 28].

Jail survey respondents described a variety of approaches for managing opioid withdrawal symptoms in pregnancy. Of the jails not offering MOUD, only 16% (n = 133) used a methadone or buprenorphine taper. Over a third of the facilities (n = 303) utilized second-line non-opioid medications, including antihistamines, alpha agonists, and anticholinergics, which, ideally, should only be used as adjuncts to methadone or buprenorphine tapers. Benzodiazepines, not recommended for opioid withdrawal management, were also used for pregnancy opioid withdrawal management. Among jails that used non-opioid medications for withdrawal, two particularly alarming practices were noted: one respondent mentioned use of lithium, and another described using naloxone. 14% (n = 114) of jails used prescription opioids other than methadone and buprenorphine, most commonly codeine, acetaminophen with codeine, or hydrocodone. These medications are not approved for withdrawal management [28]. Furthermore, the use of acetaminophen with codeine raises concern that jails could give too much acetaminophen while prioritizing the opioid effects of codeine. Concerningly, at 9% (n = 79) of the surveyed facilities, withdrawal in pregnancy was done “cold turkey” without any medication support.

In our interviews many women described being forced to withdraw with insufficient or no medications and limited medical attention:

They give you nothing but Ativan and clonidine, and to me, that does nothing to help with the withdrawals. […] Going through withdrawals, we experience seizures. We experience diarrhea. We experience restless leg and restless body syndromes, vomiting, no appetite, suicidal thoughts. You know, it’s extremely bad, it really is. And especially to have to be on or addicted to opioids, and then when you get incarcerated, just completely just cold turkey have to go through it is extremely bad.

Whether due to delays in administering MOUD or due to lack of MOUD, many participants elaborated their experience of withdrawal in jail while pregnant in harrowing terms, describing them as worse than any withdrawal they had ever had—with no control over their physical environment, no privacy, and limited access to fluids. While any experience of withdrawal in jail, especially without subsequent maintenance MOUD, increases the risk of return to use and overdose upon release, practices that manage withdrawal with naloxone—which precipitates further withdrawal—and lithium—a known teratogen—are particularly concerning. The insufficient or lack of treatment for withdrawal can also lead to severe physical and emotional distress, with interviewees consistently describing fears that withdrawal in jail would harm their fetuses.

Postpartum MOUD discontinuation

Postpartum continuation of MOUD is recommended, as the risk of return to use and death is even higher in the postpartum period than during pregnancy [10, 14]. Of the surveyed jails that provided MOUD for pregnant individuals, 76% (n = 384) discontinued treatment once the pregnancy ended. Of those that provided further details, withdrawal was done immediately after delivery at 70 jails, while 192 jails gradually tapered postpartum individuals’ MOUD. Of the 32 women interviewed, 5 described or anticipated MOUD discontinuation in jail if they remained in custody after their delivery:

After I have the baby, that’s it. They completely make us cold turkey quit in here. And coming off of methadone is worse than coming off of heroin. Honestly, just to be honest with you, I’d rather die than have to go through the methadone withdrawals. So, I’m really not looking forward to that at all. I’m actually really petrified. So, last time, I had a lot of very bad suicidal thoughts when I came off of methadone because it makes you feel, like, it makes you hallucinate. I don’t understand why [they don’t continue MOUD]. That’s not a medication that, you know, that I’m just prescribed to in here. That is a medication that I’m prescribed to on the street that I’ve been taking for two and a half years. I’m mandated by the court to have to take that. But once I have the baby, the jail will not allow me to take it no more.

Postpartum MOUD treatment discontinuation poses severe risks to maternal health, including fatal overdose, during a particularly vulnerable period [14]. Across interviews, participants demonstrated awareness of these dangers and extreme anxiety and distress about being taken off their MOUD.

Lack of counseling around OUD and MOUD in pregnancy

Providing accurate, comprehensive, patient-centered, pregnancy-specific counseling is a critical element of the standard care for OUD in pregnancy, allowing patients to make informed decisions about their health, including for those in jail [14, 28]. Throughout interviews, a lack of or insufficient information on OUD in pregnancy provided in jails was a common theme. One woman explained:

I mean it would definitely help, I mean just to know what I’m supposed to be going through and what I shouldn’t. Like, right now, I mean detoxing every day, is that hurting my baby still? … Like, I don’t know. So, maybe a little bit of paperwork on that wouldn’t have been a bad thing.

Many of the interviewees received inaccurate or no counseling regarding the effects of MOUD, whether on their health, that of their baby, or breastfeeding safety. This absence of pregnancy-specific counseling contributed to the fears and anxieties women experienced, and led some of them to plan their own MOUD taper or discontinuation upon leaving jail, either during pregnancy with the goal of protecting their baby or before breastfeeding, with the belief that breastfeeding while on MOUD would harm their baby.

Discussion

In these secondary analyses of a national jail survey and qualitative interviews around pregnancy OUD treatment in jail, we found many notable deviations from the standard of care. Through this mixed-methods study, we were able to describe substandard practices reported directly from jail staff while highlighting how pregnant individuals with OUD experienced them in jail custody. We recognize that much attention and research has been dedicated to expanding MOUD access to individuals in custody over the last few years, including assessing the implementation of MOUD in custody as well as capturing the perceptions of carceral staff and people with lived experience [30–36]. However, our study adds to the existing literature regarding OUD treatment for pregnant individuals in jails by delineating specific ways that treatment and care for this population deviated from the standards of care set by ACOG, BJA, and SAMHSA [13, 14, 28].

All pregnant individuals with OUD in jail should have access to continue and initiate methadone or buprenorphine. In our survey, only 18% of jails met this standard, consistent with previous studies indicating insufficient access to jail-based MOUD while pregnant [21, 22]. Our study amplifies these prior studies’ focus on availability or lack of MOUD in pregnancy by describing specific substandard pregnancy OUD practices in jail.

Beyond the lack of MOUD access in jails, our study found additional practices that are alarming in the treatment of pregnant people with OUD. When considering both methadone and buprenorphine—two equally effective medications for OUD—the decision should be left to the patient [13]. Our study found that very few jails provide pregnant individuals with a choice between methadone and buprenorphine (18%, n = 152), with two interviewees reporting being forced to switch from methadone to buprenorphine, which is not recommended due to the risk of precipitating severe withdrawal [37]. Additionally, studies with non-pregnant individuals have shown that not providing an individual’s preferred MOUD increases disengagement in treatment and risk of overdose after release [33, 38]. Many other interviewees were forced to undergo opioid withdrawal while in jail, which increases the risk of return to substance use, and the fact that it occurred so routinely to the women we interviewed supports evidence from other studies that these dangerous practices might be very common in US jails [14, 39].

During the postpartum period, individuals are particularly vulnerable to opioid overdose, a major contributor to pregnancy-related mortality [40]. MOUD has been shown to reduce the risk of overdose death in the postpartum period by as much as 60% [19]. MOUD should be continued until the individual is in a stable social situation and desires to discontinue it [14]. Jails’ postpartum discontinuation signals a disregard for the health of the mother-infant dyad following delivery, as a healthy mother in recovery optimizes infant outcomes.

This study also reveals the concerning use of non-standard medications for pregnancy OUD treatment in jail, including initiation of naltrexone as maintenance therapy, which is generally not advised for initial treatment in pregnant people [14, 41]. Moreover, while naloxone should only be administered during pregnancy in the case of maternal overdose, we found it used for management of withdrawal symptoms. One jail also cited the use of lithium to manage OUD in pregnancy, which can cause fetal cardiac defects and is not indicated for withdrawal treatment [42]. However, the respondent who wrote “lithium” was not a health care provider and thus the practice may not be accurate, or they might have meant to write “Librium,” a benzodiazepine, a class of medications sometimes used for opioid withdrawal symptoms. Finally, some jails reported the use of non-MOUD opioid agonists such as codeine or hydromorphone, either as substitutes for methadone or buprenorphine or as bridging therapy while waiting for MOUD. Withdrawal symptom management is not an approved use of these opioid medications [28]. The use of these non-standard medications raises questions about whether the training and expertise of jail medical staff is sufficient to care for pregnant patients with OUD in custody.

Thirty-six percent (n = 303) of jails surveyed treated opioid withdrawal in pregnancy with second-line treatments instead of a methadone or buprenorphine taper, which is the standard of care [14]. While these medications should be available to pregnant individuals for symptom management in addition to an MOUD taper, they do not relieve opioid withdrawal symptoms as effectively as methadone and buprenorphine and can cause hypotension and hypertension upon withdrawal [28]. Additionally, many surveyed jails indicated that they only manage withdrawal “cold turkey” without pharmacotherapy, which is not recommended per BJA guidelines [28]. Detoxification in pregnancy is associated with a 59–90% risk of return to use, which in turn can lead to fatal overdose [17]. At the same time, release from incarceration itself is associated with an extremely high risk of fatal opioid overdose, resulting from lack of MOUD access, reduced opioid tolerance, and return to use in the community [43, 44]. Forced withdrawal in pregnancy and postpartum while in custody only amplifies overdose risk upon release [45]. Thus, many of the jails that provided MOUD to pregnant individuals in custody compromised maternal health and the mother-infant dyad by discontinuing treatment postpartum. This is consistent with data from the Bureau of Justice Statistics that demonstrates limited availability of jail-based MOUD for non-pregnant individuals [23]. We can infer from this that many jails are motivated by the health of the fetus instead of a desire to improve overall maternal health outcomes.

Lastly, our findings on the insufficient or absent MOUD counseling for pregnant individuals with OUD in jails, as well as an overall lack of autonomy over their health, highlight significant discrepancies with national care and correctional guidelines [13, 14, 28]. Counseling regarding the benefits and potential risks of MOUD in pregnancy should be available to all pregnant patients with OUD, regardless of their use of MOUD [14]. Not only did jails provide insufficient information on pregnancy and MOUD; we also noted the spread of misinformation regarding breastfeeding safety while on MOUD, leading to increased confusion among pregnant individuals. Patients should receive accurate, non-coercive counseling about the benefits of MOUD in pregnancy; potential for NOWS as a treatable, expected, short-term side effect of life saving treatment; and breastfeeding safety. Accurate counseling that addresses pregnant people’s concerns, especially for their newborns, increases the likelihood of treatment engagement and adherence [14].

While jails are constitutionally required to provide health care, our study reveals how much work still needs to be done to achieve OUD standard of care for pregnant people in jails. The lack of pregnancy and postpartum MOUD in jails indicates the need for universal and enhanced implementation of standard practices and additional resources for incarcerated individuals to have time-sensitive MOUD access. It is imperative that jails expedite channels to obtain MOUD, establish ways to maintain medication options, and facilitate immediate patient transfer when medication cannot be obtained quickly on-site. The passing of the Waiver Elimination Act in 2023, which reduced barriers for providers to be able to prescribe buprenorphine, is one example of steps that can be taken to reduce barriers to MOUD access in custody [46]. Additionally, in 2024, SAMHSA released new guidance on correctional facilities applying for clinic designation so that they can dispense methadone to people with OUD if they have a primary medical diagnosis—of which pregnancy is one [47].

Moreover, our results highlight the need for increased oversight of jail-based health care and regulations, improved training of jail administration and staff concerning OUD management during pregnancy and postpartum, and knowledge of medications that are contraindicated in the management of withdrawal.

This study has limitations. One is that the jail survey relied on the knowledge of respondents about how OUD is managed in their institution, which, depending on their degree of involvement with the facility, may not be fully accurate or up to date. Although the survey recruitment materials and completion instructions instructed that a health care provider with clinical knowledge on this topic may be best suited to complete the survey, only 20% of respondents were in a health care role [21]. And thus, practices like the use of lithium for OUD withdrawal management in pregnancy may not be accurate. Additionally, the interviews we conducted were with individuals who had been in jail in only four states, thus limiting their generalizability. Although there was overlapping content seen in both the jail surveys and the interviews, we did not correlate the lived experiences of interviewees with the corresponding survey response from the jail they were in. Finally, some of our findings from the qualitative interviews, like the delays in receiving MOUD upon jail arrival, inaccurate information, and inadequate pregnancy OUD counseling, were topics we did not directly assess in the jail survey, and thus we could not correlate interview findings with our survey data in these areas.

Conclusions

In summary, our study’s findings reveal important deviations from the standard of care in the treatment of pregnant people with OUD in US jails, both from survey data reported by jail staff and from the perspectives of individuals who were pregnant in jail with OUD. Our findings highlight opportunities to improve care for pregnant and postpartum individuals with OUD in jail and draw attention to numerous alarming practices that can result in adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. The many substandard practices reported in our study cannot be ignored, and the care of pregnant people with OUD in jails must be improved to promote health equity, reduce maternal mortality, and promote principles of autonomy and justice.
