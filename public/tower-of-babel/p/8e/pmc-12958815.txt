Delayed transcriptional response of Daphnia pulex to thermal stress
Authors: Nathan J C Backenstose, Allison M Nalesnik, Margaret K Bui, Ilinca I Ciubotariu, Catherine L Searle, Mark R Christie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTemperature fluctuations impose significant physiological challenges on aquatic invertebrates, with far-reaching consequences that span from cellular to ecosystem levels. Even low to moderate heat stress can activate molecular responses that reshape development, metabolism, and reproduction. In this study, we investigated the transcriptional response of Daphnia pulex, a common grazer in lentic freshwater systems, to sublethal temperature stress (a temperature below the acute lethal limit, allowing for survival during chronic exposure). D. pulex were exposed to control (20 °C) and elevated sublethal (25 °C) temperatures to simulate an increased water temperature from a mild heat wave for 168 h. Our findings indicate a dynamic transcriptional response to elevated temperatures. Notably, differential gene expression between the control and temperature-elevated treatment increased throughout the experiment with a 3-fold increase in counts of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from 247 at 96 h to 743 at 168 h. Changes in gene expression were related to development, specifically reproduction, at 96 h, and a shift toward metabolic processes occurred at 168 h. D. pulex within the experimental treatment generally had higher mean cumulative offspring produced compared to the control treatment. Given D. pulex's role as a foundational species in aquatic food webs, the observed transcriptional response provides insight into the potential for both plastic and adaptive responses in the face of environmental change.

Introduction

Invertebrate poikilotherms, whose body temperatures conform to their ambient environment, confront physiological challenges under thermal stress, with consequences extending across several levels of biological organization (Everatt et al. 2015). Thermal variation above optimal ranges can activate a burst of cellular and molecular responses, and in extreme cases, denature proteins, which in turn can impact vital functions of an organism (Somero 2020). Understanding these physiological consequences is vital as temperature changes can disrupt enzyme kinetics (Somero 1969), alter membrane fluidity (Pruitt 1990), reduce metabolic rates (Chen and Stillman 2012), and compromise critical functions such as reproduction (Goss 1983). By integrating omics data with physiological responses, such as analyzing how functional groups of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) align with specific temperature-induced changes in metabolic pathways, a mechanistic understanding can be developed. This approach can provide insights into an organism's thermal biology, offering a framework for predicting how species will respond to environmental change.

Freshwater microcrustaceans within the genus Daphnia (Daphniidae) are ideal organisms for studying environmental stress in aquatic systems (Ebert 2022). The genus Daphnia comprises over 100 described species with a near global distribution, inhabiting waterways ranging from ephemeral pools to large lakes (Ebert 2005). Daphnia spp. are primary consumers that serve as an important link in aquatic food webs. Daphnia, along with other Cladocerans, exhibit a life history strategy known as cyclic or facultative parthenogenesis, characterized by alternating periods of sexual and asexual reproduction. Such life history strategies allow for isofemale lines (hereafter “clones”) to be maintained in the laboratory with relative ease, making Daphnia invaluable model organisms in genetics studies and eco-evolutionary research (Stollewerk 2010; Ebert 2022). Clonal lines offer a key advantage in genetic experiments by ensuring genetic homogeneity among replicates, which allows for limited variation in genetic background when quantifying variability between treatments in an experiment (Ebert 2022). A widespread and abundant representative of this genus, the species complex D. pulex, is native to and commonly found throughout the Holarctic with introductions occurring in South America (Crease et al. 2012), Japan (So et al. 2015) and New Zealand (Ye et al. 2021). This species has high-quality genomic resources for determining molecular and genetic responses to environmental change (Chaturvedi et al. 2021; reviewed in Miner et al. 2012). Genomic resources for D. pulex are well developed (Colbourne et al. 2011; Lynch et al. 2024) as its relatively small genome size (∼ 200 Mb) enables cost-effective sequencing, and its ability to be reared asexually in laboratory conditions makes it a highly tractable model species for experimental research (Ebert 2005, 2022).

Previous research with D. pulex has highlighted physiological, behavioral, and phenotypic responses to thermal stress. The upper limit (100% lethal [LD100]) of D. pulex for temperature has been documented at 36 °C (Mortimer 1936). Responses to increased temperature include changes in metabolic rates, mitochondrial function (Kake-Guena et al. 2017), and reproductive output (Goss 1983). For example, increased temperatures can lead to downregulation in various metabolic rates (Yampolsky et al. 2014), which may affect energy balance, growth, and survival. Clone-specific variation in gene expression patterns for acute and chronic heat stress exposure have also been characterized, showing initial upregulation of stress genes (eg molecular chaperones) and downregulation of metabolic genes, alongside acclimation responses and differing proteomic shifts between tolerant and less tolerant clones (Becker et al. 2018). Various life history traits related to reproduction in D. pulex vary with temperature including offspring production, age at first reproduction, size at first clutch, and time between clutches (Walls and Ventelä 1998; Ashforth and Yan 2008; Betini et al. 2020). Additionally, Daphnia can also exhibit behavioral changes, such as shifts in vertical migration and altered feeding patterns, in response to thermal stress (Glaholt et al. 2016). Such time-dependent physiological responses to thermal variation, including shifts in oxidative stress, cellular defense, growth, and energy allocation, can have strong implications for organismal survival and reproductive output (Im et al. 2020).

Despite the documented physiological and behavioral responses to elevated temperature, identifying gene expression responses in tandem with a correlate of fitness such as fecundity, has not been investigated. Quantifying changes in gene expression is essential for understanding how D. pulex may adapt to thermal stress at the cellular and molecular levels. D. pulex can respond plastically to changes in their environment, both morphologically (Lüning 1992; Black 1993) and in terms of gene expression (Yampolsky et al. 2014). This phenotypic and transcriptional plasticity is a key component to the adaptive capacity of D. pulex, allowing the species to maintain fitness in variable and stressful environments (Ghalambor et al. 2015). For example, when exposed to chemical signatures of predator species (eg kairomones), Daphnia spp. have been shown to change their transcriptional profiles for genes related to reproduction, structure of the exoskeleton, and metabolism, with those heritable changes persisting in future generations (Hales et al. 2017). A deeper investigation into gene expression responses can elucidate the molecular mechanisms of genetic adaptation to a changing environment and, more generally, enhance our understanding of evolutionary consequences of thermal stress in D. pulex populations.

Here, we analyzed the transcriptional response of six clones of D. pulex to characterize the overall response to an elevated temperature regime. After acclimation and propagation through maternal lines, representatives of six clones collected from the wild were exposed to elevated temperatures for a period of 7 d. A control and sublethal elevated temperature were used to simulate increased water temperature from a mild heat wave event. Samples were collected at two different time points during the experiment for individual RNA sequencing and the number of offspring produced daily was measured as a correlate of fitness. Our objectives were to: (i) identify changes in patterns of gene expression after short-term (acute) or prolonged exposure to thermal stress, (ii) determine the physiological and molecular mechanisms of heat stress, and (iii) measure differences in fitness measured as average cumulative fecundity per day. Exposure to sublethal thermal stress in D. pulex resulted in a dynamic transcriptional response with an increase in the number of significant DEGs after prolonged exposure compared to short-term exposure, including genes involved in reproduction, metabolic processes, and homeostasis.

Methods

Sample collection and rearing

D. pulex were collected at seven pond sites across Indiana, USA, and a single pond site in New York, USA (Supplementary Methods). At each site, 10 to 15 liters of pond water were collected and filtered using a 333-micron mesh screen and resuspended in 1 L bottles. Samples were transported from field sites to Purdue University (Indiana, USA) and examined under a dissection microscope (Olympus SZX16/SZ61). D. pulex individuals were identified to species level via morphological features, acknowledging the existence of the Holarctic D. pulex species complex, ensuring each representative had the attributes of D. pulex sensu Hebert (1995; Supplemental Methods). Samples were then isolated into individual 50 mL beakers of well water and fed 5 × 105 cells of Ankistrodesmus falcatus algae daily. After the first clutch was produced by a field-collected individual, all offspring were transferred to a 400 mL beaker of well water and fed 4.0 × 106 cells of A. falcatus algae daily. These lines were reared for several generations, such that clones were established for a single individual from each site. To standardize possible maternal and grandmaternal effects (eg Agrelius and Dudycha 2025), we created maternal lines by selecting a juvenile individual from the community beaker and rearing it alone in a 50 mL beaker, with the first two clutches of offspring discarded. An individual from the third clutch was then removed and reared alone, and this process was repeated to establish a controlled environment and density for maternal lines. Throughout the rearing process, excess offspring were removed to maintain densities of one individual per 50 mL well water. Ultimately, all experimental clones were derived from at least second-clutch offspring of third-generation maternal lines, as is standard practice to standardize environmental, maternal, and grandmaternal effects on gene expression (Searle et al. 2015; Strauss et al. 2019). Neonates were isolated from each maternal line and individually housed in 50 mL conical tubes at ambient room temperature (20 °C) for 12 d prior to being exposed to different temperature regimes in the experiment.

Experimental design

Laboratory experiments were conducted at Purdue University (West Lafayette, IN, USA). Ten 45-gallon stock tanks filled with deionized water were used to create large water baths for the control and experimental treatments of 20 °C and 25 °C under a 16:8 light and dark cycle. There were five water baths for each treatment for a total of ten baths (Fig. 1a). Floating within each water bath, D. pulex individuals were kept in uncapped 50 mL conical centrifuge tubes with each tube held in place by tube racks, ensuring the top of the tube remained just above the surface of the water bath. In total, 800 individual D. pulex were included across all ten tanks with representatives of each clone evenly distributed between control and experimental treatments (Fig. 1a). Experimental water baths were heated above ambient temperature by Hygger HG-802 aquarium heaters and circulated with Marineland Maxi-Jet 400 water pumps. To monitor water temperature within the tanks, three control and three treatment stock tanks were equipped with HOBO (Bourne, MA, USA) submersible temperature loggers. The four remaining stock tank temperatures were monitored with manual blue-spirit thermometer readings and Oaklon PCTSTestr 50 digital thermometer. Feeding was also conducted daily with each replicate receiving 5 × 105 cells of A. falcatus algae. Water changes within each conical tube were completed every other day, except for individuals selected for fecundity quantification (described in the next section). Individuals that were selected for mRNA sequencing were collected from their tubes at 96 and 168 h after the start of the experiment. All RNA-seq samples were preserved in Qiagen Buffer RLT and stored at −80 °C.

Correlates of fitness

To quantify correlates of fitness during the experiment, the reproductive capacity of each individual (fecundity) was used as a metric. Offspring were collected from each individual across the treatments once per day at a consistent time interval. Each conical tube of adult and offspring was carefully transferred into a 50 mL beaker. After refilling the conical tube with well water at the appropriate treatment temperature, the adult was returned to the respective experiment tube. The offspring were poured over filter paper, carefully folded into a 5 mL tube, preserved in 95% EtOH, and then frozen at −20 °C for storage and counting after the experiment. To quantify the offspring on each filter, the samples were removed from the freezer and thawed prior to counting under a dissecting scope. All counts were conducted three times by the same individual. A consensus of the counts was used to calculate the mean and standard deviation of cumulative offspring produced for each treatment per clone per day. At each time interval for each clone, cumulative fecundity measures between the treatments were compared for statistical differences with a Wilcoxon rank sum test and corrected for multiple comparisons with the sequential Bonferroni method. Time between clutches of offspring for each individual was also calculated, and a Wilcoxon rank sum test with sequential Bonferroni correction was used to test for significance between treatments within each clone.

RNA extraction and sequencing

D. pulex samples were individually homogenized and bulk RNA was extracted with Qiagen RNeasy Micro Kits (Germantown, MD, USA) adhering to manufacturer protocol. The mRNA libraries were prepped and sequenced by Novogene (Sacramento, CA, USA) on a single lane on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Short variant calling

Variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK; McKenna et al. 2010) RNA-Seq short variant discovery pipeline. Reads were checked for quality with FastQC (Andrews 2010) and trimmed of adapter sequence with trim-galore v0.6.10 (Krueger 2012). Paired-end RNA-Seq reads were then aligned to the D. pulex reference genome (NCBI Accession: GCF_021134715.1) with STAR v2.7.9a (Dobin et al. 2013) in two-pass mode. Alignment files were sorted, filtered for mapping quality (−q 20), and read depth was quantified with SAMtools v1.17 (Danecek et al. 2021). Reads that originated from the same DNA fragment were marked in the alignment file with the Picard Toolkit v2.26.10 MarkDuplicates (Picard Tools—By Broad Institute). Reads containing splice sites were identified and split with GATK v4.3.0.0 (McKenna et al. 2010) SplitNCigarReads. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as well as indels were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller and then flagged with the VariantFiltration subcommand. Quality by depth, root mean square mapping quality, and strand bias thresholds were set with the following commands “QD < 2.0, MQ < 40.0, & FS > 60.0”. GATK SelectVariants was used to remove the filtered calls, non-variant sites, and extract SNPs. Initial SNP calling with GATK4 produced 2,191,699 SNPs. After filtering these data for minor allele frequency and missingness, a total of 324,247 SNPs remained. One sample was removed due to > 80% missing data (noted in Supplementary Table 1).

Clonal validation

To verify the independence of the clonal lines, we analyzed the SNPs generated from the GATK pipeline. The variant call file was converted to a binary dataset with plink2 v2.00a2.3 (Chang et al. 2015) and purged of multiallelic variants and palindromic sites with the “clean_data.R” script from the RGStraP pipeline (Fachrul et al. 2023). Additional filtering with plink2 for minor allele frequency (−maf 0.05) and missing data for both individual (−mind 0.1) and genotype (−geno 0.2) were applied before re-coding to variant call format and running principal component analysis (PCA) for mitochondrial SNPs using the “–pca” flag. Additional filters for missing data for both individual (−mind 0.3) and genotype (−geno 0.1) were applied for PCA of genome-wide SNPs with R package vegan (Oksanen et al. 2025). Pairwise relatedness and pairwise FST values among clones were also calculated (Supplementary Methods).

Differentially expressed genes

To compare the transcriptional response among control and experimental replicates, we mapped and assembled transcripts to quantify gene expression. First, the paired-end RNA sequencing reads were mapped to the indexed D. pulex reference (NCBI Accession: GCF_021134715.1) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 (Kim et al. 2019). Next, the resulting alignment files were converted to binary format and sorted using SAMtools v1.17 (Danecek et al. 2021). Alignment files for each sample were used to reconstruct transcripts, assign them to known genes, and estimate their abundances with Stringtie v2.2.1 (Kovaka et al. 2019). The assembled transcripts from all samples were then merged into a comprehensive annotation file using StringTie –merge (Kovaka et al. 2019). The FeatureCounts subcommand of the Subread package v1.6.4 (Liao et al. 2013) was used to count the features of the annotation files, quantifying the number of reads overlapping with specified genomic features such as exons and genes. Feature count data, sample information, and the merged annotated transcripts were imported into the statistical software R v4.0.4 (Team 2020) and analyzed with DESeq2 package (Love et al. 2014).

A DESeq2 matrix was created that implemented the clone as a covariate and the experimental condition as the primary variable in the design for each sampling time point (96 and 168 h; see Love et al. 2025 for more details on matrix design). Analyses were performed using a standard DESeq2 pipeline; count data were normalized, size factors estimated, and a Wald test was used to identify DEGs between the controls and the experimental treatments. Results of the tests were filtered with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P-values to compensate for a false discovery rate (Padj < 0.05), and log2 fold changes (log2FC) were calculated to enumerate the magnitude of differential gene expression. To summarize expression patterns across treatments, a PCA of significant (Padj < 0.05) DEGs with log2FC > |0.2| was completed with the DESeq2 plotPCA function in R.

DEG annotation

To identify the specific DEGs identified through DESeq2, the genomic positions of DEGs were annotated. Sequences of the DEGs were extracted from the reference genome with the getfasta command of BEDtools v2.31.0 (Quinlan and Hall 2010). Functional annotation of the sequences was performed using emapper v2.1.7 (Cantalapiedra et al. 2021) based on the eggNOG orthology database v5 (Huerta-Cepas et al. 2019) with sequence searches completed by DIAMOND (Buchfink et al. 2021). Annotations were filtered for score (−evalue 0.01) and non-electronic gene ontology (GO) terms were reported for each differentially expressed gene.

Functional analysis of DEGs

For each sampling time point, the complete list of GO terms from the functionally annotated DEGs provided from the eggNOG database were concatenated and the frequency of occurrence was calculated for each term. The lists for each time point, containing the GO term and its frequency of occurrence, were summarized with the REVIGO online tool (Supek et al. 2011) using the Drosophila melanogaster ontology with the resulting list size set to Medium (0.7) and obsolete GO terms removed. For each list, multidimensional scaling (MDS) was applied by REVIGO to a semantic similarity matrix of the Biological Process GO terms, of which the first two dimensions were plotted. To group functionally similar GO terms within this reduced dimension space, clusters were identified using the K-means algorithm. The optimum number of clusters (K) was calculated using the gap statistic method with 500 bootstraps from the software package NbClust in R (Charrad et al. 2014). To provide insight into the functional characteristics of the GO terms at each sampling time point, the GO term with the highest frequency of occurrence in each cluster was determined and labeled on the REVIGO MDS plots. To provide further insight into the characteristics of the GO term clusters, the top two clusters from each time point containing the highest number of GO terms were isolated and plotted independently. The GO term descriptions for each of the clusters were parsed with a semantic isolation process to capture recurrent character strings within GO term descriptions (eg “transport” was the most frequent string at 96 h). If a GO term contained the character string that had the highest frequency of occurrence, it was annotated on the MDS plot of isolated clusters. Importantly, the K-means clustering approach was only used for visually illustrating and simplifying GO terms.

To understand the temporal dynamics of these biological processes, we compared the GO terms across clusters at different time points. GO terms from all clusters at each time point were compared for overlapping and unique terms. Terms that matched between clusters at each sampling time were compiled into a comparison matrix and plotted on a chord diagram with the circlize package in R (Gu et al. 2014). A second chord diagram was also created showing unique GO terms at each of the time points.

Results

Genetic variation revealed distinct clonal structure

The PCA of mitochondrial SNPs showed the clones largely overlapped with others within their clonal assignment (Supplementary Fig. 1). PC1 accounted for 39.7% of the variation with clones C5 and C6 clones forming an overlapping cluster and separated from the remaining four clones. Clones C2 and C3 were overlapping as well and differentiated from the remaining clones along PC2 (32.6% variation). Using data from 324,247 nuclear SNPs in principal components analysis, the 161 samples fall into six discrete clusters (Fig. 1b); these six clusters correspond to the six clones. This clonal structure is corroborated by analysis of relatedness measures and FST (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Table 2).

Elevated temperatures increased D. pulex fecundity

The control and experimental treatments were maintained at or very near their target temperature of 20 °C and 25 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). The increased temperature in the experimental treatment had a positive effect on the fecundity of the D. pulex clones. The mean cumulative fecundity on the final day of the experiment was higher in the experimental treatment for all clones except C5 compared to the control treatment, but these differences in means were only significantly different in clone C2 at two time points (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 3). Throughout the experiment, the cumulative fecundity in clone C2 was significantly higher in the experimental treatment at hours 120 and 144 compared to the control (Padj < 0.05). The experimental treatment temperature did not cause a significant change in the timing between clutches for all clones, though clone C3 was suggestive of such effects with a corrected P-value of 0.0504 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Overall, cumulative fecundity varied by both the specific clone and temperature exposure.

Measurements of fecundity in Daphnia pulex clones in the control (20 °C) and experimental (25 °C) treatment. Each panel represents the mean cumulative offspring produced for clones C1–C6 for the control and experimental treatment every 24 h. Error bars are +/− 1 standard deviation.

Transcriptomic data generated from D. pulex samples

In total, 161 RNA-seq samples were collected from 79 and 82 individuals, sequenced at 96 and 168 h, respectively. Sampling across clones and treatments was similar at each time point with N = 9–21 D. pulex individuals collected per clone and 39–41 individuals per temperature treatment (see Supplementary Table 1 for details of RNA-seq sampling effort). Illumina RNA sequencing of 161 D. pulex samples produced 1.688 terabases of data with an average total sequence length per sample of 1.049 × 1010 base pairs (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 1). When aligning reads with HISAT2, the percentage of reads mapped to the D. pulex reference genome was between 72.04 and 89.40%, with a breadth of coverage (percentage of reference genome that is covered by at least one read) that ranged from 35.27 to 65.62%, and a proper pair percentage between 64.29 and 82.93% (Supplementary Supplemental Table 1). The total number of aligned reads per sample ranged from 45.92–88.83 million (mean 61.75 million; Supplementary Table 1).

Dynamic gene expression in functional groups of genes

The experimental temperature treatment induced a dynamic transcriptional response through time with substantially more DEGs identified at 168 h than 96 h. Significant (Padj < 0.05) DEG counts for the 6 clones at the 96- and 168-h sampling times equated to 247 and 743, respectively, with more than twice as many genes upregulated than downregulated in the experimental (25 °C) compared to the control (20 °C) at each time point (Fig. 3a). There were 74 DEGs that were shared between the time points with 41 upregulated at both time points, 32 downregulated at both time points, and a single gene switching from downregulated to upregulated (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Table 4). PCA of significant DEGs (Padj < 0.05) when filtered for genes >0.2 log2FC showed a separation between the control and experimental treatment with some overlap at 96 h (Fig. 1c).

Volcano plots of all genes expressed at d) 96- and e) 168-h time points. Light blue dots in the volcano plots represent genes that were significantly upregulated (Padj < 0.05). Labeled on each panel are the most upregulated genes, which were functionally annotated as clip-domain serine protease (clipa10) and cuticular protein analogous to peritrophins 3-B (cpap3-b) at 96- and 168-h time points, respectively. Dark blue dots represent genes that were significantly downregulated (Padj < 0.05). Labeled on each panel are the most downregulated genes, which were functionally annotated as cuticular protein RR-2 (cpr23) and mitochondrial translational activator (mss51) at 96- and 168-h time points, respectively.

Annotation of significant (Padj < 0.05) DEGs revealed a shift in gene functions in the experimental group between the 96- and 168-h sampling points. DEGs annotated from the eggNOG database produced 159 (64.4%) and 477 (64.20%) hits for the early and later time points, respectively. At 96 h, the gene with the highest log2FC (7.87) was identified as clipa10, which is a clip-domain serine protease (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 5). Clip-domain serine proteases are non-digestive proteases that are found in the hemolymph of arthropods, and clipa proteins can act as regulators of proteolytic activity (Kanost and Jiang 2015). The most downregulated gene (log2FC = −11.06) was in the family cuticular protein RR-2 (cpr23), which is an insect cuticle protein (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 5). The gene with the highest log2FC (9.26) that was functionally annotated at 168 h was cuticular protein analogous to peritrophins 3-B (cpap3-b), and is described as a chitin binding, and part of the chitin metabolic process (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 6). cpap3 proteins provide structural integrity to insect cuticles (Tajiri et al. 2017), and other proteins in this family have been shown to be expressed in reproductive organs of oriental fruit fly (Bactrocera dorsalis) during pupariation, the transition to the pupal stage of metamorphosis (Chen et al. 2018). Additionally, there were nine upregulated genes at 168 h that were also annotated as cuticular proteins (Supplementary Table 6). The gene with the lowest log2FC (−2.40) at 168 h was mitochondrial translational activator mss51 (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 6).

Changes in gene function of significant (Padj < 0.05) DEGs between the control and experimental treatment were categorized with GO terms and clustered based on frequency of occurrence in the PCA (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 7, Supplementary Table 8). At the 96-h sampling point, the GO terms with the highest frequency in each cluster were broadly associated with “response”, “process”, and “development” (Fig. 4a). The clusters that contained the most terms were characterized with GO terms related to “transport” and “development” (Fig. 4b). The “development” GO terms indicate a focus on reproduction with related terms including “embryo development”, “animal organ development”, and “post-embryonic development” (Fig. 4b). At 168 h, GO terms with the highest frequency in each cluster were also associated with “process”, “regulation”, “organization”, and “transport” (Fig. 4c). The clusters with the most terms (clusters five and seven) were associated with terms related to “development” and “process”. The “process” GO terms indicated genes were associated with metabolism, such as “cellular metabolic process” and “macromolecule metabolic process” (Fig. 4d). There were 216 shared GO terms among all clusters at 96 and 168 h (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 4). The highest overlap occurred between cluster one at 96 h and cluster five at 168 h with 41 GO terms being shared. Unique terms were disproportionate between the sampling time points with eight unique GO terms found at 96 h and 276 unique GO terms at 168 h (Fig. 5b). At the 96-h time point, the GO terms are related to “stress response”, “homeostasis”, and “reproductive process” (Fig. 5c). The 168-h time point also contained the “reproductive process” GO term but also contained terms indicating a “response to stimulus” and the regulation of that response (Fig. 5c). Overall, temporal analysis of DEGs revealed a shift from development and stress-related gene functions at 96 h to metabolic and regulatory processes at 168 h, indicating a dynamic, time-dependent response to the experimental treatment in D. pulex clones.

Discussion

This study investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying D. pulex responses to sublethal thermal stress by examining shifts in gene expression and fitness (fecundity) over a 168-h experiment. We found differential gene expression between the control and experimental treatment increased throughout the experiment with a 3-fold increase in counts of DEGs at 168 h as compared to 96 h (Fig. 3a). The fecundity of the clones was measured as mean cumulative fecundity in each treatment, which we included as a readily measurable phenotype to assess whether temperature had an effect on the study organisms. Our fecundity data described that individuals in the experimental treatment produced more offspring than their counterparts in the control treatment (Fig. 2). DEGs varied in GO profiles at each time point. At 96 h, 15% of GO terms from DEGs were associated with development (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Table 7). The GO terms associated with DEGs at 168 h were largely associated with stress and response to stimulus (Fig. 4c, d, Supplementary Table 8). While the 168-h time point exhibited a larger number of differentially expressed genes, the analysis also revealed an increase in the diversity of GO terms associated with those genes compared to the 96-h time point (Fig. 5b), suggesting a broader range of biological processes affected during the continued exposure to heat stress.

The identification of specific genes and pathways involved in the thermal stress response provides a mechanistic understanding of known physiological changes in Daphnia spp. and other aquatic ectotherms. Contrary to the classic response to thermal stress (Feder and Hofmann 1999; Kumar et al. 2024), we did not observe any significant (Padj < 0.05) upregulation of heat shock proteins (HSPs). Our thermal stress treatment may not have been severe enough to trigger these pathways, or perhaps the D. pulex samples expressed these genes outside of our sampling time range (ie outside of the 96- and 168-h points). Our findings of increased DEGs associated with “stress” and “response to stimulus” at 168 h align with previous studies that have shown transcriptional response to persistent heat stress occurs in phases (Yampolsky et al. 2014). We observed an upregulation in genes involved in the metabolic process associated with chitin, such as cpap3-b, suggesting a role of cuticle remodeling in response to elevated temperatures. Cuticle structures of arthropods, such as exoskeletons, are reconstituted throughout their life cycle, even after reaching reproductive maturity and individuals must shed the existing cuticle to continue development (Merzendorfer and Zimoch 2003; Shi et al. 2016). When exposed to increasing temperatures, D. pulex development is accelerated with a shorter time interval between birth and the end of the first adult instar (Goss 1983). The discovery of upregulation in chitin synthesis genes under thermal stress is critical in environments experiencing rising temperatures and increased anthropogenic influence; the interaction of temperature stress and aquatic pollutants such as the commercially used insecticide diflubenzuron, which can inhibit the chitin synthesis pathway (Kato et al. 2022), could have effects on Daphnia population structure and overall ecosystem health. Furthermore, the differential expression of genes related to development suggest there is a potential impact of thermal stress on reproductive processes within D. pulex.

Most clones in the experimental treatment had higher mean cumulative fecundity relative to the controls for a majority of the experiment (Fig. 2). This result suggests that D. pulex may exhibit phenotypic plasticity or an adaptive response to sublethal thermal stress. While most clones had increased mean cumulative fecundity at 25 °C over the course of the experiment, the fecundity of only one clone at two time points was significantly different between conditions (Fig. 2b). Comparatively, when exposed to the same temperature conditions, the number of offspring produced per D. magna female was higher in the elevated temperature treatment (Im et al. 2020). However, within D. pulex, reproduction has previously been shown to decrease when deviating outside of the optimal temperature range of 15–20 °C (Goss 1983), and thus higher overall fecundity could have been the result of lower mean time between clutches in the experimental treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3). The upregulation of genes involved with development and reproduction at 96 h suggests that D. pulex may prioritize reproduction at the early stages of thermal stress, potentially as a biological bet hedging strategy, to increase the number of individuals. The stress response of altered reproduction has been documented in Daphnia spp. that are exposed to predator cues (karimones), and other environmental factors such as salinity or chemical exposure (Knops et al. 2001; Flaherty and Dodson 2005; Gonçalves et al. 2007; Jin et al. 2024). Although not measured in this experiment, the increase in fecundity may be associated with the production of male D. pulex, which would be a shift in reproductive mode to sexual reproduction. A switch from asexual to sexual reproduction would promote an increase in genetic diversity to allow for adaptation to a changing environment. Additionally, some of the mRNA that was quantified could have been from the unborn offspring of the sequenced animals. However, while we expect that differences in gene expression from unborn offspring would lead to increased variation across treatments, we do not expect that it would significantly alter our main results. Additionally, the offspring in the experimental group could have had a lower overall body condition, indicating there may be a tradeoff between quantity and quality of offspring that we were unable to detect (Im et al. 2020). As thermal stress persists in the environment, the transcriptional response of D. pulex changes and shifts toward stress response, metabolic processes, and cellular repair mechanisms (Fig. 5c). This shift is reflective of the persistent physiological burden on the organism, suggesting that gene expression changes have been regulated to maintain homeostasis. The identification of many more unique GO terms at the 168-h time point indicates that D. pulex may employ a diverse array of molecular mechanisms to cope with prolonged thermal stress, highlighting the complexity of its stress response.

This study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying D. pulex's response to sublethal thermal stress. The findings demonstrate that these organisms exhibit a complex and dynamic transcriptional response to elevated temperatures that changes through time. The complex response is characterized by the initial upregulation of genes involved with development and reproduction, which is then followed by stress response and homeostasis. D. pulex plays a critical role in aquatic food webs, serving as the basis for nutrient conversion into higher trophic levels (Lampert and Sommer 2007). Therefore, understanding the response of D. pulex to thermal stress in short-term and long-term time scales can help provide a mechanistic framework for predicting how species will respond to environmental change.
