Genome-scale metabolic modelling of the priority fungal pathogen Lichtheimia corymbifera
Authors: Wassili Dimitriew, Jaime David Acosta Espana, Thomas Krüger, Olaf Kniemeyer, Gianni Panagiotou, Stefan Schuster, Axel A Brakhage, Kerstin Voigt, Sascha Schäuble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungi harbor unique primary and secondary metabolic pathways that represent a hidden treasure of biochemical and natural compounds. Their metabolism is central to their ability to interact with, to adapt to and to survive in host environments, and to cause human disease and great harm. Among these is Lichtheimia corymbifera, an emerging causative agent of mucormycosis that has been classified as high priority fungal pathogen by the World Health Organization and is gaining increasing importance as a model organism for research on invasive fungal infections. We reconstructed a genome-scale L. corymbifera metabolic model and show substantial differential metabolic activity to process carbohydrate or amino acid carbon sources. We furthermore adapt the model to proteome changes depending on the presence of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and show fungal sphingolipid metabolic activity changes next to changes in cytoskeleton and tight junction associated PBMC activity over three days of cultivation. These insights underline the possibility that L. corymbifera can potentially scavenge host-derived lipids to fortify its own cell membrane. We demonstrate that in silico metabolic predictions can provide testable hypotheses and can lead to the identification of metabolic processes which are essential for the development of targeted antifungal drugs and novel solutions for balancing host challenges.

Introduction

Human fungal pathogens are increasingly recognized as a major global health concern, with rising incidence and mortality (Denning 2024). Climate change, biodiversity loss, and rising antifungal resistance not only compromise agricultural productivity, but also exacerbate clinical challenges in managing fungal infections, particularly in resource-limited settings (Seidel et al. 2024, Acosta-España et al. 2025, Case et al. 2025). Among a diverse range of fungal taxa, Mucorales form a varied order of filamentous pathogenic moulds affecting plants, animals, and humans (Acosta-España et al. 2024). These moulds are commonly found in soil, on decaying vegetables, or agricultural waste and have been identified as a high-priority group by the World Health Organisation (WHO) in 2022, particularly linked to a significant mortality rate in infected individuals (Fisher and Denning 2023, WHO 2023).

Fungi of the order Mucorales are the causative agents of mucormycosis, a rapidly progressing, aggressive, angioinvasive infection, particularly in immunocompromised hosts with impaired cellular immunity, such as individuals with uncontrolled diabetes or those undergoing post-transplant immunosuppression (Ibrahim et al. 2012, Francis et al. 2018, Hassan and Voigt 2019, Fathima et al. 2021, Shi et al. 2022). The COVID-19 pandemic further accentuated this threat, with COVID-19-Associated Mucormycosis (CAM) leading to widespread morbidity and mortality, particularly in India, where corticosteroid overuse and high diabetes prevalence created a permissive environment for fungal invasion (Raut and Huy 2021, Özbek et al. 2023). Clinically, the most prevalent Mucorales genera include Rhizopus spp., Mucor spp., and Lichtheimia spp., with the latter increasingly recognized for its virulence and environmental resilience (Hassan and Voigt 2019, Prakash and Chakrabarti 2019). Unlike Rhizopus spp., which has been more intensively studied, the biology of Lichtheimia spp., however, remains comparatively underexplored. Overall and despite therapeutic advances, mucormycosis remains notoriously difficult to treat, partly because of our limited understanding of the molecular mechanisms of its pathogenesis.

A number of virulence factors critical for fungal infection (Brunke et al. 2016) were identified including effective iron transport via the high-affinity iron permease FTR1 (Ibrahim et al. 2010), calcineurin (CaN) (Lee et al. 2013) or serine and aspartate proteases (SAPs) (Schwartze et al. 2014). For human fungal pathogens in general and Mucorales in particular, the metabolic component of infection and host-pathogen interactions is evident, albeit still fraught with many unknowns (Ene et al. 2014).

To shed new light on metabolic mechanisms, computational tools, including metabolic models, have proven their value in deciphering some of these riddles. At the core of these discoveries are genome-scale metabolic models of the organism of interest (Gu et al. 2019, Heinken et al. 2023, Tarzi et al. 2024, 2025). The reconstruction of a genome-scale metabolic model (GEM) is a labour-intense first step for metabolic modelling and requires data-driven curation steps to provide high-quality models (Thiele and Palsson 2010, Vlassis et al. 2014, Fritzemeier et al. 2017). Without requiring kinetic information, these computational models can be efficiently simulated to predict growth, phenotype and metabolic pathway activity. For Candida albicans and Aspergillus fumigatus, it was shown that the fungus appears to influence its environment towards a more growth beneficial microbial community (Mirhakkak et al. 2021, 2023). The molecular versatility of fungi may also provide metabolic synergies, as shown for S. boulardii, which can boost, e.g. the anti-inflammatory output of multi-kingdom probiotic communities (Hedin et al. 2024). While first pipelines for reconstructing fungal draft models exist (Castillo et al. 2016), data-driven manual curation is still required to generate high quality models according to community set standards (Lieven et al. 2020), which allow accurate predictions of metabolic activity profiles.

Here, we present the first (manually curated) genome-scale metabolic model of Lichtheimia corymbifera. The model was calibrated using experimental carbon and nitrogen assimilation data and used to predict metabolic preferences for sugar and amino acid substrates. We further integrated 72-h co-culture data with human PBMCs, revealing differential fungal sphingolipid metabolism and a concomitant shift in PBMC actin cytoskeleton and tight junction activity. These findings suggest that L. corymbifera may scavenge host sphingolipids for membrane incorporation, thereby enhancing survival under immune pressure. This metabolic adaptation could inform novel organ-specific therapeutic strategies for mucormycosis and may help alleviate some of its dire consequence for the affected patients.

Methods

Metabolic model reconstruction

Draft model generation

To generate a GEM for L. corymbifera, we used functions of the RAVEN metabolic modelling toolbox (v2.8.7) (Wang et al. 2018) based on the Matlab programming environment (release version 2023a) (The MathWorks Inc., 2023) for both, automatic and manual curation efforts. Of note, our automatic draft model creation and its gap-filling consumed ∼25% and 10% of the overall reconstruction time, respectively. The majority of our reconstruction efforts, however, were devoted to multiple rounds of iterative manual curation (about 65%, Fig. 1A).

We first collected three available fungal GEMs as template models: yeast9 of Saccharomyces cerevisae (https://sysbiochalmers.github.io/yeast-GEM/), the panGEM of A. fumigatus (Mirhakkak et al. 2023) and the GEM for Penicillium chrysogenum (Agren et al. 2013). These template models comprised 1162, 1444, and 1006 genes, 4130, 3621, and 1632 reactions as well as 2805, 4046, and 1395 metabolites, respectively. Using BLASTp with the GEM-associated NCBI refseq genome assemblies GCF_000146045.2, GCF_000002655.1, and GCF_000226395.1 against the L. corymbifera genome GCA_000723665.1, we determined orthologues information for 1065 Lichtheimia transcripts. This information was the basis for the automatic draft model reconstruction—the reactions were taken from the template models only if the associated genes’ orthologues were present in the L. corymbifera’s genome. In order to begin the automatic draft generation procedure, we resolved duplicate entries for metabolites originating from the three template models by exhaustively unifying their namespace. For that, we then performed search in KEGG (Kanehisa et al. 2025) and ChEBI (Hastings et al. 2016) databases using the metabolites’ MIRIAM annotations present in the models and BioServices Python package (Cokelaer et al. 2013). Our goal was to cross-annotate metabolites in the template models and find the synonyms. This step was then enhanced by manual search; after it, the identical names and identifiers were assigned to metabolites in different models that shared same MIRIAM entries and were located in the same compartments. In the next step, all template models and ortholog information were combined using the RAVEN function getModelFromHomology to generate a first draft model. This model version 1 comprised 1225 genes, 3211 reactions (7 exchange reactions), 3487 metabolites (1694 unique) connected across 14 compartments.

Next, we performed gap-filling procedures and improved the overall consistency of the model using RAVEN’s built-in function fillGaps. Metabolic reactions of the template models were utilized as a catalogue for filling pathway gaps and increasing network integrity (by resolving orphan or otherwise blocked reactions). The resulting model after gap-filling included 3652 metabolites (1857 unique), 4132 reactions and 1393 genes.

Finally, the biomass reaction was imported from the community-curated S. cerevisiae template model. In brief, our biomass reaction was reconstructed as follows: model metabolites representing existing molecules, for example, amino acids, were summarized to pseudometabolites (termed, e.g. “protein” for all amino acids). Based on the L. corymbifera genome, the distribution of the amino acids in its proteome was calculated and used to calibrate the weights in the production of the “protein” pseudometabolite. This way, all pseudometabolites (“protein”, “carbohydrate”, “DNA”, etc.) were combined and weighted to represent the amounts needed to produce 1 gram of biomass per hour. The production of the resulting pseudometabolite “biomass” can finally be used as optimization objective using FBA or FVA simulations.

Model curation

To further improve the model quality, firstly erroneous generating cycles (artefacts which create metabolites as side-products of otherwise zero-net gain reaction cycles) were detected and resolved using closed exchange reaction rates (and thus prohibiting medium influx) and analysing the nullspace of the model’s stoichiometry matrix N. The introduction of outflow reactions allows this method to detect metabolite generating cycles, which will otherwise violate the steady state assumption: any non-trivial solution of Nv = 0 (v being a flux distribution given the stoichiometry in N) that includes an outflow reaction for some metabolite relates to a respective metabolite generating cycle. Individual reaction fluxes were evaluated using COBRA toolbox (Heirendt et al. 2017) and its function optimizeCbModel based on the Flux Balance Analysis (FBA) approach (Orth et al. 2010).

To resolve redundant reactions, we next performed a search based on the large language model (LLM) ChatGPT (model ChatGPT-4o, OpenAI) (https://chat.openai.com) using the prompt provided in Supplementary Document S1. We identified 170 metabolic cycles, each comprising two identical reactions imported from different models, which were resolved subsequently by unifying these. After an additional subsequent manual search for similar cycles, the draft model included 3624 metabolites (1845 unique), 3847 reactions, and 1866 genes. Subsequently, a similar LLM-driven search was performed to identify duplicated metabolites using the ChatGPT model GPT-o1 and its function Deep Research. The search was directed to the PubChem (Kim et al. 2025) database (the prompt is provided in Supplementary Document S2). This approach yielded 49 pairs of metabolite synonyms, which were resolved subsequently. This semi-manually curated draft model (version 4) included 3575 metabolites (1796 unique), 3848 reactions, and 1866 genes.

To calibrate the model growth accuracy, we simulated growth behaviour for 66 carbon and 5 nitrogen sources and compared it to available assimilation data (Supporting Tables S2 and S3 in Schwartze et al. 2012). Upon discrepancies with the experimental characteristics, we investigated the respective metabolic flux profiles using a combination of FBA and flux variability analysis (FVA, performed as fluxVariability function of COBRA toolbox) and corrected, e.g. feasible metabolic reaction directions (Mahadevan and Schilling 2003, Orth et al. 2010). In detail, we added transport reactions for maltose, ornithine, inosine, pyruvate, and mannite to achieve growth on these substrates, which was prohibited in the draft model but demonstrated in the experiments. Likewise, we changed transport reactions for myo-inositol, L-sorbose, and glycerol to negate growth in accordance with the experimental data. The reaction of sucrose degradation was removed to represent the L. corymbifera’s inability to grow on sucrose. Further manual curation efforts included the introduction of missing pathway reactions to allow the production of arginine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, leucine, and thiamine, introducing transport reactions for water (that was, for example, necessary to allow arginine production), correction of metabolite identifiers, and chemical formulas. Upon accomplishing these steps, we completed the reconstruction of the final manually curated model comprising 1845 genes, 3853 reactions (20% change to initial draft model), and 3612 metabolites (4% change compared to initial draft model, Supplementary Data S3).

Data incorporation, model simulations, and analysis

We used our GEM to simulate and compare metabolic activity using different carbon or nitrogen sources, specifically, carbohydrates or amino acids. These data were adapted from available experimental results, where growth efficiencies on these carbon and nitrogen sources were evaluated (Schwartze et al. 2012). To compare metabolic reaction and subsystem activity we used the same adapted minimal medium as above for calibration to metabolite assimilation data. We performed FBA analyses on this minimal medium with molecules shown to be viable carbon sources (see above) and restricted the achievable growth rate to the minimum value obtained for glycine across all tested sources to keep analyses of fluxes comparable. Then we tracked lower (LB) und upper bounds (UB) per model reaction using FVA. After grouping LB and UB values for carbohydrates and amino acids per reaction, we statistically tested significance of feasible flux changes between these groups using two-sample t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P ≤ 0.05 as cut-off for significance (Supplementary Table S4) using R’s p.adjust function (v4.5.1). The fractions of significantly changed reactions per pathway were tracked and further analyzed.

Data-driven model simulation and analysis were also sought for human PBMCs [dataset (i)] and L. corymbifera data [dataset (ii)] in isolation or in presence of each other [dataset (iii)] generated in triplicates for two time-points, 24 h and 72 h, respectively (see below for further experimental details). We generated time dependent ratios per dataset with the fold change according to 72 h/24 h to determine the abundance change at later time-points. For the individual PBMC data we generated also fold-changes per time-point between the datasets (iii) and (i) [according to (iii)24 h/(i)24 h and (iii)72 h/(i)72 h]. For all human data we determined KEGG pathway maps for which we could map our proteome data using the KEGGREST package (v1.48.1) in R. We mapped the log2 for all these fold changes over respective pathway maps using the R package pathview (v1.48.0). For each protein with mappable abundance information available in any analyzed pathway map, we show four information bands per respective enzyme box (from left to right): | log2((iii)24 h/(i)24 h) | log2((iii)72 h/(i)72 h) | log2((i)72 h/(i)24 h) | log2((iii)72 h/(iii)72 h) |. For datasets (ii) and (iii) we determined the fungal proteins that are present in our GEM. We used a minimal medium definition with glucose as carbon source and restricted the achievable biomass flux to 10% of its theoretical maximum using a parsimonious FBA (pFBA) approach to minimize the sum of overall flux as additional objective next to achieving the optimal biomass yield using the toolbox COBRApy (Ebrahim et al. 2013). Restricting the biomass to 10% allowed subsequent simulations to also achieve higher biomass values upon modifying model reaction ranges, which is by definition not feasible when optimizing for the global maximum biomass yield with a constant medium. Using the flux distribution of the 24 h time-point (given by pFBA) as reference, we modified the feasible flux ranges of data-associated reactions by the mean ratios given by 72 h/24 h, which we determined separately for datasets (ii) and (iii). That is, if the ratio > 1 the flux range for any affected reaction was allowed to be larger than in the 24 h pFBA solution, while allowed reaction ranges with associated ratios < 1 were reduced by the respective ratio. After all data affected reaction ranges were modified, we repeated our analysis without imposing any flux range restrictions to the biomass reaction to allow for comparison with the 24 h time point. Subsequently, we identified reactions with at least 10% flux difference and summarized these as fractions of changed reactions per pathway. Finally, we tested the significance of the number of differentially active reactions per pathway at 72 h compared to 24 h using a 2 × 2 contingency table and Fisher’s exact test statistics (significance according to BH adjusted P ≤ 0.05).

Microbial strain and culture conditions

Lichtheimia corymbifera (JMRC: FSU:009 682) sporangiospores (spores) were obtained from the Jena Microbial Resource Collection. To induce robust sporulation, strains were cultured on malt extract agar (MEA; Merck) at 37°C for 72 h, a condition previously validated for optimal spores yield in Mucorales. Spore suspensions were harvested in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), filtered through sterile gauze to remove hyphal fragments, and quantified both by haemocytometry and colony-forming units (CFU) on MEA to ensure viability and correct for spore clumping. Calibration curves linking haemocytometer counts and CFUs were generated for each batch. Aliquots were cryopreserved in 15% glycerol at −80°C to maintain viability and thawed immediately prior to infection experiments.

PBMC infection model

PBMCs were isolated from healthy adult donors by Ficoll–Paque density gradient centrifugation, under written informed consent and protocols approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval no. 2021–2179-Material and 2025-3688-BO-A). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study, and their anonymity and confidentiality were maintained throughout the research process. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Donors with recent infections, chronic disease, or immunomodulatory therapy were excluded. PBMCs were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated, mycoplasma-tested fetal bovine serum (batch-matched; Gibco), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO₂.

Infections were performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 2 spores per PBMC (2:1), determined by CFU normalization against a standard curve rather than optical density alone. This MOI was selected based on preliminary titration experiments, balancing cell viability with detectable immune activation. Conditions thus included (i) unstimulated PBMCs, (ii) spores alone, and (iii) PBMCs of 5 biological human replicates exposed to live, recently harvested spores.

Proteomic analysis by LC–MS/MS

At 24- and 72-h post-infection, culture supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min to remove cells and debris, followed by concentration using 20 kDa molecular-weight cut-off filters (Amicon Ultra). Proteins were precipitated with methanol/chloroform/water. Precipitates were resuspended in 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate, reduced and alkylated with 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine and 12.5 mM chloroacetamide final concentration at 70°C for 30 min and digested overnight with mass spec-grade trypsin/LysC mix (Promega, 1:25 enzyme: substrate).

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Ultimate 3000 nLC system connected to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with FAIMS. Peptides were trapped (2 cm × 75 µm, 3 µm) and separated (50 cm × 75 µm, 2 µm) on Acclaim Pep Map columns at 5 µl/min and 300 nl/min flow rates. Gradient elution of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and 90% aqueous acetonitrile (B) was as follows: 0 min at 4% B, 6 min at 8% B, 30 min at 12% B, 75 min at 30% B, 85 min at 50% B, 90–95 min at 96% B, 95.1–120 min at 4% B. Positively charged ions were generated at 2.2 kV using a stainless-steel emitter attached to the Nanospray Flex Ion Source. Precursor ions were monitored at m/z 300–1200 at a resolution of 120 000 FWHM (full width at half maximum) using a maximum injection time (ITmax) of 50 ms and 300% normalized AGC (automatic gain control) target. Precursor ions with a charge state of z = 2–5 were filtered at an isolation width of m/z 4.0 amu for further fragmentation at 28% HCD collision energy. MS2 ions were scanned data-dependent at 15 000 FWHM (ITmax=40 ms, AGC= 200%). Each sample was measured in triplicate with two compensation voltages (−50 V, −70 V).

Tandem mass spectra were searched against the UniProt databases (2025/09/11) of Homo sapiens and the in-house database of L. corymbifera (JMRC: FSU:009 682) using Proteome Discoverer (PD) 3.0 (Thermo) and the database search algorithms (threshold search engine scores in parenthesis) Mascot 2.8 (>30), Comet (>3), MS Amanda 3.0 (>300), Chimerys (>2), Sequest HT (>3) with and without INFERYS Rescoring and MSPepSearch (>600, spectral library from NIST, human_hcd) with Precursor Detector node. Two missed cleavages were allowed for the tryptic digestion. The precursor mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and the fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da. Modifications were defined as dynamic Met oxidation, phosphorylation of Ser, Thr, and Tyr, protein N-term acetylation with and without Met-loss as well as static Cys carbamidomethylation. A strict false discovery rate (FDR) <1% (peptide and protein level) was required for positive protein hits. The Percolator node of PD3.0 and a reverse decoy database was used for q-value validation of spectral matches. Label-free protein quantification was based on the Minora algorithm of PD3.0 using the precursor abundance based on intensity and a signal-to-noise ratio >5.

Results

Reconstruction of the genome-scale metabolic model of lichtheimia corymbifera

The GEM for L. corymbifera was reconstructed in two major steps: draft model creation and manual curation (Fig. 1A). First, three existing fungal organisms, S. cerevisiae, A. fumigatus, and P. chrysogenum, for which GEMs exist, were chosen as template species. Based on the genomes of L. corymbifera and the three template fungi, we used BLASTp to identify the orthologues proteome. Together with the gene-to-reaction rules contained in the template GEMs, 5582 genes from the template models were associated to genes or group of genes with metabolic relevance in L. corymbifera. After unifying all metabolite identifiers across all template GEMs, gene associations were processed with the RAVEN toolbox to generate a first draft metabolic model, where information from the metabolic models of yeast (version9, https://github.com/SysBioChalmers/yeast-GEM/releases/tag/v9.0.2) (Zhang et al. 2024), A. fumigatus (Mirhakkak et al. 2023) and P. chrysogenum (Agren et al. 2013) was combined. This first draft model comprised 1225 genes, 1694 unique metabolites, and 3211 metabolic reactions including associated gene-to-reaction rules. Reactions that were not mapped to the L. corymbifera proteome, but present in the template GEMs were held back for subsequent gap-filling procedures, which improved the consistency of our draft GEM (Methods).

Finally, we performed extensive manual curation to resolve artefacts introduced during the automated reconstruction and generation of the first draft model. For example, after merging the template models and unifying the metabolite namespace, 30 imported reactions were missing essential substrates, products, or co-factors, and corrected accordingly. Using nullspace analysis (Methods), 39 cycles of connected reactions, which could carry metabolic flux despite prohibiting metabolite exchanges with the extracellular space, were identified. Three cycles included energy-carrying ATP, but none involved ATP dissipation or other metabolite net production, therefore, were not considered as (erroneous) energy generating cycles. A common reason for these reaction cycle artefacts was insufficient metabolite unification, that led to import of otherwise identical metabolic reactions from different template models. One example was 2'-deoxy-alpha-D-ribose 1-phosphate and 2-deoxy-D-ribose 1-phosphate; the corresponding reactions formed a cycling reaction set, which was resolved by unifying the metabolites and removing now-duplicated reactions. Further examples include alpha-D-glucose and beta-D-glucose and their interconversion reactions that led to the theoretical formation of a cycle with trehalose formation reactions. This cycle was preserved in order to provide the better accessibility for the model usage and different glucose variants (alpha-D-glucose, beta-D-glucose, or D-glucose) in given data. We found mass conservation violation for five of the identified flux cycling reaction sets. They could erroneously produce cellobiose (in extracellular compartment), ammonium (in cytosol and extracellular compartment), or water (in cytosol and mitochondria). In the first case, reactions involving 1,3 beta-D-glucan were considering different amounts of monomers in different template models. We resolved this cycle by considering consistently one molecule of glucose for processing 1,3 beta-D-glucan in our GEM. In the case of ammonium, we resolved the cycle by balancing nitrogen stoichiometrically correct in the reaction of pyridoxal 5'-phosphate formation. Finally, the reaction of octanoyl-CoA synthesis was stoichiometrically incorrect and led to net water production. After we corrected these reaction direction and stoichiometric inconsistencies, no model metabolite was left that could be produced without metabolite inflow via exchange reactions.

Further manual curation efforts included calibrating our model to existing carbon and nitrogen assimilation data (Schwartze et al. 2012). Among others, our draft GEM was unable to synthesize tyrosine, phenylalanine, arginine, and thiamine, which was in contradiction to experimental data. We identified a number of missing metabolic reactions in the metabolic pathways of these amino acids in our draft GEM and manually imported them from the list of available remaining reactions after gap-filling from the template models. For thiamine, we imported three reactions: production of 4-methyl-5-(2-phosphonooxyethyl) thiazole from ribose-5-phosphate and D-xylulose 5-phosphate, as well as production of 4-amino-5-hydroxymethyl-2-methylpyrimidine. To resolve arginine biosynthesis, we added the reaction of citrulline production, and balanced water co-factors between cytoplasm and other compartments via additional transport reactions. Resolving these incompatibilities improved the general consistency to the assimilation data from 41% to 80% (from 27 to 53 out of 63) for carbon and from 0% to 80% for nitrogen (from 0 to 4 out of 5) assimilation data (Fig. 1B).

Concerning secondary metabolism, we cross-checked the presence of mucoricin in L. corymbifera, a ricin-like toxin with a central role in the Mucorales virulence of Rhizopus delemar (Soliman et al. 2021). A total of 12 hypothetical proteins in the L. corymbifera genome displayed only 30.72%–47.55% sequence identity hinting at differences in secondary metabolism across Mucorales (Supplementary Table 1 of Soliman et al. 2021). Given the low sequence identity, the hypothetical status and thus incomplete gene annotation, these proteins were filtered and consequently removed to keep the metabolic model quality consistent. Final model improvements included manual corrections of different metabolites with identical identifiers (for example, myo-inositol 1-phosphate and myo-inositol 3-phosphate had the same InChI identifier), correcting identifier names to conform with SBML naming conventions (e.g. removing whitespace in identifier names, or resolving metabolite formulas including dots as separators), and manually resolving any redundant metabolite information not detected in the curation processes until this point. Of note, most homology-based model information originated from the S. cerevisae (711 genes, 1667 reactions, excluding transport and exchange reactions) and A. fumigatus template model (296 genes and 1505 reactions), while less, but non-zero information was included from the P. chrysogenum model (58 genes, 501 reactions), highlighting the need of incorporating data from multiple template model sources.

During the reconstruction process, five consecutive versions of the model were tested with MEMOTE to cross-check the model quality adhering to community standards (Lieven et al. 2020). These versions comprised 1) unification of metabolite IDs in template models and draft model reconstruction by homology; 2) gap-filling procedure completed; 3) post-gap-filling unification of metabolic reaction namespace; 4) additional unification of metabolite IDs, and 5) further manual curation efforts. We observed a steady increase of MEMOTE score (mostly by gap-filling and manual curation procedures) and achieved a final score of 73% (Fig. 1C), which is comparable to the MEMOTE scores of the template GEMs. The resulting model yielded 1845 genes, 1815 unique metabolites, and 3853 metabolic reactions. Next to the introduction of exchange reactions in the final model, we observed the most substantial increase for the transport reactions (23% increase). The total number of supported genes (51% increase) and metabolic reactions (20% increase) surpassed the increase in added unique metabolites (7% increase) (Fig. 1D). Taken together, these efforts exemplify the need to improve model consistency by the addition of transport reactions, supported genes, and gene-to-reaction relationships, all of which raised the accuracy of model-based predictive power.

The biomass function was adapted from the S. cerevisiae template model to the proteomic data of L. corymbifera (Methods). The resulting molecule distribution in the biomass composition shows that lipids are the most abundant component of biomass (Fig. 1E). In accordance with these data, most of the model reactions belong to lipid metabolism (27%), followed by carbohydrate (15%) and amino acid metabolic reactions (10%), next to necessary transport reactions (14%) to make the model consistent and connected (Fig. 1F).

Carbohydrates as carbon sources are differentially processed towards biomass than amino acids

To evaluate the metabolic diversity in processing differing medium components, we calculated reaction and pathway activity profiles of L. corymbifera using either carbohydrates including sugars like glucose and galactose, or amino acids as carbon sources. We simulated growth on 12 carbohydrates and 14 amino acids in total and predicted the individual flux range profiles that support growth (Supplementary Table S4). To investigate metabolic changes for each reaction, we used flux variability analysis (FVA) to determine their feasible lower and upper flux bounds towards biomass production given different carbon sources and compared differential activity per metabolic subsystem. To keep simulations comparable, we constrained the biomass yield to the least possible over all carbon source, thus balancing the different amounts of carbons each metabolite possesses. The fraction of reactions that change their flux significantly upon switching from carbohydrates to amino acids as a carbon source was measured for each pathway. We detected most significantly changed fluxes compared between carbohydrates and amino acids as carbon sources for lipid metabolic processes (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S5). Interestingly, reactions involved in uneven beta-oxidation pathways (both in mitochondria and peroxisomes) have undergone significant changes in feasible flux ranges, as have those involved in sphingoglycolipid metabolism. The biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids was also drastically impacted, with 97% of reactions (30 out of 31 associated reactions) exhibiting significantly changed fluxes.

To determine the direction of activity changes, we calculated mean upper and lower bounds of feasible fluxes for every reaction per carbon class and compared them for carbohydrate vs. amino acid substrates, accordingly (Fig. 2B and C). Among all investigated flux ranges 43.5% of the upper and 8.5% of the lower associated flux range values were reduced when growing on amino acids in comparison to the growth on carbohydrates, while a smaller fraction of reactions showed increased flux ranges (4.5% upper and 5.5% lower flux range increase). In the three aforementioned pathways, mitochondrial uneven beta-oxidation, peroxisomal uneven beta-oxidation, and sphingoglycolipid metabolism, almost all associated reactions have unidirectional feasible flux ranges, and, except for one (formation of 3-oxoheptadecanoyl-CoA in peroxisomes), are predicted to have less feasible maximum flux when growing on amino acids (Supplementary Table S6). Interestingly, the production of 3-oxopentadecanoyl-CoA in peroxisomes can also switch and has non-zero LB maximum flux when amino acids as carbon sources are simulated. Taken together, these data imply the preferential usage of carbohydrates as carbon sources and especially, alternative routes in converting lipids, to, e.g. vital membrane components towards improved cell wall integrity. These data not only suggest diversity in lipid degradation, but imply that L. corymbifera’s growth and potentially virulence might also be strongly dependent on beta oxidation, which is in line with other fungal pathogens (Kretschmer et al. 2012).

Lichtheimia corymbifera potentially assimilates host lipids for cell membrane integrity

To further investigate L. corymbifera metabolism in the context of infection, we generated proteome data with a series of batch culture experiments of (i) human PBMCs, (ii) L. corymbifera spores, and (iii) co-culture of both, PBMCs and fungal spores, for two different time-points (24 h and 72 h, Methods).

To simulate the fungal metabolic response to immune cells for metabolic reactions categorized into metabolic subsystem, we parameterized our GEM with the abundance data from experiments ii) and iii) for both 24 h and 72 h and compared flux rates supporting fungal biomass (Fig. 3A–D, Supplementary Data S7, Methods). After evaluating metabolic reactions possessing at least 10% differing flux rates, we first assessed most differing activated metabolic subsystems by calculating subsystem-specific fractions of changed fluxes. Although only a small fraction of the available proteome data could be mapped to metabolic reactions in our model [n = 20 for (ii) and n = 31 for (iii)], we observed a number of significantly changed metabolic activities, exemplifying the advantage of combining metabolic topology information and interdependency of simulated flux through connected metabolic reactions intrinsic to genome-scale metabolic models. While 693 metabolic reactions showed flux activity changes over time in simulations associated to both datasets (ii) and (iii), 802 and 819 only showed changed reaction activity in dataset (ii) and (iii), respectively (Fig. 3E). The subsystems porphyrin and heme as well as riboflavin metabolism possessed the highest fraction of significantly altered fluxes over time, exemplifying the critical need of iron for, e.g. redox metabolism, stress response, and for fungal survival. Interestingly, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (62% of significantly altered reactions in the experiments upon presence of the immune cells), which is vital for lipid regulation in fungi and effective mitochondrial function (Choi et al. 2024), was not significantly altered in the absence of immune cells, highlighting its pivotal role for survival upon stress. In contrast, sphingolipid metabolism was altered over time only in the absence of immune cells (Fig. 3B), suggesting the immune cells are targeting particularly fungal cell membrane rejuvenation and repair. Additional statistical analysis showed the significant differentiation of metabolic pathway activity upon presence of PBMCs based on Fisher’s exact test statistics (adjusted p≤0.05, Methods, Fig. 3C and D). Next to major genome-wide metabolic re-routing manifested by significantly altered transport reactions between different cell compartments, inositol metabolism altered significantly both in absence and presence of PBMCs, while sphingolipid metabolism and phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan metabolism altered only significantly in absence (Fig. 3F) and glycerolipid metabolism and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis in presence (Fig. 3G) of PBMCs, respectively.

Since our model simulations pointed to pronounced fungal metabolic activity changes upon PBMC presence, we next were interested in the PBMC response in face of a fungal threat. We found substantial abundance differences in human PBMCs associated proteins detected in datasets (i) and (iii) and mapped them to KEGG pathway maps (Supplementary Table S8). Most prominently, pathways associated to cytoskeleton in muscle cells (KEGG pathway ID hsa04820), regulation of actin cytoskeleton (hsa04810), focal adhesion (hsa04510), and tight junction (hsa04530) showed distinct differences upon presence of L. corymbifera including a notable time-point influence (Supplementary Fig. S9). Integrin subunit alpha 1 (ITGA), important for translating integrin-mediated focal adhesion signal towards actin polymerization, was higher in presence of L. corymbifera after 24 h (log2((iii)24 h/(i)24 h) = 2.15), but virtually the same after 72 h (log2((iii)72 h/(i)72 h) = 0.09), as its abundance could increase in the absence of the fungus over time (log2((i)72 h/(i)24 h) = 1.44) in contrast to a reduction over time in presence of the fungus (log2((iii)72 h/(iii)24 h) = −0.62). Profilin1 (PFN1) was also higher at 24 h (log2(((iii)24 h/(i)24 h) = 1.82), but substantially lower at 72 h (log2((iii)72 h/(i)72 h) = −1.86) in the presence of L. corymbifera, exemplifying its notable suppression in the sustained presence of the fungus. On the other hand, levels of vinculin (VCL), a membrane-cytoskeletal protein, increased in the presence of the fungus for both time-points (log2((iii)24 h/(i)24 h) = 3.19 and log2((iii)72 h/(i)72 h) = 2.10, while Rho, involved in sphingolipid signalling, showed an increase at 72 h in the fungal presence (log2((iii)72 h/(i)72 h) = 2.14, log2((iii)72 h/(iii)24 h) = 0.91). Interestingly, the Na+/H+ exchanger regulatory factor 1 (NHERF1) (Kammala et al. 2021), which also functions as a scaffolding protein, is already reduced at 24 h in the presence of the fungus (log2((iii)24 h/(i)24 h) = −1.42) and reduces further at 72 h (log2((iii)72 h/(i)72 h) = −2.82). We also noted substantial changes in central glycolysis metabolic reactions, particularly with increased abundance for sustained breakdown of D-fructose-1,6P via fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (EC 4.1.2.13, symbol ALDOA/ALDOC), opposed by a decrease in glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.1.12, GAPDH), bisphosphoglycerate/phosphoglycerate mutase (EC 5.4.2.11, BPGM), and enolase (EC 4.2.1.11, ENO1) at 72 h in presence of the fungus, showcasing the immune cells adaptation to generate important intermediate metabolites and energetic needs to combat the fungal infection (Supplementary Data S8, Supplementary Fig. S9).

In summary, our data exemplify a major shift in adhesion and cytoskeleton regulation and metabolic activity in the immune cell combined with a prominent shift in central carbon metabolic routes and lipid associated pathways. In combination with our model simulations, our analyses point to significant concomitant changes in both L. corymbifera and immune cell metabolism, particularly in lipid associated pathways, highlight the benefit (and need) for studying host-pathogen interactions, and may be further explored and experimentally tested towards improved defence against this fungal pathogen.

Discussion

We present the first curated genome-scale metabolic model (GEM) of L. corymbifera, one of the species of Mucorales WHO-designated priority fungal pathogens (WHO 2023), reconstructed through homology-based draft generation, extensive manual curation, and calibration with carbon and nitrogen assimilation assays. The final model achieved a MEMOTE score of 73%, comparable to curated fungal models such as for A. fumigatus and S. cerevisae (Mirhakkak et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2024). By integrating host–pathogen proteomic data from co-cultures with PBMCs and L. corymbifera, we demonstrate that the model can predict context-specific metabolic adaptations, including the reprogramming of lipid and sphingolipid pathways during immune challenge. Genomic analyses revealed an expansion of protease repertoires and iron acquisition systems (Schwartze et al. 2014), but metabolic organization and host adaptation mechanisms have remained opaque. Our GEM provides the first systems-level map of L. corymbifera metabolism and extends beyond gene annotation by linking assimilation capacity and host-derived proteomic signals to flux-level predictions.

Compared to other fungal GEMs, our L. corymbifera model reconstruction is notable for its breadth of substrate utilization and lipid metabolic capacity. For example, while C. albicans models emphasized metabolic plasticity in switching between glucose and alternative carbon sources (Mirhakkak et al. 2021), and A. fumigatus GEMs highlighted respiratory flexibility and shaping of the bacterial microenvironment in the lung niche towards growth beneficial conditions (Mirhakkak et al. 2023), our model predicts a pronounced rewiring of fatty acid and sphingolipid metabolism under immune confrontation. The predominance of sphingolipids and other glycerophospholipids as key lipid classes was recently confirmed by shotgun lipidomics in R. delemar, a major causative agent of mucormycosis (Ali et al. 2024). Specifically, proteome-informed simulations indicated altered flux through sphingolipid metabolism in our model and concomitant perturbation in host sphingolipid signalling as indicated, e.g. by increased Rho abundances over time in PBMCs challenged with L. corymbifera spores in our data. These insights are consistent with the hypothesis that L. corymbifera may scavenge host-derived lipids to fortify its cell membrane. Such lipid-centred adaptations mirror observations in Cryptococcus neoformans, where sphingolipid metabolism underpins stress resistance and virulence (Singh et al. 2017) and RhoA activation leads to actin cytoskeleton, tight junction modulation, focal adhesion and stress fibre formation (Amano et al. 1997, Munzen et al. 2024). Whilst fungi produce sphingolipids which are important as mediators for the expression of virulence, factors, host sphingolipids can also mediate adherence of fungal cells to epithelial and phagocytic cells (Jimenez-Lucho et al. 1990). Mucorales produce neutral sphingoglycolipids not described in any other fungal group (Aoki et al. 2004) making them potential therapeutic targets for the discrimination between mucormycosis and other fungal infections. The pivotal role of lipids in mediating drug resistance, particularly innate resistance to azoles, raises a need for a specific therapy which distinguishes mucormycosis from other fungal infections. This consideration is particularly important in light of mucormycosis being well-known as breakthrough infection after successful treatment of aspergillosis (Lewis et al. 2012, Axell-House et al. 2021). Moreover, the impact of lipid scavenging is also substantiated by recent experimental insights on the outcome of mucormycosis, which is crucially influenced by the concentration of albumin-released free fatty acids in serum (Pikoulas et al. 2026). Fungal growth of the Mucorales species R. delemar is supported at low concentrations, whereas elevated levels of oxidized fatty acids are deleterious to the fungus, leading to clearance of the infection. Our analysis—incorporating simulations across media and the impact of PBMCs on fungal growth—predicted differential activity in lipid pathways of L. corymbifera, corroborating these findings of Pikoulas et al. (2026) and highlighting the potential usage of our model to generate testable hypotheses about clinically relevant mechanisms, such as those influencing fungal pathogenicity.

On the host side, the contribution of tight junctions (TJs), known to be important in assembly and maintenance of epithelial cell layers, were observed. In PBMCs grown to confluency, TJs may play a crucial role for barrier remodelling that selectively allows diffusion through the paracellular pathway between adjacent cells. In the present study, during infection with L. corymbifera we have identified differential activation of several human proteins associated to the KEGG pathways for focal adhesion and tight junction, respectively. Junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs) are involved in TJ assembly of polarized cells and on the cell surface of leukocytes and play an important role in the regulation of cell polarity, barrier regulation and permeability as well as leukocyte’s migration through endo-/epithelial cell layers (Garrido-Urbani et al. 2014, Zihni et al. 2016). During inflammation, leukocytes exit the bloodstream by squeezing between endothelial cells (paracellular diapedesis). To facilitate this passage without compromising the vessel’s integrity, the endothelium mobilizes membrane from its lateral border recycling compartment to the site of junction remodelling. This provides extra membrane to seal the gaps and engages the migrating leukocyte with adhesion molecules like PECAM-1 and CD99 (Winger et al. 2014). It remains to be investigated what role the two proteins play during fungal infection. Since JAMs are connected to the actin cytoskeleton (Yamada et al. 2004), a co-involvement of human actin cytoskeleton pathways (KEGG pathway IDs hsa04820 and hsa04810) becomes evident. These were recently described to be associated to universal cell mechanics gene signatures that can assist in engineering cell mechanical properties on demand to investigate their role in physiological and pathological processes (Urbanska et al. 2025).

Integration of cytoskeletal signatures with assimilation assays further revealed that growth on amino acids downregulated global metabolic fluxes relative to carbohydrates, particularly in β-oxidation and sphingolipid pathways. This may reflect niche-specific metabolic tuning: amino acid-based growth could mimic nutrient-restricted tissue environments, whereas carbohydrate-driven metabolism may dominate in extracellular niches. These findings extend prior reports that Mucorales exhibit strong dependence on iron acquisition and stress signalling (Ibrahim et al. 2010, Lee et al. 2013) by highlighting nutrient-specific shifts in central metabolism that may modulate pathogenic potential.

One valuable application of our metabolic model is its application in a clinical context. For instance, azole drug resistance was associated with a metabolic shift in C. albicans using a comparative metabolomics study (Li et al. 2018), while both transcriptomics and metabolomics showed enriched differential metabolic processes such as tyrosine, purine, or D-amino acid metabolism in an azole-resistant strain of A. fumigatus (Ma et al. 2025). Given the available evidence and ease of generating large-scaled OMICs data for the metabolic component in fungal drug resistance, our model can be used for data-driven growth and knock out simulations. While transcriptomics allows to constrain the feasible model reaction fluxes, metabolomics can be used to constrain the input and output of the model. The integration of such data in a clinically relevant setup to study, e.g. antibiotic resistance, allows to propose most efficient metabolic targets for intervention strategies. These can subsequently be experimentally confirmed and thus limit the otherwise unmanageable number of testable intervention candidates.

We recognize several limitations. While the MEMOTE score indicates model quality comparable to other curated fungal GEMs (Lieven et al. 2020), annotation gaps remain, particularly for secondary metabolism and compartmentalized lipid pathways. Pikoulas et al. (2026) highlighted the pathogenicity decreasing impact of albumin on mucoricin in R. delemar. While we also observed differential lipid profiles during infection in our data, we did not, however, detect a major role of secondary metabolism and specifically of mucoricin in response to PBMC confrontation. This may suggest a minor influence of these immune cells on secondary metabolic activity by L. corymbifera possibly due to the early response phase within 72 h of PBMC stimulation in our experimental condition. It remains to be explored in future studies, how exactly toxins like mucoricin and others affect secondary metabolism by L. corymbifera to drive its pathogenicity over long term exposure to immune cells. Given that Mucorales genomes encode diverse non-ribosomal peptide synthetases and polyketide synthases (Schwartze et al. 2014), further integration of transcriptomics and metabolomics is required to capture these modules. Moreover, co-cultures with PBMCs provide only a partial view of the host immune environment, lacking contributions from neutrophils, macrophages, and epithelial barriers, which are central to mucormycosis pathogenesis (Hassan and Voigt 2019). Finally, although proteome-informed flux predictions suggest lipid scavenging, validation will require isotope labelling and targeted metabolomics to distinguish fungal uptake from de novo synthesis.

In conclusion, this study establishes a high-quality GEM of L. corymbifera, situating an underexplored mucoralean pathogen within the fungal GEM landscape. Beyond benchmarking curation for early-diverging fungi, the model generates biologically testable hypotheses, particularly regarding lipid and sphingolipid metabolism as potential contributors to immune evasion and persistence. By combining assimilation data, PBMC co-culture proteomics and systems-level modelling, our work advances understanding of L. corymbifera pathogenicity and provides a foundation for rational exploration of metabolic vulnerabilities as antifungal targets.
