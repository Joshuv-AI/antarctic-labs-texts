Corrosion and Electrolysis Mechanisms of Elemental Cobalt in NaCl Solution
Authors: Shengjiao Yang, Baochang Liu, Weiyi Yang, Hongxia Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To recycle/remove/protect the cobalt from polycrystalline diamond (PCD), hard alloys, and Co catalyst, this study investigates cobalt’s structural features, the inherent properties of the NaCl solution, and the interfacial chemistry at the solid–solution interface. For cobalt, the corrosion dependence on its structure is examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), and density functional theory (DFT). In NaCl solution, propertiesincluding dissolved oxygen (DO), electrical conductivity (EC), and viscosityaffect corrosion and electrolysis. Regarding interface science, the corrosion products (Co1.176(OH)2Cl0.348(H2O)0.456, β-Co­(OH)2, and Co2(OH)3Cl) are identified through XRD and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Chloride ions trigger the conversion of the β-Co­(OH)2 pathway to the Co1.176(OH)2Cl0.348(H2O)0.456 and Co2(OH)3Cl pathways, a novel phenomenon in solid-solution corrosion. The residual rate (RR) of β-Co­(OH)2, designated as RR­(β-Co­(OH)2), is originally defined to describe the transformation, which is detectable via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and modeled using a newly extended electrochemical element Wpc (based on the Finite-Length Warburg and Generalized Finite Warburg components) along with a novelly extended constant phase element (CPE) derived from the traditional CPE model. Furthermore, a new equivalent circuit model for EIS is developed to represent this conversion process in electrochemistry initially at an unstable solid-solution interface, and the concentration dependence of the effect caused by chloride ions is freshly revealed. This work also reveals other significant trends and mechanisms that depend on the NaCl solution concentration.

Introduction

The corrosion and electrolysis mechanisms of elemental cobalt are relevant to many industrial applications such as removing cobalt from polycrystalline diamond (PCD) , and polycrystalline diamond compact (PDC) − to enhance their performance, recycling cobalt resources from cemented carbide substrates − or hard alloys, preventing the corrosion of numerous Co-based materials (like Co-based alloys, , superalloys, , and Co-based magnets), and even maintaining the stability of catalysis involving Co. Therefore, a deeper understanding of cobalt corrosion and electrolysis is urgently needed.

Numerous studies on metal corrosion have employed NaCl solution as the electrolyte at a concentration of 3.5 wt % (approximately 0.6 M), − at other small concentration intervals, − or only three distinct concentration groups. However, few investigations have examined cobalt corrosion across a wide concentration range (from nearly zero to the saturation point of 5.4 M).

A key question is as follows: How does the NaCl concentration influence the corrosion of cobalt? Kaihui Dong and colleagues found that chloride ions cause nonelectrostatic migration and adsorption, thereby speeding up the pitting corrosion of aluminum alloy. Other researchers , have concluded that chloride ions attack the passive film, which is easily dissolved in a high-concentration NaCl solution. Gao et al. reported that chloride ions accelerate the hydrolysis of some components in microarc oxidation coatings on an Mg-2Sr substrate. Du and his team revealed that the interaction between dissolved oxygen (DO) and chloride ions in water accelerates stress corrosion cracking. Hisanori Tanimoto and colleagues reported that DO induces corrosion, with Cu2(OH)3Cl as the relevant corrosion product, and there is a negative correlation between the open circuit potential (OCP) of a-Zr55Cu45 and NaCl solution concentration (0.2 to 200 mM). However, few studies have examined the effect of chloride ions on corrosion products.

After investigating the structure-dependent corrosion of cobalt, this study sets multiple NaCl solution concentration groups ranging from nearly 0 to 5 M. It examines how concentration affects the DO, electrical conductivity (EC), and viscosity of NaCl solutions. The corrosion and electrolysis products are identified and used to elucidate the reaction mechanism. Additionally, the study focuses on the effect of chloride ions on these products and reveals the interface reaction mechanism between cobalt and NaCl solution by using electrochemical methods. This research holds theoretical importance and benefits the relevant industries mentioned above.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculation

For (0 0 2) and (1 0 1), the two main textures of cobalt crystal, we calculate the electrostatic potential shown in Figure d and e. The work function for the (0 0 2) face is 4.856 eV (Figure d), and the work function for the (1 0 1) face is 4.540 eV (Figure e).

Theoretically, a larger work function indicates greater difficulty for the cobalt crystal to lose electrons. In other words, a significant work function indicates chemical stability. Therefore, the (0 0 2) face is more stable than the (1 0 1) face. This point supports other scholars’ studies, which show that the arrangement of cell faces affects the corrosion rate and resistance, , and it also indicates that corrosion is structure-dependent.

Work Function from UPS

According to a study on Pt3Ni, UPS spectra differ across cell faces. The cobalt crystal has six cell faces, as shown in Figure a and b. Hence, it is meaningful to investigate the UPS spectrum of the cobalt sample. Moreover, the work function can be calculated from the UPS spectrum.

In this investigation, we examine different UPS spectra of cobalt samples, with two shown in Figure f and g. According to the method used by Baddorf, the work function can be determined from the UPS spectrum. The calculated work functions are 4.21 eV from Figure f and 3.92 eV from Figure g. However, it remains unclear which type of textures determine the work functions in this study. Indeed, the results confirm that the work function of the cobalt crystal is related to its lattice plane.

EC of the Cobalt Sample

Both sides of the treated sample (the outermost and inner sides of the original sample) exhibit the same EC value, 1 × 105 S/cm. This EC testing method does not reveal any difference between the outermost and inner sides of the original sample. It suggests that the macroscopic EC is minimally affected by the crystal texture of the cobalt sample. At least, the four-terminal electrodes cannot detect a difference in EC between (0 0 2) and (1 0 1). It can also be inferred that lattice planes have little influence on electrolysis.

DO of NaCl Solution

Figure a indicates that the temperature and NaCl solution concentration influence the DO in the NaCl solution. Temperature is the main factor affecting DO concentration. Specifically, the higher the temperature, the lower the DO concentration. The DO fluctuates over the 0.3 to 1.0 M concentration range, with the maximum DO being observed in the 0.5 to 0.7 M range. In contrast, all the DO data are at high values in the higher concentration range from 1 to 5 M.

Based on the data presented in Figure b, there is an approximate negative relationship between OCP and DO, which aligns with the negative correlation between the OCP of a-Zr55Cu45 and NaCl solution concentration (0.2 to 200 mM) and suggests that DO drives the cobalt corrosion reaction. The chemical reaction at the OCP state, where no extra voltage is applied and the corrosion rate is minimal, is roughly in equilibrium for a short period. Therefore, the Nernst equation is a sound theory that explains the approximate negative dependence. For the half-reaction, Ox + n e– ⇋ Red, the Nernst equation , can be expressed as 1 E=Eo+RTnF⁡ln⁡[Ox][Red]=Eo+0.059n⁡log⁡[Ox][Red] where E represents the electrode potential, E o is the standard electrode potential, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), n is the number of electrons involved in the corresponding reaction, F = 96485.33289 ± 0.00059 C/mol, T indicates the temperature in kelvin (when T = 298.15 K, the equation can be simplified further), [Ox] is the effective concentration of the oxidizing agent, and [Red] is the effective concentration of the corresponding material in the reduced state.

Notably, the negative relationship is difficult to reproduce because the electrode potential is influenced by many factors, such as temperature, the cobalt surface condition, the equilibrium state of the relevant reaction, and other variables. Based on the Nernst equation and the equilibrium of the related electrochemical and/or chemical reaction, it can be inferred that lower temperature and DO favor recurrence.

Viscosity of NaCl Solution

The viscosity of the NaCl solution can be modeled using a second-order polynomial (shown in Figure c) as follows: 2 Y=0.80777−0.19479X+0.09468X2 where Y represents viscosity in mPa·s/cm and X indicates the concentration of the NaCl solution in M. Generally, the viscosity of the NaCl solution can be divided into two stages. The first is a steady stage from 0 to 2 M, during which the concentration has no noticeable effect on viscosity. The second gradually increases from 2 to 5 M at room temperature. In this phase, viscosity increases significantly with concentration, and the rate of increase becomes progressively larger.

Products’ Phenomena of Real-Time Reaction

These products feature striking colors. Specifically, the color of β-Co­(OH)2 is deep red, rose-red, , or pink; ,, the color of Co2(OH)3Cl, which is called cobalt hydroxychloride, is lavender-colored or rosa-farbiges (meaning rose-red); and the color of Co1.176(OH)2Cl0.348(H2O)0.456 is blue or green. Corrosion and Electrolysis Mechanism

To distinguish it from electrolysis, this study defines corrosion, specifically self-corrosion, as the process of cobalt immersed in an NaCl solution with no voltage or only a minimal voltage. Electrolysis, however, involves a comparatively higher voltage applied to cobalt in an NaCl solution. Indeed, to be precise, electrolysis is also a form of corrosion.

EIS for Corrosion

In corrosion research, EIS, often fitted with an appropriate equivalent circuit, is a valuable technique widely used. ,,− We analyzed the EIS of cobalt samples in NaCl solution at various concentrations to examine the electrochemical reaction process. Subsequently, we developed two equivalent circuits to explain the corrosion reaction process of cobalt in an NaCl solution.

As shown in Figure a, it is a schematic diagram of equivalent circuit 1 that some scholars have used to explain their work. ,, This equivalent circuit can be expressed by the circuit description code (CDC) as R s(Q dl(R ct(Q f R f))). The equivalent circuit 2 displayed in Figure b is R s(Q dl(R ct(Q f(R f W pc)))). R s is the resistance of the solution, Q dl corresponds to the double layer capacitor related to the constant phase element (CPE), R ct represents the charge transfer resistance, Q f represents a novelly extended CPE for the capacitance of the product film (see eq ), R f denotes the film resistance, and W pc is a newly extended Warburg resistance reflecting the product conversion described above (see eq ).

The subsequent equation can determine the impedance of CPE in this paper: 24 ZCPE=1Yo(jω)n where Y o is CPE constant in Ω–1sn/Siemens·sn (S·sn) or F·sn‑1 and contains the capacitance information, j is the imaginary unit, ω represents the angular frequency, and n is the deviation index. Originally, the exponent n ranged from −1 to 1. When n equals 1, 0.5, 0, and −1, the CPE stands for capacitance, Warburg impedance, resistance, and inductive reactance, respectively. , Q dl and n dl belong to the original CPE mentioned above. Still, Q f and n f belong to a newly extended CPE with exceptional n f values outside the original range due to the product’s conversion.

The impedance of W pc, an initially extended element in this study, is defined by the following equation: 25 ZWpc=R(Tjω)p⁡tanh[(Tjω)p] where R is in Ω and j and ω are indicated as above, while T and P remain constant. When P = 0.5, the Z Wpc function acts as a Finite-Length Warburg component, representing a Generalized Finite Warburg component when P ranges from 0 to 1 (it can be checked in the software Z-View help file). This work defines Z Wpc to reflect product conversion/shift by setting P equal to or greater than 0.

In Figure c, for the 0.3 M NaCl solution within the frequency range from 10–3 to 105 Hz, four electrochemical processes (including three main ones) are marked by black arrows for the NaCl solution at 0.3 M. Specifically, these processes are (i) a charge transfer in the double layer, (ii) a mass transfer controlling process, (iii) a charge transfer within the product film, and (iv) a slight product conversion. Compared with the conditions at other concentrations (shown in Figure d–j), the mass transfer control process is observed in the 0.3 M NaCl solution due to the relatively low DO and stronger blocking effect from the β-Co­(OH)2 product film.

Weight Loss after Potentiodynamic Polarization

As shown in Figure d, the weight loss rises sharply from 0 to 3 M and then slightly from 3 to 4 M, and decreases significantly from 4 to 5 M. Therefore, the maximum weight loss is around 117.4 mg in a 4 M NaCl solution, with the second highest being very close to the first, about 113.0 mg in 3 M.

The phenomenon of weight loss mentioned above results from the following points: (i) In the absence of applied voltage, the magnitudes of EIS for 3 and 4 M NaCl solutions are the smallest within the frequency range of 10–2 to 105 Hz. (ii) When 0.5 V is applied, the magnitude of EIS decreases, and the rate of decrease becomes smaller as the NaCl solution concentration increases, until the magnitude for 5 M nearly equals that for 4 M, and the magnitude of EIS for 3 M is just marginally larger than that for 4 and 5 M. (iii) The viscosity of the NaCl solution at 5 M increases significantly after a notable rise (as shown in Figure c), which hampers the diffusion of corrosion products, leading to less weight loss at 5 M compared to 4 M.
