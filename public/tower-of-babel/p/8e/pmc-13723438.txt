Non-Invasive Evaluation of Pulmonary Artery Stenosis in Congenital Heart Disease Patients Using Phase Contrast MRI

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBranch pulmonary artery (PA) stenosis is a significant congenital heart defect causing elevated pulmonary blood pressure, trans-stenotic pressure drop, and abnormal differential blood flow to the lungs. We propose combined pressure-flow diagnostic parameters, pressure drop coefficient (CDP) and normalized energy loss (Ēloss) for improved delineation of stenosis severity. In extension to our previous benchtop experimental study, we performed in vitro experiments using phase contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC MRI) to evaluate the diagnostic parameters and stenosis severity non-invasively.MethodsSubject-specific branch PA test sections representing the main, left, and right PAs (MPA, LPA, and RPA) with a discrete LPA stenosis were manufactured from medical images using additive manufacturing. Three clinically-relevant stenosis severities, 70% area stenosis (AS), 80% AS, and 90% AS, were evaluated at 2 LPM cardiac output under pathophysiologic hemodynamics generated using a mock circulatory loop. 2D (all severities) and 3D (only 70% AS) PC MRI scans were performed to compute the velocity (or flow) and derived pressure drop across the PA test section. Interestingly, the CDPLPA computed by all three methods (benchtop, 2D and 3D PC MRI) showed distinct and non-overlapping ranges for each stenosis severity [70% AS: 11.0–14.5; 80% AS: 34.6–60.7; and 90% AS: 154.2–261.6]. Background

A stenosis in the branch pulmonary arteries (PA), clinically known as peripheral pulmonary stenosis, is a significant congenital heart defect having an estimated incidence of 836 per million live births [1–3]. The defect most often presents itself in conjunction with other congenital heart diseases (CHDs) such as tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) and truncus arteriosus; or genetic syndromes such as Williams, Alagille, or DiGeorge. It can also occur as a result of surgical procedures to correct CHDs [4–7]. Peripheral pulmonary stenosis may be present either as a discrete lesion or a diffuse narrowing in one (unilateral stenosis) or both (bilateral stenosis) branches of the pulmonary artery (PA) tree. Specifically, a residual unilateral PA stenosis, occurring after TOF repair, affects differential blood flow to the lungs resulting in impaired exercise capacity[8]. Additionally, it worsens pulmonary insufficiency and elevates proximal pressure causing RV overload [9, 10].

General guidelines for clinical diagnosis of PA stenosis include more than 20 mmHg pressure gradient across the stenosis, less than 35% differential flow to the affected lung, RV pressure load with two-third systemic pressure, and sometimes pulmonary valve regurgitation as an indirect marker [9]. Guided by the diagnosis, patients are treated with interventional therapy, including stenting or angioplasty to alleviate symptoms of the defect. Although these interventional procedures have shown to be effective in improving patient outcomes and quality of life, they are associated with procedural risks [4, 7, 11, 12]. It is important to diagnose stenosis severity accurately to minimize patient risk, and limit intervention to selected patients who require therapy. Also, reintervention is common in the clinical management of PA stenosis, which further warrants the need for accurate and continuous (longitudinal) monitoring of disease progression [11, 12].

Comparison of Current Modalities with Proposed Indices for Assessing PA Stenosis

Clinical modalities including echocardiography, computed tomography (CT), cardiac catheterization, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are standard test methods for the initial evaluation and follow-up of peripheral pulmonary stenosis [3, 13, 14].

Echocardiography is a primary non-invasive functional-anatomical modality, which is an advantage, for assessing PA stenosis but is fraught with limitations such as a) load dependent variations in flow impacting derived-pressure drop, b) inability to account for pressure recovery, c) measurement inaccuracies in branch pulmonary artery flow, and d) recirculating/vortical flow phenomenon. Furthermore, echocardiography is highly operator-dependent and can be inaccurate due to poor acoustic windows and angle-dependent Doppler measurements. Additionally, it lacks detailed anatomical resolution.

CT Angiography provides high-resolution, three-dimensional anatomical imaging of the pulmonary arteries, including distal branches. Although CT angiography is useful in detecting the presence of a stenosis [13], it lacks the ability to characterize the functional severity of the stenosis. Moreover, CT requires ionizing radiation and uses iodinated contrast, which poses allergy and risk to the kidney, particularly in pediatric populations. This causes it to be less suitable for repeated longitudinal (time) evaluation.

Catheterization is currently the primary standard-of-care clinical modality providing direct measurement of pressure drop. It enables clinicians to distinguish physiologically significant lesions from insignificant ones and supports interventional decision-making. However, catheterization is associated with procedural risk to pediatric patients due to it being invasive modality[15, 16]. Catheterization, often requires sedation in pediatric patients, is technically challenging in small or tortuous vessels. Additionally, catheterization for functional measurement does not provide anatomical detail and must be interpreted alongside imaging studies.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive, radiation-free tool for longitudinal assessment of PA stenosis in pediatric patients. MRI provides higher contrast of soft-tissues, enabling detailed anatomical visualization of the PA and surrounding structures. Advanced techniques such as phase-contrast MRI (PC-MRI) and 3D MRI for different time-points of a cardiac cycle allows quantification of important hemodynamic parameters. In other words, MRI can provide both functional and anatomical assessment sequentially in one sitting. However, MRI scan times are longer than echocardiography or CT. It often requires sedation in children or uncooperative patients, and its availability is limited due to cost concerns.

Conventional 2D PC MRI, which provides time-resolved through-plane (one-directional) velocity (and flow) data, has been used as a clinical standard of care to assess flow across obstructive lesions [8, 17]. Further, the feasibility of obtaining non-invasive pressure drop across a coronary stenosis using 2D PC MRI has been assessed in a pre-clinical and clinical setting [18]. Alternatively, 3D PC MRI offers the ability to measure and visualize the temporal evolution of complex flow field in a 3D space [19]. Our group has previously tested the feasibility of using 3D PC MRI to evaluate the non-invasive pressure field (and pressure drop) across a PA stenosis using retrospective clinical data [20]. Despite its theoretical advantages over 2D PC MRI, 3D PC MRI is still used occasionally in routine clinical practice.

MRI and echocardiography are used to assess the pathophysiology of pulmonary blood flow to the lungs in the presence of a stenosis[8], while providing anatomical details of the vessel [21, 22]. The utility of these two non-invasive modalities has been extended to evaluate derived pressure drop using simplified Bernoulli’s equation from velocity data. To the author’s knowledge, assessment of these modalities in presence of concomitant branch and stenotic geometries using either more accurate modified Bernoulli’s equation with viscous loss or Navier-Stokes equation has not been reported.

Proposed Diagnostic Index

Current diagnostic endpoints of PA stenosis are discrete -- based on either flow or pressure drop. They do not capture the complexity of branched flow from the main pulmonary artery (MPA) with asymmetric pressure drops and flow distributions. We evaluated the proposed diagnostic parameters, pressure drop coefficient (CDP: ratio of trans-stenotic pressure drop to the proximal dynamic pressure [23] - a function of square of velocity-and normalized energy loss (Ēloss: ratio of the total energy loss due to a stenosis to the flow across the stenosis). These two indices incorporates both pressure drop and flow in their formulation. Using a benchtop experiments and patient-specific vascular phantoms [24], we tested these two indices in a unilateral left PA (LPA) stenosis having three anatomical severities – 70% area stenosis (AS), 80% AS, and 90% AS. CDP showed a wider range [24] to delineate the severity of PA stenosis, because of square of velocity in its denominator. Therefore, CDP has the potential to aid in improved assessment of the PA stenosis.

2D and 3D PC MRI were used in the current study to compute the non-invasive pressure drop across the unilateral LPA stenosis from velocity data using analytical approach. Subsequently, we evaluated CDP and Ēloss to delineate the functional severity of the 70%, 80%, and 90% AS. Subject-specific vascular phantoms were used to mimic the different severities of LPA stenosis within a mock circulatory loop (MCL) that generated pathophysiologic PA hemodynamics. The utility of the MRI-based methodology for diagnosing a unilateral LPA stenosis has been assessed by comparing the results from this study with sensor-based direct pressure-flow measurements reported in our previous benchtop experimental study [24].

The present study is novel because it uses a non-invasive modality (MRI) to evaluate combined pressure-flow based CDP and Ēloss within a unilateral LPA stenosis of varying severities. The significance of this pre-clinical study is that it will lead to future clinical trials for the evaluation of CDP and Ēloss.

Experimental Setup

The pulmonary vascular anatomy of an 8-month old female subject, including the native main, left, and right PAs (MPA, LPA, and RPA), was reconstructed from a computed tomography (CT) angiogram using standard image segmentation and 3D reconstruction. To mimic a unilateral PA stenosis, a central discrete narrowing [5] was incorporated in the LPA using computer-aided design (CAD). Subsequently, additive manufacturing (3D printing) was used to obtain a physical representation of the subject-specific PA test section from the CAD model.

Test section. A typical geometry of the LPA stenosis, comprising of the converging, throat, and diverging regions, is shown in Fig. 2A. The regions proximal and distal to the stenosis are denoted by ‘e’ and ‘r,’ respectively. Based on clinical evidence [26], three anatomical severities of the LPA stenosis were evaluated: a) mild (~ 70% area stenosis (AS)), b) moderate (~ 80% AS), and c) severe (~ 90% AS). The geometric dimensions corresponding to the different stenosis severities are provided in Table 1. While only a single representative test section is shown in Fig. 1, three test sections representing the different stenosis severities were manufactured and used for the experiments. The section shown within the red box that includes the MPA, LPA, and RPA is used to develop subject-specific 3D vascular phantom based on the segmentation of CT images. The final 3D-printed model includes modifications, such as inlet/outlet extensions and interface connectors, to facilitate integration with the MCL.

Mock Circulatory Loop (MCL). Pathophysiologic hemodynamics within the stenotic PA test section were simulated using an MCL [24, 27–29], shown in Fig. 2B. Except for the PA test section, all components of the MCL were placed outside the MR scanner room (Fig. 2B). The pump, valves and fittings, instrumentation and data acquisition systems were not MR compatible and had to be placed outside the MR scanner room. This was necessary to avoid interference of magnetic field of the MR scanner with the metallic components of the pump, valves and fittings, instrumentation and data acquisition systems. Therefore, the blood analog fluid (BAF) had to travel through an extended tubing of additional ~ 25 ft due to MR scanner compatibility constraints, resulting in pressure and flow damping. The PA test section was secured to the MRI scanner bed and surrounded by saline bags. The saline bags were used to reduce artifacts due to the volume of air surrounding the test section. After securing the test section on the scanner bed, the bed was moved such that the test section was centrally located within the magnet bore. The 2D and 3D PC MRI scans were performed on the stenotic PA test section using a 1.5 T MRI scanner (Ingenia, Philips) and an 8-channel radiofrequency (RF) coil receiver.

Non-Newtonian BAF [24, 27–29] with dynamic viscosity, μ = 3.46 cP (at infinite shear rate), and density, ρ = 1057 kg/m3, was circulated through the test section using a pulsatile blood pump. Vascular compliance was simulated using air-filled compliance chambers, while the flow through each branch was controlled independently using flow control valves. An ultrasound Doppler in-line flow sensor F1 (ME−13PXN, Transonic Systems, Inc.) was placed distal to the compliance chamber (Fig. 2B) and recorded the MPA flow rate (or CO). A second ultrasound Doppler clamp-on flow sensor F2 (ME−9PXL, Transonic Systems, Inc.) was placed at the exit of the test section in the LPA branch (Fig. 2B) and recorded the LPA flow rate. F1 and F2 were calibrated up to an accuracy of ± 4% and ± 10%, respectively. The RPA flow rate was computed indirectly based on flow balance within the PA test section. Both flow sensors were coupled to a digital flowmeter console (TS410, Transonic Systems, Inc.) to acquire and display flow rates.

Pressure-Flow input. Representative experimental pulmonary artery flow and pressure pulses are reported in Figures 2C and 2D for an average of 2 LPM flow rate. The cardiac cycle duration (Tcycle) was ~ 0.9 s (Tsystole ~ 0.4 s, Tdiastole ~ 0.5 s). This benchtop experiment was conducted without the extended ~ 25 ft tubing. Similar to the benchtop experiments [24], the pMPA was maintained between 17 mmHg and 26 mmHg. To achieve this desired pressure at the inlet of the test section within the MR scanner, a higher pressure was required at the outlet of the pulsatile blood pump, located outside of the MR scanner room. This was necessary to overcome the pressure loss due to viscous damping and added compliance along the length (~25 ft.) of the tubing transporting BAF from the pump to the test section. Since the pressure within the test section could not be monitored simultaneously during the MR experiments, a pressure calibration test was conducted using the modified benchtop system having additional ~ 25 ft of tubing to pre-determine the required realistic pump outlet pressure that is applicable for MR experiments. The pMPA was varied between 18 and 25 mmHg at a fixed CO of 2 LPM and a fixed (arbitrary) QLPA/QMPA of 28%, while the corresponding pump outlet pressure was recorded for the modified benchtop experiments. A constant pressure difference (loss) of 9 mmHg was observed for all the pMPA values between the benchtop [24] and the modified benchtop experiment having additional ~ 25 ft tubing. In other words, during MR experiments the pump was operated 9 mmHg higher than the operating pressure used in the benchtop experiment, as also previously reported [24].

The flow (Fig. 2C) and pressure (Fig. 2D) values were first averaged over a cardiac cycle to obtain mean values, respectively. Subsequently, a statistical average of such mean values of the three experimental dataset was computed. The averaged pressure-flow data was used to obtain pressure drop and flow rates (Q).

A cardiac output (CO) of 2 L/min was selected. Clinically, 2 L/min CO is near the lower end of the normal range for pediatric patients, as reported in population studies [30]. This level of CO is also commonly observed in congenital heart disease patients with low-output states, causing it to be relevant and representative choice for our study. Furthermore, it also matched the conditions for which comprehensive benchtop data were available, allowing direct comparison with MRI results. The study was also limited by the specification and sizing of the currently available Harvard apparatus pump (Model 1423). It may be noted that a higher flowrate of 5 L/min Harvard apparatus pump is designed for systemic circulation of left heart in adults, whereas a lower flowrate of 2 L/min Harvard apparatus pump (Model 1423) is suited for reduced CO of right heart as in pediatric patients or for coronary circulation in adults.

Experimental Procedure

The pathophysiologic LPA flow split, QLPA/QMPA, corresponding to the 70%, 80%, and 90% AS LPAs at specific cardiac outputs (COs) was evaluated in our benchtop experimental study using pressure drop-flow rate (Δp-Q) characteristics derived from clinical data [24]. The PC MRI experiments were designed based on our prior understanding of the unilateral LPA stenosis pathophysiology. The target pathophysiologic QLPA/QMPA values set for the 70%, 80%, and 90% AS cases were 47%, 36%, and 21%, respectively. Further, the mean (time-averaged) MPA pressure, pMPA, was maintained between 17 mmHg and 26 mmHg for the different stenosis severities [24]. 2D PC MRI data was acquired for all the three LPA stenosis severities, i.e., 70%, 80%, and 90% AS, while 3D PC MRI was acquired for the 70% AS only as a means of an initial evaluation of the novel technology. Results

Discussion

The in vitro experiments involving the use of an MCL, subject-specific phantoms, and PC MRI provide a controlled environment for non-invasive diagnosis of peripheral pulmonary stenosis. Our recently published benchtop study [24] provided pathophysiologic hemodynamic data for designing the MRI experiments and assessing the importance of the MRI-based diagnostic technique.

With the advent of additive manufacturing and improved MRI technology, phantom-based PC MRI experiments have gained increasing importance among researchers for non-invasive assessment of vascular hemodynamics in a pre-clinical setting [18, 35–40]. In particular, extensive in vitro research for improving 3D PC MRI (or 4D MRI) technology has been conducted over the past few years using vascular phantoms. Most studies compute the velocity and flow field within the vessel from the phase and magnitude images for assessing indices such as peak (jet) velocity, retrograde flow, and stroke volume [41]. A few studies have extended the application of PC MRI to derive the pressure gradient across the vessel or region of interest using either the modified/simplified Bernoulli equation for 2D PC MRI data or the Navier-Stokes equation for 3D PC MRI data [18, 20, 37]. Pressure and flow rate data obtained from the phantom-based MRI scans have been mostly used as a baseline measure to assess MRI data from human subjects. However, comparison of pressure-flow rate data, obtained from MRI experiments, and direct measurements using pressure and flow sensors in phantoms have rarely been reported. Although phantom-based PC MRI experiments have shown promise in early feasibility studies, there are inherent challenges in obtaining a) a high signal-to-noise ratio, and b) a meaningful MR signal in the absence of realistic blood flow and vessel properties.

For the current study, system modifications such as a) the use of saline bags around the phantom, b) tuning of the velocity encoding value (VENC), and c) centralizing the location of the phantom within the magnet bore were necessary to acquire an MR signal with a moderate to high signal-to-noise ratio [42]. The comparison of flow rates computed using PC MRI (hereafter referred to as ‘PC MRI flow rates’) with those recorded directly by the flowmeter provided an indirect measure of the quality of the MRI data. Although the relative error in flow rate between the PC MRI and flowmeter measurements was relatively high (closer to 20%) for few cases, it is important to note that such a disparity is a consequence of inherent variabilities in both measurement techniques. For example, the flow rates recorded by the flowmeter were observed to fluctuate ± 200 ml/min of the value reported in Tables 4 and 5 for each experiment. Similarly, the PC MRI flow rates represent the statistical mean of three datasets (number of signal averages, NSA = 3) acquired by the scanner for each scan sequence during a specific experiment. Thus, the standard deviation in PC MRI measurements remains unknown because the resultant image dataset contains only the statistical mean velocity at each point in 2D or 3D space.

The quality of the MR signal may also be impacted by complex flow phenomena such as high-velocity jet, flow separation and recirculation, and shear layer instabilities occurring in the presence of a stenosis. Such flow phenomena have been known to cause imaging artifacts and signal loss, especially in the throat and distal regions of the stenosis, resulting in erroneous velocity and flow rate measurements [35, 38, 39]. O’Brien et al. [35] reported errors in flow rate measurements between PC MRI and the flowmeter ranging from less than 10% to greater than 50% in the presence of a stenosis, where the error increased with flow rate and echo time. Compared to this study, the errors (within 20%) in the present study were observed to be near the lower limit of the error range.

The error in PC MRI flow rates influences the pathophysiologic LPA flow split, which is an important parameter for evaluating the diagnostic indices. For example, the pathophysiologic QLPA/QMPA, derived from 2D PC MRI, was higher in case of the 80% AS and lower in the case of the 90% AS when compared to the benchtop results. These differences may have led to inconsistent trends in the total trans-stenotic pressure drop derived from PC MRI (= 3.49 mmHg (70% AS), 7.23 mmHg (80% AS), and 6.94 mmHg (90% AS)). Therefore, the lower E¯loss,LPA (Fig. 6B) may be due to the lower pressure drop for 90% AS case. In addition to the effect of MRI signal quality, the estimation of trans-stenotic pressure drop from PC MRI data using simplified conservation laws may be another reason for the differences between the diagnostic parameter values computed from different techniques. For example, the pressure drop was derived using an unsteady laminar flow assumption, which is known to result in an underestimation of pressure drop of up to 18% in comparison to pulsatile flow calculations [32]. Therefore, the lower MRI-based CDPLPA and E¯loss,LPA values (Fig. 6), may be a consequence of the underestimated pressure drop.

Importantly, consistent trends were observed in the diagnostic parameters for the different severities of LPA stenosis, irrespective of the variations in the pathophysiologic LPA flow split. Specifically, distinct and non-overlapping CDPLPA were observed for each case of LPA stenosis severity. This trend in CDP is similar to those observed in in vitro [27–29] and in vivo [43, 44] studies on coronary artery stenoses. Such distinct CDPLPA are achieved due to the high significance paid to the LPA flow (square of ue,LPA) in the definition of CDPLPA. This fact allows CDPLPA to have a better ability to delineate the stenosis severity compared to E¯loss,LPA, as reported in this study.

Further, this study demonstrates that PC MRI can be used a) as a non-invasive modality to quantify the pathophysiologic distribution of PA blood flow, and b) to derive hemodynamics-based indices for assessing PA stenosis in CHD patients. While this study has primarily focused on 2D PC MRI, the applicability of 3D PC MRI has also been explored. Although 3D PC MRI may be an improved technique, allowing the capture of transient velocity over the entire 3D flow domain, its accuracy and usefulness is limited by the use of a single VENC value over the entire scan volume. The VENC is typically selected to encompass the highest velocities within the flow domain. If the VENC is significantly higher than the lowest velocity in the flow domain, a low signal-to-noise ratio is observed, resulting in a less than optimum image quality. On the other hand, a VENC value that is set much lower than the highest velocity results in velocity aliasing.

As stated earlier, flow across the stenotic PA test section is highly complex and dynamic with appreciable spatial and temporal variations in the velocity field. Considering pathophysiologic flow at 2 LPM CO for the 70% AS case, the range in spatially-averaged velocity over a cross-sectional plane was observed to be 40 cm/s – 150 cm/s in the PA flow domain. The VENC value for this case was set to 130 cm/s during the 3D PC MRI data acquisition. This single value may not accurately capture the velocity in the regions with very low (i.e. in the MPA and recirculation regions distal to stenosis) or very high (i.e. in the stenosis throat and jet in the diverging region of stenosis) flow. Thus, the VENC needs to be optimized for each stenosis severity at a specific flow rate to obtain better quality 3D PC MRI data with minimal signal loss.

Limitations of Pressure Drop Calculations

Theoretical Equations. The velocity data obtained from PC MRI was used as an input to mass and momentum conservation equations for deriving the pressure drop across the stenosis. This is an improvement over the modified/simplified Bernoulli’s equations for obtaining non-invasive pressure drop data and functional diagnostic parameters. The theoretical equations for pressure drop are derived using assumptions such as time-averaged unsteady laminar flow with shear layer instabilities, Newtonian fluid, parabolic velocity profile, and empirical constants for branch loss. Only theoretical equations were used to calculate ΔpMPA-LPA as 1) invasive pressure measurements using pressure wires/transducers were not possible within the MR scanner, and 2) numerical transient calculations for pulsatile flow using CFD techniques were not the intended scope of this study. Therefore, previous studies in our and other labs [31–34] were used to discuss possible errors in ΔpMPA-LPA calculations.

Time averaged unsteady laminar flow assumption is one of the limitations of the adopted analytical approach which neglects time dependent phenomena such as transient shear layer growth in the throat section and time varying recirculation zone distal to PA stenosis. Complexities associated with temporal variation with developing flow and non-Newtonian fluid can be evaluated as a future step. On a similar note, the velocity obtained from PC MRI represents an average value over specific cross-sectional planes placed proximal and distal to the stenosis. Therefore, the local variation in velocity within the flow domain may not have been fully captured.

The translesional pressure drop obtained through the analytical approach is, in general, lower than the ones from the numerical calculations using pulsatile flow. This variation in the pressure drop is due to simplified assumptions such as time-averaged unsteady flow and Newtonian fluid. However, this variation can be reduced by using a correction factor that can account for such limitations. In our previous studies on the translesional pressure drop for moderate and severe stenoses [45, 46], the ratio of the pressure drop for pulsatile and time-averaged unsteady flow condition is determined to be approximately 1.16. The analytical pressure drop can be multiplied by this factor to obtain the corrected pressure drop, and therefore, both CDPLPA and E¯loss,LPA can be re-calculated. The corrected pressure drops will further reduce the difference between the corresponding corrected analytical and the benchtop experimental [24] values or numerical calculations using pulsatile flow, once conducted in-future.

Previous studies [32, 46, 47] have shown that mean translesional pressure drop for severe (90%) PA stenosis can be obtained within 2–18% error of pulsatile calculations (also discussed above). It should be mentioned that due to the smaller magnitude of pressure drop for the moderate (70%) PA stenosis the percentage difference between numerical and analytical results could be relatively higher as compared to the intermediate (80%) and severe (90%) PA stenosis. However, it is expected that the higher percentage difference for moderate stenosis will not affect diagnostic assessments influencing clinical treatment as the magnitude of the pressure drop is smaller. Therefore, for intermediate to severe PA stenosis, time-averaged unsteady laminar assumption can provide valuable information on hemodynamic parameters such as pressure drop under clinical settings.

An alternate method has been proposed by our group [20] for non-invasively deriving pressure drop using 3D PC MRI. The method involves the use of streamlines, computed from the captured 3D velocity field, to derive the pressure drop across the stenosis at each time point in the cardiac cycle. The resultant pressure drop across the stenosis is an integration of the incremental pressure drops computed along streamlines; thus, accounting for the velocities at each location in the 3D flow domain. Therefore, the alternate 3D PC MRI methodology for calculating trans-stenotic pressure drop may provide an improved assessment of CDPLPA and E¯loss,LPA. Furthermore, to enable widespread clinical adoption, a prospective clinical trial with a larger patient population across a range of CHD patients is required.

Conclusions

An MCL in conjunction with a subject-specific vascular phantom was useful in generating pathophysiologic hemodynamics of a unilateral LPA stenosis in an in vitro setting. 2D and 3D PC MRI was used to acquire non-invasive pressure-flow data within the vascular phantom, and subsequently compute CDPLPA and E¯loss,LPA for the three stenosis severities. To evaluate the efficacy of the MRI-based diagnostic method, the MRI results were compared with direct pressure-flow measurements obtained from a previous benchtop experimental study.

Overall, CDPLPA and E¯loss,LPA (absolute value) showed an increasing trend with an increase in stenosis severity. More importantly, CDPLPA showed distinct and non-overlapping values for each of the three stenosis severities when computed using all modalities (i.e., 2D and 3D PC MRI and benchtop experiments). Thus, CDPLPA demonstrated superior diagnostic capabilities over E¯loss,LPA in delineating a unilateral LPA stenosis severity. Further, the pre-clinical evaluations using in vitro experiments show that PC MRI may be a comprehensive and safe clinical modality for the non-invasive diagnosis of branch PA stenosis. Future research required to standardize the use of the non-invasive diagnostic methodology for congenital PA stenosis include: a) enhanced and more accurate computations of the diagnostic parameters using 3D PC MRI and b) the use of a clinical study involving human subject data to evaluate CDPLPA and E¯loss,LPA with PC MRI.
