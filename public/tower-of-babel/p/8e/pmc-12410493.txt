Unveiling rare metastasis in prostate cancer: The role of PSMA-PET and MRI in detecting penile involvement – A case report and literature review
Authors: Léon Groenhoff, Gaetano Federico Fortuna, Pietro Costantini, Anna Colarieti, Silvia Attanasio, Alessandro Carriero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study is to report a case of penile metastasis from prostate carcinoma, as it represents a very rare occurrence that clinicians should be aware of.We report a case of a 68-year-old patient affected by prostate cancer who has performed a PSMA-PET after radical prostatectomy for PSA elevation, which revealed a suspected uptake in the corpora cavernosa and corpora spongiosum, followed by multiparametric MRI examination with focus on penile involvement.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer among men, with high incidence and mortality rate.1 It is typically asymptomatic or may present with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).2 Measurement of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood level is an essential tool for prostate cancer screening3 and can be used to determine the indication for performing a prostate MRI.4 The role of multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) is to predict the presence of clinically significant lesions using the descriptors according to the Prostate Imaging Reporting & Data System v2.1 (PI-RADS) and to guide biopsy.5 The therapeutic options are varied, ranging from active surveillance, radical prostatectomy, external radiation therapy, brachytherapy and cryotherapy for localized disease, to hormone therapy and chemotherapy for advanced disease.6 After treatment, to assess any residual disease or unknown metastasis, serum PSA should be monitored7 and if necessary a MRI or prostate-specific membrane antigen-PET (PSMA-PET) should be performed.8

Investigations/imaging findings

A 68-year-old patient with prostate cancer came to the attention of our radiodiagnostic department for macrohematuria and LUTS. These data were highly indicative of clinically significant cancer, confirmed by subsequent fusion biopsy, which showed acinar prostate cancer with a Gleason Score (GS) of 10 (5 + 5).

The next step, in agreement with the patient, was a radical prostatectomy which confirmed the presence of acinar adenocarcinoma, reduced the GS to 9 (5 + 4), and revealed extracapsular infiltration, limphovascular and perineural invasion, while seminal vesicles and iliac lymph nodes involvement were absent. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of a locally advanced cancer.

Subsequently the patient was monitored by serum PSA, which one month after prostatectomy was 2.08 ng/mL, and after another month rapidly increased to 4 ng/mL. This laboratory finding was very alarming for the presence of residual disease and a PET-CT with 68Ga-PSMA was performed which revealed an uptake of radiolabelled compound in the corpora cavernosa (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Axial and sagittal fusion CT-PET. Literature review

To our knowledge and based on our research there is no comprehensive dataset or consensus in the literature regarding the true incidence of penile metastases from prostate cancer due to their rarity.

Tatkovic et al., in a retrospective study analyzing PSMA PET-CT scans, reported an incidence of penile metastasis in prostate cancer of just 0.1 %.9

Landen et al. identified 197 cases of prostate cancer with secondary penile involvement from prostate cancer, including 72 newly reported cases since 2006.10

Remarkably, only 7 of these patients had isolated penile metastases, without additional sites of disease, primarily lymph node or bone involvement. This makes our case particularly rare, as it represents an exceptionally uncommon pattern of metastatic spread in prostate cancer.

We conducted a PubMed search from March 2021 to January 2025 using combination of the keywords “PSMA-PET, Prostate cancer, and Penile metastasis” to update the reported cases since the review of Landen et al.

We identified 18 new case reports since March 2021, documenting a total of 21 newly reported cases of penile metastasis from prostate cancer in the last years (Table 1). Learning points/discussion

Our case highlights the importance of considering unusual metastatic sites in patients with elevated PSA levels during post-prostatectomy follow-up.

Awareness of penile metastasis is crucial for early detection, appropriate management and for improving outcomes in patients with aggressive prostate cancer profiles.

PSMA-PET plays a crucial role in detecting metastatic lesions in unusual locations due to its high specificity. However, false positives can occur, particularly from urinary tracer accumulation, which may mimic pathological uptake. MRI becomes essential in distinguishing true metastatic deposits from physiological findings.

As the use of PSMA-PET has increased and continues to be adopted in clinical practice in prostate cancer follow-up, it will be interesting to see whether the detection rate of penile metastases increases. Future studies may help identify risk factors for penile involvement, not only those linked to the primary tumor's characteristics but also other potential contributing factors.
