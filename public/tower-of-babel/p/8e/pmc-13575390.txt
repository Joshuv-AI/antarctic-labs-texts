Prognostic value of day 10 mobilization status for ambulation at hospital discharge in critically ill patients: a historical cohort study in Japan
Authors: Tsubasa Watanabe, Masatoshi Hanada, Yudai Yano, Masato Oikawa, Yosuke Morimoto, Takuya Fukushima, Hiroki Nagura, Yasutomo Tanaka, Rina Takeuchi, Terumitsu Sawai, Motohiro Sekino, Ryo Kozu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCritically ill patients often have long-lasting limitations in activities of daily living, particularly in walking ability. Although early mobilization increases the likelihood of independent ambulation at hospital discharge, the timing at which different mobilization levels become associated with ambulatory recovery is unclear. This study describes the timing for achieving several mobilization levels and its associations with ambulatory recovery at hospital discharge.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, patients were divided into a regained group, composed of patients whose ambulation recovered to pre–intensive care unit (ICU) admission levels by hospital discharge, and a non-regained group. Multivariable logistic regression analyses and receiver operating characteristic curve analyses were performed to examine the association between the timing of mobilization (standing, marching in place, and assisted walking) and regained ambulation at hospital discharge.ResultsIn total, 295 patients were included, and 223 (76%) of them had regained ambulation at hospital discharge. The adjusted odds ratio for standing peaked on day 10 after ICU admission (odds ratio, 5.61; 95% CI, 2.21–17.76) and declined thereafter. The optimal cutoff time linking marching in place and walking with assistance to regained ambulation was also day 10 after ICU admission (marching in place: area under the curve [AUC], 0.78; sensitivity, 0.83; specificity, 0.64; and walking with assistance: AUC, 0.79; sensitivity, 0.77; specificity, 0.68).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that mobility status on day 10 after ICU admission can serve as a pragmatic marker for identifying patients at risk of poor ambulatory recovery.

INTRODUCTION

Critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) often experience prolonged physical dysfunction and limitations in activities of daily living (ADLs) [1-3]. Approximately 40% of critically ill patients who were independent before ICU admission had limited ADLs for 5 years after discharge [2,3]. Of the patients who walked independently without walking aids before long-term ventilatory management, 70% had not regained ambulation at discharge [4]. Therefore, ambulatory recovery is extremely difficult to achieve. The ability to walk is important because it affects quality of life, social and community activities, and health care costs [5-7].

Muscle weakness in the ICU is caused by disuse [8]. Early mobilization (EMo) refers to the initiation of physical activity, such as passive range of motion, sitting, standing, or walking, during the early stages of postoperative or critical illness recovery [9,10]. EMo has been shown to improve muscle strength and increase the probability of independent ambulation at hospital discharge [11-13]. Patients receiving EMo achieved an approximately 20% higher rate of walking without assistance at the time of discharge than the control group [14,15]. Collectively, those data suggest that implementing an EMo protocol is important for maximizing the likelihood that patients will regain their ability to walk [16], although the optimal timing for EMo remains uncertain.

Liu et al. [17] reported that patients with sepsis who sat on the edge of the bed or more within 3 days of ICU admission were less dependent on ambulation assistance at hospital discharge than those who did not achieve mobilization within 3 days. However, the extent to which the timing of initiation for each mobilization level (standing, marching in place, and walking with assistance) affects gait recovery at discharge is unclear [17-19]. Early walking is important because an increase in conventional walking training influences walking independence [20]. Although EMo increases the probability of independent ambulation at hospital discharge, the timing at which different mobilization levels become associated with the recovery of ambulation remains unclear. In this study, we describe the timing at which each mobilization level is associated with ambulatory recovery at hospital discharge.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committee of Nagasaki University Hospital (No. 17082110). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design and the opt-out method of patient inclusion.

Study Design, Setting, and Patients

This single-center, retrospective cohort study analyzed patients who were admitted to the mixed adult medical-surgical ICU at Nagasaki University Hospital for the first time between April 2016 and March 2018. The inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, ICU length of stay ≥48 hours, treatment with physiotherapy, and survival to hospital discharge. We excluded patients who met any of the following criteria: pre-existing neurological disorders, major trauma or limb amputation, ventricular assist device, palliative care, or inability to ambulate before ICU admission. Patients who underwent lung transplantation were also excluded because the unique characteristics of their postoperative management affect EMo.

Data Collection

We collected baseline data on patient demographics and characteristics at ICU admission (including previous comorbidities and ADL status), medical management in the ICU, ICU-related complications, mobility-level changes until hospital discharge, and the outcome of hospital discharge from medical records. The following baseline data on ICU admission were collected: age, sex, body mass index, primary diagnosis, ICU type, postoperative status, emergency admission, emergency surgery, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores. We also collected the following data before ICU admission: Charlson Comorbidity Index score, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG-PS), dependent ADLs, and use of gait aids. ADLs were evaluated using the Katz Index [21], which consists of six items (bathing, dressing, going to the toilet, transferring, continence, and feeding). Each item was scored as 1 (independence) or 0 (dependence); a total score of 6 was defined as independence in ADLs.

In the management and treatment of patients in the ICU, we investigated the use and durations of deep sedation, mechanical ventilation, continuous hemodiafiltration, intra-aortic balloon pumping, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and the length of ICU stay. We defined deep sedation as a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale <–2 using sedatives. The ICU-related complications were delirium and severe muscle weakness. Delirium was assessed daily in the ICU by a nurse using the confusion assessment method for the ICU and was considered present if it was detected at least once. Severe muscle weakness was defined as a Medical Research Council sum score (MRC-SS) of <36 at ICU discharge [22]. The MRC-SS was evaluated by physiotherapists in patients with suspected significant muscle weakness. Interobserver agreement for an MRC-SS of <36 in the ICU was very good [22].

We assessed the level of mobilization until hospital discharge using the ICU Mobility Scale (IMS) [10], whether ambulation was independent, length of hospital stay, and discharge destination (home or another hospital). To improve clarity, we consistently express mobilization as threshold attainment using the notation IMS ≥k (k=3, 4, 6, and 7). In the IMS, grade 3 corresponds to sitting on the edge of the bed, grade 4 to standing, grade 6 to marching in place, and grade 7 to walking with assistance. Ambulation independence was defined as the ability to walk for >45 m on level ground, based on the Barthel index [17]. We categorized patients’ ambulatory status at hospital discharge into three levels: “without gait aid,” “with gait aid,” and “dependent.” There were no limitations on the type of gait aid used, such as leaning on an intravenous drip stand, cane, or walker.

EMo Protocol and Exercise Training after ICU Discharge

EMo was implemented according to our ICU rehabilitation protocol, which was developed based on a previous report [23]. We established five stages from level 0 to 4 and conducted physiotherapy at each level. The criteria for EMo initiation are cardiorespiratory stability (mean arterial pressure >65 mm Hg, FiO2 <60%, PaO2/FiO2 ratio >150, and respiratory rate <40 bpm), neurological stability, and body temperature <40 °C. Level 0 indicates that the initiation criteria have not been met, so physiotherapy is conducted only for body positioning, or no treatment is provided. Level 1 indicates that the initiation criteria have been met. Level 1 physiotherapy involves passive range of motion exercise, respiratory physiotherapy, and sitting in bed. The progression criteria for the EMo protocol are as follows: Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale between –2 and +1, systolic blood pressure >80 mm Hg, heart rate <130 or >40 bpm, SpO2 >90%, no fatal ventricular arrhythmia events, no assisted mechanical circulatory support (intra-aortic balloon pumping or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation), no new deep vein thrombosis, and intracranial pressure <20 mm Hg. Patients transition to level 2 if the progression criteria are met after the level 1 intervention. At level 2, physiotherapy involves active range of motion exercise, resistance training for the limbs, and sitting on the edge of the bed, in addition to the program for level 1. Patients transition to level 3 if the progression criteria are met when they are sitting on the edge of the bed, only one person is needed to assist in maintaining the sitting position, and the Medical Research Council score for knee extension is 2 or more. Level 3 physiotherapy is conducted with the patient standing, in addition to the program of level 2. Patients transition to level 4 if the progression criteria are met when the patient is standing with only one person helping to maintain the standing position. Level 4 physiotherapy involves marching in place and/or walking with assistance.

After ICU discharge, we continue exercise training until hospital discharge, according to the patient's physical function and activity level. When a patient does not reach level 4 before ICU discharge, physiotherapy is continued according to the ICU EMo protocol. For patients who have difficulty maintaining a sitting position, we passively move them to a chair or sit them over the edge of the bed and try to extend the sitting time. For patients who have difficulty standing, we perform standing with assistance using handrails and a four-point walker. For patients who have difficulty walking, we practice walking with assistance using an intravenous drip stand, cane, or wheeled walker and try to increase the walking distance. In addition, patients are assigned to muscular strength and aerobic exercise training groups in accordance with their physical function. All exercise training sessions are performed based on vital signs and subjective, objective, or both types of symptoms. The training frequency is 5 or 6 days weekly, and the training time is 20–60 minutes daily.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as the number of observations with percentages and medians with interquartile ranges. We defined the regained group as patients who had achieved their pre-ICU admission walking status by the time of hospital discharge and the non-regained group as all other patients. Measurements were compared between the groups. Continuous variables were compared using Student t-test, the Mann-Whitney U-test, or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies and were compared using the chi-square test.

We examined the association between mobilization and regaining ambulation using multivariable logistic regression. Mobilization was expressed as attainment of the IMS thresholds (IMS ≥3, ≥4, ≥6, and ≥7). IMS grade 5 (transfer from bed to chair) was not included in this analysis because it was considered to be less directly related to walking than the other grades. We analyzed IMS threshold attainment at ICU discharge as a predictor and performed day-specific analyses during the first 14 days after ICU admission. For this purpose, we estimated adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for each predictor and 95% CIs for each factor. We hypothesized that certain baseline and ICU-related clinical characteristics (age, ECOG-PS, SOFA score, ICU type, and duration of deep sedation) would be associated with regained ambulation [24,25]. Thus to explore those hypothesized associations, individual logistic regression models were constructed to adjust for age, ECOG-PS, SOFA score, ICU type, and duration of deep sedation. This study is exploratory, and no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Therefore, we interpret our results using point and interval estimates. We also performed subgroup analyses for age (<70 vs. ≥70 years), ICU type (surgical vs. medical), and disease severity (SOFA score <10 vs. ≥10). Goodness-of-fit tests were performed for all multiple logistic regression models. The times to achieve IMS grades 3, 4, 6, and 7 that discriminated between the regained and non-regained groups were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 17.1 (SAS Institute, Inc.). The significance level was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of Patients

In total, 1,009 patients were admitted to the ICU for the first time during the study period, as shown in the flow diagram in Figure 1. Ultimately, 295 patients were included in this analysis. Among them, 223 (76%) regained ambulation, and 72 (24%) did not. Among the patients who regained ambulation, 199 (89%) regained ambulation “without gait aid,” and 24 (11%) regained ambulation “with gait aid.” Among patients who did not regain ambulation, 30 (42%) declined from “without gait aid” to “with gait aid,” 26 (36%) declined from “without gait aid” to “dependent,” and 16 (22%) declined from “with gait aid” to “dependent.”

Comparison of the Regained and Non-regained Groups

The non-regained group was older and had lower body mass index and higher SOFA scores than the regained group. They also had higher rates of admission to the medical ICU, emergency admission, septic shock, and respiratory failure and lower rates of post-cardiovascular surgery and post-liver transplant. Before ICU admission, the non-regained group had significantly higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores and ECOG-PS grades, as well as higher rates of ADL dependency and walking aid use. During the ICU stay, this group had higher rates of deep sedation, mechanical ventilation, and continuous hemodiafiltration use, and they had longer ICU stays than the regained group (Table 1). Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed information about the primary diagnoses.

The groups did not differ significantly in the use or duration of intra-aortic balloon pumping and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, both of which affect mobilization ability. Each level of mobilization was achieved more frequently in the regained group. At ICU discharge, the proportion of patients who achieved IMS ≥3 was similar between groups, whereas the proportions achieving IMS ≥4 and IMS ≥6 were higher in the regained group. The length of hospital stay was longer in the non-regained group, and only 3% of the patients in this group were discharged home (Table 1).

Timing of Mobilization Level Initiation

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis models, the adjusted ORs for each mobilization level at ICU discharge were 1.12 (95% CI, 0.53–2.30), 1.62 (95% CI, 0.87–3.00), 2.08 (95% CI, 0.91–5.22), and 1.06 (95% CI, 0.16–21.05) for IMS ≥3, ≥4, ≥6, and ≥7, respectively. The adjusted ORs during the first 14 days after ICU admission are shown in Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 1. IMS ≥3 was not consistently associated with group assignment (regained or non-regained), except on days 5 and 6 after ICU admission. The timing of initiation of each mobilization level beyond sitting was associated with regained ambulation (IMS ≥4 from day 1 onward, IMS ≥6 from day 3 onward, and IMS ≥7 from day 4 onward). The adjusted OR for IMS ≥4 peaked on day 10 (OR, 5.61; 95% CI, 2.21–17.76) after ICU admission, and the value tended to decline after that. The adjusted ORs for IMS ≥6 and ≥7 tended to increase until day 14. Subgroup analyses showed similar trends in both the medical ICU and severe case groups (Supplementary Figures 1-3). No overfitting was observed in any model.

Prediction of Ambulatory Status Recovery

Receiver operating characteristic analyses were used to determine whether the time taken to achieve IMS grades 3, 4, 6, and 7 predicted regained ambulation status (Figure 3). The higher the level of mobilization, the larger the areas under the curve in these analyses. For IMS 7, the highest positive predictive value was 99% on day 4 after ICU admission, and the negative predictive value was 65% on day 17.

DISCUSSION

The main findings of this study are: (1) the start date for each mobilization level beyond sitting was associated with regained ambulation (IMS ≥4, ≥6, and ≥7 on days 1, 3, and 4, respectively), (2) the highest positive predictive value (99%) was for walking with assistance on day 4 after ICU admission, (3) day 10 after ICU admission was the peak adjusted OR for IMS ≥4 and the cutoff point for IMS grade 6 and 7 to regain ambulation at hospital discharge.

In this retrospective cohort, early initiation of standing and assisted walking was associated with a higher likelihood of ambulation at hospital discharge. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantitatively describe the optimal timing for the initiation of standing, marching in place, and assisted walking to maximize the chance of regained ambulation at discharge. Studies have shown that EMo improves ADL and walking ability [14,15]; however, sitting on the edge of the bed was not associated with regained ambulation in this study, except on days 5 and 6 after ICU admission. Our findings suggest that regaining ambulation upon hospital discharge is not much associated with sitting on the edge of the bed but is associated with early assisted standing and walking. Previous studies defined EMo as sitting on the edge of the bed or higher, and they demonstrated that achieving that level within the first 3 days of an ICU stay was significantly associated with walking and ADL independence [17-19]. In this study, regain indicates a return to the pre-ICU ambulatory status, rather than to recovery of normal or independent walking. This distinction is important to prevent overinterpreting the findings as complete functional recovery. Regaining pre-ICU ambulation is a challenging outcome; however, it is an important outcome because most patients who could not regain their pre-ICU ambulation were not discharged home.

The highest positive predictive value for walking with assistance was 99% on day 4 after ICU admission, which indicates that patients who began walking with assistance by day 4 after ICU admission in this cohort had a relatively high probability of regaining ambulation at discharge and that day-4 assisted walking can serve as a marker of a favorable recovery trajectory. Hori et al. [26] reported that initiating walking by day 3 after cardiac surgery prevented ADL decline in most patients, which supports the results of this study. Early walking minimizes the effects of immobility, prevents muscle atrophy, and can help patients regain ambulation. However, in the non-regained group, the median length of ICU stay was 5.6 days, which could partly explain why those patients could not walk within 4 days. In our EMo protocol, level 4 involves marching in place, walking with assistance, or both. It is possible that only marching in place was chosen because of staffing limitations or environmental constraints. Nevertheless, the trends in the adjusted ORs and areas under the curve for IMS grades 6 and 7 were similar. Denehy et al. [27] reported that an exercise program for patients that included marching in place after 5 days of ICU admission improved the 6-minute walking distance after ICU discharge. Future studies should examine whether marching in place can serve as an effective alternative to walking training.

In our analysis, day 10 after ICU admission corresponds to the peak OR for standing and emerges as a practical cutoff for marching in place and walking with assistance. These findings describe associations and likely reflect the recovery trajectory of physical function, rather than demonstrating a direct causal effect of mobilization intensity. Severe inflammation increases protein catabolism, and one week of immobility impairs the protein anabolic/catabolic balance [28]. The cross-sectional area of the quadriceps femoris in critically ill patients decreases rapidly within 10 days and remains unchanged thereafter [29]. In addition, the reduction in muscle strength caused by 12 days of immobility and inflammation did not resolve after 36 days [30]. Ten days of bed rest or lack of antigravity loading for the lower extremities in critically ill patients might make short-term recovery of walking ability difficult. Therefore, patients who are not standing within 10 days after ICU admission could be at increased risk of not regaining their ambulation and might require closer monitoring, more intensive or prolonged rehabilitation, and early planning for post-acute care and caregiver support.

Importantly, mobility status on day 10 after ICU admission was strongly influenced by baseline disease severity, comorbidities, frailty, and the intensity and duration of organ support, all of which are key determinants of discharge outcomes. In our ICU, the initiation and progression of mobilization followed our ICU rehabilitation protocol. Consequently, patients with greater illness severity or ongoing organ support tended to begin mobilization later than others and remained at lower IMS levels. The association between mobility status on day 10 after ICU admission and ambulation at discharge likely reflects the overall recovery trajectory of physical function, rather than a direct causal effect of rehabilitation intensity. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as identifying a functional prognostic marker rather than establishing a causal threshold at which EMo determines final functional outcomes.

The independent ambulation rate was higher in this study than in previous studies, despite similar illness severity [14,15,19]. This could be due to the long hospitalization period and frequent rehabilitation. The reported effectiveness of outpatient rehabilitation for critically ill patients has been inconsistent [31,32]. Nevertheless, the time and frequency of walking practice affect walking independence [20]. Frequent exercise training after ICU discharge could increase the time spent walking and the rate of independent ambulation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to provide specific timing targets for EMo components (standing, marching in place, and assisted walking) to optimize ambulation outcomes in critically ill patients. These findings can inform ICU rehabilitation protocols intended to improve discharge mobility. However, this study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center study. Reliability and generalizability might be limited by institution-specific practices, and the data presented in this study might not be applicable to other facilities. However, our EMo protocol was implemented in accordance with the research of Gosselink et al. [23], and we expect the generalization bias to be small. Second, this was a retrospective cohort study. Prospective multicenter studies are required to confirm whether the day 10 after ICU admission cutoff for standing or higher mobilization levels is reproducible across different ICUs and case-mix profiles. Third, selection bias might have occurred due to the inclusion of only patients who survived to discharge. In this study, in-hospital mortalities—whose ambulation status at discharge could not be assessed—were not deemed eligible for inclusion, which might have introduced survivor bias. Patients who ultimately died in the hospital might have received similar EMo interventions in the ICU, and the relationship between their mobilization progress and ambulation states might differ from the relationships observed in this study. Therefore, the findings of this study apply only to patients who survived to discharge and should not be extrapolated to those who died during hospitalization or to the effect of EMo on in-hospital mortality. Fourth, we could not measure muscle strength and quality in detail. When discussing muscle weakness and atrophy, it is necessary to evaluate those metrics continually. Larger multicenter prospective studies are required to validate our findings. Fifth, the outcome of this study was limited to ambulation status at hospital discharge. Therefore, we were unable to assess whether the early initiation of mobilization had any sustained effects on long-term physical function, quality of life, social and community activities, or health care costs. Future prospective studies with long-term follow-up periods are needed to determine whether the short-term benefits of EMo persist beyond hospital discharge. As a consequence of those limitations, our proposed day 4 and 10 after ICU admission thresholds represent group-level probabilities and should not be used as deterministic cutoffs for individual patients. Not all patients who fail to reach these mobility levels by the suggested days will fail to regain ambulation, and rehabilitation should not be withheld or de-escalated solely based on these time points.

In conclusion, we identified time windows during which each mobilization level was typically achieved and examined how those timings were associated with ambulation status at hospital discharge in this cohort. Our findings suggest that mobility status on day 10 after ICU admission can serve as a pragmatic marker for identifying patients at risk of poor ambulation at discharge. Additional intervention strategies might be warranted for patients who still have difficulty standing by day 10 after ICU admission.

KEY MESSAGES

▪ This study describes the timing of several mobilization levels and its associations with ambulatory recovery at hospital discharge.

▪ The optimal cutoff time linking marching in place and walking with assistance to regained ambulation was day 10 after intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and the adjusted odds ratio for standing also peaked on day 10.

▪ Mobility status on day 10 after ICU admission can thus serve as a pragmatic marker for identifying patients at risk of poor ambulation at discharge, but not as a causal determinant of walking recovery.
