Restoring low-fiber diets-induced Lachnospiraceae bacterium loss partially recovers fiber digestion and immune function in mammals
Authors: Langqing Liu, Cineng Xu, Yiyi Liu, Jie Yang, Yong Ye, Zekai Yao, Danyang Lin, Haiqi Qiu, Donglin Ruan, Yibin Qiu, Shiyuan Wang, Meng Lin, Zebin Zhang, Sixiu Huang, Fanming Meng, Enqin Zheng, Gengyuan Cai, Zhenfang Wu, Jia-Jin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMammals rely on their gut microbiota to degrade cellulose, the major component of dietary fiber. Westernized populations harbor a depleted microbiome with reduced fiber-digesting capacity and impaired immune regulation due to prolonged consumption of low-fiber diets. Comparable patterns are evident in other mammals, including Western commercial pigs raised on high-energy, low-fiber diets, exhibiting reduced diversity and abundance of fiber-degrading bacteria. In contrast, semi-free-ranging Chinese indigenous pigs consuming fiber-rich diets retain a more diverse and functionally resilient microbiota, reflecting divergent trajectories of host-microbiota co-evolution. However, the specific cellulose-degrading species lost and strategies to restore these functions remain unclear in mammals. By analyzing 473 human stool metagenomes spanning non-westernized and westernized diets, together with 251 fecal 16S rRNA datasets and 95 metagenomes from Western commercial pigs, Chinese indigenous pigs, and their crossbred progeny, we identified the Lachnospiraceae bacterium as a key symbiont enriched in non-westernized guts. This bacterium possesses an extensive Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes repertoire conferring strong fiber-degrading capacity. Notably, low-fiber diets leave a genetic signature on this keystone gut symbiont, which cannot be reversed by short-term dietary interventions alone. Reintroduction of Lachnospiraceae bacterium to germ-free mice improved feed efficiency and increased acetic acid production. Intestinal transcriptomics and peripheral blood flow cytometry revealed that it activates a broad adaptive immune response, promoting CD4+ T cell accumulation, B cell activation, and anti-inflammatory cytokine induction. Reintroduction of this bacterium also alleviated dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis. These findings highlight the preclinical functional potential of this Lachnospiraceae bacterium in mitigating low-fiber diets-induced dysfunction in mammals.Keywords: Low-fiber diets, Lachnospiraceae bacterium, Fiber digestion, Immune status, Anti-inflammatory, Probiotic

Introduction

The mammalian gut inhabits trillions of microbes and has a widespread impact on host physiology, including immune status, development, and metabolism (Sommer and Bäckhed, 2013). Diet is an important driver of microbiota composition and function, thereby influencing the interaction between host and microbiota (David et al., 2014; Flint et al., 2017; Rothschild et al., 2018). Individuals consuming a westernized diet have a diet low in microbiota-accessible carbohydrates (MACs), which results in the reduced diversity of fiber-degrading bacteria and Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZymes) of the gut microbiota compared to the non-westernized populations that consume a higher quantity of dietary fiber (Groussin et al., 2021; Pasolli et al., 2019; Smits et al., 2017). CAZymes in gut microbiota are essential for the fermentation of complex dietary polysaccharides into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Cheng et al., 2024; Mukhopadhya and Louis, 2025; Zheng et al., 2025), which directly and indirectly modulate the regulation of immune response (Chen et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025). Furthermore, although the gut microbiota co-evolves with its host, the key fiber-degrading species lost under prolonged exposure to low-fiber diets remain unclear. A growing number of diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and non-communicable chronic diseases have become increasingly prevalent in westernized populations with a continuous low-MAC diet, which is linked to the loss of gut microbiota fiber degradation function and reduced production of SCFAs (Sonnenburg and Sonnenburg, 2019b; Vangay et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2018), and it is not recoverable after the reintroduction of dietary MACs (Sonnenburg et al., 2016; Sonnenburg and Sonnenburg, 2019b).

Similar patterns are observed in other mammals, such as pigs. In the context of intensive breeding and rearing practices, Western commercial pigs (WP) are typically subjected to long-term high-energy, protein-rich, low-fiber diets to maximize growth performance. This dietary strategy leads to a diminished diversity of fiber-degrading bacteria and CAZymes (Cheng et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023). These reduced taxa and CAZymes may be inefficiently transmitted to subsequent generations (Sonnenburg et al., 2016). Consequently, under the process of microbiota co-evolves with the host, WP-associated microbial lineages have been passed down through generations. The gut microbial compositions and functions of WP have undergone tremendous changes, resulting in a noticeable decline in their ability to digest fiber (Niu et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022). In contrast, Chinese indigenous pigs (CIP) are semi-free ranging and consume a higher fiber diet, contributing to their retention of greater microbiota-mediated fiber digestion capacity and disease resistance during the host-microbiota co-evolution process (Fairbairn et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2022). The substantial divergence in the gut microbial composition and functions between WP and CIP, following a lengthy period of divergence, provides a valuable biorepository for unraveling the mechanisms underlying microbiota's roles in fiber digestion and developing strategies to enhance disease resistance in humans and pigs.

Administration of probiotic species in the gut is a powerful approach for preventing and treating human and animal diseases, such as obesity, diabetes, and IBD, which has been well documented. Among others, Lactobacillus spp. Plays protective roles against inflammatory diseases by reducing the proinflammatory cytokine and inducing production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, which has been well demonstrated in several mouse experiments involving oral administration (Chen et al., 2015; Park et al., 2018). In addition, Bifidobacterium spp. is widely used as a probiotic to alleviate various diseases, such as IBD and allergies, by altering the gut microbiota composition and increasing the production of SCFAs, thereby regulating the intestinal immune homeostasis (Fu et al., 2017; Srutkova et al., 2015). Thus, the modulation of gut microbes by probiotic species is an important strategy to recover the digestive capacity of dietary fiber and improve host health. However, taxonomic and strain-level resolution remains crucial for understanding the differential effects of probiotic species. Within functionally diverse bacterial families, certain species or strains can exert beneficial effects, for example, by producing butyrate, while others may generate metabolites that exacerbate disease under specific dietary conditions (Abdugheni et al., 2022; Vacca et al., 2020). Moreover, the mechanisms underlying these differential effects are incompletely elucidated. As such, establishing clear causal links among specific species or strains, their metabolites, and corresponding host effects remains a key challenge in probiotic research.

The progressive loss of fiber-degrading microbial lineages under sustained low-fiber diets may result in long-term functional deficits across mammalian hosts (Sonnenburg et al., 2016). Conventional probiotic approaches are unlikely to fully compensate for the loss of specialized keystone taxa required for efficient polysaccharide fermentation and SCFA production. Without identifying these missing organisms, intervention strategies may fail to restore critical microbiome-mediated metabolic functions, leaving both westernized human populations and commercial pig breeds increasingly vulnerable to inflammatory and metabolic disorders. To address this critical gap, we conducted a systematic comparative analysis across three monogastric omnivores (humans, pigs, and mice) to identify microbial taxa associated with fiber degradation and host metabolic health. Through this approach, we identified a Lachnospiraceae bacterium that is enriched in non-westernized human populations and Chinese indigenous pigs and is associated with increased acetic acid production. We further evaluated its functional role using germ-free mouse and colitis mouse models. This research seeks to identify and functionally characterize key fiber-degrading microbial taxa that may help restore microbiome-mediated metabolic and immunological functions under low-fiber dietary conditions.

Materials and methods

Description of human and pig samples

For the human cohort, The stool metagenomic datasets of healthy individuals were used, totaling 473 metagenomes, which include 8 populations (Table S1): 112 from Madagascan (Pasolli et al., 2019), 23 from Peruvian (Obregon-Tito et al., 2015), 67 from Tazanians (Rampelli et al., 2015; Smits et al., 2017), 11 from Italians (Rampelli et al., 2015), 57 from American (Hannigan et al., 2018; Obregon-Tito et al., 2015), 70 from Swedish (Bengtsson-Palme et al., 2015), 61 from Australian (Feng et al., 2015), and 72 samples from Chinese (Wen et al., 2017). The non-westernized population includes Peruvian, Tanzanian, and Madagascan, who originate from hunter-gatherer or rural communities with non-Western diets (high fiber diet), mainly relying on self-grown rice and wild plants and meats. Others were westernized populations with the consumption of industrialized Western diets (high fat and protein, but low fiber diet). All human data used in this study were obtained from publicly available repositories. The original studies were conducted in accordance with approved ethical standards, and no additional ethical approval was required for this secondary analysis.

For the discovery cohort, a total of 251 porcine stool samples from 2 geographical environments with 4 different feed types were used (Table S2–S3). Overall, the samples include 5 breeds: Dahuabai (26 samples), Lantang (87 samples), Duroc (36 samples), Landrace (52 samples), and Large White (50 samples). All of 251 pigs were fed a similar diet with a low fiber content (2.7∼3.6% fiber) for 130 days after weanling (d30). At 160 days of age, the stool samples of all pigs were collected for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. In addition, 45 (Dahuabai, n = 5; Lantang, n = 10; Duroc, n = 15; Landrace, n = 10; Large White, n = 5) porcine stool samples were randomly selected for shotgun metagenomic sequencing.

For the validation cohort, 50 pigs enrolled in two distinct breeds (25 Liang Guang Small-spotted pigs (LGSS) and 25 LGSS × Duroc crossbred pigs), which were bred in the same pen and fed the same diet with a relatively high fiber content (11% fiber) for 130 days after weanling (d30), and each pig was separated by steel bars. All of them can be available the same amount and type of feed each day. At the end of the experiment, we collected stool and serum samples along with back fat measurement by an Aloka 500 V SSD B ultrasound (Coromertics Medical Systems, USA). All stool samples were dipped in liquid nitrogen, which was then transported and stored at −80 °C freezer until DNA was extracted.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and data analysis

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from the stool samples of the discovery cohort using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (DP712, TIANGEN, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by the forward primer 314 F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and the reverse primer 806 R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). For each sample, the 16S rRNA gene was amplified under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min followed by 34 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 57 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were quantified by gel electrophoresis, pooled, and purified for reactions. Library preparation was performed using TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Thermo Scientific, Schwerte, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Finally, sequencing was performed using the Illumina PE250 platform at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) to generate raw fastq reads.

The raw fastq reads were imported into the Qiime2 platform (available at http://qiime.org/) after removing the barcode and primer sequence. For each dataset, demultiplexed sequencing reads were denoised using a divisive amplicon denoising algorithm (DADA2) pipeline including filtering and trimming of the reads to generate Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Callahan et al., 2016). Analysis was conducted on samples rarefied to 24,000 reads. Only ASV with an average relative abundance greater than 0.01% and detected in at least 50% of samples were included in the downstream analysis. Rarefied data were used for α-diversity measures using the Chao1 (richness) and Shannon indices.

Metagenomic sequencing, quality control, and assembly

Fecal DNA was extracted at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd using a TIANGEN kit according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The resulting concentrations and the quality of bacterial DNA were measured by Qubit and agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA libraries were constructed following the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Then, the library preparations were paired-end sequenced by Illumina 150 bp PE strategy.

Raw fastq reads were quality-controlled, trimmed for adaptors and reads aligned to the pig genome (Sus scrofa 11.1, available at https://www.ensembl.org/biomart) were filtered out using the KneadData toolkit (v0.5.1) (McIver et al., 2018). The remaining high-quality reads were then individually assembled with MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) (Li et al., 2015) with the parameters -k 21, 29, 39, 59, 79, 99, 119, and 141. To make full use of metagenomic data, the clean data for each sample were aligned to the assembled contigs by Bowtie 2 (v2.4.4) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) and acquired the unassembled reads, which were then pooled and co-assembled by MEGAHIT. All the assembled contigs were used for further analysis.

Gene catalog construction, quantification of taxa, and functional profiling in metagenomic data

Genes were predicted in assembled contigs by Metagenemark (Zhu et al., 2010) with default parameters. Then, a non-redundant gene catalog was constructed by removing the redundant sequences (95% sequence identity, 90% alignment coverage) using CD-HIT (v4.8.1) (Li and Godzik, 2006). Taxonomic classification of the non-redundant genes was acquired by aligning them against the Non-Redundant Protein Sequence Database (NR database) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (available from ftp://ftp.ncbi.nih.gov/blast/db) with DIAMOND alignment algorithm (Buchfink et al., 2015) (the top result with e-value < 1e−5 was selected). The abundance of all bacterial species was assessed from a non-redundant nucleotide fastq file using Salmon software (v1.5.1) (Patro et al., 2017).

The functional profiles of all predicted protein sequences were obtained by annotating against the eggNOG (Evolutionary Genealogy of Genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups, v5.0) protein databases (Huerta-Cepas et al., 2019) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) by the DIAMOND alignment algorithm (the top result with e-value < 1e-5 was selected). Carbohydrates Active Enzymes (CAZy) were identified using HMMER (v3.1b2) (Pattabiraman and Warnow, 2021) assigned genes to the CAZy database, with an e-value < 1e-5 cutoff. The phylogenetic origins of CAZymes-encoding genes were determined using MEGAN (Huson et al., 2007).

Germ-free (GF) mouse intervention study with Lachnospiraceae bacterium

A total of 40 C57BL/6J GF mice (6-week-old) were randomly assigned to four experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence (5 males and 5 females were kept separately for each group) to minimize selection bias and housed in cages under sterile conditions, feed ad libitum, and had free access to water. After 2-week adaptation, mice in the Group1 (G1) received a high-fiber diet (AIN-76A Rodent Diet with 35% Cellulose), while mice in the Group2 (G2) received a low-fiber diet (AIN-76A Rodent Diet with 5% Cellulose) with Lachnospiraceae bacterium administered by gavage. Mice in the Group 3 (G3) and Group 4 (G4) received a high-fiber diet (G3) or a low-fiber diet (G4) with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) gavage were used as the control. While strain-level resolution is not achievable from our metagenomic datasets, the Lachnospiraceae bacterium identified in human and pig samples represents a taxonomically related lineage within the same species. Given that members of the Lachnospiraceae bacterium species are known to share conserved functional capacities, particularly in fiber fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production, DSM 24404 was selected as a representative, culturable strain to experimentally test the functional role suggested by our metagenomic analyses. DSM 24404 is initially isolated from a clinical sample in the form of a human gut biopsy and taxonomically classified as a member of the Lachnospiraceae bacterium species and purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, Virginia, USA). It was cultured in Columbia Blood Medium (DSMZ Medium 693) at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. For the following four weeks, mice were gavaged three times a week with 300 μL of either Lachnospiraceae bacterium (1 × 107 CFU/day, 1% glycerol in PBS) or sham-gavaged (1% glycerol in PBS). During the intervention study, body weight and feed intake were recorded for each day. And the fecal samples were collected before the experiment and after each gavage for quantitative PCR (qPCR) to quantify the abundances of Lachnospiraceae bacterium in treated mice to confirm colonization. After 4 weeks of intervention, all mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Venous blood was taken from the inner canthus of each mouse for flow cytometry and metabolomic analysis. Then, the gastrointestinal tracts were quickly removed from the body. The colon tissues were dissected by opening longitudinally along the mesenteric border, and its contents were washed with saline to immediately preserve in buffered formalin for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and stored at −80 °C for RNA-sequencing. Histological scoring was performed in a blinded manner, with the investigator unaware of group allocation during analysis. Data from male and female mice were pooled for analysis because each experimental group was designed with an equal male-to-female ratio, thereby minimizing potential sex bias. In addition, no obvious sex-dependent differences were observed in the measured phenotypes.

Confirmation of colonization of Lachnospiraceae bacterium by quantitative qPCR

Mouse fecal bacterial DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) as described above. DNA concentration and integrity were assessed by a Nanodrop and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Confirmation of colonization was achieved with Lachnospiraceae genome specific primers (F: 5′-ACC GCA TAA GCG CAC AGC-3′, R: 5′-GGT TAC CTT GTT ACG ACT T-3′) and Universal 16S Primers (F: 5′-ACT CCT ACG GGA GGC AGC AGT-3′, R: 5′-ATT ACC GCG GCT GCT GGC-3′) that were previously published (Reeves et al., 2012). Quantitative PCR analyses were performed using the SYBR Green qPCR Master mix (Thermo Scientific) following previously established protocols for bacterial quantification in gnotobiotic mouse fecal samples (Gomes-Neto et al., 2017).

Targeted SCFAs of serum quantitation by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

Serum samples of pigs and GF mice were centrifuged from peripheral blood and stored at −80 °C until analysis. For SCFAs extraction in targeted metabolomics detection, 50 μL of previously thawed serum and 100 μL of phosphoric acid (0.5% v/v) solution were added to a 1.5 mL EP tube and vortexed for 3 min. Then, 150 μL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) containing a mixture of internal standards was added. The mixture consisted of D4-acetic acid (DLM-12-10, Nanjing Haolv), D5-propionic acid (HIB183, Nanjing Haolv), D7-butyric acid (HIB209, Nanjing Haolv), 2-methylvaleric acid (C15147200, Dr. Ehrenstorfer), and D15-2-ethylcaproic acid (IR-21448-500mg, isoreag). The sample was then vortexed for 3 min and ultrasonicated for 5 min. After that, the mixture was centrifuged at 12000 r/min for 10 min at a temperature of 4 °C. The supernatant was collected. Finally, GC-MS/MS analysis of SCFAs was performed with an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with a 7000D mass spectrometer with a DB-FFAP column (Agilent Technologies Santa Clara, CA). Injection was performed in splitless mode with helium as carrier gas. The oven temperature program, injector temperature, and mass spectrometric conditions were set as described previously (Bianchi et al., 2011). Quantification was achieved using calibration curves constructed with authentic standards (purity ≥99%). Method validation parameters including linearity (R2 > 0.99), recovery, precision, limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ) were consistent with established guidelines (Bianchi et al., 2011).

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of GF mice isolated from blood were first resuspended. Flow cytometry was performed as previously described (Krishnarajah et al., 2022). Specifically, the surface staining was performed with anti-mouse monoclonal antibodies: FITC anti-mouse CD45, APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD3, 7AAD, PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD4, and APC anti-mouse CD19. The live CD3+/CD4+/CD8- T cells were subsequently analyzed by a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) Calibur (BD Biosciences, Allschwil, Switzerland). The gating strategy of the representative flow cytometry for the CD4+ and CD8+ live cell populations is shown in Fig. S1.

Dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced colitis and intervention by Lachnospiraceae bacterium

Twenty-four 6-week-old SPF C57BL/6 mice were selected for this study. After a two-week period of adaptation feeding, mice were randomly divided into three distinct groups (n = 8 mice/group): (i) the DSS with PBS treatment group; (ii) the DSS with Lachnospiraceae treatment group; and (iii) the control group. To ensure the colonization of the Lachnospiraceae, a 6-day bacterial inoculation process was carried out, while the other two groups were given water and PBS. Subsequently, colitis was induced by administering a 3.5% (w/v) solution of DSS (Teixeira et al., 2019) with a molecular weight ranging from 36 to 50 kDa (obtained from Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai, China) was administered freely in their drinking water for a total of 9 days. The control group had unrestricted access to distilled water during this period. To monitor the progress of the mice, the body weights and Disease Activity Index (DAI) were recorded daily (Ho et al., 2022). At the end of intervention, the mice were sacrificed. Colon tissues were acquired quickly and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Additionally, a portion of the colon tissues was fixed in paraformaldehyde for histological assessments.

Colon tissue histology

The colon tissues of GF and DSS-induced mice were dissected by opening longitudinally along the mesenteric border, and any luminal contents were thoroughly rinsed three times with 5 mL of isotonic PBS. For the histological analysis of the colon tissues from GF mice, the colon segments were initially fixed in buffered formalin for a 24-h period. Following fixation, they underwent a process of dehydration and were subsequently embedded in paraffin. These paraffin-embedded tissues were then meticulously processed and sectioned, after which they were subjected to staining with H&E and perform the histological scoring using the method described by (Zhang et al., 2021).

Analysis of cytokines in colon tissue by quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was meticulously extracted from colonic tissue using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). Subsequently, a reverse transcription reaction was performed utilizing the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, NJ, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR master mix (Vazyme Biotech, Q711) in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions on a QuantStudio 6 Flex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To determine relative expression levels, the 2-ΔΔCt method was applied, and the data was normalized to the β-actin gene. The specific primer sequences utilized in the qRT-PCR are provided in Table S4.

Analysis of cytokines in colon tissue by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The colon tissues were homogenized in PBS with 0.5% v/v Tween 20 containing a protease inhibitors cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) at a ratio of 50 mg in 500 ml buffer and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatants were collected for quantification of multiple inflammatory cytokines using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) (BMS607-3, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Interleukin-10 (IL-10) (BMS614, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (88-7314-88, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RNA sequencing and analysis

The colonic tissues of mice from high-fiber diet (HFD) + Lachnospiraceae bacterium and HFD + PBS group were used for RNA sequencing analysis. Total RNA was extracted from colonic samples by Trizol (ThermoFisher, USA) following the manufacturer's manuals. The RNA integrity and concentration were assessed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and a Nanodrop-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). The paired-end libraries were prepared by Illumina TruSeq mRNA Library Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego) and were sequenced following the standard procedure on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, and paired end reads were generated. Quality trimming was performed to remove sequence adaptors and low-quality bases using Trimmomatic to generate clean data. Then, high-quality read sequences were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) by HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2019) to generate SAM files. SAMtools was used for sorting and indexing the aligned bam files. Finally, the gene abundance tables were generated by StringTie (v1.3.4) (Pertea et al., 2015). Gene counts were normalized using TPM (Transcripts Per Million). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014). Pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (v3.14) R package (Yu et al., 2012).

Statistical analysis

The “DESeq2” (v1.28.1) R package (Love et al., 2014) was used for the differential abundance analyses based on relative abundances and to calculate the Fold change for each bacterial family. PCoA was conducted by the “vegan” (v2.5.7) and “ape” (v5.5) packages (Paradis et al., 2004) in R based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities. Analysis of alpha diversity was performed by “vegan” package quantified as observed CAzymes-encoding genes and Shannon index. Random Forest analyses were performed with the R package “randomForest” (v4.6) using 5-fold cross-validation. Microbiome Multivariable Associations with Linear Models (MaAsLin 2) (Mallick et al., 2021) was used to associate breeds to specific bacterial species excluding the influence of diet and environmental conditions. The identification of differentially abundant bacterial species between two groups was performed by Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) coupled with the effect size (LEfSe) algorithm (Segata et al., 2011). Statistical significance among multiple groups was determined through the Wilcoxon rank sum test. All statistical figures were visualized in R (v.4.0.3) using the “ggplot2” package (v3.3.4).

Results

Exposure to low-fiber diets leads to the loss of Lachnospiraceae bacterium in mammalian guts

Non-westernized groups (n = 202) consumed high-fiber rural diets, whereas westernized groups (n = 271) consumed industrialized Western diets rich in fat and protein but low in fiber (Table S1). After controlling for the effect of country using MaAslin analysis, we performed LEfSe algorithm in combination with multivariate analysis to identify specific bacterial species characteristic of the non-westernized and westernized populations. The bacterial species with LDA scores (log10) > 2 and p < 0.001 were identified as differentially represented between the non-westernized and westernized populations. Notably, the Lachnospiraceae bacterium (LDA scores = 3.03, p = 1.55E-17; Fig. 1A; Table S5) was identified as a significantly abundant species in the non-westernized group. In westernized populations, the low relative abundant of Lachnospiraceae bacterium may be due to undergoing prolonged exposure to low-fiber diets.

To explore whether prolonged exposure to low-fiber diets will leave a persistent host-associated or genetic signature on this keystone gut symbiont, we collected 251 fecal samples from WP and CIP that were raised under comparable housing conditions and fed diets with similarly low fiber content (2.7∼3.6% fiber) (Fig. 1B; Tables S2 and S3). Fecal microbial composition was obtained from 16S rRNA gene sequencing, yielding 1906 ASVs after rarefaction to 24,000 reads per sample. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) with PERMANOVA revealed significant differences in microbiome composition, with breed, diet, and geographical environment explaining 28.6%, 33.1%, and 26.9% of variance, respectively (Fig. 1C). Taxonomic annotation showed that the top 10 bacterial families accounted for 82.54% of total community composition (Table S6). Notably, Lachnospiraceae family was more abundant in CIP than WP (Fig. 1D). To control confounding factors, we corrected the effect of diet and environment using MaAsLin 2. We identified ASVs contributing most to group separation, among which five of the top ten predictors were classified within the Lachnospiraceae family (Fig. 1E; Table S7). Differential analysis further identified 10 bacterial families with different abundance between CIP and WP, including significant enrichment of Lachnospiraceae family in CIP (Fig. 1F).

To investigate Lachnospiraceae and their functional differences between CIP and WP at species level, we performed shotgun metagenomic sequencing of 45 fecal samples randomly selected from 251 individuals. The PCoA based on the metagenomic data showed consistent results with the 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (Fig. S2). Differential abundance of microbes between CIP and WP were identified with LEfSe (LDA score >2, p < 0.001) and confirmed by MaAsLin. In total, 14 species showed significant differences between the two groups (Table S8). Among which, we demonstrated Lachnospiraceae bacterium in Lachnospiraceae family was the most significant species associated with CIP (Fig. 2A), which further refined taxonomic resolution compared to the 16S amplicon data. Functional potential of the gut microbiota derived from metagenomic data was further characterized using KEGG pathway analysis. In total, 51,332 KEGG orthologs (KOs) were assigned to 54 pathways. Out of which, 16 pathways were different between breeds (LDA score >3, p < 0.001, (Table S9). The most significant pathway was ‘Carbohydrate metabolism’ and was enriched in CIP, indicating superior fiber digestion capacity compared to WP (Fig. S3).

All the enzymes were annotated as fiber-targeting enzymes (shown at the top of each bar) in CIP group.

To further dissect the genetic basis of this fiber digestion capacity, we subsequently catalogued genes encoding CAZymes. We identified 572 degradative CAZymes grouped into 6 CAZy families, such as 273 glycoside hydrolases (GH), 85 polysaccharide lyases (PL), and 16 carbohydrate esterases (CE). LEfSe analysis identified 115 CAZymes over-represented in CIP (66.1 % GH, 6.96 % CE, 8.70 % PL), while 44 over-represented in WP (Fig. 2B; Table S10). The alpha-diversity of CAZyme genes was significantly higher in CIP (Fig. 2C; p = 8.1E-10). All of the top CAZymes in CIP (LDA scores >3 and foldchange of TPM (CIP/WP) > 2) belong to families that target dietary fiber polysaccharides, whereas the top CAZymes in WP were lysozyme. We found that Lachnospiraceae bacterium alone contributed around 10% of the CAZymes specific for polysaccharides (Fig. 2D). Despite short-term exposure to a low-fiber diet, this observation suggests that CIP harbored gut microbiota with a strong fiber-degrading capacity, in which the Lachnospiraceae bacterium plays a key role.

An independent validation cohort confirmed the vital role of Lachnospiraceae bacterium in fiber digestion

In order to validate the robustness of the finding from the discovery cohort of pigs, we further perform the analysis on gut microbiota in an independent validation cohort. To control for confounding effects of the environment (mostly geographical dispersals) and diet on gut microbiota, we selected 25 LGSS pigs (Chinese indigenous pigs) and 25 LGSS × Duroc (Western commercial pigs) crossbred pigs, which were housed in the same pen and fed the same diet with relatively high fiber content (11% fiber) for 130 days after weanling (30 days after birth) (Fig. 3A). We confirmed the distinct microbiome compositions between LGSS and LGSS × Duroc samples by a clear separation in PCoA analysis results (Fig. 3B). Then, we applied the LEfSe analysis to identify the differential bacterial species between the two groups (LDA scores >2, Fig. 3C; Table S11). LGSS pigs have a higher abundance of the Lachnospiraceae bacterium relative to LGSS × Duroc pigs, which is consistent with the discovery cohort where this species was more abundant in CIP group. Additionally, three species of the genus Prevotella (Prevotella_sp.P2-180, Prevotella_sp.P5-92, Prevotella copri) was the most significantly enriched species in LGSS pigs. We next examined the phenotypic difference between groups and found that LGSS pigs exhibited higher backfat thickness (Fig. S4; Table S12).

Functional profiling with KEGG and the CAZy database showed that KOs related to “carbohydrate metabolism” were significantly enriched in LGSS pigs (Fig. 3D; Table S13). We further compared the significantly differential CAZymes identified in each group across discovery and validation pig cohorts. Venn diagram highlights the microbiome overlap and similarity between CIP group (discovery cohort) and LGSS (validation cohort), as well as WP group (discovery cohort) and LGSS × Duroc (validation cohort) (Fig. 3E). CIP and LGSS pigs shared 45 CAZymes that showed significant differences in abundance, out of the 50 CAZymes detected in LGSS (Table S14). We further measured serum SCFAs levels in the validation cohort and found significantly higher amounts of acetic acid, butyric acid, propionic acid, isobutyric acid, and caproic acid in LGSS pigs (p < 0.01, Wilxcon test; Fig. 3F; Table S15). Collectively, these findings show that prolonged low-fiber diets leave a persistent host-associated signature that diminishes the abundance of the Lachnospiraceae bacterium and weakens fiber-degrading capacity, an effect that short-term dietary interventions cannot readily reverse.

Germ-free mice gavage experiment confirmed the causal role of Lachnospiraceae bacterium in fiber figestion and immune homeostasis

To further evaluate the causal relationship between the Lachnospiraceae bacterium and dietary fiber degradation, as well as its potential impact on immune status, we conducted a gavage experiment in which live Lachnospiraceae bacterium was administered to GF mice (Fig. 4A). A total of 40 animals were assigned to 4 groups (n = 10 each) and fed different combinations of feed and Lachnospiraceae bacterium: 35% fiber diet plus Lachnospiraceae bacterium (abbreviated to G1: HFD + Lachnospiraceae), 5% fiber diet plus Lachnospiraceae bacterium (G2: LFD + Lachnospiraceae), 35% fiber diet plus PBS (G3: HFD + PBS), and 5% fiber diet plus PBS (G4: LFD + PBS) group. Successful colonization of Lachnospiraceae bacterium in G1 and G2 groups was confirmed by qPCR analysis on fecal DNA (Fig. S5).

Phenotypic measurements revealed that mice colonized with the Lachnospiraceae bacterium (G1) showed no significant differences in body weight or fat accumulation, but exhibited a significantly lower average daily feed intake compared with the GF control (G3) (Fig. 4B; Table S16), which indicates that Lachnospiraceae bacterium colonization improved the host feed efficiency. The quantitation analysis of serum SCFAs showed that mice colonized with the Lachnospiraceae bacterium had higher levels of acetic acid compared to GF controls (Fig. 4C; Fig. S6; Table S17). We next examined colonic histology and observed that Lachnospiraceae bacterium-colonized mice exhibited an increased crypt depth (Fig. 4D and E). Collectively, the Lachnospiraceae bacterium ferments dietary fiber to SCFAs, which simultaneously strengthens the gut barrier.

To further investigate how Lachnospiraceae bacterium modulates the host gene expression, we performed RNA-seq on colonic tissues of mice in HFD + Lachnospiraceae and HFD + PBS groups. Colonization with Lachnospiraceae bacterium resulted in the up-regulation of 386 genes and down-regulation of 329 genes (log2|FC| > 1 and FDR <0.05; Fig. 5A; Table S18). Gene ontology (GO) analyses of DEG showed that that 43 out of 47 significantly enriched biological pathways were immune-related (p < 0.05; Fig. 5B; Table S19), including B cell activation, immunoglobulin mediated immune response, complement activation, IL-10 production, and response to IFN-β. Notably, 39 of the top 40 upregulated genes in Lachnospiraceae bacterium-colonized mice were immunoglobulin (Ig) genes, including heavy (IGH), kappa (IGK), and lambda (IGL) variables (Fig. 5A; Table S18). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in peripheral CD4+ T cells in Lachnospiraceae bacterium-colonized mice, while CD8+ T cell counts remained unchanged compared to the HFD+PBS group (Fig. 5C and D).

Expression of anti-inflammatory genes including Il-10, Irf4, Prg2, Cd28, and Ido1 was also significantly increased in colon tissues of Lachnospiraceae bacterium-gavaged mice. These genes were enriched in GO: 0032613 (Interleukin-10 production), and GO: 0035456 (Response to interferon-beta). Given that Interleukin-10 and interferon-beta are key cytokines known to suppress the progression of intestinal inflammation, these findings suggest that the Lachnospiraceae bacterium confers protection against inflammation by promoting the production of these anti-inflammatory cytokines. Taken together, beyond enhancing fiber digestion, the Lachnospiraceae bacterium plays a crucial role in shaping host immune status and promoting anti-inflammatory responses.

Lachnospiraceae bacterium alleviates Host's colitis

To investigate the causal relationship between the Lachnospiraceae bacterium and its role in the host's anti-inflammatory processes, we administrated the Lachnospiraceae bacterium to specific pathogen free (SPF) mice and induced colitis by treating them with DSS (Fig. 6A). After administration of 3.5% DSS in the drinking water for 9 days, the DSS-induced mice showed severe weight loss and colonic length shorten compared to the control group (Fig. 6B–D), indicating severe inflammation. Importantly, we observed a substantial alleviation of weight loss and colonic length shorten in the DSS + Lachnospiraceae group compared to the DSS + PBS group (Fig. 6B–D). To gauge the severity of colitis in the DSS-induced mice, we employed the DAI scores, a widely accepted parameter that takes into account factors such as body weight loss, colon length reduction, rectal bleeding, and diarrhea. The evaluation of DAI scores showed that the administration of Lachnospiraceae bacterium significantly reduced DAI scores compared to the DSS + PBS group (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, we performed histological scoring of H&E stained colonic tissues to further validate the anti-inflammatory effects of Lachnospiraceae bacterium. This analysis revealed less severe histological injuries in the DSS + Lachnospiraceae group, including reduced loss of goblet cells and crypts, diminished mucosal erosion, and decreased inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 6F and G).

To investigate the mechanism by which the Lachnospiraceae bacterium ameliorates colitis, we evaluated the levels of key inflammatory cytokines in colonic tissues using qRT-PCR and ELISA. The results showed that gavage with the Lachnospiraceae bacterium increased the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and reduced the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α in colitic mice (Fig. 6H and I). Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms through which this bacterium promotes anti-inflammatory cytokine production require further investigation.

Discussion

Long-term deprivation of MACs in Westernized populations has been linked to the loss of numerous fiber-degrading microbial species and impaired immune regulation. These changes are thought to contribute to the increasing incidence of inflammation-associated diseases, including diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory disorders (Sonnenburg and Sonnenburg, 2019a). Similar patterns are observed in other mammals, including Western commercial pigs, which are typically raised on high-energy, protein-rich, low-fiber diets. Such dietary regimes are associated with reduced diversity and abundance of fiber-degrading bacteria. In contrast, Chinese indigenous pigs, which are often semi-free-ranging and consume fiber-rich diets, harbor a more diverse gut microbiota with greater fiber-degrading capacity and enhanced disease resilience. This divergence likely reflects distinct trajectories of host-microbiota co-evolution. Importantly, the anatomical and physiological similarities between porcine and human gastrointestinal tracts, together with the substantial overlap in gut microbiota composition and function (∼96%), support the use of pigs as a relevant translational model for studying host-microbiota interactions (Xiao et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2013). Unlike human studies, which are often confounded by genetic diversity and environmental variation (Forslund et al., 2015; Miyoshi et al., 2020), leveraging a cross-species framework, we consistently identified a higher abundance of Lachnospiraceae bacterium in non-Westernized human populations (Pasolli et al., 2019) and in Chinese indigenous pigs. Functional analyses further suggest that this bacterium is associated with fiber degradation and modulation of immune responses. Notably, our findings indicate that prolonged exposure to low-fiber diets leaves a persistent host-associated signature, characterized by a reduced abundance of Lachnospiraceae bacterium, which is not readily reversed by short-term dietary interventions. Whether targeted reconstitution of Lachnospiraceae bacterium can reverse these long-term diet-induced impairments in fiber degradation and immune homeostasis remains unclear.

As a core gut bacterial family, averaging 22.4-23.4% abundance in humans (Sorbara et al., 2020) and 10.77% in pigs, Lachnospiraceae encompasses 318 classified species and numerous unclassified strains (Sayers et al., 2025). Members of the Lachnospiraceae family are well studied for encoding CAZymes that hydrolyze dietary polysaccharides into SCFAs such as acetate and propionate, which accumulate in the colon and peripheral circulation (O. Sheridan et al., 2016). Our data confirmed higher microbial CAZyme-encoding genes and elevated SCFA levels in CIPs. SCFAs act as key immune regulators by influencing Treg cells, epithelial cells, and dendritic cells (Smith et al., 2013), thereby supporting gut barrier integrity and attenuating inflammation (Makki et al., 2018). Crypt depth reflects mucosal thickness, and goblet cell activity supports continuous mucus layer production (Johansson et al., 2013; Wrzosek et al., 2013), which serves as a critical barrier preventing microbial translocation and reducing inflammation. Following the colonization of GF mice with Lachnospiraceae bacterium, we observed a suite of metabolic improvements, including enhanced feed efficiency, reduced intake, and increased serum acetic acid, indicative of more efficient fiber degradation. Acetic acid is the end product of colonic fermentation of fiber and the most abundant SCFAs in the circulation, which can provide energy for the host and travel across the blood-brain barrier into the central nervous system to suppress appetite (Frost et al., 2014). Mechanistically, SCFAs act via G-protein-coupled receptors GPR43 (also known as FFAR2) on enteroendocrine cells to promote the release of satiety hormones glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY) (Brooks et al., 2017). In parallel, acetate activates GPR43 on vagal afferent neurons, transmitting peripheral satiety signals directly to the brainstem and hypothalamus (Goswami et al., 2018). Furthermore, acetate crosses the blood-brain barrier and directly modulates hypothalamic orexin neurons via GPR43 activation to suppress appetite (Forte et al., 2024). Therefore, the reduction of feed intake in Lachnospiraceae-colonized mice can be explained by an increase in circulation acetic acid, which provides additional energy on the one hand and suppresses appetite on the other.

SCFAs are known to help maintain gut barrier homeostasis and reduce inflammation by modulating pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Colon transcriptomics and flow cytometry further revealed upregulation of immunoglobulin genes and activation of immune pathways involving B cell activation, complement, IL-10 production, and response to IFN-β. V(D)J recombination during B cell development drives immunoglobulin diversity, underpinning adaptive immunity (Victora and Nussenzweig, 2022). The activation of B cells positively regulates adaptive and autoimmune immune responses through optimal CD4+ T cell activation and cytokines production such as IL-10, and IFN-β (LeBien and Tedder, 2008). We also found increased peripheral CD4+ T cells, aligning with studies linking high fiber intake or Lachnospiraceae abundance to CD4+ T cell expansion (Sbierski-Kind et al., 2022). Additionally, anti-inflammatory genes (e.g., Il-10, Irf4, Prg2, Cd28, and Ido1) were upregulated and enriched in IL-10 and IFN-β pathways, both critical for suppressing intestinal inflammation (Chen et al., 2019; Kole et al., 2013). These immunomodulatory effects including B cell activation, CD4+ T cell accumulation, and anti-inflammatory cytokine induction are likely mediated by microbial-derived metabolites, particularly SCFAs such as acetate, which we found significantly elevated following Lachnospiraceae colonization. Previous studies have reported that SCFAs engage G-protein-coupled receptors GPR43 and promote sustained mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling, leading to expression of Blimp-1, a transcriptional repressor that drives plasma cell differentiation and IL-10 production in regulatory B cells (Luu et al., 2025). For CD4+ T cells, direct SCFA-GPR43 signaling has been shown to blunt histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity, downregulate pro-inflammatory pathways (NF-κB), and modulate T cell differentiation (Han et al., 2024). In addition, SCFAs inhibit histone deacetylases, promoting chromatin remodeling that facilitates IL-10 transcription and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (Mann et al., 2024). Together, these previously reported mechanisms indicate that SCFAs can regulate B cell metabolic reprogramming and antibody production, promote regulatory T cell differentiation, and induce IL-10 production, which may partly explain the enhanced immune status of Chinese indigenous pigs versus western pigs. While these pathways were not directly tested in the present study, they provide a plausible framework for interpreting our findings.

Comparing our findings with previous mono-colonization studies, we note that Lachnospiraceae bacterium induces immune responses overlapping with those of Bacteroides fragilis, a well-studied regulator of innate and adaptive immunity. Like B. fragilis, which restores CD4+ T cell levels in GF mice and induces IL-10 and IFN-β to prevent intestinal inflammation and viral infection (Round and Mazmanian, 2010; Stefan et al., 2020), the Lachnospiraceae bacterium activates similar immunomodulatory pathways. Notably, the metabolic outcomes of Lachnospiraceae colonization may vary depending on the type of dietary fiber consumed. In our study, GF mice were mono-colonized with a single Lachnospiraceae bacterium and administered cellulose, an insoluble fiber. Although cellulose is generally considered poorly fermentable, its presence was associated with significantly increased levels of SCFAs, particularly acetate, indicating that it can support microbial metabolic activity under defined gnotobiotic conditions. Previous studies have shown that highly fermentable fibers, such as konjac flour or pectin, preferentially enrich Lachnospiraceae and promote butyrate production through cross-feeding interactions within complex microbial communities, thereby enhancing gut barrier function and reducing inflammation (Huang et al., 2023; Shetty et al., 2022). In contrast, other types of insoluble fibers, such as lignin and chitin, have been reported to potentially modulate luminal retention time and substrate accessibility, which may facilitate Lachnospiraceae expansion and SCFA production (Baky et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025; Teullet et al., 2023). Taken together, these observations suggest that different types of dietary fibers, particularly soluble versus insoluble fibers, may exert different synergistic effects with Lachnospiraceae. Direct comparative studies on defined fiber types under controlled gnotobiotic conditions will be required to elucidate the mechanisms of substrate-driven synergistic effects involving Lachnospiraceae.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals that the Lachnospiraceae bacterium is a key fiber-degrading species lost under prolonged exposure to low-fiber diets. We further show that prolonged low-fiber diets leave a persistent host-associated signature that reduces its abundance, which short-term dietary interventions cannot readily reverse. Gavage experiments support a role for this bacterium in promoting fiber degradation, increasing SCFA levels, and modulating immune-related responses. Together, these findings highlight the preclinical functional potential of this bacterium as a candidate probiotic for restoring microbiota-associated functions in westernized populations under low-fiber dietary conditions.
