A pipeline for developing AI-driven models to predict molecular initiating events: a case study on neural tube defects
Authors: Job H Berkhout, Merel Florian, Domenico Gadaleta, Aldert H Piersma, Harm J Heusinkveld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs) describe the sequence of molecular and cellular events that lead to toxicity. Each pathway begins with a Molecular Initiating Event (MIE) and ends in an Adverse Outcome. Early identification of chemical activity on MIE-relevant protein targets supports first-line toxicity assessment and helps researchers prioritize mechanisms for subsequent experimental investigation. Here we present an automated AI pipeline that converts raw ChEMBL bioactivity data into optimized deep learning models for MIE prediction. The pipeline builds on the Knowledge-Guided Pre-training of Graph Transformer (KPGT) framework, which represents chemical structures as knowledge-enriched molecular graphs. It integrates data curation, molecular graph generation, and model training and tuning. This integration enables users to construct target-specific prediction models in a seamless and reproducible way, starting from initial data and ending with deployable AI. We demonstrate its use in a neural tube defect (NTD) case study, where fine-tuned KPGT models outperformed traditional Support Vector Machine models with a radial basis function kernel (SVM-RBF) when predicting MIEs linked to developmental toxicity. The results highlight the potential of AI-driven toxicity modeling to accelerate AOP development, improve endpoint prioritization, and prioritize chemicals for experimental follow-up. By providing an end-to-end, data-to-model workflow, the pipeline lowers the technical barrier to using modern graph-based neural architectures in toxicology. It offers a reproducible route to deployable MIE prediction models that support AOP development, compound prioritization, and early-stage chemical safety evaluation.Keywords: Computational toxicology, Deep Learning, Adverse Outcome Pathway, Neural Tube Closure

Introduction

Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs) offer a structured framework to understand how chemicals cause harmful effects by linking early molecular events to observable toxic outcomes [1–3]. Each AOP begins with a Molecular Initiating Event (MIE), continues through Key Events (KEs), that lead to an Adverse Outcome (AO) [4, 5]. The MIE provides a critical component in an AOP as it presents the primary point of contact of a chemical, which can trigger the cascade of downstream toxic effects [1, 6, 7]. Predicting MIEs thus provides a mechanistically anchored strategy for early hazard identification and prioritization in chemical safety assessment [1, 6, 7].

Computational tools, such as quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models, play a key role in the prediction of such chemical-biological interactions [8, 9]. These models relate chemical structure to biological activity, enabling first-line screening of compounds and supporting efforts to reduce animal testing [10, 11]. However, traditional QSAR approaches, rely on predefined molecular descriptors, including physicochemical properties, topological indices, and graph-based fingerprints. Because these descriptors are fixed, QSAR models struggle to generalize across diverse chemistries or limited labeled datasets [12, 13].

Recent advances in machine learning and deep learning offer promising alternative approaches. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Graph Transformers represent molecules as graphs and learn molecular descriptors directly from raw chemical structures [14, 15]. With techniques such as fine-tuning, these models can achieve strong performance even in data-sparse toxicological settings and help overcome key limitations of classical QSAR approaches [16, 17]. When integrated with the AOP framework and applied to MIEs, these models enable a mechanistic bridge between chemical structure and a potential toxic outcome.

In this study, we leverage the Knowledge-Guided Pre-training of Graph Transformer (KPGT) model to achieve this goal [18]. KPGT uses domain-informed pre-training to enhance downstream prediction of molecular properties and demonstrates strong performance. Hence it is suited for toxicological modeling, where structural diversity and data imbalance are common challenges.

We leveraged KPGT to build a deep learning pipeline for MIE prediction that handles data curation, model training, fine-tuning, evaluation, and analysis, with selected training components adapted from the original implementation [18]. As a proof-of-concept, we applied this pipeline to a case study on neural tube defects. Neural tube defects, such as spina bifida and anencephaly, are among the most prevalent human congenital malformations with substantial neurological and developmental consequences [19]. They are caused by failure of neural tube closure during early embryonic development, between day 23 and 30 of human gestation. [19–21]. This process is highly susceptible to disruption as proper neural tube closure relies on tightly coordinated cellular behaviors and signaling interactions. Genetic variations and environmental exposures—including pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and industrial chemicals—have been implicated in increasing the risk of neural tube closure defects [22, 23].

A growing body of epidemiological studies, case–control analyses, and experimental animal models continues to link specific chemical exposures to adverse developmental outcomes [24]. Still, many developmental toxicants remain unidentified, in part due to the limited number of chemicals tested in standard regulatory assays, the high cost and time requirements of developmental toxicity studies, and the complexity of embryonic processes that involve multiple overlapping molecular pathways [25, 26]. As a result, potentially hazardous compounds can enter or remain on the market without adequate evaluation. Therefore, we need more mechanism-informed tools that can prioritize chemicals for testing and guide targeted experimental validation [27].

In this project, we developed a deep learning pipeline leveraging KPGT to predict MIEs associated with neural tube closure defects. We evaluated the pipeline’s predictive performance by benchmarking it against a traditional Support Vector Machine with Radial Basis Function kernel (SVM-RBF) in a ligand–target interaction task. We then conducted ablation studies to assess the specific contributions of pre-training, fine-tuning, and a weighted loss function. Finally, we demonstrate that our automatic pipeline performs well across various protein targets involved in neural tube development. These analyses illustrate how knowledge-guided graph transformers can advance MIE prediction and support mechanism-based chemical risk assessment to protect human health and the environment.

Methods

Model overview

This study applies the Knowledge-Guided Pre-training of Graph Transformer (KPGT) model to predict Molecular Initiating Events (MIEs) associated with neural tube defects (NTDs). KPGT combines a line graph representation of molecules with a Graph Transformer architecture (LiGhT) to capture complex structural information (Sect. Molecular graph representation). For downstream prediction, we apply a two-layer perceptron with ReLU activation, consistent with the original implementation [18].

Molecular graph representation

Molecular graphs are transformed into molecular line graphs to encode the relational information of the edges within the original graph [18]. This provides a representation for learning complex molecular properties.

A molecular graph G=(V,E), where V={vi}i=1v represents the set of nodes (atoms) and E={ei,j}i,j=1v denotes the set of edges (chemical bonds), forms the foundation for encoding molecular structures. Here the number of nodes is represented by Nv. Each node vi in this graph has initial features represented as xiv∈RDv and each edge ei,j has initial features xi,je∈RDe. Dv represents the size of the node feature vectors and De indicates the size of the feature vectors for edges. The initial features xiv and xi,je are computed with RDKit [28]. These include features like bond type, conjugation for bonds, atom type and degree for atoms [18].

The transformation of a molecular graph into a molecular line graph, denoted as G^=(V^,E^), involves a unique encoding process. For each edge ei,j in G that connects two nodes vi and vj, a corresponding node v^i,j is created in G^. An edge is then formed between every pair of these new nodes if their corresponding edges in G share a common node. Automatic curation pipeline

To ensure rapid reproducible and scalable data processing, we implemented an automatic curation pipeline in Python that converts raw ChEMBL activity data into deep learning ready datasets for KPGT model training. This workflow processes ChEMBL bioactivity exports (CSV format) and applies a series of rule-based filters, standardization steps, and conflict-resolution rules to produce clean, analysis-ready binary classification datasets (active vs inactive) for KPGT training.

1. Activity curation: Only potency-relevant endpoints were retained, including IC50, EC50, AC50, Ki, Kd, and similar pChEMBL-compatible values [32]. Records flagged by ChEMBL as invalid or labeled with ambiguous terms (e.g., "inconclusive", "not determined") were excluded. When available, Standard Value, Standard Units, and Standard Relation were used to classify compounds as active or inactive. In line with standard toxicological benchmarks, compounds with activity values below 10 μM (i.e., < 10,000 nM) were labeled as ‘active‘, whereas compounds above this threshold were labeled as ‘inactive‘. Entries with "greater than" relations (e.g., ‘>10 μM‘) were conservatively classified as ‘inactive‘. If numeric data were missing, activity classification was inferred from free-text comments, following curated rules (e.g., comments containing "active", "inactive", or "no inhibition").

2. SMILES standardization: To ensure consistency in molecular representation, all SMILES strings were processed using RDKit’s standardization tools. Each SMILES underwent a three-step normalization procedure: (i) extraction of the largest fragment, (ii) removal of charges, and (iii) conversion to a canonical tautomeric form. Subsequently, molecules were kekulized, stripped of stereochemistry, and converted into canonical kekulé SMILES strings [28]. Records with invalid or unprocessable SMILES were discarded.

3. Conflict and duplicate removal: Conflicts were identified when the same SMILES or ChEMBL ID appeared with both activity labels. These entries were removed to avoid introducing noise. For non-conflicting duplicates, only a single representative entry was retained.

4. Final export: A complete Excel file was generated for provenance, including both raw and curated entries. In addition, two CSV files were exported: one containing SMILES, binary class labels, and ChEMBL IDs; and a minimal version excluding the ChEMBL ID column for direct use in KPGT training. Only entries with binary labels (‘active‘/‘inactive‘) and valid SMILES were retained. The resulting datasets retained their native class distribution and split across train/validation/test using stratified sampling.

5. Model inference and application to unknown chemicals: Each trained KPGT model can be directly reused for predicting molecular activity of new compounds without retraining. Alongside the exported model checkpoint, the pipeline exports an inference_meta.json file containing the configuration, feature dimensions, and normalization constants required for reproducible inference. The accompanying predict.py script accepts any CSV file with a smiles column, performs identical feature extraction steps as in training (molecular graph construction, fingerprinting, and descriptor generation), and outputs probabilistic activity scores for each Molecular Initiating Event (MIE) target. Compounds exceeding a user-defined threshold (default = 0.5) are classified as active. This inference module allows rapid, consistent screening of new or external chemicals using any trained KPGT model.

Applicability domain considerations: The exported model checkpoint stores the trained KPGT parameters, while the accompanying inference_meta.json file stores the configuration and preprocessing metadata required for reproducible inference (e.g., dataset type, number of tasks, feature dimensions, and graph construction settings). This metadata ensures consistent preprocessing between training and inference, but it does not by itself define a chemical-space applicability domain. In practice, the applicability domain of each trained model is primarily determined by the chemical space represented in the curated ChEMBL training dataset and by successful parsing and molecular graph construction from input SMILES (RDKit validity and line-graph featurization). Predictions are therefore expected to be most reliable for compounds that fall within training-supported chemical space, whereas extrapolation to structurally distant chemotypes may be less supported.

6. Implementation and reproducibility: The pipeline was implemented in Python (≥3.10) using RDKit (≥2022.03), Pandas, and OpenPyXL. It can be executed via the command line with configurable arguments for input path, output directory, pChEMBL cutoff, and activity threshold. All code and documentation is openly available in GitHub: https://github.com/MerelFlorian/NeuroTox-KPGT.git.

This pipeline ensures that downstream graph-based models are trained on standardized, biologically relevant, and non-conflicting molecular data, in alignment with best practices for QSAR and GNN modeling [33].

Training and optimization

Models were implemented in Python v3.10.13 using PyTorch v2.3.0 [35], DGL v2.2.1 [36] and CUDA v12.1 [37], based on the codebase by Li et al. [18, 38]. Training was performed on an RTX 3080 GPU (32 GB RAM) or a Snellius HPC node with 16 GB RAM [39].

Optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer [40], with regularization techniques including dropout [41], weight decay [42], gradient norm clipping [43] and early stopping (patience = 6 epochs, max 50). Hyper-parameter tuning

Hyper-parameters (dropout, learning rate, weight decay, early stopping) were optimized via random search using Ray Tune 2.23.0 [44] and ASHAScheduler. We selected hyper-parameters using validation balanced accuracy (BA), which is robust to class imbalance [45]. Tuning used a dedicated 10-fold split: eight folds for training, one fold for validation, and one fold for testing (Fig. 2). We used the validation fold during training to monitor performance across epochs, trigger early stopping, and choose hyper-parameters; we kept the test fold completely unseen during training and model selection and evaluated it exactly once after tuning. After fixing hyper-parameters, we assessed performance with 10-fold stratified cross-validation on the full dataset using the same settings (see Sect. Evaluation metrics). Loss function

To address class imbalance, we used a weighted binary cross entropy with logits (BCEWithLogits) loss function [35]. This loss function combines a sigmoid activation layer and a binary cross-entropy loss function into a single layer, incorporating an additional weight pc for active data points. Here, wn represents the weight associated with the n-th loss component, yn is the target value, xn is the input feature, and σ(.) denotes the sigmoid function. We select a weight pc of (N inactive samples/N active samples) for each batch. This approach aims to equalize the influence of active and inactive samples during training by assigning a higher weight to the less frequent class [47]. We applied weighting only when the minority-class prevalence fell below 0.35 (i.e., min{πactive,πinactive}<0.35). For roughly balanced splits (prevalence ≥0.35) we set pc=1, recovering the standard (unweighted) binary cross-entropy. See section Ablation studies: identifying optimal KPGT configuration for pipeline integration for the effect of weighting on performance metrics.

Evaluation metrics

We used stratified 10-fold cross-validation for model training and evaluation [48]. Here the dataset is divided into 10 folds in a random stratified manner, ensuring proportional representation of labels across splits (Fig. 2). One fold was held out as a test set to reduce the risk of data leakage during hyper-parameter tuning; others were used for training/validation.

The primary classification metric was the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) (Eq. 8). Descriptive statistics include the median and interquartile range (IQR) over the cross-validation folds.

In first-line toxicity assessment, missing positive cases is more problematic than missing negative ones, as you may overlook possible harmful chemicals. Hence, we included the median and IQR of the balanced accuracy (BA) (Eq. 11), together with the sensitivity (SEN) (Eq. 9) and the specificity (SPE) (Eq. 10). However, these metrics were excluded from the statistical comparison between models because the BA can be misleading. It may appear higher even when either SEN or SPE is low, potentially concealing deficiencies in accurately predicting one class over the other. To provide additional insight, the median and IQR of the true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN) counts are reported unless otherwise indicated.

Results

In this study, we developed a deep learning pipeline for prediction of MIEs using KPGT. As a case study, we applied this pipeline to MIEs related to neural tube closure (NTC) defects. To assess predictive performance, we benchmarked KPGT against a SVM-RBF model implemented in VEGA 1.2.5 using HDAC inhibition as a representative prediction task [49]. We then analyzed the impact of model components using three ablation studies that isolate the effects of pre-training, fine-tuning, and weighted loss functions (Sect. Ablation studies: identifying optimal KPGT configuration for pipeline integration). Finally, we developed an automated pipeline that integrates KPGT and applied this to additional targets relevant to neural tube closure.

Predicting HDAC inhibitors

Proof-of-concept: KPGT outperforms the benchmark SVM-RBF

We tested whether KPGT improves predictive performance over SVM-RBF using 10-fold stratified cross-validation. Two variants of KPGT were compared: a fine-tuned model (KPGT-FT) and a feature extraction model (KPGT-FE), where only the final classifier is trained (See App. Section Settings KPGT Fig. 12). We evaluated model performance using the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) 8). This metric accounts for all elements of the confusion matrix and is suitable when positive and negative outcomes are equally important [45].

Because cross-validation folds are not independent, we used Friedman’s ANOVA to compare the models without violating statistical assumptions [51, 52]. Post-hoc significance was assessed with the Nemenyi test [53].

There was a significant performance difference between the models (χ2(2)=6, p<0.05) (Fig. 3a). The median MCC of KPGT-FE and KPGT-FT were 0.645 and 0.670 respectively. This was higher compared to the median MCC of the SVM-RBF, which is 0.581. Post-hoc analysis showed that this difference was significant for KPGT-FT (p=0.04) but not for KPGT-FE (p= 0.90). Although KPGT-FT achieved a slightly higher median MCC than KPGT-FE, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.07). KPGT-FT also showed lower variability across folds compared to KPGT-FE and the SVM-RBF, as reflected by a narrower interquartile range (IQR). KPGT-FS: From-scratch training Removing pre-training led to a decrease in median MCC and a higher interquartile range (IQR), indicating reduced consistency across folds. While this model performed slightly better in identifying true negatives (higher SPE), it came at the cost of reduced sensitivity (SEN) and balanced accuracy (BA). The MCC difference compared to KPGT-FT was not statistically significant (p = 0.08), but the performance trend suggests pre-training is beneficial for robustness.

KPGT-OS: No fine-tuning The off-the-shelf model showed the lowest and most inconsistent performance across all metrics. Median MCC dropped to nearly zero (-−0.020), and the model struggled with both true positive and true negative identification (low SEN and SPE). This configuration performed significantly worse than KPGT-FT (p = 0.01), highlighting the critical role of fine-tuning for adapting the model to specific bioactivity datasets.

KPGT-SL: Standard loss function When the weighted loss function was replaced with a standard BCEWithLogits loss, the model maintained a high MCC and achieved the highest sensitivity across all variants. However, specificity decreased, leading to a slightly reduced balanced accuracy. Although not statistically different from KPGT-FT (p = 1), this trade-off indicates that the weighted loss helps mitigate class imbalance by reducing false negatives, albeit at a minor cost to sensitivity.

Conclusion Among the evaluated configurations, KPGT-FT—pre-trained and fine-tuned with a weighted loss function—achieves the most balanced performance, with the highest median MCC and BA. This supports its use as the default configuration in our MIE prediction pipeline. Fine-tuning is the most decisive factor, with additional gains from pre-training and weighted loss helping to improve robustness and counter class imbalance.

A robust pipeline for automatic development of MIE prediction models

To facilitate rapid model development across various MIE targets, we implemented a fully automated pipeline that contains data curation, preprocessing and model training. This pipeline transforms raw ChEMBL bioactivity data into standardized input for deep learning, enabling end-to-end development of KPGT models with minimal manual effort (Sect. Automatic curation pipeline). Once curated, the data is directly passed to downstream modules for making splits and molecular graph construction.

The pipeline consists of four modular scripts: (i) autocurate.py, which filters and standardizes ChEMBL bioactivity data by selecting potency-relevant endpoints, assigning binary activity labels, cleaning SMILES strings using RDKit, and resolving duplicate or conflicting entries; (ii) make_splits.py, which generates stratified 10-fold splits while maintaining label balance across folds; (iii) prepare_molecular_graphs.py, which converts SMILES into molecular line graphs required by the KPGT architecture; and (iv) experiment.py, which executes model training and evaluation using predefined settings. Each module is fully scripted and requires no manual intervention once input data is provided a raw ChEMBL CSV file). All scripts are executed sequentially using the provided bash script run_pipeline.sh.

By eliminating most manual steps, this pipeline enables the deployment of dozens of MIE-specific models in parallel, significantly accelerating the development of AI tools for chemical safety assessment. In practice, such an approach supports scalable first-line screening efforts, where newly prioritized targets can be added to the modeling workflow with minimal delay.

We applied this pipeline to ten NTC-related targets to demonstrate the predictive performance of our automatic curation and standardized training workflow. The modeled targets represent upstream molecular activities that align with core signaling modules known to regulate neurulation and neural tube closure. Specifically, fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR1–4) and mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1/ERK2) reflect the FGF/ERK signaling axis involved in proliferation, caudal progenitor programs, and morphogenetic movements [55–57]. Bone morphogenetic protein receptor (BMPR)-related targets represent BMP signaling that contributes to dorsoventral identity and hinge-point regulation [58–60]. Smoothened (SMO) represents Hedgehog signal transduction, which influences midline patterning and ventral specification [58, 61]. Cytochrome P450 family 26 (CYP26) represents retinoic acid metabolism that shapes RA gradients and thereby anteroposterior patterning [55, 56, 62, 63]. Histone deacetylase (HDAC)-related activity represents epigenetic regulation that can modulate transcriptional programs across multiple neurulation processes [56, 64]. Together, these targets provide mechanistically anchored entry points for training and benchmarking MIE-linked bioactivity predictors in an NTC-relevant context.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the potential of a graph-based deep learning pipeline to support the identification of molecular initiating events (MIEs) related to neural tube defects. By leveraging Knowledge-Guided Pre-training of Graph Transformer (KPGT), the model effectively learns structural representations of chemicals and predicts bioactivity with high performance. The comparison with the benchmark SVM-RBF model showed that fine-tuned KPGT offers clear advantages, particularly in sensitivity and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), reinforcing the value of deep learning architectures in early-stage toxicity assessment.

The architectural strength of KPGT lies in its ability to operate directly on molecular graphs. Unlike classical models that rely on predefined descriptors, KPGT learn molecular descriptors directly from raw chemical structures [14, 15]. This ability may reduce bias introduced by handcrafted features and enables the model to infer subtle structural patterns relevant for toxicity [6]. This is in line with the finding that the performance of SVMs is reliant on the quality of the input features [65].

At the same time, structure-based bioactivity models remain constrained by the chemical space represented in the training data. Public resources such as ChEMBL reflect historical screening priorities and medicinal chemistry practice, which can lead to uneven coverage across chemotypes and enrichment of specific chemical series. Moreover, compound selection for testing is often hypothesis-driven, which can enrich datasets for expected actives and contribute to class imbalance. As a result, predictive performance can vary across underrepresented structural classes, even when the learning algorithm does not rely on predefined scaffolds or handcrafted descriptors. We therefore interpret model performance as conditional on the curated training distribution, and consider predictions most reliable for compounds that fall within training-supported chemical space. This consideration also motivates the use of applicability domain diagnostics when deploying automated target-specific models.

Our ablation studies showed that performance gains were largely driven by fine-tuning for adapting the model to specific MIE prediction tasks. The off-the-shelf model (KPGT-OS), lacking fine-tuning, failed to produce meaningful predictions (MCC = −0.02), underscoring the necessity of task-specific training. In contrast, the contribution of pre-training was more subtle. While training from scratch (KPGT-FS) resulted in slightly lower and more variable performance compared to the pretrained model and fine-tuned model version (KPGT-FT). However, the difference was not statistically significant, suggesting that pre-training offers a stabilizing benefit rather than a dramatic performance leap. This outcome may reflect the phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting, where fine-tuning overrides previously acquired knowledge acquired during pre-training [66, 67]. Future work could explore techniques like layer-wise learning rate decay to preserve pre-trained representations and improve transfer learning. Here, the learning rate is lower in the earlier layers to preserve the general, low-level features learned during pre-training [18, 68].

Similar to previous studies, we observed variable performance across targets, likely driven by dataset size, structural similarity of compounds, and class imbalance [33, 34]. Smaller datasets like CYP26 suffered from lower predictive accuracy, while structurally similar actives and inactives in FGFR4 reduced model specificity. These limitations point to the need for larger, more balanced datasets and better characterization of the model’s applicability domain.

Model performance is also bound by the heterogeneity of public bioactivity resources. ChEMBL aggregates assays across laboratories and experimental formats, which introduces variability in endpoint definitions, assay conditions, and measurement noise. As a result, target-specific datasets may contain residual label uncertainty that limits achievable predictive performance even for strong models. In automated deployment, simple applicability-domain diagnostics (e.g., similarity-to-training measures) can help flag potentially out-of-domain inputs and support more cautious interpretation of predictions.

To counter class imbalance, we implemented a weighted loss function. This function did not substantially improve MCC, highlighting the trade-off between reducing false positives and increasing false negatives. In a toxicological context, where failing to identify a hazardous chemical (a false negative) can have severe consequences, maximizing sensitivity is often most important. Therefore, the choice between a standard or weighted loss function should be context-dependent, tailored to the specific risk assessment scenario and the acceptable balance between false positives and false negatives.

A key contribution of this work is the development of an automated pipeline that integrates data curation, preprocessing, and model training. This system transforms raw ChEMBL bioactivity data into clean, analysis-ready datasets to develop KPGT models with minimal manual workload. Importantly, automation did not compromise model performance. KPGT models created with the automated pipeline performed competitively to previously published SVM benchmarks (Table 5). As a result, researchers can deploy tailored models for different toxicity mechanisms with minimal manual effort, accelerating hypothesis generation and expanding the applicability of AI in chemical safety assessment.

While predicting MIEs is a powerful starting point, the simplicity of the AOP framework means it often overlooks additional layers of biological complexity. Downstream regulatory mechanisms, compensatory pathways, or repair processes can prevent a key event from progressing, even when an upstream MIE occurs [5]. Incorporating these processes into models could reduce the risk of missing critical information and improve predictions for later-stage toxicological outcomes. Nonetheless, MIE-based predictions have already proven highly effective in subsequent toxicity assessments and remain among the most reliable indicators of overall hazard within the AOP framework [9, 33].

Further refinement could also come from integrating explainable AI (XAI) approaches. While KPGT delivered strong predictive performance, its outputs offer no direct insight into the molecular features driving each decision, a trade-off seen in predictive toxicology [69]. Understanding which molecular structures lead to accurate predictions or misclassifications is valuable. It can shed light on limitations of the model by providing context for misclassifications. Not only does this facilitate further model improvements, but it also builds trust among researchers and society [70].

Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of using KPGT for MIE prediction. However, the value of this pipeline lies in its integration as a first-line screening tool within a broader New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) ecosystem. By rapidly screening chemical inventories for potential MIEs, the pipeline can prioritize chemicals for further investigation. Focusing resources towards those chemicals that are most likely to pose a hazard. For example, predictions indicating that a chemical can trigger MIEs related to HDAC inhibition or FGF signaling, could guide targeted in vitro experiments in human-relevant systems or inform more complex dynamics systems models [58, 71]. Data from these follow-up studies can validate MIE predictions or serve as inputs for other in silico approaches, creating a cascade of evidence that enhances transparency and biological relevance in regulatory decision-making. Such a tiered approach supports the 3Rs (Reduction, Refinement, and Replacement of animal testing) by focusing on chemicals of highest concern and grounding hazard assessment in mechanistic understanding.

Ultimately, this work contributes to a growing toolbox of NAMs that promote safer chemical development. The strong performance of fine-tuned KPGT models, combined with automated curation and compatibility with subsequent mechanistic modeling, positions this approach as a practical tool for early hazard identification in chemical safety assessment. With continued improvements and integration into broader NAM frameworks, it has the potential to bridge the gap between computational predictions and real-world toxicological outcomes.
