Predictors of intraoperative donor heart turndown after initial acceptance for transplantation
Authors: Armaan F. Akbar, Alice L. Zhou, Jessica M. Ruck, Sorush Rokui, Dane Paneitz, Ahmet Kilic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveBecause of the low use of potential heart donors, it is important to maximize transplantation and minimize discard of accepted offers. We analyzed national registry data to investigate risk factors for intraoperative turndown of donor hearts that were initially accepted for transplantation.MethodsAll adult deceased donors who donated ≥1 organs during the period from January 2005 to March 2023 in the United Network for Organ Sharing database and received an acceptance or provisional acceptance by a heart team were categorized by heart allograft use versus intraoperative turndown (ITD). We investigated risk factors for ITD using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for covariates with P < .2 on univariate analysis.ResultsOf 48,240 potential adult donors who received an acceptance from a heart transplant center, 43,401 (90.0%) of donors resulted in heart use, 1262 (2.6%) resulted in heart ITD, and 3577 (7.4%) resulted in heart nonuse for other reasons. ITD increased from 0.75% in 2005 to 2.2% in 2023 as a proportion of potential heart recoveries annually (P-trend = .02). On adjusted analysis, the strongest independent risk factor for ITD was donation after circulatory death (adjusted odds ratio, 2.60; 95% confidence interval, 1.86-3.62; P < .001), followed by older donor age (age ≥50 [vs <30] years [adjusted odds ratio, 2.02; 95% confidence interval, 1.59-2.56], P < .001). Additional independent risk factors included left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, >10 transfusions during terminal hospitalization, Hispanic and Other (vs White) race/ethnicity, death attributable to stroke, and hypertension.ConclusionsWe identified several factors associated with intraoperative turndown that can inform anticipatory intraoperative evaluation and team coordination while preserving access to donor hearts.

Central Message

Nationally, 2.6% of accepted hearts were turned down intraoperatively. A small but nonzero rate is expected; donor factors can guide anticipatory evaluation and intraoperative scrutiny.

Perspective

Intraoperative turndown (ITD) of donor hearts is uncommon (2.6%). While a theoretical ITD rate of 0% would be ideal, a small but nonzero rate is expected as some findings only become apparent intraoperatively. Our findings identify donor factors linked to ITD that can guide anticipatory evaluation and intraoperative scrutiny without discouraging procurement.

Heart transplantation remains the treatment of choice for the growing population of patients with end-stage heart failure in the United States, but its use is limited by the supply of donor hearts, resulting in high waitlist mortality for the most medically urgent candidates.1,2 New attempts to expand the donor pool have included the use of ex vivo heart perfusion for better evaluation and resuscitation of marginal hearts,3 consideration of expanded criteria brain-death and donation after circulatory death (DCD) donors,4,5 and advancements in hypothermic organ preservation and transport.6 Even so, less than one third of hearts from potential donors are currently accepted for transplant.7

To both increase candidate access to transplantation and optimize use of transplant team resources, it is important to maximize transplantation of accepted offers. Therefore, it is paramount to minimize discard of hearts accepted for transplantation, which may occur after evaluation in the operating room, or after procurement but before implantation in the recipient. In 2020, discard of donor hearts after procurement was reported to be 1%,8 but the likelihood of intraoperative donor heart turndown (ie after evaluation in the operating room but before procurement) is poorly understood, and to our knowledge, has never been investigated. Intraoperative turndown (ITD) may vary with center- and regional-level differences in heart acceptance practices. In addition, on October 18, 2018, the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) revised their heart allocation policy to better prioritize medically urgent candidates. As part of this change, the local donation service area level of allocation for donor hearts was replaced with allocation to the most medically advanced candidates within a 500-nautical mile radius around the donor hospital. As a result, teams now travel farther to procure hearts and spend more on organ acquisition, which may affect teams’ decisions about which organ offers to pursue, and therefore have an impact on ITD.9, 10, 11

Donor heart turndown at the recovery site leads to resource waste and may lead to negative candidate outcomes. Therefore, we analyzed national registry data to investigate risk factors for ITD of donor heart grafts that were initially accepted for transplantation. We examined trends in heart ITD at the individual, center, and region levels.

Methods

Study Population

Using the UNOS database, we identified all adult (age ≥18 years) deceased donors who donated ≥1 organ between January 1, 2005, and March 31, 2023, and received an acceptance or provisional acceptance of the heart from a heart transplant center. Potential donors were classified in the ITD group if the heart was declined at the recovery site after evaluation in the operating room and compared with donors with heart use. We excluded potential donors with heart no-use for reasons other than ITD. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent (IRB00352819) on December 19, 2022.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was ITD of donor heart allografts. Associations between donor characteristics and ITD were evaluated, as were potential differences in ITD in the eras before (January 1, 2015, to October 18, 2018) and after (October 19, 2018, to March 31, 2023) the heart allocation policy change on October 18, 2018. Annual and center-level trends in ITD of donor hearts during the study period were examined. Finally, ITD patterns across the United States were mapped to visualize heart ITD in each UNOS region.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline donor characteristics were examined using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Continuous variables were first examined for distribution; because they were generally skewed, results are reported as median (interquartile range) and compared using nonparametric tests. A multivariable logistic regression model was fit to assess the relationship between donor characteristics and ITD, adjusting for era and donor characteristics with P < .2 on univariate analysis. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Given the recent adoption of DCD for heart transplantation, we performed a prespecified sensitivity analysis restricting DCD estimates to the modern era (January 1, 2019, to March 31, 2023). Trends over time were assessed using Cuzick tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationship between center volume and heart ITD. We used χ2 tests were used to compare ITD rates in each UNOS region by era. We summarized variable-level missingness and conducted a complete-case analysis for multivariable modeling; variable missingness is reported in Table E1. All statistics were performed using StataSE 18 (StataCorp).

Results

Study Population

During the study period, 48,240 potential adult donors received an acceptance or provisional acceptance of the heart from a heart transplant center. Of these, 43,401 (90.0%) of donors resulted in heart use, 1262 (2.6%) resulted in heart ITD, and 3577 (7.4%) resulted in heart nonuse for other reasons. Therefore, approximately 90% of accepted donor recoveries ultimately resulted in transplantation, whereas 2.6% ended in ITD. Potential donors with heart nonuse for reasons other than ITD were excluded from further analysis, and a final cohort of 44,663 potential donors was analyzed and compared by heart ITD.

Trends in ITD of Donor Heart Allografts

ITD of potential heart donors increased from 13 in 2005 to 106 in 2022. As a proportion of potential heart recoveries performed annually, ITD increased from 0.75% in 2005 to 2.2% in 2023 (P = .02; Figure 1). After adjustment for other donor characteristics, the strongest independent risk factor for ITD was DCD (aOR, 2.60; 95% CI, 1.86-3.62; P < .001; Table 2), followed by older donor age (age ≥50 [vs <30] years [aOR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.59-2.56; P < .001], age 40-49 [vs 30] years [aOR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.51-2.17; P < .001], age 30-39 [vs 30] years [aOR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.37-1.89; P < .001]). Additional independent risk factors included left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50% (aOR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.24-2.00; P < .001), >10 transfusions during terminal hospitalization (aOR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.21-2.03; P = .001), Hispanic (vs White) race/ethnicity (aOR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.23-1.68; P < .001), other (vs White) race/ethnicity (aOR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.21-2.18; P = .001), death caused by stroke (vs anoxic brain injury) (aOR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.12-1.71; P = .002), hypertension (aOR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.11-1.53; P = .001), cardiopulmonary resuscitation (aOR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.07-1.49; P = .005), cytomegalovirus seropositivity (aOR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.04-1.49; P = .01), greater total bilirubin (aOR, 1.05 per mg/dL, 95% CI, 1.02-1.08; P < .001), and greater creatinine (aOR, 1.05 per mg/dL; 95% CI, 1.02-1.09; P = .005). Compared with the preallocation change era (January 1, 2015, to October 18, 2018), consideration in the postallocation change era (October 18, 2018, to March 31, 2023) was associated with lower odds of ITD (aOR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.66-0.88; P < .001). Other factors associated with lower odds of ITD included greater hematocrit (aOR, 0.83 per 10%; 95% CI, 0.72-0.95; P = .006) and blood type AB (vs A) (aOR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.23-0.88; P = .02). Sensitivity Analysis

In analyses restricted to the modern DCD era (2019-2023), the association between DCD and ITD persisted with similar magnitude and direction (Table E2).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of the UNOS database, we found that from 2005 to 2023, 2.6% of donor hearts initially accepted for transplant were later turned down after evaluation in the operating room. This proportion decreased after revisions to heart allocation policy in October 2018, particularly in the Southwestern United States. We found that the strongest independent risk factors for heart ITD were DCD, age ≥50 years, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, and receipt of >10 transfusions during the terminal hospitalization. Importantly, nearly 9 of 10 procurement trips culminated in transplantation, underscoring the high yield of recovery efforts despite the small proportion of ITDs.

Although a zero turndown rate could be achieved by declining more marginal offers pre-emptively, such an approach would likely reduce transplant opportunities. A low but nonzero national ITD rate (∼2%-3%) may therefore reflect an acceptable balance between broad access and prudent intraoperative decision-making. Our findings are intended to flag scenarios where additional prerecovery data or intraoperative scrutiny might mitigate unexpected turndown, rather than to discourage procurement.

Although previous studies have examined predictors of donor heart use, to our knowledge, no studies have described ITD of donor hearts. We found that ITD was relatively rare, occurring for only 2.6% of accepted heart donors. This is encouraging, although we did note that this figure varied substantially by region and by center. In addition, ITD is distinct from discard after heart procurement, which has been reported to be 1% nationally.8 We were unable to evaluate specific reasons for ITD in our cohort, but we hypothesize that ITD likely results from structural and physiologic problems, whereas postprocurement discard may additionally be caused by recipient factors and collaborative organ assessment between the procuring and implanting teams. A cumulative discard rate, calculated as ITD in combination with postrecovery discard, is approximately 3.6%. Although discard appears low on a national level, due to poor overall heart donor use of less than one third of potential donors,7 it is in the best interests of heart transplant centers to maximize transplantation of acceptable hearts.

We identified several donor characteristics associated with ITD, including DCD, greater donor age, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, receipt of >10 transfusions, Hispanic or other race/ethnicity, death caused by stroke, hypertension, and greater bilirubin and creatinine. With the exception of Hispanic or other race/ethnicity, the majority of these risk factors are intuitively associated with poor heart quality. DCD was the strongest identified risk factor, and the greater risk of ITD attributed to these hearts may have been attributable to concerns over delayed progression to asystole or prolonged warm ischemic time, parameters that are only partially captured in the registry dataset and which do not affect heart procurement after brain death.12,13 In addition, the majority of DCD hearts may be subject to functional assessment using ex vivo heart perfusion, which may influence decision-making. Other factors that have previously been associated with donor nonuse in other studies include age ≥50 years, stroke, hypertension, and left ventricular dysfunction,14, 15, 16 and donor-recipient race matching has also been associated with better recipient outcomes and could be considered by procurement teams.17 However, these risk factors would likely have been considered before the offer acceptance, as well. The severity of these factors may have only been revealed during on-site evaluation, prompting ITD. The decision to attempt to recover donor hearts with identified risk factors in an effort to maximize donor use must be carefully weighed with potentially wasted travel and organ acquisition costs.

The incidence of ITD declined modestly after the 2018 allocation change and has remained stable, suggesting that the issue may be improving nationally, though variability persists across regions and centers. Nonetheless, the absolute number of affected procurements remains meaningful, with 106 ITDs in 2022 alone. This was primarily attributable to a decrease in ITD in region 5 (where ITD was the greatest throughout the overall study period), from 8.3% to 4.6%. These findings suggest that the regional differences in ITD were partly driven by a small number of centers with greater ITD rates, particularly in Region 5, which contributed disproportionately to national variation. The 2018 policy change reorganized organ allocation to the sickest candidates within 500-nautical miles of the donor hospital, rather than within the donation service area. Previous studies have demonstrated greater geographic sharing as a result of the allocation change.18 It is possible that the policy change facilitated changes in organ acceptance practices in order to serve a more geographically appropriate candidate population in region 5, which may have led to lower ITD. Despite greater travel distances and associated costs after the policy change, ITD reassuringly did not increase in any UNOS region, although this may have also resulted from changes in decision-making by transplant teams about which organs are most cost-effective to travel to procure. We additionally found that greater center volume in accepting donor heart offers was correlated with greater intraoperative decline, showing that more aggressive centers were more willing to travel to recover hearts but ended up turning down more hearts after evaluation in the operating room as a result. Previous studies have demonstrated that greater center acceptance rates are associated with lower waitlist mortality,19 and centers must be aware of the costs associated with their acceptance practices and weigh them against the benefit of potentially usable organs.

Although we found several characteristics to be statistically significantly associated with heart ITD, many of the differences we observed may not be clinically significant. Furthermore, the complex provider decision-making in the operating room cannot be fully captured by our analysis. However, we believe our findings can help navigate this decision-making process to hopefully optimize donor use and reduce waste of recovery team time and resources. First, donors exhibiting multiple strong risk factors for ITD, such as age ≥50 years, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, and >10 transfusions during terminal hospitalization may be subject to additional scrutiny and referred for additional imaging, like serial echocardiograms,20 which may help in evaluation and save resources in the event of turndown before recovery efforts. In a recent multicenter prospective study, Khush and colleagues21 showed that of potential donors with an initial echocardiogram showing left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, 58% demonstrated reversibility of left ventricular dysfunction within 24 hours and many went on to donate their heart. Second, the use of specialized donor management facilities may serve to rehabilitate donor organs initially deemed unsuitable for transplant, with a potential role for stroke volume-based volume resuscitation and hormone therapy in heart donors.22 Third, the employment of local organ recovery teams and consistent communication and collaboration between recovering and implanting facilities may increase the efficiency of organ recovery, reduce ITD, and reduce ITD-associated costs.23 Finally, ex vivo heart perfusion systems have used with increasing frequency to evaluate and recondition extended criteria donor hearts.5 Greater center use of ex vivo heart perfusion may reduce ITD, but the costs of ITD must be balanced against the costs of starting an ex vivo heart perfusion program.24 In the lung transplant literature, Halpern and colleagues25 recently demonstrated that ex vivo lung perfusion is profitable at an institutional level, despite increasing hospital costs, although no analogous study exists examining the cost effectiveness of ex vivo heart perfusion. Although our model does not define a strict threshold at which programs should alter recovery procedures, the presence of multiple strong risk factors (eg, donor age ≥50 years, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, receipt of >10 transfusions) may reasonably prompt additional scrutiny. In such cases, centers might consider repeat echocardiography, more detailed hemodynamic assessment, or closer review of terminal hospitalization events before deploying recovery teams. For teams already en route, these findings may encourage heightened intraoperative vigilance and more deliberate communication with implanting centers. Importantly, we emphasize that these measures are intended to enhance preparedness rather than discourage procurement, given that the vast majority of recovery efforts still result in transplantation.

Our study has several limitations. The UNOS database has a limited number of pre-existing variables for analysis. For hearts that were not transplanted, we did not have the distance traveled by the recovery team, though distance may have an impact on time constraints and may have impacted the decision to decline hearts on-site. Because OPO-level identifiers are not available in the UNOS dataset, we were unable to assess variation in ITD across OPOs. UNOS data do not reliably distinguish between normothermic regional perfusion and direct procurement and perfusion, limiting our ability to examine these DCD subgroups separately. In addition, specific anatomic and physiologic disturbances that may have caused ITD were not available and could not be assessed. Although ITD accounted for 2.6% of accepted donor hearts, nonuse for other reasons (7.4%) represented a larger contributor to overall discard. Thus, although reducing ITD is important, strategies to address other reasons for nonuse may have even greater impact on improving donor heart use.

Conclusions

We found that 2.6% of donor hearts initially accepted for transplant nationally were turned down after evaluation in the operating room. Strong risk factors for intraoperative decline, including DCD, age ≥50 years, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, and >10 transfusions during terminal hospitalization, can be used to identify donors at the greatest risk for ITD and help recovery teams manage expectations and resources. Identifying donors at greater likelihood of ITD may help teams plan anticipatory evaluation and coordination, supporting efficient use of recovery pathways without limiting procurement attempts.
