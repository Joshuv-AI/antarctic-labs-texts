Introduction

The heart and the lungs are closely linked together in both physiology and anatomy. Physiologically, they supply the tissues within the body with oxygenated blood and are responsible for clearing the carbon dioxide produced at those same tissues. Anatomically, they share the closed cavity of the thorax. Because of these close relationships, interactions between the heart and lungs have been recognised since the early days of modern medicine. They were probably first described by Stephen Hales in 1733 after cannulating the internal jugular vein of a horse.1 Many studies describing the physiological principles of these interactions followed, and medical techniques improved.

Positive pressure ventilation (PPV) was introduced as a major therapy within the intensive care unit by Bjorn Ibsen in the early 1950s and markedly decreased mortality from polio at that time.2 PPV changes the pressures within the thoracic cavity during the respiratory cycle: instead of negative pressures during spontaneous inspiration, a mechanical ventilator delivers positive pressure to ensure adequate ventilation. This changes the cardiopulmonary interactions as well. The most marked change encountered in the ICU was a profound drop in both blood pressure and cardiac output (CO) with the start of PPV.

Arthur Guyton was the first to describe the mechanism behind this drop in CO.3 4 He stated that with elevation of the pressure in the right atrium (PRA) systemic venous return is opposed. Since the right ventricle can only generate as much output as there is preload, CO is diminished. The positive pressure delivered to the lungs by PPV is transferred to the right atrium as they share the thoracic cavity. He concluded that with PPV the PRA will be higher and CO will be less. The effect of PPV on the left ventricle, however, is not the same. Elevation of the intrathoracic pressure will reduce the pressure gradient between the left ventricle and the abdominal aorta.5 This reduction in transmural pressure of the left ventricle makes it easier for the heart to maintain CO.

Mechanical Ventilation (MV) is one of the most common therapies within paediatric critical care, with PPV as its most-used mode. It is used for acute respiratory failure as well as a supportive therapy after major surgery. This makes understanding of the (patho-)physiologic processes induced by MV on both ventricles and the vascular system a basic skill for clinicians in the PICU. These paediatric cardiopulmonary interactions with PPV are therefore commonly described within the literature.6,8 These descriptions are mostly based on the principles as described above. They also include studies of the physiology of both animals and (sick) adults. Data on the actual clinical effects of MV on the circulation of critically ill children are scarce. When clinical trials are conducted, they are mostly done in specific populations or comparing different changes in mechanical ventilation and/or outcomes.9,12 A search conducted on 24 February 2026 in JBI Evidence Synthesis, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Open Science Framework and PubMed showed no published systematic or scoping reviews (nor protocols of reviews in progress) on this subject.

This proposed scoping review will be conducted in order to systematically map the research done in this area, as well as to identify any existing gaps in knowledge. The following research question was formulated:

What is known from the literature about the effect on cardiovascular parameters of different ventilation strategies or adjustments of specific parameters of MV (concept) in children receiving MV (population) in paediatric critical care (context)?

Methods

The proposed scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews.13 14 The protocol is registered with the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/H436N). The search is scheduled for the first of June 2026, and we plan to finish the paper before 31 December 2026.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria have been developed using the population, concept and context framework to ensure a structured and transparent approach to study selection.13 The criteria were informed by a preliminary search of literature showing. So, a broad range of studies will be included to get a broad scope of evidence to answer the review question.

This review will include randomised and non-randomised clinical trials and observational studies including cohort studies, case-control studies and cross-sectional studies. Excluded are case reports, case series with less than 5 patients, reviews and letters. For practical and logistical reasons studies will be included when in the English language. Only full-text publications will be included. There will be no restriction regarding publication date.

Population

Eligible studies will include paediatric patients ranging from 0 to 18 years who are receiving mechanical ventilation during at least part of the duration of the study. Premature infants will be excluded due to their distinct physiologic differences.

Concept

This scoping review will include all studies where any adjustment is made in ventilation strategy. These changes can include a comparison between the period of mechanical ventilation and spontaneous breathing, switching ventilation modes or adjustment of a single ventilatory parameter.

Any outcome parameters describing CO will be included. These parameters might include but are not limited to Point-Of-Care UltraSound (POCUS) measured volume-time integral, direct cardiac output measurements with thermal dilution, suprasternal notch semi-automated CO-measurement (USCOM) or calculated CO during cardiac catheterisation. Surrogate markers for cardiac performance derived by either echocardiography and/or (non-)invasive measurements like ‘standard’ ICU vital signs monitoring will also be included.

Context

Patients in all settings of paediatric critical care will be included.

Search strategy

A three-step approach is used to identify all eligible literature. The initial strategy will be developed in PubMed using key articles to identify text words and index terms. Recall will be tested and optimised. This strategy will be adapted for the other databases. This search will then be conducted in PubMed, Embase (via embase.com), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and Web of Science Core Collection. Search strategies for all databases are added as online supplemental material. Complementary to the database searches, references of eligible studies will be screened to identify additional relevant studies. The clinical trial registries ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform will be searched for protocols of ongoing studies.

Source of evidence selection

Search results will be deduplicated in EndNote15 and imported into Rayyan for screening.16

The screening will be conducted in two main steps. During the first step, two reviewers (RH, JH) will screen titles and abstracts independently using predetermined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements will be discussed, and if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer (MK) will be consulted.

In the second step, full texts of potentially eligible studies will be retrieved. The same two independent reviewers will assess these for eligibility using the same predetermined criteria. Disagreements will again be resolved through discussion, involving the same third reviewer if necessary.

Data extraction

Each study will be charted using a data extraction table designed in Microsoft Excel. Any refinements made during the process will be explained in the scoping review report. The following key study characteristics will be extracted: author(s), year of publication, study design including population size, subjects (age and division into three categories: acute respiratory failure, post-cardiac surgery and other), intervention(s) (type of ventilation adjustments), type of outcome measurement (and reporting technique).

This scoping review will include different study types; therefore, based on the included studies, an appropriate quality assessment will be selected and applied if appropriate. Extraction methods used to make this quality assessment will be explained in the final review.

Data analysis and presentation

To ensure the quality of our work as well as to provide transparency, the work protocol and results will be tested against the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PSIMA-ScR).17 The number of studies mapped to each characteristic will be summarised by numerical counts, and the data extraction table will be presented. Critical appraisal scores of the included studies will be tabulated. Outcome measure findings will be descriptively synthesised. If there will be availability of identical outcomes and sufficient homogeneity, data will be pooled and group analysis will be carried out. Findings may also be mapped by a tabular presentation to provide an overview of the outcomes investigated in the included studies. Furthermore, research gaps in the existing literature will be identified and recommendations for future research in this field will be made

Nevertheless, the development of the framework will be an iterative process that will be refined according to what will emerge while conducting the review.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board is not required for this study since all investigations are being carried out on previously published manuscripts. There will be no involvement of new patients or other study participants of any kind.

The review will be registered and the protocol published in order to maintain methodological transparency.

Final results will be compiled and submitted for publication once the study has been completed and all data has been charted/analysed.
