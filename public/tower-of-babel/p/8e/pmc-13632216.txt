Psychiatric nurses’ work experiences in non-psychiatric wards in light of their specialized competencies: a qualitative content analysis
Authors: Fatemeh Taheri, Seyyed Abolfazl Vagharseyyedin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs mental health professionals, psychiatric nurses have the knowledge and skills to play a key role in responding to the psychological needs of physically ill patients. However, their presence in non-psychiatric wards is often accompanied by role ambiguity and limited use of specialized competencies. The aim of this study is to explain the experiences of psychiatric nurses concerning the role of their specialized competencies on their work experiences in non-psychiatric wards.MethodThis qualitative study employed a conventional content analysis approach. Data were collected between February and December 2024 through unstructured interviews with 16 psychiatric nurses working in non-psychiatric wards of different hospitals in Iran. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection using the qualitative content analysis method described by Graneheim and Lundman (2004).ResultsThree main categories and ten subcategories were identified: (1) “Usefulness of mental health nursing skills in physical care alongside the perceived need for improvement” with subcategories “clinical application of mental health nursing skills”, “early identification and anticipation of psychological outcomes”, and “perceived need for empowerment in applying mental health nursing skills”; (2) “Enhancement of interpersonal and interprofessional interactions” with subcategories “improved interaction with the treatment team”, “establishing more effective communication with patients”, “attention to nonverbal skills and empathy in communication”, and “re-recognition of the importance of psychiatric nurses’ roles”; and (3) “Expansion and deepening of the professional role” with subcategories “generalization of mental health nursing skills to personal life and individual growth”, “performing informal advisory and supportive roles in the ward”, and “achieving a holistic approach to care”.ConclusionsPsychiatric nurses in non-psychiatric wards utilize their specialized competencies by integrating mental health care into physical care, enhancing therapeutic and interprofessional communication, and developing their professional roles. These findings also highlight the need for further development of practical competencies in non-psychiatric settings.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05016-w.Keywords: Psychiatric nursing, Clinical competence, General ward, Qualitative research

Introduction

Mental health nursing has been recognized in recent years as one of the key specialties in the health field, and its importance in promoting mental health has increasingly gained attention. This field has undergone considerable transformation over the years, and the scope of mental health nurses’ responsibilities now ranges from direct patient care to promoting community mental health and developing preventive approaches [1]. The expansion of attention to mental health in hospital and non-hospital settings has led to the role of nurses with psychiatric specialization no longer being limited to specialized units; they are also recognized in general units such as medical, surgical, pediatric, and emergency wards as a resource for behavioral assessment, crisis management, and facilitating interprofessional care [2, 3]. Specialized education equips psychiatric nurses to address the mental health needs of patients with various physical health conditions [4].

The integration of mental health services into general care settings has been recognized as a vital component of comprehensive patient management [5]. Patients hospitalized in non-psychiatric units often experience problems such as psychological distress, anxiety, depression, and adjustment difficulties related to physical illness [6]. Psychiatric nurses, by utilizing their skills in assessment, diagnosis, and evidence-based interventions, play a central role in identifying and responding to these needs [7]. Their presence ensures the provision of integrated care that addresses both physical and psychological dimensions, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and increasing patient satisfaction [8].

The professional foundation of mental health nursing is built upon having in-depth knowledge of mental health disorders, therapeutic communication skills, crisis intervention strategies, psychotherapeutic techniques, and familiarity with psychopharmacology [9]. These competencies enable psychiatric nurses to effectively assess patients’ mental health status, develop individualized care plans, and implement evidence-based interventions [10]. In addition, they facilitate effective collaboration with multidisciplinary teams, thereby enhancing continuity and coordination of care [11]. Empirical evidence suggests that the involvement of psychiatric nurses in non-specialized settings can improve care quality and reduce the length of hospital stay [12].

However, the presence of psychiatric nurses in non-psychiatric settings is accompanied by challenges. One of the most significant challenges is the limited awareness of other healthcare professionals regarding mental health issues. Moreover, psychiatric nurses sometimes encounter resistance or skepticism from colleagues about their role. Additionally, when moving between psychiatric nursing specialization and traditional care practices in non-psychiatric environments, ambiguity or role conflict may arise [13–15].

The motivations of mental health nursing master’s graduates who choose to work in non-psychiatric units are diverse. Some graduates select these units due to their interest in applying their skills in more diverse and dynamic environments, while others are motivated by their enthusiasm to support mental health and the opportunity to influence broader systemic changes [16].

In Iran, psychiatric nurses who attain a Master’s degree in psychiatric nursing often find employment in non-psychiatric hospital wards. The organizational and clinical environments in these settings differ considerably from those of specialized psychiatric facilities. While this transition allows nurses to apply their specialized mental health competencies in general healthcare contexts, it simultaneously introduces challenges related to role adaptation and professional integration. Nevertheless, there is a paucity of research concerning how psychiatric nurses perceive and utilize these competencies within the Iranian healthcare system.

Given the limited understanding of this phenomenon, qualitative inquiry is particularly valuable for exploring psychiatric nurses’ perspectives and experiences in depth. Although a small number of qualitative studies have examined psychiatric nurses’ experiences in non-specialized settings, most have been conducted in Western healthcare systems. Consequently, evidence regarding how psychiatric nurses experience these roles in different cultural and organizational contexts, including Iran, remains limited. Exploring these experiences may provide insights for workforce planning, nursing education, and the effective utilization of psychiatric nursing competencies in general healthcare settings. Therefore, this study was undertaken to explain the experiences of psychiatric nurses in relation to the role of their specialized competencies on their work in non-psychiatric wards.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted using a qualitative method with a conventional content analysis approach based on the framework of Graneheim and Lundman (2004). This approach enables the researcher to extract categories and concepts directly from the data without applying a predetermined theoretical framework or pre-established classifications [17]. In addition, this method allows researchers to gain a deeper understanding of the factors influencing psychiatric nurses’ professional roles and the outcomes related to patient care by examining their perspectives, challenges, and coping strategies [18, 19]. Such an understanding can help identify barriers and opportunities, develop targeted educational programs, improve interprofessional collaboration, and formulate effective policies for integrating mental health services into general care. This study is reported in accordance with the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guideline.

Settings

The study was conducted between February and December 2024 in non-psychiatric wards of governmental hospitals located across different regions of Iran. These included internal medicine, surgery, emergency, neonatal intensive care, adult intensive care, dialysis, and cardiac units, which are typically environments where psychiatric nurses interact with multidisciplinary teams, including general and specialist physicians, general nurses, psychologists, and social workers, and were therefore selected as the research settings.

Participants

The participants included 16 nurses with a Master’s degree in psychiatric nursing who were working in non-psychiatric wards in various cities of Iran in 2024. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was used to cover a range of characteristics such as work experience, type of ward, and geographical location of service. Inclusion criteria were having a Master’s degree or higher in psychiatric nursing, at least one year of work experience in a non-psychiatric ward, and willingness to participate in the study and share experiences. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue participation during the interview, inability to provide relevant experiential information, withdrawal of cooperation, or the occurrence of behaviors or conditions that prevented continuation of the interview (e.g., aggressive behavior or severe emotional distress). Data collection procedure

Data were collected through in-depth, unstructured interviews conducted either face-to-face or, in some cases, online due to the geographical dispersion of participants. Interviews were conducted by the first author, a PhD candidate in psychiatric nursing with prior experience in qualitative interviewing. The researcher had no prior professional relationship with the participants. The interview guide was designed based on the research objective and included open-ended questions about nurses’ experiences of the impact of their specialty orientation on their professional roles in non-psychiatric settings. The main question was: “How has your specialization in psychiatric nursing influenced the way you perform your duties and roles in the ward where you work?” Follow-up questions were then asked to obtain more information and clarify the nurses’ statements, with participants’ responses guiding the direction and content of these questions. The interview guide for this study is submitted as Supplementary file1(S1). Interviews were conducted at the workplace or at a location agreed upon by the participant and, with their consent, were fully audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim immediately after each session. Interview duration ranged from 45 to 60 min, with a mean duration of 52 min.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection and was based on the qualitative content analysis method of Graneheim and Lundman (2004). First, the interview transcripts were read several times to enable the researcher to become familiar with the overall content of the data. Then, meaning units related to the study objective were identified and rewritten in shorter phrases. These units were extracted as conceptual codes, and similar codes were grouped into subcategories and then into main categories. Finally, the content of each category was described and labeled [17]. MAXQDA software version 2022 was used to organize the data. Coding and categorization were reviewed by the research team, and any differences were discussed until consensus was achieved.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of the findings, the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985) including dependability, credibility, confirmability, and transferability were applied [20]. To enhance credibility, participants reviewed summaries of the preliminary findings and confirmed that the interpretations reflected their experiences. In addition, peer checking by two PhD nursing students and review by the research team members were carried out. To ensure confirmability, all documentation, including audio files, transcripts, and analytical notes, was maintained throughout the study. Furthermore, reviewing the analysis process by the research team helped reduce individual bias and increase the stability of the results. To ensure transferability, a rich and detailed description of the study context and conditions was provided.

Results

The participants in this study consisted of 16 mental health nurses from different cities in Iran. These nurses were working in various wards, including ICU, emergency, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, internal medicine, and other specialized units, and their work experience ranged from 2 to 23 years.

Analysis of the interview data led to the identification of three main categories and ten subcategories, which explain different dimensions of their experiences of role performance in non-psychiatric settings. These categories were: (1) effective application of mental health nursing skills in physical care alongside the perceived need for improvement, (2) enhancement of interpersonal and interprofessional interactions, and (3) expansion and deepening of the professional role. In the following sections, each category, along with its subcategories and sample participant quotations, is presented.

Usefulness of mental health nursing skills in physical care alongside the perceived need for improvement

The participating mental health nurses believed that mental health nursing skills and approaches have been highly useful in caring for patients with physical conditions. These skills improved the quality of communication, reduced patients’ stress, and enhanced the quality of physical care. However, participants also pointed to educational gaps and the need to strengthen psychotherapeutic skills.

“Clinical application of mental health nursing skills,” “early identification and anticipation of psychological outcomes,” and “perceived need for empowerment in applying mental health nursing skills” are the three subcategories of this category.

Clinical application of mental health nursing skills

Participants reported that mental health nursing skills, including communication techniques and stress management, directly influence physical care; in other words, these skills provide a foundation for carrying out physical treatments with greater patient acceptance and cooperation.

I talk to patients about their concerns and thoughts, and based on their condition, I use stress management techniques to help them feel calmer. (P3)

I had a patient with delirium that no one could communicate with. By reorienting him to time and place and gradually building a rapport, I gained his trust to the point where he would cooperate in taking his medication himself. (P14)

Early identification and anticipation of psychological outcomes

Psychiatric nurses reported that one of the prominent functions of their specialization is the ability to identify hidden psychological symptoms or those arising from physical illness. Participants indicated that this diagnostic ability allows them to anticipate the patient’s emotional trajectory or adjustment to treatment and to provide timely psychological interventions or referrals to relevant specialists.

If I see a patient showing psychiatric symptoms, I’m more vigilant with them, because they might exhibit dangerous behavior. Especially when signs of delusion are observed; this is very important and needs to be investigated sooner. (P4)

Sometimes a patient appears physically fine, but from the way they speak or the look in their eyes, I can tell they’re anxious inside. These signs might not be obvious to others, but for us who have worked with psychiatric patients, they are very meaningful. (P7)

Perceived need for empowerment in applying mental health nursing skills

Despite evidence of the effective application of their skills, most participants emphasized the lack of practical training and the need to strengthen psychotherapeutic competencies. They stated that the existing theoretical courses at the graduate level are sometimes insufficient to handle complex clinical situations and that practical workshops and simulation-based training are essential.

In our courses, we mostly studied psychotherapy techniques theoretically, but if the training was more practical and in-depth, it would be much more useful to us when dealing with patients and their families, especially mothers. (P1)

If a psychiatric nurse learns psychotherapy skills in a practical way and is trained in a way that they can actually perform therapy, they can be much more effective. Unfortunately, most of our training has been theoretical. (P3).

Enhancement of interpersonal and interprofessional interactions

Participants noted that psychiatric nursing specialization significantly improved the quality of their professional and interpersonal interactions, including those within the treatment team and with patients and their families.

“Improved interaction with the treatment team,” “establishing more effective communication with patients,” “attention to nonverbal skills and empathy in communication,” and “re-recognition of the importance of psychiatric nurses’ roles” are the four subcategories of this category.

Improved interaction with the treatment team (physicians, colleagues, supervisors)

Participants stated that their mental health-oriented approach enhanced their professional position within the treatment team. They felt that by applying psychiatric nursing knowledge in clinical situations, they could play a more effective role in patient-related decision-making. This approach led physicians and other team members to consider psychiatric nurses’ perspectives as part of the treatment process and use their input in clinical decisions. Additionally, participants reported that the mental health-oriented approach improved professional interactions within the team and increased collaboration among colleagues.

When the doctor comes for rounds, I tell him, ‘Doctor, could it be possible for the injection you want to administer not to be intramuscular, and instead be given intravenously?’ At first, he doesn’t agree, but when I explain to him that the patient’s stress, discomfort, and insomnia last night were because of it, believe me, he listens to what I say. (P3)

I used to pay less attention to how I communicated with my colleagues, but now I try to be more deliberate and proactive in my interactions with them, and this change has made them take my opinions and suggestions more seriously. (P11)

Establishing more effective communication with patients

Participants reported that applying active listening skills, providing appropriate feedback, and using simple language increased patient trust and improved their cooperation in medical care. They noted that this supportive interaction reduced patient resistance to treatment and facilitated the delivery of care.

The way I interact with patients is completely different from my colleagues… For example, we had a patient with a history of addiction and severe paranoia. My colleagues were complaining that he wouldn’t take his medication, but I would explain to him in detail what purpose each medication served. For instance, I’d say, ‘This is an antibiotic I’m giving you…’ This is how I would gain his trust. (P7)

Most patients don’t easily establish communication, but by repeating their sentences, active listening, and making eye contact, I was able to gain their trust and establish more effective communication with them. (P5)

People come into the emergency room, and really, everyone wants their own work done immediately and they have very little patience. In such situations, I try not to argue with the patient. I listen to their concerns and reassure them in a way that conveys their anger is natural and is because of their illness process. I tell them, ‘You have the right to be worried about your treatment and to want your work done as quickly as possible, or to receive the care you desire. However, given the emergency room conditions, we attend to each patient one by one. If you can be a little more patient, we will definitely get to you. (P2)

Attention to nonverbal skills and empathy in communication

A prominent aspect of participants’ experiences was the importance of nonverbal skills (eye contact, body language, silence) and empathy in communication. Participants identified mastery of nonverbal communication and deep empathy as one of the most significant impacts of their specialization on interactions with patients across all units.

I look at patients and try to understand: why is this person, who is so consumed with worry, tearing themselves apart like this? Are they anxious? Are they in pain? Or, take someone who keeps pulling their hair out—why are they doing that? Or when they’re talking to me and moving their hands up and down, what is their body language actually telling me? (P8)

When interacting with patients, I used skills such as empathy, reflection of feelings, and opening communication to establish a more effective connection. This would lead to patients trusting me and cooperating better in their treatment. (P15)

Re-recognition of the importance of psychiatric nurses’ roles

Participants reported that after some time working in non-psychiatric units, the treatment team members, patients, and families gradually recognized the unique and influential role of psychiatric nurses in improving communication and calming the ward environment. This recognition sometimes manifested through colleagues assigning more complex responsibilities and, at other times, through physicians relying on their advice.

I felt that with older patients… we had about three or four cases like this. I feel that whenever a patient shows psychiatric symptoms or starts talking a bit incoherently, my colleagues say, ‘You’re a psychiatric nurse and you’re familiar with these patients; so you take care of them. (P9)

Once, I told the physician, ‘This patient is delusional, and it’s a specific type of delusion.’ Then the physician came and prescribed medication for him. Of course, the patient was about to be discharged, but he still prescribed psychiatric medication for him. (P8)

Expansion and deepening of the professional role

Experiences of the psychiatric nurses indicated that their knowledge and skills play a significant role in enhancing their professional capabilities and personal growth.

“Generalization of mental health nursing skills to personal life and individual growth,” “performing informal advisory and supportive roles in the ward,” and “achieving a holistic approach to care” are the three subcategories of this category.

Generalization of mental health nursing skills to personal life and individual growth

Participants reported that the skills acquired during their specialization had effects beyond the work environment, leading to better management of personal relationships, increased self-awareness, and regulation of personal emotions. This internal transformation, in turn, positively influenced their work performance and enhanced empathy and professional resilience.

Studying psychiatric nursing doesn’t just help me in the emergency department; it’s also helpful in my personal life. Even when I’m crossing a busy intersection or walking past pedestrians on the sidewalk, it still helps me. It’s like it somehow takes care of your own mental health too. (P2)

My anger management skills are much higher than my colleagues, and understanding patients in different situations is much easier for me. All of this ensures that the care you need to give to the patient isn’t delayed. (P6)

Performing informal advisory and supportive roles in the ward

Psychiatric nurses frequently highlighted their informal advisory role for colleagues and patients, which included listening to concerns, providing short-term psychological guidance, and supporting them in coping with family and work-related stress.

When I’m on shift, my colleagues come to me even more than the patients do. As I go from bed to bed, I keep my wits about me and I see the children, and I can tell if one of them is stressed or anxious. So many of our children bite their nails down to the quick, especially the ones hospitalized here. I can diagnose the anxiety and I know what to do; with stress, it’s the same I know how it should be managed. (P3)

It’s happened many times that colleagues want to confide in me as a psychiatric nurse. Many times, they’ve switched their shifts so we could be alone, or even after a colleague has left our unit, they’ve called many times and said, ‘Just talking to you lessens my psychological pressure a little and I can make decisions better.’ For this reason, my colleagues usually call me a lot. Some patients know my degree and even have my number; when they’re feeling really unwell or have a family problem, they call the unit or my personal number, and we talk, and their problem gets resolved. (P10)

Achieving a holistic approach to care

Participants emphasized that psychiatric nursing specialization promotes a comprehensive approach to care, simultaneously addressing patients’ physical, psychological, and social dimensions. They believed this holistic approach enables the identification of secondary but determining issues (e.g., accompanying anxiety, lack of social support, or adjustment problems) and leads to the design of more comprehensive care plans.

A woman with a diagnosis of brucellosis constantly complained of chest pain. After performing an ECG and other assessments, the physician announced that there was no specific problem and that the cause of her discomfort was probably stress and anxiety. I tried to talk with her and learned that her husband is always at work and she has a two-to-three-year-old child who has been left with her mother-in-law. She said that her mother-in-law takes good care of the child, but she is still worried about her child. I told her that she had the right to feel this way and tried to help her regain a sense of calm. After that, during rounds with the physician, I brought up these concerns. After examining her, the physician said, ‘Aha, so this is what has been causing your discomfort. I will try to expedite your treatment so you can return home sooner and be with your child.’ This experience was precisely something I had not seen in my other colleagues; I hadn’t even seen about 3–4% of my colleague’s pay attention to other aspects of the patient’s condition or dig a little deeper. (P4)

In my opinion, nursing is not just about attending to the patient’s physical condition; paying attention to their moods and concerns is also very important. (P15)

Other nurses usually pay less attention to the psychological aspects of patients. For example, when a patient with diabetes comes in, the focus is mostly on blood tests, FBS, HbA1c, and reporting to the physician. But I, as a psychiatric nurse, see these things and try, in the course of care, to establish a bit more communication with the patient to see if they have any other concerns or not. (P9)

Discussion

This study aimed to explain the experiences of psychiatric nurses regarding the role of their specialized competencies on their work experiences in non-psychiatric wards.

The findings revealed that a specialization in psychiatric nursing elevates the role of these nurses in non-psychiatric settings from a merely physical-care level to a holistic, relationship-oriented level accompanied by professional development. Based on data analysis, three main categories were extracted: " Usefulness of Psychiatric Nursing Skills in Physical Care alongside Perceived Need for Enhancement,” “Enhancement of interpersonal and interprofessional interactions,” and “Expansion and deepening of the professional role,” along with ten subcategories, which reveal the multi-layered dimensions of psychiatric nurses’ role enactment in non-psychiatric units. The findings provide context-specific insights from Iran into how psychiatric nursing competencies are enacted in general hospital settings and contribute to a broader understanding of mental health nursing roles across diverse healthcare systems. The present results align with the international literature on the integration of mental health nurses and Mental Health Nurse Consultants in general hospitals. This body of literature emphasizes the early identification of psychiatric problems, interprofessional consultation, and improved patient outcomes [21].

Usefulness of psychiatric nursing skills in physical care alongside perceived need for enhancement

Findings showed that participants utilized skills such as therapeutic communication, stress management, psychological assessment, and prediction of psychological outcomes in caring for patients with physical illnesses. They believed that these skills increase patient cooperation, reduce risky behaviors, and enhance the quality of physical care. These results are consistent with studies emphasizing the valuable role of psychiatric nurses in general clinical settings, where they improve patients’ access to specialized mental health services through practical and care-oriented interventions [22].

Results related to the subcategories of “Clinical application of psychiatric nursing skills” and “Early identification of psychological outcomes” indicated that the presence of psychiatric nurses enhances patient acceptance of medical treatments and cooperation. These findings are consistent with Wand et al. (2010), who showed that psychiatric nursing interventions in the emergency department can reduce the need for physical restraints [23]. Furthermore, the ability to detect early psychological signs enables psychiatric nurses to anticipate patients’ emotional trajectories and implement timely interventions, a competency recognized as a core skill in psychiatric nursing [24].

However, participants highlighted the “need for empowerment” and lack of practical training in psychotherapy, pointing to a significant gap in the educational system. Research evidence suggests that nurses working in non-psychiatric wards often feel inadequately prepared to meet patients’ mental health needs [25]. Even psychiatric nurses require supplementary training in areas such as crisis management, legal considerations, and community-oriented roles to perform effectively in new environments [26]. Continuous education for providing psychological and spiritual care has been identified as a fundamental need in all clinical settings [27]. Participants also emphasized the gap between theoretical instruction and practical application, which aligns with Duyilemi (2025), who reported that academic psychiatric nursing training is often not optimized for high-pressure non-psychiatric environments, leaving nurses challenged by complex physical–psychological interactions [28].

Integrating evidence-based educational programs, including short-term psychotherapy training for physically ill patients with psychological needs (e.g., anxiety management, chronic disease adaptation, and family crises), could enhance the effectiveness of psychiatric nurses’ roles in non-psychiatric wards [21].

Enhancement of interpersonal and interprofessional interactions

The findings showed that psychiatric nursing specialization improves nurses’ interactions with patients, families, and healthcare teams. Participants reported that their mental health-oriented approach led to their perspectives being taken more seriously in clinical decision-making, and that other team members actively sought their support in complex behavioral and emotional situations. This finding is consistent with systematic reviews showing that the presence of mental health nurses in general settings increases staff trust, reduces interprofessional tensions, and enhances collaboration within multidisciplinary teams [29].

Using advanced communication skills, empathy, and attention to nonverbal cues, psychiatric nurses were able to gain patients’ trust and enhance their participation in treatment processes. Therapeutic communication, a core component of nurse–patient interaction in mental health, has been shown to be highly valuable and transferable to other clinical settings [30]. Studies indicate that specialized communication skills are a key competency of psychiatric nursing in general wards [31].

Acceptance of psychiatric nurses’ perspectives by physicians and other team members reflects recognition of their professional importance, leading to enhanced professional status and more effective participation in clinical decision-making. This finding aligns with research highlighting the role of psychiatric nurses in facilitating interprofessional collaboration and supporting staff in caring for complex patients [22]. Evidence also shows that active participation of mental health specialists in general settings can improve staff attitudes toward psychiatric patients, reduce fear and stigma, and increase the willingness to provide more humane care [32]. whereas general nurses often face challenges in effectively communicating with patients with psychiatric problems [33, 34].

Expansion and deepening of the professional role

The study’s findings revealed that the impact of psychiatric nursing specialization extends beyond the workplace to deeper levels of professional identity, personal growth, and individual role performance. In the subcategory “Generalization of psychiatric nursing skills to personal life and individual growth,” participants indicated that education and experience in mental health enhanced self-awareness, emotional regulation, conflict management, and interpersonal relationships in their personal lives, which indirectly increased resilience and professional sustainability in high-pressure work environments. Correspondingly, literature on mental health nursing and holistic nursing emphasizes self-care, reflective practice, and emotional regulation as essential for preventing burnout and maintaining professional well-being, identifying the development of these capacities as integral to psychiatric nursing identity [35].

One of the most significant outcomes of psychiatric nursing specialization was achieving a “holistic approach” to care. Participants believed that psychiatric training helped them simultaneously consider the physical, psychological, and social aspects of patients. This person-centered, holistic approach forms the basis of contemporary mental health nursing, focusing on overall recovery and well-being [36, 37], while nurses in general wards often have limited opportunity for holistic care due to high workloads and focus on technical tasks [38]، According to the present study, psychiatric nurses were able to extend this perspective to physical care settings.

The “informal advisory and supportive” role for colleagues and patients represented another dimension of the expansion of psychiatric nurses’ professional role. This finding indicates that psychiatric knowledge and skills are not only valuable for patients but also serve as an important resource for the entire healthcare team, aligning with the role of liaison or consultant nurses in supporting staff in managing complex patients [22]. Finally, the generalization of skills to personal life and individual growth reflects the transformative nature of this specialization, which, by enhancing self-awareness and emotional regulation, strengthens the professional resilience of psychiatric nurses.

Limitations of the study

The qualitative nature of this study limits the generalizability of the findings. However, achieving data saturation and the consistency of the findings with existing literature support the trustworthiness and analytical rigor of the results. To facilitate participation and maintain sample diversity, some interviews were conducted online. Although this approach enabled in-depth data collection, limited access to certain nonverbal cues may have affected the interpretation of subtle communicative nuances. In addition, data were collected only from psychiatric nurses; therefore, future studies could include non-psychiatric nurses and other healthcare professionals to provide additional perspectives.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings indicate that specialized psychiatric nursing skills can be effectively applied in caring for patients admitted to non-psychiatric wards. The ability to establish therapeutic communication, identify psychological issues early, and provide brief supportive interventions improves patient cooperation and facilitates the physical treatment process. These results emphasize the need for systematic integration of mental health approaches into general care.

At the same time, participants’ perception of insufficient practical training highlights the necessity of revising nursing curricula. Developing in-service training and enhancing psychotherapy skills can increase psychiatric nurses’ capacity to manage complex clinical situations. Additionally, recognizing the role of psychiatric nurses within multidisciplinary teams facilitates their effective participation in clinical decision-making and contributes to the improvement of care quality.

Conclusion

Psychiatric nursing specialization expands nurses’ professional roles in non-psychiatric wards and can consequently enhance the quality of care. By adopting a holistic perspective and utilizing communication and assessment skills, psychiatric nurses operate beyond physical care, contributing to improved patient experience and team performance. However, full utilization of this capacity requires strengthening practical training and revising educational and organizational policies within the healthcare system.
