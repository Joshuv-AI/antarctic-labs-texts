ATRX loss in sarcomas is associated with dysregulated gene and transposable element expression, loss of DNA methylation, and worse survival
Authors: K. Demanelis, J. Leix, M. A. Burgess, B. A. Nacev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:ATRX (α-thalassemia/mental retardation syndrome X-linked) is a tumor suppressor and key regulator of transcriptionally repressive epigenetic states. We investigate the impact of somatic ATRX loss of function on gene expression, DNA methylation, and transposable element (TE) expression in common soft tissue sarcoma tumors, a family of rare cancers.Methods:Using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), we analyzed 234 tumors from patients with dedifferentiated liposarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, soft tissue leiomyosarcoma (LMS), uterine LMS, and myxofibrosarcoma. Corresponding clinical outcome and patient data, DNA sequencing, RNA sequencing, and 450K methylation data were integrated to assess ATRX-dependent features.Results:ATRX loss was associated with significantly altered gene expression with the greatest impact in LMS/uterine LMS with reduction of expression of 31 (33%) genes and 63 (67%) genes up-regulated (false discovery rate <0.05), consistent with a role for ATRX in transcriptional silencing. Methylation profiling identified 269 differentially methylated CpGs (false discovery rate <0.05), with a marked hypomethylation effect (88% of CpGs) across all sarcomas. ATRX loss was associated with loss of TE silencing with 97% (n = 93) of differentially expressed TEs up-regulated, indicating that ATRX loss contributes to a marked de-repression of TEs. The median overall survival in LMS patients was 81.0 versus 34.9 months for tumors with ATRX retention and loss (plog-rank = 0.0023).Conclusions:ATRX loss in sarcomas leads to DNA hypomethylation, increased expression of TEs, as well as transcriptional dysregulation affecting key oncogenic pathways. ATRX mutational status may serve as a potential biomarker for prognosis and therapeutic stratification. Future clinical trials investigating epigenetic therapies could offer novel treatment strategies for ATRX-deficient sarcomas.

INTRODUCTION

Sarcomas are a heterogeneous group of rare malignant tumors of mesenchymal origin, encompassing >100 histological subtypes with distinct clinical and molecular characteristics.1–3 Despite advances in molecular profiling, the mechanisms driving sarcomagenesis remain incompletely defined. Deeper investigation into molecular pathways underlying transcriptional dysregulation and epigenetic alterations is needed to uncover therapeutic targets.

ATRX, α-thalassemia/mental retardation syndrome X-linked, is a chromatin remodeling protein involved in histone H3.3 deposition, heterochromatin maintenance, and telomere stability.4 ATRX loss of function (LOF) alterations have been implicated in various cancers, including gliomas, neuroblastomas, and sarcomas.5–9 In sarcomas, somatic ATRX loss has been observed in approximately up to 30% of leiomyosarcomas (LMS)10 and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas (UPS),11 and less commonly in bone tumors.12,13 ATRX contains multiple functional domains including the SWI/SNF domain, which modulates chromatin remolding, and the ATRX-DNMT3-DNMT3L (ADD) domain, which regulates transcriptional silencing through DNA methylation.14,15

In mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPCs) and UPS models, loss of ATRX expression is linked to epigenetic dysregulation, leading to de-repression of transposable elements (TEs) and lineage-specific gene programs.16 ATRX loss also activates the alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) pathway, which is associated with poor prognosis in LMS.17–19 TEs influence gene regulation, immunity, and viral mimicry20–23; gliomas with ATRX loss display TE and ALT activation, altering the immune microenvironment.19 Sarcoma cell lines with ATRX loss show abnormal interferon signaling,24 suggesting ATRX status may shape immune responses.

ATRX expression has been assessed by immunohistochemistry to stratify outcomes, with preserved expression linked to improved survival.25 Prior datasets are limited, however, by subtype aggregation and insufficient covariate adjustment.25 Thus, the downstream molecular consequences of somatic ATRX loss in sarcomas remain unclear.

Here, we leveraged The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) SARC dataset12 to assess the molecular and clinical impact of somatic ATRX loss across multiple sarcoma subtypes. Integrative analyses of survival, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq), and genome-wide DNA methylation revealed that ATRX loss drives widespread DNA hypomethylation, tumor-relevant gene expression changes, and TE de-repression. ATRX loss correlated with significantly shorter survival, particularly in uterine LMS (uLMS), highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and opportunities for therapeutic strategies targeting global epigenetic perturbations.

METHODS

We analyzed sarcomas from TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas SARC cohort26 focusing on dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS), UPS, soft tissue LMS (LMS), uLMS, and myxofibrosarcoma (MFS). These histologies were chosen given their frequency and established prevalence of ATRX alterations (>10%). Patient-level, tumor-level, RNA-seq, and 450K methylation data were accessed via the Genomics Data Portal27 using TCGAbiolinks12,28–30 and TCGAretriever.31 Additional details for all sections of the methods are provided in the Extended Methods in the Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129.

Defining ATRX loss

ATRX LOF was defined as nonsense, frameshift, splice site mutations, structural deletions, or homozygous copy number losses, consistent with prior criteria.12,13 In total, 49 sarcomas had ATRX LOF alterations and were classified as having ATRX loss in our analysis (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). All other sarcomas were classified as having ATRX retained.

Clinical and tumor covariates

Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, and radiation treatment exposure. Tumor-level features included histology, anatomic site, mutational burden, and fraction of genome altered. Tumor size was available for 197 cases32 and categorized as <5 cm, 5–10 cm, or >10 cm. Survival outcomes included overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) utilizing vital status and progression, respectively, and corresponding time-to-event data from TCGA.

Gene expression analysis

RNA-seq data were available for 231 tumors. Preprocessing was conducted using edgeR,33,34 and genes that had low expression across sarcomas were removed [transcripts permillion (TPM) = 0 in ≥90% of samples]. Normalization was carried out using trimmed mean of M-values (TMM),33,34 and differential expression was assessed with limmavoom.35–37 Analyses were adjusted for age, sex, and subtype, and within LMS analyses, analyses included adjustment for uterine versus non-uterine site. The adjusted P values [i.e. false discovery rate (FDR)] were computed using the Benjamini—Hochberg approach, and genes with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant.38 Pre-ranked gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) were carried out using clusterProfiler against MSigDB Hallmark gene sets.39,40 Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and FDR-adjusted P values were reported. Over-representation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was tested among gene ontology (GO) biological processes terms with Fisher’s exact tests (FETs), restricted to terms containing 10–500 genes.41,42

DNA methylation

Illumina 450K data were processed with TCGA SeSAMe pipeline.43,44 After exclusion of poor-quality and cross-reactive probes, 289 058 CpGs remained. Beta-values were converted to M-values {log2 [beta/(1 − beta)]}, and site-specific associations with ATRX loss were evaluated using limma and adjusted for same covariates as described in the gene expression analyses.35,36 Enrichment of CpG relation to island and genomic features were assessed with FETs. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified with DMRcate.45 To evaluate cis-regulatory effects, linear models evaluated the associations between CpG methylation and gene expression (TPM, log2-transformed), and these models were adjusted for age, sex, and histology. Over-representation of significant genes (FDR < 0.05) was then analyzed for functional enrichment among GO biological processes terms to identify pathways impacted by ATRX-associated methylation changes.40–42

Transposable element expression

Using the data from a previous study by Kong et al.46 examining TE expression across TCGA samples, we extracted TE expression data (see Expanded Methods in Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). In total, 109 sarcoma samples had available TE expression data. TE expression values were reported in TPM and corresponded to 1206 TEs. We utilized the annotation file from the atena package in R47 (hg38) to determine information related to TE repeat class and family. The description of TE class and family assignments are provided in the Expanded Methods of the Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129. All analyses were conducted in limma35,36 to evaluate the impact of ATRX loss on TE expression and adjusted for covariates described in gene expression analyses.

Telomere biology

Telomere length (TL) estimates previously derived by Barthel et al.48 using whole genome and exome sequencing data from TCGA tumors and the TelSeq software49 were analyzed as tumor-to-normal TL ratios (log-transformed). Associations with ATRX loss were tested with linear models adjusted for age, sex, histology, and sequencing type. Previously derived telomerase signature scores using RNA-seq data from TCGA tumors were analyzed similarly.48 RNA-seq derived TERRA expression, dichotomized as positive or negative, was compared by ATRX status using FETs.

Immune infiltration

Immune cell proportions from TCGA PanImmune Atlas50 were derived using CIBERSORT.51 Analyses focused on cell types present in ≥10% of tumors. Proportions were compared by ATRX status using Mann—Whitney U tests, with stratification by sarcoma subtype.

Survival analysis

Survival outcomes (OS and PFS) were assessed using Kaplan—Meier (KM) curves and log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazard models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for age, sex, subtype, and tumor size; LMS/uLMS analyses also adjusted for uterine versus non-uterine site.

Analyses were carried out in R 4.3.3. Significance threshold was defined as FDR < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

Our analysis included 234 sarcoma tumors from patients (aged 20–90 years) diagnosed with soft tissue sarcoma subtypes: DDLPS, UPS, LMS, uLMS, and MFS (Table 1). Forty-nine (21%) sarcomas had predicted somatic loss of ATRX, consistent with other independent cohorts with somatic alterations or protein expression.10–12 Among sarcoma subtypes, UPS (n = 17/50, 34%) and uLMS (n = 9/29, 31%) had the highest frequency of ATRX loss (Figure 1A). The distribution of ATRX genomic alterations included LOF mutations (n = 31, 63%), structural variants (n = 3, 6%), and copy number deletions (n = 15, 31%) (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). No patient characteristics were associated with ATRX status. On average, a larger percentage of genome was altered in sarcomas with ATRX loss versus retained [mean = 45.9 (standard deviation = 21.5) and mean = 35.1 (standard deviation = 25.2), P = 0.0036].

ATRX loss in sarcomas is associated with subtype-specific transcriptional dysregulation

ATRX maintains heterochromatin structure and regulates gene expression.52,53 Given prior evidence of transcriptional dysregulation in ATRX-deficient MPCs,16 we evaluated transcriptome-wide changes in sarcomas. ATRX loss was associated with reduced ATRX expression across all subtypes (Figure 2A, pwilcox < 0.05). Adjusted analyses identified 27 DEGs overall (FDR < 0.05), with ATRX as the most significant association observed (log2-fold change (FC) = −1.08, 95% CI −1.41 to −0.75, P = 9.2 × 10−10, FDR = 1.9 × 10−5) (Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, Supplementary Data Table 1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).

Subtype-specific analyses revealed that no DEGs were identified with ATRX loss in DDLPS (FDR > 0.05), but 94 and 17 DEGs with ATRX loss in LMS/uLMS and UPS/MFS were identified (FDR < 0.05), respectively. Most DEGs in UPS/MFS (15/17) were up-regulated with ATRX loss, including SS18L1 (implicated in SWI/SNF dysregulation), WNK3 (cell survival), and MSI1 (stem cell marker) (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). In LMS/uLMS, 63 DEGs were up-regulated with ATRX loss, and these DEGs mapped to telomere maintenance (HNRNPA2B1, AURKB, EXOSC10), Wnt signaling (ARHGEF19, PTK7), and p53 signaling (NUAK1, AURKB, KDM1A) pathways; reduction of expression of DEGs notably mapped to innate immunity (DHX58, IFI35, TRIM21, APOBEC3C) pathways (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table S5, Supplementary Data Table 2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).

Among the Hallmark gene sets, GSEA identified global up-regulation of G2M checkpoint and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways, and reduction of expression of interferon and interleukin 6 (IL-6)-JAK-STAT signaling with ATRX loss across all sarcoma types (FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figures S1 and S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). ATRX loss in LMS/uLMS tumors was associated with up-regulation of MYC targets and unfolded protein response pathways while it was associated with reduction of expression of MYC targets in UPS/MFS, illustrating distinct subtype-specific transcriptional effects of ATRX loss (Figure 2D, Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).

Sarcomas with ATRX loss are characterized by genome-wide DNA hypomethylation

ATRX-deficient sarcomas are characterized by widespread DNA hypomethylation in vitro.14 Given the role of ATRX in maintaining chromatin states through DNA methylation,54 we examined methylation changes across the genome. We identified 269 CpGs differentially methylated by ATRX status (FDR < 0.05) (Figure 3A); 88% were associated with decreased methylation, indicating a preferential hypomethylating impact (Figure 3B, Supplementary Data Table 3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc. 2026.100129). ATRX-associated CpGs were enriched in CpG shores (pFET < 0.05), regions where methylation is less stringently maintained during DNA replication (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). Differentially methylated CpGs annotated to genes, including BMF, DRG2, and CCNI which regulate apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and proliferation, respectively (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).55–57 We identified 16 DMRs, including a region of hypomethylation within the DAXX gene body, previously known to complex with ATRX, and a region of hypermethylation in DRG2, previously linked to reduction of expression in ATRX-deficient sarcomas and ALT-associated tumors (Supplementary Data Table 4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).58

Subtype analysis revealed no associations between CpG-specific methylation and ATRX loss in DDLPS and UPS/MFS (FDR > 0.05); however, ATRX loss was strongly associated with differential methylation in LMS/uLMS (2243 CpGs, FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S4, Supplementary Data Table 5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129), with 91% of differentially methylated CpGs associated with decreased methylation. ATRX-associated CpGs were enriched in TE regions, consistent with hypomethylation-driven TE derepression,59 but depleted in genic regions, CCCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) binding, and DNase hypersensitive sites (DHS) (pFET < 0.05), suggesting ATRX loss preferentially disrupts methylation within repetitive elements (e.g. TE) rather than chromatin accessibility loci (e.g. DHS, CTCF) (Figure 3C). We identified 363 DMRs in LMS/uLMS, including in DAXX and fibroblast growth factor genes (FGF6, FGF23, FGF3, FGF21) (Supplementary Data Table 6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).

Among the differentially methylated CpGs associated with ATRX loss that annotated to a gene, 158 (122 unique genes) and 1138 (643 unique genes) CpGs corresponded to a gene with expression across all sarcomas and LMS/uLMS, respectively. Methylation at 74 and 653 CpGs was associated with corresponding gene expression among all sarcomas (Supplementary Data Table 7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129) and within LMS/uLMS (Supplementary Data Table 8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). These CpGs were more often positively correlated with expression (53% in all sarcomas; 86% in LMS/uLMS) and located in gene bodies (54% and 70%, respectively). Pathway analyses demonstrated enrichment in MHC protein complex assembly and RAS signaling across all sarcomas (Supplementary Data Table 9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129), while LMS/uLMS additionally showed enrichment in reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related metabolic processes (Supplementary Data Table 10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129).

Expression of TEs is enhanced in sarcomas with ATRX loss

Given our prior work demonstrating the association of ATRX loss with de-repression of TEs in sarcoma cell lines and mouse models,16 we analyzed TE expression in tumors focusing on the overall directionality of the effect of ATRX loss. Among 109 sarcomas with TE expression data,46 96 TEs were differentially expressed with ATRX loss in sarcomas (P < 0.05), with 97% (n = 93) up-regulated, underscoring the role of ATRX in TE silencing (Supplementary Data Table 11, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). TE dysregulation was most pronounced in LMS/uLMS and UPS/MFS with ATRX loss, where 134 and 176 TEs were differentially expressed (P < 0.05), respectively, with >99% up-regulated in sarcomas with ATRX loss (Figure 4A). Notably, TEs in tumors with ATRX loss among LMS/uLMS and UPS/MFS subtypes were more likely to have increased expression than decreased or unchanged expression (pbinomial < 0.0001). This effect was not observed in DDLPS.

TEs with expression associated with ATRX loss were enriched in long interspersed nuclear elements (LINE) and long terminal repeat (LTR) classes (pFET < 0.05) (Figure 4B) and in endogenous retrovirus (ERV) and LINE1/LINE2 families (pFET < 0.05) (Figure 4C), consistent with prior Atrx KO MPC studies showing de-repression of ERV and LINE-1 elements.16 In UPS/MFS, TEs with expression associated with ATRX loss were significantly enriched in LTR elements (pFET < 0.05), whereas TEs with expression associated with ATRX loss in LMS/uLMS were enriched in both LTR and LINE classes (pFET < 0.10) (Figure 4B). At the TE family level, UPS/MFS tumors with ATRX loss displayed a distinct overrepresentation of ERV and LINE1/LINE2 families (pFET < 0.001) (Figure 4C).

ATRX loss impacts immune infiltration in sarcomas

Using immune infiltrate estimates derived from CIBERSORT,51,60 we evaluated the association between ATRX status and proportion of immune cell types (Figure 5A, Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). Compared with sarcomas with ATRX retained, sarcomas with ATRX loss had lower median proportion of CD4+ T cells (0.12 versus 0.09, pwilcox = 0.032), specifically CD4+ T resting memory cells (0.09 versus 0.06, pwilcox = 0.046), and higher median proportion of M2 macrophages (0.39 versus 0.47, pwilcox = 0.018). The reduction in resting memory CD4+ T cells associated with ATRX loss may reflect impaired immune readiness and contribute to the immune-suppressive tumor microenvironment. Among subtypes, DDLPS with ATRX loss had a lower median proportion of CD4+ T cells (0.13 versus 0.08, pwilcox = 0.0074). LMS/uLMS sarcomas with ATRX loss (when compared with ATRX retained) had lower B cells (0.03 versus 0.01, pwilcox = 0.0065) and higher macrophages M0 (0.00 versus 0.09, pwilcox = 0.0015). The median proportion of regulatory T cells was lower in UPS/MPS with ATRX loss versus retained (0.02 versus 0.01, pwilcox = 0.015).

MicroRNA expression is dysregulated in sarcomas with ATRX loss

ATRX loss broadly impacts microRNA (miRNA) regulation. Expression of 34 miRNAs was up-regulated in sarcomas with ATRX loss while expression of three miRNAs was reduced, including hsa-mir-34a, hsa-mir-34c, and hsa-mir-150 (P < 0.05), consistent with the role of ATRX as an epigenetic silencer (Supplementary Figure S5, Supplementary Data Table 12, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). Both hsa-mir-296 and hsa-mir-197 reached an FDR = 0.05, and these miRNAs have been shown to associated with angiogenesis, a critical process for tumor growth and metastasis.61,62 When stratified by subtype, ATRX loss was associated with miRNA expression for 5, 27 (79.3% up-regulated), and 29 (92.6% up-regulated) transcripts in DDLPS, LMS/uLMS, and UPS/MFS, respectively (P < 0.05). Unique to LMS/uLMS, let-7e, let-7f-1, and let-7f-2 expression was increased in LMS with ATRX loss compared with those with ATRX retained (P < 0.001) potentially consistent with prior analysis of LMS TCGA samples,13 where miRNA expression patterns had distinctly lower expression in LMS/uLMS.

Telomere length is significantly longer in tumors with ATRX loss compared with those with ATRX retained

Prior studies have shown that ATRX or DAXX mutations promote ALT-mediated telomere elongation, resulting in longer telomeres than in wild-type tumors.10,48,63 We sought to evaluate the impact of ATRX loss on TL dynamics. In our analysis of the tumor to normal TL ratio, sarcomas with ATRX loss had significantly longer TL relative to normal tissue compared with ATRX-retained cases [ß = 0.29 (se = 0.14), P = 0.036], after adjustment for age, sex, subtype, and sequencing type (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). This effect was strongest among UPS/MFS [ß = 0.77 (se = 0.21), P = 0.0058]. Telomerase expression scores were not associated with ATRX status. Notably, ATRX loss conferred a 2.70-fold (95% CI 1.00—9.32-fold) higher likelihood of positive TERRA expression (pFET = 0.046); all UPS/MFS with ATRX loss expressed TERRA (pFET = 0.029). These findings align with prior evidence that TERRA at telomeres promotes instability and ALT-mediated telomere elongation in sarcomas.64

ATRX loss correlates with poor overall survival in leiomyosarcoma

ATRX loss was associated with shorter OS in LMS/uLMS [median survival of 81.0 versus 34.9 months for ATRX retained versus loss (plog-rank = 0.0023)] (Figure 5B, Supplementary Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). This association persisted after adjustment for age, sex, tumor size, and subtype (LMS/uLMS) (HR = 2.89, 95% CI 1.11–7.49, P = 0.030). Within LMS/uLMS, ATRX loss was associated with shorter PFS (median survival of 29.4 versus 7.0 months, for ATRX retained versus loss, respectively, plog-rank = 0.011) (Figure 5C). This association, however, was attenuated after adjustment for tumor size (pcox-PH = 0.22). The negative correlation with OS persisted in the uLMS cohort and no effect of ATRX status on PFS in LMS or uLMS subtypes after adjusting for age and tumor size (Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorc.2026.100129). These findings suggest that ATRX loss may serve as a prognostic indicator for worse OS in patients with uLMS.

ATRX loss exerted a modest protective effect on OS and PFS in DDLPS. ATRX loss was associated with longer OS in DDLPS [median survival of 84.7 versus 37.1 months for ATRX loss and retained (plog-rank = 0.028)]. This association, however, was attenuated in the Cox-proportional hazards model after adjusting for tumor size, age, and sex (HR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.06–1.11, P = 0.068). PFS was also longer in DDLPS with ATRX loss, median PFS 48.7 months, compared with those with ATRX retained, median PFS 18.7 months (plog-rank = 0.075). This association persisted after adjusting for tumor size, age, and sex (HR = 0.31, 95% CI 0.10–0.93, P = 0.036).

DISCUSSION

Soft tissues sarcomas exhibit ATRX loss in up to 30% of cases; however, the biologic consequences and therapeutic implications remain unclear. Here, we profiled the molecular and clinical landscape of ATRX loss across sarcoma subtypes using genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic data from TCGA. ATRX loss occurred in ~21% of cases, most frequently in UPS and uLMS, and was associated with subtype-specific transcriptional dysregulation, genome-wide DNA hypomethylation, and de-repression of TEs, particularly in LMS/uLMS and UPS/MFS. Tumors with ATRX loss demonstrated altered immune infiltration with reduced proportion of CD4+ T cells and increased proportion of M2 macrophages. Clinically, ATRX loss predicted worse OS in LMS/uLMS, but favorable outcomes in DDLPS, suggesting its context-dependent role in sarcoma tumor biology.

ATRX loss produced widespread transcriptional dysregulation, particularly of genes linked to tumor progression. ATRX loss was associated with increased global TE expression, consistent with the known role of ATRX in silencing TEs in MPCs.16 Widespread DNA hypomethylation accompanied ATRX loss, possibly contributing to transcriptional activation of genes and TEs. These findings emphasize the important role of ATRX in transcriptional silencing and the need for future mechanistic studies.

Mechanistically, ATRX-deficient MPCs show increased chromatin accessibility, including at Wnt-ß-catenin pathway genes, which acquire active chromatin marks in ATRX-deficient UPS.16 Consistent with that observation, Wnt/ß-Catenin pathway genes were transcriptionally up-regulated, suggesting ATRX loss could promote tumor plasticity and resistance to differentiation.65 Our prior work showed that UPS cells with ATRX loss were more sensitive to tegavivint, a ß-catenin antagonist, supporting WNT dependency in this context.16 Given that tegavivint is in clinical trials for other mesenchymal tumors (NCT04851119), WNT-targeted therapy merits exploration in ATRX-deficient sarcomas.

ATRX loss was associated with epigenetic dysregulation in the form of a genome-wide hypomethylating effect. Several differentially methylated genes, including BMF, DRG2, and CCNI, regulate apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and proliferation. Moreover, we observed reduction of expression of interferon signaling, consistent with prior UPS and MPC models,16 suggesting a compensatory mechanism to suppress innate immune activation downstream of TE expression. In LMS, ATRX loss correlated with decreased expression of innate immune regulators such as DHX58 and TRIM21. Their loss may blunt innate immune responses, fostering an immune-suppressive tumor microenvironment. These findings highlight the interplay between ATRX loss, TE depression, and immune modulation, suggesting therapeutic opportunities to restore innate immune signaling and enhance immunotherapy responsiveness.

Survival analysis revealed striking subtype-specific differences. In LMS/uLMS, ATRX loss was associated with significantly worse OS and PFS, particularly in uLMS, where ATRX loss is more prevalent. Whether this effect is absent in LMS due to distinct biology or limited sample size remains uncertain. In contrast, ATRX loss in DDLPS correlated with improved survival and lacked major transcriptional or methylation effects, underscoring subtype heterogeneity in ATRX function. Together, these data suggest ATRX loss serves as a context-dependent modifier of sarcoma biology, with divergent effects across subtypes.

While our findings provide valuable insight, it is important to acknowledge their limitations. Sample sizes were modest, reflecting the rarity of sarcoma, and our results require validation in larger cohorts. Heterochromatin profiling was not feasible in tumor samples, limiting integration with prior cell line studies. Methylation data were derived from 450K arrays rather than whole-genome profiling, which may restrict resolution of ATRX-associated methylation changes. Finally, single-cell RNA-seq could clarify how ATRX loss shapes immune cell populations within the tumor microenvironment.

Overall, ATRX loss impacts sarcoma biology through transcriptional dysregulation, DNA hypomethylation, TE depression, and modulation of immune signaling, with distinct consequences across subtypes. In LMS/uLMS, ATRX loss portends poor outcomes and highlights potential vulnerabilities in WNT signaling, ALT-mediated telomere maintenance, and innate immune suppression. Conversely in DDLPS, ATRX loss associated with improved survival, emphasizing the need for subtype-specific analyses. These results not only advance understanding of ATRX biology in sarcomas but also highlight therapeutic opportunities including epigenetic modulators, WNT/ß-catenin inhibitors, ALT-targeted approaches, and strategies to enhance immunogenicity. Future studies should investigate ATRX as a clinically actionable biomarker to refine treatment strategies in sarcoma.
