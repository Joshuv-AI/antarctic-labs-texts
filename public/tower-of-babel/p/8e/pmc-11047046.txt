Ambulatory investigation of palpitations, pre-syncope and syncope with AliveCor KardiaMobile and Wellysis S-Patch – a local joint working project
Authors: Sarah Birkhoelzer, Elena Cowan, Brijesh Anantharam, Peter Haworth, Kaushik Guha, Amanda Hurst, Stacey Brown, Milena Leo, Paul Kalra, Senthil Kirubakaran
Published: 2024

Introduction

Palpitations, pre-syncope and syncope are common presentations to cardiology. Frequently conventional Holter monitors or event recorders do not lead to symptom–rhythm correlation, therefore patients often present to the emergency services. The aims of this project were: (i) to demonstrate symptom–rhythm correlation with AliveCor KardiaMobile and Wellysis S-Patch, (ii) to demonstrate diagnosis for patients presenting with symptoms of palpitations, pre-syncope and syncope, (iii) to evaluate patient experience.

Materials and method

Patients referred to our Cardiology Department with symptoms of palpitations were offered an KardiaMobile 6L ECG for ambulatory monitoring. Patients uploaded ECG recordings via their smartphone to a software portal over a 3-month period, where the diagnosis made by the software algorithm was confirmed by the project team. Patients with symptoms of pre-syncope and syncope were offered an S-Patch for continuous ambulatory ECG monitoring over 21 days. Patient demographics, diagnosis, symptom–rhythm correlation, management and patient experience were evaluated.

Results and discussion

Out of 122 patients, 14% (17/122) were excluded as the device was not utilised either due to lack of symptoms or software concerns. Data of 105 patients (78 KardiaMobile, 27 S-Patch) were analysed. Participants' mean age was 54 years (range 17–89 years), 65% women, 96% white British, 43% did not take any medication. Palpitations was the presenting complaint in 76.2% (80/105), pre-syncope in 14.3% (15/105) and syncope in 9.5% (10/105). 40% (39/105) experienced symptoms a couple of times per year, 20% (20/105) monthly, and 40% (39/105) multiple times per month. Symptom–rhythm correlation was achieved in 80% (84/105). Sinus rhythm (SR) was detected in 63% (66/105), atrial fibrillation (AF) in 10.5% (11/105 – all KardiaMobile), supraventricular tachycardia in 10.5% (11/105, 10/11 with KardiaMobile) with subsequent ablation in 8.6% (9/105). Mean duration of symptom–rhythm correlation with KardiaMobile was 41 days, mean number of uploaded ECGs was 26. The S-Patch was in situ for 504 hours (21 days), providing analysable recordings with a mean length of 268 hours (11 days). Feedback was obtained in 88% (92/105). In most cases (78%), no issues were reported; 10% reported difficulties using software and 6.7% had problems with connectivity. 52% of patients using the S-Patch reported issues:skin irritation in 30% (8/27), resulting in premature termination of monitoring. The service was reported as very good or excellent in 75% (79/105) of cases.

Conclusion

The project demonstrates the feasibility of ambulatory ECG monitoring service with a high diagnostic yield and excellent patient satisfaction. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)-approved KardiaMobile has a good analysis algorithm and user-friendly software. Patients using the S-Patch reported difficulties in using the software more often, and skin irritation significantly limited its use beyond a few days.

Frequent detection of SR suggest potential for cost savings if these devices were used in the community and referrals to cardiology were reserved for those with detected rhythm abnormality. KardiaMobile offers a high yield of AF detection within 6 weeks. Early diagnosis of AF and subsequent anticoagulation can prevent stroke and subsequent costs associated with stroke admission and disability.
