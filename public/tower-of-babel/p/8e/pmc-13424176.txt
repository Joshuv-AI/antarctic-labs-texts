Label-free proteomics and single-cell mass cytometry analysis of 5 immune tissues from mice with artesunate administration reveal immunosuppression of artesunate via BST2 and MDSCs
Authors: Rongjian Hong, Aiting Wang, Yiyang Li, Xianting Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artesunate is a worldwide prevalent antimalarial medicine, which has also been demonstrated for anti-tumor, anti-virus and anti-autoimmune diseases. Yet the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Here, we examined immunological features of 5 immune tissues (spleen, bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes and peritoneal cavity) of mice with artesunate administration through label-free proteomics and high-dimensional single-cell mass cytometry (CyTOF). Label-free proteomics uncovered dramatic alterations for the interferon-I (IFN-I) induced proteins, especially BST2 and IFIT1 downregulation in spleen and bone marrow. CyTOF analysis further indicated that the percentage of MDSCs was significantly elevated in spleen and bone marrow. The follow-up in vivo LPS-induced inflammatory model confirmed that pretreatment with artesunate attenuated the inflammatory damage, and the expression of BST2 was decreased in the spleen of mice with artesunate administration. Together, our findings presented an immune landscape of multiple tissues of mice with artesunate administration. These datasets provide comprehensive biological resources to develop therapeutic strategies for inflammatory diseases.

Introduction

Artemisinin is a sesquiterpene lactone compound containing peroxide extracted from Artemisia annua. Artemisinin was recognized for the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine as an antimalarial drug with significant efficacy [1,2]. Artemisinin has multiple derivatives, mainly including artesunate, dihydroartemisinin and artemether. These derivatives further improve the bioavailability of artemisinin and the therapeutic effect for the antimalarial application [3,4]. Artesunate (ART) is an artemisinin derivative indicated for the initial treatment of severe malaria [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends ART as the first-line treatment for severe malaria [6,7].

Apart from being the first-line antimalarial medicine, ART has also been tested for other indications, such as anti-cancer and anti-virus treatments [8,9]. ART shows the potency on nerve injuries and immunoregulatory disorders [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Meanwhile, ART relieves the graft rejection [14]. Previous studies have reported that ART could inhibit the tumor cell expansion, invasion, and induce the tumor cell apoptosis for cancers that occur in the lung, liver and breast [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. ART was also reported to increase the titer of the rabies-neutralizing antibodies as an adjuvant to the rabies vaccine [21]. Although ART has been demonstrated to be effective for various indications, its progress to the clinic is rather slow since the systematic and in-depth knowledge of the pharmaceutical mechanisms for ART is still lacking. The comprehensive features of immune responses after artesunate administration remain poorly investigated.

To provide a more detailed insight into the nature of ART with a focus on the immunoregulation, here we demonstrated a systematic characterization of 5 major immune tissues (spleen, bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes and peritoneal cavity) after ART administration. We performed the label-free proteomics and the CyTOF to identify the alterations in protein level and immune cell composition. Based on the analysis, we further demonstrated that ART could attenuate the LPS (Lipopolysaccharide)-induced inflammation in vivo partially via downregulating the expression of BST2 (Bone marrow stromal antigen 2), one of the IFN (Interferon)-induced proteins.

Thus, our study not only demonstrated the alterations of multiple immune tissues at proteomics and immune cell composition after ART administration, but also provided data resources for the data-driven design of de novo therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Mice

In vivo studies were performed in BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old). Mice were obtained from Shanghai JieSiJie Laboratory Animal Company. Experimental procedures were performed according to protocols approved by the Laboratory Animal Center at Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Animal Ethical Approval No. 202101056 by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University).

Mouse procedures

For proteomic and CyTOF experiments, BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old) were randomly assigned into 4 groups: (G1) Control (n = 5); (G2) 1 mg/kg artesunate (n = 5); (G3) 10 mg/kg artesunate (n = 5); (G4) 100 mg/kg artesunate (n = 5). Mice were infused with mock (0.9% NaCl) or artesunate (Cat#MB7317, MeilunBio, dissolved in 0.9% NaCl) by intragastric administration for successive 5 days, respectively. The reason for ART dosage and time period: in order to conform to the clinical medication as much as possible, we selected the ART administration time period and dosage for modeling based on the dosage instructions (oral, 50 mg once, twice a day, for 5 consecutive days) of the already marketed ART tablets (National Medical Standard H10880055). The dosage for mice is calculated based on the body surface area method, which is approximately 13 mg/kg [22]. Based on this dosage, we set a 10 mg/kg ART dose group. In addition, to investigate the differences in ART dose gradients, we selected the low-dose ART group (1 mg/kg) and the high-dose ART group (100 mg/kg) at multiples of 10. Mock- and artesunate-administered mice were sacrificed on Day 6. Five tissues (spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, bone marrow and peritoneal cavity) were collected for proteomic and CyTOF analysis.

For the artesunate pretreatment experiment, BALB/c mice aged 8–10 weeks old were randomly assigned into 4 groups: (G1) Control (n = 5); (G2) LPS only (n = 5); (G3) 10 mg/kg artesunate pretreatment, then LPS (n = 5); (G4) 100 mg/kg artesunate pretreatment, then LPS (n = 5). Mice were intraperitoneally administered with mock or artesunate for 7 consecutive days before LPS (L2880, Sigma) injection. On day 8, LPS (3 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally into mice [23]. After 24 h, mice were measured for the clinical severity score and the body weight. The clinical severity scores were as follows: 1, healthy; 2, unsmooth fur; 3, bow back; 4, unopened eyes, difficulty in movement; 5, dead. Score 4 was used as the experiment endpoint. Then, mice were sacrificed. The spleen and the bone marrow were subject to specific protein expression analysis.

Sample preparation for proteomics

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion were performed as previously described [24]. Each mouse tissue sample was ground by syringe piston through a 40 µm cell strainer in a 6 cm plate with RPMI 1640 complete medium. The cell suspension was collected into a new 15 mL centrifuge tube. After centrifugation, cell pellets were resuspended in red blood cell lysing buffer to remove red blood cells. The remaining cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium through the 40 µm cell strainer. Then, cells were washed with PBS for 3 times. After centrifugation, cells were lysed by RIPA buffer (P0013K, Beyotime) containing PMSF and protease inhibitors (P1008, Beyotime), followed by sufficient vortexing on ice for 30 min. After ultrasonic fragmentation, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA total protein assay (Pierce). Protein was precipitated by acetone at −20 °C overnight. Centrifugation was performed at 20,000 g for 10 min. Protein was resuspended with 7 M guanidine hydrochloride and 38.5 mM DTT (Final concentration) at 55 °C for 1 h. Then, 87.7 mM Iodoacetamide (Final concentration) was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Proteins were transferred into 10 kd ultrafiltration tubes, followed by centrifuging at 14,000 g for 15 min. Protein was resuspended with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and mixed with trypsin in a ratio of protein to trypsin (50:1) and digested at 37 °C overnight. Then, 10 kd ultrafiltration tubes with peptides were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 20 min. Add 50 mM NH4CO3 to the 10 kd ultrafiltration tubes after centrifugation. The 10 kd tubes were centrifuged again at 12,000 g for 10 min. To terminate the trypsin reaction, we added 1% formic acid (FA) into the collection tubes. Finally, samples were desalinated by the GL Science Monospin C18 tip.

Proteomics

Peptides from Sp, LN and Thy (Fraction 1) and peptides from BM and PC (Fraction 2) were taken partially and mixed up to 100 µg, respectively, for fractionation by Pierce High pH Reversed-Phase Peptide Fractionation Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 84868). All peptides were freeze-dried. Before analysis, the dried peptide samples were re-dissolved with 0.1% FA for LC-MS/MS detected by Easy nLC1200/Q Exactive plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated in a C18 column (50 mM × 20 cm, 3 µm) as previously described [25]. The flow rate was 300 nL/min. The effective gradients were as follows: 0–3 min, 2–6% buffer B (80% ACN; 0.1% FA); 3–95 min, 6–20% buffer B; 95–107 min, 20–32% buffer B; 107–108 min, 32–100% buffer B; 108–120 min, 100% buffer B. DDA MS parameters were as follows: (1) MS: 350–1800 scan range (m/z); 70,000 resolution; 3e6 AGC target; 50 ms maximum injection time; the top 20 precursors (charge state ≥ +2) were broken by high-energy collision (HCD), with a standardized collision energy (NCE) of 28. (2) HCD-MS/MS: 17,500 resolutions; 1e5 AGC target; 45 ms maximum injection time; filter dynamic exclusion duration 30 s. DIA MS parameters were as follows: (1) MS: 350–1800 scan range (m/z); IT: 20 ms; 70,000 resolutions; 3e6 AGC target, set 35 variable windows (Supplementary Table 3); (2) HCD-MS/MS: 17,500 resolutions; 1e6 AGC target; IT: 60 ms.

Proteomics data analysis

DDA raw data, used for spectral library construction, were built in the search engine of Proteome Discovery (Version PD. 2.4, Thermo Scientific). The digestive enzyme was set to trypsin, and the maximum leakage was set to 2. The precursor mass tole and fragment mass tole were set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. The other parameters of the spectral library were set according to the software’s default settings. Peptides/proteins from Fraction 1 satisfying FDR ≤ 1% were used to build the final spectral library. The proteome spectral library was accessible in iProX. detailed in the “data availability” section. DIA raw data were identified using Spectronaut (Version 11.0, Biognosys AG). Mouse (organism ID 10090, 16968 reviewed entries) protein sequences were used in data analysis.

Single-cell preparation for cytometry analysis

Fresh tissues were taken from artesunate- or mock-administered mice. First, tissues were dissected thoroughly in the complete RPMI 1640 medium and collected into new centrifuge tubes on ice. The tubes with cell suspension were centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, the cell suspension was resuspended with red blood cell lysis buffer (C3702, Beyotime) for 1 min to remove red blood cells. We added the complete RPMI 1640 medium to terminate the reaction and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Afterward, the cells were resuspended with the complete RPMI 1640 and filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer into a new centrifuge tube. Single cells were ready for mass cytometry analysis. Before freezing, samples were stained with cisplatin and fixed with 3.2% PFA. After centrifuging at 600 g for 5 min, samples were frozen with 10% DMSO in the cell staining buffer (500 mL PBS, 0.02% NaN3, 2.5 g BSA).

Mass-tag barcoding

Samples were taken from −80 °C and transferred into flow tubes. After being washed with the cell staining buffer, cells were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (101330, Biolegend). To minimize inter-sample staining variation, we applied the strategy of mass-tag barcoding. First, we stained the samples of Sp and BM with different mass-tag CD45 antibodies and mixed the cells of Sp and BM from the same serial number mouse. Afterward, each sample was labeled with a unique barcode using the 20-Plex Pd Barcoding Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metal-tagged antibodies

All anti-mouse antibodies for CyTOF were listed in Supplementary Table 2. Pre-conjugated antibodies with BSA- and azide-free to metal isotypes were purchased from Abcam and Biolegend. We conjugated the antibodies with metal isotypes using Maxpar®X8 Antibody Labeling Kit (Fluidigm) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell surface staining for CyTOF

The cells were incubated in the surface antibody mixture for 30 min at room temperature and washed twice with the cell staining buffer. Then, cells were centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min.

Intracellular staining for CyTOF

The cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00–5523–00, Bioscience). Then, cells were incubated with the intracellular antibody mixture for 45 min at room temperature. Cells were washed twice and centrifuged at 800 g for 5 min. Finally, the cells were incubated with 125 nM Ir overnight at 4 °C.

CyTOF profiling acquisition

The CyTOF acquisition was performed as previously described [26,27]. Briefly, the stained cells were washed and resuspended with 10% EQ 4 element beads (Fluidigm) in deionized water. The filtered cells were acquired on a CyTOF instrument (Fluidigm) at an event rate of < 300 events per second.

CyTOF profiling analysis

The CyTOF profiling was normalized and debarcoded by the CyTOF software (7.0.5189.0). Cells were manually gated to eliminate the dead cells and doublets at the platform of Cytobank. Living single cells were analyzed by R packages (Cytokit, Rtsne).

Western blot (WB) analysis

Cells from Sp and BM were washed using PBS for 3 times. Then, cells were lysed by the RIPA buffer with PMSF and protease inhibitors. Proteins were quantified by the BCA assay kit and heated at 95 °C for 5 min in the loading buffer. We used 10% SDS-PAGE gels to separate the proteins. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane at 300 mA for 1 h. The membrane with proteins was blocked by 5% skim milk in TBS-Tween (TBST) for 1 h. Afterward, we incubated the membrane with primary antibodies including BST2 (1000x, ab272169, Abcam), IFIT1 (1000x, ab236256, Abcam), IRF3 (1000x, ab68481, Abcam), STAT1 (1000x, ab281999, Abcam) and β-actin (10000x, 30101ES50, Yeasen) respectively overnight at 4 °C. After washing 3 times in TBST, we incubated the membrane with 2nd antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and developed it with the Enhanced ECL Chemiluminescent Substrate Kit (36222ES60, Yeasen).

Proliferation assays

CD3+ T cells were obtained from healthy Balb/c mice and isolated by MojoSort Mouse CD3 T cell Isolation Kit (Biolegend, #480023). MDSCs were obtained from ART-10 mg/kg (by gavage, 5 days) administered mice and isolated by EasySep Mouse MDSC (CD11b+Gr1+) Isolation Kit (STEMCELL, #19867). Primary CD3+ T cells were labeled with 0.5 µM CFSE (Biolegend, 423801) for 20 min. Then, CD3+ T cells co-cultured with MDSCs at a ratio of 2:1 with Con A (2.5 µg/mL, eBioscience) stimulation were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium for 96 h. The fluorescence intensity gating in CD4+ or CD8+ T cells was assessed by a flow cytometer.

ROS assay

The splenic or the bone marrow cells from control (0.9% NaCl) or ART-10 mg/kg (by gavage, 5 days) administered mice were stained with PE-ROS in a 96-well plate for 45 min at 37 °C according to the instructions of the Cellular ROS Detection Kit (Orange fluorescence, Abcam#186028). Then, we analyzed the fluorescence using a microplate reader (BioTEK) and a flow cytometer.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed with PBS and blocked with Fc-blocker (100x) for 10 min. Then, cells were stained with antibodies for 15 min on ice. Except the PE-Cy7-Arg1 (Invitrogen, 25-3697-80) and PE-Cy7-Rat IgG2a Kappa isotype control (Invitrogen, 25-432181) with 1:200 dilution, other antibodies including PE-CD3 (Biolegend, 100307), APC-CD8a (Biolegend, 100711), PE-Cy7-CD4 (Biolegend, 100527), APC-Gr1 (Biolegend, 108411), FITC-CD11b (Biolegend, 101205), PE-CD45 (Biolegend, 103105) were diluted at 1:100. Finally, cells were washed and resuspended in 200 µL of CSB buffer and analyzed by a flow cytometer (NovoCyte Quanteon). For the Arg1 assay, we used the Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set (Thermo fisher, 88-8824-00) for intracellular staining.

Results

Overview of proteomics analysis of 5 tissues with ART administration

To better understand the immunomodulatory function of artesunate and the underlying mechanism, we infused the BALB/c mice with ART and collected 5 central or peripheral immune tissues (spleen, bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes and peritoneal cavity) for the label-free proteomics and the CyTOF characterization (Fig. 1A). For assessment of the dose-dependent alterations after artesunate administration, we classified 3 groups of artesunate-administered mice with 1, 10, 100 mg/kg artesunate treatment respectively, as the Group2–4 (G2–4, G1 as the control). Our liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based label-free proteomics identified an average of 5072 proteins in 5 tissues, while the spleen (Sp) got many more protein-groups than other tissues (Fig. 1B). Among the significantly differential proteins (SDPs, filtered by absolute log2 ratio ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05) of 5 tissues, lymph nodes (LN) got many more SDPs, especially the up-regulated SDPs. The bone marrow (BM) had many more down-regulated SDPs (Fig. 1C,D). The thymus (Thy) and the peritoneal cavity (PC) had fewer SDPs than other tissues (Fig. 1D). Compared with G2 and G3, G4 (ART 100 mg/kg) had many more SDPs (Fig. 1E). BM and PC, as the main innate immune tissues, shared 17 intersectional SDPs (Fig. 1F). Then, we performed the integrative GO enrichment analysis of the intersectional SDPs in BM and PC. We revealed profound alterations in response to virus, interferon-beta and interferon-alpha (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Fig. 1A,B). Meanwhile, we analyzed the SDPs in the adaptive immune tissues. Sp and LN shared many more intersectional SDPs, while Thy, Sp and LN shared fewer intersectional SDPs than only comparing Sp and LN. There were only 9 intersectional SDPs between Sp, LN and Thy (Fig. 1H). Besides the signaling pathway of response to virus, we also found the altered signaling pathway to erythrocyte development in Sp, LN and Thy (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Fig. 1C,D). Furthermore, there were only 3 intersectional SDPs in all 5 tissues and 28 intersectional SDPs in at least 3 tissues (Fig. 1J). Among the 28 intersectional SDPs, we found significantly altered pathways of response to virus and interferon-alpha (Fig. 1K, Supplementary Fig. 1E,F). Neutrophil degranulation, immune response to virus and interferon-beta were associated with ART administration in the splenic cells

To characterize the host response of each tissue induced by ART administration at the proteomics level, we next analyzed the proteomic profiling of each tissue. Herein, we further analyzed the proteomic dataset of the splenic cells in each group (Fig. 2A). Partial Least-Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) discriminated the splenic cells in control (G1) from the splenic cells in G2, G3 and G4 based on the abundance of detected proteins (Fig. 2B). G4 was identified with many more SDPs compared to G2 and G3, and most of these differential proteins (filtered by absolute log2 ratio ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05) in G4 were up-regulated. The number of SDPs increased with the ART dosage, especially the up-regulated proteins (Fig. 2C). The spleen displays ART-dose dependency at the proteomic level. To identify the biological processes and pathways impacted by the ART administration in the spleen, the enrichment analysis was performed based on the differential proteins. The Integrative Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for biological processes indicated high correspondence with neutrophil degranulation, immune response to virus and interferon-beta in ART-administered mouse splenic cells (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 2A,B). Moreover, we found tissue-specificity of the spleen in G3 and G4 based on the GO enrichment QC analysis of differential proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2C). In addition, we also found tissue-specificity of the bone marrow in the GO enrichment QC analysis based on the SDPs of splenic cells, which suggested that the spleen and the bone marrow may share correlative signaling pathways with the ART administration. There were 32 intersectional SDPs observed in G2, G3 and G4 (Fig. 2E). GO enrichment analysis of these 32 intersectional SDPs also pointed to the signaling pathway of response to virus and interferon-beta (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. 2D,E). Then we focused on the proteomic profiling of G4 with the highest dose of ART administration, which revealed more SDPs than G2 and G3, especially the up-regulated SDPs (Fig. 2G). Compared with G1, there were some notably up-regulated and down-regulated differential proteins in G4 (Fig. 2H,I). Particularly, some interferon-induced proteins, such as IFIT1, IFIT3 and BST2, were observed and profoundly down-regulated in ART (100 mg/kg)-administered splenic cells. The quantitative analysis was shown in Fig. 2J,K. BST2 and IFIT1 were notably down-regulated with ART administration, both in Sp and BM

We analyzed the proteomic profiling of BM cells (Fig. 3A). There were 36 intersectional SDPs observed in 3 ART-administered groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Consistent with the spleen, GO enrichment analysis of intersectional SDPs in BM cells of G2, G3 and G4 for biological processes indicated the high correspondence with the signaling pathway of response to virus and interferon-beta (Fig. 3B). Moreover, we analyzed the correlative signaling pathways based on the group-specific SDPs of G2, G3 or G4, which suggests that different doses of ART may have different functions in specific signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The PLS-DA faithfully discriminated ART-administered BM cells in G2, G3 and G4 from BM cells in G1 based on the abundance of detected proteins, especially G4 (Fig. 3C). The number of down-regulated differential proteins was inversely proportional to the dose of the administered-ART, while the up-regulated proteins were not (Fig. 3D). The GO enrichment analysis revealed the higher correspondence between the ART administration and the signaling pathways of response to virus and interferon-beta (Supplementary Fig. 3C–E). There were 72 down-regulated and 33 up-regulated differential proteins in G4 compared with G1 (Fig. 3E). The GO enrichment analysis of G4 differential proteins revealed a profoundly up-regulated pathway of neutrophil degranulation and down-regulated pathways of response to virus and interferon (Fig. 3F). There were 6 notably down-regulated proteins in G4 compared with G1 (Fig. 3G). Four of them were interferon-induced proteins, namely IFIT1, ISG15, IFI44 and BST2 (Fig. 3G,H). Particularly, IFIT1 and BST2 were also notably down- regulated in splenic cells of G4 compared with G1 (Fig. 2I,K). Taken together, in Sp and BM, which respectively represent the adaptive and the innate immune tissue, ART administration was notably correlated with the response of interferon. MDSCs were elevated after ART administration in splenic cells

To further investigate the immune microenvironment at the cell composition level after ART administration, we performed CyTOF analysis of CD45+ cells from the splenic and BM cells, which were notably affected after ART administration, as indicated above at the proteomic level. To avoid the influence of experimental operation and variation in machine conditions, we adopted a barcoding strategy. The splenic and BM cells were distinguished by two metal-labeled CD45 antibodies (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 7). We designed the CyTOF panel targeting 28 antigens, which clarified the heterogeneity of the immune cell populations into CD4+T cells, CD8+T cells, B cells, myeloid cells and natural killer (NK) cells. To further resolve the cell-type alterations, 32 subclusters were identified, including 10 B cell populations, 15 T cell populations, 6 myeloid cell populations and an NK cell population (Supplementary Table 1). Then, we particularly analyzed the single-cell composition profiling of splenic cells. We created a map of diverse immune cells and finally categorized 20 cell clusters of splenic CD45+ cells after reducing the high-dimensional data into two dimensions according to the quantification of the marker expression (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 8A). Notably, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) were elevated with increasing dose of ART administration, especially the cell subset of MHC IIlo MDSCs (Fig. 4C,D,G–H). MDSCs have emerged as major regulators of immune response in cancer and other pathological conditions [28]. Previous studies have shown that MDSCs are involved in the treatment of autoimmune diseases and increasing transplant tolerance [29,30]. We also found the elevated percentage of macrophages/monocytes in G3 and G4 compared with G1, but no difference in cDCs and NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 8C,D). Meanwhile, in the adaptive immune system, we observed decreased total B, MZ B, IgM+ B cells, and increased FO B, IgG1+ B cells after dose-dependent ART administration. These results revealed that naïve B cells were activated and class-switched to IgG1+ B cells after ART administration (Fig. 4E–H, Supplementary Fig. 8E–G). Moreover, we found slightly increased CD4+ T effector memory cells in G2 and G4, but we did not find a significant difference in CD4+ T, CD8a+ T or B1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8H–K). Other cell subtypes were below 1.5%. Thus, at the single-cell composition level, MDSCs were significantly elevated after ART administration in splenic cells, especially in the group of ART 100 mg/kg (G4). In the proteomic profiling of the spleen, IFN-induced proteins were significantly downregulated in G4. MDSCs and macrophages/monocytes were increased in BM cells after ART administration

Then, we analyzed the BM cells at the single-cell composition level by CyTOF (Fig. 5A). In the BM, most of the immune cells are innate immune cells, while fewer are adaptive immune cells, especially T cells (Fig. 5B,C). We categorized BM cells into T-cell subtypes, B-cell subtypes, innate-immune cell subtypes and an NK cell subtype according to the marker expression (Fig. 5D–E). We also found elevated MDSCs with increased dose of ART administration in BM cells, similar to what we observed in splenic cells (Fig. 5F,G). In addition, we found slightly increased macrophages/monocytes, activated macrophages/monocytes and NK cells after ART administration, especially in the ART 100 mg/kg group (Fig. 5F,H–J). We did not find a significant difference in mDCs, cDCs or pDCs (Supplementary Fig. 9A–C,G–I). In the adaptive immune system, we observed decreased B cells and MZ B cells in all ART-administered groups, and slightly increased FO B cells in G2 after ART administration (Fig. 5F,K, Supplementary Fig. 9D,J–K). There was no significant difference in the percentage of total T, CD4+ T or CD8a+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 9E–F,L–N). Other cell subtypes were below 0.1%. The bone marrow, as the central immune tissue, transfers B cells to peripheral immune tissues, such as the spleen [31]. In the CyTOF analysis of splenic cells, we found that B cells were activated after ART administration, which partially explained why B cells in the bone marrow were decreased. Taken together, in the BM, after ART administration, innate-immune cells, especially MDSCs and macrophages/monocytes, were primarily increased. Combined with the splenic analysis at the single-cell composition level, ART administration achieved immunosuppression through increased MDSCs. Furthermore, to analyze the function of the increased MDSCs after ART administration, we isolated MDSCs derived from the bone marrow of ART-administered mice and co-cultured them with T cells. After stimulation with Con A, we found that the proliferation of CD8a+ and CD4+ T cells in the ART-administered MDSC co-cultured group was inhibited, and the ROS level was elevated (Supplementary Fig. 10A–H). ART attenuated LPS-induced inflammatory damage in vivo

Proteomics and single-cell composition analysis have collectively implied that ART administration may benefit immunosuppression. GO enrichment of the intersectional SDPs in BM and PC showed correspondence between ART administration and the signaling pathway of response to LPS (Fig. 1G). Therefore, we established an in vivo LPS-induced inflammatory model to determine whether artesunate had an immunosuppressive function. According to the proteomic and single-cell composition profiling data, we tested two doses of artesunate (10 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg). We injected BALB/c mice with ART intraperitoneally for successive 7 days and then challenged them with LPS (Fig. 6A). After 24 h, mice were measured for the clinical severity score and weight loss. Mice challenged with LPS after 100 mg/kg of ART administration (G4) showed lower inflammatory severity score than LPS only group (G2) and pretreatment of 10 mg/kg ART group (G3) (Fig. 6B). However, pretreatment of 100 mg/kg ART did not restore the high body weight loss (Fig. 6C). Consistently, the spleen of 100 mg/kg ART group was significantly smaller than LPS only group (Fig. 6D). Therefore, pretreatment of 100 mg/kg of ART indeed significantly attenuated the LPS-induced inflammation, with spleen pathology similar to control. To explore the underlying mechanism by which ART attenuates the LPS-induced inflammation, we further detected the relevant signaling protein expression. According to the proteomic data, IFN-induced proteins, IFIT1 and BST2, were significantly downregulated both in the spleen and the bone marrow. Moreover, LPS could induce BST2 expression in a manner dependent on the TLR4/TRIF/IRF3 pathway [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Therefore, we analyzed the IFIT1 and BST2 expression in the spleen and the bone marrow, respectively. BST2 is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein and forms cysteine-linked dimers [36,37]. In our study, we found that BST2 presented as both a dimer and a monomer in the spleen, but only as a monomer in the bone marrow. In the spleen, the ART 100 mg/kg group (G4) showed lower expression of BST2 in dimer and monomer than the LPS only group (G2), which was consistent with the proteomic data as shown above. While the ART 10 mg/kg group (G3) did not show such a tendency with the expression of BST2 (Fig. 6E,F). In addition, we observed no statistical difference in IFIT1 among all the 4 groups (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, we analyzed the expression of IRF3 and STAT1 for regulating BST2 transcriptionally. We found that the expression of STAT1 was significantly decreased in the ART 100 mg/kg group (G4) compared with the LPS only group (G2) (Fig. 6E,G). We found the decreased tendency of IRF3 in G4 compared with G2 (Fig. 6E,H). In the bone marrow, we did not find a significant decrease in the tendency of BST2 expression with ART administration (Fig. 6I,J). Consistent with the splenic cells, no statistical difference was observed for IFIT1 expression in the bone marrow (Fig. 6J). Discussion

Artesunate, as one of the artemisinin derivatives, is a first-line anti-malarial medicine worldwide. Previous studies have indicated that, besides the antimalarial function, artesunate is also potent for cancer and autoimmune disease treatment. However, the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms of artesunate’s systemic interactions with multiplex immune tissues are not well known.

In this study, we combined label-free proteomics and CyTOF to interrogate how artesunate affected the major immune tissues at proteomic and single-cell composition levels. To illustrate the shift of the multiplex immune system, we investigated 5 major immune tissues (spleen, bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes and peritoneal cavity), which cover both innate and adaptive immune responses. We examined 3 doses of artesunate to unveil the potential dose dependency. Thus, 100 biological samples with different doses of artesunate from different immune tissues were collected and analyzed. Among the 5 immune tissues, the spleen and the bone marrow showed significantly different proteomic regulation patterns with control in a dose-dependent manner. ART treatment downregulated IFN-induced proteins, especially BST2 and IFIT1, in both the spleen and the bone marrow at the proteomic level.

We then investigated the immune-cell composition of spleen and bone marrow through CyTOF. We designed the CyTOF panel with 28 biomarkers to identify T cell, B cell, myeloid cell subtypes and an NK cell subtype. In the spleen, we found elevated MDSCs, especially MHC IIlo MDSCs. MDSCs were reported as a group of immune cells suppressing the immune response [38]. Artesunate, especially 100 mg/kg, would probably suppress the immune response by increasing the percentage of MDSCs. In addition, we found that B cells were partially activated to class-switch to IgG1+ B cells, followed by artesunate stimulation. In the bone marrow, we also found elevated MDSCs in the ART-administered groups, especially the dose of 10 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg treatment groups. The bone marrow is a central immune tissue in which most of the immune cells are myeloid cells. Besides MDSCs, we found that the percentage of naïve and activated macrophages/monocytes was also increased after ART administration. Innate immune response was activated in the bone marrow, while adaptive immune response was activated with increased class-switched B cells in the spleen.

Combined with proteomics and CyTOF data, our results implied that artesunate may downregulate IFN response and suppress the immune response by elevating the percentage of MDSCs. Therefore, we hypothesized that pretreatment with artesunate would attenuate inflammation through downregulating the expression of IFN-induced proteins. Moreover, previous studies revealed that LPS stimulation could induce BST2 expression [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. In our proteomic data, we found significantly decreased expression of BST2 not only in the spleen, but also in the bone marrow. Thus, we proposed the LPS-induced inflammation in vivo validation model. BALB/c mice were pretreated with 10 mg/kg or 100 mg/kg artesunate for successive 7 days and then challenged with LPS. Twenty-four hours after LPS injection, we measured the inflammatory severity score. We found that pretreatment with 100 mg/kg ART significantly attenuated the inflammatory damage to the spleen. Furthermore, we analyzed the expression of BST2 in the spleen and the bone marrow. We found that the expression of BST2 in the spleen was significantly decreased in the group of 100 mg/kg ART as compared to the LPS-only group. However, the expression of BST2 in bone marrow had no statistical difference in the ART-administered group compared with the LPS-only group. The spleen is the peripheral immune tissue and serves as the first battlefield for external stimulation. We found serious splenomegaly in the LPS-only group. A 100 mg/kg ART pretreatment alleviates the splenomegaly. This suggests that ART remits the LPS-induced inflammatory response in the spleen. This may partially explain why significantly decreased expression of BST2 was observed in the spleen. In our study, the duration of stimulation was only 8 days, including 7 days for ART pretreatment and 24 h for LPS stimulation. There was not enough time to pass the effect of ART stimulation extensively to the bone marrow. The immune response mostly occurred in the peripheral immune tissues.

In summary, our study systematically investigated proteomic and cellular immune response for 5 major immune tissues (spleen, bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes and peritoneal cavity) in vivo, with artesunate administration based on integrative analysis of label-free proteomics and CyTOF. Our results indicated that pretreatment with artesunate would attenuate the LPS-induced inflammatory damage to the spleen, probably via downregulating BST2 expression. This study not only introduces a high-quality proteomic data resource and the cell composition CyTOF analysis of ART administration, but also provides the potential therapeutic target of ART for inflammatory diseases.
