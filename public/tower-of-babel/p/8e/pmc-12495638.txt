Tubacin alleviate the reproductive toxicity of deoxynivalenol in mouse oocytes and zygotes via strengthening microtubule stability
Authors: Hui Luo, Jianhua Chen, Zhihan Guo, Yanyan Zhu, Yipin Wang, Tian Wu, Siyue Yin, Cao Li, Youqiang Su, Yao Chen, Yun Qian, Congxiu Miao, Ruizhi Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeoxynivalenol (DON) is one of the most common food contaminants, widely present in grain products. Studies using murine and porcine models show deoxynivalenol exposure impairs oocyte maturation and mitochondrial function; however, the exact mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that DON exposure markedly altered the microtubule nucleation-associated protein expression in oocytes. Pharmacological inhibition of HDAC6 with Tubacin rescued maturation arrest and restored expression of key microtubule regulators KIF11 and TPX2, which are key factors for microtubule nucleation and spindle stabilization. More importantly, we developed an in vivo TUBB8 oocyte-specific knock-in model. In our expressing human β-tubulin isotype model, Tubacin reconstructed normal spindles and drastically restored polar body extrusion in DON-exposed oocytes. Mechanistically, DON represses mRNA translation and disrupts ribosomal function. In our investigation, DON interfered with fertilized zygote cleavage by disrupting microtubule and microfilament networks, and Tubacin enhanced microtubule acetylation, stabilizing the network and rescuing developmental arrest. Taken together, this study establishes Tubacin's therapeutic potential against DON-induced reproductive toxicity, and validated the novel TUBB8 oocyte-specific knock-in mouse model as a rapid evaluation of exposure and potential treatment of environmental pollutants to female reproductive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02432-4.Keywords: Deoxynivalenol, Tubacin, Oocyte maturation, Spindle, Multi-omics

Introduction

Mycotoxin contamination of agricultural products constitutes a major threat to global food security and human health, with Fusarium-derived trichothecenes representing the most prevalent class of foodborne toxins [1]. Deoxynivalenol, the most abundant trichothecene, exhibits exceptional thermal/chemical stability that facilitates its bioaccumulation through the food chain [2]. Chronic low-dose exposure induces multisystem toxicity, with growing epidemiological evidence linking DON to reproductive impairments, particularly increased risks of preterm birth, intrauterine growth restriction, and subfertility across mammalian species [2, 3]. Mechanistic studies reveal that the exposure of DON disrupted ovarian steroidogenesis by inhibiting granulosa cell proliferation and sex hormone synthesis [4], as well as directly impairing oocyte quality through spindle assembly defects and erroneous kinetochore-microtubule attachments [4–6]. Despite these advances, the molecular basis of DON-induced oocyte incompetence remains poorly characterized, particularly in human-relevant contexts.

There are dual limitations to current therapeutic strategies against mycotoxin reproductive toxicity. While antioxidant approaches such as melatonin supplementation show partial efficacy in rodent models by improving mitochondrial function [5], profound interspecies differences complicate direct translation to humans. Notably, mouse and human oocytes exhibit fundamental disparities during maturation, encompassing variations in both nuclear and cytoplasmic mechanisms. In mouse oocytes, microtubule nucleation predominantly occurs via microtubule organizing centers (MTOCs), followed by spindle assembly and chromosome segregation. However, human oocyte maturation is primarily orchestrated by evolutionarily distinct human microtubule organizing centers (huoMTOCs), which differ in protein composition and generate spindles with unique structural characteristics compared to murine models [7, 8].

Critically, the molecular landscape of spindle assembly diverges significantly between species. The primate-specific TUBB8 gene was found to be exclusively expressed in human oocytes and early embryos and encoded the only abundantly expressed β-tubulin isotype in these cells [9]. Studies have profiled the known TUBB8 mutational spectrum associated with female infertility, including oocyte maturation arrest, fertilization failure, early embryo development arrest, and recurrent miscarriage [10–15]. These observed differences suggest that conventional animal models (e.g., mice, pigs) may not fully recapitulate the consequences of DON exposure on human oocytes. Therefore, these interspecies divergences highlight the importance of developing humanized models to more accurately assess the reproductive toxicity of DON and potential therapeutic countermeasures.

Emerging evidence positions epigenetic modulators as promising protectants against environmental toxicants. Studies have shown that exposure to mycotoxins enhances the chromatin accessibility of the global transcriptome and the histone modification levels of key gene promoters, indicating that the toxicity of mycotoxins can be eliminated or alleviated by histone modification inhibitors [16, 17]. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi), initially developed for cancer therapy through their chromatin remodeling capacities [18, 19], demonstrate unexpected reproductive benefits-preserving spermatogenesis under oxidative stress and rescuing oocyte quality in PCOS models via mitochondrial stabilization [20, 21]. Their therapeutic potential extends to embryogenesis, where HDACi enhance blastocyst development through improved DNA repair efficiency [22]. However, systematic investigation of HDACi's capacity to counteract toxin-induced oocyte defects remains lacking, particularly in models recapitulating human microtubule biology.

In this study, we investigated the mechanism of DON-induced oocyte meiotic failure. We found DON treatment inhibited ribosomal function and repressed the translation of many mRNAs, as corroborated by structural analysis. In our humanized TUBB8 oocyte-specific knock-in mouse model, DON exposure was also found to dramatically inhibit first polar body extrusion. Strikingly, co-administration of the HDAC6 inhibitor Tubacin restored the oocyte maturation process by enhancing microtubule acetylation and stabilizing the microtubule network. In addition, DON disrupted microtubule and actin microfilament networks, interfering with zygote cleavage. These findings collectively elucidated the rescue effects of Tubacin on DON-induced reproductive toxicity in oocytes, and validated the TUBB8 oocyte-specific knock-in mouse model as a physiologically relevant platform for evaluating environmental pollutants and potential treatment in female reproduction.

Materials and methods

Inhibitor treatment

All inhibitors were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, D2650) as 1000 × stocks. To investigate the toxicity of deoxynivalenol, mouse GV oocytes were cultured in an M2 medium supplemented with deoxynivalenol (MCE, HY-N6684) at varying concentrations. To inhibit HDAC6, mouse GV oocytes were cultured in an M2 medium supplemented with Tubacin (MCE, HY-13428) at a concentration of 4 μM.

Preparation and culture of mouse oocytes

The experimental protocols involving animal subjects in this investigation were authorized by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Nanjing Medical University. Ovarian materials from female mice aged 3–5 weeks were utilized as the source of germinal vesicle (GV) stage oocytes, following a 46–48-h interval post-administration of 7.5 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG; Ningbo Second Hormone Factory, Zhejiang, China). Oocyte retrieval was conducted through microdissection of cumulus-oocyte complex employing sterile needles, with subsequent incubation in M16 culture medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# M7292) under sterile conditions.

To maintain meiotic arrest during specimen preparation, the culture system contained 200 μM 3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# I7018) dissolved in M16 medium, which was preserved under paraffin oil (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# M5310) and incubated at a constant 37 °C. For in vitro maturation experiments, collected oocytes underwent sequential culture phases: initial incubation for 7 h to attain metaphase I (MI) stage, followed by extended culture for 15 h to reach metaphase II (MII) stage. This maturation process was initiated by transferring oocytes to fresh M16 medium devoid of meiotic inhibitors, maintained at physiological temperature (37 °C) with 5% CO2 supplementation throughout the culture period.

Mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University and were conducted following the institutional guidelines for the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University. CRISPR/Cas9-based genome engineering was employed to construct C-terminal 3 × HA-tagged TUBB8-WTflox/flox knock-in mouse strains (C57BL/6 J background). Gdf9-Cre mice were obtained from Youqiang Su Laboratory (Shandong University) and maintained on identical C57BL/6 J genetic backgrounds. To generate oocyte-specific TUBB8-WT knock-in mice, female mice carrying the TUBB8-WTflox/flox alleles were crossed with Gdf9-Cre males. Genotyping for LoxP and Cre was carried out using PCR amplification. Mice were genotyped by PCR using primers for TUBB8 LoxP (Forward: 5’-CTGGATCACAGG TGTGGAGT-3’, Reverse: 5’-GGCTACATGCAGGCAAAC-3’), and Gdf9-Cre (Forward: 5’- TCTGATGAAGTCAGGAAGAACC-3’, Reverse: 5’- GAGATGTCCTTCACTCTGATTC-3’). Mice were housed in a 12–12 h light–dark cycle, with constant temperature, and food and water were provided ad libitum. All animals were maintained and used according to the guidelines of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Oocytes were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked for routine immunofluorescence. In brief, oocytes were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde overnight and then permeabilized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.5% triton X-100 (PBT) for 20 min at room temperature (RT). For cold-stable microtubule assay, oocytes were kept on ice for 10 min before fixation. All antibody incubations were performed in PBS-BSA at 5 mg/ml overnight at 4 °C (for primary antibodies) and at 20 mg/ml for 2 h at RT (for secondary antibodies). Primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-DCTN1 (Proteintech, 55,182–1-AP), mouse anti-Pericentrin (BD Biosciences, 611,814), rabbit anti-HA (Abcam, ab9110), mouse anti-HA (MBL, M180-3), rabbit anti-TACC3 (Abcam, ab51976), rabbit anti-TPX2 (Proteintech, 11,741–1-AP), rabbit anti-γ-Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T6557), Cy3-β-Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, C4585), rabbit anti-α-Tubulin (Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate) (CST, 5063S), mouse anti-α-Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T9026), rabbit anti-alpha Tubulin (acetyl K40) (Alexa Fluor® 647 Conjugate) (Abcam, ab218591), rabbit anti-KIF11 (Sigma-Aldrich, HPA010568); Secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Highly Cross-Adsorbed anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, A11034), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated Highly Cross-Adsorbed anti-mouse (Invitrogen, A11032). Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated Highly Cross-Adsorbed anti-rabbit (Abcam, ab150083). DNA was stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1005).

Oocytes were mounted on glass slides for confocal imaging using an SP8 laser scanning microscope (Leica) or LSM 900 (ZEISS). Immunofluorescence intensity was measured by acquiring images of control and experimental groups under identical imaging parameters on the same microscope, followed by data analysis using Image J software. Confocal images were collected as z-stacks at 0.5 µm intervals to visualize the entire meiotic spindle and are displayed as sum intensity z-projections to show the spindle.

ROS evaluation

The intracellular production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was quantified using a laser confocal microscope, in conjunction with the ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), sourced from Beyotime (S0033S). Oocytes were incubated with DCFH-DA at a concentration of 10 μmol/L for a period of 20 min at 37 °C. After the incubation, the samples were thoroughly washed with MEMα medium. Imaging was then conducted using fluorescence microscopy, with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. Identical acquisition parameters (including exposure intensity and digital gain) were applied to all oocyte groups during DCFH-DA imaging.

JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential assessments were conducted using JC-1 dye (Yeasen, 40705ES03). First, incubate the oocytes to be tested in the dark at 37 °C in a medium containing an appropriate amount of JC-1 dye for 15 min for staining. Subsequently, thoroughly wash the oocytes twice with pre-heated PBS or buffer to remove unbound free dyes. Finally, resuspend the oocytes in fresh buffer and immediately detect them using a confocal microscope. The level of mitochondrial membrane potential can be evaluated by analyzing the change in the ratio of red to green fluorescence.

ATP evaluation

Upon oocyte lysis, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was aspirated while avoiding pellet disturbance. Subsequently, 100 μL ATP detection working solution (Beyotime, S0027) was added to the pellet, followed by 5-min incubation at room temperature. Luminescent signal quantification was performed using a luminometer (Biotek synergy HTX).

RNA extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from pooled embryo samples (5 embryos per biological replicate) using the Takara CellAmp Whole Transcriptome Amplification kit (Takara, 3734) following the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR amplification was performed using the Vazyme ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Q711) on a QuantStudio 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each reaction was run in technical triplicate. Gene-specific primer sequences used for amplification are listed in Table S1.

Smart-seq2 RNA-sequencing analysis

Five oocytes per sample were collected from the Control, DON-treated, and DON + Tubacin for RNA-sequencing. Each sample was lysed with 1 µL lysis buffer, and immediately used for cDNA synthesis via the SMART-seq2 method. The sample preparation of oocyte for TUBB8-KI mice and the control group was also carried out according to the above procedure. The differential gene expression was analyzed with the R package DESeq2. Genes with |log₂FC|≥ 1.5 and adjusted P value (by the Benjamini–Hochberg method) < 0.05 were considered DEGs. GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the clusterprofiler package in R.

Enrichment network analysis

Due to the huge number of GO ontologies, to find the key ontology, simplifyEnrichment (v.1.12.0) [23] and aPEAR (v.1.0.0) [24] packages were used to cluster the GO terms.

Model generation and structure analysis

Molecular docking between deoxynivalenol and tubulin dimer (3JAS) was conducted using AlphaFold3, and the interaction was visualized with PyMOL. The model depicting the inhibition of eukaryotic ribosomes by deoxynivalenol was derived from published cryo-electron microscopy data (4U53) and visualized using Visual Studio Code with the Protein View plugin.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently conducted at least three times, and the data are presented as mean ± SEM using Prism software (GraphPad Software). Differences between three groups were analyzed using One-way ANOVA with Šidák correction for multiple comparisons, with p values denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001 to indicate significance levels. Nonsignificant results are indicated as ns.

Results

Tubacin alleviate deoxynivalenol-exposure caused mouse oocyte maturation arrest

To investigate the effects of deoxynivalenol on mouse oocyte maturation, varying concentrations of DON (1, 2, 3, and 6 μM) were supplemented in the oocyte in vitro maturation medium (Fig. 1A,B). A dose-dependent decrease of polar body extrusion (PBE) rate was observed in DON-exposed oocytes compared with controls, showing a statistically significant reduction in the 2 μM DON group (Fig. 1C). Complete meiotic arrest was induced by 6 μM DON, as evidenced by the absence of first polar body extrusion, whereas co-treatment with Tubacin significantly restored PBE rates and meiotic failure (Fig. 1C). No additional benefit was observed with higher Tubacin concentrations than 4 μM Tubacin in DON-exposed oocytes (Fig. 1C). Treatment of oocytes with 4 µM Tubacin alone exerted a mild inhibitory effect on meiotic progression (Fig. 1C). Importantly, this modest off-target effect was significantly outweighed by Tubacin’s robust rescue of DON-induced defects, confirming its therapeutic benefit. Based on these findings, 2 μM DON and 4 μM Tubacin were selected for subsequent investigations of meiotic arrest mechanisms.

To elucidate the cellular basis of DON-induced meiotic failure, immunofluorescence analysis was performed to assess spindle architecture and chromosomal organization. Control oocytes displayed characteristic barrel-shaped spindles with chromosomes aligned at the metaphase plate, whereas severe spindle disassembly and chromosomal misalignment were observed in DON-exposed oocytes (Fig. 1D, E). Remarkably, Tubacin co-treatment was found to restore normal spindle morphology and chromosome alignment (Fig. 1D, E).

Considering the critical role of microtubule organizing centers (MTOCs) in bipolar spindle formation, the spatial distribution of MTOC-associated proteins was systematically characterized through multi-color immunofluorescence imaging. Pericentrin and γ-Tubulin were detected as tightly focused signals at spindle poles in control oocytes (Fig. 1F, G). In contrast, DON exposure caused the dispersion of these MTOC markers, with both Pericentrin and γ-Tubulin showing abnormal distribution patterns or reduced expression (Fig. 1F-H). This defective localization was effectively rescued by Tubacin co-treatment (Fig. 1F-H).

Given the energy-intensive nature of the oocyte maturation process and the known association between mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantitatively assessed using fluorescence-based assays. DON-exposed oocytes exhibited significantly elevated ROS signals compared to controls when measured using DCFH-DA fluorescence (Fig. 1I, J). Furthermore, mitochondrial membrane localization and ATP levels were assessed. Tubacin was found to restore mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP levels impaired by DON treatment, indicating restoration of mitochondrial function (Fig. S1A-C). These collective findings demonstrate that Tubacin mitigates DON-induced spindle defects and microtubule disorganization while reducing oxidative stress in maturing oocytes.

Mechanistic insights into DON-induced microtubule destabilization

To investigate DON-induced cytoskeletal alterations, immunofluorescence analysis was conducted for key microtubule dynamics regulators and spindle assembly factors. The fluorescence intensity of microtubule nucleator TPX2 was significantly reduced in DON-exposed oocytes compared to controls (Fig. 2A, B), and co-treatment with Tubacin restored TPX2 localization patterns to control levels. Notably, aberrant cytoplasmic accumulation of spindle stabilizer KIF11 was observed in DON-treated oocytes, which was rescued by Tubacin supplementation (Fig. 2C, D). These results demonstrate that Tubacin counteracts DON-induced cytoskeletal destabilization through coordinated regulation of microtubule-associated factors. Moreover, molecular docking revealed potential binding of DON to multiple sites on the inside part of α/β-tubulin dimers (Fig. 2E), suggesting structural interference with microtubule polymerization dynamics and microtubule stabilization.

Given the central role of KIF11 in microtubule stabilization, cold-induced disassembly assays were performed to evaluate microtubule resilience. Cold-resistant microtubule lattice and spindle length were significantly reduced in DON-exposed oocytes following 10-min cold treatment, while Tubacin co-treatment preserved microtubule stability (Fig. 2F, G). Consistently, acetylated α-tubulin levels showed a significant reduction in DON-exposed oocytes, and Tubacin restored microtubule acetylation to normal levels (Fig. 2H, I). This comprehensive analysis establishes microtubule acetylation as a critical regulatory node in DON-induced meiotic defects, suggesting pharmacological stabilization of microtubule post-translational modifications as a potential therapeutic approach for toxin-associated fertility impairments.

Tubacin protects against defects induced by DON in TUBB8 oocyte-specific knockin mouse model

Meiotic spindle assembly and maintenance of the architecture stability in human oocytes are mainly mediated by the microtubule encoding the primate-specific gene TUBB8, whereas murine spindle assembly predominantly depends on microtubule-organizing centers (MTOCs). This interspecies divergence highlights the critical need for experimental models that more faithfully replicate human reproductive physiology; thus, we generated a TUBB8 oocyte-specific knockin (TUBB8-KI) mouse model (Fig. 3A). DON exposure recapitulated meiotic defects in knockin oocytes, with Tubacin demonstrating comparable rescue efficacy to wild-type controls (Fig. 3B, C). No significant differences in spindle morphology or maturation rates were observed between control oocytes and TUBB8-KI oocytes (Fig. 3D), establishing its reliability for human-relevant reproductive toxicity evaluations. To investigate TUBB8 expression patterns in knock-in mice and its influence on endogenous tubulin, metaphase-I oocytes from wild-type and TUBB8-KI mice were collected for Smart-seq2 RNA-seq analysis. TUBB8 expression was moderate relative to other tubulin isotypes following knock-in (Fig. S2A, 2 C). Critically, the overall transcriptomic profile remained largely unaffected by TUBB8 knock-in. Only 11 genes were differentially expressed in knock-in oocytes versus controls, including one down-regulated and ten up-regulated genes (Fig. S2B). Among these, mRNA levels of endogenous tubulins Tubb4b and Tuba3a were elevated (Fig. S2B), suggesting compensation for increased total β-Tubulin levels due to exogenous TUBB8 insertion. This may reflect upregulation of α-Tubulin and β-Tubulin transcripts to maintain the balance of the abundance of α/β-tubulin heterodimers, ensuring cellular functional capacity.

Mechanistic analyses revealed that DON exposure abolished the polarized distribution of MTOC-associated components, as evidenced by the diffuse cytoplasmic localization or diminished expression of Pericentrin and DCTN1, respectively (Fig. 3E-H). Tubacin treatment effectively rescued their characteristic bipolar enrichment (Fig. 3E-H), suggesting the restoration of MTOC-mediated spindle organization. Furthermore, DON-induced suppression of KIF11 was observed to correlate with impaired bipolar spindle formation (Fig. 3I-J). Tubacin intervention not only normalized KIF11 expression levels but also re-established its canonical bipolar localization pattern (Fig. 3I-J), providing direct evidence of spindle stability rescue through pharmacological modulation.

DON-exposure inhibits mRNA translation in oocytes

Low-input Smart-seq2 profiling was performed to characterize DON-induced transcriptional alterations in maturing oocytes. Spearman correlation analysis revealed clear segregation of experimental groups with high inter-replicate consistency (Fig. 4A). The mRNA profiling in control, DON exposure, and DON + Tubacin oocytes was shown in Fig. 4B. DON exposure induced 432 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, 416 upregulated and 16 downregulated), while Tubacin co-treatment reduced DEGs to 112 (49 upregulated, 63 downregulated) (Fig. 4C and Fig. S3A), demonstrating partial restoration of transcriptional homeostasis through microtubule stabilization. It was well documented that DON inhibits protein synthesis primarily by interfering with ribosome function [25]. Indeed, the structural analysis demonstrated high-affinity binding of DON to the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) within the 28S rRNA of ribosomal 60S subunits (Fig. 4D), providing evidence for impaired peptide bond formation and translation elongation. This ribosomal targeting correlated with abnormal maternal mRNA accumulation (Table S2), suggesting DON-mediated inhibition of maternal mRNA decay during meiotic progression.

GO enrichment analysis identified significant transcriptional suppression of cytoskeletal regulators and mitochondrial/ribosomal components in DON-exposed oocytes (Fig. 4E,F). Notably, cytoskeleton-related effectors included microtubule dynamics controllers (Tuba4a, Cdk5rap2, Stau2) and actin filament organizers (Unc45a, Ccdc60, Reps2) were significantly up-regulated upon DON exposure, while organelle-associated DEGs encompassed mitochondrial transporters (Prkag2, Slc25a54, Timm50, Tor1b, Mpv17l) and ribosomal biogenesis factors (Rbm41, Gpn1, Rpl36al, Eif3l, Nalf2) were remarkably elevated in DON-induced oocytes. (Fig. 4G). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed up-regulated genes enriched in ribosomal functions and down-regulated genes associated with ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, suggesting DON potentially impacts ribosomal mRNA translation and ubiquitin-dependent degradation pathways (Fig. S3B). These findings suggest that DON impairs oocyte maturation possibly through disruption of microtubule-mediated transport and organelle-specific functions. DON destabilizes cytoskeletal architecture, and simultaneously impairs energy metabolism and protein synthesis via mitochondrial/ribosomal dysfunction, collectively compromising oocyte developmental competence.

Following Tubacin supplementation, we observed that differentially expressed genes were enriched in the biological process of chromosome separation (Fig. S3C). This is associated with that most DEGs in DON-induced oocytes with maturation arrest were enriched in asymmetric division-related cell cycle progression (Fig. S3D). In addition, the DEGs were involved in GTPse binding and active transmembrane transporter activity, which is consistent with the above observations that Tubacin supplementation in DON-induced oocytes leads to the meiotic progression restored and chromosomal aligned. The protein–protein interaction network (PPI) further demonstrated that Tubacin may enhance the activity of ATPase and electron transfer within the mitochondrial inner membrane (Fig. S3E). Thus, we speculate that Tubacin coordinates mitochondrial bioenergetics, and cytoskeletal remodeling to rescue meiotic competence.

DON exposure resulted in abnormal zygote migration and cleavage

The developmental competence of fertilized oocytes following DON exposure was investigated through pronuclei migration and cleavage analysis (Fig. 5A, B). A dose-dependent impairment of 2-cell embryo formation was observed, with fertilized oocytes exhibiting greater sensitivity to DON toxicity than GV-stage oocytes (Fig. 5B). Complete cleavage arrest occurred at 3 μM DON, and 2 μM Tubacin restored 2-cell rates to 62.5% (Fig. 5B). The activation of zygotic genome activation (ZGA) was further assessed by quantifying the expression for key markers, including Muervl, Hsp70.1, eIF1A and Zscan4d. Tubacin effectively reversed DON-induced dysregulation of Muervl, eIF1A and Zscan4d (Fig. S4A), confirming its role in safeguarding epigenetic reprogramming during early embryogenesis. Furthermore, our data demonstrate that DON exposure severely compromises blastocyst development, while Tubacin co-treatment significantly restores developmental rates (Fig. S4B,C).

Further investigation revealed both cytoskeletal disruption and microtubule acetylation defects. Tubacin intervention specifically restored acetylated microtubule density and improved cleavage rates as well as F-actin amount and pattern, establishing microtubule stability as the primary determinant of developmental competence (Fig. 5C-E). The partial rescue achieved through pharmacological acetylation enhancement identifies microtubule post-translational modification as a critical therapeutic target for eliminating mycotoxin-induced developmental arrest.

Discussion

This study establishes microtubule stabilization as the primary mechanism through which the HDAC6 inhibitor Tubacin rescues DON-induced female reproductive toxicity. As the predominant trichothecene contaminant in global grain supplies, DON exhibits a particular tropism for germ cells, perturbing spindle assembly and mouse oocyte maturation through microtubule destabilization. Critically, by integrating pharmacological intervention with a humanized physiological model, we provide the first demonstration that the humanized TUBB8 oocyte-specific knockin mouse model shows DON disrupting spindle architecture through coordinated reduction of α-tubulin acetylation and MTOC disorganization. This combined approach uniquely bridges species-specific translational gaps, demonstrating that Tubacin treatment restored acetylation levels and rescued polar body extrusion efficiency, positioning microtubule stabilization as a critical therapeutic strategy for oocyte quality rescue.

The gamete-specific toxicity of DON likely stems from the unique asymmetric division governing meiotic progression. It has been well-documented that DON-exposed oocytes display spindle assembly and chromosome congression failure [5]. However, the underlying molecular details remain to be elucidated. Our data corroborate and extend prior findings by demonstrating that distinct from somatic cell toxicity mechanistically [26, 27], in oocytes 2 μM DON reduced acetylated α-tubulin levels, thus increasing spindle defects [5]. Zygotic cytoskeletal networks exhibited even greater sensitivity, with 3 μM DON inducing complete cleavage arrest through failure of pronucleus migration and cytoskeletal destruction, suggesting developmental stage-dependent vulnerability. This aligns with Toutounchi et al.’s seminal work showing DON’s dose-dependent embryotoxicity [28].

As a specific HDAC6 inhibitor, Tubacin enhances microtubule stability by increasing α-tubulin acetylation. It was reported that tubacin has positive effects on oocyte maturation and spindle assembly and maintenance [29]. Our study reveals that Tubacin treatment significantly restored microtubule acetylation and spindle stabilization in DON-exposed oocytes. Acetylated α-tubulin assembles within stabilized microtubule structures to enhance cellular resilience against low-temperature exposure and pharmacological interventions [30–32]. In our study, the cytoprotective effects of Tubacin primarily arise from its microtubule-stabilizing properties through three possible mechanisms as follows. Firstly, HDAC6 inhibition prevents DON-induced microtubule depolymerization by maintaining α-tubulin acetylation, a post-translational modification essential for microtubule stability. Secondly, Tubacin may promote the microtubule dimer polymerization and thus stabilize the spindle after DON affects spindle assembly factor translation during oocyte maturation. Lastly, Tubacin may potentially restore normal intracellular transport by stabilizing the cytoskeleton, thereby alleviating reactive oxygen species-induced mitochondrial damage. Experimental evidence reveals that perturbations in microtubule stability directly result in compromised mitochondrial mobility [33]. Notably, unlike melatonin and other broad-spectrum antioxidants whose efficacy was constrained by interspecies variability in receptor distribution and redox regulation [34–38], Tubacin directly targets evolutionarily conserved microtubule networks.

Since our computational structural modeling suggests that DON exposure perturbs microtubule polymerization dynamics by directly interfering with tubulin dimer assembly, another possibility is that DON affects microtubule polymerization and the normal transport of spindle factors to the spindle for assembly. While Tubacin likely enhances microtubule stability through HDAC6-mediated acetylation, its specific effects on protofilament elongation kinetics remain mechanistically undefined, necessitating more evidence in further investigations. Notably, our transcriptional profiling revealed that a subset of genes maintained aberrant expression patterns despite Tubacin rescue, and it is necessary to investigate to uncover mechanisms underlying their resistance to deacetylase restoration.

While the expression of human TUBB8 in this model enables direct interrogation of human-specific tubulin isotype functions, we note that functional outcomes may be constrained by inherent interspecies limitations. Although core tubulin-protein interactions were conserved, the binding kinetics of human TUBB8 within murine cellular environments may diverge from those observed in native human oocytes. Furthermore, microtubule spindle assembly may be modulated by species-specific cytoplasmic environments, including regulating kinases, chaperone proteins and post-translational modifiers.

These findings collectively elucidated the rescue effects of Tubacin on DON-induced reproductive toxicity in oocytes (Fig. 6), and validated the TUBB8 oocyte-specific knock-in mouse model as a physiologically relevant platform for evaluating environmental pollutants and potential treatment in female reproduction.
