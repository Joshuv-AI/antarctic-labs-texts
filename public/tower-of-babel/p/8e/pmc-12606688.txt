Interactions of Patent Ductus Arteriosus and Ventilation on Preterm Pulmonary Vascular Disease
Authors: Christopher W Myers, Paul M Heerdt, Waldemar A Carlo, Namasivayam Ambalavanan, Samuel J Gentle
Published: 2025

Introduction

Among the 20% of extremely preterm infants who develop bronchopulmonary dysplasia‐associated pulmonary hypertension (BPD‐PH), up to 40% will die within 1 year of diagnosis [1]. To our knowledge, there is no known intervention that can reduce risk for BPD‐PH development, as very few randomized trials have investigated BPD‐PH prevention.

Prior observational studies have identified invasive ventilation and a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) exposure among the strongest predictors of BPD‐PH development [2]. Less invasive respiratory support strategies reduce risk for bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) or death [3]. Although randomized trials of PDA interventions have not shown a reduction in BPD [4], key questions persist—such as whether device closure at later postnatal ages may improve respiratory outcomes. Another critical residual knowledge gap is whether these common exposures specifically modulate BPD‐PH development. This specific and most severe BPD phenotype has not been systematically reported in randomized trials of respiratory or PDA management [3, 4].

We hypothesized that ventilator days and PDA exposure days increase the risk of BPD‐PH in a dose‐dependent and synergistic manner.

Methods

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at the University of Alabama at Birmingham on preterm infants born between 2017 and 2020. The Institutional Review Board at the University of Alabama at Birmingham waived the need for informed consent as it involved secondary research use of identifiable private information under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4). Infants were included if 220/7 and 286/7 weeks' gestation at birth and on respiratory support from postnatal Day 28 through 36 weeks' postmenstrual age. Infants were diagnosed with BPD‐PH using echocardiographic screening initiated on postnatal Day 28 and continued monthly until discharge. Infants were diagnosed with BPD‐PH using previously reported criteria [2]. Exclusion criteria included major congenital abnormalities, absence of echocardiographic data, and death before postnatal Day 28.

Comparison groups were infants with BPD alone and BPD‐PH. Demographic and birth characteristics compared between groups included gestational age, birth weight, small for gestational age, race, sex, preterm premature rupture of membranes (> 24 h before delivery), and histologic chorioamnionitis by placental pathology. Small for gestational age was defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile for gestational age. Clinical exposures comparisons included respiratory support (at postnatal Day 28 and 36 weeks' PMA) and pharmacologic exposures (corticosteroids for BPD and inhaled nitric oxide). Demographic and clinical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test and the Mann−Whitney U test. To assess for interactions between PDA exposure days and ventilator days, we employed two complementary strategies: principal component analysis (PCA) and comparisons of BPD‐PH frequency by strata of PDA exposure days and ventilator days.

To determine sample size adequacy, we used a subjects‐to‐variable ratio of > 10:1 before conducting multivariate PCA analysis. For PCA analysis, a Kaiser−Meyer−Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was also performed to determine testing appropriateness. Each variable was centered and scaled for equal distribution of variables that differed between comparison groups: birth weight, gestational age, PDA exposure days, and ventilator days. Following covariance matrix calculations, eigenvalues and eigenvectors were used to select principal components using a cumulative explained variance threshold of 75% and visually depicted using a biplot.

Lastly, we analyzed the proportion of infants that developed BPD‐PH within the following discrete subgroups informed by the postnatal age of systematic echocardiographic screening and distribution of ventilator days: (1) < 30 ventilator days and (a) < 30 and (b) ≥ 30 PDA exposure days as well as (2) ≥ 30 ventilator days and (a) < 30 and (b) ≥ 30 PDA exposure days. This subgroup analysis was compared using Fisher's exact test. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.0.

Results

There were 398 infants born during the study period. Among these infants, 178 did not develop BPD, for which they were excluded from the analysis, and three infants with BPD were transferred, precluding precise estimates of invasive ventilation exposure. Of the remaining 217 infants available for analysis, 135 developed BPD alone and 82 developed BPD‐PH. Compared to infants with BPD alone, those with BPD‐PH were born at a lower gestational age (25 weeks; IQR 24–26 vs. 26; IQR 25–28; p < 0.001) and birth weight (624; IQR 536–738 vs. 781; IQR 633–960; p < 0.001). More infants with BPD‐PH had a hemodynamically significant PDA at or beyond 28 postnatal days (32% vs. 19%; p = 0.002) and were exposed to a higher number of ventilator days (61; IQR 27–156 vs. 11; IQR 3–35; p < 0.001) compared to infants with BPD alone. This provided a subject‐to‐variable ratio of ~50:1.

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was sufficient (0.63; p < 0.001) for PCA analysis. Two principal components were retained which explained 76.8% of the total variance (PC1 = 52.8%, PC2 = 24.0%). The loadings for PC1 were −0.79 for gestational age, −0.76 for birth weight, 0.76 for ventilator days, and 0.57 for PDA exposure days. The loadings for PC2 were −0.72 for PDA exposure days, −0.42 for gestational age, −0.41 for birth weight, and −0.31 for ventilator days. Among infants with less than 30 ventilator days of ventilator exposure (n = 119), more infants with greater than 30 PDA exposure days developed BPD‐PH compared to infants with less than 30 days (50% vs. 15%, p = 0.003). Conversely, among infants with greater than or equal to 30 ventilator days (n = 98), the proportion of infants developing BPD‐PH was similar between infants with ≥ 30 days of PDA exposure and those with less (69% vs. 55%, p = 0.26) (Figure 2).

Discussion

In this observational analysis, both duration of ventilation and PDA were uniquely associated with BPD‐PH development. These exposures commonly occur among the extremely preterm population and may be potentially modifiable.

As the pulmonary vasculature of extremely preterm infants is vulnerable to multiple simultaneous injuries, we utilized PCA analysis to consider interactions between duration of PDA and ventilator exposures. In considering the biplot, the directionality and strength in the component loadings differed between PDA and invasive ventilation exposures, suggesting these may independently modulate BPD‐PH development. We previously reported that longer durations of PDA exposure are associated with risk for BPD‐PH after adjustment for baseline risk factors [2].

In a previous analysis, PDA exposure duration and invasive ventilation duration were related to BPD development [5]. Infants exposed to > 15 PDA exposure days and > 10 ventilator days were at increased risk for grades 2 and 3 BPD. PDA exposure days were not associated with BPD development in infants exposed to < 10 ventilator days. By contrast, infants in the present study exposed to fewer ventilator days had a higher rate of BPD‐PH if also exposed to > 30 days of PDA exposure. Whereas clinicians' PDA intervention decisions may be predicated on infants' respiratory support, this suggests PDA persistence beyond 30 days may not be benign in infants with limited ventilator exposure. Moreover, these exposures may be additive, as nearly 70% of infants with both persistent PDA and ventilation exposures developed BPD‐PH.

Strengths of this investigation are the multiple analyses for interactions between two common exposures that may increase infants' risk for BPD‐PH and a large population of consideration. However, these findings are single‐center and observational. Invasive ventilation and PDA exposure may not be mechanistically related to BPD‐PH development. Furthermore, institutional echocardiographic criteria for hemodynamically significant PDA and pulmonary hypertension did not fully conform to established guidelines. Specifically, pulmonary artery acceleration time and right ventricular ejection time—measures that may provide more accurate estimates of right ventricular pressure—were not included in BPD‐PH assessments, which instead relied on alternative indices [6].

Conclusion

Ventilator days and PDA exposure days were independently and synergistically associated with risk for BPD‐PH in extremely preterm infants. Whether modification of these exposures reduces risk for BPD‐PH requires further investigation.
