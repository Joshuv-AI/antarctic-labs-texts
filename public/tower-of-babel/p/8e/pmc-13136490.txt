Molecular mechanism underlying regulation of chalcone synthase by chalcone isomerase-like protein
Authors: Song Wang, Li-Ying Ma, Zhou-Geng Xu, Riga Wu, Ji-Ping Qu, Jie Hao, Chen-Jing Hu, Zhi-Yu Chen, Miao-Lian Ma, Wen-Yi Zhang, Teng-Yu Xie, Jing-Jing Xu, Mu-Lan Zhu, Ai-Xia Cheng, Peng Zhang, Jia-Wei Wang, Fang Yu, Jian-Xu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlavonoids are essential for plant growth and environmental adaptation. Chalcone synthase (CHS) directs metabolic flux into flavonoid biosynthesis, but its catalytic promiscuity limits the biosynthetic efficiency. Although the chalcone isomerase-like protein (CHIL) has been characterized as physically interacting with and regulating CHS, the underlying mechanism remains elusive. Here, we report the crystal structure of the CHS-CHIL complex, revealing that CHIL modulates CHS function by gating the substrate-binding pocket entrance through its β-hairpin region. Molecular dynamics simulations indicate that regulation occurs early in the catalytic cycle, affecting substrate binding or early intermediate formation. CHIL enhances CHS activity and product specificity by promoting CoA release and stabilizing key amino acid residues. His36 within the β-hairpin is functionally critical; its substitution with leucine in Arabidopsis and other plant species markedly improves CHS catalytic efficiency and specificity. This regulatory mechanism is evolutionarily conserved across land plants, from bryophytes to angiosperms. Guided by evolutionary analysis, we engineer a CHIL variant (H36E/F37T) that significantly enhances CHS activity. Collectively, our findings establish a conserved binding-conformational regulation paradigm that governs metabolic flux into the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway and provides practical strategies for enhancing flavonoid production and composition in crops.Subject terms: Secondary metabolism, Enzyme mechanisms, X-ray crystallography

Introduction

Flavonoids represent a diverse and fascinating polyphenolic group of plant specialized metabolites with widespread and significant implications for human health, such as anti-aging, anti-inflammatory and reducing the risk of cardiovascular diseases1–4. They are ubiquitously distributed in land plants and play critical roles in plant growth, development, and defense mechanisms against pathogens and environmental stresses1,5,6. Flavonoids contain three rings (C6-C3-C6) as the basic skeleton, and their biosynthesis comes from the general phenylpropanoid pathway1,3,7. Through a sequential cascade of enzymatic reactions catalyzed by key enzymes like chalcone synthase and chalcone isomerase, the fundamental metabolic precursor L-Phenylalanine is converted into structurally diverse flavonoid subclasses, as illustrated in Fig. 1a.

Chalcone synthase (CHS) stands as a pivotal enzyme in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway of plants, catalyzing the initial committed step that initiates the synthesis of a wide-ranging assortment flavonoid compounds7–9. Belonging to the type III polyketide synthase (PKS) enzyme family, CHSs have been isolated and their structures elucidated from numerous plant species, including alfalfa (Medicago sativa)8, fern species (Cyclosorus parasiticus, Stenoloma chusanum)10,11 and Arabidopsis thaliana12. Functioning as a homodimer, CHS possesses two independent active pockets that facilitate a series of decarboxylation, condensation, and cyclization reactions, ultimately generating the production of naringenin chalcone (NC) from one p-coumaroyl-CoA and three malonyl-CoA molecules10–12 (Fig. 1a). Each CHS monomer is composed of two structural domains: an upper domain that houses the active site with key catalytic amino acids, and a lower domain that provides the necessary spatial configuration for the formation of the tetraketide intermediate required for chalcone formation13. Notably, the Cys-His-Asn catalytic triad and αβαβα pseudo symmetric motif is highly conserved in plant CHSs10–12.

Like other enzymes involved in plant specialized metabolism, CHS also generates side products during its catalytic reactions14–20. CHS catalyzes the formation of naringenin chalcone (NC) as the main product, alongside the derailment side product 4-coumaroyltriacetic acid lactone (CTAL) (Fig. 1a)21,22. This phenomenon, known as “catalytic promiscuity”, is prevalent in plants and serves as a fundamental mechanism for environmental adaptation and adaptive evolution21. However, it reduces the efficiency of flavonoid biosynthesis in plants and poses a significant challenge for leveraging CHS in synthetic biology approaches, whether in plant-based systems or microbial platforms, for the production of specific flavonoid compounds with potential health benefits. Interestingly, previous studies have demonstrated that the chalcone isomerase-like protein (CHIL) plays an important role in regulating CHS catalytic promiscuity through protein-protein interactions21,22.

Chalcone isomerase (CHI) is the second committed enzyme in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, which catalyzes the intramolecular and stereospecific cyclization of naringenin chalcone to form naringenin22,23. Phylogenetic analysis indicates that CHI is exclusively found in vascular plants, and CHI-fold proteins can be classified into four distinct types22,24. Type I or type II CHIs exhibit catalytic activity, which isomerize naringenin chalcone to form (2S)-naringenin, or catalyze naringenin chalcone and isoliquiritigenin (6’-deoxychalcone) to generate (2S)-naringenin and liquiritigenin (5-hydroxyflavanone), respectively; type III CHIs, which can bind fatty acids and are believed to be associated with fatty acid metabolism, lack any detectable CHI catalytic activity; similarly, type IV CHIs, also known as CHILs (chalcone isomerase-like proteins), have mutated several key amino acid residues essential for catalytic function, resulting in a complete loss of CHI catalytic activity22,24,25. A loss-of-function mutation in CHIL was found to decrease accumulation of flavonoids and anthocyanins in Ipomoea nil26. Similar results were observed in Arabidopsis thaliana, where overexpression of CHIL partially rescued the phenotype by enhancing flavonoids production27. A proposed mechanism suggests that CHIL physically interacts with CHS altering its catalytic promiscuity and subsequently improving the efficiency of flavonoid biosynthesis21,22,28,29. Despite the identification of CHILs across various land plant species and the characterization of their conserved function, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying their activity remain elusive.

In this study, we found that the expression patterns of CHS and CHIL were synergistically coordinated. Subsequently, the CHS-CHIL complex was successfully expressed and purified, and its crystal structure was determined. Integration of structural and biochemical analyses revealed that CHIL binds to CHS through an extensive interface, modulating the enzyme’s product specificity. And a notable conformational change occurred in the β-hairpin region of CHIL. Furthermore, the H36L mutant significantly enhanced CHIL’s regulatory function on CHS, demonstrating potential for enhancing the nutritional value of crops. Enzymatic assays confirmed that CHIL proteins from five major lineages of land plants consistently enhanced the activity and product specificity of their cognate CHSs, demonstrating functional conservation across land plant evolution.

Results

Regulation the activity and product specificity of CHS by CHIL

Before investigating the structure and function of the CHS-CHIL complex, we first analyzed the specific cell types and developmental stages in which the CHS and CHIL proteins are expressed and exert their functions. We analyzed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from a previous study encompassing six Arabidopsis leaf developmental stages (S1–S6)30. The results revealed that genes within the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway were significantly enriched at the mature S3 stage (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). Analysis of cluster 4 showed preferential expression of CHS (AT5G13930) and CHIL (AT5G05270) in C4 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1e), with enrichment specifically observed in epidermal cells at stages S1, S2, S3, and S6 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Correlation analysis identified stage- and cell-type-specific co-expression patterns between CHS and CHIL (Supplementary Fig. 1f), suggesting that CHS and its regulator CHIL act synergistically to regulate the flavonoid metabolic pathway. The significant correlation between CHS and CHIL in the epidermal cells of Arabidopsis leaves indicates their involvement in flavonoid biosynthesis, which may assist the leaves in resisting adverse environmental stresses such as ultraviolet radiation.

Subsequently, to investigate the role of noncatalytic CHIL in determining the product specificity of CHS, we cloned and expressed the CHS and CHIL proteins of Arabidopsis thaliana (Supplementary Fig. 3). The purified proteins were performed the in vitro enzymatic assays, and the reaction products were analyzed by HPLC-MS. Two prominent product peaks (P1 and P2) were observed during the HPLC-MS analysis when CHS was assayed with p-coumaroyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA as substrates, which is consistent with previous studies (Fig. 1b)10,11. Through comparison with authentic standards and tandem MS/MS analysis, P1 and P2 were identified as the major product of (2S)-naringenin (an isomer of NC that forms via a non-enzymatic isomerization) and the byproduct CTAL, respectively (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, compared to assays containing only CHS, the addition of CHIL significantly altered the product profile: the proportion of CTAL decreased from 27.5% to 15.9% of total products, while the production of (2S)-naringenin increased approximately 1.5-fold (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 5). These results support previous proposals21,22, and strongly indicate that CHIL functions as a specialized auxiliary protein, largely increasing the yield of the main product to modulate CHS product specificity.

Since CHIL belongs to the CHI family, could catalytically active CHI also interact with CHS in a similar manner to regulate its enzymatic activity and product specificity? To analyze the functional differences between CHI and CHIL, we performed pull-down assays with CHS using each protein individually. The results demonstrated that CHIL bound to CHS, whereas CHI did not (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, co-expression and purification of CHS and CHIL by gel filtration confirmed their stable interaction and complex formation (Fig. 1d). Subsequently, purified CHI and CHIL were individually incubated with CHS, and their effects were assessed by enzymatic assays. The results showed that CHIL dramatically enhanced both the activity and product specificity of CHS. In contrast, CHI only slightly increased CHS activity, potentially attributable to CHI utilizing the CHS product NC as its substrate, thereby indirectly increasing metabolic flux through the pathway (Fig. 1e). Phylogenetic analysis also confirmed that CHI and CHIL form distinct clades despite sharing the conserved CHI-fold domain24. These findings demonstrate that CHI and CHIL have undergone distinct functional differentiation during evolution.

The overall structure of CHS-CHIL complex

To elucidate the structural basis for CHIL’s specific binding to CHS (unlike CHI) and its consequent regulation of CHS activity and product specificity, we determined the crystal structure of the CHS-CHIL heteromeric complex. Through a two-step purification strategy, the CHS-CHIL complex of A. thaliana was purified, crystallized, and condition optimized. Its crystal structure was successfully determined at a resolution of 1.9 Å using the molecular replacement method (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 1). The crystal structure of the CHS-CHIL complex reveals a heterotetrameric assembly within the asymmetric unit, comprising two CHS and two CHIL subunits. This arrangement adopts a distinctive “blooming flower” architecture when viewed along its central axis. At the structural core, two CHS monomers form a symmetric homodimer, with each flanking CHIL subunit bound to an individual CHS monomer. Notably, the two CHIL subunits lack direct intermolecular contacts (Fig. 2a, b).

The CHS structure within the CHS-CHIL complex retains the characteristic αβαβα thiolase fold8,12, consistent with functional conservation among CHS orthologs. To investigate structural changes induced by complex formation, we superimposed CHS homodimers from A. thaliana (AtCHS, PDB ID code 6DXB) and Cyclosorus parasiticus (CpCHS, PDB ID code 7VF0) with the complexed CHS, whose root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) are 0.219 Å and 0.375 Å for the Cα atoms, respectively. The results revealed that the overall structure of CHS in CHS-CHIL complex closely resembled AtCHS and CpCHS, including the NAR and CoA binding pockets. However, subtle conformational differences were observed in the loop regions at the top of the upper domain (Fig. 2c).

Similarly, structural comparison of free AtCHIL (PDB ID: 4DOK) with complex-bound CHIL revealed high conformational conservation with RMSD values of 0.352 Å for the Cα atoms (Fig. 2d). The CHIL component of CHS-CHIL complex possesses nine β-strands and seven α-helices, forming the characteristic CHI-fold domain conserved across the CHI protein family23,24. While the global CHIL architecture remains largely unaltered upon CHS binding, a significant conformational change occurs in its β-hairpin region. This conformationally remodeled region inserts into the substrate binding pocket entrance of CHS, potentially modulating substrate access or product release (Fig. 2d).

The interface of CHS and CHIL in the complex

A detailed analysis of the interaction surface between CHS and CHIL was further conducted. The interaction surface is formed by α-helices and loops from the two CHS molecules and one CHIL molecule, burying a total surface area of 2,758 Å2 (Fig. 3a–c). As underscored in Fig. 3a–c, the CHS-CHIL binding interface is formed by two principal contact areas, ITF1 (Interface 1) and ITF2 (Interface 2), collectively creating an inverted “L” shape. The ITF1 contains residues Ile33, Ile35, His36, Phe37, Leu38, Val40, and Phe42 from CHIL, which mainly form hydrophobic interactions with residues Leu57, Lys60, Arg63, Met64, Lys67, Leu211, Leu214, Val215, Ala218, Leu219, Leu273, Leu274, Pro278, Pro313, and Ala314 from CHS (Fig. 3b). These residues from CHS form a hydrophobic pocket, functioning like a “lock”, while the β-hairpin of CHIL protrudes into the hydrophobic pocket, acting as a “key” to form a flexible switch. The ITF2 contains residues Lys94, Lys97, Gln100, Gln104, Asn107, and Tyr138 from CHIL, which form hydrogen bonds with residues Asp212, His272, Asp101, Asp261, His272, Lys275, Asp257, and Arg265 from CHS, respectively (Fig. 3c). Additionally, the residues Arg110 of CHIL and Lys151, Tyr147, Asp141 of CHS form hydrogen bonds with residues Asn107 of CHIL and Asp257, His272 of CHS, respectively, to dictate their correct positioning. The side chain of residues Ile96 forms hydrophobic interactions with Lys94 and Gln100 of CHIL, to assist in orientating these residues correctly. Notably, the lower part of ITF2 exhibits greater interaction stability than the upper part, suggesting that the lower part may provide a dynamic hinged pivot that allows the upper part to move more flexibly.

To verify the significance of the interaction surfaces involved in CHS-CHIL, we performed mutation-based functional analyses. In vitro pull-down experiments showed that the CHS mutations D212A, D261A, H263A, H272A, and K275A in ITF2 significantly reduced the interactions between the CHS and the CHIL (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 7a). Accordingly, these mutations also severely affected the activity of both the CHS-CHIL complex and isolated CHS (Fig. 3e). These key ITF2 residues are adjacent to the active pocket (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Such mutations may impair CHS function by affecting substrate binding or catalytic activity. Therefore, observed effects in CHS mutants may not solely reflect disrupted CHS-CHIL interactions. Thus, CHIL mutants targeting ITF2 were constructed and analyzed by pull-down and activity assays. The interactions between CHS and CHIL were slightly decreased by K94A mutations, and severely impaired by K97A, Q100A, Q104A, Y138A, and M3A (K94A/K97A/Q100A) (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 7b). Consistently, K94A minimally affected enzymatic activity, whereas other single mutations severely impaired it. The multiple mutations M5A (K94A/K97A/Q100A/Q104A/N107A) and M6A (K94A/K97A/Q100A/Q104A/N107A/Y138A) completely abolished the regulatory function of CHIL (Fig. 3g). Similarly, the key amino acid residues in ITF1, located within and around the β-hairpin of CHIL, were selected for mutational analyses. As shown in Fig. 3h, i, the interactions between CHS and CHIL were moderately impaired by H36A but severely impaired by F37A and M4A (I35A/H36A/F37A/L38A) mutant (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Furthermore, we performed ITC experiments on several representative mutants of the CHIL protein from ITF1 and ITF2 (Supplementary Fig. 9). The results showed that the binding affinity between CHIL and CHS was 7.8 μM. Compared to wild-type CHIL, the H36A mutant still bound to CHS, but with weakened binding affinity, as reflected by a 50% increase in the KD value. In contrast, no detectable binding was observed for the F37A, M4A, K97A, Q100A, or M3A mutants of CHIL, as no reliable KD values could be determined. Thus, these mutations (from ITF1 and ITF2) almost eliminated the beneficial effects of CHIL on CHS. Collectively, these results suggested that both ITF1 and ITF2 mediate the biological functions of CHIL, confirming that CHIL regulates CHS activity and product specificity through binding modulation.

Site 36 is central to the regulatory function of CHIL

In the structure of CHS-CHIL complex, we observed the β-hairpin region of CHIL protruding into the catalytic pocket entrance of CHS, while also forming the vital interaction interface ITF1 (Figs. 2, 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 8b). This structural arrangement suggests that the β-hairpin region of CHIL may play a regulatory role in CHS function. Thus, the reaction products of NAR and CoA were modeled into the CHS-CHIL complex from CpCHS (PDB ID code 7VF0), which revealed the significant steric hindrance between the CHIL’s β-hairpin and CoA (Fig. 4a, b). To investigate this phenomenon, we co-purified the GST-tagged CHS with 6×His-tagged CHIL using Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography, then incubated the column with CoA for 30 minutes. After washing with ten column volumes of lysis buffer, a substantial amount of CHS was eluted, resulting in a sub-stoichiometric CHS:CHIL ratio (Fig. 4c). This biochemical evidence confirms an antagonistic relationship between CoA and CHIL, explaining our failure to obtain crystals of the CHS-CHIL complex with bound NAR and CoA despite several attempts. These findings suggested that CHIL modulates the activity and product specificity of CHS by accelerating CoA release to increase reaction rates.

To further investigate the role of β-hairpin region in CHIL, we first analyzed the conservation of amino acid residues in this area. As shown in Fig. 4d, the residues from Glu34 to Phe37 are highly conserved in plants (Fig. 4d). Structural analysis of the CHS-CHIL complex revealed that residue His36 of CHIL influences the position and orientation of residue Phe220 through hydrophobic interactions with residues Val215 and Leu219 of CHS (Fig. 4e). Notably, Val215, Leu219, and Phe220 in CHS are also evolutionarily conserved in plants (Fig. 4d). Phe220 is defined as one of the key residues to form the active site of CHS, providing van der Waals interactions for substrate binding and participating the catalytic process8. The mutations of V215A/L219A and F220A abolish CHS activity, similar to the F271A mutation. Previous studies have shown that Phe271 separates the coumaroyl-binding site from the cyclization pocket and may function as a mobile steric gate during successive rounds of polyketide elongation, underscoring its essential role in CHS catalysis8. To assess the function of position 36 in CHIL, His36 was replaced with different types of amino acid residues to evaluate its role, which possessed different sizes and charges of the side chain. Replacing it with valine (H36V), charged residues (H36D and H36E), or polar residues (H36Y and H36N) severely impaired the CHIL function, whereas substitution with hydrophobic amino acid residues (H36I, H36F and H36W) resulted in product distributions similar to those of the CHIL wild-type (Fig. 4f). Strikingly, substituting methionine or leucine for histidine (H36M or H36L) increased naringenin yield by 39% or 79%, respectively, with its proportion in the total products also rising from 88% to 91% or 95% (Fig. 4f).

Sequence alignment and structure analysis unequivocally demonstrated that CHIL exhibits extraordinary conservation throughout plant evolution. Based on this conservation, we investigated whether the wild-type CHIL protein of Arabidopsis thaliana and its functional-enhanced H36L mutant could regulate the activity of CHS in heterologous species. Functional characterization revealed that CHIL from Arabidopsis thaliana significantly enhanced both the catalytic activity and product specificity of CHSs from rice, maize, and soybean (Fig. 4g). Notably, the H36L mutant further potentiated CHIL’s regulatory capacity in a species-independent manner (Fig. 4g). Furthermore, we generated H38L and H41L mutants at the corresponding sites in ginkgo and rice CHIL, respectively. Functional assays revealed that these mutants elicited significant enhancements in CHIL-mediated CHS regulation, with activity improvements of 14% and 20% observed in ginkgo and rice, respectively (Fig. 4h). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the His36 residue is critical for CHIL-mediated regulation of CHS activity. The H36L mutation confers a significant enhancement to wild-type CHIL function across diverse plant species, confirming its conserved regulatory role in chalcone biosynthesis.

Taken together, CHIL regulates CHS activity through the contact surface ITF1 and ITF2 (Figs. 3 and 4, Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Specifically, via its β-hairpin domain, CHIL enhances the enzymatic activity and product specificity of CHS by facilitating CoA release and aiding in the positioning of key amino acid residues within CHS (Figs. 3 and 4, Supplementary Fig. 8b). Based on these structural and biochemical analyses, the presence of CHIL should enhance the catalytic efficiency of CHS by increasing the kcat values for both substrates. Given this, a more detailed analysis of the enzyme kinetics was conducted. For malonyl-CoA, the enzymatic kinetic parameters were characterized for individual CHS, as well as for CHS in combination with wild-type CHIL and representative CHIL mutants (Supplementary Table 2). The results demonstrate that CHIL addition markedly enhanced CHS catalytic efficiency, increasing both Vmax and kcat by approximately 80%, while also raising the Km value by about 53%. Kinetic characterization of CHIL mutants further revealed distinct functional effects: the H36L mutant, which enhances CHS activation compared to wild-type CHIL, elevated Vmax and kcat by an additional 36%, indicating a strengthened regulatory capacity. In contrast, two binding-deficient mutants, M4A located at ITF1 and Q100A located at ITF2, exhibited kinetic parameters similar to those measured in the absence of CHIL. This supports the conclusion that the CHIL-CHS interaction is essential for the observed catalytic enhancement. Similarly, for p-coumaroyl-CoA, CHIL addition significantly improved CHS catalytic efficiency, with Vmax and kcat increasing by approximately 50% and Km rising by about 65%. The H36L mutant further enhanced this effect, elevating Vmax and kcat by an additional 66% relative to wild-type CHIL. The enzyme activity assays confirmed that CHIL significantly enhances the catalytic activity of CHS. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting a 2- to 15-fold increase in kcat in the presence of CHIL across diverse plant species21.

Evolution of CHIL

To further investigate the role of critical residues in the evolutionary relationship between CHIL and CHI, we compared their differences within the activity pocket and at the CHS contact surface (Supplementary Fig. 10). Superposition analysis revealed that Trp184 and Tyr185 in CHIL, with their bulky side chains, occupy the same active site positions as in MsCHI (PDB ID code 1EYQ) and AtCHI (PDB ID code 4DOI) (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Other residues that form the substrate-binding pocket are identical between CHIL and MsCHI, except for Thr31/Arg36, Asp32/Gly37, Ile33/Leu38, Val90/Gly95, Val92/Lys97, Ile96/Leu101, Gln100/Glu105, Gln104/Lys109, Leu105/Val110, and Thr108/Asn113 (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Furthermore, we analyzed the CHS-CHIL complex interface residues on CHIL and compared them with AtCHI. Within ITF1, several residues were conserved, except for Ile33/Leu49, His36/Gln52, Phe37/Gly53, and Leu38/Lys54 (Supplementary Fig. 10c). In contrast, within ITF2, only Gln100/Gln104 remained identical (Supplementary Fig. 10d).

To comprehensively elucidate the evolutionary trajectory of CHIL proteins in plants, we selected 18 representative species spanning diverse phylogenetic lineages for analysis, including a cyanobacterium (Anabaena cylindrica), a green alga (Chlamydomonas reinhardtii), a liverwort (Marchantia polymorpha), a moss (Physcomitrella patens), two lycophytes (Selaginella martensii and Selaginella kraussiana), three ferns (Azolla filiculoides, Odontosoria chinensis and Lindsaea orbiculata), two gymnosperms (Ginkgo biloba and Pinus tabuliformis), and seven angiosperms. The angiosperms comprised a basal angiosperm (Amborella trichopoda), three eudicots (Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Glycine max), and three monocots (Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Triticum aestivum). Using the Arabidopsis thaliana CHIL sequence, we performed BLASTP searches against protein databases, identifying 7,240 candidate genes predominantly from the Magnoliopsida class (Supplementary Table 3). To ensure representativeness, candidate genes from these 18 species listed above were selected for subsequent analysis. Leveraging residues involved in the CHI active site and the CHS-CHIL complex interface as discriminatory criteria, we developed a Structure-based Key Amino acid residues clustering plus Sequence Alignment method (SKSA, see methods for details) (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 11). Ultimately, 11 CHIL proteins from 10 plant species were successfully expressed in E. coli and stably purified, which served as the foundation for constructing a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 13). Employing the same strategy, the evolutionary tree for CHI proteins was also constructed (Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 12a). The results suggested that the CHIL and CHI proteins originated in bryophytes and have been conserved across land plant species, encompassing lycophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, and angiosperms.

To validate the functional roles of the cloned CHILs, 11 CHS candidates were obtained from the corresponding 10 plant species using the same strategy employed for searching and cloning CHILs. Similarly, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on these CHS protein sequences (Supplementary Fig. 12b, Supplementary Fig. 14). Comparison of the critical residues mentioned above revealed that most are highly conserved. Enzymatic assays of CHIL with corresponding CHS from various plant species demonstrated that CHIL proteins from distinct evolutionary lineages consistently modulate the product specificity and enhanced the catalytic activity of CHS (Fig. 5b). Notably, the production of (2S)-naringenin increased 2.4- to 5-fold in gymnosperms and angiosperms, but dramatically increased between 6- to 14-fold in bryophytes, lycophytes, and ferns (Supplementary Fig. 12c). These results further indicate that the product specificity and enzyme activity of CHS in bryophytes, lycophytes, and ferns are generally lower than in gymnosperms and angiosperms.

Furthermore, we analyzed the interaction interfaces ITF1 and ITF2, and the results revealed differences in the residues at sites 36 and 37 of the CHIL β-hairpin region between euphyllophytes (ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperm) and non-euphyllophytes (bryophytes and lycophytes). Specifically, the glutamate (E) and threonine (T) present at sites 36 and 37 in non-euphyllophytes have been substituted with histidine (H) and phenylalanine/tyrosine (F/Y) in euphyllophytes. Site-directed mutants H36E and H36E/F37T of CHIL were constructed, and the enzymatic assays demonstrated that the H36E/F37T double mutant significantly enhanced enzymatic activity by approximately 16% (Fig. 4f). These findings not only clarify the evolutionary divergence of CHIL functional sites but also provide a foundation for designing crops with high flavonoid content based on evolutionary history.

Collectively, our findings provide compelling evidence that the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway likely emerged as a crucial specialized metabolic adaptation in land plants following their transition from aquatic to terrestrial habitats. This pathway enabled them to cope with environmental challenges such as UV-B radiation, drought, and interactions with pathogens and herbivores.

Discussion

Flavonoids, renowned for their diverse biological activities, confer substantial health benefits to humans. In plants, the biosynthesis of flavonoids represents a highly coordinated process, precisely regulated by developmental cues and environmental stimuli5–7,31–33. This pathway diverges from the general phenylpropanoid pathway at the rate-limiting step catalyzed by chalcone synthase (CHS)34,35. The regulation of CHS genes occurs at the transcriptional level with multiple transcription factors controlling its expression, including the WD40, MYB, and basic Helix-Loop-Helix (bHLH) families36–38. Additionally, the protein expression and accumulation levels of CHS are also fine-tuned by post-transcriptional, and post-translational levels39–42. However, due to CHS’s catalytic promiscuity, which results in the production of various by-products, the underlying molecular mechanism governing the regulation of its product specificity at protein-protein interaction levels remains largely unknown.

In transcription level, single-nucleus RNA-seq analysis revealed co-expression of CHS and CHIL during Arabidopsis leaf development, with peak activity at mature (S3) and early senescence (S4) stages (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). This co-expression suggests critical roles in flavonoid metabolism and plant environmental stress adaptation, highlighting their synergistic potential for enhancing stress resilience across species. In protein-protein interaction level, our findings confirmed that CHIL protein (type IV chalcone isomerase) can interact with CHS to modulate its catalytic activity and product specificity (Fig. 1)21,22,28. To elucidate this regulatory mechanism, we determined the crystal structure of the CHS-CHIL complex (Fig. 2). The overall structure of the CHS-CHIL complex revealed that CHS still maintains a symmetrical homodimer, consistent with previously reported the isolated CHS crystal structures, indicating that this dimerization is essential for its catalytic function8,10–12. Two CHIL molecules bind opposite the CHS core, each tightly interacting with a single CHS monomer without direct CHIL-CHIL interaction. This configuration enables independent regulation of each CHS monomer by a CHIL molecule, providing mechanistic flexibility.

The crystal structure of CHS-CHIL complex further showed that the CHS-CHIL interface comprises two interaction interfaces, ITF1 and ITF2, forming an inverted “L”-shaped hinge (Fig. 3a). The hinged pivot is stabilized by multiple hydrophilic interactions between the amino acid residues from CHS and CHIL for the enzyme complex (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 8b). If any critical residues in ITF1 or ITF2 were mutated, thereby disrupting the interaction between CHS and CHIL, CHIL would lose its ability to regulate both the catalytic activity and product specificity of CHS (Fig. 3d–i). Notably, the β-hairpin region of CHIL, which protrudes toward the entrance of CHS’s substrate-binding pocket, undergoes positional and conformational changes during CHS’s catalytic reaction. This structural transformation of CHIL accelerates CoA release, thereby modulating the catalytic activity and product specificity of CHS. Specifically, the residue His36, located in the top end of the β-hairpin of CHIL, regulates the catalytic activity and product specificity of CHS by adjusting the position and orientation of residue Phe220 through hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 4a, b, e). The high conservation of these residues across plant species indicates a fundamental regulatory mechanism (Fig. 4d, e). Furthermore, we rationally designed several mutants at the His36 position, and the enzymatic assay revealed that the H36L mutation significantly enhanced the regulatory capacity of CHIL (Fig. 4f–h). Meanwhile, the ITC experiment revealed that the H36L mutant displayed an approximately 5.2-fold greater binding affinity for CHS relative to the wild-type CHIL (Supplementary Fig. 9). Our results imply that CHIL could serve as a potential target for manipulating flavonoid metabolic flux within the phenylpropanoid pathway in plants.

Furthermore, our kinetic analysis reveals that CHIL increases the Km of CHS for both substrates while maintaining comparable kcat/Km ratios. We emphasize that kcat/Km primarily reflects catalytic efficiency near zero substrate concentration, which differs substantially from actual metabolic conditions. In enzyme-substrate condensates like metabolons, substrate concentrations remain high relative to Km. Under such conditions, kcat becomes the primary determinant of catalytic rate. Consequently, CHIL’s enhancement of kcat provides a plausible mechanism for its function in accelerating product synthesis in physiological environments. Similar results have been reported in previous studies21. Based on our structural and biochemical analyses, a simplified functional model for the CHS-CHIL complex is proposed (Fig. 4i). In the presence of CHIL, it binds to CHS via the contact surface ITF1 and ITF2; upon the binding of substrates p-coumaroyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA into the catalytic pocket, the β-hairpin of CHIL undergoes positional and conformational changes. This opens the active pocket channel to permit substrate entry, subsequently promotes CoA release from CHS to enhance its catalytic activity, and meanwhile, the hydrophobic residues at the tip of CHIL’s β-hairpin stabilize the reaction intermediates to increase the formation of the main product of CHS.

Following the biochemical experiments, we performed conventional molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to further investigate how CHIL modulates CHS activity across different catalytic stages. We examined three representative binding states of CHS in the presence and absence of CHIL: substrate-bound (p-coumaroyl-CoA or malonyl-CoA) and product-bound (NAR and CoA). Time evolution analyses show that RMSD of heavy atoms of proteins and ligands reached stable plateaus within 25 ns and the fluctuations of the RMSD in all 200 ns simulations is very low (maximum standard deviation: 0.26 Å, Supplementary Fig. 15a). To quantitatively assess the stability of substrates and products within the CHS binding pocket, we calculated the RMSD of the ligand heavy atoms after aligning to the initial protein structures. In the substrate-bound simulations, p-coumaroyl-CoA exhibited an average RMSD of 4.0 Å (median: 4.1 Å, standard deviation (std): 0.7 Å) in the presence of CHIL, compared with 5.6 Å (median: 5.4 Å, std: 1.9 Å) in the CHIL-free systems. Similarly, malonyl-CoA exhibited an average RMSD of 4.5 Å (median: 3.8 Å, std: 2.0 Å) with the presence of CHIL, versus 6.2 Å (median: 6.2 Å, std: 2.1 Å) without CHIL (Supplementary Fig. 16). By calculating the angle of CoA moiety of the substates (Supplementary Fig. 16b), we observed that the fluctuation of the angle of p-coumaroyl-CoA (std: 24.1°) or malonyl-CoA (std: 32.9°) is markedly lower than those (std: 39.6° and 40.9°, respectively) in the CHIL-free systems. This indicates that CHIL restricts the conformational flexibility of bound substrates. The NAR exhibited very stable in both cases with the averaged RMSD of 2.5 Å (median: 2.4 Å, std: 0.8 Å) at the present of CHIL, compared with 2.3 Å (median: 2.2 Å, std: 0.3 Å) in the absent of CHIL. Except NAR, the lower RMSD values and narrower RMSD fluctuation in the CHIL-bound systems indicate that CHIL can enhance the occupancy of substrates in the binding pocket. In the product-bound simulations, CoA displayed an average RMSD of 6.5 Å (median: 6.3 Å, std: 2.2 Å) in the presence of CHIL, compared with 4.2 Å (median: 4.5 Å, std: 1.2 Å) in the absence of CHIL (Supplementary Fig. 16). The CoA moiety exhibits pronounced angular fluctuations between 40° and 96° in replicas 1 and 3, whereas in the absence of CHIL the angles remain relatively stable-around 47° in replica 3 and 123° in replicas 1 and 2 (Supplementary Fig. 16b). These observations suggest that CHIL can make substrates more stable in their binding pockets and CoA less stable, which may contribute the occurrence of catalytic reaction of substates and the release of CoA. We further analyzed the motion of the CHIL’s β-hairpin by calculating the angle defined by the direction vector from the Cα atom of T31 to that of I35 relative to its initial orientation. We observed that the β-hairpin can rotated up to 60° in the three binding states and the orientation of the β-hairpin ends with ~45° degree in most of 200 ns simulations (6/9) (Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, in replica 1 of the CoA-bound system, the β-hairpin adopts a much more stable conformation centered around ~17°. This stability correlates with hydrogen bonds formed between the terminal nitrogen of CoA and the carboxylate oxygens of E34 (occupancy: 47.0%) or the backbone oxygen of F37 (occupancy: 5.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 17), suggesting that CoA directly interact the β-hairpin in this replica. By contrast, CHIL forms only limited interactions with p-coumaroyl-CoA and almost none with malonyl-CoA. The markedly higher frequency of CoA-CHIL interactions, together with the pronounced fluctuations of CoA in CHIL-bound simulations (Supplementary Fig. 17), indicates increased CoA mobility. This enhanced mobility may facilitate its release from the active site. Together, these observations suggest that CHIL can promote catalysis by stabilizing substrates and facilitating product release, thereby accelerating the overall reaction cycle. Meanwhile, we performed MD simulations to investigate whether CHIL differentially stabilizes canonical products (NC and NAR) versus derailment by-products (BNY and CTAL). Four CHS-CHIL systems were constructed, each bound with a single ligand, and RMSD of heavy atoms of proteins and ligands reached stable plateaus within 25 ns (maximum deviation: 0.27 Å, Supplementary Fig. 15b). The ligands maintained binding poses close to their initial positions, as reflected by their average RMSD values (CTAL: 4.1 Å, BNY: 3.4 Å, NC: 3.0 Å, NAR: 3.4 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 18). Notably, CTAL displayed two distinct RMSD peaks (3.25 Å and 5.86 Å), suggesting the existence of two alternative binding positions (Supplementary Fig. 18). Although CTAL was relatively less stable, these results indicate that CHIL does not significantly alter the stability of the final bound products, implying that its regulatory effect likely occurs at earlier stages of the catalytic cycle, such as substrate binding or intermediates.

Sequence and structural analyses, along with interaction studies involving CHS, revealed a low similarity between CHI and CHIL (Supplementary Fig. 10). This underscores the distinct differences between CHIL and CHI, despite CHIL’s classification as a type IV CHI protein. Recent studies have proposed that CHIL may function acts as a metabolite sensor to modulate the flavonoid pathway, with the CHS-CHI-CHIL ternary system governing the quantity and type of flavonoids produced in plant cells43,44. Although co-crystallization attempts for the CHS-CHIL complex bound to NAR and CoA was unsuccessful, our future research will focus on optimizing the conditions to determine the structures of the CHS-CHIL-NAR complex, the CHS-CHIL-CHI complex, and the CHS-CHIL-CHI-NAR complex. This work aims to elucidate how CHIL senses substrates to modulate CHS function.

Based on our comprehensive investigation of CHIL proteins, we developed a structure-based method combining key amino acid residue clustering with sequence alignment to enable more convenient, accurate, and rapid identification of CHILs from plant genome databases. Through this approach, 11 CHILs from various plant lineages, including bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms, were cloned, expressed, purified, and characterized their functions. The enzymatic assays confirmed that all CHILs could regulate the catalytic activity and product specificity of their corresponding CHSs (Fig. 5). Notably, CHILs from bryophytes, lycophytes, and ferns exhibited stronger regulatory effects compared to those from gymnosperms and angiosperms. These findings expand our understanding of CHIL function and provide promising targets for synthetic biology applications in crop design and microbial flavonoid production45.

Methods

Gene cloning and protein purification

Total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat No./ID: 217084). Total RNA (1 µg) was treated with 1 µL of DNase I (1.0 unit/μL; Thermo Fisher, Cat No./ID: EN0521) and complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were synthesized with the All-in-one Kit (Vazyme, Cat No./ID: R333-01). To construct plasmids for prokaryotic expression of full-length CHS (residues 1-395), CHIL (residues 1-209) and CHI (residues 1-246) of Arabidopsis thaliana in Escherichia coli, the coding sequence of these genes were PCR-amplified and cloned into pETDuet-1 vector with a 6×His-tag at the N-terminus. The constructs for point mutations were generated by overlap PCR and verified by DNA sequencing. All the plasmids were transformed into the E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain or Rosetta (DE3) cells. The transformed bacterial cells were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with ampicillin at 37 °C to an OD600 of 0.8, and then induced by 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, Sangon Biotech, Cat No./ID: A100487) for 12 h at 18 °C. The cells were harvested and resuspended in buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethane sulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Sangon Biotech, Cat No./ID: A100754). Cells were lysed by a high-pressure cell disruptor at 15,000 p.s.i. (pounds per square inch), and the lysate was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 45 min. The supernatant was loaded onto a Ni2+-NTA affinity column (Qiagen, Cat No./ID: 30230) and washed with buffer A plus 20 mM imidazole. Proteins were eluted by buffer A plus 250 mM imidazole and purified by gel filtration using a Superdex 200 Increase column (Cytiva, Cat No./ID: 28990944) in buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl) on an ÄKTA pure system. The sample was eluted at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The elution profile was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm. Peak fractions were collected and concentrated for subsequent structural and biochemical studies.

Crystallization, data collection and structure determination

Protein was concentrated to 10 mg/mL for crystal screening, and crystals of the CHS-CHIL complex were grown at 20 °C using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method by mixing 0.5 μL of protein with 0.5 μL of reservoir solution containing 0.2 M trimethylamine N-oxide dihydrate, 0.1 M Tris pH 8.5, 20% (w/v) polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 2000. All crystals were transferred into cryoprotectant solution containing their respective mother liquor plus 30% (v/v) glycerol, before being flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage. Data collection was performed at BL19U1/ BL18U/ BL02U beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) under 100 K liquid nitrogen stream (wavelength = 0.9798 Å). The data were processed with HKL300046, and the initial phase was determined by molecular replacement. All of the models were manually built in Coot47, and were refined by iterative rounds of manual adjustment with Coot and refinement with Phenix48. Data collection and structure refinement statistics are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

In vitro enzymatic assay

The enzymatic assay process and HPLC parameters were applied according to the previously reported studies10,11,22. Specifically, the CHS and CHS-CHIL complex enzymatic assays mixture, including 50 μM p-coumaroyl-CoA (Shanghai YuanYe Bio-Technology, Cat No./ID: S25276), 100 μM malonyl-CoA (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No./ID: M4263), and 2.5 μg of each purified proteins in 20 mM Tris HCl buffer at pH 7.0 reached the final volume of 100 μL for 10 min at 35 °C. The reactions terminated by the addition of 10 μL of 10% glacial acetic acid and 200 μL methanol. After centrifugation, a 10 μL volume of the supernatant was separated through a reverse-phase C18 column (Agilent® ZORBX SB-C18, 5 μm 4.6×150 mm) using an Agilent 1260 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with a 6460 Triple Quadrupole LC-MS system. Samples were analyzed by electrospray ionization (ESI) in negative ionization mode. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and a flow rate of 1.0 ml/min was used. A binary gradient system was employed, consisted of solvent A (methanol) and solvent B (water plus 0.1% formic acid). A linear gradient of 30% A and 70% B to 80% A and 20% B was supplied over 20 min. Standard naringenin (Shanghai YuanYe Bio-Technology, Cat No./ID: B21596) solutions of reference compounds were used for calibration.

For kinetic characterization, assay mixtures contained 2.5 μg of purified CHS and 2.5 μg of purified CHIL (either wild-type or mutants) with varied substrate concentrations (5-100 μM). Kinetic parameters for p-coumaroyl-CoA were determined in the presence of 100 μM malonyl-CoA; similarly, malonyl-CoA kinetics were assessed with 50 μM p-coumaroyl-CoA. Reactions proceeded for 10 min at 35 °C and were terminated by adding 10 μL of 10% glacial acetic acid and 200 μL methanol. Product analysis was performed as described above. Kinetic values were derived by nonlinear regression to the Michaelis-Menten equation using GraphPad Prism 10.

In vitro pull-down assays

The wild-type and mutated versions of CHS, CHIL and CHI were generated by PCR-based mutagenesis. The oligonucleotide primers are given in Supplementary Table 4. The 6×His-tagged wild-type and mutated CHIL or CHI proteins were co-expressed with Glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tagged wild-type and mutated CHS in E. coli Rosetta (DE3). After cell disruption and centrifugation, the supernatants were loaded to Ni2+-NTA affinity resin (Qiagen, Cat No./ID: 30230). The nonspecific bound protein was washed off. The resin was boiled, examined with Sodium Dodecyl Sulphate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and visualized by Coomassie Blue staining.

ITC analysis

ITC experiments were performed with the MicroCal ITC200 system (Malvern Instruments) at 20 °C in PBS buffer (Biopico Life Sciences, Cat No./ID: 830101). Proteins were purified as described above, using PBS as the buffer; For detection of CHIL and its mutants binding to CHS, the syringe was filled with 0.5 mM CHIL or its mutants, and the cell was filled with 50 μM CHS protein. The sample in the syringe was added to the sample cell by sequential injections of 2 μL aliquots followed by 120 s of equilibration after each injection. There was a total of 20 injections. For analysis, the heat released by each injection was integrated, and the background was subtracted. The data were fit to the Wiseman isotherm with the Origin ITC analysis package. The experiments were repeated at least three times for each sample.

Phylogenetic tree construction

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of CHS, CHIL, and CHI proteins, we performed separate BLASTP searches using Arabidopsis thaliana CHS (AT5G13930.1), CHIL (AT5G05270.1), and CHI (AT3G55120.1) sequences against protein databases of other selected species. For each of the three protein families, a separate phylogenetic tree was constructed to identify the closest homologs across species based on phylogenetic proximity. Selected sequences included: Marchantia polymorpha (CHIL: PTQ28039; CHI: PTQ28320; CHS: PTQ44319), Physcomitrella patens (CHIL: Pp6c4_13620V6.3; CHI: Pp6c26_2490V6.3; CHS: Pp6c24_6740V6.1), Selaginella kraussiana (CHIL: SK08G12910.mRNA1; CHI: SK06G16750.mRNA1; CHS: SK02G19610.mRNA1), Selaginella martensii (CHIL: Sm_0626930.1; CHI: Sm_0310740.1; CHS: Sm_0158520.1), Azolla filiculoides (CHIL: Azfi_s0242.g059817; CHI: Azfi_s0030.g024403; CHS: Azfi_s0168.g054633), Ginkgo biloba (CHIL: evm.model.chr1.2875; CHI: evm.model.chr4.1966; CHS: evm.model.chr6.1424), Pinus tabuliformis (CHIL: Pt5G49490.1; CHI: PtQG07910.3; CHS: Pt3G28890.1), Amborella trichopoda (CHIL: XP_011622657.1; CHI: XP_006850685.1; CHS: XP_011627026.1), Odontosoria chinensis (CHIL: PX600431; CHI: MK216764; CHS: ON462260.1), Lindsaea orbiculata (CHIL: MK216766; CHI: MK216765; CHS: MK216767), Solanum lycopersicum (CHIL: Solyc05g052240.3.1; CHI: Solyc05g010310.3.1; CHS: Solyc05g053550.3.1), Glycine max (CHIL: KRH53728; CHI: KRG92978; CHS: KRG94730), Oryza sativa (CHIL: Os11t0116300-01; CHI: Os03t0819600-01; CHS: Os11t0530600-01), Zea mays (CHIL: Zm00001eb163860_P001; CHI: Zm00001eb062510_P001; CHS: Zm00001eb322090_P001), Triticum aestivum (CHIL: TraesCS5B02G145800.1; CHI: TraesCS5A02G475600.1; CHS: TraesCS2B02G558400.1), Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (CHIL-like: XP_042928144.1; CHI-like: XP_042918416.1; CHS-like: XP_042914738.1), and Anabaena cylindrica (outgroup; CHIL-like: WP_015213863.1; CHI-like: WP_015214598.1; CHS-like: WP_015217128.1). Phylogenetic trees were then generated using IQ-TREE250 with ultrafast bootstrap and SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test parameters (-bb 1000 -alrt 1000), with Anabaena cylindrica designated as the outgroup. Phylogenetic tree were visualized using the ggtree package51.

SKSA algorithm development and implementation

To systematically identify and classify CHIL proteins across diverse plant species, we developed a Structure-based Key Amino acid residues clustering plus Sequence Alignment (SKSA) algorithm. This method integrates structural and sequence-based features to improve the specificity of CHIL protein prediction. The SKSA workflow operates through a two-step analytical process. First, it determines the sequence homology between a query protein and experimentally validated CHIL proteins using BLASTP and multiple sequence alignment. Second, for confirmed CHIL homologs, SKSA quantitatively evaluates their potential regulatory activity by analyzing the conservation patterns of key residues associated with CHI active sites and CHS-CHIL complex interfaces. This residue-level assessment provides a structural basis for predicting CHIL functional diversity and evolutionary conservation (Supplementary Fig. 11). The algorithm was implemented in Python, employing standard bioinformatics libraries for alignment and clustering. The full source code, datasets, and usage documentation are available on GitHub at https://github.com/xuzhougeng/SKSA. The code supporting this study has been deposited in Zenodo and is accessible via: 10.5281/zenodo.18618601.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq data analysis

We reanalyzed single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) data from six developmental stages (S1-S6) of Arabidopsis thaliana leaves, sourced from a prior study30, where S3 represents the fully expanded mature stage and S4 marks the onset of senescence with marginal leaf yellowing (Supplementary Fig. 1). Epidermal (Epi) cell transcriptomes from S3 and S4 were integrated using Seurat v4.352 (R package). Following the standard Seurat workflow, the top 20 principal components were selected for dimensionality reduction and clustering, with cell clusters visualized via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between S3 and S4 were identified using the Wilcoxon test in FindMarkers52. The top 200 DEGs underwent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler53 (R package). For downstream analysis, we retained only cells with non-zero expression of both AT5G13930 and AT5G05270 genes and excluded cell types with fewer than three such cells. Hypergeometric tests (P < 0.01) on FPKM-normalized count data revealed significant enrichment of these genes in Epi cells at S1, S2, S3, and S6 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Pearson correlation analysis further revealed stage- and cell-type-specific co-expression patterns: significant positive correlations between AT5G13930 (CHS) and AT5G05270 (CHIL) were observed across all cell types at S1, S2, S3, and S6. Notably, mesophyll (Mes) cells showed significant correlations exclusively at S1 and S2, whereas epidermal (Epi) cells exhibited significance at S2, S3, and S6. By contrast, bundle sheath (BS) cells displayed significant correlation only at S3 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

To elucidate how CHIL affects different stages of the CHS catalytic process, we performed MD simulations on six systems corresponding to three representative binding states with or without CHIL: substrate-bound (p-coumaroyl-CoA or malonyl-CoA) and product-bound (NAR and CoA). Initial complex structures were prepared as follows. For CHS, we used the experimentally determined crystal structures available from this work (PDB ID: 9WBE) to ensure accurate active site geometry. For the product-bound ligands NAR and CoA, binding poses were extracted from a previously resolved CHS-product complex (PDB ID: 7VF0) to maintain experimentally validated ligand positions. For other components (CHIL, p-coumaroyl-CoA, and malonyl-CoA), we used AlphaFold354 to predict the dimeric CHS-CHIL assemblies following the alignment to the CHS crystal structure for their coordinates. The predicted CHIL structure was used instead of the crystallographic model to avoid steric clashes with substrates or CoA observed in preliminary modeling. All systems were constructed using CHARMM-GUI (Version: 2.0)55,56 with the CHARMM36m57 force field for proteins and CGenFF4.658 for small molecules. Protonation states were assigned at pH 7.0 using PROPKA3.159. Systems were solvated with TIP3P water60 and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. The simulation boxes measured approximately 97 × 89 × 73 Å3 (without CHIL) or 121 × 100 × 91 Å3 (with CHIL), containing ~59,000 or ~104,000 atoms, respectively. Energy minimization was carried out using steepest-descent and adopted-basis Newton-Raphson algorithms61 (50 steps each). Solvent relaxation was performed under NVT conditions for 5 ps with restraint force constant of 400.0 kJ/mol/nm2 on protein backbone and small molecule heavy atoms and that of 40.0 kJ/mol/nm2 on protein sidechain atoms. Production simulations were conducted using OpenMM7.662 at 303.15 K and 1 bar with a 2 fs timestep. Long-range electrostatics were computed using the particle-mesh Ewald method63 with a 12 Å real-space cutoff, and Lennard-Jones interactions were smoothed between 10 Å and 12 Å. Hydrogen-involving bonds were constrained with SHAKE64. Each simulation was run for 200 ns and repeated three times with different random seeds. For each simulation, one frame was saved every 0.1 ns and 2000 frames were generated for analysis. Trajectory analyses, including RMSD and ligand movement quantification, were performed using MDAnalysis2.365. PyMOL3.1 was adopted to illustrate structures. In addition, to examine the potential role of CHIL in balancing the formation of naringenin chalcone versus derailment products, we constructed four additional CHS-CHIL systems bound with either the canonical products (NC and NAR) or the byproducts (BNY and CTAL). The CHS and CHIL structures were obtained from this study, while the bound small molecules were modeled using AlphaFold3 predictions (except NAR, taken from PDB ID: 7VF0). System preparation, force field parameters, and simulation protocols were identical to those described above. The data used in this study are publicly available in Zenodo at 10.5281/zenodo.18160349.

Statistics

Two-tailed Student’s t test and ordinary one-way ANOVA with uncorrected Fisher’s LSD test were performed to determine the statistical significance between different samples in quantification. Significant differences are indicated by different lettering at P < 0.05 unless otherwise stated. All statistic results and graphs were generated by GraphPad Prism 10 (www.graphpad.com). The detailed descriptive statistics and results are given in Source Data file.
