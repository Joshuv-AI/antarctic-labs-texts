Augmenting Large Language Models With National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines for Improved and Standardized Adjuvant Therapy Recommendations in Postoperative Breast Cancer Cases
Authors: Serene Si Ning Goh, Ragunathan Mariappan, Grace Soo Woon Tan, Jiali Yao, Fook Ming Hew, Yenshing Yeo, Samuel Guan Wei Ow, Wee Yao Koh, Nesaretnam Barr Kumarakulasingh, Teng Hwee Tan, Bee Choo Tai, Mikael Hartman, Kee Yuan Ngiam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEMultidisciplinary breast tumor boards (MTBs) are essential for optimizing breast cancer treatment but face challenges related to logistics, variability in expertise, and lack of standardization. Large language models may support clinical decision making. This study evaluates the accuracy of adjuvant therapy recommendations generated by an artificial intelligence (AI)–driven tool, TheSerenityBot (TSB), in comparison with Claude-2 and GPT-4, using expert MTB consensus as the reference.METHODSPostoperative breast cancer cases reviewed at the National University Hospital, Singapore, between June and November 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. Eligible patients were women with invasive or preinvasive breast cancer who underwent surgery. Metastatic cases were excluded. TSB, a Claude-2–based model augmented with 2023 National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines, generated adjuvant therapy recommendations across seven treatment modalities. Outputs from TSB, Claude-2, and GPT-4 were evaluated for concordance with MTB recommendations. Model performance was assessed using generalized estimating equations.RESULTSFifty patients were included (mean age, 59.8 years); 75.5% had hormone receptor–positive tumors, and 60.0% underwent breast-conserving surgery. TSB demonstrated the highest overall accuracy (0.89), followed by Claude-2 (0.86) and GPT-4 (0.78). GPT-4 showed significantly lower accuracy in genetic testing recommendations (odds ratio [OR], 0.05 [95% CI, 0.015 to 0.149]; P < .001), whereas Claude-2 was less accurate in radiotherapy recommendations (OR, 0.41 [95% CI, 0.17 to 0.98]; P = .040).CONCLUSIONA guideline-augmented AI tool such as TSB shows promise in supporting adjuvant therapy decisions in breast cancer. To improve clinical relevance, future iterations will incorporate individualized patient factors, broader guideline frameworks, and electronic health record integration. Prospective trials are ongoing to assess the real-world impact.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer care increasingly adopts a multidisciplinary approach, integrating expertise from surgical and medical oncology, radiation oncology, radiology, pathology, and supportive care. Multidisciplinary tumor boards (MTBs) provide a structured platform for interdisciplinary discussions, ensuring guideline-based, patient-centered care. Studies from Scotland and Sweden have shown improved survival after MTB implementation.1,2 However, challenges remain in maintaining effective and consistent discussions, influenced by power dynamics and variable participation.3,4 MTBs also demand substantial administrative effort—preparing summaries, collating imaging and pathology reports, and ensuring data availability, all of which affect decision-making quality.5 Time pressure further compounds these issues.6

A more structured, data-driven approach may enhance MTB discussions. Standardizing workflows, integrating decision-support tools, and leveraging digital platforms could improve efficiency while ensuring multidisciplinary consensus. Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 (OpenAI) and Claude-2 (Anthropic) have shown promise in generating context-aware clinical outputs. Studies evaluating artificial intelligence (AI)–generated breast cancer recommendations report varied concordance with expert consensus. One found that ChatGPT-3.5 aligned with MTBs in 70% of cases,1 whereas another reported 58.8% agreement for preoperative treatment recommendations.7 Conversely, Lukac et al3 reported only 16% concordance, raising reliability concerns. General-purpose LLMs, trained on broad internet data, often lack the specificity and structure required for medical decision making. Reported agreement rates range from 16% to 70%,7-9 highlighting the limitations of unstructured models and the need for domain-specific augmentation.

To address this, researchers at the National University Hospital (NUH), Singapore, developed TheSerenityBot (TSB), a domain-adapted tool built on Claude-2 and aligned with the 2023 National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) breast cancer guidelines. Using a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework and structured prompts, TSB simulates NCCN-consistent reasoning without retraining the base model. This study evaluates the concordance of adjuvant therapy recommendations from TSB, GPT-4, and Claude-2 against NUH's MTB decisions after curative breast cancer surgery, assessing the feasibility of guideline-augmented AI tools in multidisciplinary care.

METHODS

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board, approval No. 2023/00857.

Study Population

Consecutive women who underwent surgery for breast cancer were reviewed in the MTB at the NUH between June and November 2023. Eligible cases consisted of women with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of breast cancer after surgical intervention, encompassing both invasive and preinvasive lesions. Patients with metastatic breast cancer were excluded from the study. Relevant deidentified clinical data, tumor characteristics, and surgical details were collected.

Adjuvant Therapy Modalities

At NUH, weekly MTBs are held to discuss adjuvant therapy for patients after breast cancer surgery. The MTB comprises specialists from medical oncology, radiation oncology, surgical oncology, radiology, and pathology. The recommendation after the MTB consensus for each of the seven adjuvant therapy modalities: adjuvant chemotherapy (AC), endocrine therapy (ET), radiotherapy (RT), genetic testing (GT), targeted therapy, repeat surgery, and oncotype testing, was used at the gold standard reference for subsequent analysis.

GPT-4 and Claude-2

LLMs can be viewed8 as compressing vast amounts of internet-scale data (eg, 600 TB) into a relatively small model (eg, 14 GB) to predict the next word in a sentence. As a result, LLMs may hallucinate because of their limited exposure to diverse contexts in the training data and the constraints of the next-word prediction objective. Therefore, LLMs are well-suited for tasks requiring creative output, such as creative writing and brainstorming, where parroting and hallucinations are acceptable. GPT-4 by OpenAI and Claude-2 by Anthropic are leading examples of these innovations, known for their enhanced contextual understanding and nuanced language generation, and are widely used in clinical decision support systems. Both models have shown potential in health care applications, including generating clinical recommendations, making them relevant benchmarks for evaluating TSB's performance in postoperative breast cancer care.6-13

Novel Breast Cancer Model—TSB

However, medical tasks require strict adherence to treatment guidelines, leaving no room for hallucinations or assumptions about treatments or patients. It is essential to critically evaluate standard LLMs by asking questions such as what data were they trained on? Are the data relevant to the specific medical task? Are the learnings applicable to the targeted geographical region? The medical tasks demand more than a standard, out-of-the-box LLM. To address this, the NUH in Singapore developed TSB, an innovative AI chatbot powered by Claude-2 as the underlying LLM. TSB provides adjuvant therapy recommendations that are fully aligned with the NCCN guidelines. Therefore, we adopted the RAG approach, which enhances LLMs by integrating external knowledge sources, such as the NCCN guidelines, enabling more accurate and contextually relevant text generation. The selection of Anthropic's Claude-29-11 stemmed from its capability to accommodate training corpuses with high context lengths at the inception of this study. At the time of the study, Claude-2 had a context length of 100,000 tokens, surpassing the 30,000-token context length of GPT-4.9,12,13

The report automation workflow reduces the workload for junior doctors by automatically generating patient case reports from various data sources like operative, radiology, and pathology notes, which are preprocessed and transformed into a prose-report format for the MTB. The treatment recommendation process uses indexed treatment guideline information through an indexing engine called Kendra in a RAG system.

Model Prompts and Output

Anonymized clinical data were automatically formatted into prompts, checked by two independent clinicians (S.S.N.G., G.S.W.T.) to be deemed accurate before input into TSB, GPT-4, and Claude-2. The prompts reflect a structure akin to an English-based MTB case presentation: “What is the recommended adjuvant therapy for a (X) year old female patient with breast cancer (tumor size, nodal status, presence of metastases), estrogen receptor (ER) expression (+/–), progesterone receptor expression (+/–), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status (+/–), grading (1-3), premenopausal status (+/–), and breast cancer risk factors (such as personal or family history of breast cancer if applicable), following surgery (type of breast and axillary surgery) with postoperative histology (margins, final tumor size, lymph node involvement)?” Figure 2 displays the user interface for TSB and showcases the output section where treatment recommendations are presented based on the patient details entered previously. For each of the seven categories of postoperative treatment, a dichotomous recommendation (yes or no) is provided along with the rationale. A drop-down menu titled “Sources” available at the bottom displays the information supporting the recommendations, including excerpts from the treatment guidelines and links to the respective documents.

Outcome Measure

All automatically generated reports were verified for accuracy before being used as prompts for TSB in this pilot study. The primary outcome was overall performance of the three AI models across seven therapy modalities, using human consensus from MTB as ground truth. The performance of each model was also evaluated across seven individual therapy modalities in comparison with the ground truth. Secondary outcomes were to identify variables that influenced the performance of each model.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data were presented as No. (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (IQR) and were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. To compare the overall performance of AI models across seven modalities for each patient, a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was used, with the dependent variable being the models' accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score across all modalities, and the independent variable being the model used, with TSB serving as the reference. For the comparison of models' performance based on accuracy in individual modalities, separate GEE models with a logit link function were used. In these models, the dependent variable was the binary accuracy (yes/no) for the specific modality and the independent variable was the AI model. All GEE models treated the repeated measurements for each patient across the various AI models as clusters, with statistical significance set at 5% (P < .05). The analysis was conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.1), with the geepack package (version 1.3-11) used for the GEE models.

RESULTS

All automatically generated reports were accurate with no hallucinations and used as prompts for all three AI models. Fifty women were included with a mean age of 59.8 ± 12.6 years. A total of 22.6% patients had noninvasive cancers, and 77.4% had invasive breast cancers. Most tumors were hormone-positive (75.5%), with a minority showing HER2 positivity (10.0%) or being triple-negative (13.2%). In terms of surgery type, 60.0% were wide local excisions and 40.0% were mastectomies. The MTB most frequently recommended RT in 82.0% of cases, followed by ET at 72.0%. Overall Model Performance

TSB achieved an overall accuracy of 0.89, compared with 0.86 for Claude-2 and 0.78 for GPT-4 across all seven modalities. In the comparative analysis using GEEs, Claude-2 showed a nonsignificant reduction in accuracy compared with TSB (estimate, –3.00 [95% CI, –6.36 to 0.33]; P = .080). GPT-4 demonstrated significantly lower accuracy (estimate, –11.2 [95% CI, –15.31 to –7.19]; P < .001).

Precision

TSB achieved a precision of 0.85, compared with 0.78 for Claude-2 and 0.60 for GPT-4. Relative to TSB, Claude-2 showed a nonsignificant decrease in precision (GEE estimate, –6.50 [95% CI, –14.2 to 1.22]; P = .0989). GPT-4 demonstrated significantly lower precision (GEE estimate, –24.6 [95% CI, –30.8 to –1.8]; P < .001).

Recall

The recall was 0.82 for TSB, 0.73 for Claude-2, and 0.86 for GPT-4. Claude-2 had a significantly lower recall compared with TSB (GEE estimate, –8.83 [95% CI, –17.4 to –0.2]; P = .0441). The difference between GPT-4 and TSB was not statistically significant (GEE estimate, 4.33 [95% CI, –4.1 to 12.7]; P = .312).

F1 Score

The F1 score was highest for TSB at 0.80, followed by Claude-2 at 0.76 and GPT-4 at 0.70 (Tables 2 and 3).

In triple-negative breast cancer cases, TSB recommended GT for all eligible patients (3 of 3, 100%), whereas Claude-2 and GPT-4 each did so in only one case (1 of 3, 33.3%). Error Analysis of Discordant Cases

In the ET domain, TSB recommended treatment for three cases of low ER-positive ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), whereas the MTB assessed the potential benefit as low and suggested that treatment decisions be individualized after patient discussion. In the Oncotype Dx testing domain, a discordant case involved a patient with a T2N1 tumor and unclear menopausal status. TSB recommended Oncotype Dx testing, whereas the MTB recommended AC.

DISCUSSION

Multidisciplinary care in breast cancer management has consistently demonstrated a positive impact on survival outcomes.2,14 However, the large volume of clinical data generated throughout a patient's journey may be overwhelming and underutilized if not effectively organized or visualized for the entire multidisciplinary oncology team.15 Clinical decision support systems, powered by AI such as TSB, have the potential to automate the generation of clinical summaries and facilitate guideline-concordant recommendations. To our knowledge, this is the first pilot study of a generative AI model, integrated with evidence-based guidelines, aimed at enhancing breast cancer care.

TSB demonstrated ability to summarize data and provide evidence-aligned adjuvant treatment recommendations. A key strength lies in its capacity to reduce physician workload by processing and synthesizing vast amounts of data from clinical records, pathology, imaging, and existing treatment plans. Moreover, it can help standardize recommendations according to established guidelines. In settings without MTBs, such tools could bridge resource gaps, ensuring that patients receive equitable, guideline-concordant treatment regardless of institutional infrastructure or geographic location.16 Despite its benefits, discrepancies between AI-generated outputs and human clinical decisions were observed. One discordance occurred in three cases of low ER-positive DCIS for which TSB recommended ET. The MTB assessed the benefit as low, based on limited ER expression. While NCCN guidelines generally recommend ET for ER-positive DCIS, these are based on higher ER thresholds. Emerging evidence suggests that tumors with low ER expression may act more like ER-negative cancers,17-19 with reduced responsiveness to ET. Accordingly, the MTB exercised clinical discretion and withheld treatment. This highlights the need for the AI model to incorporate clinically meaningful ER expression thresholds into future versions. A second discrepancy involved a patient with T2N1 disease and unclear menopausal status. TSB suggested Oncotype Dx testing, whereas the MTB recommended AC if the patient was premenopausal, consistent with the RxPONDER trial.20 The AI did not identify the missing menopausal status as a critical variable. Ideally, it should have flagged this as a data gap requiring clarification through clinical history or biochemical testing. Future iterations will incorporate such data verification mechanisms. In another case, the AI recommended GT for a patient who had already undergone testing. This reflects a limitation in longitudinal data recall. Future versions should verify whether previous recommendations have been completed to avoid redundancy. Similarly, TSB automatically recommended RT after lumpectomy in two patients without considering margin status or the need for re-excision. The MTB preferred to await final pathology. To address this, conditional logic has been implemented to ensure that RT recommendations are context-sensitive.

To foster clinical trust, mitigating the black box nature of AI is essential. TSB improves interpretability by referencing relevant clinical guidelines when generating recommendations, thereby enhancing transparency. No hallucinations were observed in this 50-case evaluation, which may be attributed to structured training data, defined guardrails, and constrained inputs. Unlike open-ended generative AI models, this system operates within strict clinical parameters, reducing the risk of fabricating content. Nonetheless, hallucinations remain a challenge in AI adoption. Vigilant monitoring and transparent logic pathways are crucial to maintain user confidence. Importantly, TSB is not intended to replace clinical expertise but to enhance decision making. Designed as an assistive tool in AI-augmented MTBs, it facilitates real-time synthesis of data and supports standardized decision making while keeping human oversight central.

Although this proof-of-concept study demonstrates the potential utility of guideline-augmented AI in breast cancer care, several limitations warrant discussion. First, the small sample size (n = 50) limits generalizability, particularly given the clinical heterogeneity of breast cancer subtypes and treatment strategies. Second, the model currently does not incorporate patient-specific variables such as comorbidities, preferences, functional status, or financial constraints. Integrating these into the AI's logic will be essential for personalized care. Third, the MTB was used as the reference standard although MTB decisions may diverge from formal guidelines. Future work should compare AI outputs with both published guidelines and real-world decision making across different settings. AI tools must also navigate multiple, sometimes conflicting guidelines. Frameworks from the NCCN, ASCO,21 and European Society for Medical Oncology22 serve as essential resources, but may differ in recommendations, such as criteria for GT. For clinical relevance, our AI software must not only align with guidelines but also synthesize and reconcile their differences. Transparent presentation of these discrepancies would support informed, context-sensitive clinical choices. Looking ahead, future iterations of TSB will integrate multiple international guidelines, dynamic logic for conditional recommendations, and connection to local electronic health record systems.23 Maintaining relevance will require regular updates to guideline content and AI logic, supported by dedicated clinical informatics personnel. The quality of input data is critical for AI effectiveness. Precision oncology depends on structured, accurate, and patient-specific information.7,24,25 Our team is developing a prospective workflow in which TSB screens cases ahead of MTB meetings. Outputs will be reviewed in real time to support efficient, informed discussions. This model will be evaluated in a multicenter clinical trial to assess its impact on workflow efficiency, decision quality, and patient outcomes.

In conclusion, TSB represents a promising step in AI-supported breast cancer management. It automates clinical data summarization and supports evidence-based decision making, with alignment to guidelines and minimal risk of hallucination. Discrepancies observed in complex cases offer important insights for model refinement. Enhancing contextual understanding, integrating longitudinal data, and incorporating individualized variables will be critical.
