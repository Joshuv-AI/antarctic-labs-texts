Regulatory networks of coresident subgenomes during rapid fiber cell elongation in upland cotton
Authors: Lan Yang, Wenqiang Qin, Xi Wei, Rui Liu, Jiaxiang Yang, Zhi Wang, Qingdi Yan, Yihao Zhang, Wei Hu, Xiao Han, Chenxu Gao, Jingjing Zhan, Baibai Gao, Xiaoyang Ge, Fuguang Li, Zhaoen Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCotton, an intriguing plant species shaped by polyploidization, evolution, and domestication, holds particular interest due to the complex mechanisms governing fiber traits across its two subgenomes. However, the regulatory elements or transcriptional networks between subgenomes during fiber elongation remain to be fully clarified. Here, we analyzed 1462 cotton fiber samples to reconstruct the gene-expression regulatory networks that influence fiber cell elongation. Inter-subgenome expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) largely dictate gene transcription, with a notable tendency for the D subgenome to regulate A-subgenome eGenes. This regulation reveals synchronized homoeologous gene expression driven by co-localized eQTLs and divergent patterns that diminish genetic correlations, thus leading to preferential expression in the A and D subgenomes. Hotspot456 emerged as a key regulator of fiber initiation and elongation, and artificial selection of trans-eQTLs in hotspot456 that positively regulate KCS1 has facilitated cell elongation. Experiments designed to clarify the roles of trans-eQTLs in improved fiber breeding confirmed the inhibition of GhTOL9 by a specific trans-eQTL via GhWRKY28, which negatively affects fiber elongation. We propose a model in which the GhWRKY28–GhTOL9 module regulates this process through the ESCRT (endosomal sorting complex required for transport) pathway. This research significantly advances our understanding of cotton’s evolutionary and domestication processes and the intricate regulatory mechanisms that underlie significant plant traits.Key words: cotton fiber, eQTL, regulatory network, domestication

Introduction

Polyploidy is widespread in eukaryotes, with many important crop species such as banana, cotton, and wheat being polyploid (Schaart et al., 2021). Allotetraploid species are particularly common in plants and arise when two different diploid species hybridize to form a new species. Allopolyploid formation usually entails the merging of two independent gene pools, leading to genomic instability and subsequent modification. This process can trigger significant genomic changes, including large-scale structural variations such as deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations (Soltis et al., 2015). With the advent of whole-genome sequencing, structural variation has been documented in several polyploid crop species such as cotton (Yang et al., 2019), rice (Wang et al., 2023a), tomato (Alonge et al., 2020), and soybean (Liu et al., 2020). In accordance with genomic variations, silencing or biased expression of duplicate genes has been observed in various polyploid species (Song et al., 1995; Kashkush et al., 2002; Adams et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2004). One noteworthy finding from studies of polyploidy is that the expression of homoeologous genes (i.e., genes that belong to the two different subgenomes in allotetraploids) can be highly variable, but the underlying mechanisms that drive this variation remain poorly understood.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have been instrumental in shedding light on how genes are regulated across different species such as Arabidopsis (Zhang et al., 2011b), maize (Fu et al., 2013), tomato (Zhu et al., 2018), and cotton (Li et al., 2020). These studies have identified two main types of genetic variants that influence gene expression: cis expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs), which are located close to the gene of interest, and trans-eQTLs, which are found far from the gene. In humans, it has been observed that trans-eQTLs may contribute to over 70% of the genetic complexity that affects gene expression (Liu et al., 2019). Unlike cis-eQTLs, trans-eQTLs often show specificity to certain tissues or stages of development, suggesting that their regulatory roles may be highly specialized (Consortium, 2020; Aygun et al., 2023). The proposed mechanisms by which trans-eQTLs exert their effects on distant gene expression include mediation by transcription factors (TFs), alterations in epigenetic landscapes, effects from non-coding RNAs, changes in RNA editing and splicing processes, and variations in signaling pathways. However, these mechanisms remain largely theoretical and await experimental confirmation.

Gossypium hirsutum is a highly valuable crop grown for fiber, protein, and seed oil. It originated from the hybridization of two diploid cotton species (with A and D genomes) 1–2 million years ago (Flagel et al., 2012). It was domesticated ∼5000 years ago in Mexico and then further improved in the United States, resulting in modern cotton varieties with long, white fibers suitable for textiles; wild cotton species have sparse, coarse brown fibers. Previous research has shown that domestication has altered cis-regulatory elements in the cotton transcriptome (Wang et al., 2017). The process of fiber cell development is multifaceted, encompassing several stages, namely initiation, elongation, secondary cell wall thickening, and maturation (Kim and Triplett, 2001). Among these stages, fiber elongation is of particular interest to researchers and growers, as it directly affects fiber quality and value.

In this research, we explored the genetic coordination between the A and D subgenomes during fiber cell elongation. Our analysis used genomic data from 421 cultivated G. hirsutum accessions and 19 landraces, along with transcriptomic data from 1462 samples, to identify eQTLs. Our research explored the influence of cis-eQTLs and trans-eQTLs on the genetic correlation between homologous genes. By identifying trans-eQTLs, we uncovered an essential regulatory network that significantly impacts fiber development. In addition, we performed a thorough analysis of eQTLs influenced by domestication and artificial selection, revealing their profound effects on cotton’s eQTL landscape and associated agronomic traits. Lastly, we performed experimental validations to demonstrate the utility of our comprehensive dataset for the improvement of fiber breeding strategies.

Results

Gene expression in fiber cell elongation

The crucial stages of fiber cell elongation occur at 5 days post anthesis (DPA), the initial elongation period, and at 10 DPA, the rapid elongation period (Shan et al., 2014; Jan et al., 2022). For this reason, both of these stages were included in our transcriptome analysis. In total, 1410 samples were used for mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq): 698 fiber samples collected at 5 DPA (290 accessions with two replicates and five accessions with three replicates) and 712 fiber samples collected at 10 DPA (346 accessions with two replicates); the samples originated from a core collection of upland cotton. mRNA-seq was also performed on 10 DPA fibers collected from 19 semi-wild cotton accessions (landraces). Sequencing generated a total of 37.5 billion and 36.1 billion paired-end reads for the 5-DPA and 10-DPA samples, respectively (Supplemental Table 1). The reads were aligned to the high-quality TM-1 reference genome (Yang et al., 2019), and the expression of each gene was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). The higher the gene-expression level, the lower the coefficient of variation, indicating a stronger conservation across different materials (Supplemental Figure 1A). We observed that expression levels of the 60% of genes detected in over 90% of the samples were higher than those of the 26% of genes expressed in fewer than 10% of the samples (Supplemental Figure 1B).

Next, we performed a principal component analysis on the gene expression profiles of all samples (Supplemental Figure 1C). Samples could be clustered into three groups that were highly correlated with developmental stage, implying that developmental stage had a greater influence on gene expression than did genotype. By comparing gene expression levels between 5 DPA and 10 DPA, we found that 17.9% of the genes were negatively correlated between 5 DPA and 10 DPA, indicating that these genes had opposite expression patterns at the two stages. In addition, 73.6% of the genes were highly correlated between stages (Supplemental Figure 1D). During different stages of fiber development, the proportion of positively correlated genes was significantly higher than that of negatively correlated genes, suggesting that certain biological pathways are consistently active throughout these developmental stages. Concurrently, gene expression varied dynamically during developmental stages, indicating that some biological functions may undergo transformation or regulation.

Genetic architecture analysis reveals that trans-eQTLs show developmental stage specificity

Because gene expression differed between 5-DPA and 10-DPA fibers, we further explored the genetic basis of this variation using a GWAS. The GWAS was implemented using EMMAX (Zhou and Stephens, 2012) with gene expression as the phenotypic trait. In total, 21 312 and 35 463 eQTLs were associated with the expression of 7551 and 11 841 genes at 5 DPA and 10 DPA, respectively (eGenes, i.e., genes whose expression was associated with one or more eQTLs) (Figure 1A). With the exception of chromosomes A08 and D11, all other chromosomes had eQTLs in proximal telomeric regions (Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). Notably, on A08, many eQTLs were located in the middle of the chromosome, where three large inversions have previously been identified (Yang et al., 2019).

We classified all eQTLs on the basis of the distance to nearby eGenes, designating each eQTL as either a local eQTL (cis-eQTL, <1 Mb) or a distal eQTL (trans-eQTL, >1 Mb or on a different chromosome). At 5 DPA, cis-eQTLs and trans-eQTLs accounted for 23.38% and 76.62% of all detected eQTLs, respectively. At 10 DPA, the corresponding values were 14.67% and 85.33%, indicating a dynamic shift in eQTLs during fiber elongation (Supplemental Figure 4A).

Among the 5-DPA samples, 3389 eGenes were regulated by cis-eQTLs, and 5285 were regulated by trans-eQTLs. At 10 DPA, 3561 eGenes were regulated by cis-eQTLs, and 9501 were regulated by trans-eQTLs. At 5 DPA, 42.29% of the eGenes were regulated by a single eQTL, and this percentage increased to 48.38% at 10 DPA. By contrast, 57.71% of eGenes at 5 DPA and 51.62% at 10 DPA were regulated by multiple eQTLs (Supplemental Figure 4B). cis-eQTLs had a stronger effect on expression variation than trans-eQTLs (Figure 1B), consistent with findings from other organisms (Zhang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018).

A total of 4076 eGenes were shared between the 5-DPA and 10-DPA stages, whereas 3475 eGenes were detected only at 5 DPA and 7765 eGenes were detected only at 10 DPA (Figure 1C). A gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that common eGenes were enriched for pathways related to microtubule cytoskeleton organization, fatty acid β-oxidation, and oxidation–reduction processes. eGenes expressed only at 5 DPA were enriched for pathways such as brassinosteroid mediated signaling, xylan metabolic processes, cell cycle regulation, and water transport, and eGenes expressed only at 10 DPA were enriched for pathways including plant-type secondary cell wall biogenesis, glucuronoxylan metabolic processes, xylan biosynthetic processes, and regulation of secondary cell wall biogenesis (Supplemental Figure 5). Among the shared eGenes, only 27% of eGenes and their corresponding eQTLs were conserved between the two stages. For example, the 3-ketoacyl-coenzyme A (CoA) synthase gene KCS12 was associated with the same cis-eQTL at both 5 DPA and 10 DPA. Expression levels of KCS12 were moderately correlated between these stages (R = 0.22; p = 2.4E−05). KCS12 expression at both 5 DPA and 10 DPA was positively correlated with fiber micronaire (FM). The correlation coefficients observed (which ranged from 0.13 to 0.22) were marginally higher than those between plant height and the eGene OsPIE1, whose impact on plant height was previously validated through Cas9-mediated mutagenesis (Zhang et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that overexpression of KCS6, a homolog of KCS, can improve FM (Figure 1E) (Huang et al., 2020). In this study, the eQTLs associated with eGenes exhibited various degrees of conservation and divergence across developmental stages. Specifically, 31% of eGenes were associated with different eQTLs at each stage, a pattern that can be described as “divergent,” indicating that these genes are uniquely regulated by stage-specific eQTLs. A further 42% of the eGenes demonstrated partial overlap in eQTLs between stages, suggesting that these genes are regulated by “less conserved” eQTLs, in addition to having eQTLs that are unique to each developmental phase. The remaining eGenes showed a higher level of “conservation,” meaning that they were regulated by similar eQTLs across different stages, exhibiting a more consistent pattern of expression (Figure 1D). This conservation likely relates to the fundamental functions of the genes and their stable expression under various conditions. This analysis reveals the complexity of gene expression regulation during the elongation of cotton fiber cells, in which gene expression is influenced by both conserved regulatory mechanisms and factors specific to particular developmental stages.

Asymmetric and dynamic subgenomic transcription regulation in fiber elongation

As a tetraploid species, upland cotton contains diploid A and D subgenomes (Chen et al., 2020). Our eQTL analysis at 5 DPA and 10 DPA highlighted a stronger presence of intra-subgenomic eQTLs compared with inter-subgenomic eQTLs (Supplemental Figure 6). Specifically, at 5 DPA, 49.57% of A-subgenome eQTLs regulated D-subgenome genes, with only 3.62% affecting exclusively inter-subgenomic transcription; 45.95% influenced both subgenomes (Supplemental Figure 7A, left). By contrast, 61.49% of D-subgenome eQTLs regulated A-subgenome genes (Supplemental Figure 7A, right). A similar pattern was observed at 10 DPA, emphasizing the dynamic regulatory roles of eQTLs from both subgenomes (Supplemental Figure 7B).

Furthermore, at 5 DPA, 39.99% of A-subgenome eGenes were regulated by D-subgenome eQTLs, a slightly higher percentage than the 34.78% of D-subgenome eGenes influenced by A-subgenome eQTLs (Supplemental Figure 7C). By 10 DPA, these percentages increased to 52.65% for A-subgenome eGenes and 43.08% for D-subgenome eGenes (Supplemental Figure 7D). These findings underscore a predominant influence of D-subgenome eQTLs on A-subgenome eGenes, indicating unbalanced transcriptional control across subgenomes during fiber cell elongation.

Regulatory factors influencing genetic correlations between subgenomic homoeologous genes

In allotetraploid upland cotton, homoeologous genes from the A and D subgenomes are duplicated within a single nucleus (Comai, 2005). However, how this duplication influences the expression of these homologous genes and the roles that cis- and trans-acting eQTLs play in this process are unclear. A total of 26 261 orthologous gene pairs were identified between the A and D subgenomes using the InParanoid algorithm (O’Brien et al., 2005). The genetic correlation for expression for each homoeologous pair was evaluated at two developmental stages (5 DPA and 10 DPA). The same number of gene pairs was randomly selected as a control group. Consistent with expectations, the control group’s correlations clustered closely around zero, indicating that randomly chosen gene pairs are not related in terms of gene expression. By contrast, for homoeologous pairs, the distribution of correlations showed two peaks, one narrow peak near zero and the other a broad peak near 1. Our results suggest that 1.39% (5 DPA) and 0.74% (10 DPA) of gene pairs exhibit independent regulatory effects on each other’s expression. By contrast, the majority of homoeologous pairs (98.61% at 5 DPA and 99.25% at 10 DPA) show co-directional changes in expression between their two members, indicating a predominant trend of synchronized expression variation (Figure 2A). To test whether eQTLs are related to this synchronized expression variation, we compared the genetic correlation between homoeologous pairs with or without co-localization eQTLs. Compared with homoeologous pairs without co-localization eQTLs, homoeologous pairs with co-localization eQTLs showed stronger genetic correlations, indicating that co-localization eQTLs can coordinately regulate homoeologous gene expression (Figure 2B).

We classified homoeologous gene pairs into five groups on the basis of their associated eQTLs. When a trans-eQTL regulated both homoeologous genes, there was a strong positive genetic correlation, suggesting that these gene pairs worked together in fiber development (Figure 2C). By contrast, the genetic correlation was reduced when homoeologous pairs were not concurrently regulated by trans-eQTLs, and this reduction was particularly noticeable when both genes of a pair were regulated by cis-eQTLs (Figure 2C). Thus, our findings suggest that the divergent regulatory patterns between homoeologous pairs have diminished the genetic correlations between them. This reduction in turn has diversified their gene expression patterns.

Because regulatory eQTLs and genetic correlations diverged significantly between homoeologous genes, we next explored gene expression biases between the A and D subgenomes. We considered a homoeologous gene pair to exhibit an expression bias when one homolog exhibited expression (as FPKM) <0.1 and the other >2 (Grover et al., 2012; He et al., 2022). More gene pairs with biased expression were discovered at 10 DPA than at 5 DPA, suggesting that gene-biased expression is related to development (Figure 2D and 2E; Supplemental Figure 8A and 8B). Furthermore, the D subgenome had more gene pairs with biased expression than the A subgenome, despite the significant role played by the A subgenome in upland cotton fiber development (Jiang et al., 1998; Chen et al., 2020).

Gene expression can be influenced not only by eQTLs but also by local single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the vicinity of the gene. We cataloged the SNPs present within 2 kb upstream or downstream of genes with biased expression. The less-expressed homoeologous gene generally contained more SNPs that could result in non-synonymous mutations, stop-codon gains, or upstream effects compared with the more highly expressed homoeologous partner (Supplemental Figure 9A and 9B); the types of SNPs contained in homoeologous genes without dose-biased homologs were not significantly different (Supplemental Figure 9C and 9D). For instance, GhBXL2, a gene essential for secondary cell wall metabolism and plant development (Goujon et al., 2003), showed significant differential expression between two members of the homoeologous gene pair; GhBXL2_At was highly expressed and contained no SNPs, whereas GhBXL2_Dt was expressed at lower levels and contained eight SNPs (Figure 2F). We also found that GhBXL2_At had a positive correlation with fiber length, whereas GhBXL2_Dt was not correlated with fiber length, suggesting that the two homoeologous genes play different roles during fiber elongation. However, it is worth noting that the biased expression of homologous genes is not always affected by SNP variation. Factors such as the number of transposons near the homologous gene, the exchange of homologous fragments, and epigenetic modifications may all lead to dose biases in homologous genes (Bird et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020; He et al., 2022). In summary, we concluded that both transcriptional regulation eQTLs and local SNPs may contribute to the expression of orthologs, resulting in biased expression and subsequent functional divergence

Regulatory network underlying fiber elongation during critical developmental stages

We used hot_scan (Silva et al., 2014) to identify trans-eQTL hotspots for 5 DPA and 10 DPA and detected 555 and 849 hotspots, respectively. Interestingly, only 124 hotspots were shared between the two developmental stages (Figure 3A and Supplemental Table 2). In addition, hotspots tended to be located in the A subgenome at 5 DPA, but this pattern reversed at 10 DPA, when a slightly higher number of hotspots was detected in Dt compared with At (Dt/At = 464:385; Supplemental Table 3).

The number of hotspots was higher at 5 DPA than at 10 DPA for four (A06, A08, D02, and D07) of the 26 chromosomes analyzed (Supplemental Figure 10). Specifically, among these four chromosomes in which 5-DPA hotspot numbers were higher, chromosome A08 exhibited the greatest difference in hotspot number between the two stages (Supplemental Figure 11). As previously shown (Yang et al., 2019), this chromosome has three large-scale inversions that restrict meiotic recombination, and approximately 68% of the A08 hotspots were located within the inverted regions at 5 DPA. Because of substantial overlap among the genes regulated by hotspots within the inversion regions, we consolidated the 43 hotspots in the inversion regions into a “super-hotspot” (Supplemental Figure 12A and Supplemental Table 3). The A08 inversions may affect phenotypic characteristics of the cotton plant, and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) analysis revealed that genes located within the inversions were enriched for fatty acid degradation and α-linolenic acid metabolism pathways (Supplemental Figure 12B). Overall, these findings contribute to our understanding of the regulatory network involved in fiber elongation and also provide valuable insights for future studies on the genetic basis of fiber yield and quality.

Hotspot456 is involved in fiber development

To explore the intricate regulatory network between eQTLs and eGenes in elongating fiber cells, we assessed the number of eGenes regulated within each hotspot. We identified hotspot456 on chromosome D11 at 10 DPA as particularly noteworthy, as it regulated the highest number of eGenes. This hotspot controlled the expression of 1877 genes, which collectively contribute to a significant network relevant to fiber development. These genes were instrumental in constructing a network that integrated nine fiber traits and 470 eQTLs. In this network, genes located in hotspot456 were found to function in very-long-chain fatty acid (VLCFA) biosynthetic process (GO:0042761), cellulose synthase activity (GO:0016761), plant-type secondary cell wall biogenesis (GO:0009834), and ethylene biosynthetic process (GO:0009693) (Figure 3B and 3C; Supplemental Table 6).

TFs play a crucial role in fiber development. A total of 94 TFs were identified in hotspot456, including GhWRKY16, a TF that enhances the expression of GhCesA6D_D11, GhHOX3, GhMYB109, and GhMYB25 by binding to their promoters (Wang et al., 2021) (Figure 3D, Supplemental Figure 14, and Supplemental Table 4). Genes in hotspot456 were also involved in multiple hormone pathways that participate in regulating the elongation of cotton fibers. For instance, exogenous brassinosteroid (BR) treatment can promote fiber elongation, and BR may in turn promote fiber elongation by affecting the expression of ethylene-related genes (Sun et al., 2005; Shi et al., 2006). The interactive signaling pathways of ethylene and auxin can influence both fiber elongation and secondary cell wall thickening (Wang et al., 2023b). Auxin and gibberellin are also important hormones that regulate fiber length; auxin modulates the transcription of GhGA3OX4D and GhGA20OX1D-2 through GhARF18 to regulate the biosynthesis of gibberellin (GA), thereby promoting fiber elongation (Figure 3D) (Zhu et al., 2022). These genes are known to be involved in fiber initiation and elongation, and all were located in hotspot456. Other TFs previously reported to participate in fiber development were also identified within this hotspot (Machado et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021).

A subnetwork of 54 genes significantly correlated with 11 fiber traits was identified using the MCODE plug-in of Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003). The 54 genes were regulated by 12 hotspots, indicating that these were functionally related (Supplemental Figure 14). Known cellulose synthesis genes (two CESA4, three CESA7, and one CESA8) (Huang et al., 2021) and MYB86 (Sun et al., 2020) were identified within the subnetwork, suggesting a role for these 54 genes in fiber development. The genes in question represent important candidates for fiber improvement studies. Overall, our findings suggest that hotspot456 is crucial for fiber development, and the genes in this hotspot represent plausible candidates for studying cell elongation and fiber development.

Hotspot456 gene KCS1 is a domestication gene during fiber quality improvement

Domestication and improvement have resulted in significant changes to cotton fiber, shifting short, colored seed trichomes into long, white fibers. To better understand the impact of artificial selection on the regulation of gene expression in cotton, we investigated the overlap between eQTLs and selective sweep regions. Using the top 5% FST values (FST > 0.7), 17 098 putative sweep regions were identified in a comparison of 714 upland cotton and 229 semi-wild cotton accessions (Figure 4A). Within these regions, 445 expression GWAS (eGWAS) signals were significantly associated with 2471 eGenes at 5 DPA, and 457 eGWAS signals were significantly associated with 5109 eGenes at 10 DPA. These eGenes were found to be enriched for various pathways, such as cutin, suberin, and wax biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Supplemental Table 5).

One critical component of fiber elongation is the VLCFAs, which are synthesized by the enzyme 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase (KCS) (Tian et al., 2022). The roles of KCS family genes have been well studied in modern cultivars (Qin et al., 2007; Huang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023). However, the impact of artificial selection and breeding on the KCS genes remains poorly understood. eGene annotation revealed that GhKCS1, which encodes KCS, was located within hotspot456 and was found to be regulated by a sweep-region-associated eQTL with an FST value of 0.8 (Figure 4B). A trans-eQTL (−log10 p value = 7.3) regulating KCS1 expression was observed from 46.46 Mb to 47.1 Mb on D04 (Figure 4B). Haplotype analysis clustered the genotypes of semi-wild cotton and cultivated (improved) cotton in the GWAS locus into two groups, Hap.A and Hap.B (Figure 4C). In semi-wild cotton, most accessions (86%) carried Hap.A, whereas in cultivated cotton, only 5% carried Hap.A (Figure 4D). Accessions carrying Hap.B had greater fiber length than those carrying Hap.A, confirming that Hap.B was the favorable haplotype for cultivars (Supplemental Figure 15). Thus, Hap.B was likely selected during improvement efforts, consistent with the fact that fiber length is a major target of cotton breeding.

We next investigated the function of GhKCS1 in fiber cell elongation using CRISPR-Cas9 technology in upland cotton. Two knockout mutants, Ghkcs1-cr1 and Ghkcs1-cr5, were identified and found to have significantly shorter fiber length, seed length, and seed width than the control (Figure 4E and 4F). Fiber length was reduced by up to 19% in the Ghkcs1-cr1 and Ghkcs1-cr5 mutants (Figure 4G). Seed length and width were reduced by up to 10% and 7%, respectively (Figure 4H and 4I). In Ghkcs1-cr5, the 100-seed weight was reduced by up to 19% (Figure 4J). Taken together, these results suggest that KCS1 played a crucial role in the domestication and improvement of upland cotton by contributing to fiber elongation and seed-size enlargement.

Genetic improvement using a trans-eQTL

Weak eQTL effects on genes are thought to have greater phenotypic impact than strong eQTL effects, with individual trans-eQTL SNPs influencing broad regulatory networks, highlighting the potential of weak trans-eQTLs for pinpointing trait-related genes (Võsa et al., 2021). A trans-eQTL marker linked to the regulation of over 20 genes remained stable across generations and technologies after introduction into the Mo17 inbred genetic background through back-crossing experiments, demonstrating the heritability of trans-eQTLs and their potential utility in crop genetic enhancement (Swanson-Wagner et al., 2009). Approximately 85.33% of trans-eQTLs were identified at 5 DPA and 76.62% at 10 DPA, with over 52% of these trans-eQTLs containing one or more TFs. These TFs potentially mediate the regulation of trans-eGenes, with the percentage of such trans-eGenes reaching 85% at 10 DPA and 81% at 5 DPA. Analysis of TF binding motifs in the 2-kb promoter regions of trans-eGenes revealed significant enrichment of motifs associated with TF families such as AP2/EREBP, MYB, and WRKY (Figure 5A), suggesting a mechanism whereby trans-eQTLs regulate eGenes via TF interactions (Võsa et al., 2021).

At developmental stages critical for fiber cell elongation, such as 10 DPA, trans-eQTLs play a pivotal role in regulating gene expression networks. Here, we focused on Gh_A06G106100, identified as a TOM1-like protein (GhTOL9), which shares significant sequence similarity with At. GhTOL9 exhibited both stable cis-eQTLs originating from chromosome A06 and an additional trans-eQTL located on chromosome A10 spanning 32 272 421 bp to 34 217 049 bp identified at 10 DPA, suggesting intricate regulatory mechanisms involving both types of eQTLs. Notably, GhTOL9 displayed a weak negative correlation with fiber length (R = −0.12, p = 0.017, Supplemental Figure 16A) at 5 DPA, but this correlation was diminished at 10 DPA (R = −0.043, p = 0.41, Supplemental Figure 16B), underscoring the temporal specificity of the 10-DPA-specialized eQTL influencing GhTOL9 expression. Further analysis of cis elements in the GhTOL9 promoter revealed the presence of WRKY-binding W-box motifs, consistent with our identification of GhWRKY28 in the trans-eQTL associated with GhTOL9. This finding suggests a potential regulatory mechanism whereby GhWRKY28 protein modulates GhTOL9 expression by binding to its promoter region. Plant TF prediction (Tian et al., 2020) supports this hypothesis, indicating that GhWRKY28 possesses the requisite ability to regulate GhTOL9.

To explore the regulation between GhWRKY28 and GhTOL9, we performed a yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay by cloning the GhTOL9 promoter into a PLacZI2u plasmid and the coding sequence (CDS) of GhWRKY28 into a Pb42AD plasmid. Our results confirmed the interaction between GhWRKY28 and GhTOL9 (Figure 5B). Analysis of the GhTOL9 promoter region revealed two WRKY28 binding motifs (i.e., W-boxes) at the following positions: −1673 bp to −1668 bp and −391 bp to −386 bp (relative to the predicted transcription start site) (Figure 5C). After dividing the promoter sequence into eight fragments, we tested the binding activity of GhWRKY28 to each fragment and found that only the fragment from −401 bp to −204 bp, which carried a W-box motif, showed binding activity (Figure 5C). We next used an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to clarify how GhWRKY28 interacted with the W-box motif. The assay indicated that GhWRKY28 bound to the W-box located from −391 bp to −386 bp. In this assay, the GhWRKY28 protein directly bound to a 35-bp probe designed for the −404 bp to −370 bp region containing TTGACT; however, addition of a competitor probe significantly decreased GhWRKY28 binding to the labeled probes (Figure 5D).

To further investigate the relationship between GhWRKY28 and GhTOL9, a transactivation assay was performed through a luciferase assay in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Two reporter plasmids were constructed for GhTOL9, one with a mini CaMV35S promoter and one without a mini CaMV35S promoter. An effector plasmid was constructed that contained GhWRKY28 driven by the CaMV35S promoter. Transient expression analyses indicated that GhTOL9 activity was inhibited when GhWRKY28 was expressed in both reporter plasmids (Figure 5E).

To investigate the roles of GhWRKY28 and GhTOL9 in cotton fiber development, we performed overexpression (OE) and genome-editing experiments. We obtained the knockout mutants Ghtol9-cr1 and Ghtol9-cr2 for the eGene GhTOL9 (Figure 6C), both of which exhibited shorter fiber lengths than the control samples (Figure 6A and 6D). Similarly, overexpression of GhWRKY28 also resulted in shorter fiber lengths (Figure 6B and 6E). RT–qPCR assays showed that the expression of GhWRKY28 was increased approximately 20-fold in the GhWRKY28-OE1 lines compared with the wild type (Figure 6F). We assessed the expression levels of GhTOL9 in GhWRKY28-OE1 plants and observed suppression of GhTOL9 in the presence of GhWRKY28, further indicating that GhWRKY28 negatively regulates fiber length through GhTOL9 (Figure 5G). In summary, our findings indicate a strong association between eQTLs and various developmental stages in plants. Moreover, these eQTLs can serve as valuable tools for identifying candidate genes for crop improvement, particularly in the context of cotton fiber development.

Discussion

Upland cotton is a highly desirable crop that provides long fibers suitable for the textile industry. Cotton fibers are single-celled trichomes that grow from seeds and are capable of reaching up to 60 mm in length in allotetraploid Gossypium barbadense and 35 mm in G. hirsutum. To better understand the regulatory networks that facilitate cell elongation, we performed transcriptome and eGWAS analyses at two stages of rapid cotton fiber-cell elongation. The eQTLs and regulatory networks illustrated here represent a significant step forward in terms of our understanding of cotton fiber-cell elongation and should facilitate future research efforts.

Homoeolog-specific eQTLs from the A and D subgenomes are essential for fiber elongation

Upland cotton was formed by the hybridization of the diploid A and D genomes, followed by chromosome doubling, approximately 1–2 million years ago (Flagel et al., 2012). Polyploidization significantly altered the cotton transcriptome, but little is known about how eQTLs are distributed among orthologs from the A and D subgenomes. Overall, the expression of A- and D-subgenome homoeologs was highly differentiated, and only a small proportion of homoeologous genes showed highly correlated expression (Figure 2A). Note that co-localized orthologs showed more highly correlated expression (with a peak at 1.0), whereas non-co-localized orthologs showed correlations ranging from −0.1 to 1.0.

On the basis of their regulatory eQTLs, the homoeologs were divided into five groups. In most cases, orthologs had no associated eQTLs, consistent with the fact that many factors regulate gene expression, such as DNA methylation (Yang et al., 2021) and 3D genome architecture (Wang et al., 2017). In tomato, the pan-genome can capture missing heritability (up to 24%) by increasing the estimated heritability through inclusion of additional causal structural variants that resolve incomplete linkage disequilibrium (Zhou et al., 2022). In this study, we used only a single linear reference genome, which may have resulted in some missing eQTLs for the homoeologous gene pairs. Hence, a pan-genome might be used as a reference in future work to fully address missing heritability.

Divergent eQTL distributions between homoeologous genes likely contributed to the amount of homoeolog-specific bias (in gene expression) that we observed in our population-scale analysis, particularly during fiber development. Biased expression was more prevalent at 10 DPA than at 5 DPA. In a previous study of five cotton accessions, there was variability in the number of homoeologous genes that showed expression bias during fiber development (ranging from 5910 to 2775) (Yoo and Wendel, 2014). Comparing the A and D subgenomes, we found that D-subgenome genes showed more biased expression than A-subgenome genes at both 5 DPA and 10 DPA. Note that this is a different situation from analyzing homoeolog expression bias in a single individual, where the number of genes exhibiting A-subgenome or D-subgenome bias is approximately equal (Yoo and Wendel, 2014). Our findings are consistent with recent GWASs of upland cotton (Ma et al., 2018), which have suggested that the D subgenome is more important to fiber development than the A subgenome. In addition to the above findings, we also discovered that mutations in the vicinity of a gene (i.e., located within 2 kb upstream or downstream) may be responsible for low homoeolog expression. With regard to the subgenome-biased expression of the TF gene PRE1 in cotton fiber elongation, previous studies have demonstrated that natural variation in the canonical TATA-box in the promoter region of this gene is responsible for its biased expression (Zhao et al., 2018). Biased expression of BXL2 in the A subgenome of cotton is also thought to play a role in fiber elongation. Here, only GhBXL2_At, but not GhBXL2_Dt, was significantly associated with fiber length. This novel finding provides a potential new target for cotton breeding efforts.

Human selection on trans-eQTLs in hotspot456 that regulate KCS1 has facilitated cell elongation

The process of fiber development is complex and regulated by a multitude of factors interacting in a network. trans-eQTLs typically operate in a cluster-like manner, and these clusters are known as hotspots. In total, 555 and 849 hotspots were identified for 5-DPA and 10-DPA fibers, respectively. At 10 DPA, hotspot456 contained the greatest number of eGenes; these were enriched for several pathways, including cellulose biosynthesis and VLCFA biosynthesis, both crucial processes for fiber development. Thus, it is likely that hotspot456 plays a significant role in fiber development.

A total of 94 TFs were identified from hotspot456, including several TFs crucial for fiber development. For example, the well-studied MYB–WD40–bHLH complex, which plays an important role in fiber cell development, was detected in hotspot456. In addition, GhWRKY16 was found to bind directly to the promoters of GhCesA6_D11, GhHOX3, GhMYB109, and GhMYB25, inducing their expression and thereby promoting fiber initiation and elongation (Wang et al., 2021).

A Pearson correlation analysis identified a subnetwork of 54 genes that were correlated with 11 fiber-related traits. Notably, the cellulose synthase genes CESA4, CESA6, CESA7, and CESA8, as well as MYB86, which are all implicated in secondary cell wall formation (Sun et al., 2015), were included in this subnetwork. In addition to these established genes, the subnetwork also contained novel candidate genes that will require additional research. These discoveries offer valuable genetic resources for investigating the intricacies of fiber development, although the precise effects of these genes on fiber characteristics await further experimental characterization.

The KCS gene family comprises at least 58 members in upland cotton, but the functions of most KCS genes in fiber elongation remain largely uncharacterized (Xiao et al., 2016). Recent research revealed that GhBES1.4, a master TF of the BR signaling pathway, directly binds to the GhKCS10_At promoter, increasing endogenous VLCFA content and leading to longer cotton fibers (Yang et al., 2023). The effects of historical human selection on KCS expression are not fully understood. However, an eQTL for GhKCS1 was located within a sweep region identified in a comparison of cultivated and semi-wild cotton. This eQTL can be divided into two haplotypes, with the favorable Hap.B experiencing strong positive selection and increasing in frequency from 14% in semi-wild cotton to 95% in cultivated cotton. By knocking out GhKCS1, we found that it has multiple functions in fiber cell and seed elongation. This suggests that GhKCS1 was a crucial regulator of both seed and fiber characteristics during domestication.

The GhWRKY28–GhTOL9 module is essential for fiber elongation

Our findings suggest that trans-eQTLs play a more significant role in fiber development than local eQTLs. trans-eQTLs exert their influence on the expression of distal genes, potentially by modulating the expression of proximal genes. Notably, TFs are often found in trans-eQTL intervals, suggesting that these eQTLs may influence phenotypic traits through the action of TFs. Our study highlights the GhWRKY28 TF as a pivotal regulator that orchestrates the expression of the physically distant GhTOL9 gene through a developmentally stage-specific trans-eQTL.

TOL proteins, which act as multivalent ubiquitin receptors, are crucial for the targeted delivery of ubiquitinated cargo to the vacuole through the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) pathway (Korbei et al., 2013). In Arabidopsis, the redundancy and functional significance of the nine identified TOL proteins underscore their role in recognizing ubiquitinated proteins to determine protein fate. These proteins have been shown to bind to ubiquitin in vitro and localize to both the plasma membrane (PM) and early endosomal structures. Plants with combined mutations in higher-order tol genes exhibit significant disruptions in endosomal cargo trafficking and severe developmental defects, demonstrating the indispensable role of TOLs in degradation of PM proteins. Interactions of TOL2 and TOL6 with ESCRT machinery components, as well as their binding to K63-linked ubiquitin through conserved ubiquitin binding domains, are critical. Mutations in these domains compromise ubiquitin binding and localization, effectively nullifying the ubiquitin receptor activity of TOL6 (Moulinier-Anzola et al., 2020).

Our investigation identified GhWRKY28 as a key upstream regulator of GhTOL9, mediated through trans-eQTL analysis. GhWRKY28 binds to the W-box in the promoter region of GhTOL9, thereby suppressing its transcriptional activity. We propose a model in which the GhWRKY28–GhTOL9 module plays a crucial role in modulating fiber elongation, providing new insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying this process. Under natural conditions, GhWRKY28 is expressed at low levels, enabling the normal transcription of GhTOL9. Given the conserved roles of TOL proteins in the ESCRT pathway across plants, we hypothesize that GhTOL9 likely binds to ubiquitinated proteins. It may also interact with ESCRT-I, facilitating the degradation of these ubiquitinated proteins and thereby promoting fiber cell elongation. However, when GhWRKY28 is overexpressed, it suppresses GhTOL9 expression, leading to a compromised ESCRT pathway. This impairment in the pathway responsible for degrading ubiquitinated proteins on the PM ultimately affects fiber elongation (Figure 7).

Proposed model of fiber length regulation by GhWRKY28 and GhTOL9 in cotton.

Under normal conditions, low levels of GhWRKY28 allow for the transcription of GhTOL9. GhTOL9 is hypothesized to bind to ubiquitinated proteins and interact with ESCRT-I, thereby facilitating their degradation and promoting fiber cell elongation. By contrast, overexpression of GhWRKY28 suppresses GhTOL9 expression by binding to the W-box in its promoter, disrupting the ESCRT pathway and impeding the degradation of ubiquitinated proteins on the plasma membrane. This disruption ultimately affects fiber elongation.

The specific types of ubiquitinated proteins that are crucial for fiber elongation remain unidentified, underscoring the need for further research to reveal the precise roles of the GhWRKY28–GhTOL9 module in this process. Nonetheless, this study sheds light on the complex genetic regulation of fiber development in cotton, emphasizing the significance of eQTL mapping for advancing our understanding of complex traits.

Methods

Plant materials

In 2018, 421 upland cotton (G. hirsutum) core accessions were cultivated in a field in Anyang (36°6′21.39″ N, 114°23′58.2″ E), Henan, China. Cotton bolls were tagged on the day of flowering to mark zero days post anthesis (DPA). Three replicates were set up for each accession (at 5 DPA, there were 103 accessions with one replicate, 290 accessions with two replicates, and five accessions with three replicates; at 10 DPA, there were 20 accessions with one replicate and 346 accessions with two replicates). Fiber was collected from at least five cotton bolls from different plants; the samples were combined and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. In addition to the work at Anyang, 38 semi-wild cotton accessions were cultivated in the field in Sanya (18°15′31.45″ N, 109°31′6.8″ E), Hainan, China. Fiber samples were collected for these accessions at 10 DPA using the methods described above. The fiber samples were used for RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and sequencing

For each sample, total RNA was extracted using a Spectrum Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Sigma, STRN250). The RNA samples were then used to construct sequencing libraries using the Illumina VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Each library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150-bp paired-end reads).

RNA-seq data mapping and analysis

The raw RNA-seq data were filtered to remove adapters and low-quality bases using Trimmomatic (v.0.32) (Bolger et al., 2014). Clean data were mapped to the G. hirsutum reference genome using HISAT2 (v.2.1.0) (Stegle et al., 2012) with default settings. Picard tools (v.1.139, http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) were used to remove PCR duplicates. Sequencing reads with a mapping quality of less than 25 were removed using SAMtools (v.0.1.19) (Li et al., 2009). Sequencing reads that mapped to multiple genomic loci were removed from the mapping files using custom Perl scripts. The remaining high-quality reads were used to quantify gene expression (as FPKM) using StringTie (v.1.3.4) (Pertea et al., 2015) with default settings. After obtaining the gene expression levels for each sample, we used R to calculate the average expression values across two or three replicates of each gene, which were then used as the expression phenotypes for genome-wide association analysis.

SNP calling

We used BWA-MEM (v.0.7.10-r789) (Li and Durbin, 2009) to align whole-genome resequencing data from all 419 accessions (from the RNA-seq analysis) to the new G. hirsutum reference genome and to identify SNPs in the population. Uniquely mapped reads were parsed to identify SNPs using GATK (v.4.1.3) (McKenna et al., 2010) as described previously (Wang et al., 2017). VCFtools (v.0.1.15) (Danecek et al., 2011) was used to merge SNPs from each accession, after removing those with a minor allele frequency of less than 5%. ANNOVAR (Wang et al., 2010) was used to annotate the remaining SNPs, including those representing genic variants (i.e., synonymous or non-synonymous mutations, as well as mutations within intronic regions, untranslated regions, and regions 2-kb upstream of the transcription start site or 2-kb downstream of the transcription termination site) and intergenic variants.

GWAS for fiber traits

A GWAS was performed using data collected in 2014 and 2015 for the following traits: boll weight, fiber elongation, fiber length, fiber strength, fiber weight per boll, lint percentage, maturity, micronaire value, seed index, spinning consistency index, and uniformity. The GWAS included genomic data from 421 accessions. The association analysis was carried out using EMMAX (v.20120210) (Kang et al., 2010). The significance cutoff was set to p < 6.5 × 10−7 (i.e., 0.05/n, where n represents the total number of SNPs) to help identify putative candidate genes for GWAS loci. Different thresholds were used for 5 DPA and 10 DPA owing to sample loss. The cutoffs used were p < 2.62 × 10−7 for 5 DPA and p < 2.71 × 10−7 for 10 DPA (Li et al., 2020).

Identification of expression QTLs

To obtain a normal distribution of expression values for each gene, a normal quantile transformation was performed for expression values using the qqnorm function in R (v.4.2.1) (R Development Core Team, 2021). We calculated the mean expression of genes with biological replicates, then removed those with expression levels less than 0.1 (i.e., FPKM < 0.1). In total, 51 120 genes for 5 DPA and 51 692 genes for 10 DPA were included in the GWAS analysis. An R script was used to generate quantile–quantile (q–q) plots and Manhattan plots based on the p values obtained for each SNP. The SNPs were grouped into linked regions using the following criteria: if the distance between two neighboring SNPs was <1 Mb and r2 was >0.2, the SNPs were assigned to the same region. Regions that contained at least three significant SNPs were considered eQTL blocks. For regions that did not meet this criterion, the SNPs contained therein were treated as false positives. The SNP with the minimum p value in an eQTL block was considered to be the primary SNP.

To identify the genetic mechanisms underpinning the eQTLs, the position of each eQTL was compared with that of its associated gene. An eQTL was defined as a cis-eQTL if the eQTL was located less than 1 Mb from its associated gene. By contrast, an eQTL was defined as a trans-eQTL if the eQTL was located at a distance (i.e., greater than 1 Mb or on a different chromosome) from its target gene. Using hot_scan (Silva et al., 2014), trans-eQTL hotspots were identified by running the software for all eQTLs on each chromosome (-m 5000 -s 0.05).

Correlations between FPKM values and phenotypic traits

We calculated correlations between gene expression at 5 DPA and 10 DPA using the cor.test function in R (https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/stats/versions/3.6.2/topics/cor.test). More than 51 000 of all 73 624 genes were positively correlated, and more than 13 000 genes were negatively correlated. We used the same method to calculate correlations between gene expression and the phenotypic traits measured in 2014 and 2015, including trait means and BLUP values. If a given gene was positively or negatively correlated with the same trait two or more times, it was regarded as a candidate gene stably associated with the trait.

Network construction

To systematically describe the pattern of associations among the eQTLs, eGenes, and phenotypic traits, we constructed genetic networks linking these three elements. We used genes regulated by several eQTLs to construct a basic network, then linked genes to traits on the basis of their correlations. eQTL hotspots were highlighted in this genetic network. In this analysis, the genetic regulatory network was constructed using genes known to be involved in a given pathway and their potential regulators, following previous studies with modifications. The network was constructed using Cytoscape (v.3.6.1) (Shannon et al., 2003) with the edge-weighted force-directed algorithm. Subnetworks of our networks were identified with the MCODE Cytoscape plug-in (Bader and Hogue, 2003), which can be used to identify crucial genes from a larger network. The parameters were set to their default values: Node Score Cutoff = 0.2, K-Core = 2, Max. Depth = 100.

Identification of domestication sweeps

To detect domestication sweeps, a genome-wide scan was performed to look for evidence of selective sweeps. In the genome scan, FST was compared between cultivated and semi-wild populations using the parameters “--fst-window-size 50 000 --fst-window-step 10 000.” FST ratios were calculated in 50-kb sliding windows with a step size of 10 kb. Regions with scores ranking in the top 5% were defined as putative selective sweeps.

Transcription factor predictions

The TFs in Arabidopsis were downloaded from PlantTFDB (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/), and homeologous TFs of Arabidopsis and G. hirsutum were compared using blast (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Enrichment of TF binding sites is indicated by the number of TFs within the same family. Homer software was used to predict motifs in the promoter regions of the eGenes (http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/), and these motifs were compared with the core binding sequences of TFs to identify a gene set regulated by TFs as mediators in trans-eQTLs.

Yeast one-hybrid assays

For Y1H experiments, the CDS of GhWRKY28 was cloned into the Pb42AD vector. The GhTOL9 promoter sequence was then ligated into the PLacZI2u vector. Pb42AD–GhWRKY28 and PLacZI2u–GhTOL9 were then co-transformed into Y1HGold. The empty vector combination of Pb42AD and PLacZI2u was also co-transformed into Y1HGold as a negative control, and Pb42AD–RGN1 and PLacZI2u–proLOG were co-transformed as a positive control. SD/-Trp/-Ura plates with appropriate nutrients were used to select the transformants, and positive clones were transferred to SD/-Trp/-Ura plates. After 24 h of growth, positive clones were cultured in a shaker at 30°C for 24 h in excess medium before being transferred to SD/-Trp/-Ura/Gal/Raf/X-Gal plates.

Dual-luciferase assay in N. benthamiana

The 2-kb GhTOL9 promoter sequence was amplified and inserted into the pGreenII0800-LUC and pGreenII0800mini-LUC vectors, which were used as reporters. The open reading frame of GhWRKY28 was cloned into the pGreenII-SK vector as an effector plasmid. The plasmids were then transformed into GV3101. Agrobacterium harboring reporter and effector constructs were co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. The empty pGreenII0800-LUC, pGreenII0800mini-LUC, and pGreenII-SK vectors; the pGreenII0800-LUC and pGreenII0800mini-LUC reporter vectors; and the pGreenII-SK empty vector were used as controls. Luciferase signals were captured and analyzed using a Tanon 5200 Multi Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

An EMSA was carried out as described previously (Smith and Delbary-Gossart, 2001). The amplified CDS of GhWRKY28 was fused in-frame with His tags and transformed into E. coli pCNZ1. Oligonucleotide probes were synthesized and labeled with biotin at their 5′ ends (Invitrogen). The EMSA was performed using the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).
