Identification of IL18/IL18R1 signaling as a predictive biomarker of SGLT2 inhibitor efficacy in type 2 diabetes
Authors: I-Jou Teng, Chien-Hsing Lee, Ying-Chen Chen, Sheng-Chiang Su, Chieh-Hua Lu, Peng-Fei Li, Chia-Luen Huang, Li-Ju Ho, Ming-Hsun Lin, Hsin-Ya Liu, Feng-Chih Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) are widely used to manage type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) to ameliorate hyperglycemia, albuminuria, and obesity, with established cardiorenal benefits. However, predictive biomarkers for therapeutic responses remain limited. In this study, seventy patients initiating SGLT2i therapy were evaluated clinically and through plasma proteomics and transcriptomics. Treatment significantly improved HbA1c (71.2%), albuminuria (47.5%), and weight (58.6%), with benefits in insulin resistance, β-cell function, blood pressure, uric acid, and body composition. Proteomic analysis identified IL-18R1 reduction linked to improved glycemic control, albuminuria, fat mass, and liver function. Transcriptomic profiling revealed genetic alterations in blood mononuclear cells, reinforcing anti-inflammatory changes. Receiver operating characteristic analysis indicated high baseline IL-18/IL-18R1 predicted overall response (area under the curve: 0.75, p = 0.005; cutoffs: IL-18 = 244.29 pg/mL, IL-18R1 = 325.97 pg/mL). IL-18/IL-18R1 signaling emerges as a promising biomarker for predicting therapeutic responses, guiding clinical decisions regarding SGLT2i use in T2DM management.Subject areas: Human metabolism, proteomics

Introduction

Sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) constitute a class of antidiabetic agents that provide multifaceted therapeutic benefits extending beyond glycemic control. Initially designed to mitigate hyperglycemia by facilitating urinary glucose excretion through the inhibition of glucose reabsorption mediated by SGLT2 in the proximal renal tubule, SGLT2i have been shown to promote weight loss and, notably, to confer significant cardiovascular and renal protective effects.1 These cardiorenal benefits are now supported by large-scale clinical trials, such as the EMPA-REG OUTCOME,2 CANVAS,3 DECLARE–TIMI 58,4 CREDENCE,5 DAPA-CKD,6 and DAPA-HF7 studies, and have reshaped the treatment paradigm for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Accordingly, clinical guidelines increasingly recommend SGLT2i not only for glycemic control but also for the prevention and management of chronic kidney disease (CKD), heart failure, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), irrespective of baseline glycemic status.8,9,10

According to the 2021 IDF Diabetes Atlas,11 the prevalence of T2DM in the Taiwanese adult population is 13.1%, highlighting the significant challenge of managing diabetes associated metabolic dysregulation and complications. Recent conceptual shifts in the understanding of T2DM pathophysiology have led to the recognition of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a systemic continuum beginning with adipose tissue dysfunction and progressing through insulin resistance, T2DM, chronic kidney disease, subclinical cardiovascular abnormalities, and ultimately overt ASCVD.12 Within this framework, SGLT2i occupies a unique therapeutic niche, offering multifaceted benefits across obesity, hyperglycemia, CKD, heart failure, and cardiovascular disease. However, despite their broad spectrum of action, clinical observations frequently report heterogeneous responses to SGLT2i therapy. For instance, discrepancies have been reported between SGLT2i-mediated improvements in glycemic control and weight loss.13 Additionally, some studies suggest that the relationship between glycemic control and albuminuria reduction may not always be directly correlated.14 At present, CKM evaluation remains largely organ-centric, relying on markers such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). Yet, in the CREDENCE trial of T2DM with CKD shows that integrated biomarker panels, combining NT-proBNP, high-sensitivity cardiac Troponin T (hs-cTnT), growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15), and insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7 (IGFBP7), enhance the prediction of cardio-renal outcomes.15 In parallel, urinary proteomics and metabolomics reveal SGLT2i induced shifts in inflammatory, fibrotic, and metabolic pathways linked to clinical benefit.16,17 These findings underscore the need for multi-omic strategies that capture CKM biology and therapeutic response beyond glucose lowering.

To address this need, we conducted a prospective study in Taiwanese patients with T2DM initiating SGLT2i for glycemic, weight, or cardiorenal protection. Participants underwent serial, multidimensional phenotyping, including biochemistry, grip strength, and body composition alongside plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collection for deep molecular profiling. Using Olink proximity extension assays, we quantified 181 inflammatory and cardiovascular proteins from minimal volumes of plasma. This method has previously been applied in studies such as EMPEROR for heart failure18 and in biomarker discovery for eclampsia19 and COVID-19.20 This integrated clinical molecular design aims to explore molecular alterations associated with SGLT2i treatment and identify predictive biomarkers, particularly focusing on the IL-18/IL-18R1 signaling pathway, to support precision medicine approaches in the management of T2DM and CKM syndrome.

Results

Effects of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor therapy on anthropometric measures, biochemical parameters, and body composition

The baseline characteristics and longitudinal data for all 70 subjects receiving SGLT2i therapy are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 60.0 years, with 44.3% being female, and the median duration of diabetes was 6.8 years. Four different SGLT2i agents were prescribed, with dapagliflozin being the most frequently administered (72.9%). At baseline, the median body weight was 72.7 kg, the median BMI was 27.3 kg/m2, and the median waist circumference was 93 cm, with a waist-to-hip ratio of 0.93. The median baseline HbA1c level was 7.6%, and the median UACR was 63 mg/g.

At both the 3-month and 6-month follow-up assessments, SGLT2i therapy was associated with significant improvements in fasting glucose and HbA1c levels, alongside a reduction in insulin resistance, as measured by HOMA-IR, and an enhancement in β-cell function, as indicated by HOMA-β. Furthermore, SGLT2i therapy led to reductions in the levels of uric acid and blood pressure. The glucosuria induced by SGLT2i exerts distinct metabolic effects, promoting a negative energy balance, which translates into clinically meaningful reductions in body weight, BMI, and waist circumference as detailed in Table 1.

Additionally, bioelectrical impedance analysis was employed to evaluate SGLT2i-driven alterations in body composition. As illustrated in Table 1, SGLT2i therapy significantly decreased both total fat and lean mass, with a more pronounced effect on fat mass, while demonstrating no significant impact on total bone mineral content. As a result, SGLT2i administration was particularly effective in reducing regional adiposity and visceral fat area, contributing to a decrease in the fat-to-lean ratio across multiple anatomical regions, including the arms, legs, and trunk, while preserving grip strength despite a mild reduction in appendicular skeletal mass indexed to height2 (ASM/Ht2).

Baseline and dynamic protein levels correlating to sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor therapeutic responses

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of SGLT2i, subjects were stratified into effective and ineffective responders, as described in the methodology. Overall, SGLT2i therapy demonstrated substantial clinical benefits, with 71.2% of patients achieving a clinically meaningful reduction in HbA1c, 47.5% exhibiting significant improvement in albuminuria, and 58.6% demonstrating notable weight loss among the total cohort of 70 individuals (Table S1). To identify potential predictive biomarkers associated with therapeutic response and assess SGLT2i-induced alterations in circulating proteins, Olink proteomics study, encompassing 181 proteins, was performed on plasma samples obtained from 44 selected patients with T2DM before and after SGLT2i administration (Table 2). These participants were selected to capture a spectrum of therapeutic responses, including both effective and ineffective responders, based on changes in HbA1c, UACR, and body weight. This stratified sampling approach was proposed to detect proteomic signatures associated with differential treatment efficacy. These selected individuals exhibited therapeutic responses (Table S1), and improvements in anthropometric measures, biochemical parameters, and body composition (Tables S2) representative of the total cohort.

As summarized in Table 2, several circulating proteins exhibited significant correlations between their baseline levels and post-treatment alterations in relation to SGLT2i-mediated therapeutic efficacy. Specifically, baseline concentrations and therapy-induced changes in interleukin-18 receptor 1 (IL-18R1), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 11B (TNFRSF11B), NT-proBNP, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (SERPINE1), and matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2) were strongly correlated with HbA1c response. With respect to weight reduction, interleukin-5 (IL-5) and complement component C1q receptor (CD93) emerged as candidate predictive biomarkers. Furthermore, IL-18R1, SERPINE1, and aminopeptidase N (ANPEP) were identified as key proteins linked to overall therapeutic outcomes, based on both baseline levels and their dynamic alterations following SGLT2i treatment.

Associations between circulating protein alterations and clinical phenotypic improvements following sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor therapy

To elucidate the relationship between SGLT2i-induced phenotypic improvements and alterations in circulating protein levels, we analyzed and illustrated the correlation coefficients between protein level changes and various clinical parameters, adjusting for age, sex and BMI. These parameters include anthropometric measures, body composition metrics, glycemic indices, liver-related features and nephrotic markers, as depicted in Figure 1A.

Among the circulating proteins associated with blood pressure or body composition changes, activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM), CD93, and neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 3 (NOTCH3) exhibited positive correlations with blood pressure, whereas growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3) showed negative associations. Additionally, interleukin-1 receptor type 2 (IL-1R2), tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), and E-selectin (SELE) were positively correlated with weight, total fat mass, regional fat mass, and fat-to-lean mass ratio (arms, legs, or trunk). SELE further demonstrated a positive association with visceral fat area. On the other hand, GDF15 presented negative correlations with regional lean mass (arm, leg, and trunk) and ASM/Ht2.

Several circulating proteins were linked to improvements in glycemic control, insulin resistance, and β-cell function. IL-18R1, cathepsin D (CTSD), integrin beta-2 (ITGB2), low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), and SELE exhibited positive correlations with fasting glucose or HbA1c, while CD93, interleukin-2 receptor alpha (IL-2RA), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) were negatively associated with glycemic control. Furthermore, SELE, CTSD, and SERPINE1 showed positive associations with HOMA-IR, whereas LDLR and chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1) displayed negative correlations with HOMA-β.

Alternations of multiple plasma proteins were positively correlated with changes in liver-related markers, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and triglycerides (TG). These proteins include ALCAM, aminopeptidase N (ANPEP), cluster of differentiation 163 (CD163), CTSD, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), IL-18R1, IL-1R2, ITGB2, LDLR, SELE, TFPI, and TNFRSF11B. Also, these circulating molecules generally exhibited negative associations with low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels. After false discovery rate (FDR) corrections, significant associations with AST and/or TG persisted for ALCAM, ANPEP, CD163, CTSD, IL-18R1, IL-1R2, and LDLR (Figure 1A, marked with “#”).

Dynamic changes in nephrotic markers, such as blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and uric acid (UA), were positively correlated with GDF15, FGF23, heparan sulfate proteoglycan 2 (HSPG2), and trefoil factor 3 (TFF3), while showing negative associations with interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1A), Interleukin-5 (IL-5), and secretoglobin family 3A member 2 (SCGB3A2). Additionally, changes of IL-18R1 demonstrated a positive correlation with ΔUACR, whereas alterations in FGF23 were negatively associated with changes in UACR.

Overall, SGLT2i mediated alternations in several plasma proteins were linked to improvements across multiple phenotypic variables (Figure 1A). Specifically, we observed ΔIL-18R1 positively associated with changes of total fat mass, fasting glucose, HbA1c, ALT, AST, TG, and UACR (β = 0.31 to 0.71), but negatively related to ΔLDL (β = −0.38). ΔIL-1R2 displayed positive associations with alterations of weight, regional fat-to-lean mass ratios (arm, leg, and trunk), and liver-related features (β = 0.30 to 0.54). SGLT2i-mediated alteration of GDF15 exhibited negative correlations with changes of all regional lean mass and ASM/Ht2 (β = −0.47 to −0.37), but positively associated with nephrotic parameters (BUN, creatinine, and UA) (β = 0.32 to 0.41). ΔCD93 was linked to changes of blood pressure, ASM/Ht2, visceral fat area, fasting glucose, and HbA1c (β = 0.50 to 0.61 or −0.44 to −40). The alterations of CD163 and ALCAM were positively correlated with diastolic blood pressure, weight, and arm fat mass, and liver-related features (β = 0.33 to 0.54). TFPI level was related to waist circumference, weight, total and regional fat mass, and TG (β = 0.29 to 0.43). TFF3 level was significantly associated with total cholesterol, BUN, creatinine, and UA, and PI3 showed correlations with blood pressure, liver-related features, and creatinine (β = 0.38 to 0.45 or −0.31 to −0.50). These findings underscore the multifaceted role of SGLT2i-induced alterations in circulating protein levels and their potential contributions to systemic metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular improvements.

Proteomic insights into inflammatory pathway modulation by sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors in clinical responders

To further investigate the dominant molecular effects of SGLT2i therapy associated with favorable clinical responses, we examined plasma proteins exhibiting significant changes among patients classified as effective responders based on improvements in HbA1c (HbA1c reduction ≥0.6%), UACR (UACR decrease ≥30%), or body weight (weight loss ≥2 kg) individually (Figure 1B), as well as those demonstrating simultaneous effective improvements across all three parameters (Figure 1C). Across these responder groups, SGLT2i treatment repeatedly modulated a subset of proteins, notably increasing levels of PI3, TFF3, and FGF23, while decreasing IL-18R1, TFPI, CD163, ALCAM, and IL-1R2 (Figures 1B and 1C, p < 0.05). Among individuals exhibiting all effective responses, a total of 15 proteins were significantly downregulated, including IL-18R1, IL-1R2, TNFRSF11B, SERPINE1, CD163, TFPI, and LDLR (Figure 1C, p < 0.05). Functional pathway enrichment analysis of these proteins revealed significant attenuation of inflammatory and immune-related pathways, such as interleukin-1 signaling, T-helper 1 cytokine production, TNF signaling, complement and coagulation cascades, as well as lipid and atherosclerosis (Figure 1D). Collectively, these findings highlight the complex interplay between circulating proteins and clinical outcomes, providing mechanistic insights into the therapeutic benefits of SGLT2i therapy.

Transcriptomic profiling of peripheral blood mononuclear cells reveals immunomodulatory and therapeutic effects of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors related to weight loss and cardiorenal protection

To further detail the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory impact of SGLT2i therapy, RNA-sequencing was performed on PBMC samples from three patients with T2DM before and after the 3-month administration of SGLT2i. The heatmap revealed significant variation between individual patients (Figure 2A). Volcano plot analysis showed increases in Methanethiol oxidase (SELENBP1) and Hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF1A), alongside decreases in Interleukin-2 (IL-2), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase TRIM69 (TRIM69), E3 SUMO-protein ligase EGR2 (EGR2), Apolipoprotein(a) (LPA), C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2), Tumor necrosis factor alpha-induced protein 8-like protein 2 (TNFAIP8L2), VIP peptides (VIP), and Cathepsin L2 (CTSV) after three months of therapy (Figure 2B, n = 3, p < 0.05). Gene network analysis in IPA highlighted key functional changes, including the enhancement of pathways associated with weight loss, CD4 T cell differentiation, and Th17 cell differentiation, as well as the inhibition of processes linked to monocyte movement, kidney injury, and heart contraction (Figure 2C).

IL-18/IL-18R1 signaling predicts clinical response to sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor therapy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus

Baseline IL-18R1 levels and their dynamic changes were correlated with HbA1c levels and the overall therapeutic response to SGLT2i therapy in Table 2. Although IL-1–receptor signaling was the most affected pathway, the greater reductions in IL-18R1 occurred in patients who achieved favorable outcomes, and these alterations was positively associated with multiple phenotypic improvements including glycemic control, total fat mass reduction, and decreased UACR (Figure 1A). Collectively, these observations suggest that IL-18R1 could serve as a biomarker for predicting clinical response to SGLT2i therapy in type 2 diabetes.

To evaluate this, ELISA validated concentrations and logistic regression models were employed to assess the predictive value of IL-18, IL-18R1, and their combination. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed that each marker, both individually and in combination, significantly predicted overall therapeutic response (Figure 3A). Notably, IL-18R1 alone and the combined IL-18/IL-18R1 panel demonstrated superior predictive performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.750. When examining individual clinical outcomes, circulating IL-18R1 levels significantly predicted weight response (AUC = 0.644, p = 0.03), while associations with changes in HbA1c and UACR did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3B).

The combined IL-18/IL-18R1 panel achieved an AUC of 0.750 (95% CI: 0.568–0.932, p = 0.002), with cutoff values of 244.29 pg/mL for IL-18 and 325.97 pg/mL for IL-18R1.

(B) ROC curves for IL-18R1 were further analyzed for individual clinical outcomes. AUC values were 0.584 (95% CI: 0.414–0.754, p = 0.11) for HbA1c reduction, 0.652 (95% CI: 0.480–0.824, p = 0.075) for UACR reduction, 0.644 (95% CI: 0.505–0.783, p = 0.03) for weight loss, and 0.750 (95% CI: 0.568–0.932, p < 0.001) for overall therapeutic response.

Discussion

Given the multifaceted benefits of SGLT2i in the treatment of T2DM, this study employed high-throughput Olink proteomic analysis to assess circulating protein alterations in 44 patients following SGLT2i therapy. Through targeted proteomics and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) validation, IL-18R1 and IL-18 emerged as promising biomarkers linked to clinical outcomes. These findings have the potential to refine individual risk-benefit evaluations for SGLT2i treatment, thereby supporting more targeted therapeutic approaches in clinical practice.

In this study, SGLT2i therapy effectively improved glycemic control, reducing HbA1c levels by around 0.6%. Consistent with prior studies,21,22,23 these clinical observations also revealed modest weight loss and improvements in UACR, reinforcing the metabolic and renal benefits of SGLT2i. Additionally, significant changes in body composition, including fat mass reduction and muscle mass decline, were observed, aligning with previous findings.24

Beyond glycemic control, recent evidence suggests that SGLT2i therapy exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects, modulates energy metabolism, and enhances nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation.25 To explore circulating flatulated proteins, we conducted Olink proteomic profiling to assess a total of 181 inflammatory and cardiovascular biomarkers. Particularly, IL-18R1 emerged as a significant biomarker, displaying elevated baseline levels in patients with effective therapeutic responses. Following SGLT2i therapy, IL-18R1 levels significantly declined, correlating with improvements in fasting glucose, HbA1c, total fat mass, UACR, uric acid, AST, ALT, and triglycerides. Given that circulating IL-18 signaling is implicated in diabetes and metabolic syndrome,26 and that IL-18R1 functions as a key regulator of inflammatory pathways and insulin resistance,27 its post-treatment reduction may reflect decreased systemic inflammation and enhanced metabolic regulation. Pathway analyses in favorable responders showed decreased activity in IL-1 receptor signaling, inflammatory responses, Th1 cytokine production, TNF signaling, complement and coagulation cascades, and pathways related to very low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR) activity. Specifically, reductions in IL-1R2 correlated with decreased total fat mass, regional fat mass (trunk, arm, and leg), and improvements in liver-related metabolic markers (AST and triglycerides). The concurrent reductions in IL-1R2 and IL-18R1 likely reflect the inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome, potentially mediated by increased ketone production induced by SGLT2i therapy.28,29

ROC analyses further validated IL-18R1, both independently and in combination with IL-18, as an effective predictive biomarker of therapeutic response. Notably, IL-18R1 significantly predicted weight reduction outcomes, highlighting its specific utility in assessing SGLT2i efficacy. These findings suggest that IL-18R1 could serve as a putative biomarker for evaluating the therapeutic benefits of SGLT2i in patients with T2DM.

Beyond IL-18R1, alterations in circulating levels of GDF15 were negatively associated with changes in regional lean mass and positively correlated with nephrotic parameters (BUN, creatinine, and uric acid). Given the role of GDF15 in energy homeostasis with the regulation of appetite and body weight30 and its pharmacological induction by metformin,30,31 these findings suggest a mechanistic link between GDF15 and SGLT2i-mediated cardio-renal-metabolic benefits.

Additionally, macrophage related proteins, CD163, CHI3L1, and CD93, exhibited significant reductions after SGLT2i treatment. Since CD163 and CHI3L1 are established markers of macrophage activation,32 their downregulation emphasizes the attenuation of systemic inflammation as a key mechanism underlying SGLT2i-mediated therapeutic benefits, particularly in adiposity and weight loss regulation. These alterations align with previous perspectives that SGLT2i suppresses inflammatory pathways by downregulating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activity, thereby reducing the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators from endothelial cells and macrophages.33 Moreover, PBMC RNA-seq demonstrated this anti-inflammatory shift, with the increased expression of SELENBP1 and HIF1A, alongside decreased levels of IL-2, EGR2, LPA, CCR2, TNFAIP8L2, and VIP. The increased expression of SELENBP1 supports enhanced cell differentiation,34 while elevated HIF1A facilitates tissue repair and adaptation under metabolic stress conditions.35 Reductions in CCR2, IL-2, EGR2, TNFAIP8L2, LPA, and VIP expressions collectively indicate diminished inflammation, reduced monocyte recruitment, and attenuation of pro-inflammatory signaling,36,37,38,39,40,41 which may contribute to improved metabolic health outcomes from SGLT2i therapy.

Taken together, this study presents a novel approach combining detailed clinical phenotyping with broad Olink-based plasma proteomics to integrate SGLT2i responses in T2DM. We identify IL-18R1 alone and with IL-18 as a circulating protein predictor of overall therapeutic response, and map additional candidates (GDF15, IL-1R2, CD163, CHI3L1, CD93) to pathways consistent with SGLT2i-related metabolic and cardiorenal benefits. Although constrained by a modest sample size and single-center design, this work provides a testable framework for biomarker-guided, precision prescribing of SGLT2 inhibitors. Nevertheless, the findings underscore the importance of integrating multi-dimensional clinical parameters with broad plasma proteomic profiling to elucidate biomolecular associations that could guide future therapeutic strategies for diabetes care, including the evaluation of CKM progression.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations exist in this study, including: (1) although 70 patients were enrolled, the discovery proteomics subset was modest (n = 44) and the PBMC RNA-seq analysis was very small (n = 3), limiting statistical power and generalizability, especially limited significant results after FDR correction; (2) the single-center and observational study restricts causal inference, without a concurrent control group; (3) covariate adjustment was limited to age, sex, and BMI, so residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future, adequately powered cohorts should incorporate more comprehensive adjustment (e.g., baseline glycemic control and renal function) and employ formal causal-inference approaches; (4) follow-up was restricted to 6 months, so longer-term effects could not be assessed; (5) ROC cutoffs derived by Youden’s J are provisional and may not generalize across populations, necessitating external multi-center and multi-ethnic validation with calibration and decision-curve analyses before clinical application; (6) the clinical utility of IL-18/IL-18R1-guided SGLT2 inhibitor use will require standardized SOPs, and formal cost-effectiveness and impact studies. These limitations underscore the need for larger, independent, and ethnically diverse cohorts within a phase 2–3 biomarker-validation framework consistent with TRIPOD/PROBAST,42 including external replication, calibration, and decision-curve analysis.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study enrolled 70 patients who regularly attended follow-up visits at the outpatient Endocrinology and Metabolism clinic of Tri-Service General Hospital. Recruitment was conducted between April 2022 and February 2023. Participants were eligible if they were aged 20 to 80 years with a diagnosis of T2DM, meeting at least one of the following criteria: inadequate glycemic control (HbA1c > 7%), overweight (BMI ≥24 kg/m2), or elevated urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR ≥30 mg/g). These criteria were designated to identify individuals likely to derive therapeutic benefits from SGLT2i treatment. The proportions of participants meeting one to three criteria were visually represented in the supplementary data (Figure S1). Individuals were excluded if they were pregnant, or presented acute illness, such as urinary tract infection, acute pancreatitis, cerebrovascular accident, myocardial infarction, heart failure, renal failure, hepatic failure, or psychiatric diseases. Additionally, individuals undergoing chronic treatment with sex hormones or glucocorticoids were excluded. Written informed consents were obtained from all participants, ensuring their agreement to the confidential handling and use of their data. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Tri-Service General Hospital (TSGHIRB numbers: 2-108-05-052 and A202105153).

Participants attended laboratory visits at three time points: baseline (prior to SGLT2i administration), 3 months, and 6 months following treatment initiation. At each visit, anthropometric measurements, maximal grip strength of the dominant hand, and biochemical variables were assessed using previously established protocols.43 Fasting glucose and c-peptide levels were applied to calculate the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and β cell function (HOMA-β) via HOMA2 calculator.44 The body composition was analyzed utilizing bioelectrical impedance technology via the InBody 720 device, with all participants adhering to a minimum fasting period of 8 h. During the assessment, subjects stood in an upright position to ensure accurate electrode placement, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Method details

Definitions for SGLT2i therapeutic responses

All 70 subjects were followed-up for 6 months with 3 visits (before, 3-month after and 6-month after SGLT2i treatment). The maximal therapeutic effects during 6-month follow-up were utilized to assess responses based on changes of HbA1c, UACR and body weight. According to previous publications, HbA1c reduction ≥0.6%,21 a decrease of UACR ≥30%23 or weight loss ≥2 kg22 was generally considered as effective response.

When evaluating the HbA1c response following SGLT2i administration, we categorized participants who received SGLT2i as an add-on therapy and those who switched from another oral hypoglycemic agent separately, given the potential influence of the original hypoglycemic agent on HbA1c levels. Effective responders were defined as individuals with baseline HbA1c ≥ 7% who achieved a reduction of ≥0.6% after treatment, whether SGLT2i was added to existing therapy or switched from another oral hypoglycemic agent. The criteria for ineffective responders differed between treatment scenarios. For add-on therapy, ineffective responders included participants with baseline HbA1c ≥ 7% who did not achieve a reduction of ≥0.6% (e.g., a decrease of <0.6% or an increase in HbA1c), as well as those with baseline HbA1c < 7% whose HbA1c rose to >7% during treatment. For individuals switching from another oral hypoglycemic agent to SGLT2i, ineffective responders were defined as those with baseline HbA1c ≥ 7% who experienced an increase of ≥0.6%, and those with baseline HbA1c < 7% whose HbA1c increased to >7% after the switch.

For the evaluation of UACR response following SGLT2i administration, only participants with a baseline UACR of ≥30 mg/g were assessed. Effective responders were defined as individuals who achieved a reduction in UACR of ≥30%, whereas those with a reduction of <30% were classified as ineffective responders.

Regarding weight response after receiving SGLT2i, participants who experienced a reduction in body weight of ≥2 kg were categorized as effective responders, while those with a weight reduction of <2 kg were designated as ineffective responders.

Proteomics measurements and following analysis

For plasma preparation, participants underwent a minimum fasting period of 8 h prior to blood sample collection. Blood samples were drawn into EDTA tubes and immediately processed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min (HERMLE Z400K). The supernatant containing plasma was carefully collected and stored at −80°C until further use. Plasma protein abundance was measured using a proximity extension assay with the Target 96 Cardiovascular III and Target 96 Inflammation panels (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden). This technology utilizes oligonucleotide-linked antibodies combined with qPCR, generating normalized protein expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale.45

PBMC isolation with RNA-sequence analysis

PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll-Paque PLUS density gradient centrifugation. Fresh whole blood was diluted 1:1 with filtered phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and carefully layered onto Ficoll without mixing. After centrifugation at 400×g for 20 min with reduced deceleration, the plasma layer was removed, and the PBMC interface was collected into new tubes. Cells were washed twice with PBS by centrifugation at 500 rpm for 10 min to remove residual contaminants (Thermo HERAEUS Megafuge 16R centrifuge, 75003181 with Swinging Bucket Rotor, 7500365). The final pellet was resuspended in Trizol reagent for RNA extraction and stored at −80°C for downstream analysis.

Total RNA library was prepared using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep, Ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus (20040529, illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with 100 ng of input RNA and 12 PCR cycles performed on a ProFlex PCR System (4484073, ThermoFisher). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (v1.5 chemistry) with 150-nucleotide paired-end reads, generating an average of 51 million raw reads per library (range: 27.4–73.5 million). Base calling and conversion to FASTQ format were performed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq.sh program. Data preprocessing included quality assessment, duplicate detection, and adapter sequence removal using CLC Genomics Workbench (v21). Reads were mapped to the Homo sapiens reference genome (GRCh38), and differentially expressed genes were identified using the same platform, with significance set at a false discovery rate (FDR) p < 0.05. Transcript abundance was quantified as transcript per million (TPM) using a negative binomial Generalized Linear Model (GLM) for intergroup comparisons.

ELISA assay

Human plasma levels of IL-18 (Abnova, Walnut, CA, USA) and IL-18R1 (Sunlong Biotech, Hangzhou City, ZJ, China) were detected using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of standards or samples prepared in diluent buffer were added to 96-well microplates pre-coated with capture antibodies and incubated for 1.5 h at 37°C with gentle shaking. Subsequently, plates were washed, and 100 μL of HRP-conjugated detection antibodies were added to each well and incubated for an additional 1 h at 37°C. Following another wash step, 100 μL of substrate solution was added and incubated in the dark for 15 min at 37°C. The reaction was terminated with 50 μL of stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and sample concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve. Each sample was measured in duplicate, and the mean value was used to determine the final concentration.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In the plasma proteomics measurement with Olink platform, a total of 89 proteins from the Cardiovascular III panel and 76 from the Inflammation panel were detected in more than 75% of the samples. Paired Student’s t-tests compared pre- and post-therapy samples in the analyses of effect responders. Post-therapy protein alterations were transformed into linear scales, and missing values were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) method (k = 5). Pre-therapy baseline protein levels and their alterations after SGLT2i therapy were correlated with SGLT2i therapeutic response via performing Sperman correlation with adjustment for age, sex and BMI in SPSS software version 22 (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Associations between Δprotein expression and Δclinical variables were adjusted for scaled age, sex, and BMI, with multiple testing corrected using the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Statistically significant associations were visualized as heatmaps. These analyses were performed in R (v 4.4.1)46 using the packages ggplot2 (v 3.5.1),47 stringr (v 1.5.1), Hmisc (v 5.1-3), dplyr (v 1.1.4), pheatmap (v 1.0.12), tidyverse (v 2.0.0), ggplotify (v 0.1.2), heatmaply (v 1.5.0), ggbiplot (v 0.6.2), and DMwR2 (v 0.0.2). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using ShinyGO (v 0.80).48

RNA sequencing data were filtered to retain genes with fewer than 30% missing values or those with over 90% missing in one group but less than 10% in others. Missing values exceeding 90% in a group were imputed using 20% of the lowest detection values, while all remaining missing values were imputed using the KNN method (k = 2). Relative RNA quantities were log2-transformed. Paired Student’s t-tests were used for statistical analysis, with multiple testing corrected via the Benjamini-Hochberg method.49 Hierarchical clustering was applied, and results were visualized as heatmaps. Enrichment analysis of significantly altered transcripts post-treatment was performed using QIAGEN ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA).50 Data processing, statistical analysis, and visualization were conducted in R (v 4.4.1)46 using ggplot2 (v 3.5.1),47 stringr (v 1.5.1), Hmisc (v 5.1-3), dplyr (v 1.1.4), pheatmap (v 1.0.12), tidyverse (v 2.0.0), ggplotify (v 0.1.2), heatmaply (v 1.5.0), ggbiplot (v 0.6.2), and DMwR2 (v 0.0.2).

After measuring circulating IL-18 and IL-18R1 levels by ELISA kits, logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the association between IL-18 levels, IL-18R1 levels, and SGLT2i treatment outcomes in patients with T2DM. The primary outcome, overall therapeutic response, was modeled using a binomial family. Age, sex, and BMI were standardized with z-scores and included as covariates. Three separate models assessed the predictive value of IL-18 alone, IL-18R1 alone, and both combined. Predicted probabilities were used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for area under the curve (AUC) estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI), while Youden’s J index identified optimal cutoff points. Further logistic regressions were performed for weight change, HbA1c, and UACR using the combined IL-18/IL-18R1 model, adjusting for scaled age, sex, and scaled BMI. All analyses were performed in R (v 4.4.1)46 using pROC (v 1.18.5), broom (v 1.0.7), ggplot2 (v 3.5.1),47 and dplyr (v 1.1.4). Results were presented as ROC curves, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.
