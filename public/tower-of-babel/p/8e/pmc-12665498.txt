Toward unraveling molecular grammars for dsRNA-binding proteins: substrate recognition to binding mechanisms
Authors: Jaemin Jeon, Yoosik Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Long double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) are recognized by innate immune response proteins, thereby initiating the integrated stress response. As these RNAs adopt an A-form helical structure, immune sensors recognize dsRNAs primarily based on their structural features, such as the length of the double-stranded stretch and the triphosphate at the 5’ end, rather than on specific sequences. This structure-dependent, sequence-independent mode of RNA recognition is also characteristic of many dsRNA-binding proteins (dsRBPs). Consequently, multiple dsRBPs share a common pool of dsRNA substrates, leading to a complex regulatory network in which proteins modulate each other’s activation status and signaling activities. With the development of advanced analytical techniques capable of studying RNA sequences and structures at single-nucleotide resolution, research into dsRNA-protein interactions has advanced significantly. This review discusses the long dsRNA-interacting dsRBPs encoded in the human genome, their RNA substrates, recognition mechanisms, and the downstream effects of protein-RNA interactions, with the aim of deepening our understanding of dsRNA recognition and signaling.

INTRODUCTION

In the 1970s, researchers discovered that cells infected with poliovirus produce dsRNAs that inhibit protein synthesis (1, 2) and trigger the interferon (IFN) response (3). Subsequent studies demonstrated that these dsRNAs are byproducts of viral replication or generated during the transcription of the viral genome. For instance, a positive single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) virus synthesizes a complementary RNA to its genomic RNA and utilizes it as a template to replicate the viral genome. As such, recognizing viral dsRNAs becomes a primary defense mechanism, with innate immune receptors having evolved to bind to these long dsRNAs to initiate antiviral and inflammatory responses (4). Interestingly, these innate immune response proteins recognize viral dsRNAs based on their structural features, such as the length of the dsRNA and end modifications, like the presence of 5’-triphosphate (5’-ppp), making these proteins known as pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). This sequence-independent interaction is partly attributed to the structure of dsRNAs, which form an A-form helix with a narrow major groove and a wide minor groove, impeding the binding protein’s ability to access individual RNA bases (5). At the same time, this binding mechanism enables the PRRs to interact with a broad range of viral dsRNAs and initiate innate immune responses against them.

Over the last two decades, it has become apparent that dsRNAs are not exclusive to viruses, and significant quantities of endogenous dsRNAs exist in uninfected mammalian cells (6), largely due to the prevalence of mobile repeat elements like Alu in primates (7). Additionally, PRRs can detect these endogenous dsRNAs, as they also form A-helical structures over several hundred base pairs (bp) in length. This interaction triggers the activation of antiviral machinery, leading to inflammation and cell death in the absence of infection. Notably, such abnormal immune responses occur under stress and when cellular processes are dysregulated, positioning cellular dsRNAs as key factors in the development of inflammatory and degenerative diseases.

In addition to PRRs, cellular dsRNAs with immunogenic activity can also interact with dsRBPs. These proteins are essential for several cellular functions, including microRNA (miRNA) biogenesis, adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing, RNA stability, and translational control (8-11). Characterized by highly conserved dsRNA-binding domains (dsRBDs), many dsRBPs bind to a common pool of dsRNAs (Table 1) (12). However, dsRBPs exhibit unique regulatory actions on their target dsRNAs, participating in diverse cellular processes. Recent studies have also highlighted the distinct substrate specificity of dsRBPs. For instance, different dsRBDs recognize unique structural features such as bulges and interior loops, enabling dsRBPs to identify specific, albeit partially overlapping, sets of cellular dsRNAs (13-15). Additionally, studies also began to investigate the sequence-specific interaction of dsRBDs. In particular, a study by Stefl and colleagues revealed sequence-specific interaction to G-X8-A and G-X9-A motifs for Adenosine Deaminase Acting on RNA 2 (ADAR2) (16).

The downstream signaling pathway following dsRBPs activation via dsRNA interactions has been well-established. Nevertheless, the substrate specificity of dsRBPs and the biochemical mechanisms that enable dsRBPs to distinguish their specific dsRNAs have not been fully elucidated. Yet, increasing structural knowledge about dsRBPs and dsRBDs offers new perspectives on dsRNA-protein interactions. In this review, we summarize the latest findings that clarify the molecular principles of dsRNA-dsRBP interactions, focusing on the structural attributes of dsRBDs and the molecular determinants of dsRNA recognition. We also describe the molecular features of dsRBPs and their varied mechanisms of binding. Of note, we focus our review on presenting dsRBPs that can interact with immunogenic long dsRNAs or that contain at least one canonical dsRBD that can interact with long dsRNAs. Together, this detailed review seeks to provide an updated understanding of the intricate relationships between dsRNAs and their binding proteins.

Structures of dsRBDs

DsRBDs selectively recognize dsRNAs within the diverse structural and chemical milieu of nucleic acids. This specificity arises from two principal attributes of the dsRNA’s A-form helix: the relatively narrow, deep major groove and the hydroxyl groups on the ribose sugars (Fig. 1). The dsRBD’s core structure comprises 70 amino acids, forming an α-β-β-β-α fold with two α-helices on one side of a three-stranded antiparallel β-sheet (17). This consistent topology and its distinctive features have been rigorously validated through NMR spectroscopy and X-ray crystallography studies. While many amino acids remain conserved across the domain, the N-terminal two-thirds exhibit greater variability, which contributes to the functional diversity seen in various dsRBPs.

Molecular recognition by dsRBDs is facilitated through three interaction regions: helix α1 and the loop between β1 and β2 interact with the dsRNA’s minor grooves at intervals of one turn, while the N-terminal tip of helix α2 connects with the phosphate backbone across the major groove. A single dsRBD typically binds a dsRNA segment ranging from 11 to 16 bp in length (18). Interaction with dsRNA is restricted to one face of the helix, preserving the rest of the surface for additional interactions (18). Moreover, dsRBDs can recognize imperfect dsRNAs with bulges and internal loops, which widen the major groove and allow amino acids to penetrate, enhancing sequence-specific recognition (19).

dsRBDs can be classified into type-1 (dsRBD-I) and type-2 (dsRBD-II). The canonical form, dsRBD-I, binds to dsRNAs and exhibits good amino acid conservation in the three essential regions involved in RNA binding. In contrast, dsRBD-II shows conservation only at the C-terminal end of the domain and does not bind to dsRNAs, despite having a similar three-dimensional structure to that of dsRBD-I (12, 19, 20). Notably, biochemical studies have shown that dsRBD-II functions as a protein-protein interaction domain, facilitating both homo- and heterodimerization with other proteins possessing dsRBDs (21). Besides the canonical α-β-β-β-α structure, dsRBDs may include structural elements that confer additional substrate recognition capabilities. For instance, DiGeorge syndrome critical region 8 (DGCR8), a component of the microprocessor complex, contains two dsRBD-Is that work together in interacting with specific regions of primary-miRNAs (pri-miRNAs). Specifically, one dsRBD associates with the ssRNA-dsRNA junction, while another interacts with the stem-loop structure of the pri-miRNA (22). This distinct binding capability stems from the well-defined secondary structure of DGCR8, which includes rigid linkers, in contrast to the flexible linkers seen in other dsRBPs. Additionally, the C-terminal tail (CTT) of DGCR8 contributes to the structural integrity (23). The linkers and CTT are crucial in maintaining the relative positioning of the two dsRBDs.

A single dsRBP can contain multiple dsRBDs, ranging from one to five; however, the implications of possessing multiple dsRBDs are not well-understood for most proteins. There is no correlation between the number of dsRBDs and binding affinity to dsRNAs (15). Furthermore, in proteins containing multiple dsRBDs, individual dsRBD-Is often display distinct affinities to dsRNAs (24). Multiple dsRBDs may act cooperatively to enhance overall binding (25). Despite extensive biochemical research, how the variation in the number of dsRBD-I units contributes to the primary cellular function of the protein remains uncertain, underscoring the need for further studies into their structural and functional diversity.

ADAR1 and RNA editing

ADAR1 and ADAR2 are highly conserved enzymes that catalyze the chemical conversion of A-to-I on dsRNAs through the deaminase reaction. Both enzymes exhibit a common domain architecture, with dsRBDs at the N-terminus and a catalytic deaminase domain (CDD) at the C-terminus (Fig. 2) (26). ADAR1 includes Z-DNA/RNA-binding domains and three dsRBD-Is, whereas ADAR2 possesses two dsRBD-Is and lacks the Z-DNA/RNA-binding domain. A-to-I RNA editing leads to two functional outcomes: it causes recognition of inosine as guanosine and disrupts dsRNA secondary structure by convertting the A-U pair to G-U wobble (27, 28). Thus, ADARs can modify the coding potential of mRNAs (when the editing occurs in the coding region) (29, 30), induce alternative splicing (editing in introns) (31, 32), influence miRNA targeting (editing in the 3’ UTR) (33, 34), and modulate the immunogenicity of dsRNAs (through disruption of dsRNA secondary structure) (35-37).

ADARs selectively deaminate specific adenosines in the vicinity of the double-stranded regions where the protein binds, leaving most adenosines unaltered (38). This site-selective editing is highly accurate, consistently targeting the same adenosine within a stretch of adenosines. Both ADAR1 and ADAR2 induce symmetric, strand-specific editing and primarily act on imperfectly duplexed RNAs. At the same time, the RNA substrate specificities of the two proteins only partially overlap (39-41). While the factors that determine ADAR binding selectivity are not well understood, their editing activity is influenced by the sequence, structure, and length of the duplex RNA. Structural features, such as bulges, mismatches, or loops, facilitate targeting and efficiency of ADAR editing (42). Specifically, both ADAR1 and ADAR2 exhibit a preference for the 5’ nearest-neighbor sequence U > A > C > G, whereas their 3’ nearest-neighbor preferences slightly differ: ADAR1 prefers G > C ≈ A > U, while ADAR2 shows a preference for G > C > U ≈ A. The 5’ nearest neighbor exerts the greatest influence on editing efficiency, largely governed by the catalytic domain (43). Additionally, ADARs show a preference for A:C mismatches at the editing site over A:A, A:G, or A:U pairs (44). They also have distinct offsets relative to structural disruptions: -26 nucleotides for ADAR2 and -35 nucleotides for ADAR1 (41).

Recent studies have investigated the molecular basis of the distinct substrate specificities of two ADARs. By comparing full-length ADAR proteins with their isolated catalytic domains, researchers demonstrated that neighbor preferences are predominantly determined by the catalytic domain. However, analysis of full-length ADAR2 versus its catalytic domain alone revealed that the dsRNA-binding motifs of ADAR2 specifically influence discrimination of adenosines with a 3’ G. (43). X-ray crystallography studies suggest that the RNA-binding loop contributes to the differences in substrate selectivity between ADAR1 and ADAR2, as the loop contacts the minor groove near the editing site and extends into the adjacent major groove (45). This loop sequence is conserved among ADAR2 enzymes but varies within the ADAR1 family, contributing to the variation in editing-site selectivity between ADAR1 and ADAR2 (45). Additionally, the zinc-binding site present on the surface of ADAR1, absent in ADAR2, may affect substrate specificity (46). The different dsRBDs of ADARs also contribute to substrate specificity. A solution study of ADAR2 dsRBDs revealed that they form base-specific interactions within the minor groove of the RNA, which are crucial for binding and subsequent editing (16). Additionally, experiments involving dsRBD variants showed that the distinct offsets between ADAR1 and ADAR2 are not determined by the number of dsRBDs, but rather are inherent characteristics embedded within individual dsRBD (41).

RIG-I-like receptors and the innate immune response

Melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), and laboratory of genetics and physiology 2 (LGP2) are members of the RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) family of proteins. These intracellular PRRs are essential for detecting viral dsRNAs and initiating the innate immune response (47-49). The three proteins share a distinctive set of dsRBDs and an ATPase core (Fig. 2) (50). Additionally, RIG-I and MDA5 possess two amino-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domains (CARDs), which are vital for initiating the downstream signaling cascade upon binding to dsRNAs. Specifically, a CARD from either RIG-I or MDA5 interacts with a CARD from the mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS), leading to MAVS oligomerization. This oligomerization activates transcription factors, such as nuclear factor-kB, interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), and IRF1, crucial for the induction of type I IFNs and inflammatory cytokines (51, 52). In contrast, LGP2 lacks the signaling domain necessary for immune signaling. Instead, it acts as a supportive protein, enhancing the signaling activities of RIG-I and MDA5 rather than independently driving the antiviral signaling (48).

Although RLRs are involved in sensing exogenous dsRNAs, they do not contain canonical dsRBDs and exhibit specific RNA preferences. RIG-I recognizes short, blunt-ended dsRNAs (<500 bp) with 5’-ppp or 5’-diphosphate (5’-pp) (53, 54), whereas MDA5 responds to long dsRNAs of ≥ 500 bp (55). Although RIG-I can interact with dsRNAs of any length, it fails to induce antiviral responses when bound to long dsRNAs, especially the high molecular weight polyinosinic:polycitidylic acid (poly(I:C) (56). Similarly, LGP2 prefers blunt-ended dsRNAs over those with internal double-stranded regions or RNAs with overhangs. In addition, the presence of a 5’-ppp, Cap0, or Cap1 does not affect LGP2’s binding affinity (57). When bound to dsRNAs, RIG-I and MDA5 create filamentous structures via their CARD domains as a part of their activation process, though there exist significant differences in the formation and functioning of these filaments. RIG-I forms ATP-dependent filaments that are shorter and less stable than those of MDA5. Recent investigations have shown that neither cooperative binding to RNAs nor oligomerization on RNAs is necessary for RIG-I activation (58, 59). The dsRNA binding footprint of RIG-I spans between 8 and 10 bp (60, 61). In its inactive state, RIG-I adopts a closed conformation, with its CARDs hidden (62). RIG-I’s CTD binds tightly to 5’-ppp or 5’-pp, while the helicase domains encircle the dsRNA, causing the CARDs to be released from their auto-repressed state (53, 60, 61, 63). MDA5 forms cooperative filaments along the dsRNA, with each MDA5 molecule covering 14 or 15 bp, depending on the helical twist (64). MDA5 recognizes the internal duplex structure of dsRNA and surrounds it with a closed ring through interactions with the CTD and helicase domain (65). Unlike RIG-I, the CARDs of MDA5 are always exposed, regardless of the ligand (66). The proximity of CARDs during MDA5 filament formation facilitates their oligomerization, forming a scaffold that supports the binding and oligomerization of MAVS CARDs. When bound to the dsRNA, MDA5 molecules form filaments in a head-to-tail arrangement along the RNA strand (65). Finally, LGP2 demonstrates low selectivity for nucleic acids and has an RNA footprint of approximately 9-10 bp (67). It favors blunt-ended regions over internal RNAs. LGP2 can bind to dsRNA as either a monomer or dimer but does not engage in higher-order oligomerization. Although LGP2 does not oligomerize independently, it aids the nucleation of MDA5 filament assembly at internal dsRNA binding sites. A recent single-molecule study revealed that LGP2 detects the movement of MDA5 and inhibits its motor activity. This inhibition facilitates the assembly of MDA5 CARDs into oligomers that are capable of interacting with downstream signaling components (57, 68).

Although RLRs have evolved to sense RNAs originating from viruses, these proteins can also recognize endogenous dsRNAs, especially under stress conditions. Endogenous dsRNAs can form from inverted repeat sequences, such as Alus, mitochondrial dsRNAs (mt-dsRNAs), and retrotransposons (69). These repetitive elements can pair to form dsRNA structures, which are potential ligands for RLRs if not properly modified (69, 70). Notably, the inappropriate activation of RLRs can lead to excessive immune activation and contribute to immune-related diseases such as Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome (71). Cells prevent misactivation of RLRs through multiple molecular mechanisms, including A-to-I editing by ADAR (Fig. 3A). By altering the A-U pair to a G-U wobble, the dsRNA structure is disrupted, which diminishes recognition by RLRs (28, 72). Furthermore, ADAR can compete with RLRs and other dsRBPs for binding to similar pool of dsRNAs (Fig. 3B). As a result, ADAR1 deficiency leads to overactivation of MDA5 and ensuing abnormal immune responses. Remarkably, the MDA5 G495R mutant is capable of forming filaments and initiating IRF3 dimerization in response to both A-to-I edited and unmodified inverted Alus (IRAlus). However, wild-type (WT) MDA5 exhibits lower filament formation on IRAlus, and the filaments produced are significantly shorter than those by the G495R mutant. Additionally, RNase A protection and RNA-seq analysis of cytosolic RNAs from ADAR1-knockout (KO) cells show a dramatic increase in Alu enrichment, from 3% to 63% (73). Genome mapping further demonstrated that this enrichment was specifically confined to IRAlus and occurred in the same genes as those identified in cells expressing the G495R mutant in ADAR1 WT cells (73). These studies collectively suggest that RLRs can detect endogenous dsRNAs, particularly IRAlus, and may trigger aberrant immune responses and contribute to immune-related diseases when defense mechanisms are compromised.

In addition to the structural differences among RLRs, ATP hydrolysis plays a pivotal role in RIG-I and MDA5’s capacity to differentiate viral RNAs from endogenous RNAs. While the exact mechanism underlying RNA discrimination remains elusive, several studies have indicated that ATP-based translocation serves as an essential proofreading mechanism for RLRs (57, 74-76). RIG-I translocates directionally from the end of the dsRNA into the stem region, and the 5’-ppp end regulates the translocation. In comparison, MDA5 preferentially attaches to the dsRNA at a recessed 3’-end and moves directionally toward the 5’-end, continuing until it encounters an impediment, such as another bound protein (like LGP2) or the RNA’s terminus (77). During this translocation, ATP hydrolysis triggers conformational changes in MDA5 and RIG-I, facilitating proofreading that supports protein dissociation from sub-optimal RNA ligands, such as endogenous dsRNAs (64, 78). Notably, the MDA5 filament demonstrates ATP hydrolysis-dependent protein displacement activity. ATP hydrolysis propels the expansion of MDA5’s RNA binding footprint from 14 to 15 bp, enhancing the protein’s ability to eject other proteins, including viral proteins or other RBPs, along its path. Filaments comprising 18 or more subunits are necessary to achieve maximal protein displacement activity, which is pivotal for antiviral immunity by clearing self-dsRNAs to enable accurate recognition and signaling activation (68). LGP2’s ATPase activity, which hydrolyzes ATP only in the presence of RNA, is equally stimulated by various dsRNAs irrespective of RNA end modifications. Unlike RIG-I and MDA5, LGP2’s translocation is noncooperative (57). Notably, a recent study suggested that LGP2 was unable to displace streptavidin from the dsRNA with internal biotin modifications (68). However, another study contradicted this by demonstrating that LGP2 effectively displaces biotin-streptavidin interactions using its ATPase activity (59).

Protein kinase R

Protein kinase R (PKR) is a critical protein in the innate immune response, activating antiviral signaling upon detection of viral dsRNAs. While RLRs predominantly initiate type I IFN and inflammatory responses, PKR phosphorylates the alpha subunit of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 (eIF2α), which results in the suppression of global translation and initiation of caspase-dependent apoptosis (79). Moreover, the role of PKR extends beyond antiviral activities; overactivation or dysfunction of PKR has been associated with the pathogenesis of cancer, degenerative diseases, and inflammatory conditions (80, 81).

The human PKR comprises three functional domains: two dsRBD-Is at the N-terminal region and a kinase domain at the C-terminal region (Fig. 2). Two dsRBDs are separated by 23 amino acid linkers, facilitating PKR dimerization upon dsRNA binding (82, 83). Both dsRBDs are essential for high-affinity interaction with dsRNAs (84). Upon binding to long dsRNAs (>33 bp, with peak activity at approximately 79 bp), PKR undergoes dimerization and autophosphorylation, subsequently phosphorylating eIF2α (85). Moreover, PKR can interact with short dsRNAs that include stretches of ssRNA, with autoactivation enhanced when RNA contains a 5’-ppp end (86). In contrast to RLRs, activation of PKR only requires dimerization, thus allowing more flexibility regarding the length of dsRNAs. Consequently, PKR can be activated by a variety of endogenous dsRNAs, such as IRAlus, long interspersed nuclear elements, endogenous retroviral elements, and mt-dsRNAs (87). Similar to MDA5, the activation of PKR by dsRNAs is weakened by A-to-I editing by ADAR (88). However, ADAR-dited RNAs can still bind to PKR and activate the kinase (7). Notably, PKR can bind to endogenous dsRNAs despite the presence of multiple bulges and internal loops (7).

The requirement for dimerization in PKR activation can be exploited to create RNA inhibitors for PKR. When short dsRNAs ranging from 16 to 33 bp are present, PKR can recognize the RNA, but it is too short to facilitate binding of two PKR molecules. Consequently, short dsRNAs trap PKR in a monomeric form, thereby inhibiting PKR dimerization and subsequent activation (89). A well-known endogenous short RNA inhibitor of PKR is noncoding RNA 886 (nc886), which binds strongly to PKR and inhibits its activity (90). However, the precise molecular mechanism by which nc886 inhibits PKR activation remains unclear, as nc886 does not form a definitive short hairpin RNA structure. Recent studies have revealed that circular RNAs (circRNAs) with short dsRNA segments can suppress PKR activation 103- to 106-fold more effectively than small chemical inhibitors, such as compound 16 and 2-aminopurine (91). A subsequent single-molecule study demonstrated that these circRNAs bind to PKR for significantly longer durations compared to perfect dsRNAs or hairpin-structured linear RNAs, providing insight into the molecular mechanism through which these RNAs function as potent PKR inhibitors (92). Additionally, the presence of bulges within the dsRNA progressively reduces PKR activation as these bulges move toward the center of the duplex or increase in size (93). Notably, while the presence of one or more bulges reduces PKR activation compared to perfect dsRNA, trans-bulged RNAs activate PKR more effectively than cis-bulged RNAs (93).

The activation status of PKR can be regulated even in the absence of dsRNAs by dsRBPs equipped with a dsRBD-II. In this scenario, the dsRBD-II attaches to the N-terminal region of PKR, disrupting its closed conformation. Two prominent regulators of PKR are the transactivation response RNA binding protein (TRBP) and the protein activator of PKR (PACT); both contain a single dsRBD-II in their C-termini. Notably, TRBP serves as an inhibitor of PKR, while PACT activates PKR under stress conditions (Fig. 3C) (94-96). Nonetheless, for both proteins, the precise regulatory mechanism on PKR activation remains obscure, as they do not serve as competitive inhibitors of PKR. Moreover, recent studies have challenged the traditional belief that PACT activates PKR, positing instead that PACT inhibits PKR (97, 98). Employing a single-molecule approach, a study proposed a novel mechanism where PACT binds to the same RNA substrate as PKR, but rather than acting as a competitive inhibitor, it impedes PKR’s movement along the RNA and blocks PKR dimerization (98).

TRBP and PACT

TRBP and PACT, as dsRBPs, play roles in antiviral response and miRNA biogenesis through their interactions with PKR and Dicer, respectively (99-102). The two proteins share substantial homology, each featuring three dsRBDs, each connected by unstructured linker regions (12, 103). Domains 1 and 2 are dsRBD-I, whereas the C-terminal domain 3 is dsRBD-II (Fig. 2). The dsRBD-II mediates protein-protein interaction with PKR and Dicer (99, 101). Despite their structural similarities and common interacting partners, TRBP and PACT demonstrate significant mechanical and functional differences. TRBP displays ATP-independent sliding or diffusion activity along the dsRNA, facilitated by the independent movement of its two dsRBDs, with the diffusion distance being determined by the length of the linker connecting them (15, 104). Although PACT’s domain composition closely mirrors that of TRBP, its propensity to slide on the dsRNA is markedly reduced, likely due to the shorter linkers connecting its two dsRBDs (15).

In addition to exhibiting different movements along the RNA, the third dsRBD distinguishes the homodimerization tendencies of the two proteins. TRBP binds to dsRNAs, such as siRNAs, with higher affinity than itself via homodimerization, while PACT demonstrates lower affinity for siRNAs compared to its own homodimerization (105). This difference stems from the weaker dimer formation by TRBP’s third dsRBD (TRBP-D3) compared to that of PACT. Interestingly, substituting two amino acids in TRBP-D3 can alter its homodimerization tendency, making it more similar to that of PACT (24). Additionally, TRBP and PACT form stable heterodimers via dsRBD-1 and dsRBD-2, which blocks PACT’s interaction with PKR, preventing PKR activation by PACT (94, 106).

As outlined, the primary protein interactors of TRBP and PACT are PKR and Dicer. In the case of PKR, TRBP and PACT’s ability to regulate PKR activity is largely dependent on stress conditions and cellular contexts. Stress-induced phosphorylation of TRBP and PACT modifies the affinities within the PKR-PACT, PACT-TRBP, and PACT-PACT complexes, allowing for finely controlled, transient activation of PKR with precise temporal regulation (94-96). Oxidative stress induces TRBP phosphorylation, decreasing TRBP-TRBP interactions and allowing free TRBPs to inhibit PKR (107). Moreover, phosphorylation of TRBP during cell division increases its potency as a PKR inhibitor by enhancing its binding affinity to the kinase (108). Conversely, phosphorylation at serine 287 markedly boosts PACT’s capability to activate PKR by reducing its interaction with TRBP (94). The interaction between TRBP/PACT and Dicer impacts miRNA biogenesis by influencing Dicer’s slicing activity (109, 110). PACT and TRBP partake in processing pre-miRNAs and in RISC loading. They bind to Dicer via their C-terminal dsRBDs and exert varying influences on Dicer-mediated processing. Notably, TRBP boosts Dicer’s stability and RNA-binding affinity, promoting efficient pre-miRNA cleavage in RNA-rich environments (110).

The Dicer-TRBP complex displays two distinct RNA recognition modes. One set of interactions is transient, while the other maintains stable binding to the complex (110). Although Dicer alone establishes this dual mode of binding, TRBP modulates the energy landscape of the Dicer-RNA interactions, enhancing substrate recognition efficiency. In the absence of TRBP, Dicer’s cleavage activity is markedly reduced by competing RNAs. Conversely, when TRBP is present, Dicer is impervious to the inhibitory effects of surplus tRNA. This implies that TRBP acts as a gatekeeper, preventing Dicer from engaging with non-specific cellular RNAs and ensuring its specificity for precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) (110). In contrast, PACT appears to stabilize the Dicer protein itself, rather than its interaction with pre-miRNAs, with Dicer levels slightly decreasing upon PACT depletion (111). Additionally, PACT enhances the specificity of substrate recognition. The Dicer–PACT and Dicer–Ago2–PACT complexes show a preference for pre-miRNA over pre-siRNA processing (109). Although both Dicer–Ago2–PACT and Dicer–Ago2–TRBP process pre-let-7a and pre-miR-34c with comparable efficiency, Dicer–Ago2–PACT is less efficient at processing pre-siRNAs compared to the Dicer–Ago2–TRBP complex (109). Yet, the regulatory effects of TRBP and PACT on pre-miRNA processing appear to be context-dependent, as neither TRBP nor PACT KO altered global miRNA expression patterns in HeLa cells (108).

Staufen

Staufen (STAU) is a key protein involved in the transport, localization, and decay of mRNAs within cells (112, 113). In humans, two homologs, STAU1 and STAU2, exist. Although their sequences and dsRBDs share some similarities, the mRNA substrates of the two proteins overlap by only about 30% (114). Moreover, STAU1 is widely expressed across different cell types while STAU2 is predominantly found in the brain and heart (115). Both STAU proteins consist of four tandem pairs of dsRBDs (dsRBD2-5 for STAU1 and dsRBD1-4 for STAU2) and a microtubule-binding domain (TBD) (Fig. 2). In addition, STAU1 contains a STAU-swapping motif (SSM) for dimerization while STAU2 has a similar motif in its C-terminal domain (Fig. 2). STAU1 consists of two dsRBD-Is (dsRBD3 and 4) and two dsRBD-IIs (dsRBD2 and 5) while all dsRBDs of STAU2 are type I (116). The dsRBD3 and dsRBD4 primarily mediate RNA binding activity in STAU1. These two domains bind adjacent regions of the target RNA and work together to achieve high binding affinity. The tandem arrangement is essential for strong and specific RNA binding, as each domain alone binds with a much lower affinity (117). Similarly, mouse Stau2 (with greater than 90% sequence similarity to human STAU2) contains two tandem dsRBD pairs that function as integrated tandem domains, with each pair capable of binding RNA independently but transiently. Stable, high-affinity RNA binding is achieved only when both tandem domains engage on RNA binding, allowing the protein to recognize combinations of RNA secondary structures (118).

Human STAU1 and STAU2 both form homodimers and heterodimers through the SSM, located near the dsRBD5. The SSM of one STAU1 molecule interacts with the α-helices of dsRBD5 from another STAU1 molecule, creating a domain-swapped dimer (119). This interaction is primarily hydrophobic and results in a stable dimer formation both in vitro and in cells. STAU2, like STAU1, contains a similar motif and a degenerate dsRBD5 that can dimerize with itself or with STAU1 (119). Dimerization of STAU1 and STAU2 is critical for the assembly of STAU-mediated mRNA decay (SMD) complexes. Both STAU1 and STAU2 homodimers increase the efficiency and stability of UPF1 recruitment to the STAU-bound mRNA (119). Enhanced helicase activity of UPF1 then remodels the mRNP complex and recruits mRNA decay machinery (120). To induce SMD, STAU1 or STAU2 have to bind to a STAU-binding site (SBS) within the 3’ UTR of target mRNAs that exhibit double-stranded secondary structure (113, 116). In addition to mediating SMD, STAU binding can facilitate mRNA transport within cells. The TBD of STAU interacts with tubulin and microtubules, enabling mRNA movement via motor proteins (121). Moreover, STAU is recruited into stress granules (SGs) during cellular stress, sequestering the associated RNAs into these structures (122). Lastly, STAU can bind to the 5’ UTR of mRNAs via 5’ UTR SBS in target mRNAs, which results in enhanced translation as STAU1 interacts with actively translating ribosomes (116).

STAU1 and STAU2 recognize dsRNAs as short as 8 bp, but a length of ≥12 bp is required for significant binding (123, 124). Genome-wide analysis of STAU-associated mRNAs revealed that targets of STAU1 shared longer 3’ UTR and stable secondary structures (3-4× longer than non-targets) (125). In addition, the 3’ UTRs of STAU targets are markedly enriched for three distinct types of secondary structures (125). STAU1 can also recognize specific sequences on target RNAs as revealed by high-resolution NMR and crystal structures. The dsRBD4 uses helix α1 to interact with two pyrimidines and one purine from the minor groove side. The β1-β2 loop anchors the dsRBD4 at the end of the dsRNA, while lysines in helix α2 contact the phosphodiester backbone from the major groove. The dsRBD3 employs a similar mode of interaction, making specific contact with a guanine base in the minor groove via helix α1 (124).

DExH-box helicase 9

DExH-box helicase 9 (DHX9), also referred to as RNA helicase A, is a versatile dsRBP crucial to nucleic acid metabolism and circRNA biogenesis. DHX9 functions as an ATP-dependent RNA helicase, capable of unwinding RNA or DNA duplexes, including DNA:RNA hybrids (R-loops) (126, 127). DHX9 is comprised of multiple functional domains, as shown in Fig. 2. Like its counterparts in the DHX family, the helicase core domain of DHX9 consists of two RecA-like domains. Additionally, DHX9 possesses two dsRBDs at its N-terminus, an RNA polymerase II-interacting minimal transactivation domain (MTAD) between dsRBD II and motif I of the helicase core domain, and a helicase-associated domain 2 (HA2) proximal to the C-terminal end of the helicase core domain (128, 129). At the C-terminus, DHX9 features an oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide-binding fold (OB-fold) and a glycine-rich RGG-box, facilitating interaction with single-stranded nucleic acids (130).

DHX9 is characterized as a helicase that prefers unwinding RNA-containing substrates, demonstrating structure-specific activity. Furthermore, it necessitates a 3’-single-stranded overhang and ATP hydrolysis for activation (131). While the helicase can bind to both DNAs and RNAs, it exhibits a higher affinity for structured RNAs (132). It also shows a preference for more intricate, multi-stranded nucleic acid structures, such as R-loops and G-quadruplexes (126). Notably, DHX9 displays the highest unwinding efficiency for RNA G-quadruplexes, followed by R-loops, DNA G-quadruplexes, D-loops, RNA forks, and DNA forks in decreasing order of effectiveness (126, 133).

One distinctive attribute of DHX9 is its involvement in SGs initiated by dsRNAs. These SGs are molecular condensates that arise in response to various stressors, including viral dsRNAs, and are increasingly associated with the RLR signaling activation centers (134). Both canonical and DHX9-specific SGs require Ran GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1) and G3BP2 for phase separation. Unlike classical SGs, which typically consist of mature mRNAs, DHX9-associated SGs are enriched with damaged intron RNAs (135). Additionally, the molecular mechanisms of DHX9 SGs differ from those of classical SGs: DHX9 SGs actively initiate translation shutdown, while classical SGs form as a result of translation shutdown (135, 136). Translation arrest induced by arsenite or poly(I:C) treatment still occurred in G3BP1 KO and auxin-inducible degron-tagged G3BP2 (G3K) cells (135). Interestingly, while 4sU ＋ UVA treatment induced eIF2α phosphorylation in WT cells containing DHX9 SGs, this phosphorylation was absent in G3K cells (135). Additionally, SGs in DHX9-depleted cells exhibited higher dsRNA levels compared to controls (135). Thus, DHX9 plays a crucial role in helping cells regulate dsRNA levels within SGs and shielding them from detrimental dsRNA-induced immune responses (135).

In addition to forming SGs, DHX9 can suppress multiple immune pathways. It interacts directly with the ADAR1-p110 isoform in an RNA-independent manner. Interestingly, the interaction between DHX9 and the ADAR1-p150 isoform is RNA-dependent. The simultaneous knockdown of DHX9 and ADAR1 leads to a robust activation of innate immunity pathways, including PKR, MDA5, and OAS-RNase L, underscoring their collaborative role in suppressing viral mimicry responses (137). Furthermore, DHX9 is involved in the formation of cytosolic dsRNA-induced foci (dRIFs) alongside other dsRBPs, such as ADAR1 and PACT (Fig. 3D) (138). These dRIFs assemble in response to elevated levels of endogenous dsRNAs due to ADAR1 deficiency or the introduction of exogenous dsRNAs (138). Distinctly separate from SGs, P-bodies, or RL-dependent bodies, dRIFs do not typically overlap with these structures and act as activation centers for PKR and other dsRNA sensors (138, 139).

DHX9 negatively regulates circRNA formation by binding to inverted complementary sequences, such as IRAlus, which are located in the intronic regions adjacent to exons. In both humans and mice, DHX9 primarily interacts with intronic RNAs. Detailed analysis shows that DHX9-binding sites are highly concentrated on Alu elements: approximately 60% of DHX9 peaks occur on or within 100 nucleotides of Alu repeats, in stark contrast to only 3-10% for control RBPs (140). Additionally, DHX9 preferentially targets Alu elements that have complementary binding partners nearby, demonstrating the protein’s specificity for base-paired IRAlus elements (140). The presence of Alu repeats and complementary sequences around exons is closely associated with circRNA formation. By unwinding these sequences, DHX9 disrupts the base-pairing required to bring splice sites into close proximity, thus inhibiting back-splicing and reducing the formation of circRNAs (140).

Nuclear Factor 90

Nuclear Factor 90 (NF90) is a multifunctional DNA and RNA binding protein known for its roles in gene expression regulation, splicing, mRNA stability, and cellular response to various stimuli (141). Recent research has also connected NF90 to the biogenesis of miRNAs and the maintenance of circRNA stability (142). NF90 contains domains associated with zinc fingers (DZF), nuclear localization signal (NLS), two dsRBD-Is, and C-terminal low-complexity regions (Fig. 2) (143). Interestingly, the tandem dsRBDs of NF90 closely resemble those of ADAR2 both in structure and in their mode of RNA recognition (144). This structural similarity indicates that, like ADAR2, NF90 can recognize specific sequence motifs within dsRNAs. Indeed, NF90 recognizes G-Xn-A motif like ADAR2, albeit n=8 or 9 for ADAR2 while n=10 for NF90 (144).

NF90 has an isoform, NF110, which shows a stronger association with chromatin, but has weaker dsRNA binding. NF90 is more prominent in mRNA export and stabilization (145). Both proteins share N-terminal sequences and dsRBDs, but differ at the C-terminus (143). NF90 and NF110 are unstable in their monomeric forms and generate stable heterodimeric complexes with NF45 via the DZF domain (146). A recent study revealed that NF45−NF90 complexes undergo significant conformational changes upon binding to a dsRNA. Specifically, the dsRBDs and DZF domains rearrange, and new interactions form between the domains when bound to the RNA (147). This is consistent with a previous study showing that NF45-NF90 complex binds a dsRNA with 10-fold higher affinity than NF90 alone, enabling oligomerization on long dsRNA substrates (148).

NF90 can interact directly with PKR via its C-terminal region, which binds to the C-terminal region of PKR (149). NF90 can also bind PKR via dsRBD interaction in an RNA-dependent manner (148, 149). NF90 regulates PKR activation and phosphorylation in response to dsRNA stimulation. Interestingly, NF90 promotes PKR phosphorylation at a physiological concentration while high concentrations of NF90 inhibit PKR via competition (149). Additionally, NF90 is a substrate for PKR-mediated phosphorylation, which may influence NF90’s subcellular localization and function (148). Under normal conditions, NF90 is mainly found in the nucleus, but phosphorylation by several kinases triggers its relocation to the cytoplasm (150). This translocation enables NF90 to function in mRNA stabilization, cell cycle regulation, and antiviral responses (149-151).

NF90 plays critical roles in post-transcriptional gene regulation through RNA binding. For binding, NF90 requires 18 bp as a minimal length, and pri-miRNAs are well-known RNA substrates of NF90 (144). NF90 binds pri-miRNAs at stem-loop structures, competing with the Drosha/DGCR8 microprocessor complex to reduce the mature miRNA output (152). Additionally, genome-wide screening revealed that NF90-bound and modulated pri-miRNAs tend to have highly stable RNA structures, characterized by longer duplex regions and fewer or smaller bulges (153). Moreover, NF90 promotes the formation of circRNAs by binding to complementary intronic RNA sequences flanking the circularizing exons to facilitate the back-splicing event (149). Furthermore, NF90 binds structured RNA regions, particularly the AU-rich elements in the 3’ UTR of mRNAs, which allows NF90 to regulate mRNA stability and translation (154). Specifically, NF90 stabilizes the target mRNA by competing with decay machineries (151, 155) and promotes the formation of a looped mRNA structure by binding to both the 5’ and 3’ ends to enhance the efficiency of translation initiation (156). Notably, NF90 can destabilize viral mRNAs by promoting the formation of SGs to sequester viral RNAs and translation machinery, thereby inhibiting viral mRNA translation and promoting their degradation (157). Moreover, in certain circumstances, NF90 can inhibit translation either by physically blocking the recruitment or progression of ribosomes or by recruiting the RISC complex to mediate mRNA degradation (158, 159).

SON DNA and RNA binding protein

SON DNA and RNA binding protein (commonly known as SON) functions as both a DNA- and RNA-binding protein and an mRNA splicing cofactor that regulates cell cycle progression (160). SON features several structural components related to RNA processing, including a large arginine/serine-rich (RS) domain, a glycine-rich motif (G-patch), and a dsRNA binding motif (Fig. 2) (161). Notably, the SON protein contains various unique repetitive sequence motifs, extending nearly 1,200 amino acids, which constitute about one-third of the protein. These motifs are characteristic of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), which facilitate liquid-liquid phase separation essential for the formation and maintenance of membraneless organelles (162). The IDRs of SON, together with Serine/Arginine repetitive matrix 2 (SRRM2), assemble multiple RNA and protein components of nuclear speckles, membraneless nuclear bodies enriched with splicing factors, and serve as scaffolding proteins (163). SON plays a key role in regulating the localization and availability of splicing factors within nuclear speckles, whereas SRRM2 provides structural support to stabilize these factors (163).

SON is also capable of binding to DNAs that are rich in repetitive G or C tracts and GC dinucleotide repeats near transcription start sites (TSSs) (164). Moreover, it interacts with chromatin markers at TSSs, such as H3K4me3, that are linked to transcriptionally active promoters (164). This dual DNA- and RNA-binding capability allows SON to facilitate the recruitment of splicing factors to transcription sites, effectively linking transcription initiation with co-transcriptional RNA processing. Additionally, SON’s repetitive RS domain shares similarities with the carboxy-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II, which coordinates RNA processing events, including splicing (165).

Through these mechanisms, SON regulates the splicing of genes involved in cell cycle progression, DNA repair, centrosome maintenance, and pluripotency, such as CEP131 and KATNB1 (166). Furthermore, SON enhances the splicing of transcripts with weak or dual-specificity splice sites by attracting essential splicing factors, like SC35, U1-70K, and U2AF65. This recruitment ensures precise exon inclusion or skipping during alternative splicing events (167). Consequently, dysregulation of SON results in widespread splicing defects, with exon skipping being the most prevalent issue (168). Consequently, SON has been associated with cancer, neurodevelopmental disorders, infectious diseases, and autophagy-related conditions (169-172). For instance, SON is highly expressed in ErbB2-positive breast cancer patient samples, and its expression is inversely correlated with patient survival (172). The knockdown of SON resulted in intron retention of ErB2 and ErB3 transcripts, reducing the expression of the encoded proteins (172).

Although SON was discovered approximately 40 years ago, the mechanisms underlying its RNA-binding activity remain largely unexplored. SON preferentially binds to and enhances the splicing of transcripts characterized by weak splice sites, where inefficient or unstable spliceosome formation occurs (167). This implies that SON might exhibit specific affinity toward RNA sequences containing suboptimal splice sites (167). Given the presence of structured RNA elements, such as inverted Alu repeats near splice sites, SON’s dsRNA-binding capability could be important in recognizing these weak splice sites. Currently, the specific dsRNA substrates of SON have yet to be identified. Unraveling these substrates may provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning SON-mediated splicing regulation.

DISCUSSION

The sheer diversity of cellular sources of dsRNAs indicates a variety of complex regulatory mechanisms that ensure balanced cellular responses to these endogenous molecular cues for antiviral signaling. Considering the dynamic nature of dsRNA-RBP interactions, different dsRBPs may engage with a similar pool of cellular dsRNAs and influence the activation state of one another (173). However, most research still concentrates on individual proteins, seeking to decipher the molecular nuances of RNA substrate recognition and cellular ramifications following binding to target dsRNA. The specificities and binding modes of various dsRBPs are detailed in Table 2. Notably, dsRBPs demonstrate similar, yet distinct, substrate specificities, potentially playing a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis with respect to endogenous dsRNAs.

Since the determination of the first structure of a dsRBD by NMR, our understanding of the structural and biochemical aspects of dsRBPs and their signaling cascades has advanced considerably. Emerging experimental techniques, such as single-molecule biophysical methods, have clarified the unique binding profiles of dsRBPs, providing a foundation for predicting optimal RNA binding sites for specific dsRBDs. Despite numerous breakthroughs in recent decades, certain aspects still require clarification. For example, the impact of RNA mismatches and internal loops on RNA recognition by dsRBDs, and ultimately by dsRBPs requires further investigation. A recent study has identified several putative dsRBPs; the knockout of these proteins impacted the IFN response to poly(I:C) and viral infections (174). However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which these newly identified dsRBPs interact with cellular dsRNAs await clarification.

One of the reasons for the rising interest in cellular dsRNAs is their close association with the misactivation of dsRBPs and human disease in the absence of pathogenic triggers (69). More importantly, downregulating cellular dsRNAs has shown therapeutic potential, especially for osteoarthritis and Sjögren’s disease (81, 175). Concurrently, activating dsRNA sensors and triggering acute inflammation can elicit an anticancer response (176, 177). Indeed, the therapeutic efficacy of epigenetic chemotherapy heavily relies on the extent of dsRNA sensor activation (178, 179). This innate immune response works synergistically with other immunotherapeutic methods, thereby enhancing treatment outcomes (180). Consequently, dsRNA sensor antagonists and oncolytic viruses have become promising tools in cancer therapy, greatly benefiting from a deeper understanding of dsRNA recognition by dsRBPs within cells.

In conclusion, research into dsRNA-dsRBP interactions will illuminate the biomedical possibilities of this RNA-mediated immune signaling mechanism. As our knowledge of dsRBPs and their specific modes of dsRNA recognition expands, it will offer new insights into disease pathology and foster the development of novel therapeutic approaches.
