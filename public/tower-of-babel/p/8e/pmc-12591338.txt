Reconsidering ANH in Cardiac Surgery: Time for Broader Evaluation
Authors: Muhammad Adnan, Sohaib Raza, Zernain Toor, Shah R. Rehman
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We have read with great interest the recent article by Sebt et al.,[1] titled “Acute Normovolemic Hemodilution Significantly Reduces RBC Transfusion and Lactic Acidosis Following Cardiac Surgery—A Propensity-Matched Study.” It presents timely and valuable findings regarding the effectiveness of acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH) in cardiac surgery. Their demonstration of reduced red blood cell (RBC) transfusion and a significant 53% reduction in lactic acidosis is clinically relevant. We write to commend the authors for their contribution and offer some additional perspectives.

While their results support the use of mild-volume ANH as a blood conservation strategy, I propose that we now broaden the research agenda to explore long-term clinical outcomes and functional recovery. Existing literature, including this study, has largely focused on perioperative and short-term markers, such as transfusion rates, ICU stay, and lactic acidosis. However, postoperative anemia and inflammatory responses have implications for the standard of living.[2] Therefore, newer prospective studies could investigate whether ANH contributes to better neural outcomes, physical endurance, or 30- to 90-day readmission rates.

Additionally, the impact of ANH on the immune system and infection rates deserves attention. Although this study did not find significant differences in postoperative infections, it is possible that ANH, by reducing transfusions, may lower the risk of transfusion-related immune system changes by lowering nosocomial infections.[34]

Biomarker-based personalization of the ANH protocol is another area of interest. The study protocol utilized a standardized withdrawal of 5–8 mL/kg of blood and crystalloids for volume replacement. However, integrating real-time hemodynamic monitoring or biomarkers, such as oxygen saturation, lactate clearance, or coagulation testing, may allow for individualized ANH strategies. This could optimize both safety and effectiveness, particularly in patients with low cardiac reserve or acute or chronic kidney injury.[5]

Lastly, the question of cost-effectiveness—which is largely unaddressed—should be explored in upcoming investigations. Newer researchers should focus on comparing ANH to standard care, helping policy makers make informed decisions, which could prove to be lifesaving in low-income countries where blood products are scarce.

In summary, this important study by Sebt et al. provides strong justification to re-examine and potentially expand the role of ANH in modern cardiac surgical practice. Future work should extend these insights into personalized care and long-term patient-centered outcomes. The economic effectiveness of ANH should also be analyzed.

Sincerely,Muhammad Adnan,Sohaib Raza,Zernain Toor,Shahrukh Rehman
