The effect of Malva sylvestris mouthwash on chemotherapy-induced stomatitis and associated pain in patients with cancer: a triple-blind randomized clinical trial
Authors: Zahra Salmaninejad, Fariba Mazhari, Seyed Mohamad Ebrahim Pourhosseini, Mahboobeh Raeiszadeh, Tabandeh Sadeghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesStomatitis is among the most debilitating complications of cancer treatment, particularly chemotherapy. The use of medicinal plants has been suggested as a potential therapeutic approach to mitigate this condition. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of Malva sylvestris (mallow) mouthwash on chemotherapy-induced stomatitis in cancer patients.Materials and methodsThis triple-blind randomized clinical trial was conducted in 2024 on 70 cancer patients with chemotherapy-induced stomatitis who attended medical centers in an urban area of Iran. Participants were randomly allocated to two groups using the minimization method. The intervention group received 15 ml of Malva sylvestris mouthwash three times daily for 14 days, while the control group received standard chlorhexidine mouthwash. The severity of stomatitis and associated pain were assessed at baseline and on days 3, 7, and 14 using the World Health Organization (WHO) Mucositis Scale and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain, respectively. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.ResultsBefore the intervention, no statistically significant differences were observed in mean stomatitis or pain scores between the two groups. By day 7, the intervention group demonstrated significantly lower mean scores for both stomatitis and pain compared with the control group (P < 0.001). Moreover, the reduction in stomatitis severity over time was greater in the intervention group than in the control group (P < 0.05). On day 14, although the intervention group maintained lower mean stomatitis scores, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.08).ConclusionThe findings suggest that Malva sylvestris mouthwash has a beneficial effect in reducing the severity of stomatitis and associated pain in patients undergoing chemotherapy. Therefore, its use may be recommended as a supportive intervention for patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals involved in cancer care.Trial registrationTrial registration number: IRCT20150713023190N15.Date of registration: 2024/07/20.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15158-w.Keywords: Cancer, Chemotherapy, Stomatitis, Pain, Medicinal plants, Mouthwash, Malva sylvestris

Background

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide [1]. The global burden of this disease is projected to increase in the coming years, largely due to demographic shifts such as population growth and population aging across various regions [2]. With the rising incidence of cancer, effective management of the disease and its associated complications has become a critical concern for both patients and healthcare providers [3]. The three most common treatment modalities for cancer are surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [4]. Despite its well-documented adverse effects, chemotherapy continues to be a widely used therapeutic option for many cancer patients [5]. While it has substantially improved survival rates, chemotherapy is also associated with numerous short- and long-term side effects that negatively affect patients’ quality of life [6].

Stomatitis is one of the most common complications of cancer treatment, particularly chemotherapy, with an incidence ranging from 40% to 100%, depending on factors such as the type of malignancy, chemotherapy regimen, specific chemotherapeutic agents, patient age, and the level of oral care [7]. It results from an inflammatory reaction of the epithelial mucosa to the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy [8]. Clinically, stomatitis presents with erythema, atrophy, or ulceration of the oral mucosa [9] and may affect multiple oral structures, including the tongue, throat, cheeks, lips, gums, and the roof or floor of the mouth [10]. Painful ulcers, often accompanied by submucosal bleeding and secondary infections, may develop and lead to significant difficulties in speaking, eating, and swallowing [11]. These complications can severely impair a patient’s quality of life, necessitate chemotherapy dose reductions, and ultimately compromise overall treatment outcomes and survival [12].

Currently, a range of strategies is employed to manage stomatitis [13]. However, many conventional treatments show limited efficacy and may be associated with additional adverse effects. Consequently, there has been growing interest in the use of herbal mouthwashes, which are generally associated with fewer side effects [14]. Herbal medicines are increasingly recognized as viable alternatives in contemporary healthcare due to their greater patient acceptability and cost-effectiveness [15].

Malva sylvestris (commonly known as common mallow or red mallow) belongs to the Malvaceae family [16] and is native to Europe, Western Asia, and North Africa [17]. Phytochemical analyses indicate that the plant’s leaves and flowers—the most commonly utilized parts—contain a wide range of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, mucilage, terpenoids, phenolic derivatives, coumarins, sterols, tannins, saponins, and alkaloids. These constituents contribute to the plant’s anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective properties [18]. Previous studies have suggested potential therapeutic benefits of Malvaceae family plants in managing oral complications. For instance, Ghorbani et al. reported that a mouthwash formulated with Althaea officinalis root extract significantly reduced chemotherapy-induced oral inflammation compared with a conventional mouthwash [14]. Similarly, Rezaeipour et al. found that a combination of Malva sylvestris and Alcea digitata Alef was effective in preventing acute radiation-induced mucositis in cancer patients [19]. However, no study to date has specifically investigated the effects of Malva sylvestris alone on chemotherapy-induced stomatitis. Given the high prevalence of stomatitis among patients receiving chemotherapy, the present study aimed to evaluate the effect of Malva sylvestris mouthwash on chemotherapy-induced stomatitis in cancer patients. We hypothesized that Malva sylvestris mouthwash would reduce the severity of chemotherapy-induced stomatitis and associated pain compared with chlorhexidine. The sub-hypotheses were that patients using Malva sylvestris mouthwash would demonstrate lower pain scores and reduced stomatitis severity on days 3, 7, and 14 compared with those using chlorhexidine.

Methods

Study design

This triple-blind randomized clinical trial—where patients, researchers administering the intervention and assessments, and data analysts were blinded—was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20150713023190N15; https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/76820, registered on July 20, 2024). The study was conducted in 2024 among cancer patients referred to medical centers affiliated with Rafsanjan University of Medical Sciences, Rafsanjan, southeast Iran.

Sampling and randomization

The sample size was calculated as n = 28.55 with STATA software, using a mean comparison formula (n1 = ), with an effect size of 0.6 for stomatitis as well as σ₁ = 0.76 and σ₂ = 0.64 according to a previous study [14], and α = 0.05, β = 0.1. The significance level was set at p< 0.05, and the study power was assumed to be 90%. Given the possibility of sample dropout (20–25%), 35 patients were allocated to each group. The results of the Kaiser-Mayer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy (KMO= 0.73) and Bartlett’s sphericity (X2= 296.11, P<0.001) tests showed that the sample size was sufficient.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: full consciousness, provision of informed consent, confirmed cancer diagnosis, absence of primary or secondary oral cancer, receipt of chemotherapy without concurrent radiotherapy, presence of chemotherapy-induced stomatitis of grades I–III, history of at least one chemotherapy session, age above 20 years, no allergy to Malvaceae plants, absence of oral mucosal lesions before chemotherapy, and not being pregnant or breastfeeding. The exclusion criteria included irregular mouthwash use (defined as missing three consecutive days during the 14-day intervention or nine doses in total), concurrent use of other mouthwashes, fever exceeding 38.5 °C, withdrawal from the study, unwillingness to continue treatment, or any condition preventing further participation, such as health deterioration or death. The participants who met the eligibility criteria were randomly allocated into two groups using the minimization method. Initially, patients were stratified according to key variables, including stomatitis severity and pain intensity. The first eligible participant was assigned to either the intervention or control group by coin toss, while subsequent participants were allocated to the group with the lower cumulative scores for stomatitis grade and pain. In cases where scores were equal, random allocation was repeated [20].

Instruments

Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire assessing variables such as age, gender, and duration of illness, along with the WHO Oral Mucositis Grading Scale and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain assessment. The WHO Oral Mucositis Grading Scale is a five-point instrument defined as follows: grade 0 indicates no mucositis; grade 1, erythema without ulcers but with pain; grade 2, the presence of ulcers and erythema while the patient remains able to consume solid food; grade 3, extensive ulcers and erythema accompanied by an inability to eat solid food; and grade 4, the most severe stage, characterized by mucosal bleeding, extensive inflammation, and the inability to eat or drink [21]. In Iran, the content validity and reliability of the Persian version of this checklist were confirmed by Ashk Torab et al., with an inter-observer reliability of 0.93 [22]. The VAS, a standardized tool for measuring pain intensity, ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10 (intolerable pain). Its validity and reliability have been confirmed in previous studies [23].

Intervention

Before study initiation, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who also received detailed instructions on the correct use of the mouthwash and the procedure for reporting potential side effects. The drug preparation is mentioned in the appendix. Each participant was provided with two bottles labeled A or B, along with a washable measuring cup. Participants in both groups were instructed to wash their hands three times daily (after breakfast, lunch, and dinner), brush their teeth with a soft toothbrush and mild toothpaste, and rinse their mouths with 15 ml of the assigned mouthwash for three minutes after each meal over a period of 14 days. They were also advised to refrain from eating or rinsing their mouths for at least one hour after mouthwash use [14, 24].

In the intervention group, participants received Malva sylvestris mouthwash in addition to the standard mouthwash, while the control group received only the standard 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash (Behsa Company, Iran), which is commonly recommended for mucositis management. The Malva sylvestris extract produced a color similar to chlorhexidine (pink-purple) and lacked a distinct taste or odor. Furthermore, the inclusion of ethanol and glycerol in the herbal formulation resulted in a taste closely resembling that of chlorhexidine mouthwash. To maintain blinding, both the herbal and control mouthwashes were dispensed in opaque bottles labeled A and B. Neither the participants, the administering researcher, nor the data analyst were aware of the group allocations. After completion of data analysis, the identity of the solutions in bottles A and B was disclosed by the pharmacist.

Oral examinations were performed by the third author, an oncologist, using the WHO Oral Mucositis Grading Scale and the VAS for pain. Assessments were conducted at baseline and on days 3, 7, and 14 of the intervention period [24]. To ensure consistency, all evaluations were carried out between 4:00 and 6:00 p.m. on the scheduled days. During the study, one participant from the intervention group and three participants from the control group withdrew (Fig. 1). This trial was conducted in accordance with CONSORT guidelines, and a completed CONSORT checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS-22 software, employing descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) and analytic statistics including the independent samples t-test, repeated measures ANOVA, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that quantitative variables were normally distributed (p > 0.05), allowing the use of parametric tests for data analysis. Independent samples t-tests showed that the mean baseline stomatitis scores were 1.76 ± 0.55 in the intervention group and 1.78 ± 0.55 in the control group, confirming homogeneity at baseline. Three days after the intervention, the mean stomatitis score in the intervention group was lower than in the control group; however, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.08). On day 7, a significant difference emerged, with the intervention group showing a lower mean score compared to the control group (P < 0.001). By day 14, the intervention group maintained a lower mean score, though the difference was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.07).

Intra-group comparisons and the interaction effects of time and group were assessed using repeated measures ANOVA. Assumption checks confirmed that sphericity (Mauchly’s test, P > 0.05), normality, homogeneity of variance, and linearity were all satisfied (P > 0.05). Repeated measures ANOVA revealed a consistent decrease in stomatitis scores over time in the intervention group. In contrast, the control group showed no significant change on day 3 (P = 1), though significant reductions were observed on days 7 and 14, indicating earlier improvement in the intervention group (Table 2).

Regarding pain associated with stomatitis, the mean baseline pain scores were 4.70 ± 2.58 in the intervention group and 4.18 ± 2.27 in the control group, indicating homogeneity at baseline. Three days after the intervention, the intervention group exhibited lower mean pain scores, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.21). The effect of Malva sylvestris mouthwash on stomatitis and pain scores on day 7 was − 0.96 and − 0.65, respectively. According to Cohen’s guidelines for effect size interpretation (0.2 = small, 0.5 = moderate, 0.8 = large), these values represent a large effect. Consequently, 96% of the reduction in stomatitis scores and 65% of the reduction in pain scores on day 7 in the intervention group can be attributed to the Malva sylvestris mouthwash (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

The present study found no statistically significant differences in mean stomatitis or pain scores between the intervention and control groups at baseline. However, by day 7 post-intervention, significant differences emerged (P < 0.001), with the intervention group demonstrating lower mean scores for both stomatitis and pain, as well as a more pronounced reduction in stomatitis severity over time. Although the mean stomatitis score remained lower in the intervention group on day 14, the difference was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.07). While no previous studies have specifically evaluated the use of Malva sylvestris mouthwash for chemotherapy-induced stomatitis in cancer patients, various medicinal herbs—including members of the Malvaceae family—have been investigated with promising results. Consistent with the present findings, Ghorbani et al. reported that a mouthwash prepared from Althaea officinalis root significantly improved chemotherapy-induced stomatitis [14]. Similarly, Rezaei et al. found that a combination of Malva sylvestris and Alcea digitata was effective in alleviating stomatitis in patients undergoing radiotherapy [19]. Other studies have demonstrated the benefits of herbal treatments in oral mucositis: Abdelwadoud et al. reported that chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) reduced stomatitis and associated pain in radiotherapy patients [25]; Alkhouli et al. found aloe vera effective in decreasing the incidence and severity of mucositis in pediatric cancer patients [26]; and Miranzadeh et al. showed that yarrow (Achillea millefolium) alleviated chemotherapy-induced mucositis [24]. The herbal formulation used in the present study differs from those in the aforementioned studies, underscoring the need for further research to compare the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of different medicinal plants for the management of chemotherapy-induced stomatitis.

The findings from the present study indicated that pain caused by stomatitis decreased at each stage in the intervention group compared to baseline. However, there was no statistically significant difference between days 7 and 14, indicating that the majority of the pain reduction occurred within the first week. In the control group, pain scores also declined compared to baseline, though the reduction was more gradual than in the intervention group. Supporting these findings, Oshvandi et al. reported in their study on the effectiveness of sage (Salvia officinalis) extract for preventing stomatitis-related pain in children with leukemia that pain levels decreased in both groups, but were significantly lower in the intervention group [27]. Similarly, Roldan et al. demonstrated that patients receiving methylene blue experienced a significant reduction in mucositis-related pain by the second day compared to the control group [28]. Naibaho et al. found that a mouthwash containing normal saline and baking soda effectively reduced stomatitis pain in cancer patients [29]. Moreover, Arshadi Bostanabad et al. showed that a Satureja hortensis extract gel was effective in alleviating mucositis-related pain in children undergoing chemotherapy [30]. Collectively, these studies underscore the positive effects of various herbal treatments on stomatitis and its associated pain.

Malva sylvestris possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties, as well as mucosal protective effects. The flavonoids and mucilage present in Malva sylvestris inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which are elevated during chemotherapy-induced mucosal damage. Its phenolic compounds scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby reducing oxidative stress that contributes to mucosal ulceration. Terpenoids and tannins help prevent secondary infections in ulcerated mucosa, which can exacerbate pain and delay healing. Additionally, mucilage forms a protective layer over the oral mucosa, soothing irritation and preventing further damage. The significant reduction in stomatitis and pain scores observed by day 7 aligns with the expected timeline for anti-inflammatory and mucosal healing effects. The early onset of symptom relief suggests that Malva sylvestris may accelerate epithelial recovery and reduce nociceptive signaling [18].

The findings of the present study indicate that Malva sylvestris mouthwash has a beneficial effect on chemotherapy-induced stomatitis and associated pain in cancer patients. When used alongside standard chemotherapy, herbal mouthwashes may represent an effective strategy to improve patients’ quality of life. These results provide a scientific basis for recommending herbal therapies as supportive treatments to manage chemotherapy-induced side effects and enhance conventional care.

Nevertheless, the study has limitations, including its single-center design, small sample size, short follow-up period, and use of chlorhexidine as a control despite limited guideline support. These limitations underscore the need for future research involving larger, more diverse populations and comparative analyses between Malva sylvestris and other herbal or pharmacological interventions.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that Malva sylvestris (mallow) mouthwash effectively reduces chemotherapy-induced stomatitis and associated pain in cancer patients. Given the low cost and favorable safety profile of herbal remedies, this intervention may represent a valuable supportive treatment for patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals. Further research, particularly larger multicenter trials, is warranted to confirm these findings and establish broader clinical recommendations.
