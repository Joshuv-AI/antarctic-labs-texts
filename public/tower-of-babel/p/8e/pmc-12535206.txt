Biologics for Chronic Rhinosinusitis With Nasal Polyposis: Current Landscape and Future Directions
Authors: Jerlon Chiu, Judd Fastenberg, Charles Tong, Brianne Navetta‐Modrov, Sonya Marcus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis (CRSwNP) is a type 2 inflammatory disease that often persists despite medical and surgical treatments. Recently, biologic therapies targeting key cytokines—interleukin (IL)‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13—and immunoglobulin E (IgE) have transformed management options for patients with severe or recurrent disease. Dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab are FDA‐approved biologics proven to reduce polyp size, improve symptoms, and enhance quality of life in clinical trials. Guidelines now endorse the use of biologics in select patients with evidence of type 2 inflammation and inadequate control after standard treatments. However, challenges remain, including high costs, uncertain treatment duration, lack of direct comparisons, and absence of validated biomarkers to guide selection. Additionally, the optimal role of biologics relative to endoscopic sinus surgery is not yet clear. Emerging therapies—including long‐acting anti–IL‐5 agents and new targets like TSLP and IL‐33—may further broaden options. Research is also needed for patients with non‐type two inflammation who respond poorly to current treatments. Ultimately, personalized approaches based on disease phenotype and biomarkers are crucial to optimizing outcomes. This review summarizes current evidence and future directions for biologic use in CRSwNP, focusing on efficacy, patient selection, surgery integration, and precision medicine opportunities.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis (CRSwNP) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the nasal and paranasal sinuses that affects approximately 1.1% of adults in the United States [1]. This disorder imposes a substantial burden on patients’ quality of life, contributing to symptoms such as nasal obstruction, hyposmia or anosmia, facial pressure, and associated comorbidities such as sleep disturbance, anxiety, and depression [2]. Globally, the incidence and prevalence of chronic rhinosinusitis are rising, underscoring its growing public health significance [3]. Although the pathogenesis of CRSwNP remains incompletely understood, it is most commonly associated with type 2 inflammation characterized by eosinophilia and elevated cytokines such as IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13. However, type 1 and type 3 inflammatory endotypes have also been identified, particularly in non‐Western and non‐White populations [4]. Furthermore, multiple inflammatory pathways may coexist within a single individual, adding complexity to disease classification and treatment response [5].

Conventional management strategies for CRSwNP include intranasal corticosteroids (INCS), nasal saline irrigations, and oral corticosteroids, with endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) reserved for cases refractory to other treatments. Despite these interventions, a subset of patients continues to experience uncontrolled disease. The therapeutic landscape has expanded with biologics targeting type 2 inflammation. To date, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved three biologics for CRSwNP: Dupilumab (2019), Omalizumab (2020), and Mepolizumab (2021). This narrative review synthesizes current evidence and emerging directions regarding the role of biologic therapies for CRSwNP.

Methods

This review is a narrative synthesis of the current literature on biologic therapies for CRSwNP. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception through July 2025. Search terms included combinations of “chronic rhinosinusitis,” “sinusitis,” “nasal polyposis,” “biologics,” and “monoclonal antibodies.” Priority was given to Phase II and III clinical trials and guideline or consensus documents.

Current Landscape: The Evidence

Pathophysiology and the Rationale for Biologic Therapy

CRSwNP is characterized by type 2 inflammation in most Western populations. This immunologic profile is marked by elevated IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 levels and increased eosinophils and IgE. IL‐4 and IL‐13 promote IgE class switching and impair epithelial barrier function, whereas IL‐5 is essential for eosinophil proliferation, activation, and survival [6]. This immunopathologic foundation has supported the clinical application of biologic agents that target key mediators of type 2 inflammation. The rationale for biologic therapy in CRSwNP parallels its demonstrated effectiveness in other type 2 inflammatory conditions, such as asthma and atopic dermatitis.

Type 2 Inflammation Cascade in CRSwNP: This diagram illustrates the key components of the type 2 inflammatory pathway implicated in CRSwNP. Epithelial injury releases alarmins—TSLP, IL‐25, and IL‐33—which activate type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) and Th2 cells. The resulting inflammatory milieu contributes to tissue remodeling and nasal polyp formation.

Current FDA‐Approved Biologics for CRSwNP

As of 2025, three biologics have received US FDA approval to treat CRSwNP: dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab. These agents target distinct components of the type 2 inflammatory cascade and have all demonstrated efficacy in reducing polyp burden and improving sinonasal symptoms.

Dupilumab, which targets IL‐4 and IL‐13, was initially approved by the FDA for atopic dermatitis in 2017 and became the first biologic approved for CRSwNP in 2019 after pivotal Phase 3 trials (SINUS‐24 and SINUS‐52) demonstrated significant improvements in nasal polyp score (NPS), nasal congestion, and quality of life compared to placebo [7].

Omalizumab, which prevents IgE from binding to its receptor, was first approved for asthma treatment in 2003 and received approval for CRSwNP in 2020. Its efficacy in CRSwNP was demonstrated in two Phase 3 trials (POLYP 1 and POLYP 2), revealing improvements in NPS and nasal symptoms, particularly among patients with comorbid allergic sensitization [8].

Mepolizumab, which targets IL‐5, is the most recent biologic approved for CRSwNP, receiving approval in 2021. Mepolizumab improved NPS, nasal obstruction scores, and time to surgery, particularly in patients with elevated eosinophil levels [9].

Candidates for Biologics

Current guidelines, including those from the European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps (EPOS 2020) and expert consensus statements, recommend considering biologic therapy for patients with evidence of type 2 inflammation who have not achieved adequate symptom control with standard medical and/or surgical interventions [10].

To guide clinical decision‐making, the European Forum for Research and Education in Allergy and Airway Diseases (EUFOREA) proposed five core criteria for identifying patients who may benefit from biologic therapy: (1) evidence of type 2 inflammation (e.g., eosinophilia, elevated IgE), (2) two or more courses of systemic corticosteroids per year, (3) 22‐item Sino‐Nasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) score of 40 or higher, (4) loss of smell, and (5) comorbid asthma. Biologics are recommended for patients who meet at least three criteria after surgery or four criteria without surgery [11].

Evaluation by an otolaryngologist is crucial before starting biologic therapy to verify the presence of nasal polyps and rule out other diagnoses, such as nasal tumors, which can be mistaken for polyps. Monk et al. highlighted cases where specialist assessment ensured the proper use of biologics, emphasizing the importance of a thorough endoscopic evaluation before treatment [12].

Despite these recommendations, questions still exist about when to consider surgery versus a biologic, the ideal duration of biologic therapy, and whether lifelong treatment is necessary. Once a biologic is started, EUFOREA recommends evaluating treatment response at 16 weeks and again at 1 year, with discontinuation suggested if there is inadequate improvement in symptoms, nasal polyp score, or quality of life. However, more evidence is needed to determine the appropriate timeframe for assessing nonresponse.

Although no standardized protocols exist for tapering or stopping biologic therapy, emerging evidence shows that durable responses may continue even with dose reduction. A recent study by van der Lans et al. evaluated the outcomes of tapered dupilumab therapy in patients with CRSwNP. In this two‐year prospective study, patients who showed a strong response within the first 6 months maintained long‐lasting effectiveness when the dupilumab dosing interval was gradually extended, without losing disease control or experiencing significant symptom recurrence [13]. This indicates that personalized tapering plans could be feasible once clinical stability is achieved, potentially reducing treatment costs and long‐term exposure. However, larger studies are needed before formal tapering guidelines can be recommended.

Biologics vs. Endoscopic Sinus Surgery

ESS has long been the standard treatment for patients with CRSwNP refractory to medical therapy. Biologics now represent an alternative, but multidisciplinary consensus statements have previously cautioned against using biologics in patients with lower disease burden or minimal symptoms [14]. However, as the efficacy of biologics continues to be demonstrated, the integration of biologic treatment into the multimodal treatment paradigm for CRSwNP remains undefined. Furthermore, questions persist regarding whether biologics should be considered a first‐line treatment option, and if so, under what circumstances.

Multiple studies have compared sinonasal outcomes in patients treated with biologic therapy versus endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS). Dharmarajan et al. found that ESS was associated with a greater short‐term reduction in polyp burden [15], while Miglani et al., in a multicenter comparative cohort study, similarly demonstrated that at both 24 and 52 weeks, ESS achieved SNOT‐22 improvements comparable to dupilumab while outperforming omalizumab, and produced greater reductions in polyp size than all biologic therapies [16].

A systematic review and meta‐analysis by Kim et al. also concluded that ESS was more effective than dupilumab in reducing polyp burden in the early months after treatment; however, by one year, outcomes converged, with dupilumab demonstrating superior improvements in olfactory function [17].

Beyond symptom‐based outcomes, quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs)—a composite metric integrating length and quality of life—have been used to assess comparative effectiveness. Parasher et al. reported greater QALY gains with dupilumab compared with ESS [18], whereas Scangas et al. found similar improvements following both interventions [19].

Extended Sinus Procedures

For patients with severe or recalcitrant CRSwNP, extended sinus surgery such as Draf III frontal sinusotomy may offer durable symptom relief. In a retrospective cohort of 87 patients undergoing Draf III surgery, significant improvements in SNOT‐22 scores were maintained at 24 months, and only 14.9% ultimately required dupilumab rescue [20]. This suggests aggressive surgical intervention may reduce the subsequent need for long‐term biologic therapy, although prospective head‐to‐head trials comparing Draf III directly with biologics are lacking.

Synergy and Sequential Approaches

Most studies of biologic therapy for CRSwNP compare outcomes against placebo or ESS alone, with few examining their combination. Garvey et al. reported that patients receiving ESS while on biologic therapy had greater and more sustained reductions in polyp burden at 12 months compared to those on biologics alone [21]. Pelletier et al. found short‐course perioperative dupilumab improved long‐term olfaction and lowered serum IgE [22], while Liu et al. showed postoperative dupilumab reduced recurrence and improved clinical outcomes [23]. These findings suggest that perioperative or adjuvant biologic therapy can synergize with ESS to optimize polyp control and symptom relief. Larger, prospective studies are needed to determine optimal timing, duration, and patient selection.

Cost

Biologic therapies for CRSwNP are significantly more expensive than ESS, raising important considerations for long‐term management, especially since current evidence indicates biologics may need to be used indefinitely. In a cost‐utility analysis, Scangas et al. estimated that over 36 years, total dupilumab costs could reach $536,420, compared to $50,436 for ESS. To match the cost‐effectiveness of surgery, the annual price of dupilumab would have to decrease to $855. These results highlight the substantial financial challenges of using biologics as a long‐term treatment option for CRSwNP and emphasize the importance of balancing clinical benefits with economic sustainability.

The evidence suggests that ESS and biologics should not be seen as mutually exclusive but as potentially complementary. ESS provides rapid and significant debulking of polyps, while biologics may improve long‐lasting symptom control in select patients. The best order of treatments, patient selection, and cost‐effectiveness of combined strategies are important areas for future research.

Choice of Biologic

Selecting the most appropriate biologic for patients with CRSwNP remains a clinical challenge. While clinical trials provide insights into overall efficacy, they are less informative in predicting which specific biologic an individual patient will respond to best [24].

Comparison of Biologics: Head‐To‐Head Trials

To date, no completed head‐to‐head randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have directly compared biologics for treating CRSwNP. However, indirect comparisons from systematic reviews and network meta‐analyses suggest that dupilumab may provide the greatest overall benefit across key outcome measures—including nasal polyp score, nasal congestion, and olfaction—when compared to omalizumab, mepolizumab, and benralizumab [25, 26]. Nevertheless, heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and endpoints limits the strength of these comparisons. Ongoing phase IV RCTs seek to provide more definitive evidence. These include a trial comparing dupilumab to omalizumab (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04998604) and another comparing dupilumab to mepolizumab (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05942222). Results from these studies are expected to inform comparative effectiveness and safety in clinically meaningful subgroups.

Biomarkers

Although biologics have significantly improved the management of refractory CRSwNP, a substantial subset of patients continues to demonstrate suboptimal or no response. Identifying reliable biomarkers to predict treatment response and stratify inflammatory endotypes remains a critical unmet need. Proposed biomarkers include peripheral blood eosinophil count, total serum IgE, local tissue eosinophilia, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO). However, several challenges limit their clinical utility—such as the absence of standardized threshold values, limited validation in real‐world populations, and weak or inconsistent correlations with clinical endpoints [27]. Further research is essential to develop and validate predictive biomarkers that can inform biologic selection and optimize treatment outcomes in routine practice.

Special Populations

While most CRSwNP trials target broad populations with type 2 inflammation, specific phenotypic subgroups may benefit differently from biologic therapy. Among these, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS) and aspirin‐exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) represent more complex phenotypes that warrant special consideration.

AFRS is characterized by IgE‐mediated hypersensitivity to fungi, nasal polyposis, eosinophilic mucin without evidence of fungal tissue invasion, positive fungal staining or culture, and characteristic radiographic findings, such as sinus expansion and heterogeneity. Although patients with AFRS are routinely excluded from major randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of biologics, emerging data from case series and observational studies suggest that biologic therapies—particularly dupilumab—may offer clinical benefit due to their broad suppression of type 2 inflammatory pathways [28, 29]. A phase III clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04684524) is currently underway to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dupilumab in patients with refractory AFRS.

AERD is defined by the clinical triad of CRSwNP, asthma, and hypersensitivity to cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1) inhibitors. Management strategies typically include endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), aspirin desensitization therapy (ADAT), and pharmacologic interventions. However, a substantial proportion of patients remain symptomatic or are unable to tolerate ADAT. In this context, biologic therapies—particularly dupilumab—have shown promising results, improving upper and lower airway symptoms, olfactory function, and overall quality of life, even among patients with prior ADAT failure [30]. Among available agents, dupilumab appears to provide more consistent benefit in AERD than omalizumab or anti–IL‐5 agents, although head‐to‐head comparative studies in this population are currently lacking.

Comorbidities

Biologic selection must account for disease phenotype, coexisting comorbidities, patient preferences, and logistical considerations. Since FDA‐approved biologics for CRSwNP also carry indications for conditions such as asthma, atopic dermatitis, and EGPA, these comorbidities can guide therapy choice.

Other practical factors—including route of administration, dosing frequency, insurance coverage, and out‐of‐pocket cost—are critical to ensuring long‐term adherence and treatment success. Multidisciplinary coordination among allergy, pulmonology, dermatology, and otolaryngology teams may optimize outcomes in patients with overlapping type 2 inflammatory disorders.

Future Directions

Biologic therapy has significantly transformed the management of CRSwNP, but challenges remain. Current FDA‐approved biologics target key type 2 inflammation pathways involving IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and IgE. Clinical trials continue to evaluate these biologics, focusing on their effects on olfaction and quality of life. Additionally, novel agents targeting other type 2 inflammatory cascade components are under investigation. Promising candidates include biologics targeting upstream epithelial cytokines such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) and IL‐33.

Expanding Type 2 Targets

Anti‐IL5

Benralizumab, an anti–IL–5 receptor monoclonal antibody approved for EGPA and severe eosinophilic asthma, showed modest improvements in nasal polyp scores in the OSTRO phase 3 trial. However, it failed to show significant benefits in patient‐reported outcomes like SNOT‐22 [31]. A second phase III trial, ORCHID (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT04157335), is ongoing to further assess its clinical utility in CRSwNP.

Depemokimab, an ultra‐long‐acting anti–IL–5 monoclonal antibody administered twice yearly, recently demonstrated efficacy in CRSwNP. In the ANCHOR‐1 and ANCHOR‐2 phase III trials, depemokimab significantly reduced nasal polyp score and nasal obstruction compared with placebo, with benefits evident at first assessment and sustained through week 52. These results met the co‐primary endpoints and showed improvement in secondary outcomes, including rhinorrhea, olfaction, Lund–Mackay CT score, and SNOT‐22 [32]. These compelling data have prompted regulatory submissions to the FDA, positioning depemokimab as a potential long‐acting biologic option for CRSwNP.

Anti‐TSLP

Tezepelumab, which targets thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), an upstream epithelial cytokine involved in type 2 inflammation, demonstrated statistically significant improvements in both nasal polyp score and nasal congestion in the phase III WAYPOINT trial [33]. It also yielded clinically meaningful gains in SNOT‐22, olfaction, and reduced need for systemic corticosteroids or surgery. Based on these positive outcomes, tezepelumab is undergoing FDA regulatory review for approval in CRSwNP.

Anti‐IL13

Lebrikizumab, an IL‐13 monoclonal antibody, is in phase 3 trials (CONTRAST‐NP) evaluating its efficacy in CRSwNP (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06338995). Given the role of IL‐13 in goblet cell metaplasia and mucus hypersecretion, it represents a promising target. Trial completion is expected in 2027.

Anti–IL–33

IL‐33 is believed to contribute to both type 2 and non–type 2 inflammation, particularly in neutrophilic endotypes. Etokimab did not achieve its primary endpoints in the ECLIPSE trial and has been deprioritized. However, itepekimab, another anti–IL–33 agent, is currently undergoing phase 2/3 trials, with anticipated results.

Beyond Type 2 Inflammation: Non–Type 2 Biologic Development

While type 2 inflammation is common in Western CRSwNP populations, a significant subset, especially among non‐white and non‐Western patients, shows type 1 or type 3 inflammation. These patients usually respond poorly to corticosteroids and current biologics. No biologics are explicitly approved for non–type 2 CRSwNP, mainly because of the absence of well‐established biomarkers and clinical trials targeting type 1 or type 3 pathways.

Conclusions

Biologics have transformed the management of CRSwNP, offering effective therapeutic options for patients who have not achieved adequate symptom control with standard medical and/or surgical interventions. Dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab represent important milestones, with new agents such as depemokimab and tezepelumab on the horizon for FDA approval. Future work should focus on comparative effectiveness, cost‐effectiveness strategies, treatment algorithms for biologic initiation, tapering, and discontinuation, integration of biologics with ESS and biomarker‐guided precision medicine.
