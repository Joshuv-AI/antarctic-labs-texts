Could animal models be used to longitudinally track intrinsic capacity during aging?
Authors: I-Tzu Chen, Wan-Hsuan Lu, Fei-Yuan Hsiao, Liang-Kung Chen, Angelo Parini, Yohan Santin, Bruno Guiard, Jean-Philippe Pradere, Rafael de Cabo, Bruno Vellas, Yves Rolland, Philipe de Souto Barreto
Published: 2026

Global population aging poses major challenges for health and social systems because of functional limitations and dependency with advancing age. The World Health Organization (WHO) recently highlighted the importance of promoting healthy aging worldwide, a process characterized by the maximization of functional ability, enabling well-being in older adulthood. This concept inspired the development of the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) program and the Intrinsic Capacity (IC) construct, with the latter serving as core element of ICOPE for clinical use. IC represents the composite of all mental and physical internal attributes of an individual. It is often operationalized through five key domains: cognition, locomotion, vitality, sensory function, and psychological capacity.

Research on IC during aging in humans is growing, [1,2] being marked by high IC variability [3]. Longitudinal monitoring must be prioritized to capture aging trajectories and identify modifiable risk factors. However, the need for a long-time window spanning decades of human life poses a significant challenge to investigating IC decline over time. In contrast, animal models offer a strategic alternative due to their shorter lifespans compared to humans. For example, the typical lifespan of a mouse is 2–3 years, whereas specific fish models (e.g., killifish) may live only 4–6 months. Thus, leveraging these models for longitudinal IC tracking offers a viable pathway that may expedite the elucidation of IC dynamics and mechanisms during aging.

Preserved functional ability can be objectively assessed through behavioral paradigms in animal studies. By using these measurements in observational or experimental settings, animal models can recapitulate the longitudinal trajectories of IC during aging. To facilitate crosstalk with humans and accurately capture age-related changes in IC, assessment tools should meet specific criteria: they should target the corresponding IC domains in humans, show a decline over time with aging, and exhibit sufficient amplitude to distinguish meaningful functional loss. In this Editorial, we discuss how longitudinal IC investigations in mice and fish may advance human research and care during aging. Particular attention will be given to assessing, in experimental models, all IC domains longitudinally, interactions across IC domains, and the definition of a set of potentially informative IC assessments in both mice and fish.

The decline of IC in animal models

In both mouse and fish models, functional capacity generally exhibits age-related deterioration characterized by distinct onset times and rates of progression across the different IC domains and measurement tools. This heterogeneity likely stems from the intrinsic nature of each IC domain and from the characteristics of the animals, such as sex and strain. Below we provide a few elements to be considered when selecting the tests/measurements for the longitudinal monitoring of IC during aging in mice and fish:

Cognition encompasses a range of mental processes, including memory, judgment, and orientation. Key components relevant to aging include working memory (brief retention of information), spatial memory (allocentric navigation), and recognition (recollection and familiarity). In mice, distinct cognitive domains exhibit heterogeneous aging trajectories. For instance, working memory, assessed via spontaneous alternation in the T-maze, tends to decline earlier (often by mid-life in C57BL/6 mice) than spatial memory, which is evaluated by time spent in the target quadrant of the water maze. [4] Conversely, object recognition often remains preserved even into late life [5]. These findings suggest that working and spatial memory assays are sensitive indicators of age-related cognitive decline in mice.

In fish, spatial memory deficits are evident when assessing ‘homebase area’—the ability to establish a reference point for exploration. For instance, the killifish failed to form the home base during the final third of their lifespan. [6] As in mice, fish exhibit impairments in spatial memory with aging, suggesting this function may be used as a measure of cognition in the context of IC.

Locomotor capacity encompasses endurance, balance, and muscle strength. In mice, age-related decline in physical function can be captured by composite indices such as the Comprehensive Functional Assessment Battery, [7] while individual functional tests exhibit distinct aging trajectories. Voluntary activity, for instance, displays the most precipitous decline, particularly among females. For example, wheel-running performance decreased by 71% between mid-life (17–18 months) and late-life (28–29 months) in C57BL/6 mice [8]. Relative to a 6-month baseline, this corresponds to an 82% deficit at mid-life, progressing to a 98% reduction by late-life. Males exhibited a similar but slightly delayed trajectory, showing a 62% decline at midlife that progressed to a 90% deficit in late-life [7]. Motor coordination, assessed via rotarod to capture balance and horizontal locomotion, followed a comparable though attenuated pattern.7, [8] Meanwhile, endurance and speed, as indicated by the treadmill test, demonstrated a similar age-related decline in both sexes. In conclusion, locomotor assessments provide insights into age-related functional decline in mice from mid-life and onwards. Among these, voluntary activity appears to be a more sensitive metric due to its profound and progressive deterioration.

Parallel to rodent models, fish exhibit age-related and sexually dimorphic declines in locomotor capacity. In killifish, the swimming tunnel assay (an analogue of the murine treadmill test) reveals a performance peak at 8 weeks, followed by functional loss in males at 12 weeks and females at 16 weeks. [9] This trajectory is mirrored in open field test (OFT), where female mobility peaks at 12–15 weeks before deteriorating significantly since 18 weeks of age [6]. In zebrafish, however, no sex differences were observed in the swimming tunnel assay over a four-week window. Notably, older zebrafish (60–71 % of the average 40-month lifespan) subjected to exercise training showed no significant changes over the observation period, whereas younger groups exhibited improved swimming performance [10]. In summary, these data validate locomotor decline as an aging phenotype in fish, particularly during the final third of the lifespan, and suggest that behavioral interventions may preserve locomotor function, but the capacity for trainability diminishes with age.

Vitality is often linked to energy balance and neuromuscular function. Research on fish vitality remains sparse; currently, there is no consensus on measurement methods for assessing vitality. In mice, energy metabolism can be measured using calorimetric cages that monitor parameters such as energy expenditure, oxygen consumption, and food intake. However, this assessment does not reveal age-related changes in metabolic profiles in early stages. [11] Neuromuscular function is commonly evaluated via grip strength, often normalized to body weight to assess physiological reserve—a paradigm largely validated in human clinical studies. Results from maximum forelimb grip strength generally parallel motor coordination trends: female C57BL/6 mice show an earlier decline, and both sexes experience a 40–50% loss of function (compared to 6 months of age) by late life [7]. It is worth noting that methodological inconsistencies (e.g., limb selection, trial count, and scoring best vs. average performance) can complicate comparisons [7,8]. Together, grip-strength assessments of vitality longitudinally may be a good choice in mice, though standardization is warranted to facilitate cross-study comparability.

Sensory capacity allows individuals to interact safely with their peers and the environment. In humans, impairments in hearing and vision significantly compromise social relationships and information processing. There is a paucity of research on sensory function in fish, and validated assays for monitoring age-related changes in hearing and vision have yet to be established. In mice, auditory function is evaluated using electrophysiological techniques (e.g., Auditory Brainstem Response, ABR) or behavioral assays, both of which determine auditory thresholds that indicate poorer hearing capacity at higher threshold levels. In CBA mice, ABR detects hearing loss earlier than behavioral tests, revealing decline in the first year that worsens significantly in late life, especially in high-frequency tones (e.g., 56 kHz). [12] Vision can be assessed similarly using behavioral and electrophysiological paradigms. Motion tracking, as measured by the OptoKinetic test (OKT, where mice view rotating sine-wave gratings), decreases significantly in C57BL/6 J mice between 2 and 14 months of age, a decline that is also evident in electroretinography, which reveals a general decline in retinal activity with increasing age. [13] On the contrary, studies demonstrated that visual reflexes remained intact in aged mice [14]. These electrophysiological and behavioral tests can provide valuable insights into age-related sensory decline in mouse models.

Psychological capacity encompasses a broad range of attributes, of which anxiety has been commonly studied. In mice, this is assessed via thigmotaxis (peripheral preference due to predator avoidance) and rearing (unsupported vertical exploration). While thigmotaxis signals anxiety, rearing is inversely related to it. Longitudinal studies indicated age-related psychological deterioration, characterized by increased peripheral activity and reduced rearing. For instance, thigmotaxis increased with age in ICR/CD-1 mice (61% external activity at 18 months vs. 48% at 6 months), while rearing decreased by 50% in both frequency and duration. [14] Collectively, these findings support the use of anxiety-like behaviors as markers for psychological capacity, particularly for capturing declines that occur in the final stages of the murine lifespan.

Anxiety in fish is typically assessed using the novel tank diving test, measuring metrics such as zone preference, immobility, and habituation. However, results deviated from patterns observed in mice. Older killifish spent more time in the center zone, seemingly indicating lower anxiety, yet maintained a strong bottom preference (approximately 80%) across all ages. On the contrary, other markers indicated deterioration: aging beyond 18 weeks correlated with increased immobility and a loss of habituation, whereas younger fish (12–15 weeks) habituated normally. [6] These findings suggest that immobility and loss of habituation may be more suitable for assessing psychological capacity in fish.

Integrated assessment of IC

To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet tracked all IC domains longitudinally in the same animals, and few have monitored more than one domain concurrently. This contrasts with human research, where comprehensive longitudinal assessments reveal that IC impairments tend to form distinct clusters. [15] While direct translation requires caution, the simultaneous assessment of all IC domains would significantly enhance the translational potential of these animal models. Furthermore, methodological variations can contribute to heterogeneity in findings; standardizing paradigms and cut-points for determining the 'minimum important change' can enhance the translational value of preclinical models of IC. Another challenge for longitudinal IC investigations in animals is to establish a better protocol for data collection: assessing different IC domains in a short time window may stress the animals, which can impact their performance; in such circumstances, a bad performance may be due to stress rather than aging.

For fish, homebase formation can be applied for spatial memory assessment (cognition). Locomotion can be evaluated with the OFT, or the swimming tunnel assay, while the novel tank diving test provides information (immobility and habituation) for psychological capacity. Clear longitudinal measurements of vitality and sensory for healthspan investigations in fish are lacking. However, certain locomotion tests, such as swimming distance, acceleration, max/average speed measured in the OFT, may serve as indicators of vitality. This approach parallels human studies, in which sleep and fatigue may, in theory, be considered markers of both psychological and vitality domains, given the absence of a consensus definition of vitality. Finally, given the scarcity of longitudinal sensory assessments, further investigation in this area is warranted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, age-related functional decline is consistently observed across multiple behavioral domains in mice, with the onset and progression following distinct trajectories across IC domains. Findings from fish models corroborate these patterns. Moving forward, research would benefit from longitudinally tracking all IC domains within the same individuals. Furthermore, complementing these functional assessments with biomarker data or interventions would enhance our understanding of underlying mechanisms and facilitate the translational potential of these animal models.
