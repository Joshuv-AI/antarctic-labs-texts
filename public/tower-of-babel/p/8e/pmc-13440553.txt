Pioneering endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration in North Africa: Results from a prospective Moroccan case series
Authors: A Moumen, S Boubia, H El Attar, R Cherkab, M Ridai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy pose a diagnostic challenge, especially in regions with a high prevalence of tuberculosis (TB) and lung cancer. Objectives To evaluate the feasibility, safety and diagnostic performance of endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) in a Moroccan centre. Methods This prospective observational study evaluated patients undergoing EBUS-TBNA from December 2021 to March 2025. Samples were analysed using rapid on-site cytology, cell block histology, and additional tests including GeneXpert and molecular profiling when indicated. Final diagnoses were established using a composite reference standard based on surgical pathology or clinical follow-up. Results A total of 180 patients were included, of whom 104 were men and 76 women (sex ratio 1.37), with a mean age of 61 years. EBUS TBNA was performed for primary lung cancer diagnosis or staging in 57.2% of cases, for evaluation of suspected mediastinal metastases in 24.4%, and for investigation of unexplained mediastinal lymphadenopathy in 18.3%. The procedure provided a diagnosis in 91.5% of patients, yielding 151 true positives and 12 true negatives (17 false negatives), corresponding to a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 100%, with no complications reported. Final diagnoses included 103 lung cancers, 39 metastatic carcinomas, 5 lymphomas, 5 cases of sarcoidosis and 3 cases of TB, with only 9 patients subsequently requiring mediastinoscopy. Conclusion In TB-endemic regions, EBUS-TBNA is safe and effective for mediastinal evaluation, providing reliable diagnoses for both malignant and granulomatous diseases. Its implementation is justified as a first-line modality in resource-limited settings. Study synopsisWhat the study adds. This prospective case series reports one of the first and largest North African experiences with endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) in a tuberculosis-endemic setting. Background

Mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy present significant diagnostic challenges in thoracic medicine, especially in regions with a high prevalence of infectious and malignant diseases. In Morocco and across North Africa, tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of lymphadenopathy, often requiring invasive procedures such as mediastinoscopy for definitive diagnosis.[1] The advent of endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) has revolutionised mediastinal staging and diagnosis, offering a minimally invasive, highly accurate and safe alternative to traditional surgical approaches.[2,3]

Despite the proven efficacy and widespread adoption of EBUS in North America, Europe and Asia, its application in North Africa remains limited. Factors such as equipment availability, lack of trained personnel and resource constraints have slowed its integration into routine practice. Recognising this gap, we report our initial experience with EBUS-TBNA in Morocco, which aimed to demonstrate its feasibility, safety and diagnostic potential in our regional context. This case series underscores the promising results obtained so far, highlighting EBUS as a valuable tool in the diagnostic algorithm for thoracic diseases in North Africa.

Methods

This single-centre study was conducted as a prospective observational case series at CHU Ibn Rochd in Casablanca, Morocco, over a 4-year period (December 2021 - March 2025). We included all patients undergoing EBUS-TBNA for evaluation of mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy during that time. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee of CHU Ibn Rochd. All patients provided written informed consent prior to the procedure.

All EBUS-TBNA procedures were performed on an outpatient basis under general anaesthesia, with airway management via a laryngeal mask airway (LMA). A dedicated linear-array EBUS bronchoscope was introduced through the LMA to systematically survey mediastinal and hilar lymph node stations. Targeted lymph nodes were identified and labelled according to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) lymph node map.[4] Transbronchial needle aspiration was carried out under real-time ultrasound guidance using a 22-gauge EBUS needle. Each lymph node station was punctured at least four times to ensure adequate sampling, and colour Doppler ultrasound was used during needle passes to avoid vascular structures, in line with current technical guidelines.[5]

Rapid on-site cytopathological evaluation (ROSE) by a cytopathologist was used for all cases, with immediate toluidine blue staining of smear slides to assess specimen adequacy. Additional material from each needle pass was collected in preservative to prepare cell blocks for subsequent histopathological analysis. Portions of the EBUS-TBNA specimen were also allocated for ancillary testing when indicated by the clinical context. For example, aspirated samples from patients with suspected TB underwent Xpert MTB/RIF (GeneXpert) polymerase chain reaction testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis; specimens suspicious for lymphoma were submitted for flow cytometric immunophenotyping; and cell-block material from confirmed or suspected lung carcinomas was used for molecular profiling. Molecular analyses included testing for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase) and ROS1 (ROS proto-oncogene 1) rearrangements, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression by immunohistochemistry, with targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) performed when sufficient tumour tissue was available.

Patients were monitored during and after the procedure for any complications, with specific attention to bleeding, pneumothorax, infection, or anaesthesia-related adverse events; any incidents were documented. A composite reference standard, consisting of surgical pathology (from mediastinoscopy or other confirmatory biopsies) and/or clinical follow-up, was used to establish the final diagnosis for each patient. The diagnostic performance of EBUS-TBNA was then assessed by comparing cytopathology results against these final diagnoses. We calculated sensitivity, specificity and overall diagnostic yield using standard definitions, with cases of disease missed by EBUS (later confirmed via surgery or follow-up) classified as false negatives. The proportion of patients requiring subsequent mediastinoscopy owing to non-diagnostic EBUS findings was also recorded as an outcome measure.

Results

A total of 180 patients (104 men and 76 women, male/female ratio 1.37) underwent EBUS-TBNA between December 2021 and March 2025. The mean patient age was 61 years (median 62 years). The main indications for EBUS-TBNA were evaluation of suspected primary lung cancer in 103 patients (57.2%), assessment of mediastinal lymph node metastasis from extrathoracic malignancies in 44 (24.4%), and investigation of unexplained mediastinal lymphadenopathy in 33 (18.3%). Notably, the cases of lymphadenopathy of unknown cause included both potentially benign causes (such as sarcoidosis or TB) and indeterminate presentations that required tissue diagnosis.

All procedures were performed under general anaesthesia in an outpatient setting, with a flexible ultrasound bronchoscope introduced via an LMA. Lymph node stations were sampled under real-time endobronchial ultrasound guidance using 22-gauge aspiration needles, and nodal locations were labelled according to the standard IASLC lymph node map. A minimum of four needle passes was made per target node, and Doppler ultrasonography was employed during needle advancement to avoid puncture of interposing blood vessels. ROSE was utilised in every case to immediately assess specimen adequacy. Cytology smears were stained with toluidine blue in the procedure suite, and additional cell-block preparations were obtained for subsequent histopathological examination. Ancillary tests were performed on aspirated material as indicated by the clinical context: for example, samples were sent for GeneXpert MTB/RIF molecular testing in patients with suspected tuberculous lymphadenitis, and immunophenotypic analysis (including flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry on cell-block sections) was conducted when a diagnosis of lymphoma was under consideration.

EBUS-TBNA successfully provided a definitive cytopathological diagnosis in 171 of 180 patients (Table 1). Malignant pathology was the most frequent finding: primary lung cancer was diagnosed by EBUS in 103 patients (57.2% of the cohort), and metastatic carcinoma from an extrathoracic primary cancer was confirmed in 39 (21.7%). In addition, EBUS-TBNA identified lymphoma in 5 patients (2.8%), with immunophenotyping confirming the presence of both Hodgkin’s and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma subtypes among these cases. Non malignant diseases were also diagnosed, including sarcoidosis in 5 patients (2.8%) and TB in 3 (1.7%). A variety of other less common diagnoses were established in the remaining 16 patients (8.8%), including benign reactive lymphadenopathy, one case of Castleman’s disease, and one bronchogenic cyst.

In patients with confirmed lung cancer, molecular profiling of EBUS-derived samples was performed when sufficient tissue was available. EGFR mutation testing was carried out in 13 patients with lung adenocarcinoma, of whom 3 (23.1%) were positive for an EGFR activating mutation. PD-L1 immunohistochemical staining was performed on 6 samples, of which one showed high PD-L1 expression. Testing for ALK gene rearrangement was done in 6 patients and for ROS1 rearrangement in one patient; none of these cases demonstrated an ALK or ROS1 rearrangement. Additionally, broad NGS panels were applied to 5 tumour samples to screen for other actionable genomic alterations. For patients in whom an infectious cause was suspected, 12 aspirate specimens were examined using the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay for M. tuberculosis, yielding 2 positive results.

EBUS-TBNA was non-diagnostic in 9 patients (5.0% of the cohort), necessitating a surgical mediastinoscopy to obtain a definitive biopsy. All 9 patients underwent mediastinoscopic lymph node sampling, which successfully established a final diagnosis in each case: one patient was diagnosed with lymphoma, 5 with tuberculous lymphadenitis, and 3 with sarcoidosis.

Diagnostic performance estimates were calculated using a composite reference standard combining surgical pathology when available and clinical and radiological follow-up for all other patients. Based on this reference, EBUS-TBNA provided a definitive diagnosis in 171 of 180 patients (95.0%). However, these estimates should be interpreted with caution, as not all patients had histological confirmation.

No procedure-related complications were observed among the 180 cases, underscoring the safety of EBUS-TBNA in our setting.

Discussion

In recent years, EBUS-TBNA has revolutionised the evaluation of mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy by offering high diagnostic accuracy with minimal invasiveness.[2,3] Our case series set out to determine whether the benefits of this technology could be replicated in a North African setting, where TB remains endemic and access to advanced bronchoscopic techniques has been limited. Consistent with expectations, we found that EBUS-TBNA was both safe and diagnostically effective in our Moroccan cohort, serving a dual role by confirming malignancy or identifying granulomatous disease (such as TB) in a single procedure. The patient demographics of our series (mean age 61 years, with a slight male predominance) mirror those seen in other thoracic pathology evaluations, suggesting that our findings are representative of the regional population requiring mediastinal assessment.

A key advantage of EBUS-TBNA is its ability to obtain real-time needle biopsies from mediastinal lymph nodes under ultrasound guidance, which has made it a widely adopted standard for lung cancer staging and the diagnosis of mediastinal metastases.[2,3,6] In our study, this approach yielded high overall diagnostic accuracy: we achieved a 95% diagnostic yield (with 91% sensitivity and 100% specificity), closely mirroring results from large international series.[5,6] The procedure also proved very safe, as we encountered no major complications, in line with previous reports that severe adverse events occur in <1% of cases.[5,7] We attribute our success in part to strict adherence to technical best practices – such as performing a minimum of four needle passes per lymph node with a 22-gauge needle, and using Doppler guidance to avoid vascular structures – which aligns with published guidelines and is likely to have contributed to the high yield in our cohort.[5,8]

Another factor that boosted our diagnostic yield was the routine use of ROSE during EBUS-TBNA. By allowing immediate assessment of specimen adequacy, ROSE helps ensure that representative material is collected, and it has been shown to increase sensitivity for detecting granulomatous conditions such as TB.[5,9] Furthermore, our experience reflects the expanding scope of EBUS-TBNA beyond basic cytological diagnosis. We obtained sufficient tissue in many cases to perform ancillary analyses such as GeneXpert MTB/RIF testing for M. tuberculosis and molecular profiling for oncology targets (EGFR mutations, ALK rearrangements, PD-L1 expression), underscoring how EBUS-TBNA can support personalised treatment planning as well as rapid infectious disease confirmation within a single minimally invasive procedure.

In a TB-endemic region such as Morocco, an important advantage of EBUS-TBNA is its ability to simultaneously evaluate for malignancy and TB, thus avoiding more invasive surgical biopsies in many cases. We diagnosed three cases of mediastinal TB lymphadenitis in our series, which demonstrates the procedure’s usefulness in distinguishing TB from cancer without resorting to mediastinoscopy. Published studies report EBUS-TBNA sensitivities for TB that range widely (~60 - 95%), influenced by factors such as bacillary load and sampling technique.[10–12] For example, Aljohaney[10] documented an 83.9% sensitivity for EBUS-TBNA in TB using combined clinical and pathological criteria, while Hassan et al.[11] reported ~79% diagnostic accuracy in a high-prevalence setting.

Several factors are likely to affect the yield of EBUS-TBNA in TB. Lymph node characteristics play a role: larger nodes (>1 cm) with necrotic centres are more likely to yield positive cultures.[11,12] Likewise, a multimodal diagnostic approach, combining cytological detection of granulomas with mycobacterial culture and nucleic acid amplification testing (e.g. GeneXpert), significantly improves TB detection rates.[9,10] In our series, integrating on-site GeneXpert analysis led to two rapid TB confirmations, highlighting the benefit of incorporating molecular diagnostics into the EBUS workflow. We also found that maintaining a meticulous technique (at least four passes per node, as per our protocol) enhanced our ability to detect TB, as ample tissue sampling is critical for this paucibacillary disease.[8,13] Even with these measures, EBUS-TBNA’s sensitivity for tuberculous lymphadenitis generally remains lower than for malignancies, so a negative result does not definitively exclude TB. False negatives can occur if granulomas are missed or if necrotic material is inadequately sampled. In such challenging cases, more invasive approaches remain important: surgical mediastinoscopy or adjunct techniques such as EBUS-guided intranodal forceps biopsy and cryobiopsy may be needed to secure a diagnosis.[5,12]

Despite its high overall performance, EBUS-TBNA is not a stand alone solution for all patients. In our series, 9 patients (5%) had non diagnostic results and required subsequent mediastinoscopy to reach a definitive diagnosis. This outcome underscores that EBUS-TBNA can yield false negatives, particularly in the presence of lymph node necrosis or fibrosis that hampers adequate sampling. Accordingly, the negative predictive value of an EBUS-TBNA biopsy, although high, is not 100%; a negative finding must be interpreted in clinical context, and additional evaluation is warranted if suspicion for disease remains (as reflected by the patients we referred for surgical biopsy). Our study is also limited by its single-centre, retrospective design and the lack of a surgical gold standard for every case, which could lead to underestimation of the true false-negative rate. Nevertheless, we attempted to mitigate these limitations by performing confirmatory mediastinoscopies in unclear cases and by using adjuncts such as ROSE and GeneXpert to maximise diagnostic accuracy.

Looking ahead, certain barriers must be addressed to fully realise the benefits of EBUS-TBNA across North Africa. These include the high initial cost of equipment, limited availability of trained operators, and the need for close multidisciplinary collaboration (among bronchoscopists, pathologists and microbiologists) to optimise specimen processing and interpretation.[5] Tackling these challenges through regional training programmes, multicentre case registries and greater integration of rapid molecular tests could further enhance diagnostic yields and reduce delays in care.

Conclusion

This study reports one of the first and largest North African experiences with EBUS-TBNA, and demonstrates that this technique can be safely and effectively implemented in a TB-endemic, resource-limited setting. EBUS-TBNA achieved diagnostic outcomes comparable to those reported by leading international centres while providing a minimally invasive alternative to mediastinoscopy for both malignant and granulomatous mediastinal diseases. Beyond its excellent diagnostic performance, the ability to combine real-time sampling with molecular and microbiological analyses highlights its growing role in precision medicine and modern thoracic oncology.

Although barriers to wider adoption remain, including equipment costs, training requirements and the need for multidisciplinary expertise, these challenges should not overshadow the substantial clinical benefits demonstrated in this study. The successful implementation of EBUS-TBNA in Morocco provides a model for other countries in the region and supports its establishment as the standard first-line approach for mediastinal evaluation. Expanding access to EBUS-TBNA across North Africa has the potential to transform the diagnostic pathway of mediastinal diseases, reduce reliance on surgical procedures, accelerate TB and cancer diagnosis, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.
