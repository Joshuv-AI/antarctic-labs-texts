Genome-wide analysis and prediction of chloroplast and mitochondrial RNA editing sites of AGC gene family in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) for abiotic stress tolerance
Authors: Furqan Ahmad, Muhammad Abdullah, Zulqurnain Khan, Piotr Stępień, Shoaib ur Rehman, Umar Akram, Muhammad Habib ur Rahman, Zulfiqar Ali, Daraz Ahmad, Rana Muhammad Amir Gulzar, M. Ajmal Ali, Ehab A. A. Salama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCotton is one of the topmost fiber crops throughout the globe. During the last decade, abrupt changes in the climate resulted in drought, heat, and salinity. These stresses have seriously affected cotton production and significant losses all over the textile industry. The GhAGC kinase, a subfamily of AGC group and member of serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) protein kinases group and is highly conserved among eukaryotic organisms. The AGC kinases are compulsory elements of cell development, metabolic processes, and cell death in mammalian systems. The investigation of RNA editing sites within the organelle genomes of multicellular vascular plants, such as Gossypium hirsutum holds significant importance in understanding the regulation of gene expression at the post-transcriptional level.MethodsIn present work, we characterized twenty-eight GhAGC genes in cotton and constructed phylogenetic tree using nine different species from the most primitive to the most recent.ResultsIn sequence logos analyses, highly conserved amino acid residues were found in G. hirsutum, G. arboretum, G. raimondii and A. thaliana. The occurrence of cis-acting growth and stress-related elements in the promoter regions of GhAGCs highlight the significance of these factors in plant development and abiotic stress tolerance. Ka/Ks levels demonstrated that purifying selection pressure resulting from segmental events was applied to GhAGC with little functional divergence. We focused on identifying RNA editing sites in G. hirsutum organelles, specifically in the chloroplast and mitochondria, across all 28 AGC genes.ConclusionThe positive role of GhAGCs was explored by quantifying the expression in the plant tissues under abiotic stress. These findings help in understanding the role of GhAGC genes under abiotic stresses which may further be used in cotton breeding for the development of climate smart varieties in abruptly changing climate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05598-0.

Background

Climate change had adversely modulated the biotic and abiotic factors fostering a diverse impact on the natural environment of crops [1, 2]. Abiotic stresses being major limiting factors for most of the crops include a diversity of environmental elements like high temperature, water deficit, water logging, Alkaline, sodic, cold, heatwaves and light [3–5]. The fast development of genomics, proteomics and cost-effective sequencing technologies has enabled the studies of structural information, functional behavior, and evolutionary developments of protein families [6, 7]. Genome-wide research direction highlights the knowledge about the plant towards its ecology and is helpful for adaptation and integrated regulation mechanism to abiotic stresses [8]. Plants are immobile and modulate their mechanisms of morphology, physiology, and developments in various ways to survive in stress conditions [9–11]. To keep plants growing in adverse conditions, genetic and evolutionary studies are important as several genes play their role and govern the expression of other genes [12–15].

Cotton cultivars exist in both diploid and allotetraploid variants. G. hirsutum (AD1), G. barbadense (AD2), G. tomentosum (AD3), G. mustelinum (AD4), G. darwinii (AD5) and G. ekmanianum (AD6) are allopolyploid cotton species from the New World. Diploid organisms have eight monophyletic genome groupings, A-K. 13 D-genome species are from the Americas, 15 from Asia and Africa, and 18 from Australia [16, 17]. Cotton has 52 species, of which seven are tetraploid and forty-five are diploid [16–18].

External environmental stimuli lead to cellular response by revisions in already working machinery of proteins; ultimately making the transcriptional system active. The most frequent mechanism in this case is protein phosphorylation, well known in prokaryotes and targeting histidine, arginine, aspartate, and lysine, but little is known about eukaryotes [19–22]. The activities of C-helix, N and C lobes upon ATP binding decide the fate of catalytic regulations of protein kinases in eukaryotes during phosphorylation. There are conserved linkages between the regions of C terminal and the regulatory regions in AGC family proteins [23].

The AGC kinase family is a subgroup kinase, which belongs to the major group of protein kinases. Steven Hanks and Tony Hunter used this term for the first time in 1995 to explain AGC as a subgroup of serine/threonine protein kinases [24]. The AGC kinases were named as AGC after their extreme similarity with other kinase groups including cAMP-dependent protein kinases PKA (also named as (PKAC), protein kinases PKG (renamed as CGK1α) and protein kinases with phospholipid-dependent protein kinase C (PKC) family [22]. The AGC family consists of 60 kinases domains and 42 are functional active domains which regulate the kinases processes and localization. The existence of splice variants and multiple isoforms of AGC kinases make it more complex [19, 25, 26]. The AGC kinases are highly conserved group among eukaryotic organisms regulating the process of cell development, metabolism, and cell death in mammals. About 60 serine/threonine protein kinases like AGC kinases are reported in literature indicating the significance of these kinases [19, 23, 26]. Further, plant AGC kinases catalytic domains have similarities with the mammalian AGC kinases proteins, but a few pieces of knowledge are available for molecular activities in plants [21, 27].

The classification of AGC kinases is based on the kinases domain differences and included six subgroups or sub-families like NDR kinases containing group of kinases AGC VI, AGC VII and PDK1; and other subgroup AGC VIIIa, AGC VIIIb [19, 20]. Most of the AGC kinases have role in different defense and developmental processes like morphogenesis, and pathogen responses in wheat, rice, tomato, and Arabidopsis [28–32]. Resistance to a bacterial blight in rice was induced through the cloning of Xa21, a known membrane-bound receptor-like kinase (RLK) having specific serine/threonine domain [28]. The programmed cell death of P. syringae pv. in tomato is due to the interaction between the AvrPto-dependent Pto-interacting protein 3 (Adi3) an AGC kinases, and Pto and 3-phosphoinositide- dependent protein kinase-1 (Pdk1) in Tomato [29, 33]. AtOXI, an Oxidative Signal Inducible 1 protein is a member of the AGC VIIIb kinases and had an important role against the pathogens [32, 34]. The orthologue of the Arabidopsis gene (AtOXI1) in rice [AGC kinase (OsOxi1)] regulates the phosphorylation of the OsPti1a Thr-233 position and results in the positive control of basal resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae pv. oryzae [30]. The WAG1 and WAG2 encoding for serine/threonine kinases showed regulation mechanisms for root architecture in Arabidopsis [35]. The other protein kinases, i.e., IREH1 and IRE3 regulated root skewing and microtubule formation in Arabidopsis [20]. The UNICORN (UCN) are members of the AGC VIII family and contributed to integument cell division, and development of flower part, embryo, and cotyledons [36]. The AGC kinases may have distinct regulatory functions towards external factors in different plant species. As a result, isolating and studying the function of specific AGC kinase genes in plants is critical. The bigger genome and multiple ploidy level in wheat is somehow a hurdle for functional genomics studies and only a set of seven AGC kinases is reported in National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database so far [31]. The AGCs are reported to play a significant role for abiotic stress tolerance in B. rapa [37]. The AGC family being novel and undefined in cotton can play a potential role in regulation of mechanism involved in abiotic stress resilience. The role of the AGC family in cotton is unknown. Therefore, it is important to identify and characterize AGC gene family members at genome level in upland cotton.

In the present study, we comprehensively examined the AGC gene family in upland cotton (G. hirsutum). Our purpose was to exploit the AGC family in cotton and identify the role of this family in the growth and development of cotton under abiotic stress conditions. We performed multiple analyses and experiments including phylogenetic analyses, chromosomal localization, conserved motif, gene structure, gene duplication, cis-acting regulatory element analyses, gene expression under abiotic stress condition using tissue specific gene expression from Cotton Functional Genomic Database (CottonFGD) and qRT-PCR expression profiling. The present work will lay the basis to elucidate the functional role of GhAGC genes, which may assist in decoding the comprehensive molecular mechanism involved in cotton for abiotic stress tolerance like high temperature, drought, and salinity.

Results

Sequence identification and retrieval

Twenty-eight AGC family genes were retrieved from G. hirsutum, 12 from A. thaliana. 19 from G. arboretum, 19 from G. herbaceum, 20 from G. raimondii, 29 from G. barbadense, 26 from G. tomentosum, 29 from G. mustelinum, 11 from T. cacao, 15 from P. patents, 14 from C. reinhardtii, 23 from S. moellendorffii, and 13 from C. capsularis(Supplementary Table S2). Sequence alignment and phylogeny

Phylostatum analysis indicated the presence of members of AGC members in primitive lineage like S. moellendorffii and C. reinhardtii. The AGC genes had shown a large-scale expansion across the plant kingdom due to their prints in multiple groups including monocots, dicots, halophytes, lycophytes, chlorophytes, and angiosperms (Fig. 1A). Phylogenetic analysis was performed to study the evolutionary history of AGC protein kinases genes in cotton and their relationship with AGC gene members in other studied organisms (Fig. 1B). A total of 287 AGC family genes from different organisms were used to construct the phylogenetic tree which divided studied AGC members into six major groups viz. a, b, c, d, e, and f. The major group (a) includes sixty-eight (68 proteins) followed by group (e) with (67 proteins), group c (43 proteins), group d (41 proteins), group f (38 proteins) and group b (30 proteins). The first four groups contain most of the members belonging to Gossypium and the last two groups have members from primitive species. The AGC group a, b c and d contain most of the members from Gossypium genus including G. hirsutum, G. barbadense, G. arboretum, G. raimondii, G. herbaceum, G. mustelinum, G. darwinii, G. tomentosum and only few members from A. thaliana, P. patents and S. moellendorffii. The existence of AGC members in Gossypium species along with primitive organisms indicates the conserveness of AGC during evolutionary process. The group e and f contain members from C. reinhardtii, S. moellendorffii, P. patens, A. thaliana, T. cacao and C. capsularis along with some members of Gossypium. The phylogenetic analysis indicated that AGC has presence in different primitive and higher organisms.

Analysis of Cis-element in promoter, Motif Finding and Gene structure Display Analysis

The 2 kb upstream region of GhAGC genes was used to examine the cis-elements using PlantCARE database. The GhAGC contains different cis-elements, i.e., LR, DS-R, ABA-R, LT-R, MeJA-R, AUX-R, MYB-DI, GA-R, SA-R and SA (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4). These elements are important for plant growth, development, and response to biotic and abiotic stresses. We constructed the gene structures to demonstrate the event of the evolutionary development among all GhAGC genes. Out of 28 GhAGC genes, 4 members contained only one exon (Fig. 4). Moreover, presence of similar kind of motifs among all studied genes of AGC family indicated the conservativeness of this family in cotton (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 5).

GhAGC localization on chromosomes, Collinearity Analysis, Gene Duplication and Ka/Ks ratio

Upland cotton is studied as a reference species to study natural polyploidy [38]. The identification of paralogous gene pairs was carried out in G. hirsutum to investigate the locus associations among GhAGC genes (Fig. 6). The GhAGC genes were present in three groups on different chromosomes. The group 1 genes were present on chromosome A01, A05, A07, A09, D01, D04, D05 and D09, followed by group 2 genes on chromosome A04, A05, A09, A11, D04, D08, D09 and D11. Similarly, group 3 GhAGC genes were localized on chromosome A01, A04, A06, A08, A13, D01, D05 and D13. Most of the genes were localized on the terminals of the chromosome and chromosomes A05 and D05 had the highest number of genes. The chromosome A06 and A08 contained the largest length but had the least number of genes. A significant converseness among the gene loci of GhAGC family was observed (Fig. 7). The synteny analysis between G. hirsutum, G. arboretum and G. raimondii revealed that these species have conserved regions. The results showed that there is strong conservation of some gene loci between G. hirsutum, G. arboretum and G. raimondii. A set of 12 paralogous gene pairs in G. hirsutum were confirmed through analysis, and all experienced segmental duplications events. To further estimate the magnitude and type of selection as per Darwinian theory, we generated Ka/Ks ratios through the events of duplication in genes. It was examined that all 12 duplicated gene pairs showed Ka/Ks values < 0.5 (Supplementary Table S6), and no pair showed Ka/Ks value > 1. The genes with neutral selection will have Ka/Ks values of 1, positive selection with a value > 1 and negative selection with value < 1. The negative selection of duplicated gene will lead to purifying selection behavior. All GhAGC genes under duplication showed Ka/Ks values less than 1. It can be concluded that genes undergone the phenomenon of purifying selection under the influence of segmental duplication.

Expression analysis of GhAGC genes under abiotic stress conditions

The FPKM TM-1 for AGC protein kinase genes was downloaded from the CottonFGD database and heatmap of cotton AGC protein Kinases was developed under abiotic stresses conditions, as well as for different developmental stages (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 7). Extracted data showed that GhAGC2, GhAGC3, GhAGC5, GhAGC9, GhAGC10, GhAGC23 and GhAGC24 were highly expressed under high temperature conditions. The expressions of GhAGC3, GhAGC9, GhAGC22, GhAGC23, and GhAGC24 are also high under salt conditions. The expressions of GhAGC2, GhAGC3, GhAGC5, GhAGC9, GhAGC22, GhAGC23, and GhAGC24 are high under drought conditions. In general, GhAGC2, GhAGC3, GhAGC5, GhAGC9, GhAGC10, GhAGC23, and GhAGC24 were upregulated in different conditions of heat, drought, salt, fiber. Based on FPKM expressions, highly expressed genes under abiotic stress conditions were selected to confirm the results through qRT-PCR. Relative gene expression results also showed similar results and confirmed the up-regulation of candidate genes under abiotic stress conditions (Fig. 9).

Identification of Chloroplast and Mitochondria RNA editing sites

Prep-CP RNA editing site prediction tool was used to examine the RNA editing sites of GhAGC genes in chloroplast. The results showed the presence of 244 RNA editing sites on 28 genes of cotton AGC family in the chloroplast (Supplementary Table 8). The results indicated an average distribution value of 8.7 editing sites per gene. The chloroplast genes GhAGC3 and GhAGC26 carried maximum number of RNA editing sites (12) while GhAGC4 had minimum editing sites (3). Further investigation regarding the position of RNA editing sites exhibited that 156 sites were observed at the first codon base while 74 were based on second codon of chloroplast genes. Most of the amino acids changes were resulted due to the conversion of cytosine to uracil (C-U).

Mitochondrial RNA editing sites prediction using Prep-mt provided the results indicating 339 sites with a distribution average of 12.1 in mitochondrial genes (Supplementary Table 9). The mitochondrial gene GhAGC2 and GhAGC9 carried the maximum number of RNA editing sites (22) and GhAGC6 carried the minimum number of RNA editing sites (10). A total of 263 editing sites in 28 mitochondrial genes were observed at the first codon base and another 155 found at second codon base. The third codon did not carry a single editing site among all genes. The analyses of RAN editing sites in chloroplast and mitochondria confirmed the six types of amino acid changes in the former and 9 types of amino acid changes in the latter. These RNA sites included the shifts of amino acids from Alanine to Valine, Histidine to Tyrosine, Proline to Serine, Threonine to Isoleucine, Proline to Leucine and Arginine to Cysteine respectively.

Discussion

Current trends in major crop productivity will not be enough to meet the needs of the rapidly growing population of the world. It is well known that abiotic stress conditions limit plant growth, development, and production, further threatened by climate change due to more erratic extreme weather events [2]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the novel extreme environmentally responsive factors that significantly enhance plant tolerance to abiotic stress [39–41]. Gene family identification and characterization are pivotal steps in understanding the functional roles of genes within organisms [16–18, 42]. In silico approaches offer a powerful means to sift through vast genomic datasets, employing bioinformatics tools and algorithms to identify homologous gene sequences and predict their potential functions based on sequence similarity and structural features. This computational exploration provides valuable insights into the evolutionary relationships and functional domains of genes within specific families [4, 5]. Integrating these complementary approaches not only validates the predictions made through in silico analyses but also provides a comprehensive understanding of the roles played by gene families in various biological processes [1, 6, 42, 43]. Genome-wide identification in cotton research provides a foundation for understanding the genetic basis of important traits, accelerating gene discovery efforts, facilitating marker-assisted breeding, and advancing our knowledge of cotton genome structure and function. This comprehensive approach is essential for driving advancements in cotton breeding, production, and sustainability in the face of evolving agricultural challenges [4, 43, 44]. Members of the AGC protein family are plant-specific protein kinases contributing in adaptation to environmental stresses. The latest advancement in sequencing technologies have revolutionized the field of crop plant genomics by producing massive amounts of sequencing data widely available [38, 45–51]. The availability of G. hirsutum genome sequence [47, 49] opened new avenues for advanced research at genome level in Gossypium species. Several investigations have been made to investigate the role of AGC genes in various plant species [19–21, 29, 31, 36, 52]. However, no comprehensive study on AGC genes in G. hirsutum has been published so far. In the current work, we investigated AGC genes in domesticated species of cotton (G. hirsutum) to look at the evolutionary linkages, localization on chromosomes, gene duplication and expression analysis of GhAGCs. This research will be useful in setting the groundwork for molecular breeding in cotton.

We found 28 GhAGC genes in the G. hirsutum genome, of which 13 were identified on homologous chromosomes while two genes might have lost their homolog during evolution. In comparison to other comprehensively studied plant AGC genes (12 AGC family genes have been identified in A. thaliana, 19 in G. arboretum and G. herbaceum, 20 in G. raimondii, 29 in G. barbadense and G. mustelinum, 26 in G. tomentosum, 28 in G. darwinii 11 in T. cacao, 15 in P. patens, 14 in C. reinhardtii, 24 in S. moellendorffii and 13 in C. capsularis). The GhAGC family is the largest with 28 phylogenetically enlarged genes. The remarkable growth and diversification of the GhAGC family implies that these GhAGCs serve critical roles in environmental adaptation and physiological maintenance of G. hirsutum, allowing it to tolerate stressful environments.

Phylogenetic and comparative genomic studies are commonly used to explore about the ancestry relationships of genes or species [4, 39–41, 53–57]. We built a rooted phylogenetic tree from an alignment of the full-length protein sequences to further examine GhAGCs evolutionary connections with similar genes from different plant species related to genus Gossypium. The phylogenetic analysis divided the GhAGC family members into six different groups. The orthologous genes from T. cacao, G. herbaceum, G. darwini and S. moellendorfii clustered in the same group with G. hirsutum depicting the evolution of AGC genes before the divergence of monocots and dicots. The groups a, e and f represent the most primitive members of AGC genes widely diversified with other species whereas the group b, c and d contained the smaller number of GhAGC genes of ancient plants in comparison with other species. The presence of AGC genes in C. reinhardtii (chlorophyte) and S. moellendorffii (lycophyte) clearly indicated its lineage connection with oldest known organisms. The phylostratum analyses indicated that AGC genes have their origin from early land plants and potential orthologs AGC genes are present throughout the kingdom plantae. The G. hirsutum, with a relatively higher number of AGC gene members as compared with ancient organisms, i.e., C. reinhardtii and in S. moellendorffii demonstrated the expansion events of AGC genes in higher plants and in cotton. Moreover, T. cacao and G. hirsutum showed close relationship, strengthening the concept that cacao and cotton share common predecessor [58]. The results of sequence logos showing the conserveness of AGC genes on C and N terminals is the proof that this family is conserved in cotton and studied species. These results could lay a foundation for studies of evolutionary patterns of AGC gene family in other species members of kingdom plantae. During evolutionary process, function-based divergence can lead to gene pairs towards non-functionalization, sub-functionalization, or neo-functionalization due to mutations in the gene transcript sequence; ultimately adding the genes in genome [58, 59].

The variation in the length of introns was found across 28 GhAGC genes and may be useful in further studies of the evolution process of this gene family. The duplication/insertion and/or deletion event could be the reasons behind these structural difference of genes [38, 49, 51]. The duplication events during the evolution had contributed to the form of new genes for various factors in crop improvement [4, 60]. Surprisingly, several of the genes in the same evolutionary groupings shared comparable motif composition and exon/intron organization. This analysis depicted the presence of motifs and their distribution in all studied genes. This showed similar patterns of gene functions within the same subfamily or family [59]. The data presented here supports the identification and characterization of cotton-based GhAGC-specific genes, which may help us better understand how these genes contribute to plant resilience, development, and growth.

Gene expression patterns can give crucial insights in programming of gene functions and are strongly associated with promoter divergence. Cis-acting regulatory elements found in gene promoter regions serve to play critical roles in regulating gene expression during development and in harsh environments. In GhAGC genes, there were different stress related elements present including MeJA-R, ABA-R, LR, DS-R, LT-R, AUX-R, MYB-DI, SA-R, MYB-LR, LR, SR & LIG-R motifs with varying numbers. There are multiple reports highlighting the mentioned motifs significance for abiotic stress tolerance in crop plants through hormonal pathways. Numerous studies have revealed that GhAGC’s are implicated in a variety of development biotic and abiotic stress responses [28–32, 37, 61]. For this study, we looked at the dynamic transcriptional alterations of GhAGC genes in response to heat, drought, and salt stresses. We utilized the expressional data available at CottonFGD to elucidate the expression pattern of different GhAGC genes under different conditions. The results of transcriptome and qRTPCR revealed that GhAGC2, GhAGC3, GhAGC5, GhAGC9, GhAGC10, GhAGC22, and GhAGC23 were found to be candidate genes showing good expression under different stress conditions, while GhAGC8 was the least expressing gene.

The study of RNA editing site in the multi-cellular vascular plant organelle genomes is significantly important for regulation of gene expression at post-transcriptional level. In present scientific study, RNA editing sites in G. hirsutum organelles including chloroplast and mitochondria for all 28 AGC genes were calculated using the Prep-CP and Prep-Mt tools. The data showed a total distribution of 244 chloroplast RNA editing sites and 339 mitochondrial among all 28 AGC genes. The replacement of cytosine (C) with uracil (U) was the case in most of the editing sites and occurred on the second codon base, followed by the first codon base with no conversion on third codon base. The current study paved the way for future research on cotton’s chloroplast and mitochondrial RNA editing processes.

Chromosomal localization analysis is a fundamental aspect of cotton genomics research, essential for understanding the organization and structure of the cotton genome. This process involves mapping identified genes and genetic elements to their respective chromosomal locations, thereby providing crucial insights into genome organization, evolution, and function [43, 62]. It facilitates the identification of gene clusters, syntenic regions, and genomic hotspots associated with specific traits or functional categories. Moreover, understanding the distribution of genes across chromosomes aids in elucidating genome evolution, gene duplication events, and regulatory mechanisms underlying complex traits in cotton [4, 18, 63].

Synteny analysis involves comparing the genomic arrangements and gene orders between related species or within different regions of the same genome. By identifying conserved syntenic blocks, researchers can infer evolutionary relationships, gene duplication events, and genome rearrangements. Synteny analysis is particularly important for understanding the evolution of cotton genomes and for transferring genetic information from model species to cotton crops. Comparative genomics tools and algorithms are used to perform synteny analysis [4, 62, 63].

Gene duplication is required for genomic development, and re-arrangements [64] and it might be segmental or tandem duplications [65, 66]. In this analysis, all the GhAGC gene pairs showed segmental duplications. Polyploidy event occurred during the evolution of flowering plants which helped them to adapt in new environment [60, 67]. The number of AGC genes in G. hirsutum is higher as compared to G. arboretum and G. herbaceum. However, gene loss always takes place after hybridization events, during the process of enhanced arrangement in the genomic sequences and chromosomes doubling. In comparison with Brassica napus and Zea mays, the genome cotton experienced minor changes. The Ka/Ks ratios indicated that GhAGC genes had been subjected to intense selection pressure, with only minor functional deviations resulting from segmental duplication. In general, Ka/Ks < 1 indicates purifying selection, Ka/Ks > 1 indicates positive selection, and Ka/Ks = 1 indicates neutral evolution [4, 59, 68]. GhAGC genes were found to be equally distributed across cotton chromosomes. Furthermore, a greater proportion of GhAGC genes were found in replicated blocks, indicating that duplication during evolution considerably increased the size of the GhAGC gene family.

Materials and methods

Sequence Retrieval of AGC protein Kinases Family members

AtAGC protein kinases were used as reference to extract AGC members from Cotton FGD (https://cottonfgd.org). Hidden Markov Model (HMM) was used to confirm the extracted GhAGC protein kinases in G. hirsutum. The AGC members were also retrieved from the targeted species including G. arboretum, G. herbaceum, G. raimondii, G. tomentosum, G. barbadense, G. mustelinum, G. hirsutum, G. darwinii, Theobroma cacao, Physcomitrella patens, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Selaginella moellendorffii, Corchorus capsularis. SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [69] and Interproscan 63.0 (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/InterProScan/) [70] were used to verify the extracted sequences. Biophysical properties including molecular weight, charge, iso-electric point and gravity of the GhAGC protein kinases genes were investigated using EnsemblPlants (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) and ExPASy (http://us.expasy.org/tools/protparam.html). Cello V.2.0 [71] was used to confirm the sub cellular localization of genes in the cell.

Construction of sequence logos and phylogeny analysis of GhAGC genes

TBtools [72] was used to construct Sequence Logos using G. hirsutum, G. arboreum, G. raimondii and Arabidopsis thaliana. The multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was executed using ClustalW program followed by construction of phylogenetic tree using Neighbor-joining method in MEGA V.7 [73].

Promoter, protein motifs, and Gene Structure Analysis

PlantCARE [74] was used to conduct cis-element analysis. For the retrieval of cis regulatory elements from the promoter region, 2Kb upstream region of GhAGC was extracted from CottonFGD. MEME tool (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) was utilized to explore the protein motifs as followed by [75, 76]. The default parameters of the suite were confirmed except a few like (i) motif occurrence marked 1 per sequence (ii) 12–65 residues in the motif width (iii) 10 for number of motifs (iv) 5 minimum number of sites of motif. The MEME.xml or mast.xml file was extracted after following set protocol. This file was then processed in TBtools to draw the motif structure. The coding and genomics sequences were retrieved from CottonFGD and added to online Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org) and gene structure displaying the introns and exons was constructed [77].

GhAGC localization on chromosomes, Collinearity Analysis, Gene Duplication, and ratio of Ka/Ks

The genomic coordinates of GhAGC members were extracted from CottonFGD (https://cottonfgd.net/). CRI assembly of G. hirsutum genome was used for mapping the positions of GhAGC genes on different chromosomes using MapChart v2.32 [78]. For collinearity analysis, the retrieval of GhAGC paralogs and orthologues followed by graphical representation through CIRCOS package [79]. The duplicated proteins were confirmed using CLUSTAL X 2.0 [80] and codon alignment was made through PAL2NAL program [81]. Non-synonyms (Ka) and synonyms (Ks) values were calculated using CODMEL program and PAML package [82]. The whole genome sequence files, and gene annotation files (gff3) of G. hirsutum, G. arboretum and G. raimondii were used for the collinearity analysis. The TBtools was used for visualization of the results.

In-silico expression analysis of GhAGC genes and RNA editing sites in Chloroplast and Mitochondria

RNA-seq data of GhAGC under drought, high temperature and salinity was retrieved from CottonFGD (https://cottonfgd.net/) under project available at NCBI Geodataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA490626). The G. hirsutum line named as Texas Marker 1 (TM-1) was used as experimental material. A heat map indicating the levels of expression of GhAGC members was developed. Fiber specific expression data was also retrieved for all studied GhAGC members. The cotton chloroplast RNA editing sites were explored using Prep-CP while mitochondrial RNA editing sites were explored using Prep-Mt software (http://prep.unl.edu/) [83]. The cut-off value was set at 0.8 in both cases.

Expression studies and functional validation of candidate genes

The relative expression pattern of selected candidate GhAGCs was estimated under salt, drought, and heat conditions through qPCR. Twenty days old seedlings of cotton accession TM-1 were subjected to salt (NaCl-300 mM), drought (PEG-20% mass fraction) and heat (42 ºC) conditions separately. All plants were subjected to stress conditions for 72 h in completely randomized design with three replicates. The samples were collected for RNA extraction and cDNA was synthesized. The GhActin was used as housekeeping gene during the expression studies. A pre-incubation at 95 ºC for 3 min, followed by 95 ºC for 10 s and 60 ºC for 30 s with 45 PCR cycles was set for the experiment. Extension step was not added as total fragment lengths of all primer pairs was less than 200 bp. The 2-ΔΔCT formula was used to determine the relative gene expression [84]. The list of used primers is available in Supplementary Table S1.

Conclusions

Current research provided nine potential genes that have a significant role in abiotic stress tolerance in cotton. GhAGC5, GhAGC9, GhAGC23 and GhAGC24 showed a significant high transcript abusndance under drought stress while higher level of expression was examined in GhAGC2, GhAGC10, GhAGC24 and GhAGC28 under salt stress. GhAGC2, GhAGC9 and GhAGC24 have higher gene expression under heat stress. The positive role and identification of potential genes may help to strength the cotton breeding program and development of high yielding climate smart cotton varieties in changing climate.
