Cell cycle follows ‘pause and play’ mechanism in salt and cold stress recovery in diverse plant species
Authors: Olivia S. Hazelwood, Kamryn A. Diehl, Vicky Hollenbeck, Joh Demura‐Devore, Dustin Herb, Joseph P. Gallagher, M. Arif Ashraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Changes to organismal growth induced by environmental stress are orchestrated at the cellular level. These periods of stress may be followed by recovery periods, when plants have the opportunity to return to normal growth conditions.However, the cell cycle mechanisms underlying recovery are poorly understood. We tested the cell cycle regulation in roots during control, stress, and recovery period for salt, osmotic, cold, and heat stresses using Arabidopsis thaliana, Brachypodium distachyon, and Lolium multiflorum.During the salt and cold stress conditions, the cell cycle pauses at gap phase and is released from gap phase during stress recovery, which depends on cell cycle regulators CDKA;1 and ICK1.Cold stress and recovery, which affect cell division only, follow a conserved ‘pause and play’ mechanism of the cell cycle.

Introduction

Environmental stresses, such as salinity, drought, cold, and heat, alter plant growth and development (Zhu, 2016; Zhang et al., 2022). Plant growth is a combination of cell division, cell elongation, and cell differentiation (Rahman et al., 2007; Ashraf & Rahman, 2019). The reduction in growth during environmental stress response alters either the cell division, cell elongation, or both (Martins et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2017; Ashraf & Rahman, 2019; Ashraf et al., 2021; Zimmermann et al., 2022, 2024; Ai et al., 2023; Hazelwood et al., 2025; Jacob et al., 2025). How distinct environmental stresses target either cell division or elongation is still being unraveled. During the environmental stress, plant root plays a pivotal role in perceiving the stress signal and integrating the external cues with the developmental circuit. For instance, the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana root almost stops growing under low temperature stress (4°C; Ashraf & Rahman, 2019). This cold‐induced root growth inhibition happens due to the reduction of cell division, not cell elongation (Ashraf & Rahman, 2019). This example highlights the integration of environmental stress into the regulation of cell division or elongation during root development.

Stress adaptation relies not only on survival during the stress period, but also on a plant's capacity to recover after stressful conditions (Illouz‐Eliaz et al., 2025; Van Hautegem et al., 2025). The success of plants' stress recovery ensures crop productivity at the end of the growing season. Recently, plants' stress recovery was explored in leaf tissue using Arabidopsis and maize (Illouz‐Eliaz et al., 2025; Van Hautegem et al., 2025), but the root tissue during the stress recovery period remains mostly unexplored. Plant growth during stress and stress recovery is orchestrated at the cellular level and manifests at the tissue, organ, and whole plant level. Cell cycle regulators control the cellular circuits important for both cell division and elongation and switch between these core functions and integrating environmental cues (Shimotohno et al., 2021). Despite the importance of core cell cycle regulators during plant development and environmental response, we have limited knowledge about the cell cycle regulation during distinct environmental stress and recovery conditions.

A comparative genetics and cell biology approach for a wide range of environmental stresses and recovery could illustrate a fundamental mechanism important for plant adaptation and, more broadly, an adaptive stress response for organisms. In this study, we initially tested the effects of four environmental stresses (salt, osmotic, cold, and heat) and subsequent recovery using Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis), Brachypodium distachyon (Brachypodium), and Lolium multiflorum (annual ryegrass). Our findings suggest that salt and cold stresses cause a ‘pause’ at gap phase during stress conditions and then resume or ‘play’ the cell cycle during the recovery period. As a result, we emphasize understanding the cellular and molecular response to cold and salt stress and recovery. Between salt and cold stress, the former inhibits both cell division and elongation; the latter inhibits only cell division. Inhibition of the core cell cycle regulator CYCLIN‐DEPENDENT KINASE A;1 (CDKA;1), important for both G1 to S and G2 to M transitions, through a chemical genetic approach and overexpression of the cell cycle inhibitor ICK1/KRP1 (INHIBITOR OF CDK 1/KIP‐RELATED PROTEIN 1) prevents salt and cold stress recovery. Altogether, we discovered a crucial cell cycle circuit that plays an important role in salt and cold stress and recovery that is conserved across distantly related flowering plant species.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials

Arabidopsis thaliana accession Columbia (Col‐0), Brachypodium distachyon Bd21‐3, and Lolium multiflorum ‘Gulf’ were used as wild‐types for experiments. The following lines were previously described: Cytrap (Yin et al., 2014), pAtPCNA1::AtPCNA1‐sGFP (Yokoyama et al., 2016), CDKA;1‐mVenus/cdka;1 (Yang et al., 2020), 35S::GFP‐ICK1 (Zhou et al., 2003), and proCYCLINA3;2‐GUS (NASC, Stock no.: N799894).

Growth conditions

For Arabidopsis, seeds were surface sterilized with 1 ml 70% ethanol for 10 min, washed twice with 1 ml autoclaved MQ water inside of the laminar flow hood, and placed on the surface of ½‐strength Murashige and Skoog (½MS) media (cat. no.: 30 630 058; BioWorld) containing 1% sucrose (cat. no.: 41 900 152; BioWorld, Dublin, OH, USA) and 1% agar (cat. no.: 40 100 072; BioWorld) in square Petri dishes (cat. no.: 26‐275; Simport). Plate openings were sealed with micropore tapes (Amazon, Seattle, WA, USA; 3M A‐1530‐0 Micropore Surgical Tape, ½ Inch), covered with aluminum foil, and kept at 4°C for 2 d. After 2 d, plates were placed vertically under constant light conditions at 22°C.

For B. distachyon, seeds were surface sterilized with 0.6% NaOCl, 0.1% Tween‐20 for 7 min shaking at 200 rpm, washed three times with autoclaved E‐pure water inside of a laminar flow hood, and left to soak at 4°C for 7 d. Imbibed seeds were placed on the surface of ½MS media containing 1% sucrose and 1% agar. Plates were sealed with parafilm and oriented vertically under constant light at 22°C.

For L. multiflorum, seeds were dehusked in 50% sulfuric acid for 15 min, shaking at 200 rpm. Dehusked seeds were rinsed with 500‐ml autoclaved E‐pure water in a sterile Gooch crucible in the laminar flow hood. Then, seeds were sterilized with 3.75% NaOCl, 0.1% Tween‐20 for 20 min shaking at 200 rpm, rinsed with 500 ml autoclaved E‐pure water in a sterile Gooch crucible in the laminar flow hood, and placed on the surface of ½MS media containing 1% sucrose and 1% agar. Plates were sealed with parafilm and oriented vertically under constant light at 22°C.

Stress and stress recovery conditions

Stress and recovery conditions and timeline are summarized in Supporting Information Fig. S1. For the Arabidopsis salt and osmotic stresses, 100 mM NaCl and 300 mM mannitol‐containing ½MS plates were used, respectively, to incubate seedlings for a 2‐d period. Cold and heat stresses were performed using 4°C and 29°C chambers, respectively, for a 1‐d period. After the salt and osmotic stress period, seedlings were transferred to control media for 2 d for the recovery period. After cold and heat stresses, seedlings were returned to control growth temperature for a 1‐d recovery period. Standard Arabidopsis growth conditions were maintained in a growth shelf with constant light conditions.

Two days after germination (DAG), Brachypodium and annual ryegrass seedlings were incubated in stress conditions for 48 h. Seedlings in control conditions were incubated on ½MS media plates at 22°C under constant light; seedlings under salt and osmotic stress conditions were incubated on 200 mM NaCl and 300 mM Mannitol, respectively, on ½MS media plates under control temperature and light conditions. Seedlings under cold and heat stress conditions were incubated at 4°C and 30°C, respectively, in control media and light conditions. After stress conditions, seedlings were moved to new ½MS media plates at 22°C under constant light for 48 h for stress recovery. All experiments took place in Percival or Conviron growth chambers.

Phenotypic image analysis

Images of Arabidopsis, Brachypodium, and annual ryegrass root growth were obtained using the Epson Perfection V600 Photo scanner (v.3.9.3.1) at 300 dpi in TIFF format. Root length was measured using the open source ImageJ/Fiji software (Schindelin et al., 2012). Root length was recorded prestress, poststress, and postrecovery. The growth during the stress period is calculated as the root length at the end of the stress period (‘poststress’) minus the root length at the beginning of the stress period (‘prestress’). The growth during the recovery period is calculated as the root length after the plants had been returned to the control conditions for the described amount of time (‘postrecovery’) minus the root length at the end of the stress period (‘poststress’).

Drug treatments

Olomoucine (cat. no.: 10 010 240; Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide and incorporated into ½MS media at various concentrations for dose response assay.

For olomoucine recovery inhibition experiments, seedlings and stress conditions were the same as described for stress and recovery experiments above. For the recovery period, seedlings were either placed on control ½MS media (−olomoucine) or ½MS media with 15 μg ml−1 olomoucine (+olomoucine). Images and measurements were taken as described above.

Microscopic imaging

The cell cycle regulation during the stress, and recovery periods for the salt stress, cold stress, and control conditions was measured through the cell cycle marker, Cytrap, which indicates S/G2 and G2/M states of the cell cycle using pHTR2::CDT1a (C3)‐RFP and pCYCB1::CYCB1‐GFP, respectively ( 15 ). The G1, S, and G2 phase of the cell cycle were visualized using pAtPCNA1::AtPCNA1‐sGFP ( 17 ) during the stress and recovery periods for the salt stress and control conditions. Fluorescent images of live Arabidopsis root meristem were imaged using a Nikon Ti‐E‐PFS inverted spinning‐disk confocal microscope equipped with ×20 (Cytrap) and ×40 silicone oil immersion (pAtPCNA1::AtPCNA1‐sGFP) objectives. The system is outfitted with a Yokogawa CSU‐X1 spinning‐disk unit, a self‐contained four‐line laser module (excitation at 405, 488, 561, and 640 nm), and an Andor iXon 897 EMCCD camera. The 488 nm and 561 nm excitation were used for Green fluorescent protein (GFP) and Red fluorescent protein (RFP), respectively. Fluorescent images were acquired using the Nikon Nis‐Elements software and processed using the ImageJ software (imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Cells in both the longitudinal (meristematic region) and radial (the entire Z axis) dimensions of the root were counted for both Cytrap and pAtPCNA1::AtPCNA1‐sGFP roots.

CDKA;1‐mVenus images were acquired using an Olympus IXplore SpinSR system (Evident, Tokyo, Japan), equipped with an inverted microscope (IX83; Evident), a CSUW1‐Sora spinning‐disk confocal unit (Yokogawa, Japan), and a Hamamatsu ORCA Fusion BT camera. Fluorescence images were obtained with a ×20 objective (UPLXAPO 20X/0.8NA), and fluorescent signals from CDKA; 1‐mVenus were detected with 514 nm excitation and 500–550 nm emission. Fluorescence confocal images were acquired using the software cellSens (Evident).

RNA‐seq

RNA Isolation was performed using Arabidopsis Col‐0 roots under stress and stress recovery conditions using Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (cat. no.: 74 904). Two sets of controls were collected because of differences in timing of salt and cold treatments (Table S1). The extracted RNA concentration and quality was analyzed using Thermo Scientific NanoDrop One (cat. no.: 13‐400‐518). A total of 32 RNA samples (4 treatments (control, salt, control 2 (corresponding with cold treatment timing), and cold), × 2 time points (poststress and postrecovery) × 4 biological replicates) were used. Poly‐A enriched RNA‐seq libraries were prepared by using Vazyme VAHTS Universal V10 RNA‐seq Library Prep Kit and sequenced at 150‐bp PE on an Element AVITI instrument by AmpSeq (Gaithersburg, MD, USA) (https://www.ampseq.com/). Reads were filtered with fastp v.0.23.1 (Chen et al., 2018), mapped with Star v.2.7.11 to the TAIR10 Arabidopsis genome (Swarbreck et al., 2008; Dobin et al., 2013), and quantified with Salmon v.1.10.2 (Patro et al., 2017). Four samples were outliers and excluded from analysis. Differential gene expression was called at false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 with package DESeq2 v.1.44.0 in R (Love et al., 2014; ‘R: The R project for statistical computing’) and visualized with package UpsetR (Conway et al., 2017) and pheatmap v.1.0.12 (Kolde, 2025). Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment was called using topGo (https://bioc.r-universe.dev/topGO/doc/manual.html), terms were reduced using rrvgo (Sayols, 2023), and the top 3 Biological Processes (BP) GO terms with dispensability scores below 0.05 in each DE comparison were visualized according to previously published methods (Bonnot et al., 2019). R package wgcna was used to build a signed gene co‐expression network from gene expression data normalized using the rlog function of DESeq2 (Langfelder & Horvath, 2008; Love et al., 2014). Network power was set to 16 (based on scale‐free topology fit index), and minimum module size was set to 30.

Beta‐glucuronidase staining

Beta‐glucuronidase (GUS) staining was performed based on a previously described method (Ashraf & Rahman, 2019). proCYCLINA3;2‐GUS seedlings were transferred to GUS staining buffer (100 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 0.5 mM potassium ferrocyanide and 0.1% Triton X‐100) containing 1 mM X‐gluc, and incubated at 37°C in the dark for 2 h. The roots were imaged with a 10× objective lens attached to the AmScope T490 Series Simul‐Focal Biological Trinocular Compound Microscope and images were captured using an 18MP USB 3.0 C‐mount Camera and AmScope software.

Statistical analysis

The raw data for each quantification were imported to the statistical software Jmp Pro17 for generating graphs and performing statistical tests.

Results

Distinctive root growth response during environmental stress and stress recovery response in plants

We investigated the root growth response of Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) during environmental stresses and recovery periods. We measured primary root length of Arabidopsis seedling roots during both a period of environmental stress (‘stress period’) and a subsequent period when plants were returned to control conditions (‘recovery period’; Figs 1a–d, S1–S3). Over the stress period, we observed c. 40%, c. 50%, and 80% primary root growth inhibition for salt stress (48 h), osmotic (mannitol) stress (48 h), and cold (4°C) stress (24 h), respectively, and 10% acceleration of primary root growth during heat (29°C) stress (24 h; Figs 1a–d, S2). These patterns of primary root growth inhibition and acceleration align with previous reports of root growth under stress in Arabidopsis and other plants (West et al., 2004; Skirycz et al., 2011; Hanzawa et al., 2013; Ashraf & Rahman, 2019; Takahashi et al., 2019; Parveen & Rahman, 2021; Ai et al., 2023; Van Hautegem et al., 2025).

When Arabidopsis plants were allowed to recover for an equal amount of time as the stress period, root growth rate recovers from salt and cold stress (Fig. 1a–d) yet fails to recover from osmotic and heat stresses (Fig. S2). Within 48 h of transfer from the salt to control plates, Arabidopsis roots grow at a similar rate to the control (Fig. 1a,b). Similarly, cold‐treated Arabidopsis roots require only 24 h to recover to 90% of the growth rate as the control (Fig. 1c,d).

Given the importance of abiotic stress recovery in plants, we hypothesized that this recovery potential may be conserved beyond Arabidopsis. We tested the effects of salt, osmotic, heat, and cold stress and recovery in two grasses – Brachypodium (Brachypodium distachyon) and annual ryegrass (Lolium multiflorum). While root growth was more variable within these two species, root growth rates still recover in Brachypodium and annual ryegrass during the salt and cold stress recovery periods, although only the increase in root growth in the cold stress recovery period was significant (Figs 1e–h, S3). In Brachypodium, the recovery from cold stress (c. 4 fold) is greater than salt stress recovery (c. twofold) compared with the control (Fig. 1e,f), while in annual ryegrass, a similar salt (c. 1.5‐fold) and cold (c. 1.5‐fold) stress recovery pattern was observed compared with the control (Fig. 1g,h). Compared with salt and cold stress recovery, the osmotic and heat stress recovery pattern is not shared across species (Fig. S3). Because of differences in root architecture across these three divergent species, a conserved cellular mechanism seems highly likely to control these changes in growth under stress and recovery.

Cell cycle response during salt and cold stress recovery

The visible root growth response during the stress and recovery periods happens through a combination of cell division and elongation (Rahman et al., 2007; Ashraf & Rahman, 2019; Ashraf et al., 2021). Cell division relies on a tightly regulated cell cycle, consisting of G1 (gap phase 1), S (DNA synthesis phase), G2 (gap phase 2), and M (mitosis). Molecular markers in plants have been used for both visualization and quantification of cell cycle phases (Colón‐Carmona et al., 1999; Yin et al., 2014; Yokoyama et al., 2016; Desvoyes et al., 2020; Hazelwood & Ashraf, 2024). In Arabidopsis, we used the cell cycle marker Cytrap (indicates S/G2 and G2/M phases) to quantify the cell cycle status, and we measured epidermal cell length to investigate cell elongation. Both salt and osmotic stresses have reduced cell division activity and cell elongation, paralleling previous observations of reduced CYCB1;1‐GUS activity during salt stress in Arabidopsis root (West et al., 2004; Figs 2a,b, S4, S6). During the recovery period, both cell division and elongation, and consequently root growth, recovers from salt stress, but not for osmotic stress (Figs 2a–c, S4, S6). This work confirms the importance of cell division activity as a key factor for growth revival during the postosmotic stress rehydration stage (Skirycz et al., 2011; Van Hautegem et al., 2025).

Previously, we demonstrated that decreased root growth in cold stress is due to a decrease in G2/M phase cell cycle progression, and not due to a decrease in cell elongation (Ashraf & Rahman, 2019). Here, we confirmed our previous findings, but we also observed that, during the recovery from cold stress, the cell cycle resumes progression through the G2/M phase (Fig. 2c,d). Compared with other stress conditions, heat stressed plants demonstrate an accelerated cell cycle during heat stress; however, their cell cycle progression falls below control conditions during the recovery period (Figs S5, S6). Altogether, our data suggest that salt and cold stress recovery for root growth is tightly correlated with the cell cycle regulation in a similar pattern (Figs 2a–d, S7).

We further tested this concept using PCNA1‐sGFP (highlights gap phase, early and late S phase) during salt stress and recovery period (Yokoyama et al., 2016). Similarly, under salt stress, more cells were observed in gap phase, yet these cells resumed normal cell cycle patterns during the salt stress recovery period (Fig. S8). In Brachypodium, a similar pattern of reduced mitotic activity is observed due to salt stress (Wolny et al., 2021). This quantitative cell biology approach during the stress and recovery periods led us to conclude that the cell cycle follows a ‘pause and play’ mechanism during the salt and cold stress recovery. While salt stress, but not cold stress, inhibits cell elongation, both stresses inhibit progression through the cell cycle, that is a ‘pause’ (Fig. S7). When the plants are allowed to recover, the cell cycle returns to normal progression; that is a ‘play’.

Salt and cold stress recovery require intact G1 to S and G2 to M transitions

The core cell cycle genes are primarily divided into three major categories – cyclins, cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs), and inhibitors (ICKs/KRPs; Cheng et al., 2015; Shimotohno et al., 2021). Cyclins, CDKs, and ICKs act in the cell cycle in a phase‐dependent manner. Relying on the existing literature to classify the role of Arabidopsis core cell cycle proteins during G1 to S and G2 to M transitions (Shimotohno et al., 2021), we identified one CDK (CDKA;1) and two cyclins (CYCD1;1 and CYCD4;1) involved in both G1 to S and G2 to M transitions (Fig. S9). Cyclins express transiently during specific cell cycle phases and degrade after that period. By contrast, CDKs remain stable during the cell cycle phase. As a result, we evaluated the role of CDKA;1 to block both G1 to S and G2 to M transitions. Unfortunately, cdka;1 homozygous mutant causes a defect during the male gametophyte development (Iwakawa et al., 2006) or it is almost impossible to have a properly developed seedling (Nowack et al., 2012).

To circumvent this issue, we took a chemical genetic approach using chemicals that inhibit CDKs in plant and nonplant systems (Glab et al., 1994; Alessi et al., 1998). We evaluated olomoucine and two related drugs, Olomoucine II and iso‐olomoucine (Fig. S10). Among these three drugs, Olomoucine II causes root twisting at a 5 μg ml−1 concentration as an additional phenotype and iso‐olomoucine does not inhibit Arabidopsis root growth even at a higher concentration (50 μg ml−1; Fig. S10c–f). Olomoucine inhibited root growth without any secondary phenotypes (Fig. S10a,b). As a result, we decided on olomoucine as the most reliable drug to use and identified a critical concentration for olomoucine to inhibit c. 50% root growth in Arabidopsis (15 μg ml−1) for use in experiments (Fig. S10b). We first tested olomoucine with cell cycle marker lines, Cytrap and PCNA1‐sGFP (Figs S11, S12). In the presence of olomoucine, both Cytrap and PCNA1‐sGFP demonstrated a slowdown of cell cycle progression, as expected (Figs S11, S12). Furthermore, when these cell cycle markers were transferred to a plate without olomoucine, the cell cycle progression resumed (Figs S11, S12).

We also evaluated olomoucine's specificity by studying its interaction with CDKA;1 in silico. The AlphaFold3 predicted structure of CDKA;1 (pLDDT = 90) was subjected to molecular dynamics simulations in the presence of either ATP or olomoucine to assess whether CDKA;1's ATP‐binding site exhibits nonspecific affinity for other adenine‐based ligands like olomoucine. Olomoucine binds to the same cleft as ATP in CDKA;1 (Figs 3a, S13; Videos S1 and S2; Holcakova et al., 2010; Sako et al., 2017). Additionally, molecular docking simulations predicted binding affinities (Kd) of 4.3 μM and 1.3 μM with ATP and olomoucine, respectively. This indicates that olomoucine binds more strongly to CDKA;1's ATP‐binding site and may be able to out‐compete ATP at lower concentrations.

Since ATP binding with CDKA;1 happens in a reversible manner, we hypothesized that CDKA;1 inhibition caused by olomoucine may also act in a reversible manner. We tested this possibility by treating the CDKA;1‐mVenus/cdka;1 transgenic line with olomoucine. Interestingly, the CDKA;1‐mVenus signal decreases dramatically after the olomoucine treatment and the signal re‐appears after a transfer to a control plate during the recovery period (Fig. 3b,c). A previous report suggested that olomoucine and its analog roscovitine not only reduce the G1 to S and G2 to M transition of the cell cycle, but also reduce the protein level of CDK1 (Schutte et al., 1997). Based on our results, olomoucine may also cause a reduction in CDKA;1 protein levels in plant systems. Hampering the cell cycle progression with olomoucine may cause a negative feedback loop to regulate the CDKA;1 protein level. Altogether, our findings and previous reports demonstrate that olomoucine binds specifically and reversibly to CDKA;1, validating its use as a targeted and reversible control for inhibiting CDKA;1 activity in planta.

To assess the role of CDKA;1 in stress recovery, we repeated the salt and cold stress and recovery experiments with and without olomoucine present in the recovery period (Fig. 3d–k). In Arabidopsis, plants treated with olomoucine had their growth reduced by c. 50% and c. 40% during the salt and cold stress recovery periods, respectively (Fig. 3d–g). In Brachypodium and annual ryegrass, we see a similar trend of growth reduction caused by olomoucine in the cold stress recovery period, with the mean root growth response lower in olomoucine treated plants (Fig. 3h–k). Although not significant, these data suggest that cold stress recovery requires intact G1 to S and G2 to M transitions of the cell cycle, similar to Arabidopsis. However, the direction of the salt stress recovery pattern is not shared in Brachypodium and annual ryegrass (Fig. 3h–k). This suggests that cell elongation factors play a larger role in salt stress and recovery in the grasses. Cell cycle regulators are highly conserved across diverse plant species compared with the cell elongation regulation (Sablowski & Gutierrez, 2022). Additionally, root growth in these grass systems has higher variability and distinct root architecture compared with Arabidopsis; these inherent features likely lead to a more variable drug treatment response (Figs 1e–h, 3h–k; Watt et al., 2009; Chochois et al., 2012, 2015). Although olomoucine demonstrates a species‐specific effect during salt and cold stress recovery periods, these data suggest that intact G1 to S and G2 to M transitions are required for cell cycle‐specific cold stress recovery across plant species.

CDKA1‐dependent cell cycle regulation is required during stress recovery

Olomoucine‐mediated CDKA;1 inhibition ensures a break in the G1 to S and G2 to M transitions. But, it is hard to assess whether olomoucine targets any other cell cycle regulators other than CDKA;1. To clarify this, we tried to find an in vivo cell cycle inhibitor of CDKA;1. We checked the previously published root single cell RNA sequencing data and protein interaction data of CDKA;1 (Shimotohno et al., 2021; Shahan et al., 2022; Nolan et al., 2023). ICK1 is one of the cell cycle inhibitors identified as a CDKA;1 interactor (Jakoby et al., 2006). Spatial expression of CDKA;1 (required for G1 to S and G2 to M transitions), CYCB1;1 (G2 to M transition marker gene), and ICK1 demonstrated that both CDKA;1 and CYCB1;1 expression are specifically high in the root apical meristem and reduce in the transitions and elongation zone (Fig. S14). By contrast, ICK1 expression is lower in the root apical meristem region and higher in the transition and elongation zone (Fig. S14). These data clearly suggest a spatial regulation of CDKA;1 and ICK1 expression during root development.

We hypothesized that overexpression of ICK1 will help to inhibit CDKs, including CDKA;1 and consequently block G1 to S and G2 to M (Fig. 4a; Zhou et al., 2003). We tested this hypothesis by subjecting an ICK1 overexpression line to salt and cold stress and recovery. We found that the ICK1 overexpression line recovers more slowly during the salt and cold stress recovery period compared with control conditions (Fig. 4b–e). Combining the results of salt and cold stress recovery in the presence of olomoucine (which inhibits CDKA;1 activity) and in the ICK1 overexpression line (which inhibits CDKA;1 activity), our observations suggest that CDKA;1‐dependent cell cycle regulation is required to maintain proper G1 to S and G2 to M transitions for the salt and cold stress recovery. However, the recovery response is not completely abolished in the presence of olomoucine or with ICK1 overexpression. This suggests the involvement of other cell cycle regulators in the recovery response.

Differential expression of cell cycle regulators during stress and recovery period

To explore which genes might be regulating the observed changes in the cell cycle, we performed transcriptomics of Arabidopsis whole root tissue during salt or cold stress and recovery conditions (Table S1). According to principal components analysis, stress sample gene expressions are typically distinct from control and recovery sample gene expression (Fig. S15). Differential gene expression analysis showed that, compared with salt treated roots, cold stress and cold recovery roots had higher numbers of differentially expressed (DE) genes vs controls (Fig. 5a; Table S2). When comparing between recovery and stress time points within treatments, a very low number of DE genes were identified between stress controls and recovery controls. Cold‐treated plants had more than 10 000 DE genes between stress and recovery, while salt treated plants had fewer than 900 DE genes (Fig. 5b; Table S2). We evaluated which BP GO terms were enriched in each DE comparison, finding enrichment across metabolic processes, cellular processes, and response to stress, among others (Fig. 5c). Genes upregulated in the cold recovery vs control and the cold recovery vs cold stress were enriched for ‘cell cycle’ BP GO term (Fig. 5c). We more closely examined expression of candidate set of cell cycle genes, and found a group of Arabidopsis cell cycle genes is differentially expressed during cold stress and recovery and, to a mild extent, during salt stress and recovery (Figs 5c, S16). CDKA;1 only shows mild DE, as is expected (Joubès et al., 2000), but other CDK‐ and cyclin‐encoding genes show downregulation under cold stress but upregulation under cold recovery (Fig. 5d). To further evaluate gene expression interrelationships, we built a gene co‐expression network with 33 modules of co‐expressed genes (Table S3). Two of these modules (Module 2 and Module 8) had 34 and 15 members with cell cycle functions, respectively. These modules were enriched for BP GO Terms related to the cell cycle and similar processes (FDR < 0.05). The module eigengene expression in these modules shows that either these genes are upregulated in both cold stress and recovery relative to the control (Module 2, Fig. 5e), or are downregulated in just the cold stress compared with the control stress and recovery or the cold recovery (Module 8; Fig. 5e). A third module, Module 10, which showed downregulation of module eigengene expression in both salt stress and cold stress, only had one cell cycle member, but was enriched for mRNA processing and splicing (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 5e). Based on these gene expression data, despite phenotypic similarities, cold‐treated roots have a distinct pattern of gene expression under stress and recovery compared with salt treated roots.

To further validate the observed expression patterns, we performed quantitative real time PCR using Arabidopsis roots to test the gene expression of CYCB1;1, CDKA1, and ICK1 during salt and cold stress, and stress recovery conditions (Fig. S17; Table S4). Interestingly, CYCB1;1 and CDKA1 expressions change during the cold, not salt, stress recovery period, while ICK1 expression remained the same (Fig. S17). This quantitative real‐time PCR data aligns with the bulk RNA sequencing data (Fig. 5d). We further confirmed the specificity of the comparative bulk RNA‐seq data by evaluating proCYCLINA3;2‐GUS expression during cold and salt stress and recovery periods (Fig. 6). During both cold and salt stress, proCYCLINA3;2‐GUS expression is reduced but reverts back to normal during the recovery period (Fig. 6). Among 61 core cell cycle genes, only a limited set, including CYCLINA3;2, are differentially expressed during the stress and recovery period. This result further highlights the importance of core cell cycle genes in cold and salt stress recovery.

Discussion

Our study revealed a cell cycle circuit required for environmental stress and recovery mechanisms conserved across Arabidopsis, Brachypodium, and annual ryegrass, species separated by 160 million years of evolution (Kumar et al., 2022). This circuit relies on the ‘pause and play’ mechanism of the cell cycle during stress and recovery that manifests as root growth (Fig. 7). Stress recovery is an important aspect of stress resilience that has not been widely considered for roots in past studies, although two recent studies explored the stress recovery aspects in leaf tissue (Illouz‐Eliaz et al., 2025; Van Hautegem et al., 2025). Therefore, we studied the cell cycle regulation during both stress and the subsequent recovery period using a wide range of approaches – root growth response, cell cycle markers, comparative transcriptomics, molecular biology, and chemical genetics. This can be a new avenue for studying stress and stress recovery in other plant systems.

The root growth response during distinct environmental stress and recovery is orchestrated at the cellular level, involving core cell cycle regulators. A handful of studies, including our previous finding, suggest that cell cycle regulation is altered during environmental stresses (salt, osmotic, cold, and heat; West et al., 2004; Skirycz et al., 2011; Hanzawa et al., 2013; Ashraf & Rahman, 2019; Takahashi et al., 2019; Parveen & Rahman, 2021; Ai et al., 2023; Van Hautegem et al., 2025). A majority of these studies focused on G2/M phase specific markers or DNA synthesis (S phase; West et al., 2004; Skirycz et al., 2011; Hanzawa et al., 2013; Parveen & Rahman, 2021; Ai et al., 2023). As a result, we lack the complete cell cycle profile during environmental stress conditions. Our study encompasses the entire cell cycle profile in the presence of distinct environmental stresses (salt, osmotic, cold, and heat) and utilizes quantitative cell biology approaches (Figs 2, S2–S7). Additionally, we followed the cell cycle progression during the recovery period. As each cycle phase relies on core cell cycle regulators, it is important to identify important protein(s) required for stress and recovery response. Prior studies, including our own findings, lack a comprehensive view of core cell cycle regulators involved in environmental stress response during stress and recovery (Ashraf & Rahman, 2019). By identifying CDKA1‐ICK1 as a key cell cycle circuit in salt and cold stress response, we have made major progress in our understanding of cell cycle response during environmental stress and recovery.

Even though salt and cold treatment showed similar patterns of cell cycle regulation, comparative transcriptomics revealed that these stress and recovery patterns still have unique gene expression differences (Fig. 5), in line with previous studies (Kreps et al., 2002). In particular, cell cycle genes appear more critical to the cold treatment stress and recovery than salt treatment stress and recovery. This transcriptomic pattern likely reflects the phenotypic response differences between salt and cold treatments: salt treatment affects both cell division and elongation, while cold treatment affects only cell division (Fig. S3). Further dissection of the differences and similarities between salt and cold stress and recovery will reveal how gene expression patterns contribute to each of these distinct phenotypes. Here, whole tissue sampling of roots means that the spatial aspects of gene expression are masked. Future experiments focusing on single cell sequencing data from root meristems during stress and stress recovery periods will provide further understanding of the spatial and temporal regulation of cell cycle regulators. Additionally, development of translational reporter lines of differentially expressed CYCLIN genes (Fig. 5d) will provide further evidence of their role during stress and stress recovery response.

Understanding the whole plant and cellular growth stress response is valuable for breeding and engineering crops with tolerance to environmental stresses. The ideal strategy for this improvement would be to focus on a genetic circuit conserved across plant species. Here, we have found a pathway that is conserved across multiple plant species and thus is likely conserved in other crop species. This mechanism provides a framework for understanding the network of genes conferring salt and cold tolerance to flowering plants, and potentially other eukaryotic species.
