Age-related differences in adjuvant chemotherapy use and outcomes in stage II colon cancer: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qiyang Zhou, Chunxian Zhou, Gang Zhang, Xinyang Xia, Xiaojun Zhou, Jianhua Lang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer remains uncertain, less is known about how age influences its use and associated outcomes in this population.MethodRetrospective cohort study. Patients who underwent radical colon cancer surgery, from 2013 to 2018, divided into age groups; 18–49 (young), 50–64 (middle aged), and 65–75 (older) years. To compare the adjuvant therapy and survival of patients with stage II colon cancer in different age groups.ResultsAt baseline, the young showed fewer comorbidities (16.1% vs. 37.2% vs. 54.9%, p < 0.001) than the middle-aged and older groups, and the pathological results showed worse differentiation types (25.8% vs. 7.4% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.017), but more microsatellite instability-high status (21.51% vs. 9.10% vs.10.57%, p = 0.005). Among patients with stage II colon cancer, young and middle-aged patients were more likely to receive adjuvant chemotherapy [odds ratio (OR): 4.196 (95% confidence interval (CI), 2.247–7.835); 2.610 (95% CI, 1.743–3.910)]. Among patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy, young and middle-aged patients were more likely to receive multi-drug chemotherapy than older patients [OR: 3.181 (95% CI, 1.255–8.064); 2.642 (95% CI, 1.386–5.036)]. There was no significant difference in survival among patients with stage II colon cancer who underwent surgery alone or surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy among different age groups.ConclusionAmong patients with stage II colon cancer, young and middle-aged patients were more likely to receive adjuvant chemotherapy and more aggressive chemotherapy regimens, but this was not associated with survival benefits compared to the older age group.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Approximately 30–40% of patients with colon cancer are diagnosed with stage II cancer and are recommended to undergo radical surgery [2]. However, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer remains controversial. While guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, and the European Society for Medical Oncology recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for patients with high-risk factors [3–5], the survival benefits remains inconsistent across studies [6, 7].

Age has emerged as a potential prognostic and predictive factor in colon cancer, influencing both treatment decisions and clinical outcomes. Some studies have indicated that young patients differ from older patients in terms of pathological, metabolic, and biological features—for example, they tend to have poorer differentiation and higher microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status [8, 9]. And older patients may be less likely to receive chemotherapy due to concerns about comorbidities and treatment tolerance, while younger patients may be more aggressively treated regardless of risk status. Despite these differences, the impact of age on adjuvant chemotherapy use and outcomes in stage II colon cancer is not well understood.

This study aims to examine age-related differences in the use of adjuvant chemotherapy and its association with survival outcomes in patients with stage II colon cancer, using a retrospective cohort design to identify patterns that could inform more treatment strategies.

Methods

Patients

This study was a retrospective cohort study. Clinical data was collected for patients who underwent radical colon cancer surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2013 and December 2018, including demographic variables, clinical characteristics, treatment, and survival status. The study inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–75 years; (2) pathological diagnosis of colon adenocarcinoma; (3) the 8th edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM stage II colon cancer; and (4) the patient had received D3 radical colon cancer surgery. The exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of distant metastasis; (2) use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy; (3) palliative surgical treatment; and (4) combination with other malignant tumors or relapse colon adenocarcinoma.

Patients with colon cancer were divided into three groups based on age: young (18–49 years); middle-aged (50–64 years), and older (65–75 years).

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Suzhou Ninth People’s Hospital (YJKY2024-014-01). Informed consent was obtained from all patients and their legal guardians, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Research variables

The main variable was the treatment method, which was classified as surgery alone or surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy. Adjuvant chemotherapy regimens were classified into single drug chemotherapy or multi-drug chemotherapies. The single-drug chemotherapy regimen used was 5-fluorouracil, similar to capecitabine. Multi-drug chemotherapy was defined as the treatment with two or more chemotherapy drugs, such as FOLFOX, CAPOX regimens. Other variables included demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, treatment, and survival status. The demographic variables included sex, age, and body mass index (BMI). The clinical characteristics included pathological grading, carcinoma nodules, vascular invasion, nerve invasion, pathological T staging, and microsatellite instability (MSI) status.

The primary outcome was the overall survival (OS) of patients who underwent surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy compared to those who underwent surgery only. OS is defined as the time from the date of surgery to death. Patient follow-up was via outpatient visits and telephone inquiries. The follow-up period ended at June 2023. The survival period was defined as the period from the date of surgery to the end of follow-up.

Statistical methods

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Classification variables are expressed as percentages, and intergroup comparisons are made using percentages and χ2 or Fisher’s tests. Continuous variables that follow a normal distribution are represented by x ± s, and inter group comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance.

Overall survival was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test statistics. Multivariable Cox models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) to assess the effect of treatment on overall survival of patients with colon cancer. To compare the use of chemotherapy among different age groups, we used logistic regression model adjusted for potential confounders. Potential confounders include gender, BMI, lesion site, comorbidities, pathological grading, cancer nodules, vascular invasion, nerve invasion, pathological T staging, number of lymph nodes sampling, and MSI status. Odds ratios (OR) and 95%CI were calculated.

Results

Clinical data

Table 1 shows the clinical and pathological characteristics of patients with stage II colon cancer in different age groups. A total of 570 patients were included in the study; 93 were Young, 231 were middle-aged, and 246 were older.

The proportion of male patients was higher in all three groups, and the lesion sites were consistent. The young group had fewer comorbidities (p < 0.001). The probabilities of developing carcinoma nodules, vessel cancer emboli, and nerve invasion were similar among the groups, with no statistical difference, however, the young group had poorer pathological types and more MSI-H status.

The proportion of young and middle-aged patients who received chemotherapy was higher (82.8% vs. 74.9% vs. 55.3%, respectively, p < 0.001). Among patients receiving chemotherapy, the proportion of young and middle-aged patients using a multi-drug chemotherapy regimen was higher than in the older group (75.3% vs. 66.2% vs. 43.2%, p < 0.001). The young group had the highest survival rate (91.4%), whereas the older group had the lowest survival rate (82.5%).

Figure 1 shows the survival status of patients with stage II colon cancer for the different age groups. There was no significant difference in survival rates among the young, middle-aged, and older groups (p = 0.110), with 5-year OS rates of 92.5%, 86.0%, and 86.3%, respectively.

Discussion

Currently, research on stage II colon cancer mainly comes from data on older patients, with little data on Young patients. In the past 20 years, the incidence of early-onset colon cancer has been rising, and approximately 10–11% of colon cancer occurs in patients aged under 50 years [10]. This shifting demographic highlights the need to explore whether age influences not only the clinicopathological features of stage II colon cancer but also the treatment strategies and associated outcomes. This study revealed significant age-related differences in the clinical and pathological characteristics of stage II colon cancer patients, highlighting the importance of incorporating age as a key factor in clinical decision-making.

In most reports, for Young patients, compared to older age groups, the histopathology of the colon cancer is more invasive, with up to 20% of patients exhibiting poorly differentiated or undifferentiated carcinoma [11–13], which is similar to the results of our study. Early-onset colon cancer has a higher incidence higher in males than in females, which may be due to the fact that males are more susceptible to harmful environments such as drinking alcohol, smoking, and other unhealthy dietary habits [14]. Due to the unique clinical and pathological characteristics of colon cancer in young patients, the data on prognosis is also less clear. Most studies have shown that although younger patients tend to present with poorer histological grades at diagnosis, their overall survival is generally better than that of older individuals [15]. In our study, we also found that younger patients exhibited a higher prevalence of MSI-H, a marker associated with favorable prognosis. Furthermore, oxaliplatin is commonly recommended as part of adjuvant chemotherapy for colon cancer. Recent studies have shown that adding oxaliplatin to fluoropyrimidine-based regimens offers no significant survival benefit in patients with stage II colon cancer, even among those with high-risk features [16, 17]. This may help explain why younger patients, despite receiving more intensive chemotherapy regimens, do not necessarily experience improved survival outcomes. At present, the main treatment methods for stage II colon cancer are radical surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy. Owing to fewer comorbidities and better physical condition in young patients, the completion rate of comprehensive treatment, including surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, is high. However, our research did not confirm that successful completion of comprehensive treatment was why young patients have a better prognosis.

In the analysis of the use of adjuvant chemotherapy regimens, we have adjusted for potential confounders, including high-risk factors recommended by guidelines. We found that the middle-aged and young age groups were more likely to receive multi-drug chemotherapy regimens, indicating that they tended to receive more aggressive treatment. Chemotherapy decisions depend on the physician’s advice and patient acceptance. Doctors may consider that older patients have poorer physical condition, more comorbidities, and greater likelihood of adverse reactions, and, therefore, are unwilling to recommend chemotherapy or aggressive treatment to older patients. Hamaker et al. found a significant correlation between treatment decision-making and age in patients with colorectal cancer. More than one-third of older patients did not consider adjuvant chemotherapy without face-to-face contact with doctors, and they often chose low-dose or simplified treatment when receiving chemotherapy [18]. Given that young patients are more likely to choose adjuvant chemotherapy and use more aggressive chemotherapy regimens, more medical care and social psychological support are needed. Adjuvant chemotherapy has potential side effects that may affect self-esteem and daily life [19], and its excessive use may increase the economic burden on society. The cost and cost-effectiveness of chemotherapy regimens are important factors that must be considered in the treatment decision-making process; therefore, it is crucial to choose reasonable chemotherapy regimens.

Clinical implications

Our findings demonstrate a pronounced age-related variation in both the utilization and intensity of adjuvant chemotherapy among patients with stage II colon cancer. Younger patients were more frequently treated with adjuvant chemotherapy and multi-drug regimens, despite the absence of a corresponding survival advantage compared with older patients. This pattern raises concerns about potential overtreatment in younger cohorts, which may subject them to avoidable toxicities without measurable benefit. These patterns underscore the need for individualized treatment planning that balances oncologic benefit with patient-specific risks, rather than relying solely on chronological age as a determinant.

Perspectives

Advances in precision oncology and decision-support technologies present new opportunities to optimize adjuvant chemotherapy recommendations for stage II colon cancer. Artificial intelligence (AI)–based tools, including large language models such as ChatGPT, are gaining attention for their ability to integrate complex clinical data, predict treatment responses, and assist in multidisciplinary decision-making [20, 21]. Future research that combines AI-driven risk stratification with molecular profiling could enable more accurate identification of patients most likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, thereby minimizing both overtreatment and undertreatment across different age groups.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the design was a retrospective cohort study, so there may have been unknown biases in patient inclusion. Second, being conducted at a single center, the findings may have limited generalizability to broader populations. Future research should address these issues through multicenter studies with more diverse patient cohorts.

Conclusion

Our study found that young and middle-aged patients generally received adjuvant chemotherapy; however, more aggressive chemotherapy regimens did not provide additional survival benefits. In patients with stage II colon cancer, there was no significant difference in survival among the different age groups, regardless of whether they underwent surgery alone or surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy.
