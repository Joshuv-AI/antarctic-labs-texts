DNA methylation reshapes the expression of adipokines and adipose factors in white adipose tissues
Authors: Meng-Yue Fang, Shi-Wei Liu, Prasanna R. Kolatkar, Ping Qi, Yue-Chen Liu, Fei Sun, Cong-Yi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The expansion of adipose tissue during obesity results in alterations in the expression of adipose-derived hormones or factors, which control whole-body energy metabolism. Epigenetics refers to the heritable modifications in gene function that arise without changes in DNA sequence and are sculped by environmental factors, lifestyles, and nutritional variables. DNA methylation is a well-characterized epigenetic mechanism, and altered expression of adipose-derived hormones or factors is partly ascribed to alterations in DNA methylation levels or patterns. This review aims to summarize the regulatory effects of DNA methylation on the expression of adipokines and adipose factors, including leptin, adiponectin, cytokines as well as insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) and IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs) under obese conditions. The feasibility of DNA methylation as a biomarker for the prediction and prognosis of obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D) would be also discussed.

Introduction

Historically, adipose tissue was regarded solely as an energy-storing fat deposit. However, upon the discovery of Leptin in 1994 [1], the knowledge of adipose tissue has been fundamentally advanced. Adipose tissue has now been recognized to function as an endocrine organ crucial for maintaining metabolic homeostasis through the secretion of a range of bioactive molecules [2, 3]. Adipokines, including leptin, adiponectin, acylation-stimulating proteins (ASP), angiotensinogen, lipocalin and so on, are predominantly produced by adipocytes and released into circulation to act on target organs [2, 3]. By contrast, other adipose-derived molecules that do not possess the typical endocrine features of adipokines, including lipoprotein lipase (LPL), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) and S100A4, are known as “adipose factors” [4, 5]. Altered release of those adipokines or adipose factors has been recognized to be a major contributor to the high prevalence of obesity, which has become a global health challenge [6]. Obesity is featured by the persistent low-grade inflammation in the adipose tissue, and connected to the systemic metabolic dysfunction [7]. Critically, complications resulted from obesity are also influenced by the dysregulated production of adipokines and adipose factors [7].

Epigenetics represents the stable and heritable alterations to gene expression or cell phenotype that take place without changes in DNA sequence, including DNA or RNA methylation, histone modifications, and noncoding RNAs [8]. Diet and exercise are well-established in the etiology, prevention and therapy of obesity and its related metabolic disorders [9]. Genetic predisposition and aging cause epigenetic variation, while environmental variables, such as nutrition and exercise, interact with the human epigenome to modify the development of obesity [10, 11]. DNA methylation, as one of the most well-studied epigenetic mechanisms, involves the chemical modification of DNA by adding methyl groups selectively to specific bases, particularly at cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites, which is catalyzed by the DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) [12, 13]. In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview on the roles of DNA methylation in regulating the expression of adipokines and adipose factors in obesity, and discuss their implications in the pathogenesis of obesity and T2D.

DNA methylation is a reversible form of epigenetic modification

DNA methylation is a chemical modification that regulates the activity of DNA elements without changing the basic DNA sequence. DNA methylation is a highly conserved epigenetic modification that is critical for regulating gene expression, establishing and preserving cellular identity, controlling the development of mammalian embryos, and onset and progression of human diseases [13–15]. Epigenetic signatures derived from metabolic tissues, including adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, pancreatic islets, liver, and blood, have been extensively studied in the context of obesity [10]. DNA methylation is a reversible form of epigenetic modification by the interplay between methylation and demethylation of CpG sites within the genome (Fig. 1).

The majority of DNA methylation takes place at cytosines that come before a guanine nucleotide, especially at CpG sites [14]. A class of enzymes known as DNMTs catalyzes DNA methylation by moving a methyl group from S-adenyl methionine (SAM) to a cytosine residue’s fifth carbon, creating 5-methylcytosine (5mC) [14]. De novo DNMTs and maintenance DNMTs are the two primary kinds of DNMTs. The de novo DNMTs, DNMT3a and DNMT3b, are in charge of determining the methylation status of cells by transferring methyl groups onto naked (unmodified) DNA. This process predominantly occurs during the early stages of embryonic development [14, 16, 17]. The maintenance DNMT, DNMT1, is responsible for preserving DNA methylation patterns throughout replication. During semiconservative replication, the parental DNA strand maintains the initial DNA methylation pattern, while DNMT1 binds to replication foci to faithfully replicate the methylation pattern by adding methyl groups to the newly synthesized daughter strand [14, 16, 18].

DNA methylation induces changes in chromatin structure and DNA conformation and stability, thereby modulating protein-DNA interactions to govern gene expression [14]. DNA methylation predominantly functions as a physical barrier, preventing transcription factors from accessing promoter binding sites [14, 19]. Specifically, methyl-CpG-binding domain proteins (MBDs) are recruited as readers, which subsequently recruit histone deacetylases (HDACs) to form suppressive protein complexes [14, 20]. As a result, the modified chromatin regions become compacted, leading to the repression of gene expression [14, 20]. Paradoxically, emerging evidence also argues that DNA methylation at CpG sites in the 5’ region mediates transcriptional repression, while CpG methylations within gene bodies might promote gene expression [21].

DNA methylation is a reversible biological process where two distinct sites allow for chemical modification of 5mC. The activation-induced cytidine deaminase/apolipoprotein B mRNA-editing enzyme complex (AID/APOBEC) can deaminate the amine group of 5mC, transforming it to thymine (Thy) [22]. Through adding a hydroxyl group to the methyl group of 5mC, ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes can catalyze the reaction and generate a 5-hydroxymethyl-cytosine (5hmC) [23]. In turn, the amine group and the hydroxymethyl group are the two sites where 5hmC can undergo chemical modification. 5-hydroxymethyl-uracil (5hmU) is produced by AID/APOBEC through deaminating 5hmC. An alternate modification involves the oxidation of 5hmC by TET enzymes, leading to the successive generation of 5-formyl-cytosine (5fC) and 5-carboxyl-cytosine (5caC). All of those products including Thy, 5hmU, 5fC, and 5caC, are next identified, excised, and replaced by cytosine [14].

DNA methylation regulates adipose gene expression in polygenic obesity

The major cases of polygenic obesity are caused by a mix of lifestyle variables and the cumulative effects of distinct genetic variants including single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [24]. Numerous physiological processes are affected by these genetic variations, such as insulin secretion and action (e.g., insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1)), energy and lipid metabolism (e.g., fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO)), and appetite and satiety (e.g., Melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R)) [25]. Common variations in genes, such as FTO and MC4R, have been identified to be associated with increased body mass index (BMI) and appetite dysregulation through genome-wide association studies (GWAS), respectively [25, 26]. Specifically, BMI is strongly correlated with DNA methylation of genes like FTO, hypoxia inducible factor 3 A (HIF3A), and IRS1 [25, 27].

Indeed, GWAS studies revealed that the highest genetic susceptibility to polygenic obesity is contributed by FTO polymorphisms [26, 28, 29], in which the minor allele raises the risk of obesity by 1.20-fold and BMI by 0.39 kg/m2 (or 1,130 g in body weight) [29]. Epidemiological and functional investigations have provided evidence that FTO increases the risk of obesity by altering food preferences and intake [29]. Additionally, Claussnitzer et al. showed that the obesity linked FTO allele represses mitochondrial thermogenesis in adipocyte precursor cells (APCs) in a tissue-autonomous manner through AT-rich interaction domain 5B (ARID5B), Iroquois homeobox (IRX) 3, and IRX5 [30]. An elevated risk of T2D, metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and acute heart failure has also been linked to the BMI-increasing allele in the FTO gene [31]. Moreover, the connection between the FTO risk allele and the likelihood of obesity is attenuated by 27% among physically active adults, highlighting the importance of physical activity especially in subjects who are genetically inclined to obesity [32]. Studies in a male discovery cohort and a female validation cohort revealed a strong positive correlation between BMI and DNA methylation at the FTO locus cg02252501 [27]. Furthermore, FTO controls the methylation of mRNAs in genes encoding the components of the dopamine signaling pathway and affects the methylation status of ghrelin mRNA [33, 34].

Studies in a syndrome with severe insulin resistance (type B) demonstrated the binding activity between an autoantibody and the insulin receptor [35, 36]. Follow up co-immunoprecipitation studies using the autoantibody from a patient with type B insulin resistance suggested the tyrosine kinase activity of the insulin receptor [37]. Insulin controls metabolism through the insulin receptor substrate (IRS)-Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-Protein kinase B (Akt) pathway, and cell growth through the rat sarcoma (RAS)-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [38]. In obesity and T2D, selective suppression of IRS2 and its downstream effectors results in decreased insulin actions [39]. In both the female validation cohort and the male discovery cohort, there is a strong positive correlation between BMI and DNA methylation at the IRS1 locus cg00305996 [27].

DNA methylation regulates the production of major adipokines

Leptin

Leptin (LEP), a hormone that is primarily secreted by adipose tissue cloned by Zhang and colleagues in 1994 [1]. Leptin serves as the afferent signal in a negative feedback loop that maintains fat mass homeostasis [40]. It acts centrally by binding to its receptor in the central nervous system (CNS), thereby reducing food intake and modulating glucose and fat metabolism by the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway [41]. The loss of body fat (i.e., starvation) results in a decrease in Leptin level, which in turn causes a positive energy balance manifested by the reduced energy expenditure but enhanced food intake. In contrast, a rise in adiposity elevated Leptin level, inducing a negative energy balance exhibited by the surpassed energy expenditure as compared to food intake [40, 42]. Remarkably, subjects with obesity generally exhibit higher Leptin levels compared to controls [42], which is coupled with leptin resistance [43], a phenotype characterized by the diminished capacity of Leptin to inhibit appetite and weight gain, primarily caused by poor Leptin transport across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and malfunction of Leptin signaling [44].

LEP DNA methylation in subjects with obesity has been the topic of several clinical investigations. Studies revealed that children with obesity and insulin resistance (IR) exhibit lower levels of DNA methylation in the LEP promoter [45]. Adolescents with obesity but absent from IR display elevated circulating Leptin levels along with higher plasma insulin and triglyceride concentrations. In contrast, adolescents with obesity and IR manifest a significantly reduced methylation frequency at the LEP-CpG51 and -CpG31 loci [45]. Indeed, a positive correlation between fasting LDL-C levels and DNA methylation at the LEP-CpG11 and -CpG17 loci in subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) was detected in subjects with severe obesity [46], LEP mRNA levels were negatively correlated with DNA methylation of LEP-CpG17 in the SAT [46]. Similar results were also observed in animal studies. Mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) had a substantial 6-fold rise in plasma Leptin content compared with control mice, which was accompanied by the persistently elevated Leptin mRNA levels [47]. Following a 8-week of HFD challenge, a significant decrease of the average methylated fraction of 16 CpG sites at the Leptin promoter was noted, while increased methylation was detected after 12- and 18-week of HFD insult [47, 48]. These findings indicate that HFD may induce early DNA demethylation at the Leptin promoter to upregulate Leptin expression. Further analysis showed that in control mice, promoter methylation levels and gene transcription were negatively correlated, while a positive correlation was observed in HFD-fed mice. These results imply that DNA methylation might function as a feedback mechanism to keep the level of Leptin within the norm. However, Leptin mRNA levels remain elevated in HFD-fed mice, implying that additional factors may influence Leptin expression and that DNA methylation feedback alone is insufficient to restore Leptin expression to normal level [47].

To learn more about the mechanisms underlying Leptin DNA methylation in obesity, additional research has been conducted on both humans and animals. Adipose tissue from HFD challenged mice exhibits significant increases of DNMT1, DNMT3a and DNMT3b expression, and MBD2 binding activity at the Leptin promoter, but RNA Pol II binding activities are notably reduced [47]. It was noted that hypoxia could be a characteristic feature of adipose tissue in subjects with obesity, leading to the upregulation and stabilization of HIF1α [49]. The body can adapt to the shifts in the microenvironment through epigenetic mechanisms including DNA methylation, which likely connects obesity-related hypoxia to the activation of adipokines [50]. Ali et al. demonstrated that visceral adipose tissue (VAT) from subjects with obesity is featured by the higher levels of HIF1α and TET1 [51]. Consistently, challenge of human adipocytes with hypoxia significantly upregulates HIF1α and TET1 levels [52]. Mechanistically, hypoxia is found to induce global hydroxymethylation, thereby reducing DNA methylation at the LEP promoter, and subsequently upregulating Leptin expression in human adipocytes [52].

In general, the factors such as lifestyle, genetic risk and environmental exposures, are quite heterogenous in subjects with obesity, while those factors can be feasibly controlled in animal studies. However, those differences may cause discrepancies in terms of Leptin expression and its methylation between human subjects and mice with obesity. Indeed, the above findings indicate that subjects with obesity have higher levels of Leptin along with reduced Leptin DNA methylation, while studies in animals revealed that mice with obesity are generally featured by the persistent increase of Leptin mRNA levels but with increased DNA methylation. This contradictory observation is likely caused by the above-mentioned factors. Specifically, DNA methylation may play a dominant role in regulating Leptin expression in human subjects with strong heterogenous genetic and environmental factors. In contrast, other epigenetic mechanisms, such as histone modifications [47], could be the major factor to modulate Leptin expression in mice housed under a homogenous condition.

Adiponectin

Adiponectin (ADIPOQ) is predominantly produced and secreted by adipocytes [53], which possesses two primary receptors, Adiponectin receptors 1 (AdipoR1) and AdipoR2. AdipoR1 is generally highly expressed in skeletal muscle, while AdipoR2 is primarily expressed in the liver [54]. Adiponectin plays a crucial role in controlling glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress by promoting glucose utilization and fatty acid oxidation via the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) [55–57].

In general, obesity or IR and T2D can induce a reduction of circulating Adiponectin levels [58, 59]. Studies in Uygur subjects with obesity (n = 48) and T2D (n = 26) revealed that the ADIPOQ methylation rate is increased along with a reduction of ADIPOQ mRNA expression [60]. In a cohort of 73 subjects with extreme obesity, Houde et al. observed that DNA methylation levels at the ADIPOQ locus in the SAT are positively correlated with BMI and waist circumference [46]. A positive correlation between fasting LDL-C levels and ADIPOQ DNA methylation levels in the VAT (CpGE1) and SAT (CpGE1 and CpGE3) has also been noted. Notably, there is a negative correlation between ADIPOQ mRNA levels and DNA methylation levels at ADIPOQ-CpGE3 in the SAT [46, 60]. The development of hypoadiponectinemia during late pregnancy raises the possibility that Adiponectin may play a role in pregnancy-induced IR [61]. Indeed, SAT ADIPOQ mRNA levels are significantly lower in women with obesity, whereas DNA methylation levels are significantly higher [62]. Moreover, plasma Adiponectin is negatively correlated with maternal BMI and homeostasis model assessment (HOMA) indices of insulin sensitivity [62].

Studies in animals revealed that the expression of Adiponectin is suppressed when DNA hypermethylation occurs in a particular area of the promoter, thereby exacerbating metabolic disorders in obesity [63]. Additionally, it was noted that pro-inflammatory cytokines triggered by obesity increase the expression of DNMT1, which selectively methylates the Adiponectin promoter [63]. Suppression of DNMT1 activity using a DNMT inhibitor improved IR and glucose intolerance induced by obesity in an Adiponectin dependent manner [63]. Furthermore, studies have been conducted to tackle the mechanisms by which dietary interventions affect Adiponectin DNA methylation in the context of obesity treatment. Dietary supplementation of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) prevents and reverses obesity induced by HFD challenge [64]. Lu et al. found that SCFA supplementation effectively restored Adiponectin mRNA expression in the adipose tissue of mice fed a HFD [65]. SCFA-induced a shift of Adiponectin expression is linked to the alterations of DNA methylation at the Adiponectin promoter regions. In fact, SCFA not only attenuates the expression of DNMT1, DNMT3a, and DNMT3b, but also inhibits their binding activity to the Adiponectin promoter [65]. Taken together, DNA methylation controls Adiponectin expression in adipose tissue. Similar as LEP methylation, ADIPOQ DNA methylation levels are linked to the biochemical and anthropometric parameters, supporting feasibility to be a powerful indicator for dietary intervention effectiveness. From the mechanistic perspective, ADIPOQ DNA methylation in adipose tissue is likely controlled by DNMTs.

Cytokines

Numerous classical cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukins (ILs), chemokines, and colony-stimulating factors (CSFs), are synthesized and secreted by white adipose tissue (WAT) [66–69]. Chronic low-grade inflammation in the WAT, especially in the context of obesity, is a key contributor to IR [7]. Therefore, the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines is elevated in both IR and T2D, which facilitate leukocyte infiltration, particularly macrophages, culminating in the formation of crown-like structures (CLS) [7]. TNF-α is essential for controlling the activities of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) and hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), thereby inhibiting lipogenesis and elevating plasma free fatty acid concentration [70, 71]. TNF-α also serves as a key modulator of IR in obesity, influencing multiple critical steps of insulin action [72]. As a pleiotropic cytokine, IL-6 facilitates thermogenic adipocyte differentiation under certain conditions [73]. STAT3, an IL-6 signal transducer, is necessary for the thermogenic adipocyte development [74, 75]. The transcription of genes necessary for thermogenesis and mitochondrial biogenesis is activated by the IL-6/STAT3 pathway [76, 77]. Adipocyte thermogenesis could be regulated by IL-6 signaling at the systemic level through mechanisms including the hypothalamic regulation of energy intake and expenditure [78, 79], and the secretion of IL-6 by skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [80–83]. Each of these processes is capable of promoting fat loss. Nonetheless, excessive IL-6 accumulation within adipose tissue, especially under the obese setting, has been linked to impaired insulin-mediated glucose uptake, insulin resistance and diabetes [84, 85]. Studies in 3T3-L1-derived mature adipocytes revealed that an anti-inflammatory factor, Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF4), plays a vital role in mitigating inflammation [86]. Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), also referred to as chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), can significantly lower adiponectin synthesis and the expression of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), thereby contributing to lipid metabolic disorders [87]. Patients with obesity and diabetes exhibit higher levels of TNF-α, IL-6, CCL2 and CSF3, along with lower expression of KLF4 [86].

Numerous clinical trials have investigated DNA methylation of cytokine genes in subjects with obesity. Studies in Uygur subjects with obesity (n = 48) and T2D (n = 26) demonstrated that the methylation-positive rate of the TNF gene is markedly reduced, coupled with significantly elevated TNF expression in the VAT [60]. A global DNA hypomethylation and hydroxymethylation in the VAT from subjects with obesity (n = 60) was also noted, particularly for the promoters of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [51]. Another study in subjects with obesity(n = 20) detected a higher methylation-positive rate of the KLF4 promoter region in omental adipose tissue than their lean counterparts (n = 20), which was associated with decreased KLF4 expression [88]. Indeed, global DNA hypermethylation in adipocytes from obese subjects is positively associated with elevated CCL2 expression. Studies utilizing stratified analyses of participants with normal weight (n = 801) and those with overweight or obesity (n = 386) indicated that higher dietary glycemic index (GI) and glycemic load (GL) are associated with reduced CpG methylation, which in turn links to increased CSF3 expression among overweight or subjects with obesity.

Other studies have probed the regulatory mechanisms underlying DNA methylation of cytokine genes in obesity. Ali et al. reported elevated HIF1α and TET1 protein levels in the VAT in subjects with obesity [51]. The average adipose tissue cytokine methylation level negatively correlates with HIF1α and TET1 protein levels in the VAT [51]. Additionally, hypoxia was shown to increase HIF1α and TET1 expression, which then promote global hydroxymethylation to enhance the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [52]. There is evidence that DNMT1 and DNMT3a mRNA/protein levels are increased in the omental tissue in subjects with obesity, while KLF4 expression is decreased [88]. The methylation-positive frequency within the KLF4 promoter region in subjects with obesity is markedly increased, particularly at CpG_33 and CpG_34 sites. Consistently, DNMT3a mRNA expression is markedly higher in individuals with obesity. These outcomes suggest that reduced KLF4 expression in the VAT under obese status may be associated with enhanced DNMT expression in adipocytes [88]. Furthermore, individuals with obesity are generally featured by the lower serum vitamin D (VD) levels [89]. Higher severity of VD deficiency is linked to increased BMI, increased total and visceral fat mass, impaired insulin sensitivity, dyslipidemia, adipose tissue hypoxia, and elevated circulating inflammatory factors [90]. Moreover, severe VD deficiency is linked to reduced DNA methylation and increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including CXCL8 and IL-12 A, in adipose tissue [90].

One-carbon (1 C) metabolism is a universal metabolic pathway that facilitates the activation and transfer of 1 C units for biosynthetic activities, such as homocysteine remethylation and purine and thymidine synthesis [91]. The one carbon cycle (1CC), consisting of the methionine and folate cycles, controls DNA methylation through modulating SAM level [91]. The solute carrier family 19 member 1 (SLC19A1) encodes a membrane folate carrier [92, 93]. It was found that human adipocytes isolated from SAT show reduced SLC19A1, along with disrupted intracellular 1CC metabolism and decreased level of folate, thereby resulting in DNA hypermethylation and increased CCL2 expression [94]. A positive correlation between the cg12698626 methylation and CCL2 expression was identified, suggesting that methylation may influence CCL2 promoter activity [94]. Indeed, two independent assays confirmed that methylation attenuated glucocorticoid receptor (GR) binding affinity and the repressive effects of cortisol [94], implying that cg12698626 methylation in adipocytes enhances CCL2 expression by modulating cellular responsiveness to endogenous cortisol.

The impact of dietary interventions on DNA methylation of cytokine genes has also been investigated. Citrus fruits are the abundant source of (poly)phenols, a class of dietary bioactive compounds capable of preventing T2D [95]. Helichrysum and grapefruit extracts have been shown to prevent carbohydrate digestion and absorption, thereby alleviating postprandial glucose level and hyperinsulinemia in rats [96]. Indeed, the antihyperglycemic effects of both extracts are coupled with reduced TNF-α mRNA levels in the epididymal adipose tissue [97]. Specifically, the grapefruit intervention group showed a higher DNA methylation level at the CpG3 locus of the TNF-α promoter, which is situated five base pairs downstream of the transcription start site [97]. Collectively, subjects with obesity have been observed to have lower DNA methylation levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines and higher DNA methylation levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that DNA methylation controls cytokine expression in adipose tissue. Moreover, DNA methylation levels in cytokines significantly correlate with biochemical and anthropometric parameters, supporting that DNA methylation could be a viable biomarker for evaluating the responsiveness to dietary interventions.

Insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) and IGF binding proteins (IGFBPs)

Increasing evidence indicates that growth factors are derived from adipose tissue, including IGFs and IGFBPs, which stimulate cell proliferation [98, 99]. Other than their major roles in growth and cell differentiation, IGFs also regulate glucose homeostasis, cardiovascular function, and lipid metabolism [100–102]. IGFs are involved in adipogenesis and adipose tissue expansion relevant to the development of obesity [103, 104]. IGFBPs exert their effects primarily by binding to IGFs and inhibiting their activity, but also exhibit IGF-independent actions through their Arginine—Glycine—aspartic acid (RGD) sequence, by interacting with integrin receptors [105]. IGFBPs have been shown to prevent the onset of obesity and IR [99], as evidenced by the elevated circulating IGF1 and IGF2, but decreased IGFBP1 and IGFBP2 in obese and T2D subjects [106, 107].

The above findings prompted the studies of DNA methylation in IGF genes in obese settings. Maternal low-protein (LP) diets have been shown to lead to low birth weight, rapid catch-up growth and the subsequent development of obesity and glucose intolerance later in life [108–110]. It was found that a prenatal LP diet and a postnatal HFD raised the mRNA levels of IGF2 and DNA methylation in adipose tissue [111]. In the VAT of rat pups exposed to a prenatal LP diet, the DNA methylation increased approximately 1.2-fold for the 6 CpG sites within the ICR/H19 DMR of Igf2-H19 gene, while DNA methylation increased approximately 1.4-fold for 4 CpG sites in the SAT of rat pups exposed to a postnatal HFD [111](Fig. 2). The increased DNA methylation at the imprinted control region/H19 differentially methylated region (ICR/H19 DMR) may account for the elevated IGF2 expression observed in adipose tissue. In support of this notion, research on the IGF2/H19 locus have shown that hypomethylated CpG islands in the 5′ region of the H19 gene act as essential binding sites for the zinc finger protein CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), whereas hypermethylation of the ICR/H19 DMR hinders the binding of CTCF proteins [112], which enables the distal enhancer to interact with the IGF2 promoter, thereby activating IGF2 transcription [113].

Methylation level of the ICR/H19 DMR within the Igf2-H19 gene has also been linked to genetic disorders associated with fetal growth, such as Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) and Silver-Russell syndrome (SRS) [114](Fig. 2). BWS is an overgrowth illness characterized by macrosomia, macroglossia, lateralized overgrowth, visceromegaly, abdominal wall defects, and an elevated risk of embryonal tumors [115]. In BWS patients, gain-of-methylation abnormalities occur at the ICR/H19 DMR (5–10%) [115, 116]. Once ICR/H19 DMR becomes methylated, it cannot bind to CTCF, leading to IGF2 expression and H19 silencing [114]. Therefore, the probands of BWS are featured by the severe hypermethylation at the ICR/H19 DMR within the Igf2-H19 gene (22 of 25 and 21 of 24 template molecules, respectively) [117]. SRS is a congenital disorder characterized by severe prenatal and postnatal growth retardation, dysmorphic facial features and bodily asymmetry [118]. In several patients with clinically typical SRS, a loss-of-methylation was noted on the ICR/H19 DMR [119]. This is linked to and likely caused by the relaxation of imprinting and biallelic expression of H19, and downregulation of IGF2. Methylation of the ICR/H19 DMR within the Igf2-H19 gene is partially lost in SRS subjects as evidenced by the decrease of methylation indices (MIs 10 to 40%, while the normal MI is around 50%) [119]. In contrast to the gain of methylation in BWS and the loss of methylation in SRS, the extent and magnitude of IGF2 dysregulated methylation in the presence of obesity are less severe at the imprinting regulatory region inside the Igf2-H19 gene. This might explain why IGF2 dysregulated methylation in obesity contributes to the development of obesity while not affecting fetal growth.

In general, individuals with obesity are featured by lower serum IGFBP2 levels compared to their healthy counterparts. Studies in Swedish Caucasians with obesity (BMI >35 kg/m2, n = 24) found reduced levels of IGFBP2 mRNA expression and elevated levels of IGFBP2 DNA methylation in the VAT [120]. Following a 3-week of HFD challenge, Kammel et al. identified diet-induced obesity (DIO)-susceptible (responder, Resp) and -resistant (non-responder, nResp) mice based on the differences of weight gain [121]. Their findings revealed that IGFBP2 expression is decreased in young Resp mice, which is associated with increased DNA methylation at intronic CpG2605. Indeed, selective methylation of CpG2605 significantly reduces reporter activity by approximately 85%, indicating that IGFBP2 expression is modulated by site-specific DNA methylation [121]. Furthermore, studies in 40 young males revealed that the increase of DNA methylation at the IGFBP5 CpG site is coupled with decreased IGFBP5 mRNA expression in the SAT following a short-term of HFD insult [122]. Together, studies in humans and mice support that obesity is associated with higher IGF levels but lower IGFBP levels. The expression of IGFs and IGFBPs in adipose tissue is regulated through DNA methylation, with IGF2 expression being specifically modulated by DNA methylation at the ICR/H19 DMR within the Igf2-H19 gene.

Resistin

Resistin secreted by adipocytes has been recognized to be associated with obesity and IR in rodents [123]. In rodents, Resistin counteracts the insulin-sensitizing actions of Adiponectin in the liver by suppressing AMPK activation [124, 125]. Although mice with obesity actually have downregulated Resistin mRNA, circulatory Resistin levels, however, are noticeably elevated and positively correlated with insulin, glucose, and lipid levels [125]. In a study conducted by Lu and colleagues suggested that reduced Resistin mRNA levels in the adipose tissue of HFD-fed mice are significantly restored following supplementation with SCFAs [65]. Moreover, changes in DNA methylation at the Resistin promoter regions are linked to the SCFA-induced change in Resistin expression, a process controlled by the reduced expressions of DNMT1, DNMT3a, DNMT3b, and MBD2, thereby preventing their binding to the Resistin promoter [65].

Effects of DNA methylation on adipose factors

Lipoprotein lipase (LPL)

The LPL gene encodes a crucial enzyme for the metabolism of triglycerides (TG). LPL is synthesized and secreted by adipocytes [4]. LPL catalyzes the rate-limiting step in the hydrolysis of TG-rich lipoproteins and is a key player in the early formation of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particle [126, 127]. Studies revealed that the D9N and N291S polymorphisms of the LPL gene influence plasma lipid levels [128]. Adipose tissue LPL expression is elevated in obesity [129]. In a cohort of 30 males with extreme obesity, Guay et al. found that DNA methylation levels at the LPL-CpGA3 locus in the VAT are negatively related to HDL-C levels [130]. Furthermore, LPL DNA methylation levels are inversely linked to relative mRNA expression levels, as well as the fasting triglyceridemia levels [130].

Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4)

The widely expressed glycoprotein DPP4 catalyzes the quick breakdown of incretins, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP), which are critical for postprandial insulin secretion [131]. Recent studies have identified DPP4 as a novel adipose tissue factor exerting autocrine and paracrine effects, by which it contributes to the impaired insulin sensitivity [5]. DPP4 expression is elevated in pro-inflammatory conditions, including obesity and T2D [132]. Studies in a cohort of 92 nondiabetic females with extreme obesity, Turcot et al. demonstrated that genotype-dependent methylation levels at the particular CpG sites within the DPP4 promoter CpG island in the VAT are linked to DPP4 transcription and variation in the plasma lipid profile [133]. The percentage of DNA methylation correlates negatively with DPP4 mRNA abundance and positively with plasma HDL cholesterol concentration. Additionally, DPP4 mRNA abundance is positively correlated to the plasma total cholesterol to HDL-cholesterol ratio [133].

S100 calcium binding protein A4 (S100A4)

S100A4, a protein belonging to the soluble calcium (Ca2+)-binding protein family, is primarily recognized as a metastasis-associated protein [134, 135]. S100A4, a newly discovered adipose factor, has been linked to inflammation in the SAT, hepato-visceral fat excess, obesity-related IR, and an increased prevalence of T2D [136, 137]. Studies revealed that plasma S100A4 levels and its expression in the VAT tissue are positively correlated to metabolic and anthropometric parameters such as IR, HOMA-IR, insulin levels, and BMI Z-Score in prepubertal non-diabetic children with obesity [138]. Specifically, one CpG site (the cg07245635) localized within the S100A4 genomic locus, was found to be 1.25-fold upregulated in the VAT samples from obese children as compared to their normal-weight counterparts. Conversely, for the cg10447638 site, an obvious 0.69-fold reduction in DNA methylation was observed, corresponding to IR in prepubertal girls with obesity, in whom IR persisted after the onset of puberty. These results support the hypothesis that different methylation of the S100A4 genomic locus may constitute one of the molecular processes underlying its dysregulation in obesity and T2D [138].

The feasibility to target adipokine DNA methylation for therapeutic purpose

Studies have examined the impact of interventions on the DNA methylation of adipokines in the context of treating obesity. As aforementioned, adipose tissue from obese subjects and animals is featured by higher levels of TNF-α and lower levels of adiponectin and resistin. In parallel, lower levels of TNF-α DNA methylation and higher levels of Adiponectin and Resistin DNA methylation are detected in the adipose tissue, which are controlled by DNMTs. Those observations suggested feasible evidence supporting that DNA methylation patterns could be served as therapeutic targets. Specifically, IR and glucose intolerance caused by obesity can be improved by directly suppressing DNMT activity using a DNMT inhibitor [63]. Suppression of DNMTs in the adipose tissue of HFD-fed mice along with SCFA supplementation successfully restore the mRNA levels of Adiponectin and Resistin, which is coupled with decreased DNA methylation in their promoter regions [65]. Moreover, the antihyperglycemic actions of Helichrysum and grapefruit extracts are associated with lower levels of TNF-α mRNA and higher levels of DNA methylation at the CpG3 locus of the TNF-α promoter in epididymal adipose tissue [97]. Taken together, treatment approaches by either directly or indirectly targeting adipokine DNA methylation patterns exhibited beneficial effect but warrants further investigations.

Summary and perspective

Adipose tissue controls systemic energy metabolism by secreting a range of adipose-derived hormones or factors. DNA methylation is vital in modulating the expression of those molecules, including leptin, adiponectin, various cytokines (TNF-α, ILs, anti-inflammatory factors, chemokines, and CSFs), IGFs, IGFBPs, and so on (Fig. 3, Table 1). In general, CpG methylation in the 5’ regions of genes is typically linked to decreased gene expression, methylation within gene bodies, however, has been shown to enhance gene expression [21]. Adipose-derived hormones or factors are tightly regulated via DNA methylation upon the challenge of inflammatory insult or metabolic stress. The reduced expression of SLC19A1 in human adipocytes perturbs intracellular 1CC metabolism, leading to DNA hypermethylation and elevated CCL2 expression, and therefore, methylation of cg12698626 in adipocytes enhances CCL2 expression by modulating the response to endogenous cortisol [94]. There is convincing evidence that both adipose tissue IGF2 mRNA and DNA methylation are increased following the exposure to a prenatal LP diet or the challenge of a postnatal HFD [111]. A potential mechanism underlying the increased IGF2 expression involves enhanced methylation of ICR/H19 DMR, which suppresses CTCF protein binding and facilitates interaction between the distal enhancer and the IGF2 promoter, thereby facilitating IGF2 transcription [112, 113].

Apart from WAT, brown adipose tissue (BAT) also manifests endocrine function. The visceral adipose tissue-derived serine protease inhibitor (Vaspin) was initially found to be upregulated in VAT of diabetic Otsuka Long-Evans Tokushima fatty (OLETF) rats [139]. Vaspin manifests an insulin-sensitizing impact on WAT under obese condition [140]. Altered serum Vaspin concentration is associated with obesity and impaired insulin sensitivity [141]. It was noted that Vaspin mRNA and protein expression are markedly induced especially in BAT in cold insulted mice [142]. BAT exhibits the lowest Vaspin promoter methylation levels, which are further reduced upon acute cold exposure [142]. It is also noteworthy that the current review only focused on those factors well recognized to be regulated by DNA methylation. The effect of DNA methylation on the expression of some adipocyte-derived factors such as Asprosin [143], and ASP [144, 145], has been excluded from current review, because of lacking experimental evidence. However, CpG islands are also noted within the regulatory regions of those factors, which warrants further investigations. It is noteworthy that DNA methylation of genes in the adipose tissue could be impacted by SNPs as well. Petr Volkov et al. conducted a genome-wide DNA methylation quantitative trait locus (mQTL) analysis and found that 592,794 SNPs were linked to DNA methylation in 477,891 CpG sites [146]. SNPs in significant mQTLs were linked to the expression of genes within the adipose tissue, and were further related to adipose gene expressions in obesity related traits. SNPs in significant mQTLs were further related to gene expression in adipose tissue and obesity related traits. These genes include Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), Apolipoprotein A-V (APOA5), and Cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP), and all of which are adipose factors [146]. However, the mechanisms by which those SNPs affect their DNA methylation are yet to be elucidated. Of note, the current review only summarized the regulatory effect of DNA methylation on the expression of adipose-derived hormones or factors in obese settings. Other than DNA methylation, additional epigenetic processes (e.g., histone modifications) also control the expression of those molecules, such as leptin, adiponectin, and secreted frizzled-related protein 5 (SFRP5) [47, 147, 148].

Some DNA methylation biomarkers are currently being utilized to predict the outcome and treatments for obesity and T2D. For example, the levels of IL-6 in obesity may be predicted by the baseline methylation score based on 116 CpGs [149]. It was discovered that T2D incidence was highly predicted by a methylation risk score of 82 CpGs (relative risk per 1 standard deviation rise in methylation risk score: 2.3 (2.07–2.56)) [150]. The risk for T2D development is also linked to DNA methylation of loci in blood DNA containing ATP binding cassette subfamily G member 1 (ABCG1) and phosphoethanolamine/phosphocholine phosphatase 1 (PHOSPHO1) [151]. Additionally, a baseline DNA methylation score was a better indicator of effective weight-loss [area under the curve (AUC) receiver operating characteristic (ROC) = 0.95-1.0] compared to the conventional parameters such as age and BMI (AUC ROC = 0.56) [152]. Moreover, baseline methylation at cg26663590 at the Nuclear Factor of Activated T Cells 2 Interacting Protein (NFATC2IP) gene causally mediated 52.8% of the effect of rs11150675 on 2-year weight-loss in the HFD group [153]. These results suggest a promising role of DNA methylation as a viable marker for obesity/T2D prediction, diagnosis and prognosis. However, further well-powered, multitrait genome-wide, and longitudinal studies on DNA methylation are required to determine the value of DNA methylation in predicting later-life outcomes [154, 155]. We summarized that the level of DNA methylation of certain adipokines is correlated with several crucial biochemical and anthropometric parameters and acts as a powerful indicator for the efficacy of dietary intervention in obesity and its related metabolic disorders. Therefore, DNA methylation levels of certain adipose-derived hormones or factors could be optimal biomarkers for predicting the outcomes and assessing the responsiveness of dietary intervention in obesity and metabolic disorders.
