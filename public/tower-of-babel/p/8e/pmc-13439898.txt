Evaluating emergency management capabilities and identifying their related factors for public health emergencies in China
Authors: Li Yuan, Gan Xiaocheng, Zhang Kexin, Zhang Qianxi, Xie Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing public health emergency management capability is an urgent governance challenge for China’s provinces. To address this, this study develops a theoretical evaluation system comprising four dimensions: prevention and monitoring, emergency processing, emergency response, and recovery and reconstruction. Within this framework, particular attention is paid to measuring each province's capacity for emergency resource reserves and foundational support conditions. To ensure objective indicator weighting, the entropy weighting method is then applied to measure emergency management capabilities across China's 31 provinces over the 12th to 14th Five-Year Plan periods. Finally, the Dagum Gini coefficient and kernel density estimation are used to identify regional disparities and dynamic evolution patterns. Furthermore, this study identifies the factors influencing the enhancement of emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies and investigates their impact. The study found that China’s public health emergency management capability index shows an overall upward trend but with a slow growth rate. Spatially, it exhibits a distribution pattern characterized by higher in the east and lower in the west, higher in the south and lower in the north. Spatial disparities have remained at high levels for an extended period and continue to widen; both inter regional and intraregional differences are prominent, collectively exacerbating the spatial polarization of China’s public health emergency management capabilities. Digital infrastructure and the level of financial development are positively correlated with regional emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies, while local government competition and human capital levels are negatively correlated. The influencing factors of emergency management capabilities also exhibit significant heterogeneity across regions, reflecting differences in infrastructure, talent structure, financial levels, and policy orientations. Strengthening inter-regional coordination and leveraging positive spatial linkages are key pathways to enhancing emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27694-6.

Introduction

While globalization has effectively promoted the integrated development of the economy, technology, and society, it has also significantly increased the frequency of public health emergencies. Since the beginning of the new century, large-scale public health emergencies, such as the SARS outbreak, the H1N1 influenza pandemic, the H7N9 avian influenza outbreak, the Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) outbreak, And the Ebola virus outbreak, have posed a grave threat to the health of the general public. These events have also led government leaders worldwide to deeply appreciate the importance of emergency management of public health emergencies [1]. Research on emergency management for public health emergencies began earlier in developed countries, such as the United States, the United Kingdom, And Japan. These nations have gradually established relatively robust theoretical And practical frameworks for public health emergency management within their borders, which have proven effective in actual events [2, 3].

Since the SARS outbreak, the Chinese government has prioritized public health emergency response, introducing a series of policies to build a comprehensive emergency management system. For example, in 2003, the State Council promulgated the Regulations on Emergency Response to Public Health Emergencies, marking China's incorporation of such responses into a legal framework [4]. Relevant departments subsequently issued the National Emergency Response Plan for Public Health Emergencies, the Measures for the Monitoring And Reporting of Public Health Emergencies And Infectious Disease Outbreaks, And the Measures for the Assessment of Public Health Risks in Emergencies, among others. These policies provide reference and guidance for the implementation of emergency response efforts [5]. The COVID-19 pandemic, which began in early 2020, was characterized by its sudden and urgent nature, unknown causes, continuity, high levels of destruction and impact, and widespread influence, posing a severe test for China’s public health emergency management system [6]. Chinese President Xi Jinping has also emphasized that, in response to the shortcomings exposed during the pandemic, efforts must be made to address weaknesses, plug gaps, And strengthen vulnerable areas,improve institutional mechanisms for major epidemic prevention And control,enhance the national public health emergency management system,and focus on raising the governance capability And level for responding to major public health emergencies [7].

In China, local governments serve as the frontline in responding to public health emergencies. They are the primary entities responsible for risk prevention, initial response, And medical treatment, playing a crucial role in emergency governance by facilitating communication between higher And lower levels And implementing initial response measures [8, 9]. Currently, what is the state of emergency management capability for public health emergencies across China’s provinces? What factors influence this capacity? These questions warrant systematic investigation and analysis to reveal the operational mechanisms underlying the formation of local emergency management capability and provide concrete reference points for government policy formulation.

The possible contributions of this paper are as follows: clarifying and defining the connotation of emergency management capability for public health emergencies based on the latest policy documents and existing literature, and constructing a corresponding indicator system on this basis; dividing the data from 2011 to 2023 into three phases according to China’s Five-Year Development Plans (the 12th, 13th, and 14th) to more clearly describe the development trajectory of public health emergency management capability across different regions; and analyzing the dynamic and static mechanisms of this capability, exploring its influencing factors, and proposing corresponding policy recommendations for China’s public health emergency management.

The research structure of this paper is as follows: Part Two is a literature review on emergency management of public health emergencies. Part Three introduces the analytical tools used in this study—namely, entropy weighting, the Dagum Gini coefficient, kernel density estimation, and spatial Durbin models—and explains the sources of the indicator data. Part Four constitutes the core of this study, using entropy power to measure China's emergency management capability for public health emergencies; using Dagum Gini coefficient and kernel density estimation tool to analyze the dynamic evolution and regional differences; and using spatial Durbin model to analyze the influence of related factors on the emergency management capability for public health emergencies in each province. Literature review

Currently, academic research on emergency management encompasses both strategic considerations at the macro level, such as the modernization of national governance, the development of legal And policy frameworks, And the deployment of digital infrastructure [10–12] And detailed Analyses of micro level settings, including hospitals, communities, And specific population groups [13]. Institutional innovation And system development have been the primary focus of most scholars [14, 15] . Scholars have systematically explored the formation of collaborative governance networks, multi stakeholder coordination models, risk based tiered response mechanisms, And the digital transformation of response processes [16–18]. The academic community unanimously agrees that it is essential to move beyond the traditional, government centric decision making model And establish a networked governance structure characterized by government leadership And the orderly participation of multiple stakeholders [19, 20]. This new mechanism emphasizes cross regional joint prevention and control based on local management, relies on technology to enable information sharing and unimpeded command and control, and ultimately aims to enhance the system’s resilience across the entire cycle, from early warning And response to recovery, to address the high complexity And uncertainty of modern public health emergencies [21]. Intelligent decision making And resource optimization are also key to enhancing the effectiveness of emergency management for public health emergencies [22]. Research in this area focuses on how to deeply integrate cutting edge technologies such as artificial intelligence, big data Analysis, And machine learning with traditional emergency management models, thereby achieving intelligent identification of event risk sources, automatic assessment of response levels, efficient coordination of emergency supplies, scientific allocation of Human resources, And precise distribution of medical resources [23, 24]. Moreover, resilience assessment And systems evaluation are key approaches for measuring emergency management capabilities in public health emergencies [25]. Academic circles are dedicated to constructing a multidimensional resilience (and vulnerability) assessment indicator system covering economic, social, organizational, infrastructural, And psychological dimensions, to conduct dynamic And comprehensive evaluations of emergency resilience And vulnerabilities in cities, rural areas, medical institutions, and even the socio psychological sphere [26, 27]. Particularly against the backdrop of the COVID-19 pandemic, factors such as the resource capacity Limits of healthcare systems, community self organization And mutual aid capabilities, information transparency, And care And support for vulnerable groups have been widely incorporated into evaluation frameworks [28].

Moreover, scholars have analyzed pathways for emergency management capabilities in public health emergencies at three levels: macro, meso, And micro. The macro level focuses on the modernization of national emergency management And the construction of smart emergency response systems, emphasizing the use of informatization, digitization, And intelligent technologies to address weaknesses in rapid response, multi departmental coordination, and information exchange processes [29]. It also proposes medium to long term pathways such as national level biological monitoring And early warning projects, revisions to laws And regulations, data integration mechanisms, And the development of a talent pool for integrated medical and public health [30] . The meso level focuses on digital governance And coordination mechanisms, highlighting the failure of traditional governance in scenarios such as monitoring And early warning, emergency command, And grassroots prevention and control amid tensions among diverse stakeholders. It advocates for a transition from government led to emergency co governance through digital platforms and institutionalized coordination, and refines the key elements and optimization directions of collaborative governance through research on the identification of information coordination factors and the review of coordination models and mechanisms [20, 31]. At the micro level, research delves into specific scenarios And critical junctures, including full process emergency decision making systems for public health events to address decision making delays And feedback lags, as well as studies on the prediction, stockpiling, allocation, And platform based optimization of medical resources and supplies to enhance supply and demand matching and operational efficiency [32].

In summary, the academic community has conducted extensive discussions on emergency management for public health emergencies. However, some gaps remain. Existing research on emergency management for public health emergencies is limited to comparative analyses of data spanning several years or a single year, without adopting a phased approach. Additionally, in the face of an increasingly dynamic social risk environment, no evaluation system for emergency management capabilities has been established to reflect societal needs, and related evaluation studies have largely focused on static aspects; research combining dynamic and static perspectives at both the national and regional levels remains scarce. Current research on pathways to enhance emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies is also predominantly qualitative or limited to unidimensional quantitative approaches.

Indicator system, data sources, and research methods

Measurement indicators

Emergency management capacity for public health emergencies

Emergency management of public health emergencies is a complex systemic endeavor involving multiple aspects. When constructing an indicator system for capability assessment, principles such as comprehensiveness, systematicity, scientific rigor, representativeness, and feasibility must be followed. Drawing on relevant indicators from the National Emergency Plan for Public Health Emergencies of China and existing research by scholars (e.g. [33–37] ), this paper expands and optimizes existing indicators in light of the characteristics of public health emergencies to construct an indicator system for China’s public health emergency management capabilities.

Prevention And monitoring constitute the primary phase of public health emergency management, focusing on early risk detection And foundational social protection. Specifically, this involves ensuring medical insurance coverage to guarantee timely access to medical care for residents And prevent delays that could lead to further spread; increasing technical investment to enhance the accuracy of pathogen identification and tracing; social assistance to provide a safety net for vulnerable groups and prevent secondary risks; and public health Literacy, which influences compliance with epidemic prevention measures. Therefore, this study selects five indicators from the dimensions of health security, technical support, and social assistance, namely, the number of urban and rural residents enrolled in basic medical insurance; public health technology expenditure; the number of urban employees enrolled in basic medical insurance; education expenditure; and the number of urban and rural social assistance recipients, to characterize prevention and monitoring capability [38, 39].

Emergency processing constitutes the core operational component of emergency management for public health emergencies, primarily manifested in the speed of response and the effectiveness of handling during the early stages of an incident. This specifically involves having an adequate number of hospitals and healthcare professionals to ensure timely admission and professional treatment of patients; widespread mobile phone penetration to support the issuance of emergency directives and the reporting of epidemic data; public transportation to facilitate the rapid transport of supplies and personnel; furthermore, densely populated areas require corresponding prioritization of resource allocation. Therefore, this study selects five indicators: number of hospitals, number of mobile phone subscribers at year end, number of public transportation vehicles per 10,000 people, provincial population density, And healthcare personnel, from the dimensions of medical resources, information networks, transportation support, And population environment to characterize emergency processing capability [27].

Reconstruction and recovery constitute the final phase of emergency management, focusing on restoring the resilience of social systems and providing a safety net after the event has subsided. Specifically, this involves ensuring sufficient hospital bed capability to treat severe cases and provide a buffer against potential resurgences of the epidemic; utilizing local fiscal revenue to support post disaster reconstruction and financial relief; and allocating social security and employment expenditures to provide a safety net for vulnerable groups facing unemployment, thereby preventing secondary social risks. Therefore, this paper selects three indicators: the number of hospital beds, local fiscal revenue, And local fiscal expenditures on social security And employment, from the dimensions of medical treatment, economic support, And social security to characterize reconstruction and recovery capability [11, 40].

Emergency response is a dynamic operational dimension that runs through the entire emergency management process, often manifesting as the efficiency of resource mobilization and the resilience of sustained operations during a crisis. Specifically, this involves sufficient public health R&D expenditure to support rapid vaccine and drug development and provide scientific and technological support; adequate expenditure on healthcare institutions to reflect the financial costs and intensity of treatment under an overburdened healthcare system; and the economic foundation of each province, which determines the confidence for recovery under extreme measures. Therefore, this study selects three indicators: public health R&D expenditure, total expenditure on healthcare institutions, And provincial GDP, from the dimensions of research translation, healthcare operations, And economic resilience to characterize emergency response capacity [23]. The concept of emergency management capacity in this study specifically refers to the level of structural resource reserves and foundational support conditions that regions possess under normal circumstances to address sudden public health emergencies. It encompasses quantifiable input indicators across four dimensions: prevention and monitoring, emergency processing, recovery and reconstruction, and emergency response. This definition emphasizes preparedness rather than process efficiency, distinguishing it from the concept of capability in some literature that focuses on the effectiveness of dynamic responses. It should be noted that the aforementioned indicators primarily reflect the potential capability reserves of emergency management in each region, rather than directly measuring operational effectiveness during the emergency response process. Factors affecting emergency management capabilities

Two considerations must be taken into account when selecting factors that influence local emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies. The selected factors should be those commonly used in existing research, And they should not overlap with the indicator system for emergency management capabilities in public health emergencies. Based on a thorough understanding of the essence of emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies And a review of relevant Literature, the following five influencing factors have been identified. Digital infrastructure serves as the technological foundation for enhancing information transmission efficiency And enabling cross departmental coordinated responses; its coverage level directly determines the sensitivity of monitoring and early warning systems as well as the efficiency of emergency command coordination [41, 42]. Local government competition, as An institutional incentive driving the enhancement of regional governance capacity, may both stimulate investment in emergency resources through race to the top competition And lead to strategic behavior due to short term performance evaluation incentives [43, 44]. The level of financial development refers to the provision of critical financial support for emergency expenditures, the expansion of healthcare systems, And risk sharing through enhanced fiscal Liquidity And resource allocation efficiency [45, 46]. Market openness emphasizes enhancing the resilience of emergency supply chains And the accessibility of healthcare services by introducing external capital, technology, And management expertise [47, 48]. The level of Human capital refers to the fundamental role played by the accumulation of highly qualified healthcare professionals And the improvement of public health Literacy in determining the quality of emergency response implementation And public compliance [49, 50]. Data source

Taking into account data availability and continuity, this study employs 31 provinces across China from 2011 to 2023 as its research sample. Data sources include the China Statistical Yearbook, China Regional Economic Yearbook, China Health Statistics Yearbook, and provincial yearbooks.

Research methodology

Entropy weight method

To objectively assess the emergency management capabilities of each province in response to public health emergencies, the entropy weight method was used to determine the weights of each indicator. Entropy values reflect the utility value of indicator information, serving as a measure of uncertainty in random events. The greater the randomness of An event, the higher its corresponding entropy value; conversely, the greater the certainty of An event, to the point of inevitability, the lower its entropy value, eventually reaching zero [51]. Dagum Gini coefficient method

This paper employs the Dagum Gini coefficient decomposition method to analyze regional disparities in emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies across China’s provinces and to explore the underlying causes of these disparities. Unlike methods for quantifying regional disparities, such as the Theil index, which overlook the specific distribution patterns within subsamples, the Dagum Gini coefficient method accurately reflects the magnitude and sources of relative disparities [52].

According to this method, the overall Gini coefficient can be decomposed into the contribution of intra regional variation, the contribution of net inter regional variation, and the contribution of super variation density. Generally, a smaller overall Gini coefficient indicates smaller regional disparities, while a larger overall Gini coefficient indicates weaker inter regional coordination [53].

Spatial Durbin model

By adjusting its coefficients, the spatial Durbin model can be transformed into more commonly used models, such as the spatial lag model (SLM) and the spatial error model (SEM), making it a versatile tool for empirical analysis [55]. Residual diagnostics

To validate the appropriateness of the spatial Durbin model specification, the following diagnostic tests were conducted on the estimated residuals:

Spatial Autocorrelation Test: Moran's I index was employed to examine whether residuals exhibited spatial autocorrelation not captured by the model. The null hypothesis posited the absence of spatial autocorrelation in residuals. The test results show that the Moran's I value for the residuals is -0.032, which is not significant at the 10% level, indicating that the model has effectively controlled spatial correlation.

Normality Test: The Shapiro Wilk test was used to examine the normality of the residuals. The results show that the W statistic is 0.976, failing to reject the null hypothesis that the residuals follow a normal distribution, indicating that the model fits well.

Heteroscedasticity Test: The Breusch Pagan test was employed to examine the heteroscedasticity of the residuals. The results show that the LM statistic is 8.73, which is not significant at the 10% level, indicating that there is no significant heteroscedasticity issue.

From a phased perspective, the sharp increase in reconstruction and recovery capacity primarily occurred during the 14th Five-Year Plan period. Provincial level

Overall, China’s emergency management capability for public health emergencies has consistently exhibited a northeast southwest spatial distribution pattern. Judging by the distribution characteristics on either side of the Hu Huanyong Line, provinces with high emergency management capacity for public health emergencies are primarily concentrated on the southeastern side, mostly in provinces with higher levels of economic development, while provinces with low emergency management capability are mainly concentrated on the northwestern side. The COVID-19 pandemic has reinforced this pattern. In the early stages of the pandemic, southeastern coastal provinces rapidly brought the outbreak under control through digital governance and ample medical resource reserves, while provinces in the northwest and northeast faced shortages of supplies and insufficient transportation capability. These disparities existed prior to the pandemic and were fully exposed during the crisis.

East west

Emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies show a gradual increase from west to east, with eastern provinces such as Hebei, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu consistently ranking at the top in terms of index scores. From a north south perspective, a long stretch of provinces with low emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies, primarily in Shandong and Guangdong, as well as the Yellow River Basin and the middle reaches of the Yangtze River, has emerged. This is largely attributable to the long-term net outflow of relevant resources to economically developed provinces, resulting in a high in the south and central regions, low in the north pattern that has not been fully resolved over time. The COVID-19 pandemic laid bare this structural contradiction, which will be a key focus of China’s public health emergency management efforts in the future. Furthermore, as regional economic clusters gradually become vital platforms for China’s sustainable development, emergency management capabilities have correspondingly shifted from isolated, point based growth to synchronized, cluster based enhancement. The Beijing, Tianjin, and Hebei region, the Yangtze River Delta, and the Pearl River Delta have all demonstrated high levels of emergency management capability.

Spatial effects of relevant factors on emergency management capacity for public health emergencies

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression can be used to preliminarily explore the relationships between various variables and emergency management capacity, but it cannot account for the spatial dependence effects arising from geographical proximity. To address this, this study further introduces Spatial Lags Models (SLM) and Spatial Error Models (SEM) to test for spatial autocorrelation in emergency management capacity. Based on model selection criteria, this study conducted LR and Wald tests. The results rejected the null hypothesis in both cases, indicating that the Spatial Durbin Model (SDM) outperforms the Spatial Lag Model and the Spatial Error Model and does not degrade to a simpler form. The Hausman test results showed a statistic of 220.43 (p = 0.000), significantly rejecting the null hypothesis at the 1% level; therefore, the Spatial Durbin Model with spatiotemporal fixed effects was selected. Taking spatial correlation into account, this study employs the maximum likelihood method for estimation and decomposes the spatial relationships between each factor and emergency management capacity into direct, indirect, and total effects.

In the eastern region, in terms of direct effects, there is a significant positive correlation between the level of digital infrastructure and financial development and local emergency management capacity, indicating that improvements in digital infrastructure and financial services contribute to enhanced emergency management capabilities. The direct effect of market openness is not significant; local government competition and human capital levels both show a significant negative correlation. Regarding indirect effects through spatial spillovers, market openness exhibits a significant negative spatial correlation with neighboring regions. This implies that the highly open market environment in the eastern region may transmit external risks to surrounding provinces by accelerating the flow of population and resources, or may lead to the concentration of emergency resources in core cities, thereby inhibiting the improvement of emergency management capabilities in neighboring regions. Although the indirect effects of local government competition and human capital levels are negative, neither is statistically significant. According to human capital allocation theory, when the stock of human capital exceeds a region’s absorption capacity, the absence of a matching institutional environment and job demand may lead to talent mismatch or knowledge depreciation. This prevents the concentration of highly educated individuals from effectively translating into regional knowledge creation and public governance efficiency; instead, it generates a negative correlation due to intensified resource competition. This phenomenon may be related to the fact that while the eastern region is economically developed, it faces significant pressure on resource and environmental carrying capacity.

In the central regions, in terms of direct effects, digital infrastructure and the level of financial development show a significant positive correlation; local government competition and human capital levels show a significant negative correlation. In terms of indirect spatial spillover effects, both digital infrastructure and local government competition generate negative spillovers for neighboring provinces. The direct effect of digital infrastructure is positive, but the indirect effect is negative. This may stem from the islanding of digital infrastructure or inconsistent standards in the central region, where provincial level development actually hinders information sharing with neighboring provinces. The negative indirect association of local government competition is significant, meaning it exerts a restraining effect on the enhancement of emergency response capabilities in surrounding areas. From the perspective of institutional economics, in regions undergoing a transition marked by intense local government competition, if performance evaluations are oriented toward short term economic growth, this may lead to a skewing of public resources toward productive sectors, while institutional provision in livelihood sectors such as public health lags behind. Consequently, the potential positive externalities of human capital are difficult to fully realize. This may reflect that, in the central region’s process of industrial relocation and economic development, the siphoning effect of certain core provinces has a negative correlation with the emergency management capabilities of surrounding provinces in response to public health emergencies.

In the western region, in terms of direct effects, digital infrastructure shows a significant positive correlation, while local government competition shows a significant negative correlation. Surprisingly, the level of financial development shows a weak negative correlation, contrasting with the positive correlations observed in the eastern and central regions. This may reflect the underdeveloped nature of the western financial system, where credit resources flow more toward short term, high return sectors rather than public health emergency preparedness. Although the level of human capital is positive, it does not pass the significance test. Regarding indirect spatial spillover effects, digital infrastructure exhibits significant positive spatial spillover, indicating that development in this area in the western region may generate positive spillover effects for neighboring provinces. However, both human capital levels and financial development levels generate significant negative spatial spillovers. The negative spillover of human capital reflects the brain drain effect highly educated talent, which is scarce in the West, is drawn to central cities in neighboring provinces, thereby weakening the emergency response capabilities of surrounding areas. Intensified competition among local governments is negatively correlated with the emergency response capabilities of adjacent provinces, which may be a unique manifestation of the Western region under multiple constraints.

In the Northeast, in terms of direct effects, digital infrastructure and the level of financial development are both significantly positively correlated; market openness is positively correlated but not significantly so; local government competition and human capital levels remain significantly negatively correlated. Regarding the indirect effects of spatial spillovers, both local government competition and human capital levels exhibit significant positive spatial spillovers, which stands in stark contrast to the Eastern and Central regions and may be related to the Northeast’s efforts in recent years to promote regional coordinated development through policy guidance. However, the level of financial development generates negative spatial spillovers, reflecting the high concentration of financial resources within the province in the Northeast, which inhibits the emergency financing capacity of neighboring provinces.

Significant associations inconsistent with expected directions have emerged in all regions, and their commonalities and specific mechanisms warrant focused analysis. The specific analysis is as follows.

Market openness generates significant negative spatial spillovers in the eastern region. This finding contradicts the conventional wisdom that openness promotes capacity enhancement. While a highly open market environment facilitates factor mobility and resource allocation efficiency, in the context of sudden public health emergencies, frequent interprovincial movement of people and goods can accelerate risk transmission, making it easier for externally introduced risks to spread to neighboring provinces. At the same time, a resource siphoning effect exists in open markets core cities, leveraging their stronger market attraction, draw emergency supplies, medical resources, and specialized talent from surrounding areas, leading to resource shortages in neighboring provinces when crises occur. Therefore, market openness in the eastern region has not helped surrounding areas enhance their emergency response capabilities through spatial spillovers; rather, it has become the source of negative correlations.

Competition among local governments exhibits a significant negative direct effect across all four major regions. This indicates that competition among local governments driven by economic growth generates widespread negative externalities. Excessive competition leads to the allocation of fiscal resources toward productive sectors, while emergency management for public health emergencies, as a typical nonproductive public good, involves long investment cycles and offers low visibility for political achievements, making it prone to systematic crowding out under competitive pressure. More notably, competition among local governments in the central region also generates significant negative spatial spillovers, indicating that competitive behavior has evolved into a short-sighted practice that restricts medical resources, directly undermining the emergency management capabilities of neighboring provinces.

The level of human capital exhibits a significant negative direct effect in the eastern, central, and northeastern regions, running counter to the notion that the more talent, the better. The core explanation for this unexpected finding lies in talent mismatch and the siphoning effect. In the eastern region, highly educated talent is overly concentrated in high paying sectors such as finance and IT, while public health and emergency management positions struggle to attract top talent due to low salaries and limited career prospects, resulting in a structural mismatch of human capital. In the central region, provincial capitals exhibit a strong siphoning effect on talent from surrounding areas, leading to the hollowing out of emergency management positions in outflow areas and a mismatch in professional expertise among talent in inflow areas. The Northeast faces issues of net population outflow and an aging workforce. Its stock of highly educated talent cannot be effectively converted into emergency response capabilities; instead, rigid expenditures such as pensions and social security are crowding out emergency response budgets. These phenomena collectively indicate that simply increasing the stock of human capital does not necessarily enhance emergency management capabilities; the key lies in optimizing talent allocation systems and aligning them with job requirements.

A paradox of direct positive effects and indirect negative effects has emerged regarding digital infrastructure in the central regions. While a province’s digital infrastructure enhances its own emergency response capabilities, it generates negative spillover effects on neighboring provinces. On the one hand, inconsistent standards and incompatible data interfaces across provincial digital platforms create digital silos, and upgrades to a province’s platform actually reinforce information barriers. On the other hand, provinces leading in digital infrastructure attract IT talent and equipment suppliers from surrounding areas to concentrate locally, thereby weakening neighboring provinces’ digital emergency response foundations. This unintended consequence reminds us that regional coordination of digital infrastructure is far more important than isolated development.

The level of financial development produces both direct negative effects and indirect negative effects in the western regions, while it produces indirect negative effects in the northeastern regions. This challenges the assumption that financial development is always beneficial to public governance. In regions with underdeveloped financial systems, credit resources tend to flow toward short term, high return sectors. However, emergency management for public health emergencies lacks collateral and stable cash flows, making it difficult to secure financial support; in some cases, financial expansion even drives up asset prices, crowding out long term public investment. Geographically, financial resources are highly concentrated in provincial capitals or regional financial centers, creating a financial siphon effect that drains funds from surrounding provinces through deposit gathering mechanisms, thereby suppressing the emergency financing capacity of neighboring regions.

Taken together, these negative correlations indicate that enhancing emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies does not simply depend on increased factor inputs, but rather hinges on the optimization of the institutional environment, resource allocation structures, and regional coordination mechanisms. Misaligned performance evaluation criteria for local governments, rigid talent allocation systems, fragmented digital infrastructure standards, and credit misallocation within the financial system can all transform factors originally expected to be positive into hidden obstacles that hinder emergency response capabilities in practice. This provides important insights for formulating regional emergency management policies for public health emergencies.

Conclusions and implications

Conclusions

Starting from the essence of emergency management for public health emergencies, this paper constructs an evaluation system for China’s emergency management capabilities based on four dimensions: preventive monitoring, emergency processing, reconstruction and recovery, and emergency response. This system focuses on the structural reserve capacity of emergency resources in each province rather than dynamic response effectiveness. Using the entropy weighting method, this study measured the emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies across China’s 31 provinces from the 12th Five-Year Plan period to the 14th Five-Year Plan period. The Dagum Gini coefficient method and kernel density estimation were employed to reveal regional disparities and dynamic evolution in provincial capability indices, while a spatial Durbin model with dual fixed effects (spatial and temporal) was used to investigate associated factors. The findings reveal the following.

China’s emergency management capacity for public health emergencies has generally shown a steady upward trend, though the growth rate remains relatively slow. A distinct step like spatial differentiation is evident in the east west direction: the eastern and central regions exhibit similar levels of population development, while the northeastern region has seen an accelerated decline in its index in recent years. In the north south direction, the emergency management capability index for public health emergencies in the southern regions is higher than that in the northern regions. From a dimensional perspective, the indices exhibit a decreasing gradient of emergency processing capability, reconstruction and recovery capability, emergency response capability, and prevention and monitoring capability, with the prevention and monitoring capability index being the lowest. Enhancing prevention and monitoring capabilities is central to improving China’s emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies.

Regional disparities in China’s emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies show a trend of continuous expansion. Interregional disparities are the primary source of overall differences, while intraregional disparities should not be overlooked; together, they exacerbate the spatial polarization of China’s emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies. In terms of distribution dynamics, changes in emergency management capabilities at both the national and regional levels are largely consistent, and both exhibit a bimodal distribution. Accelerating the spillover effects from regions with high emergency management capabilities is an important practical approach worthy of consideration.

In terms of influencing factors, emergency management capabilities for public health emergencies are closely linked to multiple factors and exhibit significant regional spatial correlations. Digital infrastructure and the level of financial development are the primary positive correlates; through the sharing of digital infrastructure and financial service resources, they can generate positive spatial spillover effects on neighboring provinces. Local government competition and human capital levels are the primary negative correlates, and their spatial correlations further inhibit the improvement of emergency management capabilities in surrounding regions. Regarding regional differences: the eastern and central regions exhibit a significant negative correlation with local government competition; in the western region, the positive effect of digital infrastructure is prominent, while the impact of human capital levels is not significant; in the northeastern region, all factors except local government competition and human capital levels show positive correlations. Fully leveraging these spatial correlation characteristics across regions to shift emergency management of public health emergencies from post event response to pre-event prediction is a key pathway to enhancing regional emergency management capabilities.

Implications

Addressing shortcomings in prevention and monitoring capabilities: exploring pathways for building public health safety defenses

Prevention and monitoring capabilities rank lowest among all dimensions of emergency management and are closely linked to overall effectiveness. This finding suggests that shifting emergency management from post event response to pre-event early warning should be a direction worth exploring. Regions with the necessary conditions may attempt to promote the integrated application of big data, artificial intelligence, and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies in the field of public health monitoring, gradually exploring the construction of real time monitoring and intelligent early warning platforms that cover multi source data. Regions with weak foundations may consider prioritizing reliance on provincial level platforms to reduce construction costs through data sharing. It is important to note that the effectiveness of technology driven solutions depends on data sharing mechanisms, digital literacy at the grassroots level, and supporting regulations. Pursuing a comprehensive and all-encompassing approach in the short term may carry the risk of a mismatch between investment and returns.

Deepening explorations of collaborative development strategies in light of widening regional disparities

Regional disparities have been identified as the primary source of overall disparities and are showing a trend of continuous expansion, while spatial interconnections have yet to be universally established. This finding suggests that strengthening the flow and sharing of emergency management resources across regions may help leverage the radiating and driving effects of more developed areas. At the national level, consideration should be given to establishing robust partnership mechanisms between the eastern regions and the central, western, and northeastern regions, and to promoting resource flow through means such as technology transfer and talent exchange. Urban clusters such as the Beijing, Tianjin, and Hebei region, the Yangtze River Delta, and the Pearl River Delta could take the lead in exploring pilot projects for integrated emergency management. It is worth noting that the effectiveness of regional coordination is constrained by local willingness to cooperate and the sophistication of interest coordination mechanisms. Forcing progress through administrative directives without stimulating endogenous momentum may make it difficult to achieve the expected results.

Capitalizing on the positive correlation with digital infrastructure to optimize the spatial allocation of financial resources

Digital infrastructure is the most robust positive correlating factor and exhibits certain positive spatial correlation characteristics. Incorporating emergency management needs into the coordinated planning of new infrastructure is a key lever for enhancing overall efficiency. Under the national new infrastructure framework, priority should be given to deploying infrastructure such as 5G networks and data centers in underdeveloped regions to provide foundational support for smart emergency management. It is worth noting that the overall effect of financial development level exhibits a negative correlation, suggesting that measures should be taken to prevent financial resource misallocation and the siphoning effect from stifling surrounding regions. Efforts can be made to guide financial institutions in developing specialized credit and insurance products for public health emergency response systems, while paying attention to local fiscal and regulatory constraints to avoid new debt risks.

Address the negative correlation between intergovernmental competition and human capital, and optimize governance and talent strategies

In this study, intergovernmental competition and human capital levels exhibit a negative correlation, with the total effect of human capital levels being significantly negative and the direct effect of intergovernmental competition being negative. This finding suggests that excessive competition and the one-way concentration of talent may hinder the improvement of local emergency management capabilities. Local performance evaluations should moderately reduce the weight of single economic growth indicators and explore incorporating regional coordinated development and public health emergency capacity building into the assessment framework. Regarding talent, efforts should be made to establish flexible mobility mechanisms to encourage cross regional exchange of experts. The tendency to prioritize recruitment over utilization must be avoided.

Future research directions and limitations

Limitations

This study has limitations in the following aspects. Although the spatial Durbin model can control for spatial dependence and some unobserved heterogeneity, its estimation results essentially reflect statistical associations between variables rather than causal inferences. The positive or negative associations between various factors and emergency management capacity may be influenced by unobserved confounding variables, and the possibility of reverse causality also exists: regions with higher emergency management capacity may be better able to attract talent and capital investment, thereby enhancing the level of digital infrastructure and financial development. From a temporal perspective, this study covers the period from the 12th Five-Year Plan to the 14th Five-Year Plan (2011–2023), but the actual implementation period of the 14th Five-Year Plan is 2021–2025>. Since data for the final two years has not yet been released, this may result in an incomplete portrayal of recent development trends. Due to limitations in data availability, the measurement indicators for certain dimensions do not fully capture the essence of emergency management. For example, the prevention and monitoring capacity lacks direct metrics for the actual operational effectiveness of monitoring and early warning systems, and the emergency support capacity lacks detailed measurements of material reserves and interdepartmental coordination mechanisms.

Future research directions

To address the aforementioned limitations, future research can be deepened in the following areas. In terms of the temporal dimension, once data for the latter half of the 14th Five-Year Plan is released, we will continue to refine the time series and systematically analyze the characteristics of change and overall evolutionary trends across the three phases from the 12th to the 14th Five-Year Plans. Regarding the indicator system, the evaluation framework will be continuously refined in line with economic and technological advancements. Emerging variables, such as the application of artificial intelligence in risk assessment and the effectiveness of smart early warning system construction, will be incorporated to ensure that measurement results better reflect the cutting edge developments in emergency management practice. At the methodological level, future research may explore the use of instrumental variable methods, difference in differences methods, or natural experiment designs to more rigorously identify causal relationships between various factors and emergency management capabilities.
