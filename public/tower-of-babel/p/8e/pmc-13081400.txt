Advancement on localized targeting via intra-articular administration of lipid-based vesicular nanoparticles for osteoarthritis management
Authors: Sourav Mohanto, Avinaba Das, Alima Misiriya, Irshad Ahammed ET, Adrija Bhunia, Mohammed Gulzar Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative arthritis with substantial global health and economic burden, especially intensified in recent year. The conventional therapeutics causes several systemic side effects via prolonged uses of oral or topical administration. To overcome the limitations of conventional therapeutics, lipid-based nanoparticles at localized site of administration represent a promising therapeutic approach for OA management, offering targeted drug delivery, enhanced bioavailability, and improved therapeutic outcomes. These nanosystems leverage their biocompatible lipid composition and nanoscale properties to overcome the significant challenges associated with conventional OA treatments. In this context, this comprehensive article summarizes the potential effectiveness of lipid-based delivery systems in OA management. The article initially summarizes the pathophysiology, role of inflammatory cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and lipid metabolism in OA management. The manuscript delves into the importance of lipid-based systems for therapeutic delivery and discusses various studies that summarize lipid-based delivery systems in preclinical settings for OA treatment, particularly focusing on the intra-articular administration of lipid-based nanoparticles in joint retention time, delayed lymphatic drainage, and electrostatic interactions with the extracellular matrix. The manuscript also enlisted a limited number of recent clinical trials and further assessed the safety profile of the localized administration of lipid-based delivery system in OA treatment. This manuscript is the first of its kind to compile and compare examples of lipid-based delivery systems against the existing literature focused on various delivery methods in OA management. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative inflammatory arthritis affecting the entire joint, involving the knees, hips, hands, and spine. This chronic OA causes pain, stiffness, edema, and loss of normal joint function, affecting the quality of life and increases thesocioeconomic burden [1]. As per the recent report, approximately 528 million people worldwide suffer from OA, with 73% prevalence rate due to aging and rising obesity rates. Women are more likely to possess OA beyond the age of 50, with a 60% prevalence rate across the globe. The knee is the most affected joint, accounting for 60–85% of different types of OA [2, 3]. Hip OA affects 11% of subjects over 45 years of age, while hand OA affects 8–16%. The regional variations exist in OA incidence, with high-income nations like the United States of America (USA) shown a higher rates and lifetime risk of 41% to 45% for symptomatic OA. OA is a major cause for disability, accounting for 2% of disability-adjusted life years. Asia has a larger burden of knee OA than USA [4, 5]. The burden of OA is expected to rise dramatically due to increased risk factors like obesity and sedentary lifestyles [6, 7].

The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) recommends non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)-based topical products for the preliminary management or prevention of hand and knee OA, especially for older patients with comorbidities or higher risk of adverse effects [8]. The oral NSAIDs are the main therapy for OA pain in the hands, knees, and hips; however, the increased rate of gastrointestinal (GIT) adverse effects, i.e., bleeding, ulcers, and nausea remains a significant concern. The NSAIDs reduce prostanoids synthesis by reversibly inhibiting the cyclooxygenase (COX)-1 and COX-2 enzymes, providing analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties. However, this therapeutics can also affect kidney function, alarming the risk of nephrotoxicity, electrolyte imbalances, and renal failure [9, 10]. In addition, the topical NSAIDs are recommended over oral NSAIDs due to their decreased toxicity and systemic exposure [11]. The topical NSAIDs enter the targeted or site-specific tissues through the skin and accumulate in the dermis, muscles, and joints at higher localized concentrations [12]. Although topical NSAIDs target local tissues and possess lower systemic toxicity, these medication can still result in application-site reactions such as pruritus, dry skin, erythema, irritation, and paresthesia [13]. Usually, the COX-2 inhibitors lower prostaglandin synthesis, reducing pain and inflammation. These interventions are recommended for GIT disorders and OA patients, but not for those at risk for cardiovascular disease [14, 15]. For safety, specific COX-2 inhibitors or non-selective NSAIDs combined with a proton pump inhibitors are recommended over non-selective NSAIDs alone [11]. The intra-articular (IA) corticosteroids are temporary anti-inflammatory medications used to alleviate pain, improve quality of life, and manage low-grade inflammation in OA. These medications modulate T- and B-cell immunological activity. The synthetic corticosteroids, i.e., triamcinolone and methylprednisolone acetate (MPA), are more effective than natural corticosteroids, often used in conjunction with local anesthetics for knee and hip OA when patients do not respond to topical or oral analgesics [16]. In addition, MPA provides early-stage pain relief, but its extended-release version offers long-lasting pain treatment with several side effects includes local tissue damage, systemic absorption causing hyperglycemia in diabetics, and repeated doses promoting cartilage deterioration [17, 18]. In the late stage of OA, joint stiffness and disability can be prevented with early intervention and therapy. The IA injections of hyaluronic acid (HA) exhibited effective repairment of low friction co-efficient before cartilage degradation in early-stage OA. However, commercial HA joint lubricants exhibit worse mechanical properties and rapid degradation, increasing the risk of infection and treatment costs. In addition, Duloxetine is also useful in treating chronic OA pain due to central pain pathway malfunction [19], as serotonin and norepinephrine imbalance is linked to central sensitization and chronic pain [20]. The vitamin supplements have been shown for potential benefits in preventing or managing OA, but more research is needed to confirm the mechanisms. The vitamin D supplementation may also improve pain and function in knee OA patients but does not prevent structural progression [21].

In this context, the macromolecular lipid-based drug delivery system improves the delivery of several therapeutics, which are primary challenges in conventional dosage forms or delivery platforms [22]. The lipid-based delivery systems not only enhance the oral absorption and dissolution of hydrophobic drugs but also enable the sustained drug release; thereby prolonging therapeutic effects and reducing the frequency of dosing. Lipid-based delivery systems offer significant advantages over traditional and alternative nanocarrier-based treatments for OA. Lipid-based delivery systems improve drug solubility [23], prolong IA residence of the drug [24], and target mechanisms [25], minimizing off-target effects compared to oral NSAIDs. Lipid-based delivery systems are also capable of altering the OA due to their ability to preserve labile medications and lubricate joints, unlike traditional nanocarrier-based approaches [26].

Thus, this comprehensive review article summarizes the potential effectiveness of lipid-based drug delivery systems in OA management. The article initially summarizes the pathogenesis, pathophysiology, role of inflammatory cytokines and other factors, and lipid metabolism in OA management. The manuscript further delves into the importance of lipid-based systems for therapeutic delivery over other nanocarrier or nanoparticles (NPs) in OA management. In addition, this manuscript further discusses various literature that summarizes or reports the lipid-based delivery system in pre-clinical settings for OA treatment. In addition, the manuscript also enlisted a limited number of recent clinical trials and further assessed the safety profile of the lipid-based delivery system in OA treatment. This manuscript has been meticulously drafted, considering both the applications of first-generation lipid-based delivery systems in OA management and current innovative approaches.

Pathophysiology, immunomodulation, and genetic mutations in OA progression

Pathogenesis, and pathophysiology of OA

The multifaceted pathogenesis of OA is mainly characterized by progressive degeneration or destruction of articular cartilage, subchondral bone remodeling, synovial inflammation, ligament degeneration, changes in periarticular muscles, and joint capsule hypertrophy [27]. Amongst these, cartilage degradation is assessed to be the hallmark feature resulting from the disproportion between anabolic and catabolic processes [27]. Articular cartilage is a dense extracellular matrix (ECM), which is mainly made up of a sparse distribution of chondrocytes and does not have any blood vessels, nerves, or lymphatic vessels. In articular cartilage, collagens are the major proteins, and plays an important role in maintaining the mechanical properties of articular cartilage [27, 28]. Despite being studied for centuries, the exact pathological mechanism of OA remains unidentified, even with an understanding of the physiological and anatomical changes investigated. At initial stage, OA was characterized via biomechanical factor like wear and tear of articular cartilage; however, it is now addressed as a chronic inflammatory joint disorder that is initiated with biochemical and cellular alteration in the synovial joint tissues with patient-specific variability [28]. As the cartilage ECM is produced by chondrocytes, the pathogenesis of OA is frequently associated with alterations in chondrocyte activities, production of inflammatory cytokines, and regulation to signaling molecules. Nevertheless, the risk factors for OA, i.e., age, obesity, genetic predisposition, acute trauma and chronic inflammation, gender (especially female), hormone profile, and metabolic syndrome remain constant, which further underline the disease’s multifaceted nature and the necessity of focused preventive measures in clinical settings [29, 30].

The progression of OA is determined by a complex interplay of molecular regulators, i.e., tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-15, oncostatin M, etc., inflammatory mediators like prostaglandins, nitric oxide, reactive oxygen species (ROS), matrix degradation enzymes or matrix metalloproteinase (MMP), aggrecans, a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS), and cathepsins [31]. As OA progress, changes in cartilage composition and the loss of cartilage integrity can be commonly observed. The compositional changes modify the cartilage material properties and boost its susceptibility to disruption by physical forces. At initial stage of OA, chondrocytes exhibited the restoration of joint function [32]. The characteristics of the cartilage material are affected by the compositional alterations due to physical stresses. OA is understood as a complex polygenic disease that is given by environmental and numerous genetic factors [27]. According to recent reports, between 40% and 80% of OA can be attributed to heredity, with a higher genetic influence in hand and hip OA than in knee OA [33]. Role of inflammatory cytokines, and MMPs in OA progression

Collagen type II is a predominant type of collagen found in articular cartilage, which initially creates fibrils followed by networks, is comparatively more stabilized than various small forms of collagen and non-collagenous proteins [35, 36]. These proteins, along with small, leucine-rich proteoglycans like decorin and biglycan, stick the collagen system to aggrecan in cartilage. As OA progress, the degradation of ECM components occurs concurrently with the loss of proteoglycans from ECM, prior to the breakdown of collagen fibrillar network [37, 38]. Several MMPs, i.e., MMP-1, -3, -9, and − 13 serve as a crucial factor in OA pathogenesis, especially in collagen type II degradation. The osteocyte and synovial fibroblast produce MMP-13, leading to collagen degradation, cartilage erosion, ulceration, and pathological joint changes such as synovial hyperplasia, synovitis, and diffuse mononuclear cell infiltration in synovial joints [38, 39]. The cartilage that undergoes ECM degradation, responsible for MMPs and ADAMTSs enzymes, certainly reduces the structural and mechanical support to the tissues [40]. The inflammatory signals like cytokines (i.e., IL-1, TNF-α), toll-like receptors, and pathogen-associated molecular patterns lead to the breakdown of cartilage structure in OA [41]. The major cytokines involved in the development and progression of OA seem to be IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and, to a lesser extent, IL-15, IL-17, and IL-18 [41]. Amongst these IL-1β, and TNF-α have been the most extensively studied or investigated [42]. In addition, IL-1β is considered as a potential cytokine involved in the etiopathology of OA via catabolic consequences at several levels [42, 43]. IL-1β revealed its effectiveness to prevent the formation of ECM components for cartilage, including collagen type II and aggrecan [44]. Moreover, IL-1β can both promote the expression of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, and boost the production of ROS [42]. The conventional therapies usually target the downregulated expression or activity of IL-1β for OA management. Although several preclinical and clinical studies have proposed anti-cytokine therapies, the translation to clinical practice remains challenging. These studies have shown some positive outcomes, but additional research is necessary to achieve the desired therapeutic outcomes [38].

Genetic mutations, cartilage injuries in OA progression

OA is recognized as a multifactorial disease influenced by various factors. Numerous studies shown that genetics plays a significant role in the development of OA. Recent research has changed the understanding of genetic links to OA, particularly the age-related form of the disease, which is the most common and often develops early [45]. In addition, OA undergoes broad genetic research, and novel OA susceptibility risk loci are identified. Subsequent mechanistic studies shed light on many genetic risks that affect the articulating joint’s cells and tissues [34]. More than 100 polymorphic DNA variations have been connected to this complex illness so far. The majority of these genetic risk variants map to non-protein-coding regions of the genome, further considered to function by controlling the expression of target genes. These variants contribute to more than 20% of the heritability of OA [34]. This disease’s genetic foundations deviate from the usual patterns of Mendelian inheritance and are most likely linked to changes in several genes [34, 46]. The multifaceted nature of OA is demonstrated by the discovery of numerous candidate genes that confer its development. Some of the genes that is related to OA disease further associated to cartilage ECM, i.e., collagen type II-A1, COL9A3, COL11A1 genes [47] related to bone density, e.g., bone morphogenetic protein 5 (BMP5), Frizzled-related protein or FRZB [48], genes related to inflammation cytokine genes (e.g., IL-1β, IL-10, IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β1), and growth differentiation factor 5 (GDF5) [47]. The prospective analyses must involve comprehensive research with large sample sizes to enhance our understanding of how epigenetics influences the development of OA [34].

Role of MMPs, SMAD3 in OA

In discussing gene polymorphism’s role in OA susceptibility, several single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in MMP genes, such as the MMP-1 (-1607 1G/2G polymorphism), has been associated with an increased risk of OA. This allele leads to amplified MMP-1 expression and accelerated collagen breakdown in articular cartilage. In addition, MMP-3 (-1171 5 A/6A polymorphism) also increases ECM degradation, predisposing individuals to OA. This allele is associated with elevated MMP-9 levels, potentially contributing to cartilage destruction [49]. The genetic mutations affect OA inflammation by increasing MMP activity, interfering with cartilage repair processes, and elevating pro-inflammatory cytokines [50]. Several studies highlighted the potential biomarkers for early OA identification and targets for anti-inflammatory treatments aimed at delaying disease progression. A crucial modulator of TGF-β signaling is essential for maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis and delaying the onset of OA. The genetic polymorphisms in SMAD3 are associated with increased vulnerability to OA, especially in large joints [51, 52]. Chondrocyte enlargement and OA-like cartilage breakdown result from increased BMP signaling due to loss of SMAD3 activity [52, 53]. The overexpression of Smad ubiquitin regulatory factor 2 (SMURF2) further contributes to OA pathophysiology, which encourages SMAD3 dephosphorylation. The degradation of ECM may be accelerated by disruptions in TGF-β/ SMAD3 signaling, which may also lead to the overexpression of MMPs [54]. This suggests that SMAD3 polymorphisms and MMPs gene variations may interact genetically in the pathophysiology of OA. The growing understanding of these mechanisms may aid in materializing personalized treatments that restrict the progression of OA by restoring TGF-β/ SMAD3 signaling and controlling MMP activity [51].

Regulation of lipid metabolism in OA

Effects of fatty acid, and cholesterol in cartilage tissue

Lipid metabolism serves as a major role in the progression and severity of OA, influencing various pathological mechanisms. Fatty acids (FAs), the building blocks of lipids, exhibited several effects on cartilage, depending on their structure. Unsaturated FAs have double bonds between their carbon molecules, while saturated FAs (SFAs) do not exhibit such double bonds. The polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs) have multiple double bonds, whereas monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs) only have one [55, 56]. Omega-3- and omega-6 (n-3 and n-6) PUFAs play a significant role in inflammatory processes as building blocks of eicosanoids like prostaglandins, thromboxanes, resolvins, and leukotrienes [56, 57], as shown in Fig. 2 (i). While n-6 PUFAs tend to promote inflammatory pathways, n-3 PUFAs generally aid in reducing chronic inflammation [58, 59], as shown in Fig. 2 (ii).

(i) Role of FA in OA involves the excess SFAs and omega-6 PUFAs in the bloodstream, which can activate NF-κB signaling pathway, either directly or indirectly, by attaching to specific receptors. This activation leads to an increase in the expression of Prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase (PTGS) or COX, IL-1, MMP13, ADAMTS5, and COL10, worsening the condition of OA. In contrast, MUFAs and omega-3 (n-3) PUFAs can reduce the production of proinflammatory molecules, like TNF-α and IL-1, while also inhibiting the activation of the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways. (ii) Role of leptin and adiponectin in OA. The white adipose tissue releases adiponectin and leptin, which attach to their respective receptors, triggering the activation of the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, leading to increased expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, MMP-2, and MMP-9. These proinflammatory cytokines and MMPs affect articular cartilage, subchondral bone, synovial membrane, and other regions, worsening the OA condition, reproduced with permission from Ref [56], under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License

In line with a recent investigation further stated that the OA is linked to more cartilage degeneration and inflammation than rheumatoid arthritis (RA) due to a significant reduction of PUFA levels derived from synovial fluid (SF) and improved FAs expression [55, 60]. The literature also describes the significant role of biochemical mediators in the pathophysiology of OA and metabolic syndrome, highlighting disease mechanisms, including altered lipid metabolism and increased systemic and cellular expression of pro-inflammatory mediators [61]. It was also demonstrated that elevation of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and total cholesterol raises the risk of OA by accelerating cartilage degradation, pain, and disability [56, 62]. On the other hand, apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) have protective roles in OA, as higher concentrations in SF possess an opposite correlation with cartilage damage and the severity of symptoms, as shown in Fig. 2. Remarkably, plasma HDL-C levels are positively associated with OA, indicating that it has a dual effect on the pathophysiology of OA [55]. A recent study indicates that adipose tissue may influence cartilage metabolism by releasing adipokines and cytokines. Furthermore, the incidence or progression of knee OA may be linked to the use of medications that lower cholesterol levels [59]. This can be supported by the numerous animal studies conducted over the last five years, which demonstrate the role of lipid metabolism in abnormal body function of OA, with the majority of these studies reporting a correlation between lipids or adipokines and OA outcomes [55].

Signaling pathways in regulation of lipid metabolism

Lipid metabolism may play a significant role in the onset and progression of OA. This relationship can be further explored using bioinformatics analysis and machine learning algorithms. Lipid metabolism affects various processes, including immune responses, inflammatory responses, and transcriptional regulation. These processes, in turn, contribute to the breakdown of the cartilage matrix and the apoptosis of chondrocytes [57]. Adipokines like leptin, which is released by adipose tissue, will be at higher levels in the SF of obese people [63, 64], leading to inflammation and cartilage deterioration, resulting in activation of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway [65]. Retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) is a member of the lipocalin family that is present in SF and blood plasma. This RBP4 contributes to cartilage damage and inflammation by activating the Toll-like receptors-4 (TLR4) receptor, which is present in cartilage cells, at higher levels of RBP4 related to MMPs (i.e., MMP-1, MMP-3) and OA-related inflammatory markers like YKL-40 [66]. The precise molecular pathway of TLR that triggers apoptosis remains unclear. The current findings may also be translated to the clinic, especially considering the potential of soluble TLR4 to serve as a biomarker of the severity and progression of OA disease [67]. Whereas a ligand-activated transcription factor called Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) is essential for controlling lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis, and its activation exerts anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective effects in OA through modulation of adipogenesis, inflammatory signaling pathways, and macrophage polarization, which is relevant in OA. The activation of PPAR-α leads to fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake, which lowers the synthesis of inflammatory mediators, i.e., MMPs, ADAMTS-5, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in order to exhibit anti-catabolic effects on knee OA chondrocytes [68]. In addition, the Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling pathway plays a significant role in lipid metabolism and the pathogenesis of OA. When the AMPK pathway is activated, it lowers the expression of MMPs and phosphorylated nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) or phospho-NF-κB; hence, blocking the phosphorylation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the IL-1β-stimulated catabolic response, ultimately controlling the progression of OA. The AMPK activation encourages chondrocyte autophagy and suppresses the pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, and also plays a significant role in chondrocyte aging and cartilage degradation [68].

Diet, and exercise on OA modulation

The current treatment options for OA are quite limited, and patients would greatly benefit from the ability to self-manage their condition. Dietary modifications may have an impact on the composition of articular cartilage and the degree of structural damage in knee OA. Lipids accumulate in the ECM and chondrocytes of articular cartilage and could contribute to inflammation, cartilage degradation, and impaired chondrocyte function. As mentioned earlier, nutrition and diet can significantly influence pain management and improve the tolerance for functional activities in individuals with OA. The current research has demonstrated that the modulation of OA symptoms can be positively influenced by diet and exercise, which can foster an anti-inflammatory environment. The Mediterranean diet, an anti-inflammatory source, where omega-3 is present in large concentrations, has been shown to have a positive influence on the management of OA symptoms [69]. The development of OA is significantly influenced by omega-3 PUFA, which have been associated with reduced cartilage degradation, the inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine chain reactions, and the formation of oxylipins, which regulate anti-inflammatory pathways [70, 71]. The dietary interventions are associated with anti-inflammatory properties that positively influence cartilage formation, enhance joint mobility, and alleviate pain.

As Obesity is a modifiable or controllable risk factor, diet and exercise are recommended as an efficient and secure means of managing OA symptoms for reducing discomfort and improving performance in clinical guidelines for knee OA [72]. The individuals with OA face increased risk factors and experience more pain when consuming high-fat diets that contain elevated levels of saturated fats. However, losing weight can be challenging due to the pain and disability associated with OA, which can make exercise difficult. Research conducted over 18 months indicated these issues are significant. Nonetheless, a modest reduction in knee pain was achieved through a calorie-restricted diet, with a minimum of 1,100 calories per day for women and 1, 200 calories per day for men, combined with gentle exercise [69]. A study by Wang et al. in hyperlipidemic mice showed that phytosterol esters, a nutrient diet, significantly reduced serum levels of LDL-C and total cholesterol. In the conditions of OA, where lipid dysregulation, oxidative stress, cartilage deterioration, and high LDL-C levels contribute to synovial inflammation, phytosterols’ lipid-regulating action is important, which might provide symptomatic relief [73]. The potential therapeutic benefits of the Keto diet (KD) in managing OA include reducing inflammation [NOD, LRR, and Pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3), IL-1β, and IL-18], increasing β-hydroxybutyrate (βOHB), which inhibits cartilage degradation (decreasing MMP13), and promoting cartilage repair by increasing collagen type II [74].

In a comparative study, the concentration of βOHB in rats was markedly elevated by the KD, and OA damage to subchondral bone and articular cartilage was substantially decreased within the KD group, in contrast to the Standard Diet group. The rats treated with KD exhibited a significant decrease in the expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome and inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18, in contrast to the sham group. Furthermore, when comparing the KD and SD groups, MMP13 was considerably lower in the KD group, whereas collagen type II was significantly higher in the SD group [74]. By reducing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, the data indicated that KD treatment may alleviate knee inflammation in OA rats. This study could reveal a novel approach for treating OA [74]. Research indicates that individuals consume a greater amount of dietary vitamin E may have a lower risk of developing OA, or their symptoms may be less severe if they already have the condition. This association suggests that vitamin E may protect joint tissues by reducing inflammation or preventing cartilage degradation, although further investigation is required [75].

Characteristics, advancements of lipid-based delivery approaches in OA management

Lipid-based NPs are emerging as promising systems for drug delivery, offering superior drug release profiles, targeted delivery, and integrated therapeutic and diagnostic functions. The IA lipid-based nano drug delivery system offers the possibility to pass the biological barriers and ensure precise and effective therapeutic activity. Moreover, lipid-based NPs extend or improve the half-life (t1/2) of therapeutic agents and further protect from GIT microenviroment. These NPs enable the co-delivery of drugs and targeting agents, allowing for precise treatment of specific cells while functioning as local drug depots that ensure sustained release over time. The following section will discuss key strategies traditionally designed lipid-based NPs, such as liposomes, niosomes, transferosomes, SLNs, NLCs, etc., for the significant potential for OA therapy [76]. Traditional treatment often suffers from poor bioavailability, systemic side effects, and rapid clearance from the joint cavity. Lipid-based drug delivery systems have emerged as a promising approach to enhance the efficacy, bioavailability, and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents for OA management. Lipid-based carriers such as liposomes, solid lipid NPs (SLNs), nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), and lipid-based microspheres have shown potential in reducing inflammation, cartilage degradation, and pain associated with OA [77, 78]. Liposomes are spherical vesicles comprising one (unilamellar) or multiple (multilamellar) concentric lipid bilayers surrounding an internal aqueous compartment, made of natural or synthetic phospholipids and cholesterol [79]. They are highly biocompatible, biodegradable, low-toxicity, and cytotoxicity, making them feasible drug carriers [80]. The formulation of HA-Lipo-diclofenac (DIC)/ dexamethasone (DEX) was developed for improved joint pain treatment in OA patients, which encouraged chondrocyte growth and had no discernible toxicity [81]. Curcumin (Cur), a bioactive molecule with anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant properties, has been explored as a potential therapy for inflammatory illnesses. However, its medicinal potential is limited due to its high biological metabolism, weak water solubility, and low bioavailability. Lipid-based NPs have been used as delivery systems for hydrophobic drugs due to their unique properties [82]. In this context, some of the recent advancements in lipid-based delivery approaches for OA management have been further discussed in the following sections.

Liposomes

Liposomes are one of the safest and conventional first-generation lipid-based nanocarriers developed in 1970, which consist of a hydrophilic core that can easily entrap hydrophilic drugs, and the outer layer can encapsulate lipophilic agents. Typically, the liposomes range in size from 80 to 200 nm, and can carry a variety of hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, with the potential to prolong the retention in localized site and even mimic natural joint lubricants. These characteristics make them particularly valuable in the management of OA, where both anti-inflammatory action and mechanical protection of cartilage are needed. Recent advancements have focused on actively loaded liposomes, which can achieve higher encapsulation efficiency and better stability compared to the conventional passive loading method [83]. The activation of synovial macrophages leads to the production of significant amounts of ROS and nitric oxide. These molecules contribute to cartilage degradation and synovitis, worsening the progression of OA. Consequently, the recent research has turned to macrophage-targeted anti-oxidant therapy as a promising approach to mitigate joint inflammation and oxidative damage. In this instance, Zhao L et al. developed an innovative FA-modified liposomal delivery system, designed to encapsulate astaxanthin (AST) and selectively target FR-β–expressing activated synovial macrophages. The in vivo study revealed that the prolonged retention of the formulation in the inflamed joint resulted in a reduction in synovial inflammation and oxidative markers. The research evidence underscores the importance of macrophage-targeted delivery in OA management. Unlike conventional therapies that act broadly, AST@Lip-FA offers a site-specific and cell-targeted intervention, neutralizing oxidative stress precisely. This approach complements other liposome-based strategies focusing on anti-inflammatory drug delivery or joint lubrication, further broadening the therapeutic landscape of OA nanomedicine [84].

Based on the site-specific potential of liposomes, Zhong Y et al. actively loaded meloxicam (MLX) in the liposomes (MLX–Ca (AC)₂ Lipo) using a calcium acetate gradient technique. This method enabled MLX to precipitate inside the liposomes, significantly enhancing drug loading and encapsulation efficiency, as shown in Fig. 3 (i). The optimized liposomes exhibited a diameter of 110 ± 2 nm, a narrow PDI of 0.05, and a surface zeta potential of − 25.3 ± 1.2 mV, indicating excellent colloidal stability. Notably, the %EE reached 98.4%, with a drug loading capacity of 6.4%, compared to 60.6% EE and 1.4% loading in passively-loaded liposomes. Furthermore, cell-based assays using TNF-α-stimulated ATDC5 chondrocytes confirmed the biocompatibility and therapeutic potential of the formulation. MLX–Ca(AC)₂ Lipo maintained over 90% cell viability and significantly reduced apoptosis, downregulation of pro-inflammatory and catabolic markers, i.e., COX-2, MMP-3, MMP-13, and ADAMTS-4, while upregulating protective markers like collagen type II-A1, aggrecan, and PRG4 (lubricin), as summarized the results in Fig. 3 (ii). The temporomandibular joint OA (TMJ-OA) was induced in a rat model through unilateral anterior crossbite. A single IA injection of MLX–Ca (AC)₂ Lipo led to notable improvements in cartilage structure, reduced subchondral bone erosion, and decreased inflammation, confirming the preservation of cartilage matrix, while Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scores indicated a significant reduction in osteoarthritic changes. Immunohistochemistry analysis further supported the therapeutic effect, showing reduced expression of COX-2 and matrix-degrading enzymes, alongside increased lubricin expression. This study demonstrated that nanotechnology can bridge the gap between pharmacological intervention and biomechanical restoration in joint diseases such as TMJ-OA [85].

Recently, HA has demonstrated gelling properties, making it one of the most attractive materials for controlled drug release in different biomedical applications. However, HA treatment and analgesic drugs have gained significant interest and considerable therapeutic application for OA, but their combination dosage raises questions about potential biocompatible interactions and therapeutic responses, which require further investigation. In this context, Ming-Cheng et al. fabricated a novel HA-Liposomal formulation to reduce joint pain. This formulation was prepared by incorporating DIC and DEX into liposomes conjugated with HA to achieve prolonged therapeutic effects for OA. The resulting DIC/ DEX liposomes had a size of 103.6 ± 0.3 nm with a high entrapment capacity of 90.5 ± 5.6%. Further, experimental evidence showed no significant toxicities in articular chondrocyte cells. However, using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS), IA injection effectively reduced inflammation to 77.5 ± 5.1% of the initial level over that period. These findings suggest that HA-DIC/ DEX liposomes are a novel, safe, and efficient drug-release formulation, offering a promising system for OA pain management [81], as summarized in Fig. 3 (iii).

In contrast to conventional single carriers, the composite carriers, including hydrogel microspheres and liposomes, can synergistically enhance the benefits of diverse materials, demonstrating superior elasticity and flexibility, thereby positioning themselves as a promising and efficient drug delivery system. The administration of anti-oxidant medication is marked by minimal drug utilization and brief retention duration within the articular cavity, hence constraining the therapeutic efficacy of anti-oxidant medications in OA. The half-life of drug circulation can now be prolonged through the utilization of delivery vehicles, including liposomes and microspheres, which are extensively employed in disease treatment. The composite carriers of liposomes and hydrogel microspheres combine the advantages of different materials, showing improved elasticity and flexibility compared to traditional single carriers. As a result, they are expected to become new local drug delivery systems. Chondroitin sulfate (Chs), a sulfated glycosaminoglycan (GAG) common in natural cartilage, has significant antioxidant properties and is biodegradable. It is used to make an injectable Chs hydrogel through covalent modification with photo-cross-linkable methacryloyl groups or Chs methacrylate (ChsMA). Based on this instance, Yuzhe He et al. developed ChsMA microgels, anchored with liquiritin (LQ)-loaded liposomes (ChsMA@Lipo) to impede the advancement of OA through dual antioxidation, as summarized in Fig. 3 (iv). The anti-oxidant activity of LQ encapsulated ChsMA@Lipo microgels demonstrates notable sustained-release kinetics owing to the dual barrier of the lipid membrane and the hydrogel matrix network. Conversely, ChsMA can eradicate ROS by enzymatically degrading it into Chs monomers. Consequently, ChsMA@Lipo, serving as a degradable and dual anti-oxidant drug delivery system, is a promising alternative for the treatment of OA [86].

Conventional liposomes frequently demonstrate burst release, resulting in transient medication action that does not ensure prolonged alleviation. From a manufacturing perspective, scalability, elevated production costs, and reproducibility pose considerable challenges, particularly for clinical-grade formulations. Several resolutions have been proposed to address these limitations, such as incorporating cholesterol into the lipid bilayer or using PEGylated lipids, which can enhance membrane stiffness and extend systemic circulation time, respectively [87]. The surface modification utilizing targeted ligands like HA or folic acid may enhance selective accumulation in inflammatory joint tissues. The incorporation of liposomes into thermosensitive or bioadhesive hydrogels can improve local retention and facilitate prolonged release. Additionally, the co-delivery of anti-inflammatory medicines or matrix-degrading enzyme inhibitors via stimuli-responsive or hybrid liposomal systems is a promising approach to improve therapeutic efficacy [88]. Several new delivery methods, such as ultrasound-triggered release and microneedle-assisted delivery, are being explored to improve tissue penetration and control drug release in the joints in recent year.

Transferosomes

Transferosomes are an emerging type of vesicular NPs designed for drug delivery via the dermal route, which possess many similarities with liposomes and are often referred to as ultra-deformable vesicles, or ultra-flexible vesicles [89]. Structurally, transferosomes are composed of a lipid bilayer that encloses at least one internal aqueous compartment, similar to the structure of liposomes [90]. However, they are distinguished by specialized surfactants known as edge activators, contributing to their elastic properties, in which edge activators include surfactants like Tween 80, Span 80, and sodium deoxycholate. However, these edge activators, either single-chain or non-ionic surfactants, destabilize the lipid layer with lower interfacial tension, enabling the vesicles to deform with minimal energy under external mechanical stress, allowing them to penetrate the dermal barrier effectively via the intracellular lipid or transcellular pathways [91]. The primary targeted characteristic of transferosomes is their remarkable transdermal penetration capability. Their elasticity and deformability enable them to traverse intercellular spaces in the stratum corneum and penetrate deeper tissues, including the synovial regions of joints [92]. This characteristic enables the non-invasive delivery of therapeutic medicines to inflamed joints, circumventing first-pass metabolism and reducing systemic side effects. Moreover, transferosomes can be designed with surface changes to improve targeting efficacy [93]. Similar to conventional liposomes, transferosomes can encapsulate a diverse array of pharmaceuticals, encompassing tiny, moderate, and highly hydrophobic or hydrophilic compounds. In recent investigations, lecithin has emerged as a biocompatible and skin-friendly phospholipid, providing additional benefits, including anti-oxidant activity and protection against UV-induced skin damage. Incorporating transferosomes into carbopol-based gels can further enhance skin adhesion, prolong residence time, and provide a convenient dosage form for topical application. In this instance, Muhammad I et al. involved the synthesis and characterization of transferosome loaded with MLX and DEX for simultaneous transdermal delivery, in which six different transferosome formulations (TFS-1 to TFS-6) were prepared using varying amounts of lecithin, Span 80, and Tween 80. The resulting vesicles had sizes ranging from 248 to 273 nm, with zeta potential values between − 62.6 and − 69.5 mV, indicating good stability. The polydispersity index (PDI) values ranged from 0.329 to 0.526, showing a fairly uniform size distribution. In addition, the entrapment efficiency was increased, with MLX ranging from 63% to 96% and DEX from 48% to 81%. Conversely, the gel formulations exhibited permeation of only 18% and 22.94% for MLX and DEX, respectively. These results indicate that transferosomal gels-loaded with MLX and DEX can be considered an alternative drug carrier for improved transdermal flux, which will likely enhance therapeutic outcomes [94].

Nevertheless, despite their many benefits, transferosomes also exhibit significant drawbacks that might impede their practical translation and scalability [92]. A major worry is their physical and chemical instability, notably owing to edge activators like Span 80 and Tween 80, which may compromise the vesicle structure over time, resulting in drug leakage and reduced shelf life. Furthermore, transferosomes exhibit sensitivity to environmental factors like pH, temperature, and ionic strength, which may modify their shape and activity [95]. A notable problem is in their intricate manufacturing process, necessitating meticulous control over composition and preparation methods to ensure uniformity, particularly during industrial-scale production. Furthermore, the use of certain surfactants as edge activators may induce skin irritation or allergy responses, hence presenting biocompatibility issues for prolonged or chronic topical usage [95, 96]. The use of various excipients, such as phospholipids, surfactants, and stabilizers, complicates the regulatory approval procedure. Moreover, the restricted drug loading capacity for certain hydrophilic or high molecular weight drugs may limit their therapeutic usage.

Nanoemulsion (NEs)

NEs are translucent, biphasic, isotropic, and kinetically stable colloidal dispersions characterized by vesicle diameters under 200 nm, generally composed of emulsified oil and amphiphilic molecules [97]. NEs function as drug delivery system particularly for therapeutics with low solubility and permeability, improving their solubility and potentially facilitating the avoidance of first-pass metabolism after oral administration. Due to their diminutive droplet size, Brownian motion dominates gravitational forces in nanosuspensions, enhancing kinetic stability and inhibiting flocculation, interface deformation, and coalescence [98]. Nonetheless, despite their kinetic stability, NEs exhibit thermodynamic instability, indicating that they cannot form spontaneously and necessitate an external energy source for preparation [99]. NEs enable the co-encapsulation of many therapeutic agents, including anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective compounds, thereby addressing the multifactorial nature of OA. Their physical stability, ease of preparation, and scalability augment their attractiveness for therapeutic application. However, it serves as a versatile and effective platform for OA therapy by enhancing the solubility and transport of therapeutic agents, facilitating localized and prolonged drug activity, and providing possibilities for targeted distribution to inflamed joints. In this context, Rivera E et al. formulated the Cur-loaded NEs to improve absorption by employing different amounts of Tween 80, NaCl, and MgCl2 for OA therapy, as represented in Fig. 4. The NEs with 15% and 20% of Tween 80 markedly enhanced Cur absorption up to 4.4-folds, exhibited an average diameter of 215 nm, and an %EE of 58%. A subsequent evaluation was performed on healthy Wistar rats via oral administration, providing a dose of 100 mg Cur/ kg, indicating that Cur absorption was significantly greater in the NEs (17-folds). Nonetheless, the COX-2 inhibitory efficacy of Cur in NEs and microencapsulated forms demonstrated inhibition levels between 55% and 67%. Conversely, the ABTS assay indicated anti-oxidant capacities ranging from 86% to 94% and 51% to 65%. The integration of Cur into a NEs improved its absorption and preserved its potential health effects [100]. The route of administration can enhance targeted delivery to specific organs while reducing side effects. IA injections, commonly used in OA treatment, deliver high concentrations of therapeutics directly into the joint space. In this context, Mohamed et al. developed colchicine NE systems (Col-NE) using a water titration technique. The further characterization shows the diameter of 103.34 ± 5.44 nm, surface charge of 34.23 ± 0.94 mV, showing better stability, and entrapment efficiency of 75.65 ± 0.34%. Additionally, the Col-NE formulations were further assessed in monosodium urate-induced arthritic model in Swiss albino mice via IA injection. Furthermore, the biodistribution pattern was studied by incorporating technetium 99 m (99 m Tc) to track Col-NE, which revealed that IA injection of 99 m Tc-ColNE-5 significantly enhanced retention and the pharmacodynamic effects of Col compared to 99 m Tc-ColS. Herein, an injectable delivery vehicle was used to enhance retention and localization of the compound inside the inflamed joint [101].

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs)

The SLNs are submicron colloidal systems with an average diameter of 10–1000 nm, consisting of a high-melting-point lipid core such as glyceryl stearate (mono- and tri-), palmitic acid, stearic acid, and behenic acid. Additionally, components like co-surfactants, preservatives, cryoprotectants, and charge modifiers are employed in SLNs to ensure better stability. The SLNs provide several advantages, including sustained drug release, protection of incorporated thermolabile drugs from degradation, which aims to enhance the bioavailability and additionally conjugating a ligand to the surface of SLNs can enable to achieve targeted therapy [102]. In this context, an investigation conducted by Jyothi et al. evaluated the effect of various carbon chain lengths and different functional groups of lipids in SLNs to incorporate MLX. The fabricated SLNs exhibited a diameter ranging from 152 ± 17 nm to 246 ± 5 nm denotes a narrow size distribution with an %EE of 89.13 ± 0.1% to 97.81 ± 0.01%. The MLX-SLNs were incorporated into chitosan gel and assessed using ex vivo skin permeation studies in which stearyl amine-based SLNs exhibited the highest flux value of 0.76 ± 0.03 µg/cm2/h. The in vivo investigation further demonstrated that the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the MLX-SLNs-gel treated group were significantly lower than the blank SLNs gel group and the positive control group. The formulation also demonstrated superior performance in X-ray analysis and OARSI, indicating its potential as a clinically viable product [103].

A notable study was carried out by Jain A et al. to develop the diacerein (DC)-loaded SLNs (DIA-SLNs), further modified using Chs to assess the synergistic effects of these compounds in addressing the pathophysiology of OA. The prepared formulations exhibited a diameter of 396 ± 2.7 nm and enhanced the bioavailability of DIA by 2.8-folds due to the unique physicochemical properties, as shown in Fig. 5 (i). The histopathological investigations evaluated their effect on the femoral-tibial joint of the rat knee and the amount of ChS and DIA at the targeted site. However, the concentration of DC was significantly higher in the case of ChS-DC-SLNs (7.8 ± 1.23 µg/ml) than that of the pure drug (2.9 ± 0.45 µg/mL), as depicted in Fig. 5 (ii). The study demonstrated that Chs effectively targets articular cartilage, indicating significant potential to improve OA therapy. However, this research confirmed the feasibility of DIA directly to articular tissue using soluble polysaccharide Chs as the targeting vector. This method may substantially enhance the concentration of anti-arthritic drugs in joints while avoiding systemic toxicity [104].

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs)

NLCs are the second-generation lipid carriers, combining solid and liquid lipids to form a disoriented matrix through component mixing, which overcomes the limitations of SLNs, like reducing drug expulsion due to the enhanced drug-loading capacity, better stability, and biocompatibility [105]. Moreover, NLCs also provides improved controlled release characteristics. Although NLCs have a lower melting point compared to SLNs due to structural imperfections, this enhancement facilitates drug dissolution [106]. Several studies indicate that NLCs are less prone to gelation during both preparation and storage, making them well-suited for parenteral formulations and aiding in the efficient separation of NPs from their surrounding medium. Moreover, NLCs can be designed for targeted administration and may facilitate the co-encapsulation of several therapeutics, providing a synergistic strategy to tackle the multifactorial pathophysiology of OA, encompassing inflammation, oxidative stress, and cartilage deterioration [107]. Preclinical investigations have shown that NLC-based formulations substantially relieve joint pain, diminish inflammation, and preserve cartilage integrity, underscoring their potential as a patient-friendly nanocarrier technology for OA therapy [108]. Ursolic acid (UA) is a promising molecule with anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and potential anti-arthritic properties. In this instance, Aftab A et al. developed NLC-loaded with Ocimum sanctum L. leaf extract (OLE) for improved transdermal delivery of UA. The OLE-NLCs comprised with a mean particle size of ~ 120 nm, with a high %EE of UA ~ 89.56% and showed enhanced permeation significantly. The in vivo studies showed better anti-arthritic activity when compared with the standard marketed DIC gel [109]. Another study conducted by Bhosale A et al. developed lornoxicam (LRX)-loaded NLCs, demonstrating a particle size of 172.1 ± 2.0 nm. The LRX-NLCs-Gel exhibited a remarkable decline in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, presented a decrease rate of inflammation, and ameliorated cartilage structure, as shown in Fig. 6. Further studies shown a substantial reduction of pain and inflammation in addition to amended radiographic and histopathological features in monosodium iodoacetate-induced OA in Sprague Dawley rats. Thus, LRX-NLCs-Gel may be a promising candidate for translating the product into a clinical platform [110].

Lipid NPs-based hydrogels for OA

Lipid NPs (LNPs)-enhanced hydrogels have attracted considerable interest as a potential possibility for improving treatment techniques. These materials integrate unique physicochemical properties with the adaptability and biocompatibility of biomaterials, providing significant advantages over conventional therapeutic approaches. The use of nanotechnology enables the specific modulation at the nanoscale for the encapsulation and controlled release of medicinal substances, including drugs and growth factors. This focused and extended administration enhances therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic adverse effects [111]. The lipid NPs encapsulated in the hydrogel have enhanced the targeted distribution [112] of disease-modifying medicines to improve the joint tissues in OA therapy, facilitating tissue regeneration and providing significant symptom alleviation [111]. Lipid nanoparticle-based hydrogels that include biopolymers like chitosan, HA, or polyethene glycol (PEG) may be designed to replicate the original tissue microenvironment. This biomimetic approach enhances biocompatibility and reduces the risk of adverse immune responses, which is crucial for successful integration and long-term effectiveness in biomedical applications.

Recently, the CH25H-CYP7B1-RORα axis, which regulates the cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes, has played a significant catabolic role in OA pathogenesis. The targeting of metabolic pathways in chondrocytes offers a promising therapeutic approach for managing OA. In this context, an investigation conducted by Zhao Z et al. developed a synergistic therapy that integrates stem cell-recruiting hydrogels with lipid NPs to regulate cholesterol metabolism; thereby augmenting the regenerative potential of the local microenvironment to attract endogenous synovial mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and facilitate their chondrogenic differentiation. The unconjugated lipid NPs were linked to the C5-24 peptide by maleimide-thiol chemistry, as shown in Fig. 7 (i). The connected LNPs demonstrated an average diameter of around 161 nm, a zeta potential of -19.5 mV, which indicates good stability, and an entrapment efficiency of 90%. Concurrently, cholesterol-regulating lipid NPs are integrated into the hydrogel for prolonged release, enhancing the inflammatory milieu linked to OA, so successfully diminishing inflammation and recruiting MSCs from the synovial membrane. These MSCs later develop into chondrocytes, facilitating cartilage repair and chondrocyte regeneration, as supported by the results exhibited in Fig. 7 (ii). This concept presents a potential siRNA-based, nonsurgical treatment approach for OA by targeting both inflammation and cartilage repair [113].

Synovial inflammation and cartilage degeneration are key pathological features of TMJ-OA. Cartilage repair in an inflammatory environment is particularly challenging due to the presence of activated macrophages. Therefore, a therapeutic approach that simultaneously suppresses inflammation and promotes cartilage regeneration is crucial. However, conventional IA injections often suffer from insufficient inflammation control, limited cartilage repair, and a lack of targeted delivery. In this context, Yang K et al. developed dual-targeted lipid NPs co-loading with kartogenin (KGN), a small chondrogenic molecule and an inflammation inhibitor for synergistic TMJ OA therapy, as summarized in Fig. 7 (iii). The LNPs were surface-functionalized with a collagen II-targeting peptide as well as with folic acid for precise delivery to chondrocytes and macrophages, respectively. Lipid-polymer hybrid NPs

Lipid polymer hybrid NPs have gained significant interest in drug delivery applications because they can overcome the limitations of both polymer and lipid nanocarriers, and the main advantage is that the charge groups included in the polymer backbones can reduce the polymeric and water interfacial tension, which yields NPs less than 30 nm [115]. Despite their ultrasmall dimensions, these structures demonstrated substantial capacity to encapsulate hydrophobic compounds and prevent premature release [115, 116]. The potential of NPs to target cartilage has emerged as an appealing technique to enhance their penetration into cartilage, hence achieving elevated localized drug concentration [116]. In this context, positively charged NPs have gained significant attention due to their ability to spontaneously associate with GAG chains of the ECM via electrostatic interactions, which helps targeting moieties such as collagen-binding peptides and anti-collagen antibodies to conjugate with NPs, influencing the capability of active targeting into the ECM [117]. In this context, the current study by Xiangzhao AI et al. developed ultrasmall lipid-polymer NPs (LP-NPs) with a diameter of approximately 25 nm. These NPs feature a core composed of poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) and a shell made from PEG-modified lipid. Their small size is expected to enhance their ability to penetrate deeply into the ECM. Additionally, the researchers coupled the LPHNPs with a short collagen-binding peptide (WYRGRLC), which aids in the entrapment of the NPs within the ECM, improving infiltration and retention within the cartilage of the mouse femoral head. The study involved administering MK-8722, a potent activator of AMPK, known for its role in regulating chondrocyte energy metabolism and alleviating the severity of OA, as summarized in Fig. 8 (i). The collagen-targeting LPHNPs, referred to as “ctLP-NPs,” exhibited a prolonged drug release over 48 h without any leakage. When injected into the knee joints of mice with collagenase-induced OA, these NPs significantly reduced the severity of the condition. This research highlights the potential of ultrasmall ctLP-NPs as a targeted drug delivery platform for medications directed at cartilage [118].

Recently, combination treatment using multiple functional therapies encapsulated in nanocarriers has been more effective than monotherapy. Safety assessment of lipid-based delivery systems in OA management

The significant biocompatibility of lipid-based NPs exhibits lower side effects in drug delivery systems. Because of this characteristic, lipid-based NPs can be used to treat bone-related conditions like OA. With the aid of lipid-based nanosized cargo systems, therapeutic substances may be released under regulated conditions, lowering the frequency of dosages and the risk of toxicity linked to higher drug concentration. A potential therapy option for OA is lipid-based delivery systems, which offer lower toxicity, regulated drug release, and biocompatibility [77]. A recent literature evaluated the safety profile of poly(2-[methacryloyloxy]ethyl phosphorylcholine) (pMPCylated)-modified liposomes through single IA injections in Sprague-Dawley rats and sheep. Following bilateral administration of 50 µL/ joint IA administration of pMPCylated liposome injections in Sprague-Dawley rats, the therapy did not result in any mortality, clinical abnormalities, or notable changes in body weight or blood parameters. The histopathological examination confirmed excellent biocompatibility, showing no signs of liposome buildup, cartilage degradation, or joint inflammation. In a preclinical study, eight female sheep were given bilateral knee injections, with no systemic toxicity or clinical abnormalities observed. Histopathological investigation confirmed excellent biocompatibility, maintaining cartilage integrity, alive chondrocytes, and no persistent NPs or synovial inflammation, as shown in Fig. 9 (i). The results strongly suggested the safety and compatibility of pMPCylated liposomes for possible OA therapy. A randomized clinical study up to 1 year also reported that the DIC lipogel and Emulgel® performed significantly better than a placebo in treating OA. The lipogel showed faster and more sustained benefits, retaining a significant decrease in pain compared to Emulgel. By the end of four weeks, there were noticeable improvements in stiffness and physical function, with lipogel being more effective than Emulgel. No documented gastrointestinal or skin side effects were observed for any of the formulations, indicating safety of the formulations. The findings suggested that the new DIC lipogel formulation offers considerable therapeutic benefits for controlling OA symptoms, with its phospholipid-based nanovesicle architecture maximizing drug transport and reducing systemic exposure, as summarized the key results in Fig. 9 (ii), and (iii). This liposomal formulation may offer the significant efficacy and tolerability for OA patients needing long-term symptom management, potentially addressing important drawbacks of existing therapy or delivery approaches [120].

In addition, another literature reported the therapeutic potential of 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC)- d-glucosamine sulphate (GAS) liposomes for the treatment of OA. The liposomes were found to be highly biocompatible and showed no cytotoxicity to chondrocytes. They also showed potential to prevent inflammation-induced chondrocyte degradation when exposed to TNF-α. The quantitative PCR analyses showed that DSPC-GAS liposomes significantly altered key molecular markers, increasing anabolic factors and decreasing inflammatory cytokines, pain-related genes, and cartilage-degrading enzymes. Immunofluorescence labeling confirmed these results, showing decreased MMP1 expression and increased collagen type II expression in TNF-α-treated chondrocytes. The liposomal formulation had continuous release and fewer adverse effects, making it a viable option for treating OA through pain management, cartilage matrix preservation, and inflammation control. These findings provide preclinical support for the clinical use of this nanocarrier technology in OA treatment [121]. In addition, Zhao et al. developed a new, less invasive treatment for OA by integrating targeted lipid NPs into a self-assembling peptide hydrogel. The hydrogel successfully attracted synovial mesenchymal stem cells (SMSCs) and encouraged the development of chondrocytes, necessary for cartilage repair. The dose-dependent toxicity tests revealed that the cell viability tests remained above 90% even at siRNA doses between 50 nM and 3 µM indicating a low toxicity profile within this therapeutic range. The study evaluated the systemic toxicity of nanoparticle disintegration using blood tests and histological inspections. The results showed no negative effects from nanoparticle disintegration, and treated joints showed less inflammation, osteophyte growth, and retained cartilage integrity [113]. Another study was conducted on the effectiveness of emu oil-enriched NLCs loaded with LRX for treating knee OA. The study evaluated parameters such as drug release, anti-inflammatory activity, pain relief, and cartilage protection. The NLCs showed efficient drug loading and minimal waste, with sustained and controlled drug release over 24 h. The NLC formulation was more efficient than free LRX in suppressing inflammation. In vivo studies on Wistar rats showed that NLC-treated rats showed reduced pain, increased joint mobility, less cartilage degradation, decreased synovial inflammation, and enhanced chondrocyte proliferation. The inclusion of emu oil improved the drug’s skin penetration, enabling localized therapy with minimal systemic adverse effects [110].

Clinical status of lipid-based NPs in OA treatment

OA affects over 500 million individuals globally, representing a significant degenerative joint disease with an economic burden exceeding $460 billion annually [123]. Current therapeutic strategies remain predominantly palliative, relying heavily on corticosteroids and NSAIDs without addressing disease modification. This limitation has driven intensive research into innovative lipid-based drug delivery systems for IA administration, offering targeted therapeutic approaches that maximize efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. Lipid-based delivery systems, primarily liposomes and lipid NPs, have emerged as promising platforms for OA therapy due to their unique characteristics including biocompatibility, enhanced drug retention, controlled release properties, and reduced immunogenicity [76, 124]. These systems address critical challenges in OA treatment, particularly the rapid clearance of drugs from the joint space, where small molecules typically demonstrate half-lives of only 1–4 h. The encapsulation of therapeutic agents within phospholipid bilayers significantly extends drug retention and provides sustained release over extended periods [125, 126]. Several clinical applications demonstrated the potential of lipid-based systems. Lipotalon, containing liposomal DEX palmitate, represents the first commercially approved liposomal formulation for OA treatment in Germany [127]. The system preferentially targets synovial macrophages after IA injection, where intracellular esterases convert the drug to its active form while preventing the side effects associated with free DEX. EXPAREL (liposomal bupivacaine) has shown clinical efficacy in periarticular pain management following knee replacement surgery, though recent comprehensive trials indicated no significant improvement over standard bupivacaine hydrochloride in postoperative recovery outcomes [128]. Recent advances include sophisticated targeting strategies using cartilage-binding peptides and decorin-derived modifications to enhance nanoparticle retention and specificity [129, 130]. These bioinspired systems demonstrate prolonged cartilage retention for up to one month and have shown remarkable success in promoting hyaline-like cartilage regeneration in animal models. Additionally, multifunctional systems combining drug delivery with cartilage lubrication properties have demonstrated synergistic therapeutic effects. The field continues to evolve toward personalized medicine approaches, with emphasis on direct head-to-head comparisons between novel DDSs and standard treatments to accurately evaluate clinical applicability and effectiveness. Challenges, and overcome strategies of lipid-based delivery systems for OA

Stability, targeting capabilities of lipid-based delivery systems via hydrogel

Several obstacles exist to the clinical use of lipid-based delivery systems for OA. Since lipid NPs can deteriorate because of oxidation, hydrolysis, or aggregation, which might impact drug delivery, one of the primary issues is their instability. Since lipid-based NPs cannot penetrate cartilage and are rapidly cleared by the immune system, they cannot provide the precise, long-term drug administration needed to address the complex pathology of OA, which includes inflammation, cartilage degradation, and bone remodeling [26, 138]. The rapid clearance of viscoelastic SF in OA joints contributes to a decrease in bioavailability and carrier retention. Additionally, variations in manufacturing processes, including differences in particle size, charge, and drug loading, can significantly impact both reproducibility and scalability [139]. NPs are useful for drug delivery but face challenges like limited loading capacity and premature release. Hydrogels, made of three-dimensional crosslinked polymer networks, are ideal for encapsulating NPs and may further enhance the therapeutic outcomes [140]. The combination of NPs with hydrogel (NPs-hydrogel) system can extend drug retention time and modulate drug release kinetics [141]. The recent study also utilized and developed a MnO2 nanozyme-encapsulated hydrogel, which releases growth factors in plasma, attenuates oxidative stress, and promotes chondrocyte proliferation. In a monoiodoacetate (MIA)-induced rat OA model, the hydrogel platform inhibited OA development through mechanical dissipation, depressed inflammation, and facilitated cartilage repair, demonstrating the potential of NP hydrogel systems in advancing OA therapeutics [142].

PEGylation for improving retention of lipid-based delivery systems in joint tissue

PEGylation is a method that enhances the duration a drug remains in the body by lowering its clearance and breakdown, allowing it to remain in the joints for a longer time. This further boosts solubility and diminishes immunogenicity, thereby improving the delivery of therapeutics to targeted tissues [143, 144]. PEGylated is a formulation designed to target cartilage and improve OA treatment outcomes. Various preclinical studies demonstrated that the PEGylated formulations effectively lower inflammation and halt the disease course. In this context, a recent study proposed a new treatment for OA using a cartilage-targeting polymer-drug delivery system, formononetin (FMN) coupled with polyethylene glycol (PEG), or PCFMN. This system aimed to increase treatment efficacy and reduce side effects associated with conventional therapies. PCFMN was found to be biocompatible in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes, promoting anabolic genes while downregulating catabolic genes. The in vivo studies showed PCFMN slows OA progression, exhibits better retention in joints, and has stronger anti-inflammatory effects than free FMN [145]. In addition, Kang et al. developed a new HA-based hydrogel with PEGylated kartogenin (KGN) micelles for the treatment of OA. The hydrogels were designed to deliver a sustained release of KGN, with an initial burst during the first 12 h and a subsequent prolonged release phase. This sustained release profile is essential for sustaining therapeutic levels in the joint space over an extended period. The study indicated that the HA/PEG/KGN hydrogels significantly inhibited the progression of OA in rat models, indicating a more powerful therapeutic effect due to their longer retention time. The hydrogels were also enzymatically degradable by hyaluronidase and collagenase, allowing for regulated breakdown within the body. These characteristics make HA/PEG/KGN hydrogels a potentially effective long-term treatment for OA. The covalent nature of the formulation increased stability, decreased HA degradation, and prolonged joint retention [146].

Improved drug release via combination of lipid-based delivery systems and polymeric NPs

NPs can improve OA treatment by providing sustained drug release, targeted delivery, and improved therapeutic effects through mechanisms such as thermosensitivity, pH-responsiveness, and photothermal activation. A thermosensitive polymeric NPs-hydrogel system has been developed for OA treatment, incorporating triamcinolone acetonide (TCA). The system’s sol-gel transition properties allow it to remain a liquid at lower temperatures and transform into a viscous hydrogel at body temperature, mimicking SF. This allows for controlled and sustained release of TCA over up to six weeks, maintaining therapeutic drug levels in the joint, resulting in more effective pain relief and reduced inflammation compared to traditional corticosteroid injections [147]. The NO-Hb@siRNA@PLGA-PEG (NHsPP) nanogenerators are a photothermal-triggered nitric oxide (NO) delivery system that combines near-infrared (NIR)-activated hemoglobin (Hb) NPs with siRNA for enhanced anti-inflammatory effects. The combination of siRNA/NO with photothermal treatment enhances therapeutic outcomes, inhibiting inflammation and increasing treatment effectiveness [148]. Several innovative or smart delivery systems like pH-responsive drug delivery systems are designed to improve OA treatment by utilizing the acidic environment in joint cavities. These systems use pH-sensitive NPs to release bioactive components, drugs, and nucleic acids, allowing targeted therapy. The controlled release rate can be adjusted to meet patient’s therapeutic needs, resulting in more efficient drug delivery and longer-lasting effects [149].

Improved penetration of lipid NPs via GAGs

A study found that small-sized lipid NPs can enhance medication penetration and retention in cartilage for OA treatment. The ECM made up of collagen fibers and proteoglycans, makes it difficult for bigger particles to move. To improve adhesion and prolong retention, cartilage-targeting ctLP-NPs were created to interact with negatively charged GAGs and examined their retention in mouse joint cartilage. It was found that ctLP-NPs were more effective in entering and remaining in the cartilage matrix than non-targeted LP-NPs. Drug-loaded ctLP-NPs significantly decreased cartilage destruction and reduced OA severity in mice with collagenase-induced OA [118]. The two DEX prodrugs were developed to improve drug delivery for OA treatment. The first, chitosan- DEX conjugate, enhances drug penetration by forming strong electrostatic interactions with GAGs in the cartilage ECM, allowing deeper infiltration and prolonging drug residence time. The second, DEX conjugated to a peptide (WYRGRL), binds exclusively to collagen type II, keeping medication in deeper cartilage zones. In an ex vivo model, these conjugates decreased inflammatory markers and slowed GAG loss, suggesting potential efficacy in treating OA [150]. OA caused by friction-related joint damage, leading to cartilage degradation, synovitis, subchondral bone damage, and osteophyte formation. The current treatments, like anti-inflammatory drugs and HA injections, have side effects like gastrointestinal issues and compromised lubrication. A study by Zhao et al. investigated P(DMA-co-MPC), a copolymer combining dopamine and poly (2-methacryloxyethyl phosphorylcholine) (PMPC), to reduce cytotoxicity and inflammation in OA. PMPC mimics phosphatidylcholine and enhances hydration lubrication, while dopamine’s hydroquinone moiety provides anti-oxidant properties. It also inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokines and catabolic genes, making it a promising therapeutic strategy [151]. Traditional treatments like NSAIDs and DEX have side effects and toxicity risks. A novel drug delivery system called HA-Lipo-DIC/DEX, combining HA and lipid-based NPs encapsulating DIC and DEX was designed to improve Knee OA treatment which could be an alternative to conventional OA treatments, offering sustained pain relief, enhanced lubrication, and reduced inflammation with fewer side effects, ultimately improving patient outcomes [81]. Articular cartilage degradation and joint capsule inflammation are often linked to lubrication dysfunction. Combining enhanced joint lubrication with anti-inflammatory therapy is considered an effective treatment approach. A super-lubricated microspheres was developed to treat articular cartilage degradation and joint capsule inflammation. These microspheres, inspired by ball-bearing lubrication and mussel adhesion, are grafted onto GelMA microspheres to create a hydration layer that improves lubrication, prevents degradation, and allows sustained drug release. The hydrogel microspheres exhibit high dispersibility, viscoelasticity, and efficient localized drug delivery, making them suitable for OA treatment, offering enhanced lubrication, anti-inflammatory effects, and pain relief while maintaining good biocompatibility and structural stability [152].

Concluding remarks

OA represents a complex, polygenic disease with substantial global health and economic implications that were dramatically amplified in recent years. While revealing critical vulnerabilities in healthcare delivery systems, it also accelerated the adoption of digital health technologies and highlighted the disease’s susceptibility during global health emergencies. There is an urgent need for improved therapeutic outcome in OA treatment and the conventional delivery approaches or therapeutics should be modified or addressed. In this context, this manuscript introduced the effective delivery approaches of therapeutics via lipid-based NPs in OA management. As we aware that, most of the conventional drugs are lipophilic in nature and therefore to cross the biological barrier, the drug requires a suitable carrier. In this context, lipid-based NPs preferred due to encapsulation efficiency of lipophilic and hydrophilic drug in core structure, sustained drug release, improved bioavaibility, which are the major challenges in conventional dosage forms. This characteristic leads to reduction of dosing frequency and offers significant advantages via IA administration in OA management. However, the current investigations on lipid-based NPs exhibited or reported certain limitations, i.e., rapid synovial clearance and poor retention lead to off-target accumulation, aggregation during lyophilization, endosomal entrapment and difficulty penetrating dense cartilage ECM, etc. The future lipid NPs need to feature advanced responsive mechanisms targeting OA-specific microenvironments. The pH-responsive systems utilizing decorin-derived peptides demonstrate prolonged cartilage retention for one month, enabling sustained dual-drug release through interlayer cross-linking. The MMP-responsive PEGylated lipid NPs can also achieve preferential distribution in inflammatory joints with rapid drug release triggered by overexpressed MMPs. These bioinspired cartilage-binding peptides show promise for hyaline-like cartilage regeneration in full-thickness defects [153, 154]. In addition, the 3D-printed microfluidic devices can revolutionize lipid NPs production with 5-fold higher throughput compared to conventional systems. The Omnidirectional sheath-flow enabled microfluidics (OSEM) device produces particles < 90 nm with low polydispersity (2–8%) and > 90% encapsulation efficiency. In addition, the literature also reported the pressure-assisted micro-syringe printing enables personalized buccal films loaded with lipid carriers, demonstrating sustained release profiles for 84.11% drug release over 6 h [155]. Future systems may also integrate lipid NPs within smart hydrogels for synergistic effects. Stem cell-recruiting hydrogels combined with cholesterol metabolism-modulating LNPs promote regenerative microenvironments while improving inflammatory conditions. The injectable microenvironmentally responsive bilayer systems provide multi-layered responses with precise controlled drug release, cartilage repair, and joint lubrication capabilities [113]. The artificial intelligence (AI) integration can enable real-time optimization of nanoparticle design, predicting interactions with drugs, immune systems, and biological fluids. NPs-enabled molecular imaging will revolutionize diagnosis through enhanced detection sensitivity of key biomarkers including proteoglycans and inflammatory factors. These advances promise theranostic applications combining diagnostic imaging with targeted therapy delivery for comprehensive OA management [156]. The combination of biomarkers and imaging NPs provides a crucial opportunity for personalized diagnosis and treatment of OA. While solving the bottlenecks of existing technologies, exploring innovative applications in molecular diagnosis imaging of OA is expected to promote the development of precision medicine for OA, ultimately improving the quality of life and life expectancy of patients with OA.
