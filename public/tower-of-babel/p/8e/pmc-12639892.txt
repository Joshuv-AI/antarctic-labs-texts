Risk of dengue following prior SARS-CoV-2 infection: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Liang En Wee, Jue Tao Lim, An Ting Tay, Borame Dickens, Pei Ma, Calvin Chiew, Po Ying Chia, Yee Sin Leo, Lee Ching Ng, David Chien Lye, Kelvin Bryan Tan

Research in context

Evidence before this study

DENV-infection and SARS-CoV-2 are now endemic in tropical regions; potential interactions between co-circulating infections may exist. We conducted a Pubmed search up to 1st May 2025 using the following search terms: “dengue”, “COVID-19”, “SARS-CoV-2”, to evaluate evidence of bi-directional association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and dengue infection. Three population-based cohort studies raised the possibility of interaction between DENV-infection and subsequent SARS-CoV-2 infection, though results were heterogenous; increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection/severe COVID-19 post-DENV-infection were reported in two studies, while one study reported lower risk of COVID-19-associated mortality amongst individuals reporting prior DENV-infection. However, cohort studies evaluating the impact of COVID-19 on subsequent DENV-infection are limited; a single study reported greater odds of subsequent adult DENV-infection with prior COVID-19, though small sample size (N = 1701) and methodological limitations significantly limited generalisability. Strain-specific differences in cross-reactivity may account for potential heterogeneity in observations; prior in-vitro studies demonstrated high cross-reactivity and neutralisation of all DENV serotypes by sera from COVID-19 cases infected during earlier pandemic waves (ancestral/Delta-strains), but lower cross-reactivity and possible antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV3 infection post-Omicron COVID-19.

Added value of this study

We utilised national registry data in a setting where diagnostic testing for dengue/SARS-CoV-2-infection was widely available and both infections were notifiable diseases, to evaluate the effects of recent preceding COVID-19 on risk of subsequent notified dengue via a population-based retrospective cohort study design. Significantly increased risk of dengue was observed 31–300 days post-COVID-19; excess-burden (EB) of dengue post-COVID-19 was however estimated at 0.36 cases per-1000-individuals. Risks of subsequent dengue were numerically highest in unvaccinated COVID-19 cases but not elevated in doubly-boosted COVID-19 cases. The risk of negative-outcome controls (other infections) was not significantly elevated 31–300 days post-SARS-CoV-2 infection, and risk of dengue was not significantly elevated in negative-exposure controls (influenza vaccination).

Implications of all the available evidence

Increased risk of DENV-infection (primarily DENV3) was observed up to 300 days post-SARS-CoV-2 infection (predominantly Omicron). While not irrefutable proof for possible antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV3-infection post-Omicron COVID-19, given modest effect sizes, future studies can shed more light on potential interactions.

Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, surges in dengue-virus (DENV) transmission were reported across multiple countries in DENV-endemic tropical regions,1,2 placing significant strain on hospitals and healthcare systems. Sequential waves of SARS-CoV-2 and DENV transmission can potentially overwhelm less well-resourced healthcare systems, especially in low-and-middle-income countries where both DENV and COVID-19 are now endemic. Various population-based epidemiological studies raised the possibility of synergistic interaction between SARS-CoV-2 infection and DENV, though results were heterogenous. While some studies reported increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection and severe COVID-19 following prior DENV-infection,1,2 other studies reported lower risk of COVID-19-associated mortality amongst individuals who reported prior DENV-infection.3

Several mechanisms have been postulated to account for these observations, including antigenic cross-reactivity between DENV and SARS-CoV-2,4 which could potentially result in cross-neutralization and reduced severity,5, 6, 7 or conversely antibody-dependent-enhancement (ADE) and increased severity.8 While other epidemiological studies suggested increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection following prior COVID-19,9 small sample sizes (N = 1701) significantly limited generalisability. New-onset autoimmune sequelae have been reported in the post-acute phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection10,11; underlying immune dysfunction may thus potentially predispose to increased susceptibility for non-COVID-19 infectious diseases. However, no overall increased risk of hospitalisation attributable to non-COVID-19 infectious diseases post-SARS-CoV-2 infection was observed in a large Danish population-based cohort study (N = 2,430,694),12 though DENV-infection was not specifically studied as an outcome, given absence of DENV circulation in a non-endemic region. Restricted availability of diagnostic testing for DENV/SARS-CoV-2-infection significantly limits the ability to conduct epidemiological studies evaluating subsequent DENV-infection risk following COVID-19 in under-resourced tropical settings. We utilised national registry data in a setting where diagnostic testing for DENV/SARS-CoV-2-infection was widely available and both infections were notifiable diseases, to evaluate the effects of recent preceding SARS-CoV-2-infection on risk of subsequent DENV-infection in a population-based cohort.

Methods

Study setting and databases

Singapore is a multi-ethnic tropical Southeast Asian city-state (population: 6.03 million). Fully-subsidised SARS-CoV-2 diagnostic testing (polymerase-chain-reaction[PCR]/rapid-antigen-testing[RAT]) was widely available across healthcare settings.13,14 SARS-CoV-2 testing was fully subsidised and mandatory for all individuals presenting with symptoms of acute-respiratory-illness (ARI) to healthcare settings; results were legally notifiable to the local Ministry-of-Health (MOH) within 24-h of diagnosis and recorded in the national COVID-19 registry.14 The study period of 1st Sept 2021–31st Dec 2022 covered emergence of Omicron BA.1/2 in January 2022, which displaced Delta as the predominant strain14; as well as subsequent emergence of BA.4/5 (June 2022) and XBB (October 2022) (Fig. 1).15 Extensive public health measures, including strict testing and quarantine of exposed contacts, kept community transmission of SARS-CoV-2 in check pre-Delta; emergence of the more transmissible Omicron variant marked a shift in public health strategies away from containment and towards managing endemicity.14 Vaccination status of COVID-19 cases at the point of infection was obtained from National-Immunisation-Registry records; ≥90% of adult Singaporeans completed a two-dose primary series of mRNA COVID-19 vaccines (first rolled out in 2020),15 with booster vaccinations (≥3 vaccine doses) rolled out in Sept 2021. Vaccination/booster uptake was high due to widespread availability of free COVID-19 vaccination in primary care and vaccine-differentiated public health measures, in which restrictions on activities such as dining-out and return-to-work were placed on unvaccinated individuals.16

Similarly, DENV-infection is legally notifiable to the MOH not later than 24 h from clinical diagnosis.1 Confirmatory diagnostic testing for DENV-infection is widely available across healthcare settings in Singapore, with rapid-diagnostic-tests widely utilised in primary care,17 and PCR/enzyme-linked-immunoassay[ELISA] tests available in hospitals.1,18 Testing results are requested at point-of-notification.1,17,18 A switch in the predominant serotype from DENV1/2 to DENV3 drove a DENV3 surge in 2022 (Fig. 1); Singapore had not previously experienced a DENV3 outbreak in the past 3 decades.1 National registries for SARS-CoV-2/DENV were used to construct cohorts of adult Singaporeans first infected with SARS-CoV-2/DENV during the study period.

Cohort construction

A retrospective cohort study design was utilised; a flowchart of cohort construction is provided in Fig. 2. A cohort of adult Singaporeans (aged≥18 years) first infected with SARS-CoV-2 from 1st Sept 2021–31st Dec 2022 was constructed, together with a contemporaneous cohort of test-negative controls drawn from adult individuals presenting to healthcare settings during the same period, with documented negative SARS-CoV-2 test (PCR/RAT). T0, the index-date, was taken as date-of-test. Individuals with missing sociodemographic data or who died within 30 days of T0 were excluded; individuals infected/reinfected with SARS-CoV-2 within 300 days of T0 or with a history of dengue infection in the 5 years before T0 were also excluded to minimize potential confounding introduced by historical infection and subsequent reinfection during the study period. Rates of dengue/COVID-19 co-infection were extremely low (0.03%), even on systematic testing of all individuals presenting with undifferentiated-febrile-illness18; hence test-positives/test-negatives infected with dengue within 30 days of T0 were excluded from analysis.

Flowchart on the left-hand side indicates the cohort construction flowchart for COVID-19 test-positives; flowchart on the right-hand side indicates the cohort construction flowchart for COVID-19 test-negatives.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was notified DENV-infection, defined as a DENV-infection with corresponding laboratory confirmation (antigen/PCR/ELISA) recorded in the national registry, occurring in the post-acute period 31–300 days following T0. The initial time period of 31–300 days following T0 was chosen given initial postulation of an autoimmune mechanism underlying initial SARS-CoV-2 infection and subsequent risk of DENV-infection (e.g., antigenic cross-reactivity); other prior studies found increased risk of new-onset autoimmune disorders up to 300 days following Omicron COVID-19 hospitalisation.11

Covariates

The following covariates were included in the main analysis, classified using databases maintained by the local MOH: demographics (age, sex, ethnicity[Chinese/Malay/Indian/Others]), socioeconomic status (SES), COVID-19 vaccination status at T0, comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index), prior healthcare utilisation (defined as prior emergency-department/hospitalisation recorded in the year preceding T0), and T0 (week). Comorbidities were classified based on International-Classification-of-Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes recorded in the national healthcare-claims database (Mediclaims),11 which records claims made at public/private healthcare providers against the national medical-savings-scheme (Medisave) and the national health-insurance-plan (Medishield), enabling comprehensive capture across different healthcare settings. Housing type was used as a surrogate marker of SES, as ≥90% of Singaporeans stay in owner-occupied public-housing and purchase eligibility is linked to household income under a tiered subsidy scheme.16

Statistical analysis

Risks of DENV-infection 31–300 days following T0 (index-date) were estimated in adult COVID-19 cases, using contemporaneous test-negatives as a comparator. Baseline characteristics of COVID-19 cases/test-negatives, along with standardised-mean-differences (SMD) between-groups, were computed; proportions were presented for categorical variables and means/standard-deviations presented for continuous variables. Between-group differences were adjusted using overlap-weighting; where overlap weights were defined as 1-propensity scores for COVID-19 cases and equivalent to the propensity scores for test-negatives, thereby addressing the issue of extreme propensity scores by down-weighting patients in the tails of the propensity-score distribution.19 All available covariates were included in overlap weighting. SMD <0.1 was taken as the threshold for good covariate balance post-weighting. Hazard-ratios (HRs) of DENV-infection 31–300 days following T0 in COVID-19 cases/test-negatives were then estimated using Cox regression, with overlap weights applied; the proportional-hazards assumption was checked using graphical plots of the Schoenfeld residuals. Excess-burden (EB) of subsequent DENV-infection per 1000 individuals at 300 days of follow-up was defined as the increase/decrease in incidence rate of subsequent DENV-infection in COVID-19 cases versus test-negatives. To contextualise effect-size estimates for observed associations between COVID-19 and subsequent DENV-infection, the number-needed-to-harm (NNH) was additionally calculated by taking the reciprocal of the absolute-risk-increase. Subgroup analyses by age (18–34/35-64/≥65 years), sex, predominant SARS-CoV-2 variant in community circulation at T0 (Delta/Omicron), and COVID-19 vaccination status at T0 (unvaccinated/fully-vaccinated, boosted[single booster]/doubly-boosted) were also conducted. Risk trajectories of subsequent notified DENV-infection were estimated between COVID-19 cases and test-negatives using the Kaplan–Meier approach, with overlap weights applied, to compare between-group differences in outcome probabilities over the post-acute follow-up period. Outcome probabilities were taken as one minus-survival probabilities. Additionally, risks of subsequent DENV-infection at different time horizons (90-days/150-days/450-days/600-days) post-COVID-19 were evaluated using similar processes for cohort construction and analytical frameworks, to assess if observed associations persisted over a longer follow-up period.

The following sensitivity analyses were conducted. (1) Inverse-probability-weights were utilised as an alternative weighting scheme in place of overlap weights to estimate HRs, with use of the doubly-robust approach, where covariates used to construct inverse-probability-weights were included as explanatory variables in the model specification. (2) Unweighted Cox regression was performed as an alternative to weighting, including all specified covariates in regression models. (3) Risks of DENV-infection 31–300 days post-T0 in COVID-19 cases/test-negatives were compared using competing-risks-regression, with death taken as a competing risk. (4) As the majority of COVID-19 cases were mild and did not require hospitalisation, we evaluated if exclusion of hospitalised COVID-19 cases altered risk estimates of subsequent DENV-infection. (5) Given prior reports of false-positive DENV serology following COVID-19,20 we specifically evaluated risk of subsequent DENV-infections with NS1/PCR confirmation following COVID-19 infection; DENV cases without NS1/PCR positivity (i.e only IgM/IgG positivity) were excluded to minimise potential misclassification. (6) To adjust for possible differential exposure risk arising from occupation, occupational status (healthcare workers, essential workers) was additionally included in weighting schemes (Supplementary Material). (7) Given prior reported associations between other anthropogenic factors (e.g., population density),1,21, 22, 23 ecological factors (e.g., temperature/humidity) and transmission of SARS-CoV-2/DENV, population density and ecological parameters for the spatial unit corresponding to an individual's area of residence were additionally included in weighting schemes. Spatial units were defined by subdividing Singapore's land area into regular hexagons with a circumradius of 200m. Aggregated population size in each hexagon was obtained from Singapore's urban-planning agency.1 Information on ecological variables associated with DENV-infection in our local setting (total daily rainfall, highest 60 min rainfall, mean temperature, mean wind speed) was also obtained from meteorological data collected over the study period for each spatial unit and harmonised to a weekly time scale, based on prior published methodology.23 Clustering by location of residence (postal code) was also additionally accounted for, using cluster variance estimation. (8) To ascertain the level of unmeasured confounding required to render the risk of subsequent dengue infection in the post-acute phase following SARS-CoV-2 infection insignificant,24 E-values were calculated for the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and subsequent DENV-infection. (9) The risk of negative-outcome controls (various infections, other than DENV-infection) between COVID-19 cases and test-negatives at 31–300 days from T0 was additionally evaluated, in order to mitigate potential biases due to differences in health-seeking behaviour between COVID-19 cases and test-negatives. Increased healthcare utilization and closer follow-up/surveillance post-COVID-19 might result in higher apparent risk of a post-infectious diagnosis. Infections utilised as negative-outcome controls included: invasive-pneumococcal-disease, tuberculosis, melioidosis, and leptospirosis, identified in national healthcare-claims (Mediclaims) data using specific ICD-10 codes. These outcomes were chosen as negative-outcome controls because no prior knowledge, to-date, supports the existence of a causal association between SARS-CoV-2-infection and subsequent risk of these specific infections.12 While increased diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis was observed amongst COVID-19 cases in our local population, this likely stemmed from increased frequency of screening via chest imaging; rates of extrapulmonary tuberculosis remained constant.25 In an Australian study, profiles of melioidosis cases were similar between those with and without prior SARS-CoV-2 infection.26 All infections chosen as negative-outcome controls were also legally notifiable to the local MOH, allowing for comparable case ascertainment. (10) Finally, influenza vaccination (defined using the National-Immunisation-Registry) was used as a negative-exposure control. As association between influenza vaccination and risk of subsequent DENV-infection was biologically implausible, this helped to ascertain if increased healthcare utilization following a healthcare contact could introduce potential bias resulting in increased apparent risk of subsequent DENV-infection. Similar cohort construction rules were utilised, with T0 taken as the date of vaccination in influenza-vaccinated individuals, and randomly assigned in the unvaccinated cohort based on the distribution of T0 amongst influenza-vaccinated individuals. Risk of subsequent DENV-infection 31–300 days from T0 was subsequently contrasted between influenza-vaccinated and influenza-unvaccinated individuals.

Ethics approval

This study was undertaken as national public-health-research under the Infectious-Diseases-Act, Singapore; as such, separate ethics review by an Institutional-Review-Board was not required, under the stipulations of the Act. All data was deidentified prior to analysis.

Results

Characteristics of study cohort

1,427,943 COVID-19 cases were recorded during the study period, of which 96.4% (1,324,250/1,427,943) fulfilled inclusion criteria and were included in the final study cohort, along with 1,434,851 contemporaneous test-negatives (Fig. 2). Demographic and clinical characteristics for the COVID-19 and test-negative cohorts are presented in Table 1. ≥99% of COVID-19 cases had completed a primary COVID-19 vaccination series; 81.9% (1,083,900/1,324,250) were boosted, and 92.2% (1,221,548/1,324,250) were infected during Omicron-predominant transmission. The vast majority of COVID-19 cases had mild infection; only 2.8% (37,053/1,324,250) were hospitalised. Post-weighting, patient characteristics were balanced between SARS-CoV-2-infected and test-negative groups, with all SMDs<0.1 (Table 1).

Risk of DENV-infection in post-acute phase following SARS-CoV-2 infection

Less than 1% (0.40%, 5276/1,324,250) of COVID-19 cases were subsequently infected with DENV up to 300 days following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Of DENV-infections for which individual-level sequencing data was available (N = 2399), the vast majority (81.7%, 1959/2399) were attributed to DENV3 (Supplementary Table 1). Significantly increased risk of DENV-infection was observed 31–300 days post-COVID-19 (adjusted-hazards-ratio, aHR = 1.10[95%CI = 1.05–1.15]), versus test-negatives; the excess-burden (EB) of DENV-infection following COVID-19 was estimated at 0.36 cases (95%CI = 0.11–0.61) per-1000-individuals (Fig. 3). The number-needed-to-harm was estimated at N = 2439 (95%CI = 1639–5000) (Supplementary Table 2). Differences in probabilities of subsequent DENV-infection between COVID-19 cases and test-negatives diverged over the 31–300 day follow-up period (Fig. 3). Increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection post-COVID-19 was still observed when the follow-up period was extended up to 600 days (450-days post-COVID-19: aHR = 1.09[95%CI = 1.05–1.14]; 600-days post-COVID-19: aHR = 1.09[95%CI = 1.04–1.15]); while numerically increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection was observed 31–90 days post-COVID-19 (aHR = 1.06[95%CI = 0.97–1.15]), it did not cross the threshold for statistical significance (Fig. 3).

When risks of DENV-infection following COVID-19 were compared across subgroups, increased risk of DENV-infection post-COVID-19, versus test-negatives, was observed in both males/females and adults aged 18–34/35-64 years, but not amongst older adults aged ≥65 years (Fig. 4). During Delta, numerically elevated risk of subsequent DENV-infection in COVID-19 cases (N = 102,702) versus test-negatives did not cross the threshold of statistical significance; but when restricted to Omicron-predominant transmission, risk of subsequent DENV-infection in COVID-19 cases (N = 1,221,548), versus test-negatives, was still significantly increased (aHR = 1.12[95%CI = 1.06–1.18]) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 3). Risks of subsequent DENV-infection following SARS-CoV-2 infection were numerically highest in cases unvaccinated for COVID-19 (aHR = 1.54[95%CI = 1.17–2.03]), though confidence-intervals overlapped; risk of subsequent DENV-infection was not significantly increased in doubly-boosted COVID-19 cases (aHR = 1.14[95%CI = 0.83–1.57]), versus test-negatives (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 3).

HR = 1 is denoted by the solid vertical line; dots represent the estimates of HRs, with error bars representing the 95% confidence-intervals for those estimates.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses employing competing-risks-regression, a doubly-robust approach with inverse-propensity-weights, or unweighted Cox regression with the same variables included as covariates, did not significantly influence estimates of increased risk for DENV-infection following SARS-CoV-2 infection, versus test-negatives (Supplementary Table 4). When restricted to mild COVID-19 cases (N = 1,287,197), increased risk/EB of subsequent DENV-infection was still observed in mild COVID-19 cases not requiring initial hospitalisation, versus test-negatives (Supplementary Table 4). Increased risk/EB of subsequent DENV-infection post-COVID-19 was still observed when restricted to DENV-infections with NS1/PCR confirmation, excluding DENV-cases with positive serology (IgM/IgG) alone (Supplementary Table 4). Additional adjustment for occupation did not significantly alter risk estimates (Supplementary Table 4). Controlling for clustering by area of residence, average area-level population density and ecological variables (rainfall/temperature/wind speed) did not result in significantly different risk estimates (Supplementary Table 5). The E-value for association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and subsequent DENV-infection was 1.43, suggesting that residual confounding could explain the observed association if there existed an unmeasured covariate having a risk association at least as large as 1.43 with both SARS-CoV-2 infection and subsequent DENV-infection. Risk of negative-outcome controls (invasive-pneumococcal-disease/tuberculosis/melioidosis/leptospirosis) was not significantly elevated 31–300 days following SARS-CoV-2 infection, versus test-negatives (Supplementary Table 6). Risk of DENV-infection 31–300 days following influenza vaccination (the negative-exposure control) was not significantly different from that of unvaccinated individuals (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort comprising 1,324,250 COVID-19 cases (predominantly mild vaccine-breakthrough Omicron infections) and 1,434,851 contemporaneous test-negatives, increased risk/EB of subsequent DENV-infection was observed following SARS-CoV-2-infection, and risk trajectories of subsequent DENV-infection significantly diverged over the 31–300 day follow-up period, versus test-negatives. Risks of subsequent DENV-infection were significantly elevated across most demographic subgroups and numerically highest in unvaccinated COVID-19 cases. In contrast, no significantly elevated risk of other notifiable infections was observed 31–300 days following SARS-CoV-2 infection, versus test-negatives.

Elucidating the bidirectional impact of protection in COVID-19 and DENV-infection is of public health importance, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where both COVID-19 and DENV are now endemic. Previous population-based epidemiological studies investigating risk of subsequent SARS-CoV-2 infection after prior DENV-infection yielded mixed results. While prior population-based studies in Brazil and Singapore demonstrated increased risk of subsequent SARS-CoV-2 infection and more severe COVID-19 with prior DENV-infection,1,2 a separate study in Brazil found decreased risk of mortality at up to 60 days post-COVID-19 amongst individuals with a prior history of DENV-infection; though DENV-infection history was self-reported and the cohort size was small (N = 2351).3 This latter report from Brazil was, however, supported by mechanistic in-vitro studies suggesting DENV-infection could potentially impart broad-spectrum protective immunity against SARS-CoV-2 in endemic regions, especially during pandemic waves attributed to ancestral/Delta-variants.6 Fewer studies have evaluated the impact of COVID-19 on subsequent DENV-infection. Increased risk of symptomatic dengue following prior COVID-19 was observed in a small cohort study (N = 1701) from North India, predominantly comprising healthcare-workers; however, self-reporting of COVID-19 status might have resulted in misclassification bias and small sample size limited generalisability.9 In this large population-based cohort (N = 2,759,101) conducted using national registers for COVID-19/DENV, during a time period where diagnostic testing for both infections was widely available in primary care and frequently conducted for symptomatic individuals with undifferentiated febrile illness,13,14,17,18 we found a statistically significant association between SARS-CoV-2 Omicron infection and subsequent DENV-infection (predominantly DENV3). Possible antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV3-infection following prior Omicron COVID-19, as demonstrated in in-vitro studies,8 may provide a potential mechanistic explanation for these observations; though our findings demonstrate correlation, not causation, and the observed association was of a modest effect size, with an E-value of 1.43 for residual confounding. In our cohort, HRs for known risk factors of DENV-infection (e.g., comorbidity burden, immunocompromise) ranged between 1 and 1.5, and multiple clinical, anthropogenic and ecological factors were adjusted for; it was felt less plausible, though not impossible, that an unmeasured confounder could have a substantially greater impact on development of subsequent DENV-infection than these known risk factors by having a relative risk exceeding 1.43. Excess-burden of subsequent DENV-infection following initial SARS-CoV-2-infection was estimated at 0.36 cases per-1000-individuals, with NNH estimated at ∼2500, in the context of a pandemic surge driven by the Omicron variant during which ≥1 million COVID-19 cases were recorded. During the same period in 2022, Singapore experienced a DENV3-outbreak (incidence-rate of 570.8 cases per 100,000 person-years), with ≥32,000 DENV-infections reported.27 The diminishing force of infection during successive COVID-19 waves during endemicity may therefore limit the practical significance of the observed association for public health policy; though current incidence of COVID-19 cases may be underestimated, and SARS-CoV-2 and DENV-infection are now endemic in tropical countries.

Several mechanisms have been postulated to account for increased risk of DENV-infection following COVID-19. Persistent dysregulation of innate and adaptive immunity has been reported post-SARS-CoV-2 infection, with development of new-onset auto-immune sequelae, particularly after severe COVID-19;10,11 these auto-immune sequelae can theoretically increase risk for other non-COVID-19 infections. While no significant increase in the overall risk of hospitalisation for non-COVID-19 infections has been reported from large cohorts of COVID-19 survivors,12 these studies relied on claims-based infectious diagnoses, which may significantly underestimate the burden of milder infections not necessitating hospitalisation; additionally, risk of subsequent DENV-infection post-COVID-19 was not specifically evaluated. Alternatively, antigenic cross-reactivity between DENV and SARS-CoV-2 has been reported in the literature,4, 5, 6, 7, 8 although it remains unclear if such cross-reactive antibodies provide cross-protection or instead promote increased susceptibility/severity. Strain-specific differences in cross-reactivity and cross-protection may also occur. Several in-vitro studies reported that sera from COVID-19 cases infected during earlier pandemic waves (ancestral/Delta-strains) were highly cross-reactive to DENV and could neutralise all DENV serotypes5,7,8; this might have provided a possible mechanistic explanation for lower incidence of DENV-infection observed in our population during the Delta wave (<100 cases per 100,000 person-years in 2021),27 with no increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection following SARS-CoV-2 infection attributed to the Delta-variant. In contrast, increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection was observed post-Omicron COVID-19, in keeping with other in-vitro studies that highlighted possible antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV3 infection following prior Omicron COVID-19.8 Sera from COVID-19 survivors infected during Omicron were reported to be much less cross-reactive to DENV-infection, compared to sera from COVID-19 cases infected in earlier pandemic waves (ancestral/Delta-variant).8

Increased risk of DENV-infection post-COVID-19, versus test-negatives, was observed across most demographic subgroups except older adults, in whom immunosenescence may potentially occur. Risk of subsequent DENV-infection was not significantly increased in doubly-boosted COVID-19 cases versus test-negatives, possibly attributable to the beneficial impact of boosting on attenuating long-term autoimmune sequelae28; though wide confidence-intervals for estimates of subsequent DENV-infection in the doubly-boosted COVID-19 subgroup reflect limited precision due to relatively smaller numbers and should be interpreted with caution. Lack of increased risk for DENV-infection amongst doubly-boosted COVID-19 cases may also be attributable to potential behavioural changes; for instance, boosted individuals, being more health-conscious, may be predisposed to take additional precautionary measures to reduce risk of DENV-infection. Our epidemiological study does not irrefutably prove a potential causative immunopathological interaction between SARS-CoV-2/DENV-infection, and more robust validation is required via preclinical studies and future prospective clinical cohorts. Given endemicity of both SARS-CoV-2 and DENV-infection in tropical countries and increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection observed in unvaccinated COVID-19 cases, renewed emphasis on COVID-19 vaccination is a public health priority. Misapprehensions regarding COVID-19 vaccination, if left unaddressed, may also translate into lower conditional acceptance of potential DENV-vaccines as a future strategy for DENV-control.29

The limitations of our study are as follows. Firstly, the temporal context of this study captured a unique epidemiological moment during emergence of DENV3 in Singapore and ongoing transmission of SARS-CoV-2 Delta/Omicron variants; while this provided an ideal natural experiment, generalisability was limited and may not reflect risk attributable to currently circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants, or risk of DENV-infection attributable to other serotypes. Prior studies have highlighted diminishing DENV cross-reactivity in serum samples derived from COVID-19 survivors during the shift from Delta to Omicron.8 Additionally, results do not reflect the current complexity of hybrid immunity in SARS-CoV-2 re-infected individuals. Relatively small numbers of re-infected COVID-19 cases meant that these individuals were excluded at the point of cohort construction, but during current COVID-19 endemicity, most of the population have experienced multiple exposures to SARS-CoV-2. The majority of COVID-19 cases were vaccinated prior to infection; results may not be generalisable to settings with different COVID-19 vaccination coverage. While comprehensive nationwide registries were used to classify SARS-CoV-2/DENV-infection status, misclassification may still occur; serology was not routinely tested, and asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infections may have occurred in the test-negative group. Though a statistically significant association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and subsequent DENV-infection was observed, the modest effect size may not translate into significant practical implications for public health policy; though risk estimates are likely biased towards the null, given the possibility of asymptomatic/minimally symptomatic DENV-infections which go unrecognized and unreported.30 Housing type may not be fully representative as the sole indicator of SES. Additionally, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors influencing risk of COVID-19 acquisition and subsequent DENV-infection may still occur; for instance, potential differences in vector control measures were not adjusted for. Surveillance bias, where individuals with recent COVID-19 might have different healthcare utilization patterns affecting subsequent diagnosis of DENV-infection, may also bias estimates away from the null; though a test-negative study design was utilised, risk of negative-outcome controls/negative-exposure controls was evaluated, and healthcare utilisation was explicitly controlled for in analyses. Finally, our cohort comprised a multi-ethnic Asian population with a Chinese majority; these results may not be fully generalisable to other populations with different ethnic compositions.

In conclusion, increased risk of subsequent DENV-infection (primarily DENV3), but not other infections, was observed up to 300 days following SARS-CoV-2-infection (predominantly Omicron) in this large population-based cohort. While not irrefutable proof for possible antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV3-infection post-Omicron COVID-19 in light of modest effect sizes, future preclinical and prospective cohort studies can shed more light on potential interplay between these two infections, including strain-specific differences in cross-reactivity and cross-protection. Prior in-vitro work highlighted inhibition of DENV-infection (all serotypes) in the presence of sera from COVID-19 survivors infected with ancestral SARS-CoV-2 variants, but reduced cross-reactivity and antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV3 infection post-Omicron COVID-19, providing a potential mechanistic explanation for the DENV3 surge observed in our population over the study period. Public health strategies (e.g., vaccination) are important for control of both infections in tropical/subtropical regions where COVID-19 and DENV-infection are now endemic.
