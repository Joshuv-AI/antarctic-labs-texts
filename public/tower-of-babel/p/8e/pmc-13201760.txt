Exercise self-efficacy of physically disabled students of Lemo district primary school
Authors: Samson Getu, Mihiretu Wondimu, Adise Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Physical activity is vital for the health and wellness of every child, but students with physical disabilities frequently encounter different challenges that impact their participation. This research aimed to explore the factors affecting exercise self-efficacy among physically disabled students in primary schools. Using a mixed-method approach, a purposive sample of n = 152 students from grade 5 to 8 was selected across five public primary school in the Lemo district. Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that age (β = 0.062, t = 7.500, p < 0.05) and gender (β = 0.046, t = 3.500, p < 0.05) were significant predictors of exercise self-efficacy. Additionally, socioeconomic status (β = 0.073, t = 3.571, p = 0.001), peer support (β = 0.231, t = 3.750, p < 0.05), parental support (β = 1.021, t = 4.253, p < 0.001), teachers support (β = 0.084, t = 3.750, p < 0.05), and school administrative support (β = 0.277, t = 3.500, p = 0.001) had a significant positive effect. Totally these factors explained 86.9% of the variance in exercise self-efficacy. Qualitative analysis revealed that critical role of support systems but also face challenges such as insufficient training, lack of adapted equipment, and limited awareness among teachers, peers, parents, and school administrations. Our finding suggest that include comprehensive teacher training in inclusive education, development of peer support program, parental engagement through workshop, school accessibility audit and allocation of resource for adapted equipment.

Introduction

Worldwide, numerous children with physical disabilities (CwPDs) are marginalized from their education and social interactions, primarily because of stigma and its repercussions. Nonetheless, data regarding disability stigma is limited in low- and middle-income nations such as Ethiopia1. Participation in physical activity has been constantly linked to positive outcome for children and adolescents with disabilities who participate in physical activity often demonstrate better academic skill, higher self-efficacy, greater will power, lower feeling of inferiority2. Physical activity has been shown to strengthen perceived physical skill, which in turn enhance self-efficacy and participation aptitude3. This relationship is particularly strong among individual with higher level of disability, where self-efficacy, self –esteem, and acceptance of disability positively influence social participation4. Furthermore exercise self-efficacy, a crucial behavioral driver of physical activity, is also significantly predicted by participation in sports5. It indicates that such involvement improves a variety of aspects of life, such as social integration and psychosocial health.

Despite these benefit many children with physical disabilities barriers to participation, including limited parental awareness, little coaching expertise in inclusive settings, and low perceived community support6. Recent study on physical activity breaks in specific schools shows their practicality and acceptance, emphasizing the necessity for adaptable program designs to provide to diverse student requirements and offer different resources7.

In Ethiopia, empirical research on the exercise self-efficacy of children with physically disabilities in particularly limited. This knowledge gap hinder the development of evidence based strategies for inclusive physical education. The present study addresses this gap by examining how demographic factors age, gender, parental socioeconomic background and social–environmental influences teacher assistance, peer support from non-disabled students, and school administrative support affect the exercise self-efficacy of physically disabled students in primary schools within Lemo District, Hadiya Zone. The finding will guide educators and policymakers in creating initiatives that encourage fair participation and add to the scarce literature relevant to the Ethiopian context.

Materials and methods

Setting

The study was conducted in five public primary schools located in the Lemo district of central Ethiopia regional state. Lemo woreda is district in the Hadiya zone districts, with Hosanna city serving as its administrative capital. Hosanna, the capital of the district, is situated at Latitude 07°41′ N and Longitude 037°31′ E, noted for its mountainous landscape and hilly regions with inclines between 2 and 30%. The schools were chosen to represent varied environments, comprising urban (n = 2) and rural (n = 3) locations. Initial discussions with school administrators and relevant stakeholders began in 2023 to evaluate feasibility and enhance awareness of the research. Nevertheless, formal recruitment and data collection involving participants occurred only after ethical approval was granted by IBR on 1-15-2024 and ended on May 30, 2024.

Study design

A concurrent triangulation mixed-methods approach was utilized, combining quantitative survey data and qualitative interview data gathered at the same time. We used this design to facilitate the cross-validation of results and offered an in-depth insight into exercise self-efficacy in students with physical disabilities.

Participants

The study population constituted of (n = 152) participants physically disabled students (n = 45), teachers (n = 22), non-disabled peers (n = 35), parents (n = 30), and school administrators (n = 20).

Inclusion criteria

Participants consisted of students with physical disabilities enrolled in grades 5–8 at the chosen schools, their parents or guardians, teachers who had instructed the students for a minimum of one academic year, non-disabled classmates from the same classes, and school administrators (principals or vice-principals).

Exclusion Criteria

Students with cognitive or significant sensory impairments that would hinder participation in surveys or interviews were excluded, along with individuals who did not give consent and those not actively involved in school activities at the time of data collection.

Sampling technique and sampling size

Participants were selected employing a mix of census and purposive sampling methods to guarantee alignment with the study aims. The study sample comprises physically disabled students (n = 45), educators (n = 22), non-disabled peers (n = 35), parents (n = 30), and school leaders (n = 20). A census was conducted for all physically disabled students (n = 45) to guarantee full complete representation of this primary target group. The remaining participant groups were selected through purposeful sampling to capture diverse and relevant perspectives.

For the qualitative component, a subset of participants was selected from each group for in-depth interviews to achieve data saturation physically disabled students (n = 4), teacher (n = 5), non-disabled peers (n = 6), parents (n = 5), and school administrators (n = 4) participants. For the quantitative component, the minimum sample size was determined based on guidelines for multiple linear regression, applying the rule of 10–15 participants per predictor variable. According to this criterion, the final sample of 152 participants was sufficient to meet statistical requirements and support a comprehensive analysis.

Instruments

The data collection instrument used in our study was the Self-Efficacy for Physical Activity Scale (SEPA) developed by8. It was not under copyright and was publicly available at the time of its use in our study. To ensure validity in the present study, content and face validity were confirmed through expert review by two sport science lecturers and a pilot test with 20 students to check clarity and cultural appropriateness of the items. Construct validity is evidenced by previous research showing meaningful correlations between SEPA scores and physical activity behaviors. The reliability of our sample was assessed through internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.74, which is within the acceptable range, demonstrating sufficient reliability for research use. Qualitative data were gathered using semi-structured interview guides tailored to each participant group.

Data collection procedure

Data collection took place between January 15 and May 30, 2024, following ethical approval. Surveys were administered in person, with assistance provided to participants with reading difficulties. Interviews were conducted in the participants’ preferred language, audio-recorded with permission, and transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

The study employed both quantitative and qualitative analysis technique. Fro quantitative, descriptive statistics frequency and percentage were used to summarize demographic information of age and gender for teachers, parents, non-disable peers, and school administrators. Multiple linear regression was used to determine the significant impact of teachers, parents, non-disabled peers, and school administrators on the exercise self-efficacy of physically disabled students.

For the qualitative method, data obtained from interviews were analyzed using content analysis. Transcript were read systematically, and meaningful units of text were inductively coded, allowing codes to emerge directly from the data rather than being pre-determined. Similar code were grouped into categories and themes to identify patterns and relationships. To enhance reliability, two researchers coded the data independently, compared result and resolve differences through discussion. This systematic approach ensured that the qualitative finding added quantitative result and provided deeper insight into the lived experience of the participants.

Ethical considerations

This study, titled “Exercise Self-Efficacy of Physically Disabled Students of Lemo District Primary School,” received ethical approval from the Wachemo University institutional research review board (IBR). The approval was granted on 1-15-2024, following a thorough review of the research proposal, including the study’s objectives, methodology, and procedures for ensuring the safety and confidentiality of all participants. The study adheres to all ethical guidelines and principles as set forth by the committee. Prior to participation, all students and their guardians were provided with detailed information about the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their guardians. Participants were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. All personal information collected during the study was kept confidential. Data were anonymized and stored securely, accessible only to the research team. Participants were informed about their rights, including, the right to refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any time and the right to ask questions and receive answers about the study.

Qualitative results

Participant selection and data saturation

Interviews took place with 8 essential participants reflecting various viewpoints: 2 students with physical disabilities, 1 non-disabled peers, 1 parent, 2 school administrators, and 1 physical education teachers/experts in inclusive methods. Participants were chosen intentionally to reflect a variety of experiences concerning exercise self-efficacy among physically disabled students. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, when no new themes or insights emerged from additional interviews.

Theme 1: Teacher support

Teacher support emerged as crucial factors influencing exercise self-efficacy. Student reported different level of encouragement and guidance.

Abraham Wolde, a grade 6 student with both legs unable to move, shared:

………… “My PE teacher rarely encourages me to join in because I have a wheelchair. He doesn’t show me how to adapt the exercises, so I often feel left out and unable to participate with everyone else.” (Abraham Personal communication, April 9, 2024).

………… “My PE teacher rarely encourages me to join in because I have a wheelchair. He doesn’t show me how to adapt the exercises, so I often feel left out and unable to participate with everyone else.” (Abraham Personal communication, April 9, 2024).

This indicates limited teacher engagement in adapting activities, which negatively affects participation.

Anita Frew, a grade 8 student with a handicapped left leg, reported:

…………. “I am moderately confident. I’m good at activities like swimming and wheelchair basketball, but struggle with running or climbing. Proper support and equipment help, but some challenges lower my confidence.” (Anita .F. Personal communication, April 9, 2024).

…………. “I am moderately confident. I’m good at activities like swimming and wheelchair basketball, but struggle with running or climbing. Proper support and equipment help, but some challenges lower my confidence.” (Anita .F. Personal communication, April 9, 2024).

Her response suggests that confidence is closely linked to tailored support and access to appropriate equipment.

Mr. Mebratu Haile, PE expert with 10 years of experience, highlighted:

…………"Inclusive practices are integral in my teaching, but achieving full inclusivity requires ongoing effort in adapting methods and fostering a supportive classroom environment for all students.“(Mebratu Haile, April 9, 2024).

…………"Inclusive practices are integral in my teaching, but achieving full inclusivity requires ongoing effort in adapting methods and fostering a supportive classroom environment for all students.“(Mebratu Haile, April 9, 2024).

This emphasizes that inclusive teaching requires continuous adaptation and effort.

Theme 2: Peer support

Peers also influence students’ exercise self-efficacy. Support from non-disabled peers was described as helpful but sometimes limited by resources:

Anniye Molito, a grade 7 student, stated:

……….“We try to adapt activities for everyone, but it’s challenging. Not all games are easily modified, and we often lack equipment or training. It’s important to include everyone, but it’s not always easy.“(Anniye Molito, personal communication, April 15, 2024).

Students’ confidence is highest when support is individualized and resources are available, while challenges persist when teachers, peers, parents, or school infrastructure limit participation.

Discussion

This study analysed the factors influencing exercise self-efficacy among physically disabled students at Lemo district Primary School. The study involved a diverse group of participants from the school system, including students with disabilities, their peers, teachers, parents, and administrators. The multiple linear regression analysis provided insightful results, demonstrating that several predictors significantly influenced the exercise self-efficacy of physically disabled students. Key predictors included age, gender, socio-economic status of parents, and support from teachers, peers, parents, and school administrators. The model explained a substantial 86.9% of the variance in exercise self-efficacy, emphasizing the combined impact of these factors.

Age was positively associated with exercise self-efficacy (β = 0.062, t = 7.500, p < 0.001) suggesting that older students may have developed greater coping strategies. This align with previous research suggesting that self-efficacy trend to increase with experience and exposure to adaptive activities9. Gender also emerged as significant predictor, with male indicate higher exercise self-efficacy than female (β = 0.046, t = 3.500, p = 0.001), reflecting societal patterns in which male often receives more encouragement to participate in physical activity10.

Parental socio-economic status positively influenced students ‘exercise self-efficacy (β = 0.073, t = 3.571, p = 0.001), consistent literature suggesting that higher socio-economic background often provide greater access to resource and opportunity for participating in physical activity11.

In regarding to social support teachers significantly predicted exercise self-efficacy (β = 0.073, t = 3.571, p = 0.001). Qualitative result reveled that some students perceived limited encouragement from teacher, particularly in adapting activities for their ability. This support prior studies demonstrating that teacher engagement and inclusive practice can enhance students self-efficacy and participation12.Peers support also showed that (β = 0.231, t = 3.750, p < 0.001). Students and peers reported effort to include all participants, though challenge related to limited equipment and lack of training were examined. Peer modeling and encouragement have been shown to be critical for adolescents motivation and self-efficacy in physical activity13. Parental support was the strongest predictors (β = 1.021, t = 4.253, p < 0.001). Parents indicated that challenges such as limited awareness, and skill but acknowledged the importance of nurturing and encouragement. This align with the evidence emphasizing parental involvement as crucial for promoting exercise self-efficacy among children with disability14.

School administrative support significantly predicted exercise self-efficacy (β = 0.277, t = 3.500, p = 0.001). Administrators explains enhancing inclusive school cultures and implementing policies ensuring equitable access. Main barriers included inaccessible facilities and traditional PE activities that are not adaptable for all students. This result align with prior research explain the role of school leaders in promoting inclusive practice and supporting students diverse need15,16.

Qualitative finding ensures that supported the quantitative result. Students reported moderate confidence but indicate challenges when teachers provided limited guidance and peer lack resource. Parent emphasize the need for strategies and awareness to support their children, while school administrator indicate infrastructural and pedagogical constraints. In sum this result underline the need of multi stage intervention addressing individual, social and institutional factors to enhance exercise self-efficacy in physical disable students.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the various factors affecting exercise self-efficacy in physically disabled students at primary schools in Lemo District. Quantitative results indicated that age, gender, socioeconomic status, and social support from teachers, peers, parents, and school administrators were significant predictors of self-efficacy, collectively accounting for 86.9% of the variance. Qualitative insights revealed that insufficient teacher training, scarce resources, and infrastructural obstacles limit participation, whereas robust support systems boost confidence and participation.

The results indicate practical significances creating inclusive school settings demands not individual encouragement but also systemic changes in training, curriculum development, and institutional policies. Boosting self-efficacy in students with physical disabilities can encourage involvement in physical activities, enhance psychosocial well-being, and support educational equity.

However, the study has its constraints. Its focus on one district restricts applicability to other similar Ethiopian settings. The small sample size, though sufficient for analysis, limits wider conclusions. Moreover, dependence on self-reported data brings in possible biases connected to social desirability and memory. Future research should utilize larger, more diverse samples and a combination of data sources to validate and broaden these findings.
