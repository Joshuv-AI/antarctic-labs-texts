Real‐world genome profiling in Japanese patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma focusing on HRD implications
Authors: Toshifumi Doi, Takeshi Ishikawa, Tomoki Sakakida, Junichiro Itani, Daiki Sone, Ryuichi Morita, Seita Kataoka, Hayato Miyake, Yuya Seko, Kanji Yamaguchi, Michihisa Moriguchi, Yoshio Sogame, Hideyuki Konishi, Kyoko Murashima, Masahiro Iwasaku, Koichi Takayama, Yoshito Itoh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) poses significant challenges due to its high mortality, making it a critical area of research. This retrospective observational study aimed to analyze real‐world data from comprehensive genome profiling (CGP) of Japanese patients with PDAC, mainly focusing on differences in gene detection rates among panels and the implications for homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status. This study enrolled 2568 patients with PDAC who had undergone CGP between June 2019 and December 2021 using data from the nationwide Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics database. Two types of CGP assays (tissue and liquid biopsies) were compared and a higher detection rate of genetic abnormalities in tissue specimens was revealed. HRD‐related gene alterations were detected in 23% of patients, with BRCA1/2 mutations accounting for 0.9% and 2.9% of patients, respectively. Treatment outcome analysis indicated that patients with BRCA1/2 mutations had a longer time to treatment discontinuation with FOLFIRINOX than gemcitabine plus nab‐paclitaxel as first‐line therapy (9.3 vs. 5.6 months, p = 0.028). However, no significant differences were observed in the treatment response among the other HRD‐related genes. Logistic regression analysis identified younger age and family history of breast, prostate, and ovarian cancers as predictive factors for HRD‐related gene alterations. Despite the lack of progression‐free survival data and the inability to discriminate between germline and somatic mutations, this study provides valuable insights into the clinical implications of CGP in Japanese patients with PDAC. Further research is warranted to optimize panel selection and elucidate the efficacy of platinum‐based therapies depending on the HRD status.Keywords: Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics, comprehensive cancer genome profiling, homologous recombination deficiency, pancreatic cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma remains lethal and is expected to become the second most common cause of cancer‐related death within 10 years. 1 Approximately 10 years ago, GnP 2 and FFX 3 became the standard first‐line chemotherapy for PDAC. Owing to the spread and advancement of next‐generation sequencing, the CGP test has widely penetrated the clinical practice of PDAC, with a slight potential to receive new treatments. 4 CGP tests were approved by the Japanese National Health Insurance System in June 2019, and more than 99% of patients who had undergone CGP under national health insurance are registered at the C‐CAT, which is the nationwide CGP database of Japan. 5 Real‐world data on CGP in actual clinical settings in Japan are only beginning to be accumulated, and it is essential to analyze these results. CGP began with two types of tissue assays (NCC and F1 CDx) in 2019, and CGP with liquid biopsy (F1L) was added in 2021. The performance of each assay has not been adequately compared and it is currently up to each clinician to decide which assay to use. Therefore, to facilitate the selection of an appropriate gene panel, it was necessary to validate the differences in gene detection rates among panels.

The HRR pathway‐related genes are the most notable genomic deviations in PDAC. BRCA1 and BRCA2 are HRD‐related genes that produce proteins that repair DNA double‐stranded breaks. BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants are the most frequent causes of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer. Apart from BRCA1/2, many other HRD‐related genes, including ATM, ATR, PALB2, and CHEK2, are reported to account for approximately 15% of all pancreatic cancers, 6 , 7 High sensitivity to platinum‐based therapy and PARP inhibitors has been reported for BRCA1/2 and other HRD‐related genes in ovarian and prostate cancers, with similar results reported for pancreatic cancer. 8 , 9 In the POLO study, a randomized phase III trial, olaparib, a PARP inhibitor, showed a favorable clinical response as maintenance therapy following platinum‐based treatment for germline BRCA‐mutated metastatic pancreatic cancer. 10 First‐line platinum‐based chemotherapy for HRD PDAC resulted in better PFS than the non‐platinum regimen. However, this study enrolled 262 patients, of whom only 5.7% were Asian. 11 HRD is considered a promising therapeutic target for PDAC. Nevertheless, among the HRD‐related genes, it needs to be clarified which genes are susceptible to PARP inhibitors and platinum‐based therapies, especially in the Japanese population. Therefore, it would be clinically beneficial to obtain real‐world data on CGP for PDAC in Japan, particularly concerning HRD status. This retrospective observational study aimed to analyze real‐world data from CGP in Japanese patients with PDAC, mainly focusing on differences in gene detection rates among panels and the implications for HRD status.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This was a physician‐initiated retrospective observational study enrolling patients with pancreatic cancer who underwent CGP in Japan. Patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors who have completed, or are approaching the end of conventional treatment, or who are not receiving standard therapy are eligible for CGP under the terms of the Japanese National Health Insurance System. Two types of CGP assays, F1 (Foundation Medicine Inc., Cambridge, USA) and NCC (Sysmex Co., Ltd., Kobe, Japan), were approved in Japan in June 2019, and F1L (Foundation Medicine Inc., Cambridge, USA) was approved in August 2021. The case dataset with the primary site as the pancreas from June 2019 to December 2021 was included in the analysis, and data were extracted from C‐CAT in April 2022. The C‐CAT collects almost all CGP results and patient clinical information from the health insurance system in Japan. 5 This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Board of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (approval number: ERB‐C‐2138) and the Review Board of C‐CAT (C‐CAT control number: CDU2021‐003N). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects.

Method

We collected patient background characteristics, treatment‐related factors, and CGP test results, such as sex, age, ECOG‐PS, pathological findings, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, sampling method and location, metastatic sites, family history, pre‐CGP chemotherapy regimens, duration of treatment, type of panel, suggestion or administration of targeted therapies, and detected genetic alterations. To evaluate genomic variants that are genuinely targetable, alterations considered “Oncogenic” or “Likely oncogenic” in the C‐CAT database were deemed significant oncogenic aberrations and included in this analysis; variants of unknown significance were excluded.

To examine the performance of each panel, we compared the clinical backgrounds and frequency of genetic abnormalities detected between the panels using tissue specimens (tissue groups: NCC and F1) and liquid biopsy (liquid group: F1L). In addition, the detection rate of genetic alterations was compared between tissue panels (NCC vs. F1) and analyzed for the presence or absence of KRAS mutations.

Subsequently, HRD‐related gene alterations were defined based on previously published evidence for pancreatic cancer: BRCA group (BRCA1/2), HRD group 1 (BRCA1/2, ATM, ATR, PALB2), and HRD group 2 (ARID1A, ATRX, BAP1, BARD1, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK1/2, FANCA/C/G/L, MRE11A, NBN, PPP2R2A, RAD51B/C/D, RAD54 L). 12 To assess the treatment outcomes in each group, the TTD of the first‐line treatment and OS from the initiation of first‐line chemotherapy were estimated. The ORR and DCR of first‐line treatment were also evaluated. The treatment efficacy of GnP and FFX (fluorouracil, folinic acid, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) was compared. The effect of primary treatment in the tissue group was also compared with or without KRAS mutations. Furthermore, to evaluate the predictive factors associated with HRD‐related genetic alterations, logistic regression analyses were performed between the outcomes and each possible independent factor (age, sex, smoking, drinking, and family history).

Statistical analysis

Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables, while Student's t‐test was used to evaluate continuous variables. Statistic significance was defined as a p‐value less than 0.05. OS and TTD were estimated using Kaplan–Meier and log‐rank tests. We used the Cox proportional hazards to calculate the hazard ratio and 95% CI. A logistic regression model was used to estimate the adjusted OR with 95% CI to assess predictors of HRD‐related gene abnormalities. The multivariate logistic regression model was used with adjustments for all potential confounding factors, with a p‐value less than 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 16 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Clinical background

In total, 3074 cases with the primary site as the pancreas were registered on C‐CAT. In total, 2568 patients with PDAC were enrolled in this study following the exclusion of 506 cases with specific subtypes of pancreatic cancer, including adenosquamous carcinoma, acinar cell carcinoma, anaplastic carcinoma of the pancreas, intraductal papillary mucinous carcinoma, neuroendocrine tumor, solid pseudopapillary neoplasm, mucinous cystic neoplasm, and unknown tumor types (Figure 1). Upon grouping all patients with PDAC, the median age was 65 years, and 98% of them had ECOG‐PS 0 or 1. Of the total, 85.7% and 14.3% of patients underwent tissue DNA sequencing (F1, 62.9%; NCC, 22.8%) and ctDNA assays (F1, 14.3%), respectively. In addition, 70.3% of the analyzed tissues were obtained from the primary pancreatic lesions (Table 1). Comparing the tissue and liquid groups in terms of patient background, the tissue group exhibited younger age, better PS, and more liver metastases (Table S1).

Genetic characteristics

Overall genomic alterations (small‐scale variants, deletions, amplifications, and rearrangements) in PDAC are shown in Figure 2. The most common gene changes were those known as the “Big4,” including KRAS (85.1%), TP53 (69.1%), CDKN2A (35.4%), and SMAD4 (19.4%). The genetic alterations that followed were ARID1A (7.4%), STK11 (6.5%), MYC (3.2%), KDM6A (2.8%), and ATM (2.5%). Representative HRD‐related gene alterations are shown in Figure 3. Any HRD‐related gene alterations were detected in 23% of patients with PDAC. The frequencies of BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, ATR, and PALB2 alterations were 0.9%, 2.9%, 2.5%, 0.2%, and 0.9%, respectively. Alterations in HRD group 2 were observed in 15% of patients with PDAC. When compared by KRAS subtype, G12D (42.8%), G12V (35.7%), and G12R (11.9%) were the most prevalent, whereas G12C was observed in 2.2% of the cases. No differences were observed among the panels used (Table S2).

Second, we compared the detection rate of gene alterations between the tissue and liquid groups and found that the tissue group had a higher detection rate for all the top four genes. KRAS mutations were detected in 92.2% and 42.1% of the tissue and liquid groups, respectively. In addition, ARID1A, MYC, and ERBB2 were detected more frequently in the tissue group, whereas CHEK2 and NF1 were detected more often in the liquid group (Table 2 and Figure 4). The detection rate of KRAS alterations in the liquid group was significantly higher in patients with liver metastases than in those without liver metastases (63.7% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.0001). In the tissue group, only a slight difference was observed in lung metastases (Table S3). When comparing NCC and F1, which are both CGPs in tissue samples, all top four genes were detected more frequently in F1. Several other genes were also detected more regularly in F1, but no genes were more sensitive to detection in NCC (Figure S1). In the CGP of tissue samples, TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4 were more frequent in KRAS mutation cases than in wild‐type cases. Conversely, BRCA2, ERBB2, GNAS, BRAF, and APC gene abnormalities were frequently observed in KRAS wild‐type tumors (Figure 5).

Outcomes of the first‐line chemotherapy and treatment suggestion

The TTD and treatment response to FFX and GnP in first‐line chemotherapy for each genetic abnormality were examined. In patients with BRCA1/2 mutations, the TTD of first‐line chemotherapy was better with FFX than with GnP (9.3 vs. 5.6 months, p = 0.028) (Figure 6A). Conversely, there was no difference between the FFX and GnP in patients with BRCA1/2 wild (6.5 vs. 6.7 months, p = 0.17) (Figure 6B). Although there was no significant difference, there was a trend toward better OS in the FFX treatment group among patients with positive BRCA1/2 (Figure 6C). There was no significant difference in the OS between the FFX and GnP of the BRCA1/2 wild‐type (Figure 6D). The ORR and DCR of first‐line FFX and GnP in patients with positive BRCA1/2 were 45.0% and 25.6% (p = 0.15) and 80.0% and 80.5% (p = 0.16), respectively. A similar investigation was conducted for HRD groups 1 and 2; however, there was no difference in the treatment effects of FFX and GnP, whether only adding group 1 or 2 (Figure 7). In a study with KRAS gene mutations, a known prognostic factor in pancreatic cancer, the median TTD and OS of the first‐line treatment for patients with KRAS wild‐type was prolonged than that for KRAS‐mutated patients (7.8 vs. 6.3 months, p < 0.001 and 65.3 vs. 27.1 months, p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure S2). In the tissue group, when comparing first‐line treatment outcomes between KRAS mutants and wild‐type, FFX tended to have slightly better TTD and OS than GnP in the KRAS wild‐type group. However, this difference was not significant. In contrast, the OS curve of GnP was slightly longer than that of FFX in patients with KRAS mutation (Figure S3).

As a result of the CGP, targeted therapy was proposed in 27.6% (F1, 32.6%; NCC, 22.9%; F1L, 12.8%) of the cases. Among them, the suggested therapeutic was administered in 2.6% (F1, 2.9%; NCC, 3.1%; F1L, 0.3%) of the cases (Table S4). As representative target genes, RET fusions were detected in two cases and ERBB2 amplification in 47 cases. However, neither NTRK nor ALK fusions were detected.

Predictive factors for HRD‐related gene alterations

Factors predicting the presence of HRD group 1 gene alterations were explored based on clinical background, mainly family history. Age and family history of breast, prostate, and ovarian cancers were found to be candidate variables in the selection of factors based on findings of p‐value less than 0.05 using univariate regression. A multivariate nominal logistic regression analysis was performed using the aforementioned variables. Younger age (OR = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.96–0.99, p = 0.0023), and family history of breast (OR = 2.00, 95% CI = 1.32–2.97, p = 0.0013), prostatic (OR = 1.87, 95% CI = 1.06–3.10, p = 0.031), and ovarian cancers (OR = 3.72, 95% CI = 1.63–7.74, p = 0.029) were found to be significantly associated with HRD group 1 gene alteration. No significant relationship was evident between HRD gene alterations and a family history of pancreatic cancer (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we utilized a nationwide database (C‐CAT) to provide an overview of the CGP of PDAC in Japan. The Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare developed the C‐CAT to manage and compile genetic data of cancer patients and facilitate the secondary use of the data to advance medical innovation research. 5 We conducted this study to investigate the secondary use of C‐CAT data, which is appropriate for understanding the current status of genomic medicine in Japan, and we have previously published several C‐CAT‐related reports on pancreatic cancer. 13 , 14 Unlike our previous studies, which focused on rare subtypes or microsatellite instability‐high pancreatic cancers, this study examined differences among the panels and HRD status of patients.

Mutations in KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4 genes were observed in the descending order of frequency, similar to that in previously reported results. 15 Our study found KRAS wild‐type in approximately 15% of patients with PDAC, which was lower than that shown in previous reports. 16 KRAS mutations have been reported to have a higher positivity rate in primary tumors than in metastases. 15 The low detection rate of KRAS mutations in this study may have been due to the large number of samples obtained from metastases and liquid biopsies. The frequency of detection by KRAS subtype was not different from that previously reported. 17 The HRD‐related gene alteration detection rate in our study was also in line with that shown in previous reports (14%–24%). 15 , 16 Consistent with the results of this study, according to a meta‐analysis, the following genes were most common in PDAC with respect to HRD‐related gene mutations (germline and somatic): BRCA1, 0.9%; BRCA2, 3.5%; PALB2, 0.2%; ATM, 2.2%; ATR, 0.1%; and CHEK2, 0.3%. 4 , 18

In patients without sufficiently accessible tissue, a liquid biopsy that identifies ctDNA in the blood can provide rapid genomic test results. Many patients with unresectable PDAC are diagnosed using endoscopic ultrasound‐guided fine needle aspiration. 19 However, obtaining a sufficient amount of tissue is challenging, making liquid biopsies important. CGP using ctDNA has the advantage of a short turnaround time and provides a comprehensive understanding of intratumoral heterogeneity compared to genotyping using tumor tissues. A comparison of CGP with tissue samples and liquid biopsies revealed lower mutation detection rates for many genes in the liquid group. The SCRUM‐Japan GOZILA study, which performed ctDNA‐based sequencing of advanced GI cancer, reported that the detection rate of ctDNA in PDAC was 83.4%. This rate was lower than that in other GI cancers. 20 Among 512 patients with PDAC from the GOZILA study, KRAS mutations were detected only in 57% cases. 19 Regarding the differences depending on the metastatic organ, the KRAS mutation detection rate was significantly higher in patients with metastasis to the liver (78%), which was also the case in our study. 19 Liquid biopsy appears to be helpful for PDAC with liver metastases that cannot be biopsied. One of the limitations of liquid biopsy is false‐positive results due to CHIP. Representative genes detected by CHIP include DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, JAK2, and TP53. 21 Genes such as BRCA1/2, ATM, and CHEK2, which are potential therapeutic targets in pancreatic cancer, can also be detected as CHIP. 22 Although CHEK2 was detected more frequently in the liquid group than in the tissue group, the possibility of the presence of CHIP should be considered. Clinical annotation of the C‐CAT findings was generated automatically using the Cancer Knowledge Database, 23 which cannot completely discern CHIP. Therefore, the exclusion of CHIP is recognized as an essential issue for liquid biopsies.

In previous studies, patients with KRAS wild‐type PDAC had a better prognosis and longer OS than KRAS mutation cases, 24 , 25 which was consistent with our study. Although most studies have reported prolonged survival in patients with KRAS wild‐type PDAC, this finding is not universal. Lee et al. reported that the OS was slightly shorter in patients with KRAS G12C mutations than in those with KRAS wild‐type. However, the difference was slight and not observed in the multivariate model. 26 Potential therapeutic target gene abnormalities, such as BRAF, FGFR2, and ERBB2, are reported to be more frequently detected in KRAS non‐mutant types. 27 , 28 , 29 Similarly, we detected a high frequency of gene aberrations in ERBB2, BRAF, and BRCA2, which are potential therapeutic targets for the KRAS wild‐type. Therefore, KRAS wild‐type PDAC is an important candidate for targeted therapy. The choice of cytotoxic anticancer agents as first‐line treatment by KRAS status is controversial. In this study, FFX tended to be favorable in KRAS wild‐type; however, there are also reports of favorable OS in GnP treatment for patients with KRAS wild‐type. 30 This is a contentious topic that needs to be investigated in future prospective studies.

PARP inhibitors, effective for BRCA1/2 mutations, have recently been proven to be effective for HRD‐related genes. For instance, the effectiveness of PARP inhibitors has been demonstrated for ovarian cancer, determined as HRD by MyChoice, and metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer in patients with 15 different HRR gene mutations. 9 , 31 It has also been effective as a maintenance therapy after platinum‐based regimen for germline BRCA‐mutated metastatic pancreatic cancer. 10 Additionally, PDAC with germline or somatic BRCA1/2 gene mutations has been reported to have a favorable clinical response to platinum therapy. 32 Similar reports have been published on HRD‐related genes other than BRCA. For instance, HRD‐positive PDAC patients treated with platinum (n = 24) had a prolonged median OS compared to patients without HRD mutations. 33 In addition, other positive clinical results for platinum‐based therapy for somatic and germline HRD mutation‐positive PDAC have been reported. 34 , 35 However, consensus on HRD‐related genes is lacking, with different genes included in each study. It has also been reported that a novel scar‐based biomarker of HRD (HRDsig), which uses copy number features and selects indel features, is a predictor of FFX. 36 In the present report of 287 HRD cases, BRCA1/2 mutation‐positive patients showed a favorable response to platinum‐based agents, but no significant difference was observed in other HRDs. This finding differs from previous reports in that the genes defined as HRD‐related genes were different; the data were collected in actual clinical settings. Although a phase three randomized trial, which was reported in the European Society for Medical Oncology Congress 2023, showed that GnP had better OS than FFX in the first‐line treatment of pancreatic cancer, 37 our study supports the idea that FFX should be used in the first‐line treatment only in BRCA1/2 mutation‐positive cases. Regarding racial differences, while Western studies are prevalent, a Japanese study found that 12 HRD‐related gene mutations were reported to predict response to oxaliplatin‐based chemotherapy in 28 patients with PDAC. 34 Regarding the Asian population, a recent report from Taiwan revealed that germline homologous recombination gene mutation‐positive PDAC had prolonged OS when treated with first‐line platinum‐based chemotherapy. 38 The results of this study do not coincide with those of our report, but the background of our report is very different in that it includes many somatic mutations. This is the first study to report real‐world data from a large Japanese patient population with germline and/or somatic HRD‐related gene mutations. The role of each HRD‐related gene is different, and the definition of HRD remains controversial. Selection criteria for these genes may affect treatment efficacy judgments. 39 Furthermore, we considered only gene mutations, and there were no data identifying genomic scars, such as loss of heterozygosity, number of telomeric imbalances, or large‐scale transitions. 40 Further validation is needed to determine how to define HRD.

This study had limitations. First, because the date of disease progression was not entered into the C‐CAT data, PFS could not be evaluated, and TTD was used as an alternative measure. Second, as germline and somatic mutations could not be assessed separately, it was difficult to precisely determine which one was present. Third, the data were from the early stages of CGP introduction in Japan and may have been subject to selection bias, such as good ECOG‐PS and younger age. Although there are some limitations, this is the first extensive real‐world study in an Asian population to detail HRD status and treatment sensitivity in pancreatic cancer and to demonstrate that BRCA1/2 mutations, including somatic and germline mutations, are platinum‐sensitive in pancreatic cancer. Therefore, it is important to further compile genomic data from a larger sample of Japanese patients with pancreatic cancer.

In conclusion, analysis of actual clinical data of CGP for pancreatic cancer in Japan showed that CGP using tissue specimens has a better detection rate of genetic abnormalities than liquid biopsy, and that the detection rate varies among panels. In addition, BRCA1/2 mutation cases showed longer TTD with FFX in primary therapy, but not necessarily for the entire HRD‐related gene. In addition to the panels studied, new panels have been approved for insurance in Japan, and further studies are needed on the appropriate choice of panels depending on the situation. Further studies are warranted to precisely identify gene groups that would benefit from platinum‐based therapies.
