Achieving universal health coverage through public health expenditure: an analysis of trends in 187 countries between 2000 and 2021
Authors: Aritrik Das, Henna Bhandari, Yukti Bhandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUniversal health coverage (UHC) is a concept that includes-protection from financial risks for all; access to quality primary health services; and, access to essential medicines and vaccines that are safe, effective, high quality, and inexpensive. Public health expenditure (PHE) is expected to improve healthcare delivery in terms of access, quality and financial risk protection. To this end, this study aims to explore the relationship between progress in UHC and PHE across various countries.MethodsWe analyzed the trends in UHC and PHE between 2000 and 2021 in 187 countries, using the UHC service coverage index(UHC-SCI), a composite measure of 14 indicators spanning reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health, infectious disease, non-communicable disease, service capacity and access. Data on UHC-SCI and public health expenditure (PHE) was obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory and the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated, and a fixed-effects panel regressionmodel was used to explore the association between UHC-SCI and public health expenditures, while adjusting for economic, governance and health system factors.ResultsBetween 2000 and 2021, change in UHC-SCI was positively correlation with change in PHE(r = 0.845;p-value = 0.017), explaining 66.9% of the variability in UHC-SCI. Mean public health expenditure as a % of CHE(PHE-CHE) increased by 3.08% and 1% increase in PHE was associated with 2.71-point increase in UHC-SCI. A panel regression model consisting of economic, governance and health system indicators explained 90% of variability in UHC-SCI between 2000 and 2021. Overall UHC-SCI progress between 2015 and 2021 was associated with only 7.9% of the UHC progress since 2000, with poor progress in upper-middle-income countries despite increased PHE.ConclusionThe analysis revealed a strong positive association between public health expenditure and progress in UHC-SCI across income groups, with public funding likely playing a pivotal role in advancing universal health coverage in addition to economic development, and governance. However, the limited progress in UHC-SCI since 2015, particularly in UMICs, despite increased public expenditure, underscores the need for policies that prioritize efficient resource allocation, equity, and access to essential health services through transparent and accountable governance mechanisms, so that the allocated public health funds are efficiently used for maximum effect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14891-7.

Introduction

Universal health coverage (UHC) is a concept that includes- access to essential health services based on need, not the ability to pay; protection from financial risk; and coverage of a wide range of services, from preventive to palliative care. It stresses on access to quality primary health services and access to essential medicines and vaccines that are safe, effective, of high quality, and affordable [1]. Target 3.8 of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which involves achieving UHC worldwide, is positioned as the most crucial task of the World Health Organization (WHO) [2]. Achieving universal health coverage hinges on optimizing health financing, which is crucial for bridging the gap between healthcare needs and usage, while ensuring financial protection at the same time [3]. The way a country’s health financing is structured has a significant impact on its ability to achieve UHC goals and overall health system performance [4, 5]. While private funding plays an important role, research indicates that mandatory, pre-paid public financing is key to progressing towards UHC. Conversely, low levels of public investment in healthcare are linked to decreased financial protection and poorer health outcomes [6–8].

To measure progress for target 3.8 of the SDG, one of the indicators used is the UHC-Service Coverage Index (UHC-SCI), which captures the service coverage dimension of the UHC. Progress in UHC-SCI summarises the progress of a country in providing promotive, preventative, curative, rehabilitative, and palliative health services of sufficient quality, to the people who need them [9]. Between 2000 and 2021, the percentage of the population without access to essential health services dropped by approximately 15%. However, around 4.5 billion individuals – i.e. nearly 60% of the world’s population - still lacked access to crucial health services in 2021 [9]. Even with increasing service coverage, catastrophic out-of-pocket (OOP) health spending worsened from 9.6% in 2000 to 13.5% in 2019. OOP expenditure on health further pushed 4.9% of the global population into extreme poverty [9]. This indicates the gap that public health expenditure might need to fill up in order for service coverage to actually translate to improved health and well-being for the people. The relationship between public health expenditure and health outcomes has been studied previously, with evidence demonstrating that increased PHE improves health service coverage, equity, and financial protection. Broader health coverage and transitions in health financing, including increased government contributions are associated with better population health outcomes at the country level, highlighting the critical role of public health investment in achieving universal health coverage [6, 8].

According to the WHO, health services coverage improvements have slowed down since 2015, while the burden of catastrophic out-of-pocket health expenditure continues to increase [1, 9]. This global pattern is consistent across all regions and the majority of countries. To support progress towards UHC and achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target 3.8, we need to understand the trends and linkages between public health expenditure (PHE) and the UHC-SCI. With some exceptions, health financing research in the past has mostly focused on total health spending, examining trends and levels of health expenditure, with less attention being paid to the specific contribution of these funds to the improvements in UHC-SCI as a measure of progress [9]. Few studies explore long-term associations with respect to global income group disparities. This study adds to the existing literature by providing a global, long-term analysis of the relationship between public health expenditure (PHE) and Universal Health Coverage (UHC) progress across 187 countries from 2000 to 2021, disaggregated on the basis of country income-groups. This analysis also incorporates economic, governance and health system related factors that may confound or modify the association between PHE and UHC progress, Unlike previous studies that focus on mortality or disease burden, we examine UHC-SCI, a service coverage-based indicator that better reflects access to essential health services and in turn, universal health coverage.

Methods

A secondary data analysis was conducted to study the trends and associations between UHC and public health expenditure. The UHC-SCI is a composite measure of 14 indicators spanning reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health, infectious disease, non-communicable disease, service capacity and access (Table 1) [9]. Data on UHC-SCI was obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory, and was available from 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2017, 2019, and 2021 [11].

Data on PHE was obtained as a % of the country’s Gross Domestic Product (PHE-GDP). This reflected government expenditure on health services and was available from Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) [12]. Data on current health expenditure (CHE), public health expenditure as a % of CHE (PHE-CHE), out-of-pocket health expenditure as a % of CHE (OOPE-CHE), and external contributions as a % of current health expenditure (EXT-CHE) was also obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory [11]. A list of countries (N = 187) has been provided (Table S1). Data for health expenditures was available for all years for most countries with a few exceptions which have been noted in Table S2.

Data was first cleaned in Microsoft Excel, and countries for which data was uniformly available between 2000 and 2021 were selected. We analyzed the trends in UHC and PHE between 2000 and 2021 in 187 countries. We further analyzed trends for the same countries between 2015 and 2021; to understand the patterns developing as progress in UHC coverage reportedly slowed down [1]. Countries were classified according to World Bank income group classifications as of 2021, namely; (i) Low-income countries (LIC) = 22; (ii) Lower-middle income countries (LMIC) = 50; (iii) Upper-middle income countries (UMIC) = 51; and (iv) High income countries (HIC) = 62.13 This was done to maintain consistency and to simplify the comparison of trends across income groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated, and linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the association between UHC-SCI and PHE-GDP.

A fixed effects panel regression model was used to assess the association of selected health financing indicators with UHC progress, alongside economic, governance and health system covariates. Apart from the health financing variables discussed previously (e.g., PHE-GDP, PHE-CHE, EXT-CHE and OOPE-CHE), the variables of Gross Domestic Product per capita adjusted for purchasing power parity (GDP PPP), government effectiveness score and control of corruption score for the corresponding time period were extracted from the World Bank database [13] and included in the model. Data on health worker density and hospital bed density for the corresponding time period were extracted from the WHO Global Health Observatory and included in the model [11] These variables ensure a holistic assessment of economic, governance and health system factors that are likely to influence the relationship between public health spending and UHC progress. Variables with high skewness, such as GDP per capita (GDP PPP) and Hospital Bed Density, were log-transformed to normalize their distribution. Government Effectiveness and Control of Corruption scores were standardized to facilitate comparability. Missing values were imputed through linear interpolation, except in cases where data was not available for ≥ 5 years in which case they were excluded. Robustness checks were conducted to ensure that the missing data was not sufficient to introduce bias.

A variance inflation factor (VIF) of ≥ 10 was considered for exclusion of variables, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered as significant. Due to high VIF, OOPE-CHE and PHE-CHE were excluded from the final model. The final equation after such exclusion was as follows:

PHE-GDP reflects the level of public health expenditure relative to the size of the economy, whereas EXT-CHE reflects the share of current health expenditure financed from external sources. Accordingly, while the coefficients for these variables represent the appropriate financing trends, they should be interpreted as distinct associations with UHC-SCI and not as directly comparable effect sizes.

Results

The mean UHC-SCI score of 187 countries, increased by 19.52 (SD = 7.31) points, from 46.01 (range − 13.31–83.15) in 2000 to 65.54 (range − 29.4-91.04) in 2021. There was an overall increase of 1.12% (SD = 1.82) in PHE-GDP, from 2.99% (range − 0.06–22.25%) in 2000 to 4.11% (range − 0.32–12.63%) in 2021. A strong positive correlation was detected between the trends in UHC-SCI scores and PHE-GDP, overall (r = 0.845; p-value = 0.017). Linear regression analysis indicated that change in PHE-GDP accounted for 66.9% of the variability in UHC-SCI scores for 187 countries between 2000 and 2021 (Fig. 1).

Mean public health expenditure as a % of CHE (PHE-CHE) increased by 3.08% (SD = 16.13) from 51.33% in 2000 to 53.91% in 2021. PHE-CHE and its association with UHC-SCI score was analysed for 187 countries, and a strong positive correlation (r = 0.695;p-value < 0.001) was detected (Fig. 2(a)) for the time period from 2000 to 2021. Overall, variability in PHE-CHE was able to explain 45.7% of the variability in UHC-SCI.

In LICs, there was an overall decrease in mean PHE-CHE from 29.06% in 2000 to 23.71% in 2021, with an average decrease of 4.68% for the countries within the group. There was also a moderate positive correlation with mean UHC-SCI scores between 2000 and 2021 (r = 0.510;p-value = 0.015), with variability in PHE-CHE describing 22.3% of the variability in UHC-SCI in LICs (Fig. 2(b)).

In LMICs, an overall increase of 0.6% in mean PHE-CHE was noted, from 38.87% in 2000 to 39.43% in 2021. A strong positive correlation was found between mean UHC-SCI scores from 2000 to 2021 and PHE-CHE (r = 0.717;p-value < 0.001), with variability in PHE-CHE during this period predicting 49% of the variability in UHC-SCI scores in LMICs (Fig. 2 (c)).

There was an overall increase in mean PHE-CHE from 50.31% in 2000 to 56.87% in 2021, with an average increase of 6.65% among UMICs. A very strong positive correlation was detected between mean UHC-SCI scores and PHE-CHE in UMICs (r = 0.918;p-value < 0.001), during the 2000–2021 period. Variability in PHE-CHE explained 83.5% of the variability in UHC-SCI scores in UMICs during this period (Fig. 2(d)).

Among HICs, there was an increase in mean PHE-CHE, from 67.16% in 2000 to 72.80% in 2021, with an average increase of 5.64% among the countries withn the group. There was a strong positive correlation between mean UHC-SCI scores and PHE-CHE for HICs (r = 0.685;p-value < 0.001), and variability in PHE-CHE explained 44.2% of the variability in UHC-SCI scores during the 2000–2021 period (Fig. 2(e)).

Table 3 describes the associations of mean OOPE-CHE and mean EXT-CHE with mean UHC-SCI scores. Strong and significant negative correlation between change in OOPE-CHE and UHC-SCI scores was observed among countries from all income groups. In case of external contributions to current health expenditure (EXT-CHE), the association was significant and strong for LICs and LMICs, but was not significant for UMICs and HICs.

The fixed effects panel regression model explains 91.1% (R² = 0.911) of the variation in UHC-SCI between 2000 and 2021 (Table 4), indicating a strong explanatory power. Overall, a 1% increase in public health expenditure (as % of GDP) is associated with a 2.71-point increase in UHC-SCI, while a 1% increase in external health contributions (as % of CHE) was associated with a 0.36-point decrease in UHC-SCI. Government effectiveness and hospital bed density were also significantly linked to increase and decrease in UHC-SCI, respectively. Economic development (GDP PPP) was associated with the UHC-SCI progress across all income group classifications.

Among HICs, a 1% increase in public health spending was associated with a 2.65-point rise in UHC-SCI, as was better governance. Hospital bed density was negatively associated with UHC-SCI progress in HICs. In UMICs, while public health spending was associated with UHC-SCI increase, external health contributions were linked to reduction in UHC-SCI. Similar to HICs, government effectiveness was associated with increase in UHC-SCI and increasing hospital bed density was negatively associated with UHC-SCI. In LMICs, a 1% increase in public health spending was associated with a 3.47 point increase in UHC-SCI. Control of corruption was also strongly associated with UHC-SCI increase in LMICs, and 1-point increase in hospital bed density was associated with a 3.32 point decrease in UHC-SCI. For LICs, a 1% increase in public health spending was associated with a 1.35 point increase in UHC-SCI between 2000 and 2021. Control over corruption was strongly linked to UHC-SCI increase, while hospital bed density was negatively associated with UHC-SCI.

Discussion

This secondary data analysis of globally standardised indicators, highlights the strong links between public health expenditure and universal health coverage. PHE-GDP accounted for 66.9% of the variability in UHC-SCI between 2000 and 2021. A 1% increase in public health spending was associated with a 2.71-point increase in UHC-SCI between 2000 and 2021, and only 0.53-points between 2015 and 2021. There was a decrease in PHE-CHE in LICs, and a meagre 0.6% increase in LMICs during this period. Increase in PHE-CHE was most prominent among UMICs, and it was correlated with UHC-SCI scores for all income group classifications. External contribution to current health expenditure was significantly correlated with UHC improvement in LICs and LMICs, while strong negative correlation was detected for the relationship between out-of-pocket expenditure and UHC progress among countries across all World Bank income groups. Notably decrease in OOPE-CHE was not adequately compensated by an increase in PHE-CHE, especially in case of LMICs and LICs. Fixed effects panel regression analysis indicated that public health expenditure, external contributions to current health expenditure, economic development (GDP PPP), government effectiveness and hospital bed density significantly predicted the variability in UHC-SCI overall (90%).PHE-GDP was positively associated with UHC-SCI across all income groups (highest in LMICs), while hospital bed density was negatively associated with UHC-SCI across all income groups (highest in LICs). Control of corruption was also positively associated with UHC-SCI progress in LMICs and LICs.

A previous analysis of 118 low and middle-income countries found that public health expenditure was weakly correlated with UHC indices (r = 0.309), and along with governance and stability, accounted for 67% of the variability in UHC index [14]. Our panel regression analysis incorporating economic, governance and health system related factors was able to predict 90% of the variability of the UHC-SCI, with a 1% increase in public health spending being linked to a minimum of 1.27-point increase in UHC-SCI in UMICs and a maximum of 3.47-point increase in UHC-SCI in LMICs. Regional analysis from Southeast Asia also detected weak correlation between PHE and UHC (r = 0.266) [15]. Our analysis similarly found that while PHE-CHE was moderately correlated with UHC-SCI for LICs (r = 0.510), public health expenditure coupled with external contributions was able to account for > 90% of UHC-SCI variations and trends for LICs and LMICs. There remains a wide disparity in the relationship between health expenditures and UHC progress. HICs and UMICs have consistently remained strongly reliant on PHE-GDP and PHE-CHE. Public health expenditure comprises 72.8% of current health expenditure in HICs as compared to 23.7% in LICs and 39.4% in LMICs. The lower contribution of PHE-CHE and the increased reliance on external contributions for both LICs and LMICs over the years, does not bode well for optimal resource allocation and sustainable public health development in line with sovereign priorities [10, 16]. Overall, our analysis found that external contributions had a negative association with UHC-SCI, the effect being particularly significant in UMICs. The positive association of governance effectiveness with UHC-SCI was particularly pronounced in HICs, while the association of corruption control with UHC-SCI was significant for LMICs and LICs. While the former displays the impact of an efficient working government structure in allocating public health expenditure resources, the latter indicates the need for transparency and accountability in the handling of public health expenditures, especially in LMICs and LICs [16–18].

Between 2015 and 2021, the overall association between public health expenditure and UHC progress has become weaker and non-significant. While association with PHE-GDP remained significant only in HICs during this period, control of corruption was found to be significantly associated with UHC-SCI increase in HICs and LMICs. Overall decline in UHC progress was seen since 2019 as well. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on service disruption, and bloated health budgets for COVID vaccination etc. also needs to be considered as a part of this setback in UHC progress [17]. Our analysis suggests that UHC progress has slowed down since 2015 for HICs and even more so for UMICs, despite significant increases in public health expenditure during this period. On the other hand, UHC progress during 2015–2021 was strong for LICs, accompanied by a strong increase in PHE-GDP during this period, though the association was not significant in the adjusted analysis. There was increased reliance on external contributions among LICs during this time period. Previous analysis of efficiency of countries in their progress towards UHC, found that increasing health expenditure is likely to have decreasing impact on the UHC-SCI sub-indices as they are more focused on primary care coverage [18]. The negative association of hospital bed density with UHC-SCI increase during the 2000–2021 period indicates that investments focused at increasing health system capacity at levels other than primary care, may continue to be counterproductive in terms of progress towards UHC. It may also mean that this form of health system enhancement is not reflected adequately in the measurement of UHC by the service coverage indicator in it’s current state. There is a need to consider expanding the monitoring indicator framework and re-evaluating the health system priorities of a country which has made significant UHC progress as indicated by current monitoring standards. These findings may also reflect that after a certain level of UHC attainment, public health expenditure may not be such a significant contributor to UHC progress. The possibility of inefficient use of public funds for implementation of cost-ineffective strategies also needs to be considered and rectified [10]. This is well evidenced by the strong association of government effectiveness and control of corruption with UHC-SCI increase especially in LMICs and LICs. The importance of controlling corruption in recent times, especially in HICs and LMICs in light of slowing UHC progress and the COVID-19 pandemic reiterates the need for resilient health systems supported by public health investment, good governance, transparency and strong logistics, bolstered by private as well as multilateral ties to address health emergencies, as the way forward for UHC in a post-COVID era [19].

The study analyses globally standardised indicators for SDGs for more than 180 countries, over a time period of at least 20 years. It also shows the change in patterns of health expenditure and associated UHC-SCI scores since 2015, shedding some light on the slowing of the UHC progress in recent years. The analysis, focuses on country-level means over time, to explore broad patterns, followed by a fixed effects panel regression analysis, adjusting for economic, governance and health system related factors that could potentially influence the association between PHE-GDP and UHC-SCI.

The study also has some limitations. Firstly, the quality and availability of data for UHC-SCI, health expenditure, governance and health system indicators may vary significantly across countries, particularly in LICs and LMICs. Data from earlier years, going back as far as 2000, may also have limitations in completeness and reliability, potentially affecting the robustness of long-term trend analyses. Secondly, the impact of current health expenditure on universal health coverage is likely to be distributed over subsequent years, and this was not accounted for in the analysis. However, the trends have been studied over two decades, which would reduce the above bias. Thirdly, the current analysis only focused on proportions to explore the relative importance of public expenditure in health financing structures and did not consider absolute values in USD per capita which could offer valuable insight into the patterns of government health expenditure over the years. Fourthly, due to multicollinearity, the final model retained PHE-GDP and EXT-CHE but excluded PHE-CHE and OOPE-CHE. As a result, the two main financing variables in the adjusted model were expressed using different denominators, with PHE-GDP capturing public spending relative to GDP and EXT-CHE capturing the external share of current health expenditure. This limits direct comparison of their coefficient magnitudes apart from direction of trends, but it contributes to the model with the latter acting as proxy for external donor contributions for health spending. Fifthly, while studying trends, countries were classified according to their World Bank income group classifications in 2021, despite several countries transitioning between groups since 2000. This helped maintain consistency and simplified comparisons but it serves as a limitation while interpreting group trends. Sixthly, UHC-SCI does not include financial protection and this limits the ability of the study to comment on how public health expenditure impacts catastrophic health expenditures or financial risk protection. Finally, the study’s design and statistical methods identified correlations and associations rather than causal relationships and the panel specification used here cannot determine whether observed differences reflected spending levels, financing composition, efficiency, or other unmeasured factors.

Conclusion

There was a strong association between public health expenditure and progress in universal health coverage across all 187 countries, and among each World Bank income classification group. Between 2000 and 2021, a 1% increase in PHE was associated with a 2.71-point rise in UHC-SCI, with the strongest effects observed in LMICs and HICs. However, since 2015, the effectiveness of PHE has declined, particularly in LMICs and UMICs, suggesting diminishing returns and potential inefficiencies in spending allocation. Additionally, external health contributions negatively impacted UHC progress in UMICs, while corruption significantly hindered UHC gains in LICs and LMICs.Components of the UHC-SCI may need to be re-evaluated to engineer a shift towards inclusion of secondary and tertiary health services in addition to primary health care, to effectively monitor progress in coverage after a certain level of UHC attainment as seen in UMICs and HICs. There is a need to ensure implementation of policies that prioritize efficient resource allocation, equity, and access to essential health services, through a transparent and accountable governance system, so that the allocated public health funds are efficiently used for maximum effect. Continued research into improving efficiency of health investments, especially in UMICs and LMICs is necessary to ensure a sustained improvement in UHC.
