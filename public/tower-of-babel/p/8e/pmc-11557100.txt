CORR® Synthesis: To What Degree Does the Direct Anterior Approach Improve Outcomes in THA? A Systematic Evaluation of Meta-analyses
Authors: Maio Chen, Babar Kayani, Bassam A. Masri
Published: 2024

In the Beginning…

Contrary to recent claims that the direct anterior approach is an innovative and novel surgical technique, this approach has been used in hip surgery or arthroplasty for more than 140 years. The direct anterior approach was first described in 1881 by Carl Hueter in the textbook, Grundriss der Chirurgie [44] and subsequently popularized by Smith-Peterson in 1917 and then by the Judet brothers and O’Brien in the 1950s [22, 39, 51]. The approach fell out of favor over the next few decades due to the increased risk of femoral complications and the growing popularity of Charnley’s transtrochanteric osteotomy [10]. The direct anterior approach had a resurgence after Light and Keggi [27] reported their experience with this approach in 1980 and Judet and Judet [21] published their outcomes with this approach using an orthopaedic operating table in 1985. The basic premise of the direct anterior approach is that it utilizes the internervous and intermuscular intervals between the tensor fascia lata and the sartorius muscles [30]. Conceptually, using this interval avoids detachment of the muscles from bone and thereby limits the trauma of surgery. It was concluded in an earlier review that “the orthopaedic community will embrace this technique” and that it should “be introduced into routine practice” [36]. Currently, a growing number of surgeons are adopting the direct anterior approach in THA. The 2018 survey by the American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons showed that 56% of respondents used the direct anterior approach in THA, and those who performed the direct anterior approach reported increased patient market share [40].

The Argument

Although some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that the direct anterior approach was associated with improved clinical outcomes such as less postoperative pain, less muscle damage and inflammatory response, faster rehabilitation, and decreased usage of opiate analgesics [6, 8, 29, 32, 63], these results were reported without much discussion about the clinical relevance, and the minimum clinically important differences (MCIDs) of these parameters were not discussed. At the same time, others have shown either the opposite or mixed effects or no differences in outcomes when compared with conventional approaches, making a conclusion on these benefits difficult to draw [11, 14, 19, 34, 35, 48]. Importantly, some have reported that the direct anterior approach was associated with increased risk of major complications such as nerve injury, femoral fracture, increased risk of femoral loosening, and infection [1, 2, 8, 13, 31, 42, 52]. Such serious complications are also uncommon, so most studies were not powered to detect a difference, lending a false sense of safety in using the direct anterior approach when a difference was not detected in a particular study or review. The absence of a noted difference, however, does not mean that differences did not exist. Moreover, the direct anterior approach has been associated with increased operating time, use of fluoroscopy leading to additional radiation exposure to surgeons and patients, and a steep learning curve, often estimated at approximately 100 cases [15, 28, 30, 37, 53].

Despite the many meta-analyses that have been published in the past decade to analyze the existing evidence, disagreement persists. This disagreement, in our opinion, is related to the potentially poor methodology in these meta-analyses and to biases within the studies from which the data were synthesized in the meta-analyses. Our objective was to summarize the results and evaluate the quality of existing meta-analyses of RCTs to provide an overview of study findings and to identify the relative merits and limitations of using the direct anterior approach versus conventional approaches in THA. The results should facilitate more informed discussions between patients and healthcare professionals.

Essential Elements

Search Strategy

We developed a written protocol before we began our search. Because PROSPERO did not accept registration of literature reviews using a systematic search or systematic critical appraisals, this study was not registered with PROSPERO.

The search terms used for PubMed were (meta-analysis) AND ((total hip replacement) OR (hip arthroplasty) OR (hip replacement) OR (total hip arthroplasty)) AND (approach). Aside from PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane reviews, and Google Scholar were searched using the same search terms, adapted as necessary. The eligible period for the search was from database inception through April 2024, with no language restriction being placed on the search. The bibliographies of the eventually included papers were screened to reduce the possibility of missing relevant studies.

The inclusion criteria were meta-analyses that compared surgical time, pain after surgery, speed of recovery, patient-reported outcome scores, reoperation rates, and/or complications between the direct anterior approach and conventional approaches, including the posterior approach (also called the posterolateral approach), the direct lateral approach (also called the lateral approach or the Hardinge approach), and the anterolateral approach (also called the Watson-Jones approach) for primary THA. In the first phase of screening, all meta-analyses on the relevant subject were included to get a sense of the number of meta-analyses on the subject; in the second phase, only meta-analyses of RCTs were included, including meta-analyses with study designs other than RCTs, as long as analyses stratified for RCTs only had been performed. Ultimately, the analysis in this review included only data from RCTs and not data from studies with a lower level of evidence.

Because of claims that surgical approach may affect early recovery, it was important to include publications that included early results even when these may not have had a minimum follow-up time of 2 years. Also, certain complications such as fractures and dislocations happen early; in order not to miss capturing these potential risks, we did not insist on a minimum follow-up time of 2 years. The trade-off of including studies with a shorter follow-up time is the potential of underestimating the longer-term failure due to loosening that may potentially be related to surgical approach.

The exclusion criteria were narrative reviews, systematic reviews without meta-analyses, meta-analyses not including outcomes described in the inclusion criteria, and meta-analyses comparing the direct anterior approach to approaches that were not the posterior approach, the direct lateral approach, or the anterolateral approach. Data from non-RCTs were not included. Abstracts from conference proceedings, preprint server papers, and papers published in nonindexed journals were not considered. One author (MC) and a colleague (not a study author) independently screened the titles and abstracts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine the eligibility of the publications. In case of disagreement, the differences were resolved by consensus.

Data Extraction

Before data extraction, two authors (MC, BAM) agreed on the outcomes to be extracted. These were: (1) study characteristics such as the number of RCTs included, quality assessment (that is, risk of bias [RoB] assessment), inclusion of learning cases, and declaration of conflict of interests; (2) functional and patient-reported outcomes such as the Harris hip score (HHS), Oxford Hip Score, Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, and pain, plus the follow-up time for these measures; (3) complications including infection, periprosthetic fractures, intraoperative fractures, femoral loosening, dislocation, and nerve injury; (4) operative time; and (5) blood transfusions (which was used as a surrogate for blood loss).

One reviewer performed the data extraction, and the results were scrutinized and confirmed by a second reviewer (MC). In case of disagreement, a third reviewer acted as the tiebreaker.

We did not include outcomes such as length of hospital stay, incision length, opiate use, and blood loss because these endpoints commonly were not measured in robust, standardized ways and/or because they are prone to subjective influences. For example, the length of hospital stay may be influenced by hospital occupancy rate, the surgeon’s personal preference and habit, or other circumstantial rather than medical reasons. In addition, with any surgical approach, there has been a trend toward a reduction in hospital length of stay and in outpatient hip arthroplasty over the timespan during which many of our source studies were published, which is not necessarily related to the approaches used. We did not look at blood loss but used blood transfusion as a surrogate because estimates of blood loss are notoriously inaccurate and should not be used as a definitive proof of superiority of one approach over another.

Quality Assessment

We assessed the quality of the methodology of the included meta-analyses using the updated version of the “A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews” (AMSTAR2) tool [49]. The updated tool includes 16 questions (domains) on topics such as the protocol and the methods of the systematic search, statistical methodology, assessment of RoB and heterogeneity, discussions of RoB and heterogeneity, and potential conflicts of interest. As described by Shea et al. [49], the users are instructed to first identify the “critical domains” that may “critically affect the validity of a review and its conclusions”; Shea et al. then proposed a scheme for interpreting the confidence level that readers can have in the review, that is, whether the readers can have high, moderate, low, or critically low confidence in the results and conclusion of the review. In accordance with this guidance, two authors (MC, BK) first selected the critical domains, that is, domains that were considered most relevant in answering the clinical question in focus. Afterward, the meta-analyses were evaluated by the same two reviewers (MC, BK) according to the AMSTAR2 publication and guidance tool [49]; disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus. Finally, the overall level of confidence of individual meta-analyses based on the number of deficient (critical/noncritical) domains was determined. A meta-analysis is considered to have “high confidence” when it has 0 to 1 noncritical weakness, “moderate confidence” when it has > 1 noncritical weakness, “low confidence” when it has 1 critical flaw with or without additional noncritical weakness, and “critically low confidence” if it has > 1 critical flaw with or without additional noncritical weakness [49].

With the same search terms, an Embase alert was set up; this allowed us to monitor new publications without having to search for them all the time. When new publications matching the search terms became available, an alert was sent to us. In addition, a final search of PubMed was performed using the same search terms right before the manuscript was finalized in April 2024. Results generated from the Embase alerts and the final PubMed search were reviewed and evaluated for papers with drastically different or critically important results. We did not perform data extraction and quality assessment for publications generated from these updates.

What We (Think) We Know

Systematic Search and Evaluation

We conducted the systematic search between December 2022 and January 2023. The numbers of articles returned for each database were as follows: PubMed (n = 183), Google scholar (title) (n = 23), Cochrane library (n = 5), Embase (n = 214), and Web of Science (n = 220). After duplication removal, 349 items remained (Fig. 1); among these, 42 were determined to be meta-analyses comparing the direct anterior approach with other approaches. Further scrutiny of these meta-analyses determined that 22 meta-analyses were designed to include only RCTs or had stratified analyses for RCTs (Table 1), a number we judged to be feasible to provide a realistic picture of the current state of evidence from studies of the higher level of evidence. We compiled a list of the excluded 20 meta-analyses (Supplemental Table 1; http://links.lww.com/CORR/B318). The reasons for exclusion were (1) the study design was not for RCTs only (n = 19) and (2) the study compared direct anterior approach with SuperPATH instead of a conventional approach (n = 1).

The most consistent finding from the included meta-analyses was an increased surgical time for the direct anterior approach (Table 1). Although we had intended to include meta-analyses on primary THAs only, many of these meta-analyses did not explicitly declare that only studies on primary THAs were included; we have included such studies and assumed that they did not include revision cases. We had also expected that most of the original studies would be on patients with osteoarthritis, but most of the meta-analyses did not clearly define the inclusion criteria on surgical indications. On the contrary, several of them explicitly defined their inclusion criteria to include, for example, fractures, even though it is not clear how many or whether any fractures were included in the individual studies. We decided to include all meta-analyses of RCTs in our review unless it was explicitly declared to include only conditions other than osteoarthritis.

The AMSTAR2 evaluation was conducted on all included meta-analyses except for the two publications written in Chinese [59, 61]. We defined seven domains as critical: domain 1 (definition of the patient, intervention, comparator, and outcomes), domain 2 (protocol established before the review), domain 9 (assessment of RoB), domain 13 (accounting for the RoB in interpreting or discussing the results), domain 14 (providing an explanation or discussing the heterogeneity observed), domain 15 (investigating publication bias and discussing its likely impact on the results), and domain 16 (declaration of potential conflict of interests). All studies had flaws in at least two critical domains; therefore, all studies provided a critically low level of confidence (Table 2). We have provided a summary table including all domains of our AMSTAR2 evaluation (Supplemental Table 2; http://links.lww.com/CORR/B318).

Clinical and Patient-reported Outcomes

The following results were summarized from the included meta-analyses (Table 1). The HHS was the most frequently used outcome measure for function. As such, it was the main functional outcome used by most of the meta-analyses for data synthesis. Although there are variations in the results for longer term results, better short-term (4 to 8 weeks) scores were reported [4, 25, 41, 43, 56, 61] but not for later time points [17, 26, 41, 43, 45-47, 54].

The situation is more complex with pain. Some studies found that the direct anterior approach resulted in less pain on postoperative Day 1 (sometimes also Day 2 and/or Day 3) [25, 41, 56, 60] but not others [17, 26, 45, 46]. One study found that patients had less pain on postoperative Day 2 and 2 weeks, but not on Day 1 or 6 weeks [17]; another study found that patients had less pain at 6 weeks but not at 2 weeks or 12 weeks [55].

A small number of studies detected a higher risk of complications (such as nerve injury or revision surgery) when the direct anterior approach was used [5, 43, 60], but most reported no differences in a number of complications that had been defined in our review (see the Essential Elements section). As mentioned earlier, the absence of detected differences in uncommon complications does not necessarily indicate that those differences are absent. Most of the primary studies used in these meta-analyses were not powered to detect differences in these complications and therefore were not in the position to state that a new approach was entirely safe compared with the tried-and-true approaches.

Ten studies found shorter operative times in conventional approaches [4, 9, 16, 25, 26, 33, 41, 45, 46, 64], while six studies detected no difference [47, 54-56, 59, 60]. The difference in operative time ranged from a mean difference of approximately 7 to 19 minutes; most of the 10 studies detected a mean difference > 15 minutes [4, 16, 26, 33, 41, 45, 46, 64]. We noted, however, that two of the six studies that detected no difference in operative time had mistakes in their analyses: one included a nonrandomized study as a randomized study [7], and one placed the data on the wrong side of the Forest plot [62].

Blood transfusion was consistently reported to have no difference between the direct anterior approach and conventional approaches [4, 33, 41, 54, 55].

The learning curve was a topic often not discussed or not defined in the studies. Chance discoveries, however, put some of these results in doubt. For example, a number of the meta-analyses [9, 56, 60, 64] included Bergin et al. [7] as an RCT even though the authors specifically declared in their publication that the study was not randomized. Because this study had recorded a much shorter mean ± SD operative time for the direct anterior approach (78 ± 17.9 minutes) than the posterior approach (118 ± 19.4 minutes), it likely had influenced both the direction and the magnitude of the operative time comparison in the meta-analyses that included this study. Another example involved a study investigating short-term outcomes that compared intraoperative outcomes and outcomes up to discharge (4 days after surgery) and reported only preoperative HHS [34]. Results from this study, however, were included in the comparison of pain at 2 and 6 weeks [55] and HHS at “end of follow-up” [54]. A third example involved an RCT that included 51 patients [12], but somehow in a meta-analysis this became 1793 patients in several Forest plots [5]. These are not the only mistakes we found but are just some examples to demonstrate the risks that readers of meta-analyses take in uncritically accepting the authors’ conclusions.

Notwithstanding the many mistakes we encountered in the meta-analyses and the low level of evidence according to our AMSTAR2 assessment, we consider it fair to say that based on the best-available evidence, in the hands of an experienced surgeon, THA using the direct anterior approach may result in potentially, albeit questionable, better short-term function, with the benefit detectable at up to 8 weeks, and less short-term pain during perioperative time up to approximately 2 days after surgery. Having said that, the actual impact of these differences may not be as important as postulated, and the overall benefit of the direct anterior approach may be overestimated. This bias is not easily recognized, especially when this procedure is promoted in the lay literature and in direct-to-patient advertisements, and patients may be misled by the generic and unquantified promise of a “faster recovery.” The operative time of THA with the direct anterior approach is likely to be longer even with surgeons who are reasonably experienced with the method; for surgeons who have completed hundreds of these procedures, however, it is possible that THA with the direct anterior approach may take the same amount of time as with the posterior approach.

Embase alerts generated five additional meta-analyses [3, 20, 23, 24, 58]; among these, two were meta-analyses on RCTs, neither of which resulted in different conclusions or provided newer insights than the studies we have included [3, 58]. The final systematic search of PubMed conducted in April 2024 for publications from January 2023 to April 2024 resulted in two additional meta-analyses of RCTs [38, 57]; neither produced conclusions that provided new insights.

Knowledge Gaps and Unsupported Practices

High-quality meta-analyses are supposed to provide the highest level of evidence to guide clinicians through the maze of conflicting results. Even though there have been many meta-analyses on the topic of the performance of the direct anterior approach in comparison to conventional approaches, because of the conflicting conclusions, surgeons are still left with the uncertainty of whether the direct anterior approach is worth the trouble.

Based on our AMSTAR2 evaluation, heterogeneity in meta-analyses was often not discussed. Aside from the obvious confounding factors, such as the indications and the baseline characteristics of the patient populations and differences in surgical techniques other than approach, one major confounding factor was the issue of learning curve, which has not been standardized in different studies. Similarly, although RoB in the RCTs was often assessed, it was seldom discussed how different (degree of) biases may affect the outcomes. Another confounding factor was that the primary studies used in these meta-analyses were not powered to detect differences in these rare complications. Therefore, when these studies reported no difference in an outcome that the studies were not powered to detect, it would not be appropriate to conclude that the new approach was “safe.” This has been perpetuated across many studies that primarily focused on faster recovery without specifically powering the studies to look at risks or uncommon but potentially severe complications. To offset these potential harms, preoperative counseling with patients might include mention of apparently small (and perhaps not clinically important) differences in “speed of recovery,” and surgeons should disclose that the newer approach may be associated with a higher likelihood of potentially important complications. Future research should avoid focusing so narrowly on differences of a few days or weeks of recovery, and instead studies should power their analyses to look at clinically meaningful differences in validated patient-reported outcomes using anchor-based MCIDs, and to look at less common complications, to make sure that we are not promising something of little value at the expense of a more substantial risk of a serious complication.

Barriers and How to Overcome Them

The low level of evidence we found in our research based on the AMSTAR2 evaluation is concerning, but this was not the only issue we observed. The realization that the domains concerning RoB and heterogeneity are commonly flawed is more concerning. Even though the study design of the included primary studies was heterogeneous and there could have been confounding factors not accounted for, such as the learning curve of the direct anterior approach, still no serious effort had been made to address the issue of RoB and heterogeneity, which makes it more difficult to interpret the results.

One of the main barriers to obtaining definitive answers on the value of one approach versus another has been the driver of one technique over another. The posterior approach has been a commonly used approach for THA for decades, yet early on, it was associated with a higher dislocation risk. With the advent of the direct anterior approach, and with the influence of the internet and direct-to-consumer marketing, there has been a huge push toward adopting this approach. This could create important bias in patient selection, discussion of potential differences, interpretation of the evidence, and the publication of review articles and systematic reviews, as in this study.

Another current barrier is the question of clinical relevance. According to the meta-analyses included in this review, the direct anterior approach was associated with better short-term (< 8 weeks after surgery) HHS scores of approximately 2 to 7 points compared with other conventional approaches (Table 1). Singh et al. [50] stated that “there are no published data regarding what is a clinically important difference of the HHS”; others have quoted the MCID of the HHS to be 7 to 10 points without clear clinical evidence [17]; yet others considered that an early improvement of 4 points in HHS indicated a clinical improvement [18]. Under these circumstances, it is not clear that the reported small changes in HHS, despite p values of less than 0.05, are clinically important.

Lastly, since it is not possible to blind patients from whether they had undergone the direct anterior, posterior, or lateral approach, meta-analyses are prone to the Hawthorne effect and reporter bias because of the excessive marketing drive with the direct anterior approach.

To better resolve the issue of whether one approach is superior to another, the first requirement would be to eliminate enthusiasm for a specific surgical approach from the equation and to truly understand what the current evidence shows. We hope that this study is a first step in that direction. With the summary provided in this review, the orthopaedic community needs to determine what is truly important for the patient and the healthcare system and then embark on designing an RCT that is adequately powered for the truly meaningful outcomes: Are better functional outcomes for the first 4 to 8 weeks more meaningful, or is less use of analgesics for up to 2 days more meaningful? How do we reconcile this with the potentially longer surgical time and a different complication profile? Are there situations in which a conventional approach may be more suitable for some patients while the direct anterior approach may be more suitable for others? It strikes us as unlikely that an adequately powered RCT will ever be conducted on topics such as those.

In the absence of clear, high-quality evidence, based on the inconclusive clinical results we have shown here, surgeons should not attempt to convert to a “better” approach but should do what works well in their hands to best serve their patients. The promise of faster recovery does not come without a price, and the patient needs to be well informed of objective and unbiased evidence to be in the position of making a shared decision.

Based on this CORR Synthesis study, we recommend that surgeons discuss with their patients the clinical meaning of an improvement of 2 to 7 points in HHS at 8 weeks after surgery and the need to balance this with the potential trade-off of a risk of nerve injury, especially that of the lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh, and the higher risk of femoral fracture.

After reviewing 22 systematic reviews of RCTs, we also believe that there is a need for decision analysis research based on patients’ views of these trade-offs and on the cost implication of the longer surgical time it takes to perform a hip replacement using the direct anterior approach.

5-year Forecast

Based on the minimal differences in outcomes, the recent surge in conversion from conventional approaches to the direct anterior approach is not justified. We assume that marketing pressures and incorrect interpretation of studies may have driven this change. It is our hope that this CORR Synthesis study will put these results in perspective and will give surgeons who are comfortable with practicing conventional approaches enough confidence to resist the perceived need for change and also to give their patients evidence-based counseling to dispel the myth of the substantial gains promised by the direct anterior approach. Despite this, we believe that there will be a continued surge in the direct anterior approach due to marketing pressures, with some surgeons returning to their tried-and-true approaches if they encounter difficulties with complications or with a steep learning curve. One potential push toward the direct anterior approach is the perception that this is the only approach that allows same-day discharge after THA, which is not correct, as the vast majority of our patients who undergo THA and are discharged on the day of surgery have their surgery using the posterior approach, and only a small minority with the direct anterior approach. Nevertheless, this perception alone may drive increased utilization of the direct anterior approach.

Finally, it is unknown how further adoption of technology-enabled surgery, such as robotics, will change the balance between the posterior and direct anterior approaches. For example, if robotics reduce the risk of dislocation (a perceived advantage of the direct anterior approach), this may shift the balance more toward the posterior approach, which has a shorter baseline surgical time. However, at this time, there is no evidence that robotics offer that advantage. Nonetheless, this is an exciting—as well as a concerning—time for arthroplasty given the number of technological changes that may or may not prove beneficial but certainly seem to be influencing how surgeons operate and the clinical choices that they make.
