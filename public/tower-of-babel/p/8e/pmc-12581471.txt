Investigation of pancreatic size and clinical effects in cases with late-onset fetal growth restriction
Authors: Mehmet Can Keven, Banu Derim Yeğen, Zafer Bütün, Ece Akça Salık, Emine Esin Yalınbaş, Ece Aydoğdu, Özer Birge, Atakan Tanaçan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of our study was to evaluate fetal pancreas size in cases of isolated late-onset fetal growth restriction (FGR) and to assess its relationship with clinical and laboratory findings in the early neonatal period.MethodsPregnant individuals diagnosed with late-onset FGR according to Delphi consensus criteria formed the study group, while healthy pregnant women matched for gestational age formed the control group. Fetal pancreatic circumference and corresponding percentile value and fetal abdominal circumference to pancreas circumference (FACPC) ratio measurements were obtained. Neonatal blood sugar levels, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and other metabolic parameters were recorded.ResultsA total of 154 pregnant individuals, including 67 FGR and 87 healthy control groups, were included in our study. The pancreatic circumference and pancreatic circumference percentile were significantly lower in the FGR group (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of FACPC ratio (p = 0.74). A statistically significant positive correlation was observed between the FACPC ratio and neonatal blood glucose levels (r = 0.298, p = 0.01). However, no significant correlation was observed between fetal pancreas size and short-term clinical and laboratory neonatal outcomes such as hypoglycemia or admission to the neonatal intensive care unit.ConclusionIn our ultrasound-based imaging study, we quantitatively assessed fetal pancreas size in cases of late-onset FGR. Pancreatic size was significantly lower in the FGR group. There was a significant positive correlation between FACPC ratio and neonatal blood glucose levels. The results of this study provide preliminary evidence of the possible effects of fetal pancreatic development on fetal growth and perinatal metabolic regulation. In clinical practice, fetal pancreatic biometry could be considered as an adjunct parameter during ultrasound evaluations of FGR cases.Keywords: Late-onset fetal growth restriction, Pancreas size, Clinical effects

Background

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is a common complication of pregnancy and is associated with adverse perinatal outcomes [1]. Previous studies have shown that FGR is associated with delayed cognitive development in childhood and an increased risk of developing conditions such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, coronary artery disease, and stroke in adulthood [2]. Therefore, accurate assessment of fetal growth is an important part of prenatal care [1].

FGR is defined as an estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference (AC) below the 10th percentile for gestational age, with severe cases typically below the 3rd percentile [3]. Early-onset FGR, diagnosed before 32 weeks, is less common but more severe and often linked to abnormal placental function and preterm birth [3]. In contrast, late-onset FGR, diagnosed at or after 32 weeks, is more prevalent and generally associated with milder perinatal outcomes, although it may still lead to long-term neurodevelopmental impairments [4].

The fetal pancreas, a gastrointestinal organ composed of both endocrine and exocrine components, plays a critical role in fetal metabolic homeostasis and growth regulation [5]. Among the hormones secreted by the pancreas, insulin has well-established mitogenic and anabolic effects and serves as a key regulatory hormone during development [6, 7]. While the roles of some pancreatic hormones, such as pancreatic polypeptide and somatostatin, in fetal growth remain unclear, previous research suggests that glucagon may exert an inhibitory effect on fetal growth [8].

Previous research has also established the role of insulin as a fundamental hormone in fetal development, as well as its association with fetal pancreatic agenesis, FGR, and neonatal diabetes [9]. Hughes et al. demonstrated that monogenic disorders resulting in fetal insulin deficiency can reduce birth weight by nearly 50% [10]. A semiquantitative analysis of 49 cases by Van Poppel MNM et al. confirmed the association between complete pancreatic agenesis and severe FGR, consistent with previous reports of genetically diagnosed fetal insulin deficiency [7, 9].

Given the risks associated with FGR, it is imperative to identify the contributing etiological factors. This study aimed to assess fetal pancreatic size in late-onset FGR and to explore its association with clinical and laboratory outcomes during the early neonatal period.

Methods

This prospective case-control study was conducted between December 2024 and June 2025 at the Perinatology Clinic of Eskişehir City Hospital, a tertiary care center. No formal sample size calculation was performed; the study sample was determined by the number of eligible patients recruited during the study period. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Fetal ultrasonographic evaluations were performed by a single experienced maternal-fetal medicine specialist (MCK) using a Voluson E8 ultrasound system (GE Medical Systems) equipped with a standard transabdominal curvilinear transducer.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2013). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Republic of Turkey Ministry of Health, Eskişehir City Hospital (approval date: November 18, 2024; decision number: ESH/BAEK 2024/63).

Study participants included patients diagnosed with late-onset FGR based on the international Delphi consensus. According to this consensus, late-onset FGR, diagnosed at or after 32 weeks of gestation, is defined by the presence of either: (1) An estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference (AC) below the 3rd percentile; or (2) EFW or AC below the 10th percentile, in combination with at least one of the following additional criteria: (1) A drop of more than two quartiles in fetal growth percentiles, (2) A cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) below the 5th percentile, (3) An umbilical artery pulsatility index (UAPI) above the 95th percentile [1].

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) singleton pregnancy; (2) diagnosis of late-onset FGR based on Delphi consensus criteria; (3) gestational age of ≥ 32 weeks, confirmed by crown-rump length (CRL) measurement performed between the 8th and 14th weeks of gestation; (4) a low-risk medical and obstetric profile, defined as the absence of maternal conditions such as hypertension or diabetes mellitus, as well as the absence of fetal abnormalities including structural, chromosomal, metabolic, or infectious disorders, and significant placental pathologies; (5) adequate ultrasonographic visualization of the fetal pancreas; and (6) completion of follow-up and delivery at our care center.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) multiple pregnancies; (2) diagnosis of early-onset FGR; (3) technical limitations in fetal pancreatic imaging, such as maternal morbid obesity; (4) a high-risk medical and obstetric profile, defined as the presence of maternal conditions such as hypertension or diabetes mellitus, as well as fetal abnormalities including structural, chromosomal, metabolic, infectious, or other clinically significant disorders, or placental pathologies; and (5) follow-up and/or delivery not completed at our care center.

The study consisted of pregnant patients diagnosed with late-onset FGR according to the Delphi consensus criteria who met both the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. Maternal characteristics (maternal age, gravidity, parity, number of abortions, body mass index [BMI], weight gain during pregnancy, gestational age, and smoking status), fetal characteristics (abdominal circumference, estimated fetal weight, estimated fetal weight percentile, pancreatic circumference, pancreatic circumference percentile, FACPC ratio, umbilical artery Doppler, and middle cerebral artery Doppler), and neonatal characteristics (birth weight, umbilical cord blood pH, neonatal intensive care unit [NICU] admission, blood glucose level, hypoglycemia, feeding difficulties, respiratory distress, blood lactate level, and base excess) were collected as part of the study.

Ultrasonographic evaluation

For each participant, gestational age was calculated based on the date of the last menstrual period and confirmed by crown-rump length (CRL) measurement performed between the 8th and 14th weeks of gestation. All participants underwent a general fetal anatomical assessment, followed by fetal weight estimation using the Hadlock formula, which incorporates biparietal diameter (BPD), head circumference (HC), abdominal circumference (AC), and femur length (FL) [11]. Biometric measurements, including BPD, HC, AC, and FL, were performed in accordance with the Practice Guidelines of the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) [12, 13]. In both the study and control groups, fetal pancreatic circumference measurements and percentile determinations were conducted according to the method described by Kivilevitch et al. [14].

Based on previous practices conducted by the maternal-fetal medicine specialist (MCK), the most suitable abdominal imaging plane was a transverse view at the level of the stomach and liver with the vertebrae at 6 o’clock. However, as described by Güleroğlu et al., a slightly oblique plane, with the fetal spine positioned between 3 and 5 o’clock or between 7 and 9 o’clock, provided sufficient visualization of the pancreas [15]. The stomach served as a key anatomical landmark. By tilting the transducer posterior to the stomach to locate the pancreatic tail, and rotating the probe according to the fetal back position, the pancreas could be visualized as a continuous longitudinal structure. It appeared as a mildly echogenic organ located posterior to the stomach and anterior to the left kidney, aorta, superior vena cava, and vertebral column, extending toward the gallbladder on the right (Fig. 1).

Gray-scale ultrasound of fetal pancreatic circumference (dotted lines). The pancreas is located posterior to the stomach and anterior to adjacent abdominal structures

After obtaining the optimal image of the pancreas, the image was frozen and the pancreatic circumference was measured using the freehand tracing method. All measurements were performed by a single experienced maternal–fetal medicine specialist (MCK). A control group was formed, consisting of pregnant individuals who met the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the study group and had comparable gestational ages at the time of evaluation. All participants in the control group underwent identical ultrasonographic assessments and data collection procedures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 22.0 software. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to determine whether continuous variables were normally distributed. Since continuous variables were found not to be normally distributed, these variables were expressed as median and interquartile range values. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to show whether there was a difference in median values between groups. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and comparisons between groups were made using the Chi-square test. ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the usability of fetal pancreas measurements in estimating FGR. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. The Spearman Rho test was used to determine the correlation between neonatal hypoglycemia and fetal pancreas measurements. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

There were no statistically significant differences between the FGR and control groups in terms of maternal age (median 27 vs. 29 years, p = 0.15), gravidity (p = 0.74), parity (p = 0.45), abortion history (p = 0.45), or gestational age at examination (median 36 weeks for both, p = 0.47). However, maternal BMI was significantly lower in the FGR group compared to the control group (median 27.5 vs. 29.3 kg/m², p = 0.02). Additionally, maternal weight gain during pregnancy was significantly lower in the FGR group (median 10 vs. 12 kg, p = 0.03).

Fetal biometric measurements revealed significant differences between the two groups. The FGR group had significantly lower FAC (median 286 vs. 317 mm, p < 0.001), EFW (median 2178 vs. 2822 g, p < 0.001), and EFW percentile (median 2.5 vs. 48, p < 0.001). Fetal pancreatic circumference (median 8.42 cm vs. 9.00 cm, p < 0.001) and pancreatic circumference percentile (median 48 vs. 65, p < 0.001) were also significantly lower in the FGR group. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the FACPC ratio between the groups (p = 0.74).

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of fetal pancreatic circumference and its percentile in predicting FGR (Table 2). The optimal cut-off value for fetal pancreatic circumference was 8.8 cm (sensitivity: 70%, specificity: 60%, AUC: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.60–0.77, p < 0.001). For the percentile, the optimal cut-off was the 56.5th percentile (sensitivity: 67%, specificity: 60%, AUC: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.58–0.76, p < 0.001). Discussion

Advances in ultrasound technology, particularly the use of high-frequency probes, have enabled the visualization of the fetal pancreas by providing high-resolution images [16]. In recent years, this development has paved the way for studies focusing on pancreatic size, especially in cases of gestational diabetes [17]. This prospective case-control study was designed to investigate the size of the fetal pancreas in cases of late-onset FGR and its effect on clinical and laboratory findings in the early neonatal period. This study is one of the pioneering studies on this subject.

Results from this investigation have revealed that, in fetuses with late-onset FGR, the fetal pancreatic circumference is significantly smaller and the pancreatic circumference percentile is significantly lower compared to a healthy control group. ROC analysis demonstrated a moderate diagnostic performance in identifying FGR, with optimal cut-off values for both pancreatic circumference and pancreatic circumference percentile, along with moderate sensitivity and specificity. The FACPC ratio did not differ between the FGR and control groups; however, it showed a statistically significant positive correlation with neonatal blood glucose levels.

Schwitzgebel et al. induced fetal growth retardation in a rodent model by maternal protein restriction and showed that this had permanent effects on fetal pancreatic development. In these fetuses, a significant reduction in pancreatic volume and, particularly, in the endocrine beta cell mass was reported, along with the development of glucose intolerance and type 2 diabetes at an early age postpartum [18]. These findings are consistent with the decrease in pancreatic circumference measurements observed in fetuses with FGR in our study. Furthermore, the decrease in pancreatic size accompanying FGR has the potential to affect fetal anabolic processes through insulin production and to pave the way for postpartum metabolic imbalances. The current study emphasizes that small fetal pancreas size may suggest impaired development of pancreatic endocrine function, although this remains speculative, and that this needs to be investigated further.

A recent quantitative analysis by Van Poppel et al. examined 49 cases of pancreatic agenesis diagnosed before and after birth [7]. Poppel and colleagues [7] highlighted a strong association between pancreatic agenesis and severe FGR, emphasizing the critical role of insulin hormone in fetal growth. Pancreatic agenesis represents the most severe form of fetal pancreatic developmental disorders, while partial pancreatic developmental disorder may be a much milder form and may be responsible for fetal growth retardation and postnatal metabolic disorders.

Arica et al. created a nomogram for fetal pancreatic circumference and the ratio of fetal pancreatic circumference to abdominal circumference in 233 healthy pregnant women between the 18th and 37th weeks of pregnancy and investigated how these measurements changed in 24 cases of GDM and 17 cases of FGR. They found that fetal pancreatic circumference was below the 5th percentile in half of FGR cases, but the fetal pancreatic circumference to fetal abdominal circumference ratio remained within normal limits overall [19]. Compared to the study by Arica and colleagues, the current study included a larger number of FGR cases, a more homogeneous patient population, and specifically focused on late-onset FGR, with cases clearly classified as early- or late-onset. Additionally, the current study incorporated detailed neonatal clinical and laboratory evaluations, which were not available in Arica et al. Of note, both studies demonstrated that fetal pancreas size was significantly reduced in FGR cases, although no significant differences were observed between the groups regarding the FAC-to-pancreas circumference ratio. However, in the study conducted by Arica et al., approximately half of the cases had a fetal pancreas circumference below the 5th percentile, which is inconsistent with the current study. This difference may be due to the fact that FGR cases in the study conducted by Arica et al. were not classified as early-onset or late-onset FGR and that the patient group was heterogeneous. Unlike the study by Arica et al., where the ratio of pancreatic circumference to abdominal circumference was used, we calculated the inverse (abdominal circumference to pancreatic circumference ratio, FACPC). However, since no statistically significant difference was found between the groups in either study, this methodological distinction does not affect the overall comparison.

The results of the current study suggest that FGR may be associated with abnormal pancreatic development and that this may lead to postnatal metabolic disorders. A small pancreas size in FGR may reflect poor development of beta cells, which play an important role in the endocrine function of the pancreas and produce insulin, a key hormone that regulates fetal glucose metabolism and growth. Although an absolute decrease in pancreatic size was observed in FGR cases, no statistical difference was observed between the groups in terms of FACPC ratio. This finding indicated a parallel growth decrease in fetal abdominal circumference and pancreatic size. In our study, maternal BMI and gestational weight gain were significantly lower in the FGR group compared to controls. These differences could represent potential confounding factors influencing fetal pancreatic measurements. Although our analysis primarily focused on the association between FGR and pancreatic size, it is possible that maternal nutritional status, reflected by BMI and weight gain, may also have contributed to the observed differences. Therefore, these factors should be considered when interpreting our results, and future studies with multivariate analyses are warranted to disentangle their effects. However, since FACPC is an anatomical ratio, its relationship with pancreatic endocrine function remains speculative. Future studies incorporating cord blood insulin, C-peptide, and other biochemical markers are warranted to better elucidate the functional significance of pancreatic size in FGR.

Of note, fetal pancreatic size was not significantly associated with short-term neonatal outcomes, such as hypoglycemia or the need for intensive care. This finding may be attributed to two main factors. First, perinatal complications might have been minimized due to the consistent management of all cases in accordance with current ACOG guidelines [2]. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of late-onset FGR cases, which tend to present a milder clinical course compared to early-onset FGR. Despite the lack of immediate clinical impact, the current study suggests that reduced fetal pancreatic size in FGR may indicate a potential metabolic vulnerability at birth, but long-term implications require further confirmation in future studies. Therefore, our findings provide preliminary evidence to support further research in this area.

The current study is a pioneering prospective study investigating fetal pancreas size in isolated late-onset FGR cases and includes a homogeneous patient population without additional maternal, fetal, or placental risk factors (e.g., hypertension, preeclampsia, diabetes, fetal structural and karyotype abnormalities, placental pathologies). The relatively large sample size, the use of current Delphi criteria for FGR diagnosis, and all ultrasonographic measurements being performed by a single experienced maternal–fetal medicine specialist (MCK) enhances the methodological sensitivity of the study. In addition, the inclusion of neonatal clinical and laboratory results allowed for correlation analyses and enabled the follow-up of the postpartum metabolic effects of fetal pancreatic development. These features represent the major strengths of our study.

The limitations of our study include its single-center design, the lack of long-term postpartum follow-up data, the evaluation of the pancreas based solely on anatomical and morphological measurements, the absence of hormonal and biochemical assessment (e.g., insulin or C-peptide), and the evaluation of only late-onset FGR cases. Although all ultrasonographic measurements were performed by a single experienced maternal–fetal medicine specialist, intra-observer reliability was not assessed, which may limit the reproducibility of the findings. Additionally, although ROC analysis showed statistically significant results, the diagnostic performance was only moderate (AUC 0.67–0.69), which limits its direct applicability in clinical practice. Another limitation is that no formal sample size calculation was performed, and the study sample was based on the number of eligible patients recruited during the study period. However, as one of the first prospective studies focusing exclusively on isolated late-onset FGR, these findings provide valuable preliminary data to guide future larger-scale research. Future studies should aim to address these limitations.

Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence suggesting that novel sonographic markers may improve the prediction of adverse perinatal outcomes in FGR. Indeed, Bayrak et al. compared the performance of different growth charts and reported that the WHO chart had a higher predictive value at the 10th percentile [20]. In line with these findings, our study demonstrated that pancreatic size was significantly smaller in late-onset FGR cases and may serve as an additional marker that should be further investigated for its potential to predict adverse outcomes.

Conclusion

In this prospective study, we found that the fetal pancreatic circumference and the pancreatic circumference percentile were significantly smaller in isolated late-onset FGR cases. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between the FACPC ratio and neonatal blood glucose levels. Although fetal pancreas size is not directly associated with short-term neonatal outcomes, this finding may reflect a potential metabolic vulnerability in FGR. The results support the idea that fetal pancreas development may play a role in perinatal metabolic regulation and fetal development, and emphasize the need for further genetic, hormonal, and molecular studies in both humans and animal models to better understand its clinical significance.
