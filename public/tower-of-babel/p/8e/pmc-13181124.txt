Engineering p-n heterostructure in MgO-Al2O3-CuO ternary metal oxide composites for sonocatalytic removal of pollutants
Authors: Atefeh Abin, Abbas Nikoo, Pouya Abedi, Khalil Farhadi, Mohammad Kazemzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, the development of environmentally friendly catalysts with high efficiency in degrading organic pollutants in water is of great importance. In this study, a novel p-n heterojunction sonocatalyst based on a ternary MgO-Al2O3-CuO composite was designed and synthesized via a facile self-combustion sol-gel method. The crystalline, optical, and morphological properties of the synthesized materials were characterized using XRD, DRS, FE-SEM, EDS, elemental mapping, BET, FT-IR, and pHzpc techniques. The optimized MgAlCu oxide composite (with a 1:2:2 molar ratio) exhibited a crystallite size of ~ 20.6 nm (determined by XRD) and a reduced band gap (1.88 eV) and enhanced charge separation due to the built-in p-n junction between CuO and Al2O3, which significantly suppressed electron–hole recombination. Its sonocatalytic performance was evaluated through the degradation of methylene blue (MB). Under optimal conditions (pH = 6.5, catalyst dose = 0.8 g L-1, dye concentration = 10 mg L-1, ultrasound power = 100 W), the degradation efficiency reached 85% within 60 min, representing a 12-fold and 7-fold enhancement compared to sonolysis and adsorption alone, respectively. Radical scavenging experiments confirmed the dominant role of hydroxyl radicals in the degradation mechanism. Kinetic studies revealed that the process follows the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model, with an adsorption equilibrium constant (Kads) of 0.007625 mg L− 1 and a surface reaction rate constant (Kc) of 5.7563 mg L− 1 min− 1. The composite also demonstrated excellent stability over seven consecutive cycles and favorable economic feasibility. This work introduces a rationally designed ternary metal oxide p-n heterostructure as a highly efficient, stable, and scalable sonocatalyst for advanced wastewater treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46178-6.Keywords: P-n Heterostructure, Ternary metal oxide, Self-combustion sol-gel method, Sonocatalysis, Advanced oxidation process, Methylene blueSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Nowadays, various industries such as the textile, cosmetics, and printing industries extensively use dyes in their production processes to enhance product aesthetics and boost sales. As a result, the wastewater containing these dyes is discharged into the environment and water bodies, posing significant environmental concerns1,2. Each year, approximately 7.5 metric tons of dyes enter water sources solely through the wastewater of dyeing industries3. One of the most widely used dyes, which is even employed as an indicator in water and wastewater treatment processes due to its extensive industrial application, is methylene blue (MB). When present in excessive concentrations, this dye can cause headaches, bladder issues, nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, increased urination, dizziness, and, at doses exceeding 5 mg kg− 1, potentially fatal serotonin toxicity4,5. According to the standards set by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA), the maximum allowable concentration of dyes such as MB in industrial wastewater should not exceed 1 ppm6,7. Various methods, such as adsorption, biological degradation, ion exchange, coagulation-flocculation, fenton and photocatalytic process, have been investigated for dye removal from water8–12. However, due to the complex structures of dyes, which consist of multiple aromatic rings and possess high stability, they exhibit considerable resistance to degradation13. Reducing overall costs and achieving complete mineralization of dyes are two key principles in selecting an effective dye removal method. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), such as photocatalytic and sonocatalytic methods, have demonstrated high efficiency in degrading and mineralizing complex organic compounds14–16. Unlike visible and ultraviolet (UV) light, ultrasonic (US) waves can fully penetrate colored and turbid systems. As a result, the effectiveness of the sonocatalytic method remains unaffected even in the presence of increased suspended solids and turbidity, making it a desirable approach for dye removal4,17. The degradation mechanisms of organic pollutants through sonolysis primarily revolve around the effects of acoustic cavitation and the generation of reactive free radicals18. Acoustic cavitation, as the crucial factor in ultrasonic processes, involves the formation, expansion, and sudden collapse of microscopic bubbles, leading to extreme conditions with temperatures reaching up to 5000 K and pressures exceeding 1000 atm18,19. Under these conditions, water molecules break down into reactive species, which serve a crucial role in the decolorization of organic pollutants16,20. Ultrasonic activation facilitates the production of free radicals with strong oxidative potential, enhancing the breakdown of contaminants. Additionally, the combined influence of free radical generation and localized “hot spot” effects significantly improves the efficiency of pollutant degradation21. Furthermore, ultrasound technology offers unique advantages, such as enhanced mass transfer and improved surface characteristics of solid catalysts, further optimizing the degradation process22. However ultrasonic waves alone cannot provide good efficiencies for the removal of organic pollutants. Catalysts are used to improve the performance of ultrasonic waves to both reduce energy production and increase the efficiency of removing organic pollutants19,23. In the presence of heterogeneous catalysts, the pollutant removal efficiency increases. This increase is due to the synergistic effect of ultrasonic waves and heterogeneous semiconductor catalysts24. Various metal oxides, and hydroxides, such as MgO, TiO2, ZnO, Al2O3, CuO, ZnFe2O4, CoFe2O4/mpg-C3N4 and ZnFe-LDH and others, have been utilized for the degradation of organic pollutants, including dyes. However, factors such as a wide band gap and electron-hole recombination limit the efficiency of the sonocatalytic process25–32. To overcome challenges associated with electron-hole recombination, the use of heterojunction systems has been proposed as an effective strategy33. Certain alkaline earth metal oxides, such as magnesium oxide (MgO), are considered promising catalysts due to their non-toxicity to aquatic life, abundance, ease of synthesis, and high surface area. However, its wide band gap reduces its effectiveness in sonocatalytic processes25. On the other hand, copper oxide (CuO) is a p-type semiconductor with a narrow band gap (1.2 eV) and exhibits excellent structural and optical properties. It also shows significant activity under long-wavelength energy, such as visible light34. Combining a p-type semiconductor like CuO with an n-type metal oxide semiconductor such as Al2O3 results in the formation of a p-n heterojunction34,35. This configuration reduces the band gap, enabling light absorption to extend from the ultraviolet to the visible spectrum. Additionally, due to the composite structure, electron-hole recombination is significantly suppressed34. Aluminum oxide (Al2O3), with its numerous defect sites, acts as an excellent electron acceptor and significantly enhances sonocatalytic efficiency for dye removal from aqueous systems34,36. The effective combination of semiconductors is crucial for the efficient degradation of pollutants. Consequently, various composite systems, such as CuO/CeO2, Cu2O-BiVO4-WO3, Fe2O3 on NCQDs-MgO, Fe2O3/γ-Al2O3, CuO-MgO-ZnO, Fe3O4/Al2O3/CuO, and ZrO2/CaO/MgO, have been reported for the removal of organic pollutants from water using photocatalytic and sonocatalytic methods34,37–41. For instance, Kritika S. Sharma reported a ternary composite of ZrO2/CaO/MgO decorated with phosphorus-doped carbon quantum dots for the sonocatalytic degradation of MB. The prepared composite achieved a dye degradation efficiency of 99.54% within a short duration of 30 min at a frequency of 20 kHz4. Ternary and quaternary composites leverage synergistic effects between different semiconductors to enhance charge separation, extend light absorption, and provide more active sites for cavitation nucleation38,42–44. For instance, systems like Fe3O4/Mn3O4/CuO45 and MgFe2O4/CdS/MoS246 have demonstrated high degradation efficiencies for dyes by combining magnetic recovery with enhanced sonocatalytic activity. Particularly, the engineering of heterojunctions (e.g., type-II, p-n, Z-scheme) within these composites has emerged as a dominant strategy to direct the flow of photogenerated charge carriers, thereby drastically reducing recombination losses under ultrasonic excitation47–49. While such advancements are promising, many reported systems require complex synthesis procedures, expensive modifiers (like graphene or quantum dots), or the addition of external oxidants (e.g., H₂O₂) to achieve high performance, which can increase operational costs and complexity for large-scale applications49–52. Therefore, there is a compelling need to develop efficient, stable, and economically viable sonocatalysts through facile synthesis routes that intrinsically maximize the synergistic interplay between their components without relying heavily on external additives. To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet reported the sonocatalytic activity of the ternary metal oxide system MgO-Al2O3-CuO. The sol-gel auto-combustion route was selected for its ability to produce homogeneous, porous ternary oxides with precise stoichiometry, high purity, and enhanced surface area in a rapid, energy-efficient manner53. Therefore, this work aims to design, synthesize, and evaluate a novel p–n heterostructured MgO-Al2O3-CuO composite as an efficient sonocatalyst. For the first time, ternary composites with various molar ratios (Mg-Al-Cu, 2:2:1 (MACO1), 1:2:2 (MACO2), and 1:1:1 (MACO3)) were synthesized via a simple sol-gel auto-combustion method to optimize the composition for MB removal. The optimal composite (MACO2) exhibited a 12-fold and 7-fold increase in dye removal efficiency compared to sonolysis and adsorption alone, respectively. Its performance was systematically investigated under various operational parameters (pH, catalyst dosage, dye concentration, ultrasound power), and the degradation mechanism was elucidated through kinetic modeling and radical scavenging experiments. Based on a central hypothesis, this study introduces the incorporation of a p-n junction between CuO (p-type) and Al2O3 (n-type) in the high-surface area of MgO, to enhance synergistically the charge separation and sonocatalytic activity, as well as reduce recombination. This idea not only designs a novel and highly active ternary sonocatalyst, but also provides fundamental insights into the role of heterogeneous p-n junction engineering in enhancing sonocatalytic performance for sustainable wastewater treatment.

Experimental

Chemicals

MB, Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), Hydrochloric acid (HCl), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), Citric acid mono hydrate (C6H8O7.H2O), Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), Sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), Potassium persulfate (K2S2O8), Magnesium nitrate tetrahydrate (Mg(NO3)2·4H2O), Copper(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O), and Aluminium nitrate nonahydrate (Al(NO3)3·9H2O) used during the reaction were purchased from reputable companies such as Merck and Sigma-Aldrich and used without the need for purification.

Apparatus

D500 diffractometer (Siemens) was employed for the pattern X-ray diffraction (XRD) of the MACOx (X:1, 2, and 3) at 25 °C using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) in a scanning rate of 2° per min, at 40 kV. Data have been collected with a step size of 0.05° degrees and a temperature range of 10–80°, nominal time per step of 1 s, and a slit width of 5 nm to confirm the type of structure and check the purity of synthesized MACOx. Information about the morphology and size of the MACOx was obtained with the device’s field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, TESCAN MIRA III) with 20 kV accelerating voltage. Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) using a SAMX detector has been employed to analyze and specify the relative abundance of elements. Fourier transforms infrared (FT-IR) was used on a Thermo AVATAR spectrometer in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a KBr disk. The Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (DRS) S-4100 SCINCO was used to measure the band gap of the synthesized MACOx composites in the range of 300 to 1000 nm, and UV-Visible spectrophotometry (T80+) was employed to plot the calibration curve and to evaluate the removal efficiency of MB from water at 667 nm. Specific surface area and porosity were determined by Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis using a BELSORP Mini II instrument. An atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Shimadzu AA-670, Japan) was used to determine the concentrations of Al, Cu, and Mg in the solutions. The probe sonicator processor UP100H, made in Germany, was used for sonocatalytic removal tests.

Synthesis of MgxCuy​Alz oxide nanoparticles

MgxCuy​Alz oxide nanoparticles were synthesized via a sol-gel auto-combustion method using Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, Al(NO3)3·9H2O, and Cu(NO3)2·3H2O in molar ratios of 1:1:1, 1:2:1, and 1:2:2, respectively. Citric acid monohydrate (C6H8O7. H2O) was used as the fuel. The metal precursors were dissolved in distilled water, followed by the addition of the citric acid solution. The mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 3 h to first form a sol and subsequently a gel. After gel formation, the temperature was increased to 180 °C to initiate the self-combustion process, resulting in the formation of a black powder. Finally, the obtained powder was calcined at 700 °C for 3 h in air, leading to the formation of crystalline MgxCuy​Alz oxide nanoparticle54. The synthesized nanoparticles were designated as MACO1, MACO2, and MACO3, respectively, and stored for further experiments.

Sonocatalytic degradation of MB

Synthesized MACOx nanoparticles were employed for the sonocatalytic removal of MB dye from water in a batch system using an ultrasonic probe source. Typically, a 200 mL beaker was prepared with 100 mL of MB solution at a concentration of 10 mg L−1. Then, 1 g L−1 of the synthesized nanoparticles was added to the solution and homogenized for 10 min to achieve adsorption-desorption equilibrium. After this equilibration period, an initial sample was taken and centrifuged to measure the MB concentration (C₀, eq), representing the adsorption capacity of the catalyst before sonication. Subsequently, the ultrasonic probe was immersed in the solution, delivering an output power of 40 W at pH 6.5 to the contaminated solution. The experiments were conducted in the dark and at room temperature to eliminate the effects of laboratory light radiation. Sampling was performed by collecting 2 mL of the solution every 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min to separate the sonocatalyst from the solution. The residual MB concentration was determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 666 nm. The sonocatalytic degradation efficiency was then calculated based on the concentration after adsorption equilibrium (C₀, eq), thus isolating the contribution of adsorption from the overall removal process. The Degradation efficiency (DE%) was measured based on Eq. 1, where C₀ and Cₜ (mg L−1) represent the MB concentration before irradiation (after adsorption equilibrium) and at time t after irradiation, respectively. Key operational parameters, including sonocatalyst dosage (0.1–1 g L−1), initial MB concentration (1–10 mg L−1), pH range (3–11), and ultrasonic power (20–100 W), were systematically studied to assess their influence on the nanocatalyst activity.

All reported degradation efficiencies and kinetic data points represent results from single experiments conducted under the specified optimized conditions, as the primary aim was to demonstrate the catalytic activity and establish operational trends.

Results and discussion

Study of structural, morphological, and optical properties of MgxAlyCuz oxide

The XRD patterns of MACOx nanoparticles, depicted in Fig. 1 (a-c), illustrate their crystalline structure after calcination at 700 °C. The diffraction peaks correspond well to the monoclinic phase of CuO, the cubic phase of MgO, and Al2O3, aligning with the JCPDS reference cards (CuO: 00–041-0254, MgO: 01–075-0447, and Al2O3: 00–029-0063)55,56. Notably, no additional peaks related to impurities are observed, confirming the successful formation of the CuO-MgO-Al2O3 composite. The high intensity of the diffraction peaks further indicates the high purity and crystallinity of the synthesized nanoparticles. The average crystallite size, calculated using the Debye-Scherrer Eq. 2, was found to be approximately 36.82 nm, 20.67 nm, and 36.41 nm for the MACO1, MACO2, and MACO3 samples, respectively. Furthermore, in Fig. 1a, the diffraction peaks appear significantly reduced due to the low Cu content, and the material exhibits a more amorphous nature compared to the other two samples, as evidenced by the increased noise in the pattern.

In this context, θ represents the diffraction angle, λ denotes the wavelength of the X-ray, and (measured in radians) corresponds to the full width at half maximum (FWHM), which reflects the broadening of diffraction peak intensity at half of its maximum value. D indicates the average crystallite size, and K is a constant with a value of 0.9 for Cu-Kα. Since the optimal lattice strain can have a positive impact on the sonocatalytic process by creating optimal lattice defects that provide more active sites for the reaction, sample MACO2 (with a strain of 0.746%) has shown the best performance among the investigated samples (MACO1: 1.127%, and MACO3: 0.476%). This principle has been previously studied by Bampos et al., where they demonstrated that optimizing the lattice strain in Pd-Cu improved its performance in the O₂ reduction reaction in an alkaline medium. This improvement was attributed to the changes in lattice strain affecting the d-band center and, consequently, the adsorption energy of the intermediate species57. These results, along with the crystallite size of sample MACO2, which had the smallest value and led to an increase in surface area, have contributed to the enhancement of sonocatalytic performance, as confirmed by the findings presented in the following sections.

FT-IR spectroscopy was utilized to analyze the chemical bonding of MACOx. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the FTIR spectrum of MACOx reveals multiple transmittance peaks within the 400–4000 cm− 1 range. The peaks observed between 500 and 700 cm− 1 are attributed to the Metal-Oxygen (M-O) vibrational modes within the MACOx structures. Specifically, the peak at 524 cm− 1 corresponds to Cu-O bending vibrations, while the peak at 702 cm− 1 is associated with Al-O bending vibrations58. The band around 1419 cm− 1 is linked to M-O stretching vibrations. A peak at 1508 cm− 1 is connected to Al-O stretching modes, whereas the one at 1419 cm− 1 corresponds to Mg-O stretching vibrations. Additionally, the peak at 2935 cm− 1 is distinctive for CuO59, and the weak peak at 1637 cm− 1 arises from H-O-H bending vibrations of adsorbed water molecules. Finally, the broad peak at 3442 cm− 1 is related to the stretching vibrations of absorbed water60.

The band gap of all three nanoparticle samples was calculated using DRS by analyzing the absorption spectra as a function of wavelength, shows in Fig. 2b. To determine the precise band gap values, Tauc’s equation (Eq. 3)61 was applied. The absorption coefficient (α) is expressed as a function of frequency (ν) in relation to the absorption band gap energy (Eg), where (h) is Planck’s constant and (n) is a parameter that determines the type of electronic transitions. The value of n varies depending on the nature of the transition: for direct allowed transitions, n = 1/2; for indirect allowed transitions, n = 2; for direct forbidden transitions, n = 3/2; and for indirect forbidden transitions, n = 334. The linear portion of the (αhν)2 versus photon energy (hν) plot is illustrated in Fig. 2c.

As observed in Fig. 2c, the band gap of the MACO2 nanoparticles (~ 1.88 eV) is significantly lower than those of MACO1 (~ 2.35 eV) and MACO3 (~ 2.10 eV). This reduction is primarily attributed to the higher proportion of the narrow bandgap p‑type semiconductor CuO in the MACO2 composition. More importantly, the formation of an intrinsic p–n heterojunction between CuO (p-type) and Al2O3 (n-type) creates an internal electric field at their interface, which not only further reduces the effective band gap but also promotes efficient charge carrier separation under ultrasonic excitation. The narrower band gap enhances the absorption of sonoluminescence‑generated photons, while the built‑in field suppresses e¯/h+ recombination, both factors synergistically contributing to the superior sonocatalytic activity of MACO234.

According to the FE-SEM images in Fig. 3a, the high MgO content appears to result in more unbalanced growth compared to the other structures shown in Fig. 3b and c (MACO2 and MACO3). In certain regions, the nanostructures have formed as thicker nanosheets, in contrast, in the other two structures, spherical nanostructures are more dominant, leading to a more uniform distribution. This characteristic likely increases the effective surface area, which enhances both pollutant adsorption and sonocatalytic degradation while also providing more active sites for nucleation and bubble generation. Furthermore, studies by Peng Qiu et al.62 have demonstrated that the rougher the surface of a sonocatalyst, the greater its potential for bubble generation. Based on FE-SEM images, this distinct surface morphology of MCAO2 is likely a consequence of its unique composition (Mg: Al: Cu = 1:2:2), which may alter the kinetics of crystal growth and particle aggregation during the sol-gel auto-combustion and calcination processes compared to the other ratios. The predominance of spherical nanostructures in MCAO2, as opposed to the thicker nanosheets observed in MCAO1, suggests a different growth pathway that favors higher surface roughness. Due to the superior sonocatalytic performance in dye removal, the elemental composition of MACO2 was determined through energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and mapping analysis, as shown in Fig. 3d. This figure illustrates the distribution of elements within the MACO2 structure. The catalyst surface contains O, Mg, Al, and Cu elements, with their distribution patterns represented in purple, gray, orange, and yellow, respectively. These images provide visual evidence of the homogeneous distribution of these elements within MACO2, confirming the successful synthesis of the sonocatalyst. According to the inner image in Fig. 3e, the quantitative analysis of MACO2 nanoparticles indicates the percentage composition of these elements.

The textural properties of the synthesized MACOx composites were evaluated using N₂ adsorption-desorption analysis. As shown in Fig. S1a, all three samples exhibit type IV isotherms with hysteresis loops, characteristic of mesoporous materials. The corresponding pore-size distribution curves (Fig. S1b) further confirm that the majority of pores fall within the mesoporous range (2–50 nm). According to the BET results summarized in Table S1, the MACO2 catalyst possesses the highest specific surface area (58.03 m2 g−1) and total pore volume among the series. This enhanced porosity and accessible surface area are advantageous for both dye adsorption and cavitation bubble nucleation, thereby promoting more efficient sonocatalytic degradation.

MB degradation parameters

This section investigates the effect of key operational parameters on the sonocatalytic degradation of MB dye. Factors such as pH, sonocatalyst dosage, dye concentration, ultrasound power, and the presence of enhancers and scavengers will be examined to assess the method’s efficiency in pollutant degradation and to explore the underlying mechanisms of dye removal.

Evaluation of adsorption, sonolysis, and sonocatalytic Oxidation processes for the degradation of MB

In this study, MB, an azo dye and a common water pollutant, was selected to evaluate the efficiency of synthesized nanocatalysts63. The adsorption, sonolysis, and sonocatalytic oxidation processes were examined to assess both the synergistic effect of ultrasonic waves with the catalyst and the performance of the synthesized catalysts. Initially, a 10 mg L− 1 MB solution was prepared, and each catalyst was separately added at a concentration of 0.8 g L− 1. The mixture was homogenized in the dark for 10 min to reach adsorption equilibrium. According to the results presented in Fig. 4a, the MB removal efficiency via adsorption using MACO2, MACO1, and MACO3 was determined to be 15.27%, 13.19%, and 14.67%, respectively. Additionally, a control experiment was conducted in the absence of a catalyst under ultrasonic irradiation, yielding a low degradation efficiency of 7%. This poor performance in the sonolysis process can be attributed to the inefficiency of ultrasonic waves in generating sufficient hydroxyl radicals. However, in the presence of both ultrasonic waves and catalysts, the degradation efficiency significantly improved, highlighting the crucial role of the sonocatalyst. The enhanced performance of the catalysts can be explained by their ability to create effective surfaces for cavitation bubble formation. The collapse of these bubbles not only leads to the direct pyrolysis of MB and generates high pressure and temperature but also excites the semiconductor catalysts, facilitating the generation of electron-hole e−/h+ pairs. Ultimately, e−/h+ separation enhances the production of reactive radicals, accelerating and improving pollutant degradation efficiency64. Among the tested catalysts, MACO2 exhibited superior performance and was selected for further parametric studies. This superior activity can be attributed to its lower band gap energy (1.88 eV) and rougher surface, which enhances bubble formation compared to MACO1 and MACO3. Furthermore, due to its smaller nanoparticle size, MACO2 provides a higher specific surface area for pollutant adsorption and degradation within the same reaction volume as the other catalysts65.

Effect of sonocatalyst dosage

Figure 4b illustrates the effect of MACO2 catalyst dosage on the sonocatalytic degradation of MB. To evaluate this effect, various nanoparticle dosages were tested while keeping other parameters constant. Increasing the catalyst dosage played a crucial role in activating reaction sites, thereby enhancing pollutant degradation through increased radical generation66. At lower concentrations, such as 0.2 g L− 1, the degradation efficiency significantly decreased. In contrast, increasing the catalyst dosage to 0.8 g L− 1 improved dye removal efficiency, reaching 84% within 60 min. However, further increasing the dosage to 1 g L− 1 did not yield a notable enhancement in degradation efficiency, and the removal rate remained nearly constant. This phenomenon can be attributed to the aggregation and overlapping of active sonocatalytic sites, which reduces the available surface area for pollutant adsorption and limits radical generation. Moreover, the aggregation of catalyst particles leads to the scattering of ultrasonic waves in the solution, hindering their effective transmission and ultimately reducing dye removal efficiency 67. Based on these findings, a dosage of 0.8 g L− 1 was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments.

Effect of MB concentration

MB solutions with concentrations ranging from 2 to 10 mg L− 1 were prepared. As shown in Fig. 4c, the results indicate that the efficiency of the sonocatalytic degradation process is remarkably high at lower concentrations, achieving 100% degradation. However, as the concentration increases, the occupation of active catalyst sites reduces the efficiency of energy absorption and the heat generated from bubble collapse, which is essential for activating the catalyst and producing radicals in the sonocatalytic process. Furthermore, at elevated concentrations, the increased occupation of active sites in MACO2 inhibits the efficient absorption of ultrasonic waves and diminishes the intensity of light emitted from the sonoluminescence phenomenon. Additionally, the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) remains constant, restricting their ability to effectively degrade pollutant molecules 20.

Effect of ultrasound power

Figure 4d illustrates the effect of ultrasonic power on MB removal. As the ultrasonic power increases, the removal efficiency improves. Specifically, when the power was increased from 20 W to 100 W, the removal efficiency of MB increased from 29% to 84%. This improvement can be attributed to the increased formation of hydroxyl radicals. This fact is probably due to the increased number of collapsing bubbles at high ultrasound power and the increase in catalytic sonoluminescence68,69. Moreover, the increased removal efficiency is due to the turbulence generated in the solution by the intensified ultrasound waves. This process enhances the mass transfer rate of reactive radical species, dye molecules, and intermediate compounds between the sonocatalyst surface and the bulk solution. Therefore, the improved mass transfer at higher ultrasonic power levels (from 20 W to 100 W) has significantly enhanced the decolorization process68,70. Additionally, higher ultrasonic power prevents the aggregation of MACO₂ particles as a sonocatalyst in the reaction solution. As a result, the sonocatalytic degradation of MB has improved due to the increased effective surface area of the catalyst.

Effect of pH

The effect of pH on the sonocatalytic degradation of MB was investigated and the highest removal efficiency (84%) observed at pH = 6.5 (Fig. 5a). The point of zero charge (pHzpc) of the MACO2 catalyst was determined to be 9 (Fig. 5b), indicating a positively charged surface at pH < 9 and a negatively charged surface at pH > 9. Given that MB is a cationic dye, electrostatic repulsion with the positively charged catalyst surface would be expected at pH values below 9, including both pH 3 and 6.5. However, the significantly lower efficiency at pH = 3 (43%) compared to the optimum at pH 6.5 requires a more nuanced interpretation beyond simple electrostatic considerations, involving the speciation of MB and the pH-dependent sonocatalytic mechanism. The pKa of MB is approximately 5.8. At pH = 3 (< pKa), MB exists almost entirely in its protonated cationic form (HMB2+), experiencing strong electrostatic repulsion from the positively charged catalyst surface, which severely limits its adsorption, a critical prerequisite for the surface-mediated reaction 71. Concurrently, highly acidic conditions are less favorable for the generation and oxidative activity of hydroxyl radicals (•OH), the primary reactive species in the process. In contrast, at pH = 6.5 (near the pKa), a significant fraction of MB molecules exists in their conventional deprotenated form (MB+) due to the acid-base equilibrium. These species have less electrostatic repulsion with the catalyst surface and can react with it more effectively compared to more acidic pHs. Furthermore, near-neutral pH is generally more favorable for •OH radical generation via water sonolysis67. At alkaline pH values (9 and 11), the catalyst surface becomes negatively charged, which should promote the adsorption of cationic MB72. However, the degradation efficiency decreased to 55% and 51%, respectively. This decline can be attributed to the reduced stability and scavenging of •OH radicals in alkaline media, where they rapidly react with excess OH¯ ions to form less reactive species (e.g., •O⁻), thereby diminishing the overall oxidative capacity of the system73.

Effect of enhancers and scavengers

To evaluate the impact of generated radicals and holes on pollutant degradation in the sonocatalytic process, various activators and scavengers were employed. As shown in Fig. 5c, the addition of K₂S₂O₈ and H₂O₂ enhanced the production of reactive radicals, while and anions acted as radical scavengers, and Ethanol (EtOH) and EDTA served as hole scavengers74. The following typical concentrations were used: 1.0 mM H2O2, 1.0 mM K2S2O8, 5.0 mM ethanol, 5.0 mM EDTA, and 10 mM Na2SO4 or Na2CO3. The introduction of H₂O₂ increased •OH radical formation (reactions 4–7)11, achieving 100% pollutant removal within 40 min.

Similarly, under ultrasonic irradiation, persulfate ions were converted into sulfate radicals (E° = 2.8 eV, reactions 8–1111), enhancing pollutant degradation.

Additionally, persulfate ions improved charge separation by capturing conduction band electrons, leading to the formation of oxidizing species such as sulfate anion radicals, according to Eq. 1275.

Conversely, scavengers reduced degradation by deactivating radicals, highlighting their role in oxidation processes. As depicted in Fig. 5c, scavengers initially adsorbed onto the catalyst surface, reducing pollutant adsorption and deactivating active sites. The presence of EtOH and EDTA led to 34.01% and 39.17% reductions in MB degradation, confirming their function as hole (h⁺) scavengers. Since h⁺ plays a dual role by directly oxidizing pollutants and generating hydroxyl radicals, its inhibition negatively impacted the process. Moreover, and anions decreased pollutant removal by 25.71% and 28.97%, respectively, due to hydroxyl radical scavenging (reactions 13–15)11, where these radicals (•OH) were consumed by the scavenging species, reducing their oxidative activity.

Reusability test

Ensuring the reusability of the sonocatalyst while maintaining its catalytic efficiency over the long term is a crucial and economically significant factor that requires special attention76. To evaluate this aspect, the stability of MACO₂ nanoparticles was tested over seven consecutive cycles. The results indicated that the reduction in activity during the first six cycles was negligible, and in the final cycle, the decrease in performance compared to the initial cycle was only 8.5%. This gradual decline can be attributed to minor catalyst loss during recovery, possible surface adsorption of intermediate products, and subtle morphological changes induced by prolonged ultrasonic cavitation. Nevertheless, the composite retained over 91% of its initial activity after seven uses, demonstrating remarkable structural and catalytic stability under the studied conditions16. As shown in Fig. 5d, MACO₂ exhibited remarkable stability under the studied conditions in the sonocatalytic removal process.

In line with the concern regarding potential environmental impact, performed atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) to measure metal leaching (Mg, Al, Cu) from the MACO2 catalyst into the treated water after the sonocatalytic process. The results, presented in Table S2, confirm that the concentrations of all three metals remained very low, well below levels of environmental concern. This indicates that the catalyst exhibits minimal leaching under the applied conditions, supporting its potential safe use in water treatment applications.

Kinetics study of sonacatalytic MB degradation

To investigate the kinetics of the sonocatalytic degradation of MB, the experimental data obtained from this process were analyzed using zero, first, and second-order kinetic equations. According to the results presented in Table 1, the process follows first-order kinetics, with a correlation coefficient of 0.9785. To determine the reaction order, the plots of Ct, lnCt, and 1/Ct​ were drawn against time for zero, first, and second-order reactions, respectively. The appropriate kinetic model was selected based on the linear regression coefficient11. The kinetic analysis was performed in two complementary steps to first identify the empirical rate law and then elucidate the underlying surface reaction mechanism. Initially, the degradation data were tested against integrated rate laws for zero-, first-, and second-order homogeneous reactions (Table 1). The highest correlation coefficient (R² = 0.9785) confirmed that the process exhibits apparent pseudo-first-order kinetics under the experimental conditions. However, given the heterogeneous nature of sonocatalysis where adsorption and surface reactions are dominant, a simple homogeneous kinetic model is insufficient to describe the mechanism. Therefore, the Langmuir–Hinshelwood (L–H) model which explicitly accounts for adsorption equilibrium followed by a surface reaction was applied. Notably, at low pollutant concentrations, the L–H model simplifies to a pseudo-first-order rate expression, which is consistent with our initial empirical finding. The subsequent L–H analysis (Eqs. 16-18, Fig. 6) allowed us to extract the fundamental adsorption (Kads) and surface reaction (Kc​) constants, providing deeper mechanistic insight beyond the apparent rate order.

Sonocatalytic degradation mechanism

The sonocatalytic degradation mechanism of organic pollutants, such as dyes, can be described through three different pathways67: i) Homogeneous Nucleation Mechanism – Initially, the applied ultrasonic waves to water lead to the formation of cavitation bubbles. Hydrophobic organic molecules migrate into these bubbles, where they undergo direct degradation due to the high pressure and temperature generated upon bubble collapse according to Eq. 2181.

ii) Radical Formation Mechanism. As shown in reactions (22–25), the bubble explosion induces extreme temperature and pressure, leading to the decomposition of water molecules and the subsequent generation of hydroxyl radicals. Additionally, dissolved oxygen in water also decomposes to form (•O) radicals. These radicals react with water at the bubble–liquid interface, ultimately producing hydroxyl radicals that contribute to dye degradation82.

iii) Heterogeneous Nucleation Mechanism. In this mechanism, nanoparticles facilitate cavitation by providing suitable nucleation sites for bubble formation. The collapse of these bubbles generates e¯/h+ pairs through thermocatalytic and sonoluminescence irradiation62. The generated h+ react with OH¯ ions to form highly reactive •OH radicals, while the e¯ reduce oxygen molecules to produce radicals, which further participate in pollutant degradation, as described in reactions 26–2923,62.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the synthesized catalyst features a p-n heterostructure, which enhances electron/hole separation83,84. The electric field formed between CuO and Al₂O₃ facilitates e¯/h+ transport, minimizing recombination as much as possible. Additionally, MgO further improves charge separation. UV-Visible spectroscopy results confirm the effective degradation of MB dye, demonstrating the efficiency of the designed sonocatalytic system. Limitations and outlook

While this study demonstrates the promising sonocatalytic performance of the designed MgO–Al2O3–CuO p–n heterostructure, certain limitations associated with the present methods should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide future work. The kinetic analysis relies on the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model, which assumes rapid adsorption–desorption equilibrium and a surface-controlled reaction as the rate-determining step. The energy consumption (EEO) calculations provide a valuable comparative metric but assume ideal energy transfer within the ultrasonic system. Furthermore, all experiments were conducted under controlled batch conditions using methylene blue as a model pollutant. For practical implementation, challenges related to catalyst recovery and reusability in continuous-flow systems, potential inhibitory effects of complex matrices (e.g., inorganic ions, organic co-solutes) present in real industrial effluents, and the complete mineralization pathway of the dye require further investigation. Future studies should focus on engineering macroscopic catalyst supports for easy separation, testing the composite against a wider range of recalcitrant pollutants and real wastewater samples, and employing advanced analytical techniques (e.g., LC-MS, TOC analysis) to track degradation intermediates and total organic carbon removal. Addressing these aspects will be essential for transitioning this promising sonocatalyst from laboratory-scale proof-of-concept to a viable technology for sustainable water treatment.

Conclusions

In this study, a ternary MgO–Al2O3–CuO metal oxide composite was synthesized using the sol-gel auto-combustion method with a p-n heterostructure for enhanced sonocatalytic degradation of MB. The optimized MgAlCuO (1:2:2) composition demonstrated superior catalytic performance, attributed to its reduced band gap (1.88 eV), enhanced electron/hole separation, and increased surface roughness, facilitating cavitation-induced radical generation. Its high efficiency in degrading methylene blue, a model persistent pollutant, suggests direct applicability in treating effluents from the textile, dyeing, printing, and cosmetic industries, where complex mixtures of azo dyes are prevalent. The sonocatalytic process exhibited significantly higher efficiency compared to sonolysis and adsorption alone, with optimal degradation achieved at pH 6.5, 0.8 g L− 1 catalyst dosage, and 100 W ultrasonic power. Kinetic modeling confirmed that the degradation followed pseudo-first-order kinetics, aligning with the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model, while radical scavenging studies highlighted the dominant role of hydroxyl radicals in the degradation mechanism. Furthermore, the catalyst’s exceptional stability over seven cycles and its favorable energy consumption profile (as evidenced by the low EEO value for MACO2 in Table 3) are critical metrics for economic feasibility and scale-up. This work provides a novel and scalable approach for sonocatalytic water treatment, emphasizing the potential of rationally designed ternary metal oxides in environmental remediation. Future work should address the extension of this approach to real complex wastewaters and further optimization of catalyst recovery and long-term stability under continuous flow conditions.
