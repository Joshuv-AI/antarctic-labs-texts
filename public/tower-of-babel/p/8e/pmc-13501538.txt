Hospital at Home vs conventional hospitalization: good clinical outcomes with lower cost and nosocomial complications
Authors: Eulalia Balbina Villegas Bruguera, Ana María Torres Corts, Sergio Oleaga Pérez Mendiguren, Joan Enric Torra Bou, Joan Blanco Blanco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospitalization at home (HAH) is a care model that is safe and cost-effective as an alternative to conventional hospitalization (CH). HAH reduces hospital admissions and length of stay in the wards with comparable clinical outcomes. Published studies exhibit significant heterogeneity in data analysis methods, patient profiles, health outcomes and economic costs.MethodsA retrospective observational study conducted on patients treated by our HAH service over 2 years (988 in 2019 and 1335 in 2023), analyzing epidemiological and clinical aspects, savings in CH beds utilization, and a comparative analysis with CH of nosocomial complications, use of complementary explorations and cost differences between both resources.ResultsOver 2 years, HAH activity increased while maintaining lower complication and readmission rates compared to CH. Over 90% of HAH patients were discharged home. Although the average length of stay was longer in HAH, the cost per patient per day was significantly lower in HAH (€86 vs. €215 in 2019 and €118 vs. €257 in 2023), and freed up 23–31 inpatient beds daily, improving hospital efficiency. Annual savings from HAH utilization amounted to €1.1 million in 2019 and €1.5 million in 2023. Additionally, reduced use of hospital resources (laboratory, CT and MRI) in HAH saved more than €58 000 in 2019 and €100 000 in 2023.ConclusionsFor selected patients, including frail elderly people with multiple comorbidities, HAH is an excellent alternative to CH that provides hospital-level medical care at home, avoiding nosocomial complications while maintaining care quality and generating substantial economic savings for the healthcare system.

Introduction

Admission to a hospital ward is the standard approach to meet the growing demand for patients that require hospital care. However, the conventional hospitalization (CH) poses a risk to the geriatric and multi-pathological population, who frequently experience nosocomial complications with high morbidity and mortality burdens, including infections, falls, delirium, and dependency-related skin injuries. These complications are also associated with loss of functional autonomy, increased risk of caregiver burnout, subsequent institutionalization, and elevated healthcare costs [1–3].

Hospitalization at home (HAH) is an evidence-based care model proven to be a safe and effective alternative to CH, reducing both length of stay and hospital inpatient ward admissions [4]. In HAH, patients remain at home under the care of a hospital-led team of doctors and nurses, with access to hospital-grade resources adapted to their home environment until discharge, which follows the same protocols as CH discharges.

In addition to requiring hospital-level care and treatment, general criteria for HAH admission include: Patient residence within the HAH service coverage area, availability of a caregiver who matches the patient’s functional dependence, clinical stability not requiring intensive monitoring, adequate home hygiene and sanitary conditions, and acceptance of HAH by the patient and family [5]. Failure to meet any of these criteria constitutes grounds for exclusion from HAH.

Referrals to HAH may originate from all healthcare tiers, including hospital-based resources and territorial Primary Care (PC) or specialized healthcare support teams.

HAH benefits patients in multiple ways, as it may contribute to avoiding many nosocomial complications associated with CH, such as infections, delirium, functional decline due to immobilization, loss of sphincter control due to prolonged bed rest and dependency-related skin injuries [6, 7]. Moreover, the HAH team provides and reinforces essential health education to caregivers and family members, ensuring better at-home management. Through seamless coordination with hospital and community healthcare resources, HAH guarantees continuity of care.

HAH also offers significant benefits to the healthcare system by reducing unnecessary emergency department transfers, accelerating bed turnover in emergency rooms and hospital wards, and optimizing resource utilization [8–17].

Hospital Dos de Maig (HDM) is a medium-sized general hospital that provides care for adult patients with acute medical and surgical conditions of low-to-intermediate complexity, and does not have intensive care beds. The HAH service of HDM covers a quarter of Barcelona’s population—over 425 000 inhabitants.

The majority of patients attended in HAH and CH of HDM are frail, elderly, and multi-pathological persons with common medical diagnoses such as infections, chronic respiratory disease exacerbations and heart failure decompensations. Most surgical patients admitted to CH undergo scheduled prosthetic surgery. In contrast, in HAH, surgical cases often involve postoperative complications (intra-abdominal abscesses, wound dehiscence, infected wounds) and complex ulcers.

We conducted a retrospective study of patients treated by our service in two nonconsecutive years, 2019 and 2023, in order to avoid COVID-19 pandemic-related biases. The specific objectives of our study were to demonstrate that patients admitted to HAH evolved correctly and were mostly satisfactorily discharged at home, with a lower incidence of nosocomial complications and cost savings per stay and per discharge compared to CH. We analyzed epidemiological and clinical characteristics and outcomes. We also included comparative rates of nosocomial complications, cost of three different diagnostic complementary explorations, and cost-effectiveness of HAH versus conventional care.

Patients and Methods

Patients

The study included all patients discharged from HAH and CH at Hospital Dos de Maig during two periods: January 1 to December 31, 2019, and January 1 to December 31, 2023. Data were extracted from the hospital registry and from electronic clinical records. Inclusion criteria included all patients referred to HAH or CH during 2019 and 2023 who met hospitalization criteria.

Clinical guidelines and protocols, together with procedures for monitoring work processes and reporting incidents and patient safety events, are shared by HAH and CH. The clinical complexity and therapeutic management of patients during hospital admission were assumed to be comparable for both hospitalization modalities, including rates of complex wound care, pharmacological oral and parenteral treatments, specialist consultations, patient education on various topics related to their pathologies and medication reconciliations at discharge.

General clinical and epidemiological data

For all patients (HAH and CH), the following data were collected: average age, gender percentage, and percentage of patients with a clinical profile of Great Frailty and Advanced Multimorbidity. These two terms describe individuals with chronic, severe health conditions requiring intense support at home and high use of healthcare resources, formally identified in the Catalan Health System (CatSalut). Also registered were the number of medical, surgical, and total discharges, discharge destination, total number of stays required per total number of patients attended, the overall average admission of stay (AAS), and AAS stratified by medical or surgical pathology, and the number of complaints from users of HAH and CH. For HAH, we collected data relating to the consulting resource: territorial, Emergencies or Outpatient Clinic, or hospital ward.

The number of stays was used to calculate the number of beds per day saved for CH by using HAH as an alternative to CH (n total HAH stays for any diagnosis in a year/365 days).

We also analyzed for both resources the rates of laboratory tests per discharge, CT scan, and MRI.

Complications

For both resources, we evaluated several quality indicators: urgent 30-day readmission rate for the same diagnosis, the fall-related injury rate, catheter-associated bacteremia rate, and dependency-associated ulcer rate.

Comparative costs

Finally, a comparative analysis of economic costs per daily patient between HAH and CH was performed. CatSalut calculates the same price per discharge in HAH and CH. Costs were calculated for each group from the perspective of the public insurer, so the analysis was limited to direct health care costs, expressed in euros. Other resources involved in the hospital care, such as patient work time and the costs of the informal care, were not evaluated.

We assumed that the cost of treatments in HAH and CH was comparable, both pharmacological and fungible material, since the same clinical guidelines and management protocols were followed in the two resources. Likewise, the costs related to support team personnel, such as administrative, warehouse, pharmacy and other common services, were assumed to be comparable. The comparative calculation of the cost of care included the costs of health care and support personnel in the two modalities, calculated directly using the data on labor costs. In CH, data on contracted personnel for laundry and cleaning, maintenance, food, orderlies and building security guards were included in the cost calculation. Likewise, the expenses derived from the material used by the hospital’s non-health personnel (cleaning, maintenance, storage, kitchen) were also included. In HAH we included the cost of its own contracted material transport service to home, the expenses derived from the multiple daily transfers of HAH personnel by public transport services, mobile telephones for all members, and team personal uniforms. The overall sum of costs obtained was divided by total stays to calculate the cost per patient per day of admission in both resources.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using complete discharge episodes and days of hospitalization as the units of analysis. Univariate descriptive analyses of demographics and clinical characteristics, effectiveness outcomes, use of resources, and costs were made, summarizing the categorical variables with the corresponding contingency tables including absolute values and percentages and the continuous variables with the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and median values. An analysis was carried out using a non-experimental quantitative methodology with a correlational analytical purpose to find out the cost difference of an admission in HAH and CH.

A bivariate analysis of the main variables was performed at the end of the study by type of cohort (HAH and CH). The Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables and Student’s t-test for independent data to compare continuous variables. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample and clinical results

9616 patients were included in the survey, 4056 in 2019 (988 in HAH, 24.4% of total Dos de Maig hospital admissions) and 5060 in 2023 (1335 in HAH, 26.4% of total). In HAH, 50% of patients were admitted from outpatient hospital resources, including emergency room, day hospital, outpatient clinic; 25% came directly from hospital wards to reduce CH stays; and 25% were referred by non-hospital territorial health teams, mainly PC.

The mean age and percentage of female patients of admitted patients was 77 years (53% women) in HAH and 75 years in CH (58% women) in 2019, and 78 years (55% women) in HAH and 76 years (58% women) in CH in 2023. Despite these similar results, the proportion of patients identified as having Great Frailty or Advanced Multimorbidity was clearly higher in HAH: in 2019, 45% in HAH versus 18% in CH, and in 2023, 43% in HAH versus 23% in CH.

The 8560 total stays generated by HAH admissions in 2019 implied CH savings of 23.5 beds each day on average. In 2023, paralleling the increase in staff recruitment in HAH, discharges and thus stays increased to 11 334 in total, which represented 31 beds per day saved to CH on average.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

75% of HAH patients were admitted from health resources other than the CH, so that from the beginning of hospital care the HAH team designed the diagnostic and therapeutic objectives, in the context of a greater initial clinical instability and greater need for patient surveillance.

The proportion of frail patients with multimorbidity was clearly higher in HAH. These patients require highly personalized and integrated home-centered care focused on the management of exacerbations and multiple symptoms. In addition, whereas surgical patients in CH were admitted mainly for scheduled procedures, those in HAH were admitted primarily due to postoperative complications, which increased the complexity of follow-up and care during hospitalization.

Lack of adequate home support or refusal of HAH were the primary reasons for not admitting patients to HAH and for necessitating admission to CH. In light of these considerations, the clinical complexity and therapeutic management of medical patients during hospital admission were assumed to be comparable for both hospitalization modalities. These data contradict a common criticism of HAH, based on the theoretical limitation of home teams for the management of acute complex clinical situations without transferring the patient to the CH.

In both years, AAS was higher in HAH than in CH. There are several reasons for this difference: First, many HAH admissions involved very advanced chronic elderly patients with severe functional impairment (>70%), who often arrive at our resource after recent and repeated hospital admissions and consultations, with consequent nosocomial functional decline and higher risk of serious complications, requiring more complex monitoring and care at home during admission. Second, and especially true in HAH surgical patients, these are often admitted to HAH from the hospital ward due to complications or a torpid evolution associated with their own multi-pathology and fragility. Third, the absolute rigor applied by the HAH in ensuring coordinated follow-up with non-hospital resources upon discharge from HAH, which often forces a delay in discharge. Despite these limitations, we can observe a decrease in medical and especially surgical AAS in our HAH, thanks to the implementation of discharge coordination protocols with consulting services and territorial sanitary teams, through dialogue and mutual trust among professionals.

Additionally, the comparative results with CH of different nosocomial complications were clearly favorable to the home admission. Our results are explained by the multidisciplinary work of individualized planning for each patient and the extensive educational support provided by the team at home, which provides families and caregivers with tools for better control and independent management of diverse chronic conditions.

More than 90% of patients treated at HAH evolved correctly without complications and were mostly satisfactorily discharged at home. Furthermore, these benefits extended beyond hospital discharge, as shown by our better readmission rate in less than 30 days for the same diagnosis, a consistent result with previous publications [18–21]. These excellent outcomes, together with the educational support and caregiver empowerment in the home setting, most likely explain why no complaints were registered in the service during the 2-year study period.

Our analysis of direct costs confirms the substantial savings for the healthcare system associated with HAH compared with CH, which remained at 40% per discharge episode. These highly positive results in terms of quality of care and direct costs are accompanied by freed-up CH beds equivalent to a conventional ward each day, that also contributing to improved management of surgical waiting lists for admissions.

Interpretation within the context of the wider literature

A growing number of publications in recent years provide data on the clinical benefits, safety, and low rate of nosocomial complications of admissions to HAH. Bastug et al. [22] analyzed the clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness of the outpatient parenteral antimicrobial treatment (OPAT) in 307 patients, with an end-of-­treatment success rate of 92.2%, and only 5.2% of adverse effects.

Ugarte et al. [23] demonstrate the capacity to provide effective care for selected postsurgical patients, with only 5.5% requiring transfer back to the hospital ward and a low rate of adverse events. Furthermore, diverse previous publications confirmed that economic balance is positive for HAH [22, 24, 25]. Most studies published to date in the home treatment of specific pathologies, such as infections, pneumological patients or oncological treatments. Achanta et al. [24] analyzed 34 studies included in a meta-analysis comparing the costs of HAH with those of CH, and observed that 32 studies found hospital at home to cost less. However, no uniform method exists to track and assess costs, and there is worry that the costs of hospital-at-home programs are underestimated. Another study by Johns Hopkins University of more than 1500 patients found that the HAH model achieved a 19% cost reduction compared to CH, with equal or better clinical outcomes included mortality, complications and 30-day readmissions, higher satisfaction levels, shorter average lengths of stay, and reduced use of lab and diagnostic tests [25]. However, there is no detailed cost analysis.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective observational design. First, patient allocation to HAH or CH was not randomized but largely determined by the availability of a suitable caregiver at home, which may have introduced selection bias. Second, relevant psychosocial outcomes such as caregiver burden, patient satisfaction, and quality of life were not assessed, limiting the scope of our conclusions. Third, the economic evaluation only included direct healthcare costs from the perspective of the public insurer, while indirect costs such as productivity loss, formal caregiver salaries, or the economic impact on families were not considered.

Finally, the study was conducted in a single hospital with a consolidated HAH program, which may reduce the generalizability of our results to other contexts with different organizational models or resource availability. In a hospital with greater complexity than Hospital Dos de Maig, admission diagnoses, diagnosis-adjusted lengths of stay, nosocomial burden, and costs per stay in both CH and HAH may differ substantially from those observed in our study. Furthermore, our hospital belongs to the Catalan public healthcare system; therefore, our results are not applicable to hospitals under purely private or mutual insurance-based management models, which also pursue objectives focused on maximum economic efficiency and profit generation.

Implications for policy, practice and research

Our comparative economic balance of direct health care costs with CH is clearly positive for HAH, with savings of 50% per stay and 40% per discharge compared to CH. In addition, in HAH only those complementary explorations strictly necessary for the adequate management and control of the patient during admission are requested at home, resulting in significantly lower consumption associated with complementary examinations than in CH.

As a result of the good health and economic results worldwide, we are also witnessing a general consolidation of the HAH option in Spain [26]. In fact, our HAH accounted for 24% and 27% of total Dos de Maig discharges in both years, positioning it among the top-performing public hospitals in Catalonia in terms of home-based care adoption [27, 28].

Conclusions

Our findings provide a comprehensive overview of information related to patient volumes, care quality, and economic efficiency within an HAH service and support the progressive integration of HAH models into healthcare policies, not merely as an efficient alternative but as the preferred option for selected cases and diagnoses. Such integration would optimize hospital bed capacity and resource use without substantial physical infrastructure investments, a crucial advantage in territories with high hospital demand, an aging population, and a growing burden of complex chronic conditions, reducing unnecessary emergency department visits, and minimizing complications associated with conventional hospitalization.
