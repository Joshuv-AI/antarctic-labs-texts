Exploration of virtual reality applications in myopia: A systematic review
Authors: Xiaona Ping, Juno Kim, Nayuta Yoshioka, Padmaja Sankaridurg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple interventions are available to prevent or slow myopia progression, the rapidly rising prevalence highlights the need to enhance both the development of novel approaches and adherence to existing strategies. Virtual reality (VR) is an emerging technology in ophthalmology and vision science, yet its potential applications in myopia remain underexplored.Main textTo explore the prior application, if any, of VR in myopia research. A systematic search was conducted across nine databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, and ScienceDirect, to identify journal articles and conference proceedings reporting application of VR in myopia. The search terms used were "Virtual Reality", "VR", "myopia", "nearsightedness", "shortsightedness", "refractive errors", and "refractive disorder". The search covered publications from inception to April 6, 2025. Risk of bias was assessed using the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool and the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist, with assessments performed independently by two authors.ConclusionA total of sixteen articles fit the criteria and were included in the review. Of these, seven investigated VR-based interventions for myopia, an additional four were focused on testing and diagnostic applications, three articles reported on vision training, and the remaining two articles were related to educational uses. None were related to myopia control. All studies were published between 2016 and 2024. With respect to the VR technologies, eleven studies utilized commercial VR headsets and/or smartphones paired with VR viewers as experimental devices. Fully immersive virtual environments or systems were also commonly employed in the included studies. Most studies focused on the usability and validity of VR technologies in this context.ConclusionsNone of the articles reported on the efficacy of VR in preventing or slowing myopia progression, however, our review identified the potential of VR in auxiliary aspects of myopia, including testing, diagnosis, intervention, vision training, and education. Further research is warranted to evaluate the practical feasibility, clinical effectiveness, safety, and broader applicability of VR technologies in myopia.Keywords: Virtual reality, Myopia, Testing, Diagnosis, Education, Systematic review

Introduction

Myopia is one of the most prevalent eye conditions worldwide, currently affecting 10%–30% of the adult population and nearly 80%–90% of children and young adults in parts of East and Southeast Asia.1,2 It is estimated that myopia prevalence will rise to 50% by 2050, with nearly 10% of the population estimated to have high myopia.3 High myopia significantly increases the risk of pathological complications that might result in vision impairment and irreversible blindness.4,5 There exist multiple strategies to slow or prevent myopia, but given the rapidly rising prevalence, further work is needed on other strategies as well as uptake and compliance to existing strategies.

Virtual reality (VR), a high-end user-computer interface enables real-time simulation and interaction through multiple sensory channels, including visual, auditory, tactile, smell, and taste.6 It is characterized by the illusion of participation in a synthetic environment rather than direct external observation of the real environment. This immersive experience is achieved through the integration of technologies such as three-dimensional stereoscopic displays, head and body tracking, and binaural audio, which together produce a multisensory virtual environment.7

Currently, VR is widely utilized in different healthcare domains, including medical training and education for professionals,8 surgical skills training,9 clinical assessment and diagnostic,10 mental health therapy,11,12 rehabilitation,13,14 and patient education and communication.15,16 In ophthalmology and vision sciences, VR was considered for clinical and surgical training, screening and diagnosis (such as visual field testing and strabismus evaluation), treatment and therapy of amblyopia, low vision services, and as a teaching tool (for example, use of direct ophthalmoscopy).17 A more recent systematic review considered the application of VR in the design and assessment of amblyopia (31 studies), strabismus (18 studies), and myopia (6 studies)18 and found that while smartphone-based VR viewers were common in amblyopia research, commercial standalone VR headsets were more common in myopia and strabismus studies. Ali et al. (2023) investigated the use of VR-based training for ocular (e.g., myopia, amblyopia, presbyopia and age-related macular degeneration) and neurological disorders (e.g., Alzheimer's, multiple sclerosis epilepsy and autism spectrum disorder)19 and found that VR based exercises aided in rehabilitation due to enhanced motivation and adherence to treatment in a safe and controlled environment, enable objective measurements of patient progress and performance, as well as allowing for customization to improve treatment effectiveness.19

Myopia was only addressed peripherally in these prior reviews. Iskander et al. (2022) provided a comprehensive review of the use of VR and augmented reality in ophthalmology; however, their discussion did not extend to the use of these technologies in myopia.17 Chan et al. (2023) included myopia in their study but primarily focused on the design features of VR software and its applications in amblyopia, strabismus, and myopia.18 Ali et al. (2023) concentrated on VR-based training for ocular diseases and neurological disorders, examining myopia control methods from the standpoint of vision training.19 Therefore, this systematic review aims to investigate the current applications of VR technology in myopia. This study aims to include all articles on relevant topics for the use of VR in myopia (e.g., examination, diagnosis, intervention, and education).

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A comprehensive literature search was conducted for peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings reporting on use of VR in myopia using the databases PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, and ScienceDirect. The search covered publications from inception to April 6, 2025. The systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.20 The following search terms were used for PubMed: ("Virtual Reality" [MeSH Terms] OR "VR" [All Fields]) AND ("myopia" [MeSH Terms] OR "nearsighted∗"[All Fields] OR "shortsighted∗"[All Fields] OR "refractive errors" [MeSH Terms] OR "refractive disorder∗"[ All Fields]). These terms were selected to be sufficiently inclusive so that publications related to myopia among the multitude of applications of VR were identified. The same terms with database customization were conducted for the other databases. Filters were applied to include only studies conducted on humans and published in English. The search results from all nine databases were exported to the Covidence systematic review platform (https://app.covidence.org; Melbourne, VIC, Australia).

Studies involving VR applications related to myopia were included in the systematic review. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) duplicates; 2) studies that did not report data on human participants; 3) not published in English; 4) did not use VR; 5) VR applications not related to myopia research; 6) articles that only proposed hypotheses without experimental validation; 7) articles focused on the effects of using VR on visual function or visual/ocular parameters, and not relating to myopia; 8) conference demonstrations; 9) book chapters, review articles, comments, letters, conference abstracts, case reports, or articles where the full text was unavailable. Duplicate records were identified and removed using the Covidence systematic review platform in combination with manual screening and deletion. Articles that remained after considering the exclusion criteria were further evaluated to ensure that they fit the study criteria. Thereafter, the reference lists of all included studies were manually searched and reviewed to identify additional relevant literature. Primary literature searches and screening were conducted by XP and reviewed by PS, JK, and NY. All discrepancies for study selection and assessments were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was performed by XP and PS and included: year of publication, surname of the first author, study design, country of residence, sample size, participant's age and sex, VR device or system used, VR application categories, study objectives, and VR system design. The characteristics of all included studies were tabulated and described in the systematic review.

The risk of bias was assessed unblinded using the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (ROB 2) for randomized trials21 and ROB2 for randomized crossover trials.22 The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist was used for Diagnostic Accuracy Studies,23 Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies,24 and Quasi-Experimental Studies.25 Two independent investigators (XP and NY) carried out the risk-of-bias assessment for the included articles. Any discrepancies identified during this process were initially discussed between XP and NY. If consensus could not be reached, a third investigator (PS) was consulted. Unresolved issues were subsequently adjudicated through discussion among all authors until agreement was achieved. The summarized risk of bias assessments was tabulated (Supplementary Material 1).

Results

The database search yielded 1303 articles, with an additional two articles identified from searching the references of included articles.26,27 After removing 255 duplicates, 1050 articles remained, and of these, 986 were excluded by reviewing the titles and abstracts. The remaining 64 articles were reviewed in full and of these, 16 articles were eligible and included in the systematic review. Reasons for exclusion of the 48 articles are given in Fig. 1). Table 1, Table 2 summarise the characteristics of the VR devices, as well as the study objectives and the design of the VR systems, respectively. Eleven of the 16 studies had used commercial VR headsets and/or smartphones paired with VR viewers as experimental devices, with fully immersive virtual environments or systems also commonly employed. Of the 16 articles, nine were from East Asia (Mainland China: 5, Taiwan China: 2, Republic of Korea: 1, Japan: 1), three from Southern Europe (Portugal: 2, Spain: 1), two from Oceania (Australia: 1, New Zealand: 1), one from South Asia (India), and one from Southeast Asia (Indonesia). All studies were published between 2016 and 2024. The 16 studies evaluated the use of VR in myopia intervention (n ​= ​7), testing and diagnosis (n ​= ​4), vision training (n ​= ​3), and education (n ​= ​2).

VR-based interventions for myopia

Seven articles explored the use of VR in myopia, specifically, utilizing VR technology to explore the underlying mechanisms of myopia development and to evaluate potential strategies for its control. Among these, three studies employed VR headsets to examine the effects of blue light stimulation of the optic nerve head on ocular structures in myopic individuals. Two studies assessed the short-term impact of VR headsets use on ocular parameters and tried to determine if VR is myopiagenic. Additionally, two studies utilized VR to explore the relationship between near work and myopia, examining both near work as a risk factor and the potential of VR-based interventions to mitigate its effects.

Experimental simulation of light and myopia

Three related studies used blue light to stimulate the optic nerve head using a proprietary VR application. This application was delivered via the screen of Samsung Galaxy S7 smartphone, which was either inserted into a VR system known as the VR box or used with a VR headset. Participants engaged with the application by placing the smartphone inside the VR device, enabling immersive visual stimulation. The blue-light stimulus was a localized point source (angular size approximately 3–4°), specifically aligned with the optic nerve head. It was not an ambient or background illumination. The stimulus was temporally modulated using a rectangular-wave flicker at a frequency of approximately 12–15 ​Hz, with a peak wavelength ranging from 450 to 460 ​nm and a luminance between 14.74 and 22 ​cd/m2. The typical exposure duration was 1 ​min, although some experimental protocols also included shorter (10 ​s) and longer (10 ​min) presentations. All three studies investigated blue light effects in myopic individuals: two examined electrophysiological retinal changes,32,35 while the third study considered choroidal changes.38

In the electrophysiological studies, Amorim-de-Sousa et al. (2021) demonstrated that stimulation of the blind spot with blue light increased electroretinogram (ERG) b-wave amplitude 20 ​min post stimulation.32 Additionally, pattern ERG changes were more pronounced in myopes than non-myopes. It was suggested that the changes in retinal electrical activity, likely involved the dopaminergic system located in the inner plexiform layer. In a second related study, Schilling et al. (2022) studied if varying the duration of blind spot stimulation influenced the ERG response of the myopic retina and its relationship to axial length.35 They reported a significant increase in b-wave amplitude across all stimulation protocols compared to the control condition (no stimulation for 1 ​min), and found a positive correlation between axial length and the percent change in b-wave amplitude for the 10-min stimulation protocol. It was considered that blind spot stimulation with blue light elevated the ON-bipolar cell activity in the retina, which may confer a protective effect against the development of myopia.

The third study considered changes in choroidal thickness and axial length associated with blue light stimulation of melanopsin-expressing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells at the blind spot.38 Subfoveal choroidal thickness increased significantly within 60 ​min of blue light stimulation compared to sham control (no light stimulation) and red-light stimulation. Significant choroidal thickening was observed in emmetropes (10 ​± ​2 ​μm, P ​< ​0.001) but not in myopes (4 ​± ​2 ​μm, P ​> ​0.05). The authors concluded that blue light stimulation of melanopsin-expressing axons at the blind spot increases choroidal thickness in young adults, with potential implications for regulating eye growth. These three studies provide findings to support the view that VR-delivered blue light stimulation can influence both retinal electrical activity and choroidal response, offering a promising targeted approach for myopia management. However, it should be noted that this section presents experimental simulations designed to explore the potential association between light exposure and myopia. These simulations are exploratory in nature and are not to be interpreted as clinical practice.

Head-mounted display (HMD) VR usage and myopia

Two studies explored if VR use was related to myopia development in young adults. Turnbull and Phillips (2017) investigated whether an early VR headset — specifically the Oculus DK2 (SDK version 0.8.0), originally designed for developers affects binocular vision and induces a myopiagenic stimulus with prolonged use,30 and found no adverse effects on binocular vision during short-term (40-min) use. Interestingly, significant thickening of the choroid (about 10 μm) was observed following VR trials (P ​< ​0.001) suggesting that VR headsets may not induce myopiagenic stimuli, despite the close viewing distances involved.

Yoon et al. (2021) examined the effects of using a VR smartphone-based HMD (VR SHMD) —assembled by integrating a Samsung Galaxy S6 smartphone into a VR headset—compared to using the smartphone (Samsung device with a 5.1-inch light-emitting diode screen) as a control condition. Participants used the devices for 2 ​h, and the study assessed changes in visual parameters (refraction, accommodation, convergence, stereopsis, and ocular alignment), choroidal thickness, and subjective symptoms.34 The study found significant changes in the near point of convergence (NPC), accommodation, exophoric deviation, stereopsis, and accommodative lag after using the VR SHMD, but not after smartphone use. Of relevance is the finding from the VR group reporting more severe subjective discomfort related to dry eye and neurologic symptoms than the smartphone group. However, no significant changes in refraction or choroidal thickness were observed after using either device.

Near work and myopia

Using VR, two studies investigated if near activities were associated with myopia. Li et al. (2024) examined eye movement patterns for indoor, outdoor, and reading activities using the VR headset40 and found downward gaze and fixation behaviours more prevalent for reading and indoor environments compared to outdoor settings. It was considered that downward eye movements, which can induce instantaneous axial elongation, suggest a potential biomechanical pathway for myopia progression through optic nerve traction and ocular tissue remodelling.

In another study, Yi et al. (2024) investigated whether virtual distance viewing technology can prevent ocular parameter changes induced by prolonged near work.43 Their study compared two groups: one using traditional textbooks and the other employing virtual distance viewing technology. After 4 ​h of reading, the traditional textbook group showed increased myopic spherical equivalent, decreased corneal thickness, and reduced anterior chamber depth, whereas these changes were not observed in the virtual distance viewing group. The virtual distance viewing group also showed improvements in choroidal thickness, positive relative accommodation, and amplitude of accommodation.43 These findings suggest that virtual distance viewing technology might be a promising tool for preventing the negative ocular effects associated with extended periods of near work.

VR in testing and diagnosis

Refraction test

Three studies explored the application of VR in refraction with each offering unique insights into its potential compared to classical or clinical standard methods. Two studies focused on subjective spherical refraction,29,31 while the third investigated light field VR (LFVR) technology for measuring refractive error.39 The first study validated a self-developed VR prototype incorporating an algorithm designed to perform subjective spherical refraction, thereby minimizing the need for direct patient-examiner interaction.29 The results showed a mean difference in spherical equivalent of −0.034 ​± ​0.454 dioptres (D) when compared to classical subjective refraction methods, demonstrating good precision with intra-observer variability of 0.072 ​± ​0.197 D, closely matching the classical methods' variability of 0.034 ​± ​0.195 D. The system also exhibited high reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 98.9%. In another study, the GearVision VR headset was used to assess spherical refractive errors (myopia).31 This device showed a slight myopic bias of −0.03 D compared to clinical standard subjective refraction. Impressively, 78% of refractive errors were within ±0.25 D, and 98% were within ±0.50 D of the clinical standard. The 95% limits of agreement ranged from −0.65 D to 0.45 D, and the reproducibility was high, with 95.8% of measurements having absolute differences ≤0.25 D and 100% ​≤ ​0.50 D.

The third study evaluated LFVR technology against established clinical methods, including closed-field and open-field autorefractors, retinoscopy, and subjective refraction.39 The LFVR system showed substantial agreement with these methods, with ICC values ranging from 82.7% to 85.5% (P ​< ​0.01) for spherical equivalent and 80.7%–86.7% for the first and second focal lines (all P ​< ​0.01). The repeatability was also high, with ICC values of 88.8% and 97.5% for the first and second focal lines, respectively.

Binocular imbalance and myopia

Tao et al. (2022) investigated the relationship between binocular imbalance and the development of abnormal binocular refraction (myopia) using a VR headset.36 They examined the role of visual perception abnormalities in the onset and progression of myopia. Their findings revealed that while binocular imbalance did not significantly correlate with the spherical equivalent or visual acuity of a unilateral eye, it might be associated with the imbalanced development of the refractive system between the eyes. Notably, the study observed that as predominance increased, the spherical equivalent of the dominant eye tended to become more myopic compared to the non-dominant eye. This suggests that binocular imbalance might play a role in the differential development of anisometropia.

VR in vision training

Three recent studies have explored the potential of VR for vision training in myopia. Brata et al. (2023) investigated the feasibility of a VR application designed for myopic adults to independently practise Bates eye exercises.37 This study incorporated three levels of head swing and optical swing exercises over a period of five weeks. Although no significant changes in myopia levels were observed after five weeks, participants experienced an average reduction of 0.13 D in refractive error.

Shindou and colleagues (2024) investigated a novel, non-invasive method for improving visual acuity using an HMD system with variable focal distances and Gabor stimuli-based perceptual learning.41 The researchers developed a system that adaptively adjusts the image formation distance based on each participant's visual acuity. In this study, experiment 1 involved 20 min training sessions with three different conditions, separated by 2 h intervals. Experiment 2 consisted of 10-min training sessions, conducted twice daily, twice a week, over a 2-week period, totalling 80 ​min of training. Results from Experiment 1 showed an improvement of 0.088 LogMAR, while Experiment 2 demonstrated an average improvement of 0.216 LogMAR in baseline visual acuity. Overall, the personalized VR-based approach improved visual acuity by an average of 35% in just 1/8th the training time of conventional methods.

Xu et al. (2024) investigated the efficacy and safety of VR-based visual training (VRVT) for myopia control in children.42 Participants were randomly assigned to either an intervention group (VRVT plus single-vision spectacles, with 20 ​min of daily VRVT) or a control group (single-vision spectacles only). After three months, the intervention group exhibited significantly less axial length progression (0.063 ​± ​0.060 ​mm) compared to the control group (0.129 ​± ​0.060 ​mm) (P ​= ​0.037). Furthermore, macular choroidal thickness changeded to 22.633 ​± ​36.171 ​μm in the intervention group, compared to −3.000 ​± ​31.056 ​μm in the control group (P ​= ​0.005).

VR in education

Two studies investigated the use of VR for educational purposes in relation to myopia. Tseng & Chen (2016) evaluated an educational program in VR to promote preventive behaviours among young university students with high myopia.28 The 150-min educational session included a lecture on high myopia complications, VR experiences (i.e., enabling students to perceive the visual impact of retinal detachment, macular degeneration, advanced glaucoma, and cataract), verbal persuasion through informal discussions with an individual who had overcome retinal detachment cause by high myopia, and a fault-finding game embedded within a 5-part video series on preventive concepts. The program demonstrated an immediate positive effect on participants' knowledge, perceived susceptibility and severity, self-efficacy, and preventive behaviours. Notably, significant increases in cues to action (i.e., messages or information from any resources that prompted student to take action), self-efficacy, and preventive behaviour were observed after six weeks. Moreover, Li et al. (2021) used two VR myopia games to foster empathy in individuals without myopia towards those who suffer from myopia,33 and these games were found to be highly engaging and effective in fostering empathy. Those without myopia developed a deeper understanding of the challenges faced by those with the condition, while even those with moderate myopia gained new insights into the difficulties associated with high myopia.

Discussion

This systematic review examined 16 studies exploring VR applications in myopia, including interventions for myopia, assessment and diagnosis, vision training, and education. Most studies (11/16) used commercial VR headsets or smartphone-compatible viewers with fully immersive environments. While several studies included in this review report promising outcomes regarding the application of VR in myopia research, the evidence remains exploratory and further validation is needed. Interestingly, the results also suggest there is an increasing trend in the use of VR technology for myopia. Of the 16 articles, four were published between 2016 and 2018, and twelve were published between 2021 and 2024. This trend is indicative of continuing expansion and inclusion of VR applications in myopia; however, it may reflect increasing academic research interest rather than widespread clinical adoption.

VR-based interventions for myopia

Previous studies delivered blue light through a VR headset to myopic eyes32,35,38 and found that blue light could influence retinal and choroidal responses, thus potentially serving as a strategy for myopia management. Increasingly, the choroid is considered to play a role in regulating ocular growth and the development of myopia.44,45 Choroidal thickness was found to be negatively correlated with increasing axial length, indicating that choroidal thickness may serve as a predictive biomarker for long-term changes in ocular elongation.46, 47, 48 Therefore, an increase in choroidal thickness, as found in these studies, may be a potential indicator of effective myopia intervention.

Other studies that examined the relationship between VR use and myopia development found inconsistent results.30,34 Whilst Turnbull and Phillips (2017) observed significant choroidal thickening after participants viewed virtual environments, Yoon et al. (2021) did not observe these changes. However, there were differences with the methodology with participants in Yoon et al.'s study using VR SHMD for 2 ​h, whereas in Turnbull and Phillips' study, the duration of HMD VR usage was limited to 40 ​min. These discrepancies indicate the need to further explore the effect of HMD VR device usage on choroidal thickness and to determine whether this change could influence myopia progression in children and young adults.

Additionally, two studies utilized VR to explore the relationship between near work and myopia, examining both near work as a risk factor and the potential of VR-based interventions to mitigate its effects. One of these studies found that downward eye movements during reading and indoor activities may induce transient axial elongation, a possible contributor to myopia development.40 The other study proposed that virtual distance viewing technology could serve as a promising alternative to traditional textbooks, potentially mitigating the adverse ocular effects associated with prolonged near work.43

VR in testing and diagnosis

Three studies evaluated VR in assessment of myopic refractive error, examining subjective spherical refraction using non-immersive VR systems29,39 and with immersive VR headset.31 Although each of these studies used different VR systems and methodologies, all were found to have high precision, reliability and agreement compared to classical clinical methods, indicating VR's diagnostic potential. Additionally, one study found that increased ocular dominance correlated with greater myopia in the dominant eye, though binocular imbalance didn't affect refractive system development.36

VR in vision training and education

VR has been explored for vision training and educational purposes in myopia and reviewed here for completeness, but should be regarded as experimental and exploratory in nature, and not as evidence to inform clinical practice for eye care professionals as approaches such as Bates exercise method, is considered as controversial within the field of ophthalmology and optometry due to their lack of evidence regarding clinical effectiveness and validity.49,50

One study investigated the use of a VR headset to deliver Bates eye exercises to myopic adults.37 Preliminary findings indicated a potential reduction in refractive error, but the results were confounded by a small sample size (n ​= ​14) and a short intervention period of five weeks. Similarly, Shindou et al. (2024) examined visual acuity enhancement using an HMD system with variable focal distances and Gabor stimuli.41 Although improvements in baseline visual acuity were observed, the training duration was constrained to maximum two weeks (totalling 80 ​min). Furthermore, both studies on vision training have several design flaws,37,41 which compromise the accuracy, reliability, and external validity of their findings. Another study assessed the efficacy and safety of VR-based vision training for myopia control in children over a three-month period,42 reporting positive outcomes in axial length and choroidal thickness measurements.

Furthermore, VR has been used as an educational tool to promote preventive behaviours among individuals with high myopia and to foster empathy in non-myopic individuals towards those who are myopic.28,33 These studies underscore the potential of VR not only in ocular health education but also in enhancing greater awareness and empathy regarding myopia.

Challenges and limitations of VR

Despite rapid advancements in VR technology, several challenges, limitations, and concerns remain regarding its development and widespread application for myopia management. Firstly, the resolution of current commercial VR headsets typically range from 10 to 15 pixels per degree.51,52 Although advanced designs have achieved a VR pixel resolution of 24 pixels per degree, this is still well below the human eye's resolving capability of approximately 50–60 pixels per degree.51 The relatively lower resolution of VR headsets may contribute to visual fatigue, eye strain, blurred vision, accommodation-vergence conflict, headaches, and a diminished sense of immersive. Moreover, the weight of VR headsets may cause physical discomfort, neck strain, and reduced usability for users, particularly during extended use or among vulnerable populations such as children and elderly. Heavier VR headsets shorten the duration of comfortable wear. Even the lightest headsets, weighing around 500 ​g, may not be tolerable for everyone for a full 2-h session. For every 33 ​g of added weight, the average comfortable wear time decreases by 11 ​min.53 Therefore, the weight of VR headsets is a crucial factor in their application for myopia management.

Additionally, the weight of the VR headset can negatively impact the overall VR experience and may contribute to cybersickness—an established challenge in virtual environments.54,55 Cybersickness, similar to motion sickness, is induced by virtual environments and includes symptoms such as headache, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, postural instability, and disorientation.56 It arises from sensory conflicts caused by discrepancies among visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive inputs, and is further exacerbated by VR system-related factors such as high latency, low frame rates, and wide fields of view in the visual display.57,58 Although cybersickness can be entirely avoided by simply terminating VR exposure, some individuals report severe and long-lasting symptoms—such as nausea and discomfort—even after brief periods of use.59 Currently, there are no highly effective strategies for mitigating cybersickness.55,56,60 Furthermore, in this review, seven of the 16 studies employed VR headsets and smartphones paired with VR viewers as experimental devices. However, this technology has a limited field of view and resolution, and typically tracks only angular head rotation rather than linear movement. In addition, inherent system latency would cause oculomotor problems, such as interfere with vestibulo-ocular reflex and reduced visual comfort. These aforementioned challenges and concerns should be carefully considered in the future development, experimental design, and application of VR technology for myopia management. Nevertheless, it is worth exploring how to expand the application of VR in myopia management despite these current limitations.

Strengths and limitations of the review

A key strength of this study was its extensive search strategy, which included nine digital databases covering both medical and technological fields, peer-reviewed journal articles, and conference proceedings that met the required criteria. This is important because some of the innovation in this area feature prominently in engineering, where conference proceedings are the primary research outlet. This study also explored multiple applications of VR in myopia, including testing and diagnosis, interventions, vision training, and education. Despite closely adhering to the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews, several limitations may affect the validity of the findings. Firstly, only publications in English were considered, potentially excluding relevant studies published in other languages. Secondly, the risk of bias assessment of the included studies indicated varied levels of bias, as shown in Supplementary Material 1. Additionally, as summarized in Table 1, the experimental designs varied considerably, with sample sizes ranging from 6 to 788 participants. While some studies assessed the application of VR technology in myopia through quantitative methodologies, others relied on participant questionnaires or interviews. Several studies lacked control groups and were limited by short experiment durations with limited repeat trials, potentially compromising the reliability of their findings (but at least mitigate participant fatigue). Moreover, the varied nature of the VR headsets and hardware systems used, along with differing research objectives, and the absence of standardised indicators for evaluating myopia interventions, made it challenging or impossible to compare outcomes.

Due to the substantial heterogeneity among the included studies, a meta-analysis could not be conducted. Consequently, the findings were less consistent, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions. In the future, it is expected that the effectiveness of VR applications in myopia management can be conclusively determined through further experimental research informed by this review. Furthermore, this review focused on the application of VR technology in myopia. As a result, the effects of VR usage on ocular parameters unrelated to myopia, as well as safety, cost-effectiveness, and the legal, ethical, and regulatory aspects of VR usage, were not included.

Future directions for VR in myopia management

At this stage, the exploration of VR applications in myopia includes interventions, diagnostic testing, vision training, and educational uses. VR technology has also been proposed as a potential tool for the prevention and control of myopia,61 however, its long-term effects on ocular parameters and safety in children and adolescents remain unclear. This is particularly concerning given that the primary target population for myopia onset prevention and progression control comprises children and adolescents. Most commercially available VR headsets currently on the market recommend a minimum user age of 12 or 13 years. Since early childhood is considered a critical period for visual development, there are growing concerns that exposure to such devices may affect future visual function. Studies evaluating symptoms, oculomotor function, and refractive error changes in children and adolescents suggest that VR use can induce short-term visual symptoms (e.g., tiredness, drowsiness, sore eyes, and difficulty concentrating) and transient oculomotor alterations (e.g., increased esophoria), temporarily reduced near visual acuity, and transient myopic shifts.62,63 However, these effects typically resolve within minutes after cessation of VR exposure. While VR appears to be generally tolerable in children and adolescents despite short-term visual strain, its long-term impact on ocular development remains uncertain. In contrast, another study investigating VR use in young adults reported enhanced vergence flexibility and transient choroidal thickening.30 Nonetheless, further longitudinal research is needed to determine whether these effects confer clinically meaningful protection against myopia progression. Based on this review, there remains no consensus on the short- or long-term effects of VR technology on visual function and myopia development in both children and adults.

Currently, the application of VR in myopia management remains controversial, as screen-based VR interventions may inadvertently reinforce environmental risk factors (i.e., excessive near work and screen exposure) associated with myopia progression. Furthermore, there is ongoing discussion regarding the appropriate age for introducing VR-based myopia interventions. Although early intervention is generally considered most effective for myopia control, the effects of VR technology on the developing visual system in young children are not yet fully understood, and the potential for screen addiction raises important ethical and clinical concerns. The absence of standardized protocols for VR-based myopia management further complicates efforts to compare study outcomes and establish evidence-based guidelines. Moreover, the use of VR technologies in children and adolescents necessitates careful consideration of ethical issues, as well as regulatory aspects such as VR device approval, informed consent, and safety monitoring. Therefore, further long-term, large-scale, high-quality, and standardized research is critical to evaluate the efficacy of VR technology in myopia management, assess its safety and impact on visual function in children and adolescents, and establish evidence-based guidelines for its safe and effective use. Overall, while VR demonstrates promise in myopia management, further high-quality evidence is required before its widespread implementation can be recommended.

Conclusions

This systematic review investigated the application of VR technology in myopia, synthesizing findings from 16 studies. Of these, seven focused on VR-based interventions for myopia, four on testing and diagnostic applications, three on vision training, and two on educational purposes. None of the previously reported articles reported on the efficacy of VR in preventing or slowing myopia progression, however, this review identified its potential for generating positive outcomes for myopia. It is also important to highlight that the VR adaptability of the methods included in this review is experimental in nature. Preliminary or anecdotal findings should not be interpreted as validated evidence or directly applied to clinical decision-making. Further long-term, standardized research is warranted to evaluate the feasibility, efficacy, and safety of VR-based approaches, and to explore broader applications to myopia management.
