Comparative efficacy and safety of cabazitaxel versus other taxanes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Salah Ameen Abdu, He Caiyun, Wafa Ali Asaad, Muhammad Tufail, Liao Yazhou, Sadam Ahmed Elayah, Rao Zhen, Siying Chen, Mohammad H. Shubair, Ning Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesThis systematic review addresses uncertainty around cabazitaxel's effectiveness, safety, and optimal dosing compared to other taxanes, aiming to guide personalized treatment and improve patient outcomes.MethodsA meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessed the efficacy and safety of cabazitaxel, including tumor response, survival rates, and toxicity profiles. Trials were identified from several databases, and the bias was evaluated by using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Review Manager (RevMan 5.4) was used for heterogeneity testing, sensitivity analyses, pooling hazard ratio (HR) and risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and publication bias assessment.ResultsThis meta-analysis included 10 trials with 3,377 patients. Cabazitaxel demonstrated no significant overall survival (OS) (HR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.92–1.14) or progression-free survival (PFS) (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.92–1.24) benefit over other taxanes but had higher grade 3–5 treatment-related adverse events (TEAEs) (RR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.16–2.42). Tumor responses showed slightly higher partial responses (RR = 1.19, 95% CI 0.97–1.45) and increased progressive disease response (PDR) (RR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.15–2.23), with nonsignificant complete responses and stable disease. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant difference in OS or PFS for prostate and breast cancer. However, in head and neck cancer, cabazitaxel was associated with worse outcomes. 20 mg/m2 dose had a better safety profile, while 25 mg/m2 increased neutropenia (RR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.65–1.96) and anemia (RR = 1.45, 95% CI 1.01–2.09). In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), cabazitaxel appeared more effective than non-taxane comparators in available trials.ConclusionsCabazitaxel shows no OS or PFS advantage over other taxanes and higher toxicity. 20 mg/m2 is safer than 25 mg/m2. It remains an option when other taxanes are contraindicated, not tolerated, or exhausted, with possible benefit over non-taxanes in mCRPC and worse outcomes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Dosing and monitoring should be individualized. Further research should refine patient selection, dosing, and toxicity management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02958-1.

Background

Taxanes, including docetaxel and paclitaxel, are essential in treating cancers such as breast, prostate, and head and neck malignancies [1–4]. Cabazitaxel, a second-generation taxane, was developed to address resistance seen with earlier taxanes, particularly in mCRPC after docetaxel failure, as demonstrated in the TROPIC trial [5, 6]. However, the clinical evidence surrounding cabazitaxel presents significant contradictions and gaps that complicate its therapeutic positioning. While trials such as FIRSTANA and PROSELICA have provided insights into dosing strategies and sequence-dependent effects [7, 8], the overall evidence remains contradictory across clinical contexts. In some settings, higher starting doses extend (OS) despite similar (PFS) and greater hematologic toxicity [9], while real-world cohorts report frequent neutropenia with only modest prostate-specific antigen (PSA) responses, mitigated by prophylactic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) [10]. Furthermore, comparative studies show variable results including inferior PFS and OS in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, no OS benefit in mCRPC, and inconsistent outcomes in breast cancer [7, 11–15]. These clinical positioning challenges are further compounded by the evolving treatment landscape with emerging therapeutic options, complex sequencing considerations including the demonstrated superiority of cabazitaxel over androgen-signaling inhibitor switching, and ongoing debates regarding optimal dosing (25 vs 20 mg/m2) [16, 17]. Additionally, the heterogeneity in cancer type, treatment line, supportive care protocols, endpoint selection, and baseline patient risk factors further complicates clinical interpretation [4, 18]. Consequently, our systematic review and meta-analysis aims to consolidate this disparate evidence, clarify cabazitaxel's relative benefits and risks compared to other taxanes, explore dose-dependent effects across various cancer types and treatment indications, and provide evidence-based recommendations for optimal patient selection, dosing strategies, and adverse event management in contemporary oncological practice.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA and is registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251034842), ensuring rigorous, transparent, and reproducible reporting. Adherence to these standards provided a structured, methodologically robust framework for synthesizing the current literature [19]. In order to obtain a general perspective of the subject, this review includes the most recent available evidence until January 2025-published research studies. By including this extensive research period, we aim to provide an overall and updated perspective of this promising therapeutic option so that all of the information and advances are considered. The full PRISMA checklist is provided in Additional File Table 1.

Criteria for study inclusion and exclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in English, published up to January 17, 2025, involving patients with head and neck cancer, metastatic breast cancer (MBC), or metastatic prostate cancer (mPC). Eligible studies compared cabazitaxel with other taxanes (paclitaxel and docetaxel) or evaluated cabazitaxel at different doses (20 mg/m2 and 25 mg/m2), and reported outcomes such as progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), or adverse events (AEs). Exclusion criteria included non-randomized, reviews, secondary analyses, and studies with incomplete data. Trial registries and studies with ethical approval were prioritized to ensure the inclusion of high-quality evidence. Additional details are provided in Additional File Table 2.

Identification of relevant studies

An experienced researcher (MT) created a comprehensive search strategy to identify published and unpublished research in EMBASE, MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library. The strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free‑text terms, including synonyms for cabazitaxel (Jevtana, XRP6258), other taxanes (docetaxel, paclitaxel, nab‑paclitaxel, brand names), and dosing concepts (20 mg/m2, 25 mg/m2, dose reduction). Full, database‑specific syntaxes with all keywords are provided in Additional File Table 3. SAA and HC independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a detailed full-text review of articles identified as potentially eligible. Disagreements between reviewers were initially resolved through discussion to reach a consensus. For cases where consensus could not be achieved, unresolved disagreements were independently evaluated and arbitrated by a third reviewer (NL). Additionally, bibliographies and citations of included articles were screened to ensure search strategy comprehensiveness and identify any potentially missed studies.

Outcomes assessed

Our review evaluated the effectiveness and safety of cabazitaxel versus other taxanes in head and neck, breast, and prostate cancers. Clinical outcomes included radiological progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) to assess efficacy, Response Rate to measure tumor reduction, and adverse events (AEs) to evaluate safety. Together, these endpoints provide a comprehensive assessment of cabazitaxel’s therapeutic impact, integrating clinical efficacy and patient-centered outcomes.

Data management and extraction

Two independent reviewers (SAA and LY) developed and tested a standardized data extraction form to ensure consistent and accurate data collection. The form included essential details such as the clinical setting, patient demographics, outcome measures (e.g., progression-free survival, overall survival), and adverse events. To minimize errors and improve reliability, two reviewers (SAA and WAA) carried out the data extraction independently. When disagreements occurred during this process, the reviewers discussed the issue to reach a consensus. If they were unable to agree, a third reviewer (NL) was consulted to make the final decision. This careful, multi-step approach ensured the accuracy and reliability of the extracted data, along with maintaining the integrity of the study.

Methodological quality assessment process

The integrity and methodological rigor of the included studies were rigorously assessed by the two independent reviewers (Wafa and Salah) using the Revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool (RoB 2) [20]. This tool evaluated the studies across five predefined domains, with each domain’s risk of bias classified as high [21], unclear (yellow), or low (green). An unweighted kappa statistic was calculated to measure the initial interrater agreement between the two reviewers to ensure consistency in the assessment. In cases where disagreements arose, the reviewers engaged in discussions to reach a consensus. If agreement could not be reached, a third reviewer (NL) was consulted to make the final determination. This systematic approach ensured the reliability and objectivity of the quality assessment process, enhancing the overall validity of the review.

Meta-analysis and heterogeneity assessment

Review Manager (RevMan 5.4) was used to aggregate data from included studies for the meta-analysis. Binary outcomes were expressed as risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), and time-to-event outcomes as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CI. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival were estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis in Patil et al.'s study, accounting for censored data and enabling treatment comparisons. Heterogeneity was examined using the I2 statistic and Chi-square test, with significant heterogeneity defined as I2 > 50% or p ≤ 0.10. We utilized a random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird approach) for high or moderate heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 30%) and a fixed-effects model for low heterogeneity (I2 < 30%). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses investigated heterogeneity sources, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Additionally, publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot analysis.

Results

Study characteristics

A systematic literature search initially identified 6,602 potential articles, with 255 selected for preliminary screening. After carefully evaluating titles and abstracts, 19 articles underwent full-text assessment for eligibility. Ultimately, 10 studies published between 2017 and 2024 met the inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis, while 9 were excluded due to non-randomized design [9, 17, 22, 23], review format [3, 24–26], and secondary data analysis [27]. The complete study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. A complete overview of the studies included in the analysis is presented in Table 1. The final analysis included 3,377 patients with prostate [7, 8, 14, 15, 28], breast [11, 13, 29], and head and neck cancers [12, 30]. Among these, Kummele et al. [13] and Wilmes et al. [29] examined the same patient group, as did Joshi et al. [12] and Patil et al. [30], but with varying follow-up times. Of the included studies, five compared cabazitaxel with docetaxel [12, 14, 15, 28, 30], three compared cabazitaxel with paclitaxel [11, 13, 29], one study evaluated different dosages of cabazitaxel against docetaxel [7], and one compared cabazitaxel 20 mg/m2 and 25 mg/m2 [8]. Bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool evaluated seven domains, indicating a consistently low risk of bias in random sequence generation and allocation concealment across most studies. However, a considerable risk of bias was detected in the blinding of participants and professionals and the blinding of outcome evaluation. Incomplete outcome data and selective reporting exhibited variability, with some studies showing low risk and others demonstrating unclear or high risk. The majority of studies exhibited a low risk of bias in the area of other potential sources. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses across the incorporated randomized controlled trials showed solid and uniform outcomes, as individual study exclusion did not significantly affect the overall risk ratio or related confidence intervals. Furthermore, publication bias was examined, and there was no suggestion of such bias among the studies included in the analysis. Further details regarding the sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessment are provided in the Additional File (Figs. 1 and 2).

Overall survival

A comprehensive meta-analysis synthesizing data from five studies was conducted to evaluate the impact of cabazitaxel compared to other taxanes on patients' median overall survival (OS) [7, 11, 12, 29, 30]. The pooled analysis yielded a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.03 (95% CI 0.92, 1.14; p = 0.54), indicating no statistically significant improvement in OS associated with cabazitaxel relative to other taxanes. Subgroup analyses based on cancer type revealed that cabazitaxel compared to other taxanes in prostate and breast cancer showed an HR of 1.00 (95% CI 0.89, 1.12; p = 0.94), demonstrating no significant difference in OS. Conversely, in HNSCC, the HR for cabazitaxel versus docetaxel was 1.54 (95% CI 1.01, 2.34; p = 0.04), indicating a significant difference favoring docetaxel. None of the comparisons between cabazitaxel doses (C20 versus docetaxel with HR 1.10; 95% CI 0.94, 1.28; p = 0.23, and C25 versus paclitaxel with HR 1.01; 95% CI 0.74, 1.38; p = 0.93) showed significant differences in OS (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Overall survival meta-analysis: cabazitaxel vs other taxanes. Progression-free survival

Five of 10 studies revealed the progression-free survival (PFS) outcomes of cabazitaxel compared to other taxane-based therapies, including docetaxel and paclitaxel [7, 11, 12, 15, 30]. Based on data from published clinical studies, the meta-analysis overall pooled hazard ratio (HR) was 1.07 (95% CI 0.92, 1.24; p = 0.39) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 48%), showing that patients treated with cabazitaxel and those receiving other taxanes did not differ statistically significantly in PFS. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore differences between specific comparisons. The analysis compared cabazitaxel 20 mg/m2 (C20) versus docetaxel and cabazitaxel 25 mg/m2 (C25) versus docetaxel. For C20 versus docetaxel, the hazard ratio was 1.26 (95% CI 0.97, 1.63; p = 0.14), showing no significant difference but with notable heterogeneity (I2 = 66%). Similarly, C25 versus docetaxel showed no statistically significant difference in PFS (HR 0.99; 95% CI 0.85, 1.15; p = 0.87) with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Tumor response

Cabazitaxel's efficacy in tumor response was evaluated through meta-analyses focusing on partial response (PR), complete response, stable disease (SD), and progression disease response (PDR) [7, 8, 11, 12]. For PR, cabazitaxel showed a modest but non-significant trend over other taxanes (RR = 1.19, 95% CI 0.97–1.45) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 25%), and no significant difference between 25 mg/m2 (C25) and 20 mg/m2 (C20) doses (RR = 1.17, 95% CI 0.92–1.48; I2 = 0%). In addition, in CR, cabazitaxel demonstrated no significant advantage over other taxanes (RR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.19–3.14; I2 = 0%) or between doses (RR = 2.33, 95% CI 0.34–15.75; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 5). However, in PDR, cabazitaxel was associated with a statistically significant increase compared to other taxanes (RR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.15–2.23; I2 = 0%), and the C25 dose showed higher PDR than C20 (RR = 1.59, 95% CI 1.02–2.48; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 6).Fig. 5Tumor response meta-analysis: cabazitaxel vs taxanes and dose comparison. Treatment-emergent adverse events

Adverse events grade (3–5)

A series of meta-analyses examined the risk of severe (grade 3–5) treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) associated with cabazitaxel. The primary analysis compared cabazitaxel to other taxanes, revealing a significantly higher risk of severe TEAEs with cabazitaxel, as indicated by a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 1.68 (95% CI 1.16, 2.42). However, substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 91%) was observed, prompting further investigation through subgroup analyses to identify potential sources of variation [7, 11–15]. Subgroup analyses, stratified by prior taxane exposure (docetaxel and paclitaxel), revealed consistently elevated relative risks (RRs) of severe treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) with cabazitaxel C25 (RRs 1.39 and 2.45, respectively). Despite these findings, significant heterogeneity persisted within these subgroups. In contrast, comparing cabazitaxel C20 versus docetaxel yielded a non-significant result, with a risk ratio of 0.91. Additionally, a subgroup analysis focusing on hematological adverse events revealed a similar pattern of increased risk with cabazitaxel. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive evaluation of cabazitaxel's efficacy and safety profile compared to other taxanes across different cancer types and treatment settings. By analyzing data from multiple randomized controlled trials, we assessed various outcomes, including overall survival, progression-free survival, tumor response rates, and treatment-emergent adverse events. Our findings reveal a complex interplay between therapeutic benefits and toxicity concerns, with notable variations across different dosing regimens and cancer types. The results contribute valuable insights to the ongoing discourse regarding optimal taxane selection and dosing strategies in contemporary oncology practice. Through systematic synthesis of available evidence, we identified several key patterns that merit detailed discussion and have important implications for clinical decision-making.

Survival outcomes: OS, PFS

Recent meta-analyses and systematic review indicate that cabazitaxel demonstrates comparable overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) benefits to other taxanes, with no significant differences observed in direct comparisons (OS: HR = 1.04, 95% CI 0.93–1.16, p = 0.49; PFS: HR = 1.05, 95% CI 0.89–1.24, p = 0.54). These findings are consistent with Song et al.’s (2020) network meta-analysis, which reported similar OS and PFS benefits between cabazitaxel plus prednisone (CP) and docetaxel plus prednisone (DP) (OS for CP: HR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.55–0.92; DP: HR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.61–0.92; PFS for CP: HR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.95; DP: HR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.56–1.0) [4]. However, these results contrast with Hashim et al.'s (2018) systematic review and meta-analysis, which found significant OS and PFS benefits for cabazitaxel compared to non-taxanes like mitoxantrone in mCRPC patients (OS: HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.70–0.88; PFS: HR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.61–0.97) [26]. In addition, Silva et al. (2025) systematic review supported cabazitaxel’s efficacy, reporting a longer PFS compared to abiraterone or enzalutamide (HR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.47–0.78, p < 0.001), though they found no significant difference in OS (HR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.46–1.24, p = 0.27) [21]. Notably, our subgroup analyses revealed important distinctions across cancer types: while cabazitaxel showed comparable efficacy to other taxanes in prostate cancer (OS: HR = 1.00, 95% CI 0.89–1.12, p = 0.94; PFS: HR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.92–1.14, p = 0.67), it demonstrated significantly worse outcomes in head and neck cancer (OS: HR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.01–2.34, p = 0.04; PFS: HR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.13–1.97, p = 0.005). These findings align with Jean-Pascal et al.'s conclusion of no clinically meaningful activity in head and neck cancer and Fayette et al.'s observation of poor survival outcomes (short PFS and OS) and poor tolerance with cabazitaxel in head and neck cancer [31, 32]. These discrepancies suggest that cabazitaxel's efficacy may be more pronounced when compared to non-taxanes (e.g., mitoxantrone, abiraterone, or enzalutamide) rather than other taxanes (e.g., docetaxel), and its effectiveness varies significantly by cancer type.

The lack of OS and PFS benefits may result from structural resistance mechanisms. While cabazitaxel's reduced binding to P-glycoproteins shows preclinical promise in resistant tumors, it does not consistently translate into significant survival improvements. Treatment-resistant tumor biology and patient selection likely explain the limited OS gains in this meta-analysis [24, 33–36].

Tumor response

Our study revealed mixed tumor response patterns for cabazitaxel. Partial response (PR) rates were slightly higher compared to other taxanes (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.97–1.45, p = 0.10), as evidenced by the FIRSTANA trial and Bahl et al.’s studies [7, 11]. In contrast, complete response [21] rates were consistently low, with a relative risk (RR) of 0.78 (95% CI 0.19–3.14). This finding aligns with the nonrandomized study by Koutras et al., which reported a CR rate of only 3.8% in patients treated with cabazitaxel [37]. Stable disease (SD) rates were comparable to other taxanes, consistent with Song et al.’s network meta-analysis, which found comparable clinical benefits between cabazitaxel and docetaxel [4]. Additionally, higher doses of cabazitaxel (C25) did not demonstrate significant improvements in PR or CR rates (PR: RR 1.17, p = 0.19; CR: RR 2.33, p = 0.39), suggesting that dose escalation may not effectively overcome disease resistance. These findings underscore the need for further research to optimize cabazitaxel's therapeutic potential.

Treatment-emergent adverse events

A systematic analysis of six clinical trials found that cabazitaxel significantly increased the risk of grade 3–5 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) compared to docetaxel (RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.10–2.29; p = 0.01) and paclitaxel (RR 2.45, 95% CI 0.98–6.14; p = 0.06). Cabazitaxel’s toxicity profile is marked by hematological events (RR 3.51, 95% CI 1.79–6.89; p = 0.0003), including higher rates of grade 3–5 anemia (RR 1.45, 95% CI 1.01–2.09; P = 0.04), neutropenia (RR 1.60, 95% CI 0.80–3.20; p = 0.19), and thrombocytopenia (RR 1.47, 95% CI 0.76–2.83; p = 0.25). These findings are supported by prior studies, such as Hashim et al. [26], who reported a 1.64-fold increased risk of serious adverse events, and Kreis et al. [23], who noted hematological toxicity in 61% of cabazitaxel-treated patients versus 31% with docetaxel.

Importantly, the safety profile of cabazitaxel varies significantly between the 20 mg/m2 (C20) and 25 mg/m2 (C25) doses. C20 demonstrates a more favorable safety profile, with lower rates of grade 3–5 TEAEs (RR: 1.41; 95% CI 1.28–1.55) and reduced hematological toxicities, including anemia (RR: 1.37; 95% CI 1.05–1.80), leukopenia (RR: 2.15; 95% CI 1.91–2.43), and thrombocytopenia (RR: 1.70; 95% CI 1.00–2.89). Studies by Terada, Matsuyama, and Rouyer further support this dose-dependent toxicity, with C25 linked to higher rates of neutropenia, febrile neutropenia, and anemia [9, 17, 38]. In summary, the observed hematological toxicity patterns highlight the necessity of careful monitoring, precise dose adjustments, and proactive use of G-CSF prophylaxis to effectively manage cabazitaxel-related toxicity and minimize risks [35, 39].

Study limitations and clinical considerations

Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity existed across trials. The observed heterogeneity in response patterns can be attributed to several key factors. First, the inclusion of different cancer types, such as head and neck [11, 13, 29], prostate [7, 8, 14, 15, 28], and breast cancer [11, 13, 29], each with distinct biological behaviors and treatment responses, contributed to variability. Patient demographics, disease stage, and prior therapy also influenced outcomes. Response rates differed between treatment‑naïve and previously treated cohorts. Dosing regimens varied (C20 vs C25), and comparison taxanes differed (docetaxel vs paclitaxel), further complicating responses. Individual patient data were unavailable, limiting robust adjustment and subgroup analyses. Reporting of subsequent therapies was uneven, potentially confounding overall survival. Dose‑ and tumor‑specific effects were underpowered. These differences reduced precision, hindered comparability of pooled estimates, and limited generalizability. This interplay underscores the need to consider tumor type, stage, and treatment history when interpreting responses and making decisions. Clinically, we favor risk‑adapted dosing and proactive myelosuppression prevention. Expectations for survival gains should be tempered, with judicious, patient‑selected use where alternatives are limited.

Further research direction

Further Dose‑optimization RCTs comparing 20 vs 25 mg/m2 should adopt standardized common terminology criteria for adverse events (CTCAE) reporting, explicit supportive‑care protocols (including primary G‑CSF), and prespecified patient‑reported outcomes. Pragmatic, real‑world comparative‑effectiveness studies in older and comorbid populations are needed to assess deliverability, toxicity, and quality of life. Trials should report subsequent therapies in detail to enable fair interpretation of overall survival. Economic evaluations that integrate survival, toxicity, and supportive‑care costs are warranted.

Conclusion

This review evaluates cabazitaxel’s efficacy and safety compared to other taxanes. Cabazitaxel demonstrates no significant overall survival or progression free survival benefits over other taxanes (OS: HR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.92–1.14; PFS: HR = 1.07, 95% CI 0.92–1.24), though it may be more effective than non-taxanes in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer and less effective in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Tumor response rates show slightly higher partial responses but low complete responses. The 20 mg/m2 dose offers a better safety profile than 25 mg/m2, though cabazitaxel’s toxicity remains higher than that of other taxanes, requiring careful monitoring. Clinically, cabazitaxel should not be selected with the expectation of superior survival versus other taxanes, but it is a viable option when a taxane is indicated and other taxanes are contraindicated, not tolerated, or exhausted, with choice guided by cancer type, disease stage, and prior treatments. Further research is needed to refine patient selection, dosing, and toxicity management.
