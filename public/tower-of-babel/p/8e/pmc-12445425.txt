Surgical Management of Foetal Maceration in a Domestic Shorthair Queen
Authors: Md. Rubayatul Islam, Rukaiya Mostasin, Md. Sakib Ibna Obaid, Mohammad Raguib Munif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFoetal maceration is a pregnancy pathology occasionally observed in cats following intrauterine foetal death, characterised by septic decomposition of foetal tissues, resulting in the exposure of foetal bones. This report describes the clinical management of foetal maceration in a cat and scrutinizes the findings with relevant literature to inform practice. A domestic shorthair queen, over 1 year of age and in its third parity, was presented with prolonged gestation, abdominal distension, fetid vaginal discharge, anorexia and general weakness, accompanied by a history of abortion during its first pregnancy. Prior to presentation, the cat had been treated with oxytocin, although it failed to induce parturition. At presentation, no considerable changes were observed in the cat's primary clinical and haematobiochemical parameters. Clinical examinations indicated mild dehydration and palpable firm structures with associated pain in the abdominopelvic region. Abdominal radiography and ultrasonography confirmed the presence of foetal structures, while ultrasonography also indicated the absence of foetal movements and heartbeats. Laparotomy revealed gravid uterine horns devoid of foetal pulsations, and further ovariohysterectomy was conducted to address the condition. Upon incision of the excised uterine horns, two macerated foetuses, in an advanced stage of putrefaction, were identified. The queen recovered uneventfully following postoperative medications and management, with complete wound healing observed 3 weeks post‐surgery. In brief, foetal maceration may result in prolonged gestation in cats and surgical intervention, such as ovariohysterectomy, can be an effective treatment option, particularly when breeding is not a priority, thereby preventing further uterine conditions.

Introduction

Complications in pregnant cats can arise at any stage of gestation, potentially leading to embryonic or foetal death, with subsequent pregnancy loss manifesting as foetal resorption, abortion, stillbirth, maceration or mummification (Johnston and Raksil 1987; Givens and Marley 2008). In small animals (dogs and cats), dead foetuses may either be expelled or retained within the uterus (Lefebvre 2015; Erdoğan and Akkuş 2019). Retention of an intrauterine dead foetus may result in clinical conditions (i.e., foetal maceration or mummification) if left untreated (Bozkurt et al. 2018; Alcantara et al. 2021; Spruijt et al. 2022).

Foetal maceration or mummification occurs only when foetal death takes place after the formation of foetal bones, as death prior to the development of bony structures leads to rapid tissue autolysis and subsequent resorption within the uterus (Lefebvre 2015). Foetal mummification refers to the sterile dehydration of foetal tissues within the uterus, resulting from the resorption of foetal fluids, which leads to shrivelled foetal membranes; while the uterus contracts around the foetus, moulding it into a dried and contorted mass (Lefebvre 2015; Noakes et al. 2019). Mummification occurs with a closed cervix in the absence of oxygen, thereby preventing contamination by putrefactive organisms (bacteria). In contrast, foetal maceration is characterised by the septic decomposition of foetal tissues within the uterus, facilitated by an open cervix that permits the entry of oxygen and putrefactive organisms; this condition leads to the disintegration and lysis of foetal soft tissues, leaving only the foetal bones (Tunç and Çelik 2021).

Foetal maceration remains one of the least documented reproductive pathologies in cats (Alcantara et al. 2021; Suresh et al. 2023) and can lead to potential complications, such as metritis and pyometra. This condition arises after intrauterine foetal death due to a variety of factors, including infectious agents, trauma, exposure to certain toxins or drugs, metabolic disturbances and genetic or hormonal abnormalities and is commonly observed in the later stages of gestation (Johnston and Raksil 1987; Lamm and Njaa 2012). Foetal maceration represents a clinical emergency that must be addressed promptly to ensure a favourable prognosis. This condition results in foetal loss and can significantly impact future fertility; the longer the condition remains untreated, the greater the damage to the endometrium, leading to poorer outcomes (Noakes et al. 2019; Devi et al. 2020). Common clinical signs include thickened painful abdominal loops on palpation, weakness, loss of appetite and foul‐smelling vaginal discharge; although in some cases, the discharge may be absent (Bozkurt et al. 2018; Tunç and Çelik 2021; Suresh et al. 2023). Diagnostic imaging remains crucial in aiding the clinical diagnosis of foetal maceration (Alcantara et al. 2021; Saini et al. 2024; Opaluwa‐Kuzayed et al. 2024).

Management of foetal maceration comprises medical and surgical interventions, depending on the severity of the condition. Medical treatment options include the use of prostaglandins E1 and F2α, synthetic steroids and supportive antibiotics (Noakes et al. 2019; Schreiber et al. 2018; Nevard et al. 2022). Transcervical removal of foetal parts typically succeeds these medical approaches. In cases where medical treatment proves inadequate, surgical interventions, such as hysterotomy, hysterectomy or ovariohysterectomy are indicated (Bozkurt et al. 2018; Alcantara et al. 2021; Opaluwa‐Kuzayed et al. 2024).

The present report details a case of foetal maceration in a queen with prolonged gestation, a less common presentation in feline practice. Although a limited number of similar cases have been documented previously (Alcantara et al. 2021; Suresh et al. 2023), long‐term postoperative accounts remain scarce. The current report provides a comprehensive overview of the queen's clinical events, including physical examinations, imaging findings, surgical management and postoperative follow‐up. By contextualising this case within existing literature, the authors highlight distinct features, particularly a prolonged subclinical course (no overt systemic illness) despite marked uterine pathology in the queen. This case underscores the importance of timely diagnosis of post‐term complications and contributes practical, evidence‐based insights into the clinical management of foetal maceration in cats.

Case Presentation

Case History

A 14‐month‐old female indigenous domestic shorthair cat, weighing 3.5 kg with a body condition score of 3/5, was presented to a local veterinary clinic with the complaint of prolonged gestation lasting 90 days. The anamnesis revealed a week‐long hyporexia progressing to anorexia, general weakness, periodic mild screaming and genital discharge. The queen had a history of abortion during its first pregnancy; however, its second pregnancy proceeded normally, yielding two live kittens. At presentation, the cat was in its third parity and demonstrated a distended abdomen without overt straining, along with lethargy and foul‐smelling light‐brown vaginal discharge (Figure 1). Previously, the cat had been treated with oxytocin; nevertheless, this was not effective for inducing parturition. The cat showed no signs of other diseases or abnormalities and was up to date on vaccinations and deworming. The ancestral records were unavailable as the cat was an adopted animal. Additionally, the mating records were missing; however, the owner presumed natural mating with a neighbouring tomcat.

Clinical Investigations

Physical examination, including a skin fold test on the neck region, indicated mild dehydration (less than 5% fluid loss). The mucous membranes appeared light pink in colour, with a capillary refill time of approximately two seconds in the gingival areas. Vaginal speculum examination revealed no additional local abnormalities, except for the fetid vaginal discharge emanating from a partially dilated cervix. Upon deep palpation, firm tubulonodular structures were identified within the caudoventral abdominal cavity, extending toward the pelvic inlet, along with corresponding pain response. Abdominal radiography detected foetal bony structures (Figure 2), and this observation was further supported by ultrasonography (Figure 3). Additionally, the sonographic examination revealed foetal non‐viability, as evidenced by the absence of foetal movements and detectable heartbeats. Based on the history, physical examinations and imaging findings, a presumptive diagnosis of intrauterine foetal death in post‐term pregnancy was established. Transcervical foetal extraction was considered but remained unsuccessful, necessitating surgical intervention.

Surgical Intervention

The cat initially received fluid resuscitation with an intravenous (IV) bolus of 5% dextrose in normal saline (0.9% NaCl) at 15 mL/kg body weight, administered over 16 min. With the owner's informed consent, a decision was made to proceed with ovariohysterectomy. Meanwhile, the cat had been off feed and water for over eight hours; therefore, general anaesthesia (GA) was performed. Before anaesthetic induction, the cat was premedicated with intramuscular (IM) atropine sulphate (Tropin Vet, ACME Laboratories Ltd.) at 0.05 mg/kg, followed by sedation with IM xylazine hydrochloride (HCl) (Xylaxin, Indian Immunologicals Ltd.) at 1.1 mg/kg. Approximately 10 min post‐sedation, ketamine HCl (Ketalar, Popular Pharmaceuticals Ltd.) at 17 mg/kg was administered IM for the induction of GA. Under GA, the cat was positioned in right lateral recumbency with the left flank upward. The surgical site was aseptically prepared by trimming the superficial hair, then cleansing with soapy water and 70% ethanol and finally scrubbing with 2% chlorhexidine gluconate. Then, a vertical skin incision was made in the left lateral flank, allowing for the dissection of subcutaneous tissues, abdominal muscles and peritoneum to access the peritoneal cavity and approach the ipsilateral (left) and contralateral (right) uterine horns. Haemorrhage was meticulously controlled. Intraoperative fluid therapy was maintained with IV Hartmann's solution at 4 mL/kg/hour (Hartsol, Libra Infusions Ltd.). The left and right uterine horns (Figure 4A), along with the ovaries, were exteriorised using Senn‐Miller retractors (blunt). Initially, the left ovarian suspensory ligament was manually ruptured and an opening was created in the mesovarium to facilitate ligation. The ovarian vascular pedicle was ligated with 2‐0 polyglactin 910 (Unisynth, Unisur Lifecare Pvt. Ltd.). Mosquito haemostats were used to hold the ovarian stump during ligation and detachment from the uterine horn. The severed ovarian stump was inspected for haemorrhage and then returned to the peritoneal cavity. The same procedure was repeated for the right uterine horn and ovary. No supplementary anaesthetic doses or maintenance with assisted ventilation were required; the queen sustained respiration while breathing room air. The gravid uterine horns (left and right) were found to have no foetal pulsations and were gently elevated to expose the uterine body. Next, the uterine body was grasped with non‐crushing forceps to prevent rupture and haemorrhage, while collateral uterine blood vessels were ligated with 3‐0 Unisynth. Transfixing ligatures with 2‐0 Unisynth were placed on the uterine body cranial to the cervix. The uterine horns, along with a small portion of the uterine body, were transected and removed. The uterine stump was carefully inspected for haemorrhage and then returned to the peritoneal cavity. Peritoneal lavage was done using lukewarm normal saline. Then, the laparotomy incision was routinely closed, which involved suturing the peritoneum and abdominal muscles with a single layer of Ford interlocking suture (Figure 4B) and subcutaneous fatty tissue and fascia with a simple continuous suture, using 2‐0 Unisynth. The skin edges were approximated with a subcuticular suture followed by horizontal mattress sutures (Figure 4C), using the same suture material. Finally, 10% povidone‐iodine (Povisep 10% solution, Jayson Pharmaceuticals Ltd.) was applied with sterile gauzes topically to prevent local contamination. The excised gravid uterine horns were longitudinally incised, revealing a macerated foetus within each horn (Figure 5A–C). The foetuses were decomposed, exhibiting advanced putrefaction, characterised by soft‐tissue lysis and exposure of the bones. Thus, a diagnosis of foetal maceration was confirmed and the gestational age at the time of foetal death was estimated to be between 70 and 75 days (Figure 6). Hemodynamic monitoring was not feasible intraoperatively due to constraints in the surgical facility.

Postoperative Care and Follow‐Up

Immediately after surgery, the queen was again resuscitated with IV 35 mL of 5% dextrose in normal saline, administered over 15 min. A 14‐day Elizabethan collar was recommended. The postoperative medication regimen comprised ceftriaxone sodium (Acicef‐3 Vet, Advanced Chemical Industries Ltd.) IM at 35 mg/kg twice a day for seven consecutive days, meloxicam (Mel‐Vet, ACME Laboratories Ltd.) subcutaneously at 0.2 mg/kg once a day for four days, pheniramine maleate (Asta‐Vet, ACME Laboratories Ltd.) IM at 1 mg/kg once a day for seven days, domperidone maleate (Vave, Advanced Laboratories Ltd.) orally at 1 mg/kg twice a day for five days and lactulose [Avolac Oral Solution (lactulose USP 3.35 g/5 mL), Aristopharma Ltd.] orally 1.5 mL/cat once a day for five days. Topical ointment 2% mupirocin (Bactrocin 2% Ointment, Square Pharmaceuticals Ltd.) was used twice a day for 14 days. After the second postoperative day, ascorbic acid (vitamin C) (Vasco Syrup, Opsonin Pharma Ltd.) orally at 200 mg/cat once a day for the next 2 weeks was maintained. In addition, frequent movements and jumping were restricted for 4 weeks after surgery. On the 12th postoperative day, the skin sutures were removed and the cat was in good condition. A brief clinical check‐up was conducted 22 days post‐surgery, confirming proper healing at the surgical site (Figure 7A,B). No additional diagnostic tests were performed due to the owner's constraints and the cat was subsequently discharged. Weekly follow‐up consultations were conducted via mobile phone for 15 weeks post‐surgery, with no complications reported by the owner.

Discussion

Early diagnosis of foetal death and immediate removal of retained foetal remnants are crucial for safeguarding reproductive health and future fertility in cats. When foetal remnants are identified, prompt and decisive actions are necessary to minimise the risks of prolonged inflammation, infection, irreparable damage to the reproductive tract and systemic complications (Alcantara et al. 2021; Burns and Card 2000; Bodh et al. 2014). A retained dead foetus can potentially undergo maceration under septic conditions (Suresh et al. 2023; Heil et al. 2023; Dutt et al. 2018). The present report introduces a case of foetal maceration in an indigenous shorthair queen aged over 1 year, emphasising its clinical diagnosis and management. Given the limited available literature, the findings could be valuable for gynaecological or obstetrical practices in cats, particularly concerning the pregnancy‐related complications arising from foetal maceration.

Foetal maceration is a sequel of intrauterine foetal death, resulting from failure of foetal expulsion or dystocia (Černá et al. 2024). The gestation period in cats lasts approximately 65 days and usually concludes with three distinct stages of labour (Sparkes et al. 2006; Romagnoli et al. 2019). Dystocia occurs when the normal progression of labour is disrupted, arising from either maternal or foetal factors (Jutkowitz 2005; Bailin et al. 2022). Trauma and stress during late pregnancy may also contribute to foetal death (Linde‐Forsberg 2010). In the present case, however, overt signs of trauma, stress, dystocia, or labour were absent, as reported by the owner and no infections or diseases were evident upon presentation. Therefore, the exact aetiology of foetal death remains undetermined. A hormonal screening would have been valuable; however, it was not done due to the owner's constraints. Hormonal abnormalities can potentially contribute to pregnancy complications and foetal death (Jonker 2004; Vannuccini et al. 2016; Uchańska et al. 2022). Although the queen's genetic background (pedigree) is unknown, it is reasonable to consider that sex‐linked genes and genetic aberrations (e.g., chromosomal aneuploidies) may play a vital role in intrauterine foetal death (Lefebvre 2015; Dolanc Merc et al. 2023). In the present case, the queen's breeding history, demonstrating abortion at first parity, normal delivery at second parity and foetal death with maceration at third parity (Figure 6), might underscore the involvement of underlying genetic factors adversely affecting its reproductive performance. This observation is further supported by the owner's information, which indicates that the neighbouring tomcat, presumed to have mated with the queen, maintains a healthy breeding record.

The clinical signs observed in the present case align with those reported in related studies (Alcantara et al. 2021; Opaluwa‐Kuzayed et al. 2024; Heil et al. 2023). Based on the history, clinical observations and surgical findings, the foetuses were considered to have reached full maturity for delivery (full‐term); however, the queen's inability to give birth resulted in foetal retention and prolonged gestation. Although dystocia was not evident and no clear signs of labour were observed between 60 and 70 days of gestation as per the owner's account, subtle signs of straining may have been overlooked. This minor straining could have led to partial cervical dilation, even though it did not progress to definite labour. The partially dilated cervix might have facilitated ascending infections, resulting in foetal death and subsequent decomposition of the placenta and foetal soft tissues. This phenomenon likely explains the fetid, purulent vaginal discharge in the queen.

The abdominal radiography and ultrasonography complemented the clinical findings, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of intrauterine foetal death and enabling the decision to proceed with surgical management. Surgical intervention further revealed the condition, confirming the diagnosis of foetal maceration. The macerated foetuses incurred some structural damage during manual extraction from the uterine horns, owing to their extreme fragility and devitalised state. Hence, the externalised foetal components presented a mild compromise in structural integrity and detail compared to those observed in the radiographic and ultrasonographic images. The use of radiography and ultrasonography, in this context, is substantiated by relevant investigations (Erdoğan and Akkuş 2019; Bozkurt et al. 2018; Suresh et al. 2023; Sagar et al. 2017). Additionally, the foetal Doppler heartbeat monitor demonstrated findings consistent with those observed in radiography and ultrasonography, suggesting its efficacy in facilitating the diagnosis, despite being a superficial monitoring tool. Uterine cytology was not performed as ovariohysterectomy was selected. Swab culture was also omitted, given no significant evidence of infection or inflammation in the haematobiochemical findings, as well as to reduce the overall cost of treatment.

According to the owner's information, the queen had been administered oxytocin at approximately 70 days of gestation by a local veterinary staff; however, the treatment was ineffective in inducing foetal expulsion, likely due to cervical and uterine unresponsiveness to the therapy. Literature indicates that use of uterotonic agents (e.g., oxytocin) to expel foetus (es) may be unsuccessful if the cervix remains indurated (not properly dilated), the uterus fails to contract or respond to treatment, or foetal remnants are embedded within the uterus (Sagar et al. 2017). Moreover, such attempts can potentially exacerbate post‐term complications, leading to uterine rupture and ectopic foetal maceration (Sagar et al. 2017). Taken together, the authors emphasize that the use of uterotonics in such cases should be tailored to specific indications, based on a thorough assessment of the condition of the uterus, cervix, reproductive canal, pelvis and foetus (es), thereby ensuring safe and effective application while minimising the risks of adverse effects.

The anaesthetic protocol and surgical procedure (ovariohysterectomy) employed in the present case are correlated with those reported in other investigations (Alcantara et al. 2021; Munif et al. 2022; Munif and Safawat 2024; Munif et al. 2024). A marginally higher dose of atropine (0.05 mg/kg) was used for premedication without prior heart rate measurement based on the clinician's experience, with no observed adverse effects. Ideally, atropine administration should be guided by heart rate monitoring, with the standard dosage ranging from 0.02 to 0.04 mg/kg (Robertson et al. 2018). Although intraoperative hemodynamic parameters (mean arterial blood pressure, cardiac output, heart rate and oxygenation) were not evaluated, this remains crucial in such interventions to evaluate cardiovascular stability and tissue perfusion, guiding anaesthetic and fluid management (Waddell 2023). Caesarean section or hysterotomy may be considered in cases of foetal maceration where the uterus demonstrates relatively less structural compromise and the likelihood of uterine involution is high, preserving the options for future fertility. Given the queen's eventful breeding history and the presenting condition, a poor reproductive prognosis was established, leading the owner to consent to an ovariohysterectomy. Despite a delay in presentation and diagnosis, no major systemic complications were observed. This may be attributed to the queen's overall health and the pathology being confined exclusively to the reproductive tract. Prompt surgical management is crucial in such cases; otherwise, there is a high risk of toxaemia or septicaemia, which may result in death.

During the postoperative phase, the queen received antibiotic (ceftriaxone), anti‐inflammatory (meloxicam) and antihistamine (pheniramine) medications to prevent secondary infections, inflammation and tissue reactions. The application of antihistamine was an extra‐label use, justified by its documented anti‐inflammatory and mild sedative properties (Hill and Baker 2004; Church and Church 2011), which appeared to confer clinical benefit in the present case. Additionally, a peristaltic stimulant and antiemetic agent (domperidone) and a laxative (lactulose) were administered to alleviate constipation. A topical ointment (mupirocin) was prescribed to prevent local contamination, while vitamin C supplementation was provided to promote wound healing. Perioperative fluid therapy was implemented to ensure fluid replenishment. A head collar was recommended to restrict the queen from licking or biting the surgical site and locomotion restrictions were advised to reduce the risk of wound dehiscence. The owner also ensured the queen's nails were trimmed to prevent self‐inflicted injuries (i.e., scratching the surgical site). Approximately 3 weeks postoperatively, the queen demonstrated significant recovery, with no further complications reported three and a half months post‐surgery. These outcomes indicate the efficacy of the surgical procedure and postoperative management employed in addressing foetal maceration in the queen.

Conclusion

The present report outlines a case of foetal maceration in a queen with prolonged gestation and suboptimal breeding records, suggesting that the queen's underlying genetic or hormonal abnormalities may have contributed to this condition. The diagnosis was primarily established through radiographic and ultrasonographic assessments, supplemented by clinical examinations and history. The management strategy involved ovariohysterectomy, followed by postoperative care and medication, resulting in an uneventful recovery. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the genetic and hormonal factors implicated in foetal death and maceration during late pregnancy in queens.
