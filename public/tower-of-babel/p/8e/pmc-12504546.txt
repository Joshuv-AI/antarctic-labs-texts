Hybrid beluga whale optimization based MPPT for photovoltaic powered open end winding induction motor drives
Authors: Balakrishna Kothapalli, G T Sundar Rajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a novel Hybrid Beluga Whale Optimization (HBWO) algorithm enhanced with multi-strategy mechanisms for Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) in photovoltaic (PV) systems integrated with an Open-End Winding Induction Motor (OEWIM) drive. The HBWO algorithm is developed to ensure fast and accurate maximum power extraction under dynamic irradiance, partial shading, and varying load conditions. The motor drive is governed by an improved Direct Torque Control (DTC) scheme featuring a five-level torque hysteresis controller and an optimized switching table, resulting in reduced torque ripple and enhanced system stability. Simulation-based performance analysis, conducted in MATLAB/SIMULINK, benchmarks the proposed method against traditional MPPT algorithms including Perturb & Observe (P&O), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Chimp Optimization Algorithm (COA), and Giza Pyramid Construction (GPC). Quantitative results demonstrate that HBWO achieves a DC link voltage rise time of 0.11 s, overshoot of 0.382%, settling time of 0.17 s, steady-state ripple of 0.50%, and steady-state error of only 0.04%. In terms of motor speed control, the system delivers a rise time of 0.1 s, overshoot of 0.37%, settling time of 0.18 s, and steady-state error of 0.03%. Furthermore, the torque ripple is minimized to just 0.5%, significantly outperforming conventional algorithms such as P&O (5.6%) and COA (2.2%). These improvements confirm the HBWO algorithm’s superiority in terms of convergence speed, control accuracy, and system robustness. The integration of advanced hybrid optimization with refined motor control architecture offers a comprehensive and efficient solution for renewable energy systems. This work contributes to the development of intelligent PV-driven motor systems capable of maintaining high performance and stability under real-world, variable conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15680-8.Keywords: HBWO, MPPT, OEWIM, DTC, Torque ripple reduction, Five-level torque hysteresis controller, PV system optimizationSubject terms: Engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The growing global focus on sustainable energy has led to rapid advancements in renewable energy technologies, particularly in photovoltaic (PV) systems. As a clean and abundant source of energy, PV systems have gained significant popularity for generating electricity without harmful emissions1. However, their performance is highly influenced by environmental factors like solar irradiance and temperature, which cause fluctuations in power output2. To ensure efficient energy conversion, PV systems must operate at their Maximum Power Point (MPP), which changes dynamically with environmental conditions3. This makes Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) techniques essential for maximizing energy extraction.

Among various MPPT methods, Perturb and Observe (P&O) is widely used due to its simple structure and easy implementation4. Despite its popularity, P&O suffers from limitations such as slow convergence, steady-state oscillations near the MPP, and poor performance under rapidly changing conditions. To address these issues, various bio-inspired algorithms have been introduced. Techniques like the Chimp Optimization Algorithm (COA)5 and Giza Pyramid Construction (GPC)6 have shown improved tracking capabilities. However, they often require further refinement for better stability and responsiveness.

Recent research continues to enhance traditional MPPT algorithms. In7, the importance of MPPT in improving PV performance under variable conditions was emphasized. In8, an adaptive-step P&O method was proposed to reduce steady-state oscillations, but it still showed sluggish tracking during sudden irradiance changes. The Incremental Conductance (IC) method, discussed in9, offers more accurate tracking by analyzing the power-voltage curve’s slope. Yet, its increased computational load can be a drawback for low-cost PV systems. An improved version of the IC algorithm with dynamic step sizing was presented in10,11 improving both tracking speed and accuracy. In12, fuzzy logic control (FLC) was applied for MPPT due to its strength in managing nonlinearities and uncertainties. It showed improved performance under partial shading, but its effectiveness heavily depends on the quality of its rule base and membership functions, making it complex to design. In13, an Artificial Neural Network (ANN)-based MPPT method was proposed, which predicts the MPP using trained models. Although it performed well under dynamic conditions, it requires significant training data and computational resources.

A Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)-based MPPT approach was implemented in14, leveraging the collective behaviour of bird. In15, an intelligent Fuzzy PSO (FPSO)-based MPPT technique was experimentally validated, combined with a ripple-compensated modified SVPWM inverter. In16, a single-stage off-grid PV system was designed using a buck converter and an enhanced P&O MPPT algorithm, along with a three-stage battery charging controller. Similarly,17 presented a hybrid ANN and Ant Colony Optimization (ANN-ACO) MPPT controller integrated with a three-phase shunt active power filter used in arc welding systems.

In18, a Genetic Algorithm (GA) was used for MPPT, leveraging evolutionary principles to optimize power extraction. To improve tracking speed,19 proposed the Chimp Optimization Algorithm (COA), which showed high accuracy and fast convergence under rapidly changing conditions. The Giza Pyramid Construction (GPC) algorithm was introduced in20, inspired by ancient engineering techniques. It demonstrated strong global MPP tracking in partial shading. In21, the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA), based on whales’ bubble-net hunting, provided efficient tracking in both shading and fluctuating irradiance but occasionally faced premature convergence in complex scenarios. A hybrid PSO-WOA method was proposed in22, combining the advantages of both algorithms.

On the motor control side, Direct Torque Control (DTC) is widely used for induction motors due to its fast response and minimal reliance on motor parameters. The classical DTC approach, discussed in23, directly controls torque and flux using a switching table but suffers from high torque and flux ripples. In24, a modified DTC with a multi-level inverter was introduced to reduce ripple and improve precision, although it added to the system’s complexity. Further refinements using optimized switching tables were presented in25, enhancing torque control while maintaining DTC’s fast response. In26, a five-level torque hysteresis controller significantly reduced ripple, ensuring smoother operation during transients.

In27, DTC was combined with Space Vector Modulation (SVM) to replace the conventional switching table. Further refinement was achieved in28, where adaptive hysteresis band control was integrated into SVM-DTC, resulting in better performance across varying load conditions. In29, a Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based PI controller was used to improve DTC performance for Doubly-Fed Induction Motors (DFIM). Similarly,30 presented a DTC strategy for Dual-Stator Induction Motors (DSIM) using the Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) technique. This approach outperformed conventional PID-based DTC.

Specific to OEWIM,31 implemented DTC using dual-inverter configurations, which enhanced torque density and fault tolerance, although neutral-point voltage control remained challenging. In32, a custom switching strategy was proposed to reduce inverter switching losses and increase efficiency while preserving torque precision. In33, an improved hysteresis controller for OEWIM DTC achieved lower torque ripple and better dynamic response, though it required additional computational effort. A hybrid SVM-DTC method was proposed in34, offering better ripple control and robustness under load disturbances. Multi-level inverter approaches were explored in35 to further improve efficiency and reduce ripple, but these came with higher cost and complexity. Finally,36 employed predictive control with multi-level inverters for OEWIM DTC, enhancing dynamic performance at the expense of increased computational requirements.

In37, the classical Perturb and Observe (P&O) method was combined with Direct Torque Control (DTC) for PV-powered induction motor drives. To improve accuracy,38 introduced the Incremental Conductance (IC) method within the DTC framework, which provided better MPP tracking under varying irradiance, though it struggled with fast-changing conditions due to slower response. To address these challenges,39 proposed a Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)-based MPPT approach, which improved tracking speed and accuracy, especially in partial shading. However, the increased computational demand limited its use in real-time systems.

Recent literature shows that various optimization algorithms have been developed for Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) in PV-powered Direct Torque Control (DTC) systems. However, each approach presents specific drawbacks. Traditional techniques like Perturb and Observe (P&O) and Incremental Conductance (IC) struggle with accuracy and convergence under rapidly changing irradiance. In contrast, heuristic and hybrid algorithms offer better performance but often come with increased computational complexity or require extensive parameter tuning. Most research efforts focus either on MPPT or on motor control independently, lacking a fully integrated approach that optimizes both simultaneously. Additionally, the use of MPPT in conjunction with Open-End Winding Induction Motor (OEWIM) drives remains underexplored, despite OEWIM’s well-documented benefits such as enhanced fault tolerance, better control flexibility, and suitability for renewable energy systems.

To bridge these gaps, this paper proposes a Hybrid Beluga Whale Optimization (HBWO) algorithm for MPPT, combined with an advanced DTC scheme for OEWIM drives. The HBWO algorithm improves upon the original Beluga Whale Optimization (BWO) by integrating adaptive opposition-based learning, chaotic perturbation, and a hybrid initialization strategy. These enhancements improve convergence speed, tracking accuracy, and the balance between global exploration and local exploitation. The OEWIM system, due to its robust control capabilities and high-performance operation, is integrated with a modified DTC that includes an improved switching table and a five-level torque hysteresis controller. This design minimizes torque ripple and improves flux control, essential for stable motor performance under varying environmental and load conditions.

The HBWO-based MPPT dynamically adapts to changes in irradiance and load, making it highly suitable for real-world PV systems. Its performance is rigorously evaluated through MATLAB/SIMULINK simulations across five different scenarios, including conditions like partial shading and rapid irradiance fluctuation. The proposed approach is compared against several existing MPPT methods such as P&O, Chimp Optimization Algorithm (COA), and Giza Pyramid Construction (GPC). BWO, inspired by the bubble-net hunting behavior of beluga whales, provides a balance between global and local search through spiral and encircling movements. While promising, its standalone application in MPPT can face issues with tracking accuracy and slow convergence under partial shading. The proposed HBWO addresses these challenges using opposition-based learning for better population diversity and chaotic local search to prevent premature convergence.

Despite significant advancements in MPPT techniques and motor control strategies for PV-fed electric drives, several key research gaps remain unaddressed. Traditional MPPT algorithms such as often suffer from limitations such as slow convergence under rapidly changing irradiance conditions, susceptibility to local optima, and degraded tracking efficiency under partial shading scenarios. Similarly, in motor control, conventional DTC techniques are frequently associated with high torque ripple and suboptimal dynamic performance, particularly when integrated with non-conventional inverter topologies like OEWIM drives.

To bridge these gaps, this research introduces a novel HBWO algorithm tailored for dynamic and uncertain PV environments. Additionally, it couples this optimization approach with a refined DTC structure—featuring a five-level torque hysteresis controller and optimized switching logic—to mitigate torque ripple and enhance transient behaviour. This integrated solution uniquely addresses both the energy harvesting and load control challenges, offering a holistic and efficient architecture for PV-powered OEWIM drives, which has not been comprehensively explored in existing literature. The proposed system is a renewable energy-powered drive architecture that integrates photovoltaic (PV) power generation, intelligent MPPT control, and advanced motor drive control for an Open-End Winding Induction Motor (OEWIM), as illustrated in Fig. 1. At the core of the system is the PV array, which converts solar energy into electrical power. Since the PV output is nonlinear and affected by factors like irradiance and temperature, a Hybrid Beluga Whale Optimization (HBWO)-based MPPT algorithm is used. This algorithm dynamically adjusts the duty cycle of a boost converter, ensuring that the system consistently tracks the Global Maximum Power Point (GMPP) even under conditions such as partial shading or rapidly changing sunlight—outperforming conventional MPPT techniques.

PV fed OEWIM with proposed MPPT and DTC.

The power from the PV array passes through a boost converter, which increases the voltage to meet the requirements of a common DC link. This DC link serves as both an energy buffer and a central distribution hub, supplying power to both the motor drive and an auxiliary battery storage unit.

To maintain continuous operation during fluctuations—such as drops in solar input or increased motor demand—a battery energy storage system is included. It is connected through a bidirectional DC-DC converter, which allows the battery to charge when excess energy is available and discharge when needed, stabilizing the DC link voltage and ensuring consistent motor performance.

The system’s load is an OEWIM, known for its fault tolerance, enhanced control capabilities, and higher reliability compared to standard induction motors. The OEWIM is driven by two Voltage Source Converters (VSC1 and VSC2), each connected to one end of the stator windings. These dual inverters, powered from the same DC link, allow independent control of the motor windings, improving torque and flux control, reducing harmonic distortion, and increasing system efficiency. An improved Direct Torque Control (DTC) method is used to control the motor. This includes a five-level torque hysteresis controller and advanced switching logic. Compared to traditional DTC, the improved scheme significantly reduces torque ripple, enhances dynamic response, and maintains stable performance across a wide range of conditions. It also eliminates the need for complex transformations or PWM techniques by directly regulating torque and stator flux in real time.

Mathematical model of open-end winding IM

The Open-End Winding Induction Motor (OEWIM) configuration is increasingly being adopted in advanced electric drive systems due to its enhanced control capabilities, fault tolerance, and modular design, especially in renewable energy applications like PV-powered systems. An OEWIM has each end of the stator windings accessible and independently controllable, which allows the motor to be driven by dual inverters. This dual-inverter topology enables voltage boosting without additional transformers. OEWIMs inherently offer higher reliability and robustness in the presence of faults. If one inverter leg fails, the motor can still operate—albeit with reduced performance—through the remaining inverter. OEWIMs are especially well-suited for integration with distributed generation sources, such as photovoltaic (PV) systems, due to their ability to operate efficiently at low voltage levels and their modular inverter structure, which can be adapted to varying power outputs typical of PV system.

A PV system’s power output is highly dependent on environmental conditions, particularly solar irradiance and temperature. The output of a PV panel is nonlinear, and it is affected by variations in these conditions. At any given moment, the power extracted from the PV panel is maximized at a specific operating point known as the MPP. The position of this point changes continuously with changing sunlight intensity and temperature. Without a method to track this point, the PV system would operate at a suboptimal point, resulting in less energy being converted to electrical power. MPPT algorithms are designed to dynamically adjust the operating point of the PV system so that it consistently operates at the Maximum Power Point, irrespective of fluctuations in solar irradiance or temperature.

In a PV-powered OEWIMD system, the role of MPPT is to ensure that the PV source delivers the maximum available power to the inverter that drives the motor. The efficiency of the system depends on the PV array’s ability to meet the dynamic power demands of the motor, especially when the irradiance and temperature conditions are changing. MPPT algorithms ensure that the PV array operates at its optimal power point, thus supplying the maximum power possible to the motor drive. Since the output of the PV system fluctuates with environmental conditions, an MPPT controller is needed to constantly adapt the system to these changes. In the case of OEWIM drive, where two independent inverters are used to supply power to the motor’s open-end winding configuration, MPPT can be integrated with the inverter’s control system. This integration ensures that both inverters receive the optimal power from the PV source, which is then used effectively to drive the motor, enabling precise control over speed, torque, and overall motor performance.

The proposed HBWO offers significant advantages as MPPT algorithm for PV systems. The adaptive opposition strategy allows for faster convergence by adjusting the search space dynamically, enabling the algorithm to better explore the optimal power point under varying environmental conditions. The chaos-enhanced initialization provides improved randomness and diversity in the initial population, reducing the chances of getting trapped in local optima and enhancing the global search capability. The hybrid approach combines the strengths of both Beluga Whale Optimization and opposition-based strategies, which improves the robustness and accuracy of MPPT tracking. The reduced computational complexity and improved convergence speed make this hybrid optimization technique ideal for implementation in practical PV systems, even with limited computational resources.

Battery with bidirectional converter

In the proposed HBWO-based MPPT algorithm for PV systems and the improved DTC strategy for OEWIM drives, a battery with a bidirectional converter is integrated into the system. The primary purpose of this battery is to ensure a continuous and stable power supply to the motor, even under fluctuating solar power conditions. Since PV systems inherently exhibit intermittent power generation due to variations in solar irradiance and temperature, the battery plays a crucial role in maintaining the DC link voltage stability and ensuring uninterrupted motor operation. The bidirectional converter facilitates both charging and discharging of the battery, effectively managing energy flow between the PV system, battery, and the motor drive system.

The bidirectional converter enables controlled power exchange between the battery and the DC link. During excess PV power generation, the surplus energy is stored in the battery by operating the converter in buck mode, reducing the voltage to match the battery’s charging requirements. Conversely, when the PV power is insufficient to meet the motor’s demand, the stored energy in the battery is supplied to the DC link through the converter operating in boost mode, ensuring continuous power availability. This dynamic energy management enhances system reliability and efficiency, preventing voltage fluctuations that could affect motor performance. The outer voltage control loop is responsible for regulating the DC link voltage. A reference DC link voltage is set, and the actual DC link voltage is continuously monitored. The error between the reference and actual voltage is processed by a PI controller, which generates a reference current for the inner current control loop. This ensures that the DC link voltage remains stable regardless of fluctuations in PV power or load demand, thereby maintaining steady operation of the OEWIM drive.

The inner loop regulates the battery current to ensure smooth power transfer. The reference current obtained from the outer loop is compared with the actual current flowing through the bidirectional converter. A PI controller processes this error and generates the required duty cycle for PWM control. The PWM signals are used to switch the bidirectional converter, adjusting its operation mode (buck or boost) based on power demand.

During motor acceleration or increased load conditions, the inner loop allows the battery to supply power to the DC link by boosting the voltage. Conversely, when the PV system generates excess power, the converter operates in buck mode, transferring surplus energy to charge the battery efficiently. The coordinated control of these loops ensures that power flow is dynamically adjusted, preventing voltage instability and improving overall system performance.

Beluga whale optimization (BWO)

The proposed HBWO algorithm introduces a robust and adaptive optimization strategy that addresses several limitations found in traditional and recent MPPT techniques. Unlike P&O, which relies on incremental voltage adjustments and is prone to oscillations and local optima under partial shading, HBWO integrates global search capabilities to effectively explore the power surface. Compared to heuristic approaches like PSO, which often suffer from premature convergence due to loss of diversity, HBWO incorporates adaptive exploration–exploitation balancing mechanisms to maintain population diversity and ensure convergence to the global optimum. The algorithm also improves over the COA and GPC by integrating multi-strategy mechanisms such as dynamic inertia control, elite learning, and hybrid mutation, allowing it to dynamically adapt to varying irradiance and load conditions. The HBWO is seamlessly integrated with a modified DTC scheme, enhancing its ability to simultaneously optimize power extraction and motor performance. This holistic design makes HBWO a suitable and scalable solution for real-time MPPT in complex PV-driven motor systems operating under variable environmental and operational conditions.

Beluga whales (Delphinapterus leucas) are small, white whales that live in Arctic and sub-Arctic waters. Known as the “canaries of the sea,” they produce a variety of sounds like clicks, whistles, and chirps. The name “beluga” comes from the Russian word for “white,” which reflects their pale appearance. They have a rounded forehead called a melon used for echolocation and lack a dorsal fin, which helps them swim under ice. Belugas are relatively small whales, growing between 3.5 and 5.5 m long and weighing 700–1600 kg. Males are usually larger than females. Calves are born dark-colored and become white as they grow older. Their streamlined shape makes them well-suited for icy environments.

These whales are opportunistic feeders, eating fish, crustaceans, and mollusks. They often hunt bottom-dwelling creatures like flatfish and squid, using echolocation to find prey in dark, cloudy waters. Their diet changes with the seasons based on prey availability. Belugas are very social animals, living in groups called pods that can range from a few to hundreds. They show strong social bonds, often seen playing, hunting together, and constantly vocalizing to communicate and stay connected.

For hunting, belugas often work in groups to trap prey near the surface. They can dive deep but are usually found in shallow coastal waters where food is more plentiful. In some areas, they have even been observed cooperating with narwhals during feeding. Belugas face threats from predators like killer whales and polar bears, especially during summer when they gather in crowded estuaries. Human activities like pollution and hunting also pose risks. When a beluga dies and sinks to the ocean floor, it creates a “whale fall,” supporting a deep-sea ecosystem by providing nutrients for scavengers and bacteria in otherwise barren environments. Inspired by the fascinating behaviours and survival strategies of beluga whales—such as their intricate swimming techniques, their preying methods, and the whale fall phenomenon—a novel approach to problem-solving was considered. This led to the development of a new metaheuristic algorithm, termed the BWO algorithm proposed by Changting Zhong a, Gang Li A and Zeng Meng51. The BWO algorithm draws upon the natural behaviours of beluga whales, specifically their methods of hunting, foraging, and navigating in the challenging conditions of the Arctic waters.

The primary concept behind the BWO algorithm is to replicate the adaptive strategies that beluga whales use to overcome challenges in their environment. The algorithm mimics the way belugas work together to hunt and survive in high-density environments, as well as the way they adapt to varying conditions during their migration. In particular, the algorithm draws inspiration from the way belugas coordinate their movements to optimize the location of prey, and how they navigate through complex terrains to locate food. The phenomenon of whale fall, where the whale’s body serves as a valuable resource for an array of deep-sea organisms, also provides insight into optimizing the use of available resources in a constrained environment. The BWO algorithm integrates these principles of collaboration, adaptation, and resource optimization into a powerful tool for solving complex optimization problems.

Mathematical model of beluga whale optimization

The BWO algorithm is inspired by the natural behaviours of beluga whales, such as swimming, preying, and the phenomenon of whale fall. In a manner like other metaheuristic algorithms, BWO operates with two main phases: the exploration phase and the exploitation phase. The exploration phase ensures a global search capability within the design space by randomly selecting beluga whales, which allows the algorithm to explore a wide range of potential solutions. The exploitation phase focuses on a local search within the design space, optimizing the identified potential solutions. This two-phase structure is essential for the algorithm’s efficiency, as it combines the ability to search broadly (exploration) with the ability to fine-tune solutions (exploitation).

The voltage space vector plane, commonly referred to as the αβ-plane, is divided into 12 sectors, each spanning 30 degrees. This division is fundamental for identifying appropriate inverter switching states used in the DTC scheme. These switching states are classified into two main categories: active and null states. Active switching states directly contribute to the control of torque and stator flux, while null states do not influence motor torque or flux but are essential for maintaining stability and regulating voltage under specific operating conditions.

The active voltage vectors are grouped based on their magnitude. The Small Vector Group () consists of lower-magnitude voltage vectors that are typically utilized during low-speed motor operations. These allow for fine-tuned control over torque and flux. In contrast, the Large Vector Group () includes higher-magnitude vectors suitable for high-speed conditions where stronger torque responses are required. This classification supports adaptive control, enabling the system to select suitable vectors based on the motor’s real-time speed and load conditions.

To facilitate efficient voltage vector selection, a heuristic lookup table is implemented. This table operates based on three main inputs: the flux sector () indicating the position of the stator flux in the αβ-plane; the torque hysteresis controller output (), which can take values such as + 2, + 1, 0, − 1, or − 2 depending on torque error conditions; and the flux hysteresis controller output (), which signals whether the stator flux needs to increase, remain stable, or decrease. During low-speed operations, the lookup table selects from the small vector group, while at high speeds, it switches to the large vector group, ensuring dynamic and efficient torque-flux regulation.

While the proposed Hybrid Beluga Whale Optimization (HBWO) algorithm demonstrates superior performance in MPPT and motor drive control under dynamic conditions, it is not without limitations. The integration of multiple strategies—such as adaptive opposition-based learning, chaotic perturbation, and hybrid initialization—adds to the computational overhead. This complexity may not be ideal for low-cost embedded systems or real-time applications with limited processing capabilities.

HBWO is computationally efficient and scalable. Its structure is suitable for parallel execution, especially in fitness evaluation and position updates, which can be offloaded to hardware like GPUs or implemented in real-time embedded systems. Despite being more sophisticated than basic algorithms like PSO or P&O, HBWO maintains a manageable computational complexity while delivering superior optimization performance.

The HBWO algorithm is primarily validated on a single or moderately sized PV array and motor drive system. Scaling the method to larger systems with multiple PV strings, complex grid interconnections, or multi-motor drive architectures may require substantial modifications to the algorithm and additional control layers to maintain performance and synchronization. Although effective in simulation, implementing HBWO in real-time controllers (e.g., DSPs or FPGAs) can be challenging. Real-time constraints may require algorithm simplification, such as reducing population size or iteration limits, which could affect tracking accuracy.

Conclusion

This study proposed a Hybrid Beluga Whale Optimization (HBWO) algorithm enhanced with multi-strategy mechanisms for effective Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) in photovoltaic (PV) systems integrated with an Open-End Winding Induction Motor (OEWIM) drive. The motor drive was controlled using an advanced Direct Torque Control (DTC) framework complemented by a five-level torque hysteresis controller. The effectiveness of the proposed system was rigorously validated through comprehensive simulations under five dynamic operational scenarios, each designed to reflect realistic and challenging environmental and load conditions. In Case 1 (constant irradiance and fixed reference speed), the HBWO algorithm demonstrated superior transient and steady-state performance over conventional MPPT techniques such as Perturb & Observe (P&O), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Cuckoo Optimization Algorithm (COA), and Grey Prediction Controller (GPC). Notably, it achieved a faster DC-link voltage rise time of 0.11 s, a minimal overshoot of 0.382%, a short settling time of 0.17 s, and an impressively low steady-state error of 0.04%. Torque ripple was significantly reduced to just 0.5%, compared to 2.2% and 5.6% from COA and P&O respectively. These results confirm the enhanced dynamic response and power quality provided by HBWO. In Case 2 (variable irradiance and constant speed), the proposed system consistently extracted maximum power while ensuring seamless energy management between the PV array and battery storage. It maintained high-speed tracking accuracy, suppressed voltage and torque ripples, and demonstrated excellent adaptability to irradiance fluctuations. The improved DTC control strategy played a pivotal role in minimizing torque ripple and flux variations during power transitions. In Case 3 (constant irradiance and variable speed), the system showcased robust motor control with dynamic speed tracking capabilities. HBWO ensured stable and optimal power delivery from the PV and battery units, allowing the motor to closely follow varying reference speeds. The five-level torque hysteresis controller further enhanced performance by minimizing electromagnetic torque oscillations and flux ripple, resulting in smoother motor operation compared to traditional DTC implementations. In Case 4, the system’s robustness was assessed under parameter uncertainties including rotor resistance and inertia variations. The HBWO algorithm maintained stable operation with minimal degradation in performance, demonstrating its resilience to internal parameter changes. In Case 5, partial shading conditions were simulated using time-varying irradiance patterns. Despite the presence of severe and dynamic mismatches in module irradiance, the HBWO algorithm effectively tracked the global MPP, delivering fast response (rise time ~ 0.1 to 0.11 s), low overshoot (~ 0.37 to 0.38%), and reduced power ripple. System-level responses such as PV voltage, DC-link voltage, motor speed, and torque were all well-regulated, affirming the algorithm’s adaptability to complex shading scenarios. The integration of the HBWO algorithm with a refined DTC approach and multi-level hysteresis control constitutes a robust, high-performance solution for PV-powered motor drives. The proposed architecture not only enhances energy extraction and motor control accuracy but also ensures operational stability under diverse and realistic conditions. Building on the promising results of this study, future research can explore the scalability and generalizability of the proposed system in larger-scale hybrid renewable setups involving wind, fuel cells, or grid-connected architectures. Real-time implementation and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing under complex load profiles and varying climatic conditions would further establish its industrial viability and resilience.
