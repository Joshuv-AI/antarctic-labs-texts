Cardiac arrhythmia detection via PQRST analyzed data using an optimized hierarchical fused fuzzy deep reinforcement learning
Authors: Nora Mahdavi, Reza Sadeghi, Arman Daliri, Mahdieh Zabihimayvan, Mahmoud Alimoradi, Gabrielle Knapp, Azam Bastanfard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac arrhythmia is a disorder caused by disruptions in the regular heart rhythm. Arrhythmias are categorized into two classes: sinus and non-sinus rhythms. Whereas sinus rhythms are generally low-risk, non-sinus rhythms are associated with higher risks of morbidity and mortality, including stroke and death. This research proposes a novel method, Optimized Hierarchical Fused Fuzzy Deep Reinforcement Learning OHFFDRL, which incorporates three steps for arrhythmia prediction: data preprocessing, reinforcement learning, and fuzzy deep learning. We evaluated the proposed method using a 12-lead electrocardiogram dataset comprising 10,646 patients. Our approach leverages recent advances in machine learning and medical science to achieve an accuracy of 94% in predicting non-sinus rhythms. Furthermore, the area under the ROC curve for OHFFDRL was 0.91, and the empirical ROC area was 0.90. In addition, the interpretability of the model has been analyzed with SHAP, LIME, Calibration Curve, Adversarial vulnerability, and Integrated Gradients. Our experimental results, among other findings, indicate that the most important feature for distinguishing heart rhythms is TAxis (the movement range in ventricular repolarization). These results demonstrate the potential of machine learning for the early prevention of heart disease through non-sinus rhythm prediction. The source code is available at (https://github.com/arman-daliri/OHFFDRL).Keywords: Reinforcement Learning, Cardiac Arrhythmia, Fuzzy Deep Learning, Medical Science

Introduction

Heart disease remains one of the leading causes of death globally. In the United States, up to 11% of individuals aged 20 to 40 have cardiovascular disease. Among individuals aged 40 to 60, the number of patients reaches 37%, and for those over 80, it reaches 85% [1]. Heart conditions can be categorized into different types, such as cardiovascular disease [2], coronary artery disease [3], heart failure [4], heart attack [5], and arrhythmia [6]. In some cases, these issues are caused by a stroke or heart attack, potentially leading to death. For these reasons, there has been a great deal of effort and research to predict heart diseases with a high accuracy. There are several methods to evaluate heart performance, and one of the most common ones is the Electrocardiogram (ECG) [7], which is a standard test for detecting heart arrhythmias [8].

Arrhythmia is one of the most common issues among patients with heart disease. Cardiac arrhythmia is a disorder caused by any changes in the regular heart rhythm, which can be recognized by ECG [9]. Congenital problems, unhealthy lifestyle choices, and smoking are among the common causes of cardiac arrhythmia. There are several types of arrhythmias, and one of the most important types, which is life-threatening, can be predicted based on the heart’s atrial rate [10]. Arrhythmias can be categorized into two classes of sinus and non-sinus rhythms [11]. In contrast to sinus rhythms, which are low-risk rhythms, non-sinus rhythms can be considered high risk and lead to death or stroke [12].

In several articles such as [11, 13, 14], the importance of specific problems in the non-sinus class is described. Although specific rhythms play an important role in some heart problems, it is fundamental to investigate all non-sinus rhythms together. In [14], the importance of the atrial fibrillation rhythm, which is non-sinusoidal, has been studied. The authors also investigated detection of atrial fibrillation rhythm using machine learning methods. In another recent research [13], the importance of this rhythm and its effect on heart diseases and its relationship with other rhythms have been discussed. Another work on left atrial infiltration [15], studies eleven arrhythmias in detail. However, no further discoveries on sinus and non-sinus rhythms were provided in that research. Similarly, in [16, 17], the same type of disease was studied, but neither study discussed the importance of the difference between sinus and non-sinus rhythms. To fill this gap, our work proposes a method to describe this vital difference and presents a new method to predict sinus and non-sinus rhythms.

Arrhythmia prediction has been performed using machine learning. Among the advantages of using machine learning are its ability to address time-consuming problems in medical prediction [18], compensate for the lack of medical equipment in underprivileged areas [19], and tackle the human resource problems of cardiologists [20]. While the data used in artificial intelligence (AI) is numerical, there are also many methods for predicting arrhythmias, and supervised learning is utilized when the data are labeled [21, 22]. Although significant research has been done in machine learning, there are still challenging problems. One of the most important challenges is determining which algorithm or method to use for different types of datasets [23, 24]. Another limitation is the lack of balance in the target labels of patient samples in the datasets [25, 26]. One significant problem in machine learning is also the lack of alignment of feature selection with the machine learning model to improve prediction accuracy [27].

In recent works such as [23, 28, 29], each option has been addressed separately. While addressing artificial intelligence problems is essential, making methods intelligent using artificial intelligence techniques plays a critical role in interpretable AI. In article [23], an attempt was made to present a novel method based on reinforcement learning for automatic selection of statistical classification algorithm. Additionally, this method addresses data balancing issues; however, it does not provide a solution for feature selection. In one recent study [30], researchers explored an algorithm which combines different neural networks; however, issues related to automation of the process and intellectualization were not raised. In articles [28, 29], a new method using arrhythmia data is discussed, but the interpretability of the method is not mentioned, as the focus is solely on results and evaluation of the method. To address this discrepancy, our work introduces a new approach to articulate this crucial distinction and offers a novel technique for predicting arrhythmia.

In order to solve the problems stated in this section, a novel method for the accurate prediction of important types of Cardiac arrhythmia has been proposed. The research aims to predict sinus and non-sinus arrhythmias using an imbalanced dataset. This method comprises three major steps, which are data preprocessing, reinforcement learning and fuzzy deep learning for arrhythmia prediction to establish certainty in the prediction. The novelty of this work is the development of a new machine learning method to automatically select the best sampler for different dataset types based on reinforcement learning. This new method is called Optimized Hierarchical Fused Fuzzy Deep Reinforcement Learning (OHFFDRL), and its medical novelty lies in predicting non-sinus rhythms. We evaluate the performance of the proposed method through a large variety of predictions and evaluations across various datasets. Among the findings, we report 93% accuracy in predicting sinus and non-sinus rhythms using our OHFFDRL. The major contributions of this study are as follows:

Cardiac arrhythmias

Cardiac arrhythmia arises from a malfunction in the heart’s electrical impulses, which disrupts the normal beating pattern of the heart [9]. Three prevalent forms of arrhythmia include tachycardia, bradycardia, and fibrillation. In tachycardia, the heart beats at an elevated pace; conversely, bradycardia is characterized by a slower than normal heart rate; and fibrillation features erratic heartbeats [9]. Symptoms associated with these arrhythmias may encompass difficulty in breathing, heart palpitations, dizziness, feelings of nausea or vomiting, and chest discomfort. An electrocardiogram (ECG) is typically used to diagnose these conditions. Treatment options usually involve medication; however, in cases of progression, radiofrequency ablation may be considered, and in critical situations where other treatments fail, surgical procedures may be necessary.

The process of documenting cardiac arrhythmia is done using an electrocardiogram device, which enables healthcare professionals to identify any arrhythmic irregularities based on the recorded readings. An ECG machine displays electrical signals related to heart activity, including heart rate measures [31]. The heart rhythm is depicted through a waveform termed PQRST. The rhythm begins with the P wave, followed by a brief period known as the PR interval. Next, the rhythm progresses to encompass the QRS complex, representing the heart’s electrical activity during contraction. The sequence concludes with the ST segment, which transitions into the T wave [31].

The present article outlines various types of cardiac arrhythmias categorized based on the database referenced in [23]. Generally, cardiac arrhythmias are classified into two main groups: (1) those with a sinus rhythm and (2) those with a non-sinus rhythm. Each group encompasses different heart conditions; however, the key distinction lies in the associated risks and types of arrhythmias present. A sinus rhythm in arrhythmias indicates normative sinusoidal functioning of the heart [32]. Within multiple sinus rhythms, an individual may experience an arrhythmia, yet it typically does not carry a significant risk to life. Conversely, irregular or non-sinus rhythms hinder proper heart function, leading to an elevated risk of arrhythmias [33]. This study examines a total of 11 arrhythmia variants: Sinus Bradycardia (SB), Sinus Rhythm (SR), Atrial Fibrillation (AFIB), Sinus Tachycardia (ST), Atrial Flutter (AF), Sinus Irregularity (SI), Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT), Atrial Tachycardia (AT), Atrioventricular Node Reentrant Tachycardia (AVNRT), Atrioventricular Reentrant Tachycardia (AVRT), and Sinus Atrium to Atrial Wandering Rhythm (SAAWR). Within these, there are five sinusoidal models and six irregular types.

As previously stated, sinus arrhythmias are not considered life-threatening conditions. These disorders can result in heart rhythms that are either faster or slower than normal; however, they do not represent a serious risk to patients’ lives [33]. The sinus rhythm (SR) is typically the prevailing heart rhythm found in individuals without arrhythmias [34]. Sinus bradycardia (SB) refers to a variant of the sinus rhythm in which the heart rate is lower than the typical range of 60 to 100 beats per minute, specifically dropping below 60 beats per minute [35]. Conversely, sinus tachycardia (ST) describes a condition in which the heart maintains a rapid rhythm, consistently beating at a higher rate than normal within one minute [36]. Sinus irregularity (SI) denotes a scenario in which the heart rhythm remains sinusoidal but features irregular distances between P waves, measured from one P wave to the next [37]. Another arrhythmia discussed in this article is Sinus Atrium to Atrial Wandering Rhythm (SAAWR). This condition disrupts the sinus rhythms primarily during the P wave segment [37] and is commonly observed in both very young individuals and the elderly.

The higher-risk rhythms evaluated in this article consist of six distinct types. Atrial fibrillation (AFIB) stands out as one of the most prevalent non-sinus rhythms. Characterized by an irregular heartbeat, this condition poses significant risks, including stroke and heart failure, which may result in blood clots which affect the brain or contribute to heart failure [38]. Another critical non-sinus rhythm is Atrial Flutter (AF), which is marked by a rapid, irregular heartbeat within the heart’s chambers, leading to potential strokes which can have long-term impacts on patients’ health [39]. Atrial Tachycardia (AT) represents another alarming condition that manifests as an atypical rhythm, marked by swift and abnormal beats originating from the atria [40]. Additionally, Atrioventricular Node Reentrant Tachycardia (AVNRT) is identified as a variant of Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT). Although SVT is generally considered low-risk and often manageable with medication, AVNRT is one of the most commonly encountered variants within this category [41]. Atrioventricular Reentrant Tachycardia (AVRT) is somewhat more perilous than SVT, typically presenting a heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute [42]. This study has thoroughly examined, assessed, and evaluated all of these heart rhythms.

Traditional methods in machine learning, often referred to as classification algorithms, perform classification based on statistics [55], probabilities [56], and decision trees [23]. Several articles have employed these methods to predict and diagnose cardiac arrhythmias. Research [29] aimed at diagnosing cardiovascular disease, was conducted using 12-lead electrocardiogram data and traditional methods such as Random Forest and XG-Boost. In another paper [28], researchers attempted to diagnose heart disease with ECG data by using support vector machines combined with quantum computing, achieving significant results. In all the studies mentioned, significant results were obtained. However, to address the limitations of traditional methods, the Heptagonal Reinforcement Learning approach [23] combines a new reinforcement learning method with traditional machine learning techniques to improve outcomes.

Another model of supervised machine learning in the field of classification, known as modern machine learning, is artificial neural networks (ANNs) and deep learning [22]. Artificial neural networks have been implemented to handle large volumes of data, approximate decision-making and to establish relationships between data [27]. Deep learning has been applied to cardiac arrhythmia prediction using all three data models (signal, clinical, and hybrid) [49, 50, 53]. For example, in article [49], it was used to predict cardiac arrhythmia using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and ECG signal data. Also in the article [50], three separate One-dimensional Convolutional Neural Networks were employed with clinical data to predict cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). Additionally, in article [44], a deep learning network called The sequence learning part involving long short-term memory was implemented to detect and predict cardiac arrhythmia using hybrid data. Furthermore, hybrid machine learning methods have been proposed to enhance deep learning results.

Although deep learning methods are successful in classification, they suffer from issues such as lack of interpretability and inability to learn from small datasets [26]. To address such limitations, researchers have attempted to combine machine learning methods. Optimized Hierarchical Fused fuzzy deep reinforcement learning

This section explains our novel machine learning method, Optimized Hierarchical Fused Fuzzy Deep Reinforcement Learning (OHFFDRL), and the structure of the proposed method. First, subsection 3.1 presents an overview of the method, and then, subsection 3.2 describes the OHFFDRL method.

Overview of Optimized Hierarchical Fused fuzzy deep reinforcement learning

In this subsection, general information on how the OHFFDRL method works is provided. In general, this method offers a comprehensive approach for predicting cardiac arrhythmia by using several different fields of artificial intelligence. The main goal of this research is the prediction of non-sinusoidal arrhythmia. The OHFFDRL method consists of five parts. Figure 3 shows the flowchart of the method.

Flowchart of Optimized Hierarchical Fused fuzzy deep reinforcement learning (OHFFDRL)

According to Fig. 3, which presents the graphical abstract and flowchart of the OHFFDRL method, first the data is read. Then, data preprocessing is performed, which includes handling personal items such as IDs and addresses, etc. After checking personal items, null items, spaces and outlier problems are handled. Once the data is integrated, it is balanced using the new Triangle Rate method for balancing the target label, as specifically presented for this dataset in the article [23]. Next, the first step of the proposed method, which is the optimization of the proposed artificial neural network parameters, is performed using the World Hyper Heuristic (WHH) algorithm [24], a reinforcement learning approach for optimization in the search for meta-heuristics.

After this step, to select the best classifier, we implemented heptagonal reinforcement learning [23], which, based on the Max Square parameter, selects the best candidate among algorithms if the stopping condition, which is the number of iterations, is met. Following this, the optimized proposed algorithm is executed, and if it exceeds the Max Square value, the final cardiac arrhythmia classification results are produced. Otherwise, the parameters are adjusted again, and the aforementioned process is repeated. Each section of the flowchart is described in detail below.

OHFFDRL integrates multiple components because ECG-based arrhythmia prediction involves uncertainty and strong dependencies among clinical variables. Several ECG-derived features can show overlapping ranges between sinus and non-sinus rhythms, and borderline cases are common; therefore, a fuzzy layer is used to model gradual class transitions through membership functions rather than relying on rigid thresholds. In addition, the neural network module learns nonlinear interactions among the 16 extracted ECG features (e.g., ventricular/atrial rate, PR/QRS/QT intervals, and axis-related measures). These features are physiologically linked and inherently time-related (intervals and durations), so modeling their joint patterns helps capture clinically meaningful relationships and improves robustness when measurements are noisy or when multiple variables jointly affect the final label.

The optimization and reinforcement-learning stages are included to reduce manual trial-and-error and to make model configuration more systematic. Specifically, the World Hyper-Heuristic (WHH) is used to tune the parameters of the neural–fuzzy model, including membership-function parameters, to improve performance without exhaustive manual search. After parameter tuning, heptagonal reinforcement learning (HRL) is applied to select the final classifier based on the Max Square criterion, so the model choice is driven by measured performance rather than subjective preference. Together, these steps provide a reproducible pipeline that combines uncertainty handling, interaction learning among ECG features, and automated parameter and model selection for predicting high-risk (non-sinus) arrhythmia.

Data preprocessing

In this phase, we remove all personal identifiers within the dataset, including national ID numbers, addresses, telephone numbers, and eliminate irrelevant elements such as patient identification numbers. We then structure the data to facilitate analysis of essential characteristics. To ensure the dataset is ready for use, we first detect and remove any duplicate records. Next, we examine for outlier features. These issues are resolved collectively to eliminate both duplicates and subpar entries. Recognizing the frequent presence of incorrect data in real-world datasets, all such inaccuracies are filtered out during this stage. Following this, we address any remaining null entries and assess the overall balance of the dataset.

To assess our proposed algorithm’s effectiveness, we utilize a database consisting of 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) for arrhythmia research, including data from 10,646 individuals [31]. These records were obtained via a 10-second resting ECG analysis conducted at Chapman University and Shaoxing People’s Hospital. The database encompasses cardiovascular information from patients diagnosed with eleven different types of arrhythmias. As displayed in Table 2, each patient’s record includes 16 features derived from ECG readings, as well as age and gender. These features are categorized into four distinct models: unique, discrete, numerical, and categorical. The unique model identifies each recorded sample via the Filename. Age is classified as discrete, as it is recorded as whole numbers. Continuous features are portrayed through numerical models, where each sample can yield a value within a defined range; for instance, the QRS Count for a patient’s sample may range from 0 to 225. Lastly, features that allow selections from predetermined categories are classified as categorical, such as patient gender, which may be either male or female.

In order to ensure the quality and validity of the model results, the process of identifying and managing outliers and incorrect data was carefully carried out in the preprocessing stage. For the extracted features, descriptive statistical analyses were used to identify outliers; values that were outside the valid physiological ranges for ECG parameters (such as PR, QRS, and QT intervals) were considered as noise from automatic feature extraction and were discarded from the analysis to prevent bias in model training and improve the accuracy of the algorithm. ECG signals were recorded at frequency of 500 Hz by GE MUSE systems [31]. The annotation process of this dataset was performed with very high clinical accuracy by a team of licensed cardiologists. In the preprocessing stage, in order to remove noises, digital filters were used and the signals were normalized for integration in the feature extraction process. This precise collection and labeling protocol ensure the clinical validity of the proposed model in diagnosing various arrhythmias [31].

An essential categorical attribute within this dataset is Rhythm, which defines the patterns of cardiac activity recorded in various ECG and is categorized into 11 unique types. Table 3 illustrates the distribution of these categories across a total of 5,956 male and 4,690 female ECG entries. The most frequently observed rhythms, noted in considerable quantities, consist of Sinus Bradycardia (SB) with 3,889 cases, Sinus Rhythm (SR) accounting for 1,826 instances, and Atrial Fibrillation (AFIB) documented 1,780 times. Conversely, sinus rhythms such as Sinus Irregularity (SI), SAAWR, and SR tend to be associated with younger patients, with mean ages of 34.75 ± 23.03, 51.14 ± 31.83, and 54.35 ± 16.33 years, respectively.

Information about rhythms

This study makes use of the Rhythm feature to identify and categorize different heart rhythms. Under the supervision of a cardiologist, the dataset’s eleven unique rhythm types were divided into two primary categories to improve classification accuracy. For further information regarding sinusoidal and non-sinusoidal rhythms, refer to Fig. 4. The first category includes five variations of sinus rhythms: Sinus Rhythm (SR), Sinus Bradycardia (SB), Sinus Tachycardia (ST), Sinus Irregularity (SI), and Sinus Atrium to Atrial Wandering Rhythm (SAAWR), which are all classified under the umbrella of sinus rhythms. The second category comprises non-sinus arrhythmias, featuring six separate rhythms: Atrial Fibrillation (AFIB), Atrial Flutter (AF), Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT), Atrioventricular Tachycardia (AT), Atrioventricular Node Reentrant Tachycardia (AVNRT), and Atrioventricular Reentrant Tachycardia (AVRT). The reasoning behind this classification is closely linked to the increased risks associated with various non-sinusoidal rhythms. Although sinus rhythms may also pose risks, they are typically regarded as less severe compared to those that are non-sinusoidal.

Collection of targets

Triangle rate for oversampling

This segment presents a novel measurement introduced in this research, known as the Triangle Rate (TR). The main objective of TR is to pinpoint the most efficient oversampling method for achieving dataset equilibrium. Issues associated with imbalanced datasets become apparent during the training and evaluation of classification algorithms, as the underrepresented class typically lacks sufficient sample size [58, 59]. To tackle this challenge, several oversampling strategies have been proposed, and TR is designed to determine the most suitable one for the dataset at hand. The methodology involves three specific numerical indicators to evaluate and choose the most suitable balancing technique from various alternatives. For each prospective algorithm, these indicators are determined and conceptualized as the corners of a triangle. The area of this triangle is then calculated and juxtaposed with those of alternative algorithms. The development of TR’s metrics is aimed at selecting the balancing technique that yields the smallest triangle area. The metrics incorporated in TR consist of the Calinski-Harabasz score [60, 61], the silhouette score [60, 62], and the Davies-Bouldin score [60, 63], which will be detailed in the subsequent sections.

The Calinski-Harabasz index measures the quality of clustering by employing the K-means algorithm to determine suitable groupings within a given dataset [61]. Discussion about interpretability of OHFFDRL

The SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method is widely recognized as a well-established and theory-supported approach for the explanation of complex machine learning models [94]. Founded on cooperative game theory, SHAP provides a value for each feature, indicating its influence on the model’s output. Positive values increase the chance of predicting a specific class, while negative ones reduce it. This framework benefits from strong theoretical guarantees like fairness and consistency. Figure 11 shows the SHAP summary plot using correct feature names. AtrialRate and VentricularRate appear as the most influential variables, supporting their clinical significance. QRSCount and TOffset also demonstrate noticeable contributions. Meanwhile, features such as PatientAge and QTInterval present smaller but still meaningful effects on the model’s predictions.

Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) is one of the techniques for generating local explanations of predictions made by ML models [95]. Through perturbation of input around a specific instance and observation of the response from the model, LIME tries to find an easy, interpretable model (like linear) approximating the response of the original model in that local neighborhood. It then assigns the input features weights based on their effect on the decision of the model for the current case, providing insight into features that strengthened or weakened the predicted outcome. Figure 12 illustrates current LIME analysis of a sample classified as “normal.” Features such as Gender, PatientAge, and AtrialRate exhibited strong positive influence, increasing the confidence in the predicted class. In contrast, features like QRSDuration, TAxis, and RAxis demonstrated negative contributions, suggesting they acted against the classification. Although variables such as QTInterval and TOffset also appeared meaningful in this instance, their interpretation remains context-dependent and should be considered locally.

Adversarial vulnerability describes the susceptibility of machine learning models to input perturbations that are subtle yet strategically designed to alter model predictions [96]. A widely adopted technique for probing this vulnerability is the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), which introduces minimal noise based on the gradient of the loss function with respect to the input. Evaluating a model’s behavior under such conditions is critical for understanding its robustness, particularly in high-stakes or clinical environments. As shown in Fig. 13, the proposed model demonstrates a strong degree of stability under adversarial conditions generated by the FGSM method. Despite minor perturbations applied to features such as QTInterval, QRSCount, and QOnset, the majority of input variables remained stable, and the model’s response was consistent across most dimensions. This suggests that the model maintains robustness near decision boundaries and is not easily misled by small, targeted noise—an encouraging indication of its reliability for sensitive applications such as clinical diagnostics.

SHAP, LIME, Integrated Gradients (IG), and adversarial testing yield different conclusions in our study because they address different questions about the model. SHAP provides a cohort-level view and therefore highlights features with the strongest average influence across patients, where AtrialRate and VentricularRate (followed by QRSCount and TOffset) emerge as the dominant drivers. LIME is instance-specific, and for Sample 100 it attributes the prediction mainly to Gender, PatientAge, and AtrialRate, while QRSDuration, TAxis, and RAxis contribute in the opposite direction for that particular record. IG also explains a single instance but reflects the deep network’s attribution structure; in the representative case, QRSCount receives the strongest positive attribution, whereas TOffset and VentricularRate show negative attribution, which helps explain why local evidence can differ from global SHAP rankings. In contrast, adversarial analysis is not an importance ranking but a robustness assessment; the FGSM results indicate that most features remain stable under perturbation, while larger changes concentrate in a limited subset (notably QTInterval and QRS-related measures), identifying the dimensions most sensitive under worst-case noise.

Feature importance

The feature TAxis, which displays the most substantial decrease in accuracy as demonstrated in Fig. 16, is recognized as the most critical feature for classification. This feature represents the range of motion in the T segment of the cardiac cycle, reflective of ventricular repolarization, and serves as a key marker for accurately determining the QRS axis. The TAxis measurement is particularly effective for detecting potential irregularities in cardiac function. Typically, this motion is expected to range between 15 to 75 degrees; deviations from this norm may signal underlying rhythm disturbances [100].

The second most salient feature, Ventricular Rate, indicates the ventricular activity recorded in an electrocardiogram (ECG). Normal values for this parameter typically range from 60 to 100 beats per minute. Deviations from this range can lead to non-sinus rhythms, with bradycardia being significantly influenced by alterations in Ventricular Rate. While this feature also impacts the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes [101], its relevance in our classification approach is supported by its diagnostic importance.

Finally, Atrial Rate is ranked as the third most significant feature, representing the frequency of P waves within ECG data. The accuracy of the QRS complex frequency reflects this feature’s proper functioning. A healthy P wave is associated with an accurate QRS complex and optimal overall ECG performance [102]. Conversely, any abnormalities in the P wave may disrupt the efficacy of its associated features.

The proposed model is intended as a clinical decision-support tool for screening and triage rather than a replacement for cardiologist diagnosis. In routine ECG workflows, large volumes of recordings must be reviewed and prioritized, which requires substantial time and expert effort [23, 31]; therefore, OHFFDRL can automate the initial screening step by flagging ECGs predicted as high-risk (non-sinus) for earlier physician review, enabling faster identification of clinically significant rhythms such as atrial fibrillation (AFIB). Because the model operates on interpretable clinical features such as rates and interval/axis measures, it can provide both a prediction and an explanation of influential features, helping clinicians relate algorithmic outputs to physiological mechanisms and supporting more informed decision-making. In practice, this can reduce specialist workload, prioritize urgent cases for rapid interpretation and timely referral, and schedule lower-risk sinus predictions for standard review.

By implementing these three distinct models on the current dataset, we show that each added component significantly improves the accuracy, F1-score, 5Fold and 10Fold in recognition and the overall stability of the model. Conclusion and future work

This article describes the early prevention of heart arrhythmia based on sinus and non-sinus rhythms. We propose a novel method that uses reinforcement learning, an optimization algorithm, deep learning and fuzzy logic to select the best classifier and balancer for the data. We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate the method using both balanced and imbalanced data. The results indicate that our method predicts non-sinus rhythms with 93% accuracy. We also analyze feature importance in the original dataset, showing that categorical target labels are the most significant predictors of arrhythmia. The novel contributions of this work could inspire researchers in both machine learning and medical studies.

In our ongoing research endeavors, we plan to extend the scope of our proposed methodology by evaluating it on multiple large-scale datasets, focusing specifically on diverse diseases in the healthcare domain. This expansion is pivotal not only for validating the robustness and adaptability of our approach but also for understanding its implications across various pathological conditions. Additionally, an integral aspect of our future work will involve enhancing the ensemble of classifiers utilized within the existing method. By integrating a broader spectrum of classification algorithms, we intend to explore the potential improvements in predictive accuracy and overall system performance.

Furthermore, we envision the development of an online version of the proposed method to enable real-time prediction of cardiac arrhythmias. Implementing such a live system would allow for continuous monitoring and instant analysis of patient data, thereby significantly contributing to timely medical interventions. This aspect of our research aligns with the growing demand for innovative technological solutions in healthcare that can seamlessly integrate into existing clinical workflows. Overall, these prospective directions underscore our commitment to advancing research at the intersection of data science and healthcare, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes.
