Facilitators and barriers to the practice of neonatal family integrated care from the perspective of healthcare professionals: a systematic review
Authors: Nadia Leake, Sarah Edney, Nicholas Embleton, Janet Berrington, Judith Rankin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo conduct a systematic review of barriers and facilitators to the practice of neonatal Family Integrated Care (FICare) from the perspective of healthcare professionals (HCPs).DesignA systematic search strategy was developed to identify qualitative studies exploring neonatal HCPs’ views of any of the principles of FICare. Six literature databases (CINAHL, (Cumulated Index in Nursing and Allied Health Literature) Embase, Medline, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science) were searched using the terms Healthcare Professionals, Neonatal, Environment, FICare, Education, Well-being, Culture, Partnership and Empowerment. Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were thematically analysed.Results11032 titles and abstracts and 85 full-text papers were screened. Thirty-seven studies met the inclusion criteria and reported interviews with 1243 HCPs, predominantly nurses. Three themes were synthesised in relation to barriers and facilitators: (1) ‘advocacy and acknowledgement’, whereby HCPs are expected to advocate for the emotional and mental health of the whole family, not solely the baby’s needs; (2) ‘belief and behaviour’, whereby the degree to which FICare is practised is dependent on HCPs’ belief in its benefits in relation to other activities; (3) ‘conditions and consistency’, whereby a lack of space, resources, policy and consistent practice of FICare created apathy and contradictory approaches to care.ConclusionAlthough HCPs see value in FICare, successful implementation is multifactorial and requires the expectation to deliver FICare to be aligned with resourcing at the hospital, team and individual levels. Shifting the practice paradigm to FICare remains challenging for some HCPs. Greater understanding of HCPs’ views on barriers, facilitators and how FICare practice impacts individuals is required.Keywords: Neonatology; Nursing Care; Qualitative research; Intensive Care Units, Neonatal

Introduction

Worldwide, nearly 30 million1 babies are born sick or preterm each year and admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) for vital medical care. This is often a challenging and stressful time for families, and many parents report post-traumatic stress following a neonatal stay.2 3 Family Integrated Care (FICare) can improve infant and parent outcomes by empowering parents on a NICU to be primary caregivers to their baby for all non-medical tasks and promoting the parent-professional care partnership.4 5 Practised alongside medical care, benefits of FICare include reduced mortality, infection rates, antibiotic usage and re-admission; increased breast milk feeding and earlier discharge; and improved parental well-being.4

Parental presence and participation in care are key elements of FICare and an important step forward in the care of babies. It is known that the NICU environment itself may pose harm, as babies and their parents may experience a stay in the NICU as traumatic or a ‘toxic stress’, strongly linked to poor health outcomes across the baby’s lifespan.6 Parent and professional partnership in care can minimise the negative impact of the NICU experience, and as a neuroprotective intervention, FICare may potentially reduce subsequent impairment and disability of the baby.7

Evidence shows that FICare in the NICU has a positive, sustained effect on child behaviour and improvement in self-regulation at 18–21 months corrected age,8 and Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC), the continuous and prolonged provision of skin-to-skin contact and exclusive breastfeeding/breast milk feeding, is one example of FICare practice. The 2023 WHO global positioning statement describes KMC as a ‘high-impact intervention that significantly reduces the risk of neonatal mortality and morbidity’.9 Although there is a paucity of evidence for longer-term impact of FICare, short-term well-documented benefits of increased breastfeeding at discharge may have a long-reaching impact for the baby.10

Recently, there has been acknowledgement that a paradigm shift in care delivery is required to elevate parental caregiving and partnership to a crucial element of care, integral to the neurological, emotional and cognitive development of their sick or preterm baby.5 6 Despite a growing body of evidence of the benefits of FICare for parental mental health and the health of babies, and the publication of two frameworks for practice,4 11 HCPs report it has been difficult to sustain FICare practices in the long term.11 The reasons for this are not fully understood.

The aim of this systematic review was to examine existing qualitative evidence on barriers and facilitators to the practice of FICare from the perspective of HCPs to identify gaps that could improve the practice of FICare. HCPs are defined as any staff or healthcare professional working in a neonatal setting.

Methods

The systematic review was conducted using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines12 and registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42023415507).

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported views and experiences of neonatal HCPs on any component of FICare. Both qualitative and mixed methods studies were included from the inception of the searched databases until July 2024. Studies were excluded if parent views were analysed with the HCP view, if the focus was on the impact of any pandemic or a specific intervention, for example, pain alleviation. The search was conducted in April 2022 and updated in July 2024; search terms are presented in online supplemental appendix 1. Results were exported to Endnote for the removal of 9736 duplicate records. Title/abstract screening was conducted on the remaining 11 032 studies using the Rayyan systematic review manager, by researchers (NL 80% and SE (20%)). Full-text articles were retrieved for 81 studies and screened for inclusion by NL (70%) and SE (30%). Excluded studies with reasons for exclusion are presented in online supplemental appendix 2. Where there were unresolved differences of opinion, a third researcher (JR) independently reviewed the paper and a consensus decision was made. Results were collated in Excel for data extraction.

Using Thomas and Harden’s13 thematic synthesis methods, text extracted from included studies was coded ‘line-by-line’ and loosely grouped by sentiment. This was an iterative process until distinct descriptive themes were developed from these groups. The development of descriptive themes remained ‘close’ to the primary studies, then higher-order analytical themes were synthesised. Thematic analysis was carried out using NVivo V.14 software.

Search strategy

FICare includes many NICU activities resulting in varying terminology. For clarity and reproducibility, the SPIDER (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type) tool was adopted to define the review question, inform and standardise the search strategy. Terms were based on the British Association of Perinatal Medicine (BAPM) FICare principles:4 environment, well-being, empowerment, culture and partnership with parents (online supplemental appendix 1). These terms overlap sufficiently with the International Four Pillars of FICare:11 (staff education and support, parent education, NICU environment and psychosocial support) to form the foundation of an international systematic review. Each principle and synonyms were searched with the constants of ‘healthcare professional OR neonatal’ and ‘perspectives’ (figure 1) in six electronic databases (CINAHL, Embase, Medline, PsycInfo, Scopus, Web of Science). The search strategy was checked for accuracy and scope by an information scientist at Newcastle University. The original searches were performed during 2023 and updated in July 2024.

Results

Of the 81 full-text articles screened, 30 met inclusion criteria, 3 additional articles were identified through citation searching and 4 during the updated search (figure 2). The most common reason for exclusion of articles was neonatal staff and parent views being combined during thematic analysis.

PRISMA diagram detailing the number of studies included/excluded at each stage, and reasons for exclusion at full-text screening. FICare, Family Integrated Care; HCP, healthcare professional; PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Data extraction

A tailored data extraction form was developed by one researcher (NL). The data extraction form was used to extract data from five articles and deemed suitable following this pilot. Data extracted included study aims, number of participants, neonatal setting, methodology and key findings. All data were extracted by one researcher (NL), with a second researcher (SE) checking 70% of the extracted data for correctness and completeness (Online supplemental appendix 4 and online supplemental appendix 5).

Characteristics and methodology of the studies

Papers were published between June 2006 and June 2024 and contained the term Family centred care, FICare or a principle of FICare as per the BAPM framework. Included studies were conducted in several countries including in Canada (n=3), Australia (n=4), Iran (n=4), USA (n=5), Brazil (n=6). Twenty-three papers stated a qualitative methodology, either descriptive, inductive or interpretative. Fourteen stated social phenomenology, interpretative phenomenology, empirical observation, realistic evaluation, field study exploratory descriptive, reflective lifeworld research and grounded theory as the chosen methodology. A total of 1243 neonatal staff are included in the 37 included papers, predominantly nurses. Years of neonatology service ranged from 1 year to 40 years. Data were collected via one-to-one interview in 28 of the 37 papers and via focus-group, observation and open-ended survey questions in the remaining papers.

Findings

Theme 1: Advocacy and acknowledgement

HCPs encountered challenges as their role in care has changed due to FICare. FICare requires that HCPs care for the emotional and mental health of the whole family, not just the baby, potentially leading to conflicting feelings about FICare activities. The thoughts and feelings expressed in this theme acknowledge this is a challenging paradigm shift for some. Parental presence and allocation of caregiving tasks to parents were demanding. There was acknowledgement that FICare can bring an emotional burden to HCPs.

Despite this, HCPs described being a bridge between parents and their babies to humanise care as rewarding and providing job satisfaction. HCPs advocate for FICare by seeking ways to mitigate barriers to parental access to the neonatal unit, enable involvement in care and participate in education initiatives. While HCPs said they recognised the value of FICare to outcomes, some experienced a threat to their own value in the workplace and a perceived devaluing of their professional purpose and expertise, combined with a need to better advocate for themselves:

We are doing enough at the moment, and it is great to be there for parents, but we should really not do more. I did not do a nursing degree for parents to do my job.15

The personal views of HCPs influenced opportunities for Skin to Skin Care (STSC) and KMC, as did their view on what constituted optimal care. For example, some believed parents holding their sleeping baby would disrupt the baby’s rest and felt it was counterintuitive.

HCPs viewed themselves as the baby’s advocate, but poor understanding of the risk-benefit balance hampered efforts to embed STSC/KMC care into practice. HCPs also acknowledged a preference of some staff to keep the baby in the incubator, citing the baby’s well-being as a reason for restricting STSC. A discordant account of HCPs’ attitudes towards FICare is evident within this theme.

Theme 2: Belief and behaviour

FICare practice is dependent on HCPs’ belief in the perceived benefit in relation to other activities. A shift in mindset, belief in, and an open mind to FICare were described as the most important factors in behaviour change to enable parents to be primary caregivers. A general belief exists that parents should be the primary caregivers to their baby, but participants wanted clarity on what that role entails:

Set guidelines on what parents are expected to help dictate and what are going to be the care providers’ roles of what we believe is the best course of action for the patient.16

Parental status as primary caregivers was not always evident in communication. Sometimes, parents were told the care plan and then asked how they felt about it. This gesture towards joint decision-making was described as ‘letting parents feel like they still had some control’,17 behaviour inconsistent with belief in parents being the primary caregivers.

Education about the benefits of FICare was deemed a facilitator of FICare:

The mindset of healthcare professionals could be influenced by educating them about values of FCC [Family centered care].18

Barriers to the delivery of FICare education were a lack of protected time, inefficient, unstructured or self-directed learning, and staff found it difficult to train new team members on an ad hoc basis. Facilitative of FICare were hospital administrators and the NICU management who provided continuing education opportunities ensuring current practices are commensurate with recent advances in clinical evidence:

Professional challenges arise when nurses confront their resources (knowledge, experiences, conceptions and attitudes)19

Lack of belief in FICare meant that FICare activities could be deprioritised. FICare was perceived to increase workload, and lack of education and belief in its benefits may leave staff unmotivated to sustain behaviour that promotes FICare activities:

Provision of FCC was not viewed as a task similar to other NICU tasks such as blood work and infant feeding20

Power imbalance between parents and staff, habitual routines and professional autonomy were also described as barriers to FICare:

[O]ne of the doctors told me that, as the doctor, they are responsible for everything about that child. Therefore, everything should be their decision.21

Belief, education, decision-making and behaviour regarding FICare practices intertwine in this theme. It is a complex interplay, which may drive the need for consistency described in the following theme.

Theme 3: Conditions and consistency

In this context, ‘conditions’ are a holistic interpretation of HCPs’ views on working conditions facilitative of FICare. It considers physical space, staffing levels and educational support, the impact of parental presence and the need for consistent guidelines on delivering FICare.

The physical NICU environment is just one element of working conditions which was seen as a barrier to FICare:

The physical structure does not help us in the matter of encouraging a true family-centered care. Sometimes the mother does not even have a place to sit, because sometimes the number of patients is beyond the capacity.22

Parental presence could have an impact on working conditions, increasing anxiety and causing distraction:

I think staff don’t want parents around, because parents look on [at, watch] their hands.23

HCPs view FICare and STSC as valuable interventions, but not all staff practise them fully, leading to a wide variation in views on FICare. This was a theme present in all countries represented in this study in the context of similar healthcare delivery systems. Subthemes and quotes (online supplemental appendix 6) were also consistent across countries.

Discussion

This systematic review reports themes from the available literature on HCPs views of the barriers and facilitators to FICare. FICare is recommended practice in many neonatal units.4 24 25 Benefits of FICare such as increased breastfeeding rates at discharge and decreased maternal stress are associated with better long-term outcomes for babies.4 10 26 Despite this evidence, HCPs report inconsistent adoption of FICare ethos and practices.11

Three central themes were synthesised from this review: advocacy and acknowledgement, belief and behaviour, and conditions and consistency. Common across these themes were the HCPs’ emotional experience of caring for babies and their families, decision-making as a professional, putting the baby first, and professional satisfaction gained through FICare practices.

Education was deemed facilitative of behaviour and cultural change towards FICare practice.18 Culture is a common term in the implementation of FICare,27 but using the term culture to describe the collective attitudes and care behaviours in NICUs under-represents the meaning behind study participant contributions. To add meaningfully to previous research, the definition of culture must be understood as ‘the way people in the organisation behave and the attitudes and beliefs that inform those behaviours’28 that is, ‘the way we do things around here’.28 The finding of belief and behaviour contributes significantly to the discourse on the barriers and facilitators to the practice of FICare. Participants cite education (knowledge), guidelines (consensus) and opportunities to collaborate with ‘FICare’ units (experience) as facilitators of FICare practice. Knowledge, consensus and experience shape belief and culture.29

The theme conditions and consistency describe working conditions conducive to practising FICare. Modernisation of the physical NICU environment is important to facilitate FICare and will take time and substantial funding. Despite the challenges of physical conditions, HCPs viewed strong leadership and optimisation of the multidisciplinary team as facilitators of FICare, increasing nursing time to practise FICare more often and consistently.

Views on parental involvement in FICare varied widely, from fervent support to extreme reservations. Unit policies emerged as a remedy for inconsistent practices, mitigating the influence of individual HCPs’ preferences.

Implications

Despite general acknowledgement of the value of FICare, HCPs expressed frustration and personal challenges practising it. There was a strong sense that education, which influences belief and behaviour towards FICare, could resolve internal conflicts and promote consistency. However, all FICare implementation strategies use education, implying that current educational programmes are insufficient to change practice. Culture and an open mindset of HCPs are important aspects of FICare implementation, yet educational programmes may have limited ability to influence these. There is a paucity of information on how to make change at the individual level in a neonatal unit, which implies that a review of current FICare education models is required.

Study strengths and limitations

This systematic review was conducted using PRISMA guidelines.12 Major strengths include a comprehensive literature search using a rigorous search strategy and multiple databases. Double screening of titles and full-text search results added to the rigour of this study. The search strategy was informed by the research protocol registered in the PROSPERO database with clear inclusion/exclusion criteria and reviewed by an information scientist at Newcastle University. Following piloting the search strategy using the electronic journals database MEDLINE, the strategy was refined and retested to ensure all relevant literature was captured. Throughout this process, theme development was tempered by reflexivity and assessment of positionality.

This study also had some limitations. Parental perspectives are an important component in FICare practices, but their inclusion was beyond the scope of this review. Inconsistent terminology of key elements of FICare may compromise the specificity of some included data.

Conclusion

With advances in medical practice, increasingly immature infants now survive. Interactions between neonatal staff and parents have evolved from professional and patient to partnership over longer durations of hospitalisation. This review highlights the lack of educational and emotional support neonatal staff receive to practise FICare in this new paradigm, which should be the focus of further research.

The philosophy of FICare is evidence-based and appropriately ambitious within neonatal care. Its role in moderating the noxious neonatal environment in conjunction with developmental care is well understood. It has been widely reported that evidence-based practices take on average 17 years to be incorporated into routine general practice.30 It is important to understand the accelerators or impediments to the implementation of FICare to reduce the delay to consistent practise of FICare and STSC.
