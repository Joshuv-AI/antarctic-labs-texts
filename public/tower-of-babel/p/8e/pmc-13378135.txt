Decoding the role of transcriptomic clocks in the human prefrontal cortex
Authors: José J Martínez-Magaña, Anna HC Vlot, Kyle A Sullivan, Daniel A Jacobson, John H Krystal, Matthew J Girgenti, Diana L Núnez-Ríos, Sheila T Nagamatsu, Diego E Andrade-Brito, Jean Merlet, Alice Townsend, Alana Wells, Christiane Alvarez, Matthew Lane, Paul E Holtzheimer, Traumatic Stress Brain Research Group, Consuelo Walss-Bass, Janitza L Montalvo-Ortiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAging is characterized by interindividual variability quantified through aging clocks, informed by biological data and are linked to diverse health outcomes. In this study, we comprehensively profiled several transcriptomic clocks, characterize their stochastic components, compare their predictive accuracy, perform associations with mental disorders, and elucidate their functional implications in three human prefrontal cortex (PFC) datasets. Our analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in transcriptomic age prediction across different clock signatures. Notably, deep learning models capture a greater proportion of stochastic variation compared to linear methods, suggesting they may be sensitive to non-deterministic components. We identified a consistent relationship between transcriptomic age and alcohol use. Using network analysis, we identified convergent biological mechanisms across different clocks despite limited gene-level overlaps, specifically, immune-related signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling. Our findings reveal associations between psychiatric traits and identify convergent biological pathways and networks in the human PFC that may influence aging and health risks.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Molecular biology, Neuroscience, Molecular neuroscience, Bioinformatics, Computational bioinformatics, Genomic analysis

Introduction

Aging is a complex biological process that progresses at heterogeneous rates among individuals. This variability can be quantitatively assessed as a proxy for biological aging, enabling the development of aging clocks.1,2,3 Aging clocks are machine learning models that identify patterns in molecular data from different tissues or body fluids, which can be mathematically trained to estimate the biological age of other samples with the same molecular profiles.2 These clocks are designed to predict several aspects of the aging process, including cellular senescence, chronological age, and mortality4,5; all of which serve as proxies of biological age. Aging clocks are an excellent predictor of mortality and lifespan,6,7 as well as disease risk.8

Among the most widely studied aging clocks are epigenetic clocks,9,10 which leverage DNA methylation at CpG sites to estimate biological age. Emerging evidence, including our own work, identified a link between epigenetic clocks and psychiatric disorders.11,12,13,14 While epigenetic clocks remain the most extensively characterized, other molecular features are also investigated. Notably, RNA expression is associated with age-dependent biological alterations,15,16 that enable the development of transcriptomic clocks.17,18 These transcriptomic clocks, although less widely studied, have shown associations with age-related disorders.16 Two principal approaches have been applied to develop transcriptomic clocks. The tissue-specific transcriptomic age based on a set of coefficients derived from a fixed number of genes, trained using a pre-trained elastic net regression model. In contrast, the knowledge-primed artificial neural network clock (KPANN) integrates RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data with the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) hallmark gene set collection to train a neural network for transcriptomic age estimation. Neural networks consist of multiple layers (neurons), each contributing to outcome prediction through its activation, which allows the model to dynamically select sets of genes as predictors of chronological age. These deep learning models are particularly adept at capturing complex, nonlinear relationships,19 which traditional linear models, such as elastic net, may overlook. Recent studies have demonstrated that deep learning approaches can effectively model these intricate interactions, enhancing the accuracy of age prediction and providing deeper insights into the biological processes underlying aging.18

The human prefrontal cortex (PFC) and other brain regions undergo molecular, structural, and functional changes during aging.20 Structural age-related alterations in the PFC in older individuals are well established by neuroimaging studies.21,22,23 A meta-analysis of 3,880 individuals revealed that older individuals exhibit a reduction in the activation of subcortical and cortical brain regions compared to young adults, as measured by functional magnetic resonance imaging during cognitive tasks.24 Molecular studies evaluating brain aging have found that the PFC may age faster than other brain regions. For example, increased epigenetic age in the PFC is observed compared to the cerebellum.25,26 Furthermore, accelerated epigenetic age in peripheral tissue has been linked to reduced cortical thickness.27,28 Recent work on aging clocks has shown that those trained in peripheral tissues do not have the same accuracy as those trained in the brain,29 with brain-trained clocks exhibiting better accuracy for chronological age prediction.30 Although some studies have evaluated epigenetic clocks in the brain, very few have examined transcriptomic clocks. Evaluating transcriptomic clocks is important given recent evidence showing age-associated transcriptomic changes in the PFC involving, for example, gene dysregulation of synaptic genes.31,32,33,34,35

Recent advances in artificial intelligence algorithms have demonstrated a superior prediction capability of biological age.36,37 However, the evaluation of different transcriptomic clocks in the brain remains underexplored. Even when these clocks successfully predict biological or chronological age, the biological implications of the included genes in the models are still lacking, emphasizing the need for a more detailed characterization. The present study aimed to comprehensively profile transcriptomic clocks (RNAAgeCalc and KPANN), characterize their stochasticity, compare their prediction with the state-of-the-art epigenetic clocks, and characterize their functional impact in the human PFC using system biology approaches from three different datasets of human postmortem brain samples. Our investigation reveals different patterns of associations of the transcriptomic clocks among psychiatric traits, including an association with alcohol use. By leveraging the learning process of the deep learning algorithm, we comprehensively characterized potential stochastic variations of the transcriptomic clocks. Applying systems biology approaches, we prioritized genes that may play a significant role in aging within the human PFC and identified highly connected modules pointing to inflammaging as one of the variations captured by transcriptomic clocks. In summary, our study offers insights into the enhanced predictive capabilities of transcriptomic clocks, their associations with psychiatric disorders, and the identification of key genes that influence aging in the human PFC.

Results

Performance of the transcriptomic clocks in predicting age

We estimated PFC transcriptomic age using eight different signatures included in RNAAgeCalc17 (DESeq2, Pearson, Dev, deMalgahaes, GenAge, GTExAge, Peters, and All) and KPANN18 across two brain banks: the VABB38 and UTHealth.39 For comparison, we also profiled the transcriptomic age in a public PFC dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accession number: GSE10255640,41), which has a similar sample size. The transcriptomic clocks exhibited substantial variability in their Pearson correlations with chronological age across the datasets (Figure S1). In the VABB cohort, nearly all the transcriptomic clocks, except for the deMalgahaes signature, showed significant correlation with chronological age, with correlations ranging from 0.20 to 0.95. The KPANN demonstrated the highest correlation (r = 0.95, p = 3.8×10−280) of any of the RNAAgeCalc signatures. In the UTHealth brain bank, only three clocks, GTExAge and Peters (both from RNAAgeCalc), and KPANN, showed significant correlations, with the KPANN again showing the highest correlation (r = 0.86, p = 1.8×10−27). In the GSE102556 dataset, GTExAge, Pearson, and Peters clocks demonstrated significant correlations, with KPANN remaining the strongest predictor (r = 0.81, p = 1.1 × 10−23).

We evaluated prediction error using the root-mean-square error (RMSE), which measures the differences between predicted values and observed values42 (Figure 1A). Except for KPANN in the VABB cohort, all models showed high RMSE. For the UTHealth dataset, all the clocks showed higher RMSE compared to the other datasets, suggesting that transcriptomic clocks display high variability in predictive performance across cohorts.

To identify whether transcriptomic clocks exhibited similar patterns, we applied a hierarchical clustering algorithm to the correlation coefficients between clocks. KPANN and GTexAge were the most closely related in their correlation profiles with chronological age (Figure 1B). Comparing across datasets, KPANN consistently showed lower correlations with clocks across PFC cohorts (Figure 1C).

Given that KPANN requires retraining the neural network in the target dataset, a step that could promote overfitting, we evaluated differences in prediction between the training and testing sets (Figure 1C). The training datasets generally display higher correlation than the testing sets, except in the VABB, where the training and testing sets had almost the same correlation. Nevertheless, KPANN performance in the testing set varied for UTHealth and the GSE102556, with UTHealth showing no correlation. These results suggest that retraining may lead to overfitting in deep learning-based clocks.

Performance of the same set of genes in predicting transcriptomic age

Given that the KPANN (trained via deep learning) had a different set of genes than the RNAAgeCalc clocks (trained via elastic net), we evaluated the predictive capacity of the same set of genes by retraining two transcriptomic clocks using the VABB and testing them in the UTHealth and GSE102556. We applied both an elastic net model and a Bayesian-optimized neural network model using the same set of 279 genes (Table S1; the coefficients for this model can be found in the same table). Our neural network clock exhibited a higher RMSE compared to our elastic net-trained clock (Figure 2A). Consistent with the results of KPANN and RNAAgeCalc, UTHealth PFC samples also displayed higher RMSE in both retrained clocks. Both models correlated with chronological age (Figure 2B), with elastic net showing higher correlations consistently across datasets (Figure 2C), surpassing those of RNAAgeCalc clocks trained with elastic net. The deep learning clock demonstrated a moderate, significant correlation (r = 0.59, p = 1.8 × 10−10) only in GSE102556.

Performance of transcriptomic clocks in predicting delta of age

Delta-age, defined as the difference between transcriptomic age and chronological age, provides a measure of accelerated or decelerated brain aging.43 Positive delta-age indicates accelerated aging, whereas negative delta-age indicates decelerated aging. We observed substantial variability in the number of individuals classified as accelerated or decelerated across cohorts and clocks (Figure S2A). Notably, KPANN consistently identified some of the highest numbers of individuals with decelerated aging. Concordance in directional classification varied across clocks (Figure S2B). Retraining transcriptomic clocks on VABB resulted in more consistent classification of PFC samples (Figure S2C).

We also analyzed the distribution of transcriptomic age stratified by its discordant or concordant effect (e.g., increased/decreased or decreased/increased, etc.) on pairwise comparisons of transcriptomic clocks. We found that the density of samples with a concordant decrease in delta-age always clustered in advanced ages for all clocks (Figure S3A). Additionally, we observed that samples showing a concordant increase in transcriptomic age had lower ages. Similar patterns were observed for both elastic net and deep learning clocks retrained on VABB and tested in UTHealth and GSE102556 (Figure S3B), suggesting comparable predictive accuracy at advanced ages, with potential differences at younger ages.

Stochastic effects of the transcriptomic clocks

Theories of aging suggest that the aging process may involve both stochastic and deterministic components.44 Methods have been developed to quantify stochasticity in aging clocks, particularly using epigenetic clocks.45,46 Building on this framework, we quantified the stochastic component of transcriptomic clocks. To do so, we retrained both the elastic net (coefficients of this model are in Table S2) and deep learning models using the same gene sets as in our previous analyses, but with a simulated random dataset to generate a stochastic version of each clock. We then evaluated correlations between the stochastic clocks and their corresponding deterministic counterparts in the UTHealth and GSE102556 datasets. The VABB dataset was excluded from this analysis, as it had been used to retrain the deterministic clocks. We observed that stochastic clocks were highly correlated with one another (Figure 3A) and showed moderate correlations with chronological age. Both stochastic and deterministic components of the transcriptomic clocks were significantly associated with chronological age (Figure 3B), except for the deterministic deep learning model in the UTHealth dataset. Notably, the stochastic clocks consistently exhibited lower correlations with chronological age than their deterministic counterparts, suggesting that both components contribute to predicting transcriptomic age, but stochasticity reduces predictive precision.

To quantify the relative contribution of stochastic variability, we calculated the ratio of variance explained (RR2) between the stochastic clock (R2 stochastic) and the deterministic clock (R2 deterministic) (Figure 3C). We found that RR2 was higher for the deep learning models than for Elastic Net models, indicating that the deep learning approach captures a greater proportion of stochastic variability in transcriptomic age. Capturing the stochastic component of transcriptomic age may provide insights into individual variability in molecular aging, which may not be evident from deterministic measures alone.

Comparison of transcriptomic clocks and epigenetic clocks

Epigenetic clocks have been the current state of the art for biological age estimation, and we compared the performance of transcriptomic clocks with that of epigenetic clocks. In a subset of PFC samples with both epigenetic and RNA-seq data from the UTHealth cohort, we profiled PhenoAge,6 Zhang201947 Horvath epigenetic clocks,48 and their stochastic counterparts,46 as well as the causal, damaging, and adaptive epigenetic clocks.49 In parallel, we included our retrained transcriptomic clock models, their stochastic versions, the KPANN, and the eight RNAAgeCalc clocks, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the relative relationships and patterns of aging signatures across both transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks. Hierarchical clustering of these clocks revealed that KPANN clustered closely with our deep learning-trained model (Figure S4), showing that even though trained under a different neural network architecture, similar patterns of the age-associated signatures could be captured by deep learning models. Our restrained and stochastic transcriptomic clocks clustered together with the stochastic Zhang2019 and the DamAge clock, suggesting that stochastic components of both transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks capture overlapping aspects of biological aging. The Horvath, cortical, and Zhang outperformed any transcriptomic clock, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.92 to 0.95. This analysis allowed us to directly compare transcriptomic and epigenetic aging measures within the same individuals, providing insights into the convergence and divergence of aging signatures across molecular layers.

Associations of transcriptomic age with psychiatric-related phenotypes

We analyzed relationships between transcriptomic age and psychiatric-related traits in the VABB and UTHealth datasets. Psychiatric traits evaluated included major depressive disorder (MDD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), opioid use disorder (OUD), opioid use, alcohol use, tobacco use, hallucinogen use, amphetamine use, cocaine use, polysubstance use, lifetime use of mental health medications (antidepressants, antipsychotics, and anticonvulsants), and different causes of death (including suicide, death by opioid overdose, trauma, and death by accidents). We performed stepwise multivariate regression analysis including chronological age, sex, RNA integrity number (RIN), postmortem interval (PMI), cell-type proportions, and two surrogate variables as covariates.

In the VABB cohort, after multiple testing correction, we identified several clock-specific associations (Table S2). Most notably, alcohol use was significantly associated with two transcriptomic clocks: GTExAge (beta = −0.69, p = 8.23 × 10−6) and deMalgahaes (beta = −0.46, p = 1.40 × 10−4) (Figure 4A). Additional associations included the DESeq clock with polysubstance use (beta = −0.35, p = 6.60 × 10−6), deMalgalhaes with lifetime antidepressant use (beta = −0.43, p = 4.63 × 10−4), and GenAge with opioid intoxication (beta = −0.54, p = 2.01 × 10−5) and MDD (beta = 0.64, p = 3.18 × 10−5). Additionally, we saw a significant effect with the Dev clock with amphetamine and hallucinogen use, nevertheless the effect sizes were very close to cero (beta <0.01).

To account for potential biases and evaluate whether associations might be confounded by chronological age, we performed parallel regression analyses using chronological age as the outcome with the same psychiatric predictors. This revealed significant associations between chronological age and amphetamine use, PTSD, and lifetime antipsychotic use, but notably, alcohol use was not significantly associated with chronological age, suggesting that the transcriptomic age associations represent biology beyond age-related confounding.

We conducted additional sensitivity analyses by including the four different PFC subregions from the VA dataset as covariates and investigating non-linear associations by including an age-squared term in the regression models (Table S3). When modeling age-squared, alcohol use remained significantly associated with the same two clocks, GTExAge (beta = −0.48, p = 9.13 × 10-05) and deMalgahaes (beta = −0.74, p = 5.30 × 10-07), after multiple testing corrections. We also identified additional associations with lifetime mental health medications that were not evident in linear models, including Peters and All transcriptomic clock signature with lifetime anticonvulsant use (beta = −0.46, p = 2.71 × 10−5, beta = −0.36, p = 1.32 × 10−4), and deMalgahaes with lifetime antipsychotic use (beta = 0.51, p = 1.25 × 10−4). These results suggest that the effects of psychiatric traits on transcriptomic age may be PFC subregion-specific and may exhibit nonlinear relationships with age.

In the UTHealth cohort, we identified several nominal associations: deMalgahaes and Peters clocks with suicide (beta = −1.09, p = 0.01, beta = −0.77, p = 0.02), Peters and All clocks with OUD (beta = −0.31, p = 0.05, beta = −0.38, p = 0.03) and KPANN with cannabis use, death by overdose, and MDD (beta = −0.74, p = 0.02, beta = −0.77, p = 0.004, beta = 0.65, p = 0.03) (Figure 4B); however, none remained significant after multiple testing correction (Table S4). Our retrained elastic net model showed a nominal association with cannabis use (p = 0.0187) that was not significant after multiple testing corrections. Neither stochastic clock version showed significant associations with psychiatric traits in the UTHealth cohort.

Functional interpretation of genes shared across PFC transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks

Biological clocks, select features that mathematically combine are excellent predictors of chronological age. However, there is a limited understanding of the biology behind these selected features or the mechanisms they are involved in.50,51 To investigate the potential functional implications of the transcriptomic clocks, we conducted the following analyses: (1) gene overlap of the transcriptomic clock signatures, (2) co-expression analysis, and (3) gene overlap with epigenetic clocks and multi-layer network clustering with cell-type-specific resolution.

We first analyzed the overlap between the transcriptomic clock signatures and identified that across clocks, there are 5,719 genes, of which 4,522 (79.10%) are unique to one transcriptomic clock signature (Table S5). Only two genes (AEBP1 and CDKN2A) were common across all the transcriptomic clock signatures—most of the unique genes were found in the KPANN clock. Suggesting that different combinations of genes are capable of predicting chronological age.

Since gene expression patterns correlate among genes, building gene co-expression networks and grouping highly correlated genes in networks may identify genes with common biological functions. Gene co-expression networks may be used to prioritize relevant genes or to infer potential transcriptional regulatory programs.52 We used several systems biology approaches to build a co-expression network of the overlapped genes between the transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks, using the gene expression levels of the three included datasets (VABB, UTHealth, and GSE102556). We identified 696 genes with connectivity across the datasets (Table S6). After identifying correlated genes, we build a co-expression network, and using random walk across each of the networks, we identified 35 highly connected genes (SLC4A1, LINC01134, INTS11, TNFRSF14, LRRC47, WRAP73, ACAP3, CCDC27, ZMYND10, TAS1R3, VPS50, PLCH2, PRKAR2B, KCNAB2, MRPL20, ACSM3, RNF223, TNFRSF18, LINC01128, SDF4, ATAD3C, GPR153, TP73, MXRA8, B3GALT6, NIPAL3, PUSL1, ANKIB1, ALS2, CD38, UPF1, CALML6, MCUB, RPAP3, and KLHL13). This set included TP73, a gene reported in the GenAge dataset as being essential for neurodegeneration in normal aging and Alzheimer’s disease.53

Epigenetic clocks are the most commonly studied biological clocks. To analyze potential shared biological pathways between these two different omic clocks, we examined whether the genes of the transcriptomic clocks overlap with those of the epigenetic clocks (Horvarth, PhenoAge, Zhang, and Causal clocks). We identified low overlap between the transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks, with 4,917 genes unique to the transcriptomic clocks and 1,339 genes unique to the epigenetic clocks, totaling 7,058 unique genes across the different clocks. We then performed a multi-layer network clustering analysis using the MENTOR-GRIN-MENTOR54,55 strategy of all the genes in the biological clocks (see STAR Methods). Strikingly, while the gene-gene overlap between different epigenetic and transcriptomic clocks is low, our MENTOR-GRIN-MENTOR analysis (see STAR Methods) of the union of all genes showed that mechanistic overlap between identified genes remains high as illustrated by their co-clustering into mechanistic modules spanning across diverse biological mechanisms (Figure S5 and Table S7).

To identify more detailed mechanistic relationships between genes associated with omic clocks, we devised a set of 285 genes considered high-confidence by their occurrence in three or more of the transcriptomic, epigenetic, or co-expression clocks (Table S7). Each gene in this set is present in at least one of the RNAAgeCalc clocks, and 199 genes (70%) are present in an RNAAgeCalc clock and at least one other clock. Functional enrichment analysis of this gene set (using gProfiler56) identified enrichments for cell proliferation and differentiation, response to stress, signaling and pathways, metabolism, immune processes, and cell death and survival (Table S8).

To identify more granular, mechanistic processes among this set of high-confidence genes, we applied MENTOR54 to partition the gene set into modules of mechanistically related genes based on network connectivity in a PFC-focused multiplex network (see STAR Methods, Table S8). The heatmap tracks in the dendrogram illustrate whether each gene is found in a given class of aging clocks (see STAR Methods for aging clock classifications). In this visualization, we observe that most modules (19/24) contain genes from both transcriptomic and epigenomic clocks (Figure 5A), although the contribution of genes from transcriptomic clocks is more significant. In addition, several modules (C1, C4, C14, C21, and C22) consist entirely of genes identified in transcriptomic clocks (Figure 5A). Only 4 modules, modules C12 enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling genes, C18 enriched for response to toxic substance, steroid hormone/lipid metabolic process, C23 representing anterograde dendritic transport, and C24; contain genes originating from every clock class of clocks. Suggesting that these modules may have convergent epigenetic and gene expression mechanisms in the regulation of aging.

To characterize the potential functional roles of genes in each of these modules, we performed a module-level functional enrichment analysis. Broadly, functional enrichment analysis of the 24 MENTOR modules revealed module-specific enrichments across of diverse biological processes and structures, including RNA modification (module C2), GPCR signaling (module C3), cell proliferation and survival (modules C8-11), the ECM (module C12), transmembrane transport (module C13), translation (module C14), EGFR and downstream MAPK and PI3/Akt signaling (module C20), and dendritic transport (module C23) (Figure 5A and Table S8). In addition, immune-related processes, cell proliferation and survival, and detoxification/metabolism/homeostasis-related terms were observed across several modules (modules C5-7 and C9-10, C8-11, and C15-18, respectively).

Finally, to reveal potential cell-type-specific functional roles, we perform a shortest path analysis between the genes in each module within each layer of the PFC-focus multiplex network. This revealed several modules for which a proportion of direct gene-gene edges are found in cell-type-specific network layers. For example, for glial subpopulations, genes from module C10, enriched for cell proliferation and regulation of immune processes, and C16, which includes the astrocyte marker GFAP57; and the AEBP158 and AQP159 which are associated with astrocytes in disease, and to a much lesser extent C12 (ECM), show an overrepresentation of shortest paths in astrocytes (Figure 5B). Genes from module C10 also show some overrepresentation in microglia, with stronger overrepresentation observed for genes from module C5 (enriched for innate immune-related processes). Finally, genes from module C4, which includes the GDF15 gene, which is a known biomarker of aging,60 are overrepresented in oligodendrocytes. In terms of neuronal subpopulation, the strongest overrepresentation is observed for genes from module 11 in CUX2-expressing layer II/III principal excitatory neurons and genes from module 24 in SST-expressing MGE-derived inhibitory interneurons and TLE4-expressing layer V/VI principal excitatory neurons.

Discussion

Here, we conducted a comprehensive study of transcriptomic clocks across multiple datasets (VABB, UTHealth, and GSE102556) of human postmortem PFC. We observed that (1) transcriptomic age prediction showed considerable heterogeneity; (2) the KPANN remained the strongest predictor but showed the weakest correlations with other clocks, while also displaying evidence of overfitting; (3) retraining on the same gene sets revealed superior performance of elastic net compared to neural network approaches; (4) delta-age estimates were consistent at both younger and older ages; (5) deep learning approaches appeared to capture stochastic effects; (6) transcriptomic clocks uncovered consistent associations with alcohol use; and (7) multilayer network analysis highlighted immune-related signaling and ECM remodeling as shared pathways across clocks, with impaired autophagy and proteostasis emerging as a potential mechanistic link between them.

We observed heterogeneity in the predictive capacity of transcriptomic clocks. While all clocks could predict age, they displayed different correlation patterns with chronological age. These differences may arise from the low gene overlap between clocks, reflecting distinct feature selection in each model. A similar phenomenon is seen in epigenetic clocks,61 where diverse sets of CpG sites are used to predict age; many achieve comparable accuracy.50 These findings underscore that interpreting transcriptomic age as a surrogate for biological aging should be done in the context of the specific clock used, as different clocks may capture unique associations that others do not.50,62

The KPANN, trained with deep learning techniques, outperforms RNAAgeCalc in transcriptomic age prediction; however, it also exhibited a stronger tendency toward overfitting. Prior work has highlighted the potential advantages of deep learning in age prediction, particularly in the context of epigenetic clocks.36,37 Because deep learning can capture nonlinear relationships that linear approaches may overlook,63 it holds promise for improving predictive accuracy. Nevertheless, whether clocks trained with such methods serve as reliable indicators of biological age remains a matter of debate. For example, in the epigenetic clock literature, higher predictive accuracy for chronological age does not necessarily translate into better prediction of biological aging processes.47 In addition, we observed that retraining using the same set of genes with linear models (elastic net) outperformed the deep learning approach. Moreover, their susceptibility to overfitting, as observed in our analysis, raises concerns about the utility of these clocks as robust biomarkers of aging.

Our findings showed that the KPANN clock had the highest correlation with chronological age yet demonstrated fewer psychiatric trait association aligns with Zhang et al.,47 who demonstrated that improved precision in predicting chronological age may paradoxically limit utility as a biomarker of biological age. Clocks optimized for chronological age prediction may become less sensitive to biological perturbations and environmental factors that accelerate or decelerate aging. This interpretation explains why GTExAge and deMagalhaes RNAAgeCalc clocks, despite lower correlations with chronological age (r = 0.47–0.69), showed consistent associations with alcohol use. These findings suggest a potential trade-off between precision in chronological age estimation and sensitivity to biologically meaningful variation related to environmental exposures and disease states, indicating that different clocks may have complementary value for understanding molecular aging.

We observed that most transcriptomic clocks exhibited greater concordance at both younger and older chronological ages. This pattern may reflect the nonlinear dynamics of aging processes, in which distinct biological mechanisms emerge at advanced ages compared to earlier stages of the lifespan.64 Deviations at older ages may also arise from limitations in model training, as most clocks are optimized within midlife ranges. Notably, a previous study demonstrated that the association between body mass index and epigenetic age was evident in younger individuals but absent in nonagenarians.65 The estimation of biological age in advanced age groups remains insufficiently characterized. Studies with exceptionally aged individuals show that their multiomic footprint may differ from that of those at other ages,66 underscoring the need for future studies to elucidate how nonlinear trajectories of molecular aging contribute to heterogeneity across the lifespan.

Recent theoretical frameworks have questioned whether clock features represent functionally important loci or simply sites accumulating stochastic variation,67,68 with some proposing that current clocks may be purely correlative without causal roles in aging.45,46 To investigate these concepts, we implemented simulation-based stochastic modeling of our transcriptomic clocks. Our analysis revealed that stochastic clocks showed moderate but significant correlations with chronological age though consistently lower than deterministic counterparts, suggesting both components contribute to age prediction. Notably, deep learning models captured greater stochastic variability (higher RR2 values) compared to elastic net models, potentially due to inherent stochasticity in optimizers like Adam.69 Hierarchical clustering in the UTHealth cohort revealed that deep learning transcriptomic clocks aligned more closely with adaptive and damage-related epigenetic clocks,47,49 while stochastic transcriptomic models clustered with causal/damage-driven signatures.70 These findings suggest nonlinear approaches may be particularly suited to modeling stochastic damage accumulation. However, we acknowledge that our study, like most aging clock research, cannot establish causality.

Our association analysis of omic clocks and psychiatric traits consistently identified two transcriptomic age signatures (GTEx and de Magalhaes) that were negatively associated with alcohol use. Prior studies have reported links between biological age and alcohol consumption, primarily through analyses of epigenetic clocks in blood. Most of these studies observed accelerated epigenetic aging associated with alcohol use.71,72,73 In contrast, the direction of effects in blood often diverges from what we observed in the brain, suggesting possible tissue-specific differences. Supporting this notion, recent brain-based studies have pointed to heterogeneous biological age estimates. One study reported a decrease in the Horvath clock,74 while another, comparing blood and brain, found no significant associations.75 Additionally, another research examining alcohol dependence, a more severe form of alcohol consumption, found also evidence of reduced in age acceleration.76 Similarly, a recent investigation of causal clocks reported no association with alcohol use.77 Emerging evidence suggests that some effects of alcohol on biological age may be reversible,78 opening the door to explaining the heterogeneous potential impact identified in several studies. We further observed a consistent positive relationship between transcriptomic clock measures and MDD, corroborating previous reports.79,80 Additionally, negative associations with multiple opioid-related phenotypes were detected, in line with earlier findings.81 Together, our findings extend the existing literature by suggesting a decrease in biological aging associated with alcohol consumption in the PFC and increase in MDD; however, further research is needed to disentangle the heterogeneity driven by alcohol consumption.

We applied the multiplex network-based tools MENTOR54 and GRIN55 to identify functional relationships among genes across aging clocks. In these tools, the multiplex network is used as a representation of the relevant, known human biology. Therefore, we included predictive expression networks (PENs) constructed from single-nucleotide RNA-seq data of the human PFC, allowing for a PFC-centric interpretation of gene-gene relationships. An analysis of all genes revealed that, despite limited gene-by-gene overlap between transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks, genes from each clock are involved in similar biological mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage repair, chromatin remodeling, stress and immune response, and neuronal signaling. Similarly, a MENTOR analysis of high-confidence age-associated genes identified in at least three individual aging clocks revealed the co-occurrence of epigenetic and transcriptomic clock-derived genes within modules across various biological mechanisms. Taken together, these results illustrate that despite minimal gene-level overlaps, transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks converge on a set of aging-relevant biological processes.

This module-level analysis highlighted immune-related signaling and ECM remodeling as prominent themes across clocks, with impaired autophagy and proteostasis emerging as a potential mechanistic link between the two. The presence of several immune-related modules (C5-7, C9-10) suggests a potential representation of inflammaging.82 For example, the microglia-enriched module C5 includes C1QA, which has been shown to increase dramatically during normal aging,83 microglial marker gene ITGAM,84 recently proposed as a Parkinson’s disease biomarker,85 and MRS4A6A, which has been linked to Alzheimer’s disease and brain atrophy,86,87 reflecting microglial contributions to immune remodeling in brain aging. Likewise, from module C6, CX3CL1 has been implicated in fibrosis, aging,88 and age-dependent neurodegeneration,89 IFITM3 modulates amyloid precursor protein (APP) cleavage in Alzheimer’s disease,90 IL10RB mediates decreased antibody production in response to immune challenges in rodent models,91 and the death receptors FAS and TNFRSF10B have been implicated in the regulation of apoptosis and cellular senescence during aging.92,93,94 Module C7 also included several inflammaging-related genes, including two major histocompatibility class II genes (HLA-DRA and HLA-DRB1) that increase expression during aging,95 HLA-B, which decreases during aging,96 and HSPA1A (associated with transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks), which is linked to aging-induced increases in oxidative stress.97 Genes in proliferation-related modules (C9-C10) further emphasize the role of senescence and autophagy in immune regulation during aging, including the canonical senescence marker CDKN2A98 and the DNA-damage response gene PARP1, which is involved in inflammation and autophagy regulation.99 Module C9 also included 2 Krüppel-like factor transcription factors linked to aging processes100: KLF2 linked to blood-brain barrier integrity101 and KLF4, which can regulate the autophagy gene SQSTM1102 whose expression decreases with age.103 Finally, module 12 introduces a potential link between (neuro)inflammation, senescence, and ECM-remodeling processes, such as age-associated fibrosis and ECM stiffening.104 Namely, obesity and aging-induced excess of the cytokine TGF-β, encoded by TGFB1, has been linked to diabetic development,105 LGALS1 has been implicated in age-associated microglia activation,106 the cell adhesion gene IGFBP7 has been linked to senescence in aging.107 Finally, the overrepresentation of these modules in glial subpopulations suggests that glial subpopulations may mediate these changes in the PFC during aging. Our network analyses provide evidence that clock genes converge on biologically plausible aging pathways, suggesting they may represent more than purely correlative markers, though whether they play causal roles requires experimental validation.

The limited gene overlap across transcriptomic clocks (only 2 genes common to all signatures) yet comparable predictive accuracy highlights an important principle in aging clock development. As noted by Porter et al.,50 the high degree of correlation among genes means that multiple different feature sets can achieve similar predictive performance. Our retraining analyses support this concept: our 279-gene elastic net model achieved performance comparable to or exceeding original RNAAgeCalc clocks despite using an entirely different gene set. However, we observed that different clocks showed distinct associations with psychiatric traits, suggesting that while multiple gene sets, can predict chronological age accurately, they may capture different aspects of aging or be differentially sensitive to specific exposures. Our multiplex network analysis (MENTOR-GRIN) revealed that despite limited gene-level overlap between transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks, the selected genes converge on shared biological mechanisms including immune signaling, ECM remodeling, and impaired autophagy/proteostasis, demonstrating that correlated genes may represent different components of underlying pathways. These findings underscore that biological age estimation should be interpreted within the context of the specific clock used, as different clocks-though equally accurate at predicting chronological age-may reflect distinct molecular facets of aging.

In summary, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic clocks across multiple human postmortem PFC datasets. While epigenetic clocks remain the gold standard, transcriptomic clocks provide complementary insights into brain aging, particularly through their ability to capture nonlinear relationships and stochastic aging components. The identified associations with psychiatric traits like alcohol use and MDD, along with the convergence of diverse clock signatures on immune signaling, ECM remodeling, and proteostasis pathways, demonstrates that transcriptomic approaches reveal meaningful aging mechanisms. Our findings highlight specific molecular pathways in the PFC that warrant investigation as potential targets for interventions aimed at promoting healthy brain aging.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, KPANN requires splitting samples into training and testing sets, reducing statistical power for phenotypic association analyses. Future studies could address this through independent replication cohorts where clocks are trained in one dataset and associations tested in separate samples, cross-validation schemes that maximize use of available data while preventing over-fitting, or development of pre-trained, validated transcriptomic clocks that can be applied without retraining, similar to widely used epigenetic clocks, enabling analysis of all available samples for association testing. Second, while we included multiple datasets (VABB, UTHealth, and GSE102556), and our analyses included a larger sample size and more diverse clocks than similar recent work in transcriptomic clocks of the PFC,79 larger sample sizes would enable more powerful detection of clock-phenotype associations and reduce the risk of false negatives. Future studies could address this through: (1) collaborative multi-site consortia pooling postmortem brain samples across brain banks to achieve sample sizes of 1,000+ donors, similar to successful efforts in epigenetic research, (2) meta-analytic approaches combining effect sizes across independent cohorts to increase statistical power while accounting for dataset-specific heterogeneity, and (3) integration of transcriptomic data with other molecular layers (epigenetics, proteomics, metabolomics) within the same individuals to provide complementary aging signatures despite modest sample sizes. Third, our findings derive from U.S. cohorts, which may limit generalizability. Future research should prioritize recruitment of postmortem brain samples from diverse ancestral backgrounds and geographic regions to assess whether transcriptomic aging signatures and their associations with psychiatric traits are universal or population-specific, and evaluation of whether clocks trained on one population maintain accuracy when applied to others, similar to recent work showing regional dependence in neuroimaging-based brain age models.20 Finally, our analyses are correlative and observational; experimental studies are needed to establish causal relationships between identified genes, pathways, and aging processes.

Experimental model and study participant details

VA brain brank (VABB)

We included 546 human postmortem brain samples of 136 individuals from the National Center for PTSD Brain Bank previously analyzed.38 The cohort included individuals of self-reported European and African ethnicity, comprising 76 males and 60 females, with a mean age of 45.45 years (SD = 12.58). Psychiatric history and demographic information were obtained by psychological autopsies performed postmortem by next of kin. Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders version 4 (DSM-IV) criteria and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders (SCID-1) interviews were adapted for psychological autopsy and were used to define the diagnosis Two trained clinicians determined the psychiatric diagnosis by consensus diagnosis. All consents were acquired for the next of kin.

University of Texas health science center at Houston (UTHealth) brain collection

We included 123 postmortem human PFC tissue samples from the UTHealth Brain Collection, which has been previously published39 and is publicly available in GEO under the accession number GSE182321 The cohort included individuals of self-reported European (n = 73), African (n = 34), Hispanic (n = 14), and Asian (n = 2) ethnicity, comprising 94 males and 29 females, with a mean age of 45.84 years (SD = 14.72). This study was in collaboration with the Harris County Institute of Forensic Sciences, with approval from the institutional review board. For each donor, detailed demographic, medical, autopsy, and toxicology information was collected. Psychiatric phenotypes and substance use histories were systematically characterized through structured psychological autopsies conducted with next of kin, including assessment of depression, mania, psychosis, onset and type of drug use, smoking, alcohol use, and comorbidities. Final consensus diagnoses or non-psychiatric control were determined in accordance with DSM-5 criteria by an independent panel of three trained clinicians.

Sex-specific transcriptional signatures in human depression (GSE102556)

We utilized only the PFC transcriptomic data from individuals found in the GEO repository (accession number: GSE102556), which included both male and female samples.40,41 Brain tissue was obtained from the Douglas Bell Canada Brain Bank at the Douglas Mental Health Institute. The study included 48 individuals of European ancestry and French-Canadian descent, we included information from two cortical brain regions, the orbitofrontal cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, for a total of 96 brain samples. Subjects were required to have died suddenly without a prolonged agonal state, and males and females were group-matched for age, brain pH, and postmortem interval (PMI). The dataset was initially generated to characterize sex differences in MDD through differential expression and gene coexpression network analyses.

Method details

RNA sequencing

For the VABB brain bank, RNA was extracted from each brain region (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, subgenual prefrontal cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) using the RNeasy Mini kit with genomic DNA elimination, as described by the manufacturer (Qiagen). The RNA integrity number (RIN) and concentration were assessed using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Libraries were constructed using a SMARTer Stranded RNA-seq kit (Takara Bio). rRNA depletion was performed using a Ribozero Gold kit (Illumina). Samples were paired-end sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq4000 with a read length of 75 base pairs and targeting a depth of 50 million reads. Sequences in the FASTQ files were mapped to the human genome using STAR (v.2.5.3a)108 with the reference genome (release 79, GRCh38). We used featureCounts,109 implemented in the Rsubread package,110 to quantify transcripts using Ensembl’s gene transfer format (GTF) annotation file.

For the UTHealth brain bank, data were obtained from previously published work.39 Briefly, RNA was extracted from 50 mg of BA9 (dorso-lateral prefrontal cortex) tissue using RNeasy Plus Mini kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and RNA integrity number (RIN) was measured for RNA quality (Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). In total, 1 μg RNA per sample was used for mRNA library construction using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA). Paired-end sequencing reads (150 bp) were generated on an Illumina HiSeq2000 platform (Novogene Bioinformatics Institute, Chula Vista, CA). Reads were mapped to the GRCh38 genome using STAR (v.2.5.3a) with default parameters. The annotation file was downloaded from NCBI (GCF_000001405.39_GRCh38.p13.gtf). Gene and transcript counts were obtained using featureCounts109 to quantify the transcripts using Ensembl’s GTF annotation file.

DNA methylation analysis

In the UTHealth dataset, total DNA was isolated from PFC samples with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (cat. 69,504; Qiagen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated DNA samples were quantified on NanoDrop (Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA), and 500 ng of DNA from each sample was bisulfite-converted using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Then, DNA methylation of the samples was evaluated using the Infinium Human Methylation EPIC BeadChip (Illumina, 2020; San Diego, CA, USA), according to standard protocols. We performed quality control of the DNA methylation data and quantile normalization, using the default parameters in the ENMix package.111

Epigenetic clocks analyses

We estimate epigenetic age using ten different epigenetic clocks. We used the MethylCIPHER package to estimate Horvath,48 PhenoAge,6 cortical clock,29 and Zhang2019.47 We estimate the stochastic components of the Horvarth, PhenoAge, and Zhang2019 clocks using the scripts previously published.46 We also estimate the causal, damage and adaptive clocks (Ying clocks)49 using Biolearn.112

Transcriptomic clocks analyses

To estimate the transcriptomic age, we utilized several machine learning-based transcriptomic clocks, all the signatures in RNAAgeCalc,17 and a clock that employs knowledge-primed artificial neural networks (KPANN).18 The RNAAgeCalc clock is a set of models that predicts tissue-specific transcriptomic age based on a fixed set of coefficients for the genes, calculated from a pre-trained elastic net model. In this analysis, we used models trained on brain tissue (DESeq2, Pearson, Dev, de Magalhaes, GenAge, GTExAge, Peters, and All) from brain tissue. We used the RNAAgeCalc R package (version R21) and analyzed raw counts from the RNA sequencing data of all samples. The KPANN clock is a model that utilizes RNA-seq data as input and the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) hallmark gene set collection113 as input to train a neural network and estimate the transcriptomic age. Based on the activation of each neuron in the neural network, the model selects a different set of genes as predictors of chronological age. Raw gene-expression matrices were converted to z-scores per gene before model fitting. For the KPANN analysis, we randomly split the postmortem brain samples of each cohort into training (⅔ of the brain samples) and testing datasets (⅓ of the brain samples).

Retraining transcriptomic clocks

We retrained the transcriptomic clocks using the VABB dataset, given that it is our larger dataset. Given that different PFC samples could be from the same individuals, we used only unique samples for training the models and the rest of the PFC samples to test the performance of the models. First gene expression data were transformed to FPKM, and low-count genes were excluded from the analysis results if more than 30% of the samples had a count per million less than one. Prior to model training, we performed a gene-wise differential expression (DE) analysis to select predictors for the elastic-net models. For each gene, we fit a linear model, adjusting for age, sex, PMI, and RIN, using statsmodels in Python. From each fit, we extracted the two-sided p-value for the age term. p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and genes with FDR ≤0.05 were retained as differentially associated with age. These significant genes were passed as the input feature set to the elastic-net and neural network pipeline.

Elastic net age prediction

We standardized gene-expression matrices (FPKM) to z-scores prior to model fitting. Elastic net linear models were fitted using ElasticNetCV implemented in scikit-learn, which balances L1 and L2 regularization and is well-suited for high-dimensional, correlated predictors. Model tuning was performed with 5-fold cross-validation and we searched a mixing parameter (l1_ratio) over the grid [0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9], and the maximum number of iterations was set to 20,000 to ensure convergence. A fixed random seed and random state of 42 was used for reproducibility. We applied ElasticNetCV in an out-of-fold manner: for each cross-validation split, models were trained on the training folds and predictions generated for the held-out test fold. These out-of-fold predictions were concatenated to produce cross-validated predictions, which were then used to compute performance metrics.

Deep learning age prediction

We implemented a fully connected feedforward neural network using the Keras Sequential API, with the same set of genes as input features that were provided to the elastic net framework. To ensure reproducibility across runs, we fixed all global random seeds to 42, enabled deterministic operations where possible, and initialized model weights using the Glorot (Xavier) initializer with a fixed seed. Hyperparameter optimization was performed with Optuna, which employs a Bayesian optimization framework via a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler. We conducted 50 trials aimed at minimizing validation mean absolute error (MAE). The search space included: (1) number of hidden layers ranging from 1 to 4, (2) number of units per layer, (3) dropout rate applied after the hidden layers, and (4) learning rate sampled on a log scale between 1 × 10−4 and 1 × 10−2. Each trial instantiated and trained a model using the Adam optimizer with mean squared error as the loss function and MAE as an additional evaluation metric. Training employed early stopping based on validation MAE to prevent overfitting, with a maximum cap of 100 epochs.

Stochastic modeling of transcriptomic clocks

We modeled the stochastic component of our retrained transcriptomic clocks using simulation-based approaches. First, we performed a differential expression analysis of the genes included in the clocks by comparing individuals aged 20 and 60 years, adjusting for sex, PMI, and RNA integrity number (RIN). Effect sizes from this analysis were transformed into gene-specific means and standard deviations, which were then used to parameterize multivariate normal (MVN) distributions. This distribution has been previously applied to model gene expression.114,115,116 The MVN was constructed using the gene–gene correlation structure estimated from the VABB expression dataset and per-gene standard deviations to compute covariance matrices. To improve numerical stability, we added a small jitter term (1 × 10−3) and applied shrinkage to the covariance diagonal to reduce perfect correlations that could destabilize penalized regression. Synthetic samples were generated by random sampling from these MVN distributions, producing 2,000 simulated samples at ages 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 years. These simulated datasets were then analyzed using the same modeling pipelines described above, including elastic net regression and deep learning models, to predict chronological age.

Predictive age accuracy of transcriptomic clocks

To evaluate the predictive performance of transcriptomic clocks, we calculated pairwise Pearson correlations between transcriptomic, epigenetic, and chronological ages. Hierarchical clustering was then applied to the resulting correlation coefficients to identify patterns of similarity among the different clock predictions, as well as their relationships with chronological age. Prediction accuracy was further quantified using the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of each predicted age, computed using Python.

Estimating the delta of age

We calculated the delta of age (delta Age) as the difference between transcriptomic and chronological age (delta Age = transcriptomic age − chronological age). Samples with a transcriptomic age greater than chronological age (delta Age >0) were classified as having increased transcriptomic age, whereas samples with a transcriptomic age lower than chronological age (delta Age <0) were classified as having decreased transcriptomic age. To assess concordance between the delta-Age values across different transcriptomic clocks, we counted the number of samples showing consistent directional changes (i.e., increased or decreased transcriptomic age in both clocks) and the number of samples showing discordant results (i.e., increased in one clock but decreased in the other). All analyses were conducted in Python.

Association of transcriptomic age with psychiatric traits

To assess the association between all transcriptomic clocks and multiple psychiatric traits, we fitted multivariable regression models with transcriptomic age as the outcome. The models included chronological age, sex, PMI, RNA integrity number (RIN), and two-factor variables for hidden effects, as well as relative cell-type proportions, as fixed potential confounders. Relative cell-type proportions were estimated using the brain cell type–specific analysis implemented in the Brettigea package,117 and factor hidden effects were estimated using the RUVseq package.118 In a stepwise approach, we then added all psychiatric traits, including MDD, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), opioid misuse, cause of death (classified as overdose, traumatic injury, or other causes), tobacco use at the time of death, alcohol use at the time of death, amphetamine misuse, cocaine misuse, and mental health treatment medications.

As a sensitivity analysis, the regressions were repeated including an age-squared term to account for potential non-linear effects of age, and PFC brain region was included as an additional covariate. The addition of these terms allowed us to verify whether observed associations remained significant after accounting for non-linear age effects. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we examined the correlation between psychiatric traits that showed significant associations with chronological age to ensure that these associations were not driven by the covariance between psychiatric age and chronological age. All correlation analysis were performed in Python, and significance was determined using Bonferroni correction.

Association of epigenetic age with psychiatric traits

We assess the association between all epigenetic clocks and multiple psychiatric traits in the UThealth dataset. We fitted multivariable regression models with epigenetic age as the outcome. The models included chronological age, sex, postmortem interval (PMI), two-factor principal components using control probes included in the EPIC arrays to capture hidden effects, and relative cell-type proportions as fixed potential confounders. Relative cell-type proportions were estimated using FlowSorted.DLPFC.EPIC dataset as reference. In a stepwise approach, we then added all psychiatric traits, including MDD, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), opioid misuse, cause of death (classified as overdose, traumatic injury, or other causes), tobacco use at the time of death, alcohol use at the time of death, amphetamine misuse, cocaine misuse, and mental health treatment medications. All correlation analysis were performed in Python, and significance was determined using Bonferroni correction.

Gene annotation of epigenetic clock

To annotate the DNA methylation sites used by each epigenetic clock, we annotated the DNA methylation sites using the CpG sites of all the included epigenetic clocks (DNAmClockCortical, Horvath, PhenoAge, Zhang2019, StocH, StocP, StocZ, YingAdaptAge, YingCausAge, YingDamAge), to their closest genes using the IlluminaHumanMethylationEPICanno.ilm10b2.hg19 package.

Functional analyses

Coexpression analysis across PFC datasets

To investigate whether the genes included in the transcriptomic clocks exhibit co-expression patterns, we constructed pairwise gene–gene correlation matrices using PyNetCor119 in the three PFC datasets (VABB, UTHealth, and GSE102556). The analysis encompassed genes from both transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks, allowing a direct comparison of co-expression. We keep only gene pairs with an absolute correlation coefficient greater than 0.2. These filtered correlations were used to build dataset-specific co-expression networks. To characterize the topological properties of these networks, we performed independent random walk analyses in each dataset and then averaged the resulting random walk scores to obtain a measure of gene connectivity. Genes with random walk scores exceeding the 95th percentile of the distribution were classified as highly interconnected.

MENTOR network embedding of functional gene connections

To identify potential molecular mechanisms of genes from all aging clocks, we applied MENTOR (Multiplex Embedding of Networks for Team-based Omics Research)54 and GRIN.55 Briefly, MENTOR computes a rank-ordered vector for each gene in an input set to all other genes in a multiplex network using Random-Walk-with Restart (RWR). The pairwise Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ) between all genes in the input set is then used to construct a pairwise distance matrix, where each entry is min[1 - ρ, 1]. This distance matrix is then used to partition genes into functionally coherent gene modules of no more than N genes using complete-linkage agglomerative hierarchical subclustering. GRIN filters false-positive genes from an input gene set by using a multiplex network combined with RWR network traversal, which compares the interconnectivity of the input gene set to a null distribution generated from random gene sets of equivalent size.55 GRIN retains genes from the input set when these ranks depart from the null distribution ranks, leveraging RWR to identify likely false positive genes based on multiplex network interconnectivity. We first partitioned the total set of genes into modules of a maximum of 50 genes using MENTOR. Next, we eliminated any singleton genes and all genes from modules of two to four genes, and then filtered modules consisting of more than four genes using GRIN. Finally, we took the genes passing GRIN filtering and partitioned them into modules of no more than 20 genes using MENTOR. The resulting partitioning was then visualized as a partitioned polar dendrogram with corresponding heatmap tracks, where each heatmap track indicates whether a gene was observed in the transcriptomic KPANN clock (KPANN), one of the transcriptomic RNA age calculation clocks (All, deMagalhaes, DESeq2, Dev, GenAge, GTExAge, Pearson, Peters), the retrained transcriptomic clock, one of the co-expression clocks (Co-expressed, RWR, RWR_score_95th), one of the epigenetic clocks (DNAmClockCortical, Horvath1, PhenoAge, Zhang2019), one of the stochastic epigenetic clocks (StocH, StocP, StocZ), or one the causal epigenetic clocks (YingAdaptAge, YingCausAge, YingDamAge).

We also applied MENTOR to identify potential molecular mechanisms of high-confidence genes, which are defined as those genes shared across three or more of the transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks. For this analysis, we also utilized the Shortest Paths functionality in the RWR-toolkit120 to identify the shortest paths between pairwise sets of genes within individual modules, thereby determining which multiplex layers represent the majority of the underlying connectivity within the identified functional modules.

Multiplex construction

The multiplex network used for functional gene embedding, as described in MENTOR54 and GRIN,55 can be viewed as a representation of known biology. For this research, we built a 24-layer multiplex containing 22,115 unique genes (by Ensembl ID), and 10,053,502 total edges. This included six layers from HumanNet V3121 (gene-neighbourhood, co-expression, phylogenetic profile, pathway database, co-citation, and genetic interaction), a combined HumanNet V3 and STRING V12.121 PPI network, a previously published dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC)-specific transcription factor (TF)-target network,122 and 16 single-cell PENs derived from 16 unique cell types of the dlPFC, namely ID2-expressing caudal ganglionic eminence (CGE)-derived inhibitory interneurons, LAMP5/NOS1-expressing CGE-derived inhibitory interneurons, VIP-expressing CGE-derived inhibitory interneurons, PV-expressing medial ganglionic eminence (MGE)-derived inhibitory interneurons, PV/SCUBE3-expressing MGE-derived inhibitory neurons, CUX2-expressing layer II/III principal excitatory neurons, RORB-expressing layer IV principal excitatory neurons, THEMIS-expressing layer V/VI principal excitatory neurons, TLE4-expressing layer V/VI principal excitatory neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, mature oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and vascular cells.123 All (sc)PENs were constructed using iRF-LOOP.124

Human aging genomic resources (HAGR)

To assess whether the genes in the transcriptomic clocks have been previously associated with aging, we queried the Human Aging Genomic Resource (HAGR) database,125 an online collection of multiple databases containing genes associated with aging.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Prediction accuracy of transcriptomic clocks was quantified using root mean squared error (RMSE) across independent prefrontal cortex (PFC) datasets and across models. Associations between transcriptomic clocks and chronological age, as well as concordance among clocks, were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients (r). Correlation matrices were computed for merged and dataset-specific analyses, and hierarchical clustering was applied to correlation matrices to identify similarity patterns among clocks and datasets. For the retrained transcriptomic clocks, model performance was evaluated in independent test datasets, and generalizability was assessed by comparing training and testing correlations. We estimated the proportion of the stochastic clock contribution using the variance explained (R2). A relative metric (RR2) was defined as the ratio between the R2 of the stochastic component and the R2 of the corresponding original transcriptomic clock, providing a normalized measure of stochastic contribution. Associations between transcriptomic clocks and phenotypic traits were evaluated using multivariate linear regression models, adjusting for relevant covariates. Statistical significance was determined using Bonferroni correction for multiple testing in each dataset. Effect sizes were reported as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were conducted using standard computational frameworks in Python/R.
