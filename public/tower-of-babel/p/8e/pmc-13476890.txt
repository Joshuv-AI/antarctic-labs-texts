GLP-1 regulates osteo-adipogenic fate of BMSCs via HIF-2-AKT signaling and supports trabecular bone in glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis
Authors: Zhe Yang, Xin Li, Niujian Wu, Ling Wang, Zhanjin Lu, Lingling Wang, Shisong Han, Hongyun Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) lineage commitment contributes to metabolic bone diseases, but whether glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) regulates osteo-adipogenic fate remains unclear. Here, RNA sequencing was performed to identify potential pathways regulated by GLP-1 in BMSCs, followed by gain- and loss-of-function experiments. Furthermore, BMSC-specific Hif-2α knockout mice with glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis were treated with semaglutide to evaluate skeletal effects. GLP-1 suppressed adipogenesis and promoted osteogenesis of BMSCs in a dose-dependent manner. Transcriptomic analysis identified PI3K-AKT and hypoxia signaling as major GLP-1-regulated pathways. Mechanistically, GLP-1 inhibited AKT activation during adipogenesis and reshaped AKT signaling dynamics during osteogenesis. Loss of HIF-2α largely abolished GLP-1-mediated regulation of AKT activity. In vivo, semaglutide improved trabecular bone mass in osteoporotic mice, whereas this bone-protective effect was markedly diminished in Hif-2α-knockout mice. Our findings suggest that GLP-1 modulates BMSC lineage commitment by inhibiting adipogenesis and enhancing osteogenesis through HIF-2α-associated regulation of AKT signaling.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is the most common metabolic bone disease characterized by low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration. Beyond the well-recognized imbalance between bone formation and resorption, increasing studies indicate that the lineage commitment of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) plays a critical role in the development of osteoporosis (Zhu et al., 2024). BMSCs act as multipotent stem cells that can differentiate into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes (Walewska et al., 2023). This lineage commitment is orchestrated by a coordinated network of transcriptional programs and microenvironmental cues (Chen et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2023). Through such regulated fate decisions, bone formation and marrow homeostasis are maintained. However, under pathological conditions such as glucocorticoid exposure, aging, and metabolic stress, the differentiation balance of BMSCs will be disturbed (Zhao et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025). For example, enhanced adipogenesis will lead to reduced bone formation and increased marrow fat accumulation.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), such as semaglutide, have attracted considerable attention for metabolic benefits in type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular events (Drucker, 2024; Ussher and Drucker, 2023); however, their effects on the skeletal system remain generally neutral but controversial. Recently, some newer evidence from randomized and real-world cohorts suggests a possible reduction in fractures and osteoporosis risk in patients with sustained GLP-1 use (Chen et al., 2025; Shadbolt and Dowsey, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). In glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP), GLP-1 signaling has been shown to involve a DPP4-dependent pathway, and activation of the GLP-1 receptor mitigated microarchitectural deterioration (Wang et al., 2021). Therefore, these findings provide a biological basis for GLP-1 mediated protection within the bone marrow microenvironment. BMSCs, as common progenitors of osteoblasts and adipocytes, may represent a plausible cellular target through which GLP-1 may influence “bone-fat” balance. The lineage fate of BMSCs is regulated by intrinsic transcriptional programs, such as Runx2 and Osterix for osteogenesis, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein α (C/EBPα) for adipogenesis (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2022). Among intracellular signaling cascades, the AKT signaling has been identified as a pivotal regulator of proliferation, metabolism, and differentiation of BMSCs (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2020). The downstream effectors of AKT, including mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), differentially mediate its effects on adipogenesis and osteogenesis. Notably, it remains unclear whether BMSCs respond to GLP-1 to alter lineage fate, and if so, how GLP-1 modulates intracellular signaling cascades such as AKT signaling in adipogenic versus osteogenic contexts.

BMSCs reside in a physiologically hypoxic microenvironment, where oxygen tension critically influences their lineage commitment and metabolic status (Spencer et al., 2014). Hypoxia signaling, primarily mediated by hypoxia-inducible factors, is composed of an O2-labile α subunit (HIF-1α, HIF-2α, or HIF-3α) and a constitutively expressed β subunit. In BMSCs, HIF signaling acts as a central oxygen-sensing pathway that coordinates lineage commitment and cellular metabolism (Heeseog et al., 2024; Pouikli et al., 2022; Yihang et al., 2026). Our previous study has demonstrated that hypoxia-inducible factor 2α (HIF-2α) promotes osteogenic differentiation (OD) and metabolic reprogramming of BMSCs (Wang et al., 2024). HIF-2α is also implicated in extracellular matrix remodeling, and could regulate PI3K-AKT activity (Chen et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2024); however, the direct links between HIF-2α and AKT signaling in BMSCs remain poorly defined.

On this basis, we hypothesize that GLP-1 may regulate the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation (AD) of BMSCs through HIF-2α and the AKT signaling. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the bone-protective effect of GLP-1 and tried to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Results

GLP-1 inhibits adipogenesis and promotes osteogenesis of BMSCs in a dose-dependent manner

To investigate the regulatory role of GLP-1 in BMSC differentiation, we evaluated its effects on both adipogenic and osteogenic lineages using a dose-gradient treatment.

BMSCs were cultured in an AD induction for 2 weeks in the presence of GLP-1 at concentrations of 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 nM (Figure 1A). Oil Red O staining revealed a significant increase of the formation of mature adipocytes than the negative control group (NC), whereas GLP-1 treatment progressively reduced the adipogenesis in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1B). Correspondingly, the protein and mRNA levels of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c), PPARγ and C/EBPα were upregulated under AD conditions, while GLP-1 treatment mitigated these increases (Figures 1C and 1D). To determine whether GLP-1 also influences osteogenesis, BMSCs were subjected to OD medium for 3 weeks with the same range of GLP-1 concentrations (Figure 1E). Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining indicated an enhancement of mineralized nodule formation with increasing GLP-1 (Figure 1F). This was further supported by elevated protein levels of osteogenesis-related markers, including Osterix, activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), and Collagen I in GLP-1-treated groups (Figure 1G). There were 1,434 upregulated and 1,350 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) when comparing AD to controls (Figures S1B and S1C). In the comparison between AD + GLP-1 and AD, 1,090 DEGs were identified, including 557 upregulated and 533 downregulated genes (Figure S1D). Together, these transcriptomic findings suggest that GLP-1 attenuates adipogenesis and alters metabolic reprogramming in BMSCs.

GLP-1 attenuates adipogenesis of BMSCs via inhibition of AKT signaling

To assess the functional involvement of AKT signaling in GLP-1-mediated anti-adipogenic effects, we evaluated the phosphorylation of AKT. Among the diverse downstream effectors of the AKT signaling cascade, mTOR and GSK3β, as critical regulators involved in cell growth, survival, metabolism and differentiation, have been extensively implicated in the regulation of lineage commitment. Herein, we also examined the phosphorylation levels of mTOR and GSK3β (Figure 4A). We next determined whether AKT signaling is involved in GLP-1-mediated inhibition of adipogenesis. The specific AKT inhibitor MK2206 was used. As expected, MK2206 significantly suppressed adipogenesis, as evidenced by reduced formation of mature adipocytes and lower expression of adipogenic markers, such as PPARγ, SREBP1c and Stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1). Notably, co-treatment with MK2206 and GLP-1 did not show further suppression compared with MK2206 alone (Figures 4B and 4C). To further validate this dependency, we performed a rescue experiment using the AKT agonist SC79. Activation of AKT partially reversed the inhibitory effects of GLP-1 on adipogenesis.

To further clarify which downstream branch of AKT signaling mediates the anti-adipogenic effect of GLP-1, we selectively modulated the activity of mTOR and GSK3β using pharmacological agents. MHY1485, a specific mTOR activator, and SB216763, a selective GSK3β inhibitor, were administered in the context of AD induction with GLP-1 treatment. The results demonstrated that activation of mTOR markedly reversed the inhibitory effects of GLP-1, as evidenced by increased expression levels of SREBP1c, PPARγ, and C/EBPα (Figure 4D). This result was further supported by Nile Red staining, which showed increased adipogenesis upon MHY1485 treatment despite the presence of GLP-1 (Figure S5). In contrast, inhibition of GSK3β failed to reverse the GLP-1-induced suppression of adipogenic markers, indicating that mTOR, rather than GSK3β, acts as a key downstream effector. Taken together, these results support the hypothesis that GLP-1 suppresses adipogenesis of BMSCs in an AKT-mTOR-dependent manner.

GLP-1 promotes osteogenesis of BMSCs by accelerating the transition of AKT signaling activity

Based on transcriptomic enrichment analysis implicating that AKT signaling was involved in the regulation of GLP-1-mediated osteogenesis (Figures 3E and 3F), we examined the AKT activity during osteogenic induction. Given the long duration required for OD, we conducted a time-course analysis of GLP-1 treatment. As shown in Figure 5A, p-AKT exhibited a transient increase at day 3 upon OD treatment, but its activity gradually declined over time. Notably, in the presence of GLP-1, this pattern was altered: p-AKT remained elevated at day 3 but declined more rapidly starting at day 7 onward. Then, we assessed whether this shift of AKT signaling activity caused by GLP-1 was associated with an earlier initiation of OD. Here, we observed that the levels of key osteogenic markers, such as Osterix, ATF4, and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), were upregulated more rapidly when GLP-1 treatment (Figure 5B). Consistently, Alizarin Red S staining showed that mineral deposition also occurred earlier and more extensively in GLP-1-treated BMSCs (Figure 5C). These observations indicate that GLP-1 could promote an earlier onset of osteogenesis.

To determine whether suppression of AKT signaling is required for osteogenesis mediated by GLP-1, SC79 (a selective AKT activator) was used to treat BMSCs at different time points during OD in the presence of GLP-1. As shown in Figure 5D, SC79 treatment significantly suppressed the expression of Osterix, ATF4, and Runx2 starting from day 7, although there was no notable difference at day 3. Consistently, Alizarin Red S staining showed comparable mineral deposition between groups at day 3, but a clear reduction was observed starting from day 7 (Figure 5E). These results indicate that AKT signaling is dynamically regulated during osteogenesis, and that the downregulation of AKT signaling at the late phase is necessary for the full OD in response to GLP-1.

GLP-1-mediated inhibition of AKT signaling is dependent on HIF-2α activity

To elucidate the possible mechanism of GLP-1-induced AKT inhibition, we focused on hypoxia signaling, which was significantly enriched based on transcriptome sequencing. We first examined the expression levels of hypoxia markers, including HIF-1α and HIF-2α. Western blot analysis showed that GLP-1 selectively upregulated HIF-2α in a time-dependent manner, but not HIF-1α (Figure 6A). This result was consistent with our previous findings that inhibition of HIF-2α reduced the bone mass (Wang et al., 2024). To determine the relationship between HIF-2α and AKT signaling, we performed a series of loss- and gain-of-function experiments. As shown in Figure 6B, pharmacological modulation of AKT activity using MK2206 or SC79 did not affect HIF-2α expression. Knockdown of HIF-2α by siRNA increased AKT phosphorylation, whereas overexpression of HIF-2α led to marked suppression of p-AKT levels (Figure 6C). Similar results were observed using pharmacological modulation of HIF signaling: PT2399, a selective HIF-2α antagonist, and FG-4592 (roxadustat), a prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor that stabilizes HIF-α proteins and thereby activates HIF signaling (Figure 6D). The findings indicated that HIF-2α negatively regulated AKT activity. We next tested whether HIF-2α was involved in the inhibition of AKT signaling caused by GLP-1. As expected, GLP-1 reduced p-AKT levels in BMSCs; knockdown of HIF-2α significantly abrogated GLP-1-mediated suppression of AKT signaling, while HIF-2α overexpression potentiated it (Figure 6E). We next examined whether this HIF-2α-dependent inhibition of AKT is functionally relevant to adipogenesis and osteogenesis of BMSCs. Here, we observed that GLP-1 reduced the formation of mature adipocytes of BMSCs under AD; however, knockdown of HIF-2α abolished this effect. Notably, the addition of MK2206 restored the inhibition of adipogenesis, even in the HIF-2α knockdown background (Figure 6F). Under OD, Alizarin Red S staining revealed that GLP-1 enhanced matrix mineralization, while HIF-2α knockdown impaired this effect. Importantly, MK2206 treatment (from day 7 onward) partially restored calcium deposition, even when HIF-2α knockdown (Figure 6G). Thus, we concluded that HIF-2α was involved in the suppression of AKT caused by GLP-1.

Semaglutide prevents trabecular bone deterioration in osteoporotic mice in a HIF-2α-dependent manner

To evaluate the pathological relevance of our findings for clinical impact, we utilized a dexamethasone-induced osteoporosis model in wildtype (WT) and the conditional knockout (BKO) mice. Mice were treated with either PBS or semaglutide every other day for 4 weeks (Figure 7A). There were no differences in body, liver, or fat weights across all groups (Figure 7B) (Figures S6A and S6B). Notably, semaglutide did not reverse the dexamethasone (DEX)-induced elevation in fasting plasma glucose (FPG) in both WT and BKO mice (Figure 7C). Micro-CT revealed that DEX induced substantial trabecular bone loss in both WT and BKO mice, as reflected by reduced trabecular BV/TV, lower Tb.N, and increased Tb.Sp (Figures 7D–7G). Importantly, semaglutide significantly improved these trabecular parameters in WT mice but not in BKO mice, indicating that the bone-protective effects caused by GLP-1 are HIF-2α-dependent. Besides, we found that Tb.Th, connectivity density and trabecular bone mineral density (BMD) (Figures 7H–7J) did not show significant differences among groups, suggesting that semaglutide may enhance bone structure rather than mineral density during early intervention. We next assessed whether semaglutide could affect cortical bone parameters. As shown in Figures S7A–S7D, DEX markedly reduced cortical BMD, cortical (bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), and Ct.Th in WT mice, whereas increasing cortical bone surface (BS)/BV; however, semaglutide failed to reverse these changes in either WT or BKO mice. Histology confirmed the micro-CT findings. Safranin O-Fast Green and H&E staining showed that DEX caused marked rarefaction of the subchondral trabecular network, with thinner, discontinuous trabeculas and enlarged marrow cavities (Figure 7K). Notably, semaglutide improved trabecular connectivity and filled intertrabecular spaces in WT mice; however, these features remained largely unchanged in BKO mice, consistent with a requirement for HIF-2α. Perilipin-1 and osteocalcin immunohistochemistry further showed that DEX increased marrow adiposity and reduced osteogenic activity in WT mice, whereas semaglutide reversed these changes (Figure 7L). Collectively, these results indicate that semaglutide preserves trabecular bone microarchitecture, reduces marrow adiposity, and restores osteogenic activity in osteoporotic mice through an HIF-2α-dependent mechanism.

Discussion

In this study, transcriptomic profiling by RNA sequencing revealed that both the AKT signaling pathway and HIF-2α are involved in GLP-1-mediated regulation of BMSCs lineage commitment. Mechanistically, we identified that GLP-1 enhances HIF-2α expression, leading to inhibition of AKT-mTOR signaling during adipogenesis and accelerating the transition of AKT activity from an early activation phase to a later suppression phase during osteogenesis. Moreover, using a BMSC-specific Hif-2α knockout mouse model, we showed that semaglutide markedly improved the trabecular bone microarchitecture in GIOP in an HIF-2α-dependent manner.

Recently, GLP-1RAs have attracted considerable attention for their efficacy in diabetes and obesity management and their potential benefits in cardiovascular and metabolic health; however, the effects of GLP-1RAs on bone metabolism remain incompletely understood. Although studies in rodent models and human cell lines suggest that GLP-1RAs may exert a protective effect (Wang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2025), a large body of evidence from clinical studies shows considerable heterogeneity (Al Refaie et al., 2024; 2025; Hansen et al., 2024). Most population-based studies in type 2 diabetes report neutral average outcomes, but certain patient subgroups appear to derive skeletal benefits (Jensen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Importantly, osteogenesis is tightly coupled to systemic metabolic status (Stegen and Carmeliet, 2024; Suh et al., 2023; van Gastel and Carmeliet, 2021). Because GLP-1RAs can remodel systemic metabolism, including body weight and glucose homeostasis, the net skeletal response likely reflects both local actions within the bone marrow niche and broader systemic metabolic changes. Additionally, Bone formation is also closely coupled to bone marrow angiogenesis, and GLP-1RAs may influence skeletal outcomes by remodeling the vascular niche. In particular, the angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling phenotype (e.g., type H vessel formation) is most commonly linked to the HIF-1α (Ruan et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023). Because we did not quantify vascular endpoints, we cannot exclude that changes in bone vasculature contribute to the observed trabecular benefits. Our current data in vivo do not allow us to distinguish whether semaglutide influences BMSCs through a direct effect or indirectly through changes in systemic metabolism, endocrine cues, vascular remodeling, or other components of the bone marrow microenvironment. We mainly reported that GLP-1 exerted an overall anabolic effect by promoting BMSC differentiation into osteoblasts and suppressing it into adipocytes. This restoration of the “bone-fat” balance promotes osteoblastogenesis and bone formation (Zhao et al., 2024). Moreover, the subsequent transcriptomic enrichment analysis further suggested that the biological link between GLP-1 and its effects on BMSC differentiation was mediated via integration of hypoxia signaling (HIF-2α) with AKT signaling.

Mechanistically, our findings indicate that inhibition of the AKT-mTOR branch represents a principal mechanism by which GLP-1 suppresses adipogenesis. Consistent with this, previous studies have established that activation of PI3K-AKT signaling is essential for AD of mesenchymal stem cells (Chen et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2017). For example, Lee et al. showed that IGF-1 stimulates AD via increasing phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR (Lee et al., 2016). In addition, Chen et al. reported that FGF9 regulates the fate of BMSCs and the balance of bone metabolism in osteoporosis through the PI3K/AKT/Hippo signaling pathway (Chen et al., 2024), further supporting the pivotal role of AKT in lineage commitment. In our work, AD increased p-AKT and p-mTOR, both of which were reversed by GLP-1. Pharmacological inhibition of AKT abolished additional anti-adipogenic effects of GLP-1; whereas reactivation rescued adipogenesis, confirming functional dependency. mTOR activation reversed the GLP-1’s effects, while GSK3β inhibition did not, indicating branch specificity that aligns with previous observations that mTOR, not GSK3β, is the critical downstream mediator in mesenchymal stem cell adipogenesis.

In osteogenesis, our data support a time-dependent role for AKT signaling: an early increase may be permissive for commitment, whereas a subsequent decrease is necessary for maturation and mineralization. Moreover, GLP-1 maintained the early p-AKT peak yet enhanced its subsequent decline, which in turn promoted earlier upregulation of Runx2, ATF4, and Osterix, thereby accelerating mineral deposition. Restoration of AKT activity on day 7, rather than day 3, attenuated these effects, indicating that the OD is critically involved in the temporal regulation of AKT signaling. However, recent studies generally suggest that PI3K-AKT promotes osteogenesis throughout the differentiation process. For example, 3D-printed PLGA/black-phosphorus scaffolds enhanced hBMSC osteogenesis by activating PI3K-AKT (early stage), without resolving temporal dynamics (Long et al., 2023). In postmenopausal models, luteolin restored ovariectomy (OVX)-suppressed PI3K/AKT signaling and rescued osteogenic defects; blocking PI3K reversed luteolin’s protection, including mineralization and pyroptosis readouts in osteoblasts (Chai et al., 2024). In addition, myricitrin increased p-PI3K/p-AKT and improved trabecular indices in OVX mice, while the PI3K inhibitor partially abrogated its pro-osteogenic action in immortalized BMSCs (Li et al., 2023). Taken together, these reports seem to demonstrate that maintaining or restoring AKT activity improves osteogenesis at bulk endpoints, but they do not interrogate phase-specific requirements during differentiation, as the timing of the intervention was not specified. It makes it hard to determine at which stage AKT activity was beneficial or detrimental to the osteogenic process. Additionally, differences in readout windows, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) on day 7 and ARS on day 14, disease contexts (OVX vs. GIOP), and cell targets (osteoblasts vs. BMSCs within an osteogenic niche) likely account for the apparent discrepancy.

HIF-2α emerged as a necessary upstream regulator of GLP-1-induced AKT suppression. GLP-1 selectively increased HIF-2α expression; knockdown of HIF-2α elevated p-AKT, while overexpression reduced it. In vivo, semaglutide improved trabecular BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Sp in wild-type but not BMSC-specific Hif-2α knockout mice, confirming that the effects of GLP-1 depend on HIF-2α. Notably, the skeletal benefits of semaglutide were mostly seen in trabecular bone, while cortical parameters changed little during the treatment period. One possible explanation for this difference is the higher metabolic turnover and increased GLP-1R expression in trabecular-rich regions. Because the cortical bone turns over slowly and has a dense structure, semaglutide likely exerts little influence on its architecture over the relatively short treatment period. Therefore, we suggest that semaglutide is likely more valuable in preventing bone loss in early-stage or high-risk osteoporosis, especially GIOP, than in repairing substantial cortical damage once it has occurred.

There are four main limitations in our study. First, we used only a single disease model of GIOP, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, our mechanistic experiments relied on pharmacologic inhibitors and activators, which may have dose-response variability. Third, we did not confirm GLP-1 receptor expression in BMSCs, nor did we perform receptor-blocking assays, leaving uncertainty about the role of the receptor in mediating the observed effects. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the skeletal benefits of semaglutide in our in vivo model are direct or indirect. In future studies, we will investigate how GLP-1 is connected upstream to HIF-2α, identify direct transcriptional targets of HIF-2α, and examine whether the AKT timing effect is also present in other models of osteoporosis.

Collectively, this study supports a model in which GLP-1 regulates BMSC lineage fate in vitro by suppressing adipogenesis and promoting osteogenesis through HIF-2α dependent modulation of AKT signaling. In vivo, semaglutide attenuated trabecular bone deterioration in GIOP in a manner that required HIF-2α in BMSCs. These findings suggest that GLP-1-related signaling may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for patients at high risk of trabecular bone loss.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal models and treatment

To achieve targeted deletion of Hif-2α in BMSCs, we generated conditional knockout mice using a Cre-loxP strategy based on the Prx1-Cre system. Mice harboring floxed alleles of Hif-2α (Epas1flox/flox) were originally acquired from the Jackson Laboratory (Stock No. 008407; Bar Harbor, USA). The Cre-driver line (Prx1-Cre+/−) was kindly provided by Professor Meng Zhao (Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China). The Epas1flox/flox mice were bred with Prx1-Cre+/− mice for more than five generations under a C57BL/6 background to ensure genetic stability and tissue specificity. The conditional knockout mice (referred to as BKO) were identified as Prx1-Cre+/−; Epas1flox/flox, while littermate Epas1flox/flox mice lacking Cre expression served as wild-type controls (referred to as WT).

Dexamethasone (DEX) (HY-14648; MedChemExpress) was used to induce bone marrow microenvironmental dysfunction, mimicking glucocorticoid-related metabolic disorders in adipogenesis and osteogenesis. The WT and BKO mice were randomly divided into five experimental groups: WT, WT treated with dexamethasone (WT + DEX), WT treated with dexamethasone and semaglutide (WT + DEX + semaglutide), BKO treated with dexamethasone (BKO + DEX), and BKO treated with dexamethasone and semaglutide (BKO + DEX + semaglutide). Both male and female mice (8–12 weeks of age) were included in all groups. Group allocation was performed using a sex-stratified randomization with baseline body weight balanced across groups. All downstream analyses and outcome assessments were performed on the same cohort. Starting from week 0, mice in the DEX-treated groups received daily intraperitoneal injections of DEX (20 mg/kg) for a total of 8 weeks (Wang et al., 2024). From week 4 onward, semaglutide (Novo Nordisk, Bagsværd, Denmark) was introduced in designated groups via intraperitoneal injection at 0.1 mg/kg, administered every other day until sacrifice at week 8.

All animal handling and experimental procedures were carried out following approval by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jinan University (Approval No.20200829-08). The procedures performed complied with the World Medical Association Statement on Animal Use in Biomedical Research.

Primary BMSCs culture and treatment

BMSCs were isolated from femurs and tibias of 4-week-old mice after cervical dislocation and 75% ethanol sterilization. Bones were rinsed in PBS with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) and transferred to L-Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (L-DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA). Marrow cavities were exposed by cutting the epiphyses and flushed with the medium until the bones turned pale. The suspension was filtered through a 75 μm strainer and centrifuged. Cells were seeded in complete medium, and the dishes were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. BMSCs at passages 3 to 5 were used for osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation. To assess the dose-dependent effects of GLP-1 on BMSCs lineage commitment, BMSCs were treated with GLP-1 (7–36) (HY-P0054A; MedChemExpress) at 0, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 nM.

Method details

Adipogenic differentiation and lipid staining

For adipogenic differentiation, BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 1% sodium pyruvate, 20% FBS, and 1% P/S. The medium was adjusted to a final volume of 200 mL and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The adipogenic induction cocktail consisted of 200 μL of 0.5 mM IBMX (MB5226; Meilunbio, Dalian, China), 30 μL of 0.4 M indomethacin (A8449-1g; ApexBio, Houston, USA), 100 μL of 2 mM DEX, and 200 μL of 10 mg/mL insulin. The medium was refreshed every 3 days, and adipogenic differentiation was maintained for 14 days.

To evaluate adipogenic differentiation, BMSCs were stained with Oil Red O and Nile Red. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min, then treated with 60% isopropanol for 30 s to enhance lipid staining. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution (HY-DY1096; MedChemExpress) for 15 min. Excess dye was removed by washing with distilled water, and lipid droplets were visualized under a light microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Oil Red O staining was quantified as relative positive area.

For Nile Red staining, BMSCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and then incubated with 1 μM Nile red (N815046; Macklin, Shanghai, China) for 15 min in the dark. After staining, the cells were washed with PBS, and intracellular lipid accumulation was visualized using a confocal laser microscope (Leica). Nile Red staining was quantified as relative fluorescence intensity.

Osteogenic differentiation and Alizarin red S staining

For osteogenic differentiation, BMSCs were seeded at an appropriate density and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 1% sodium pyruvate, 20% FBS, and 1% P/S. The medium was adjusted to a final volume of 200 mL and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The osteogenic induction cocktail consisted of 200 μL of 50 mg/mL vitamin C (MB4168; Meilunbio), 1 mL of 2 mM β-glycerophosphate (MB3195; Meilunbio), and 1 μL of 2 mM DEX. The medium was refreshed every 3 days, and osteogenic differentiation was maintained for 21 days.

Osteogenic differentiation was assessed using Alizarin Red S staining. After 21 days of induction, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. A 0.2% Alizarin Red S solution (pH 8.3; A5533, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was then added dropwise to each well and incubated for 25 min. Excess dye was removed by washing with distilled water. Mineralized matrix nodules were visualized and imaged using a light microscope. Alizarin Red S staining was quantified as relative positive area.

High-Throughput sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from BMSCs and assessed for concentration and integrity by Nanodrop, Qubit, and Bioanalyzer 2100. RNA-seq was performed by Cosmos Wisdom, Hangzhou, China, including sample quality control, library construction using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit, and paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing targeted the transcriptome of polyadenylated mRNAs. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapters and low-quality bases, then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using STAR. Gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, with significance defined as adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1.

Western Blot analysis

BMSCs were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit (PK10026; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were detected by ECL and visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from BMSCs using TRIzol (15596026; Invitrogen, USA). After checking RNA integrity and concentration, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA (R333-01; Vazyme, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Q712-02; Vazyme). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. All reactions were run in technical triplicate.

The μCT analysis

Micro-computed tomography (isotropic voxel size: 10 μm, 70 kVp, 200 μA; Scanco Medical, Switzerland) was performed to evaluate bone microarchitecture, as we previously described (Wang et al., 2024). Standard parameters of trabecular bone microarchitecture were quantified, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV, bone volume/total volume), bone surface density (BS/BV, bone surface/bone volume), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). Cortical bone parameters such as cortical thickness (Ct.Th) and bone mineral density (BMD) were also assessed. Three-dimensional reconstruction and analysis were performed using the manufacturer’s software.

Histological analysis

Mouse femurs and tibias were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) at 4°C for 4 weeks. Samples were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. The sections were rehydrated in graded ethanol solutions with decreasing concentrations. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin, rinsed with distilled water, and counterstained with eosin. For Safranin O-Fast Green staining, sections were first stained with 0.05% Fast Green solution (G1053; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 5 min to visualize the bone matrix. Subsequently, sections were stained with 0.1% Safranin O solution for 30 s to stain the cartilage. Representative regions were imaged using a brightfield microscope.

For immunohistochemistry, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked with serum, and incubated with primary antibodies against perilipin-1 and osteocalcin overnight at 4°C. After incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies, signals were developed using DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. For experiments involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
