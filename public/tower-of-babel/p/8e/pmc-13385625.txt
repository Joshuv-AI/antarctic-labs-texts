Ribonuclease DIS3 delays aging and senescence by generating tRNA halves
Authors: Seokjun G. Ha, Hanseul Lee, Jisoo Park, Donghun Lee, Gee-Yoon Lee, Seokjin Ham, Sujeong Kwon, Sangsoon Park, Sieun S. Kim, Yoonji Jung, Jongsun Lee, Jooyeon Sohn, Seon Woo A. An, Heehwa G. Son, Wooseon Hwang, Hyeong-In Kim, Eunseok Kang, Yoon Ki Kim, Gwangrog Lee, Seung-Jae V. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transfer RNA (tRNA) halves (tRHs) are generated via the cleavage of tRNAs, but their roles in aging and longevity remain poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate a direct role of tRHs in aging in metazoans. Through a genetic screen using Caenorhabditis elegans, we identify DIS-3/DIS3 as a ribonuclease that catalyzes tRH generation, including 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp, from tRNAs. Among them, 5′-tRH-Gln is essential for longevity conferred by various interventions, including dietary restriction. Generation of 5′-tRH-Gln reduces translation via ribosomal protein binding and upregulates the SKN-1/NRF transcription factor responsible for lifespan extension. We further show that mammalian DIS3 contributes to tRH generation and delays cellular senescence through translation downregulation by another tRH, 5′-tRH-Cys. Overall, our data demonstrate that DIS-3/DIS3 is an evolutionarily conserved tRH-generating ribonuclease that counteracts organismal and cellular aging.

Introduction

Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) are essential components of the translational machinery. Beyond their classic roles in amino acid transfer, tRNAs can be cleaved into tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), small regulatory non-coding RNAs. tsRNAs are categorized into two types, tRNA halves (tRHs), which are generated by cleavage near anticodon loops, and tRNA-derived RNA fragments (tRFs), which are generated by cleavage at D- or T-loops1,2. tRHs are also known as tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs) because they are generated by various stress stimuli, including oxidative stress and starvation2,3. tRHs influence diverse biological processes, including gene silencing, translational inhibition, and epigenetic regulation by acting as regulators of cellular stress responses4. The stressful conditions that induce tRH generation can affect aging. In addition, multiple 5′-tRHs exhibit age-dependent level changes in Caenorhabditis elegans5–7.

tRHs are also implicated in pathological processes, including cancer progression and age-associated neurodegenerative diseases4,8. Accumulating evidence indicates that tRHs are potential diagnostic markers of cancer and therapeutic targets that directly regulate cancer progression8. In addition, age-dependently accumulated 5′-tRH-Glu causes memory impairment by disrupting mitochondria, and inhibition of 5′-tRH-Glu ameliorates memory decline in mice9. These observations raise the possible roles of tRHs in aging, but whether tRHs directly regulate aging and longevity remains unknown.

In this work, we identified DIS-3 as a tRH-generating ribonuclease in C. elegans. We showed that dis-3 was required for longevity conferred by various regimens, including dietary restriction (DR). We then found that DIS-3 generated tRHs, including 5′-tRH-Gln, which enhances longevity by inhibiting translation through binding ribosome. We further showed that the activation of SKN-1/nuclear respiratory factor (NRF), a transcription factor required for DR-induced life extension, mediated longevity conferred by 5′-tRH-Gln-dependently reduced translation. In cultured mammalian cells, DIS3 generated a subset of tRHs, including 5′-tRH-Cys, which prevented premature cellular aging by inhibiting translation. Our results indicate that DIS-3/DIS3 is a ribonuclease that catalyzes the cleavage of tRNAs to generate tRHs, thereby delaying aging in both C. elegans and mammalian cells.

Results

A targeted genetic screen identifies DIS-3 that contributes to the generation of tRHs in C. elegans

In mammals, Angiogenin (ANG) is an established ribonuclease that generates tRHs2. C. elegans does not contain homologs encoding ANG, although the existence of tRHs has been reported5–7. We therefore performed an RNAi screen targeting each of all 71 putative C. elegans ribonucleases (gene ontology resource AmiGO2, see Supplementary Data 1 for detailed list of ribonucleases) by measuring the levels of two representative and abundant tRHs6, 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c), to identify the tRH-generating enzyme. We found that RNAi targeting the ribonuclease dis-3/DIS3, which encodes a catalytic subunit of the RNA exosome complex that has both endoribonuclease and exoribonuclease activities 10,11, had the greatest effect on reducing both 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Data 1) without decreasing the levels of Gln tRNA or Asp tRNA (Fig. 1c). RNAi targeting each of nobh-1/NOB1, alg-2/AGO2, popl-1/POP1, popl-7/TMEM167A, C46G7.1/rnk-2/RNASEK, T09F3.5, ZK1127.13, hoe-1/ELAC2, and nonu-1/N4BP2 significantly decreased 5′-tRH-Gln levels, while not affecting 5′-tRH-Asp levels (Supplementary Data 1). RNAi targeting F49C12.12/rnk-1/RNASEK, tsen-2/TSEN2, or coa-6 significantly decreased 5′-tRH-Asp levels, whereas RNAi targeting xrn-2/XRN2 or endu-2 significantly increased 5′-tRH-Asp levels, while not affecting 5′-tRH-Gln levels (Supplementary Data 1). Therefore, we focused our subsequent analysis on dis-3, which affected the levels of both 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp. Because dis-3 null mutants exhibit early larval arrest12, we functionally analyzed four publicly available dis-3 missense mutants, dis-3(L75Q), dis-3(G154D), dis-3(S657F), and dis-3(A808T) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Among them, a L75 to Q change in DIS-3 [dis-3(L75Q)], which is predicted to affect the endonuclease domain of DIS-3 (Supplementary Fig. 2c), decreased the levels of both 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2d). We also confirmed the stem-loop qRT-PCR data by applying pretreatments that add 5′ phosphate to tRHs and remove 3′ end modification and nucleotide modifications (Fig. 1e). dis-3 RNAi further reduced the levels of 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp in dis-3(L75Q) mutants (Supplementary Fig. 2e), suggesting that the mutation does not fully inhibit endoribonuclease activity. DIS3 cleaves tRNAs for generating tRHs

Next, we tested the enzyme activity of DIS-3 for the generation of tRHs. We used purified recombinant human DIS3, which is the closest homolog of C. elegans DIS-3 (Supplementary Fig. 3a), for in vitro cleavage. We showed that purified recombinant human DIS3 cleaved Gln tRNA and Asp tRNA in vitro to generate 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp, respectively (Fig. 1f–h and Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). Mn2+, a metal ion cofactor optimal for the endoribonuclease function of DIS310, was required for the efficient cleavage of tRNAs by DIS3, but Mg2+, which is required for the exoribonuclease function11, was not as effective as Mn2+ (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). The cleavage of Gln tRNA and Asp tRNA by DIS3 exhibited time- and dose-dependent increases (Fig. 1i–l, and Supplementary Fig. 3e–h). Moreover, DIS3(L66Q), the orthologous mutation to dis-3(L75Q), substantially decreased tRNA cleavage (Fig. 1i–l, and Supplementary Fig. 3e–h). We also utilized native Gln tRNA isolated from C. elegans as a substrate and confirmed that DIS3 generated tRHs, whereas DIS3(L66Q) did not (Fig. 1m, n and Supplementary Fig. 3i). Furthermore, the exoribonuclease-dead mutant DIS3(D487N) was capable of generating tRH (Fig. 1m, n). These results indicate that tRNAs are cleaved via the endoribonuclease activity of DIS3. Together, these data suggest that DIS-3/DIS3 enzymatically mediates the generation of tRHs.

DIS-3 contributes to maintaining normal lifespan and dietary restriction (DR)-conferred longevity

As the levels of multiple tRHs change during aging5,6,13, we tested whether dis-3 affected aging and lifespan in C. elegans. We found that lifespan was substantially decreased by dis-3 RNAi (Fig. 2a), consistent with a previous report14, and by the dis-3(L75Q) mutation (Fig. 2b). In contrast, RNAi knockdown of disl-2, a paralog of dis-3 (Supplementary Fig. 3a), did not affect the levels of 5′-tRH-Gln or 5′-tRH-Asp, nor did it impact lifespan (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Data 1). We found that the green fluorescent protein (GFP)-fused DIS-3 transgene (dis-3 tg) partially suppressed the short lifespan of dis-3(L75Q) mutants (Supplementary Fig. 4c) but did not increase lifespan in a wild-type background (Supplementary Fig. 4d). We next determined whether dis-3 contributed to C. elegans longevity conferred by representative regimens15, including the animals with DR-mimetic eat-2(−), insulin/insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) receptor mutation daf-2(−), sensory-defective osm-5(−), germline-deficient glp-1(−), or mitochondrial respiration-impaired isp-1(−). We found that dis-3(L75Q) mutation or dis-3 RNAi reduced the extended lifespan in each of these mutants (Fig. 2c–g). In particular, the dis-3(L75Q) mutation completely suppressed longevity conferred by the DR-mimetic eat-2 mutation and food deprivation (FD), two established C. elegans DR paradigms (Fig. 2c, h)16,17, but partially suppressed that of other long-lived mutants (Fig. 2d–g). Thus, DIS-3 is essential for DR-induced longevity but is partly required for longevity conferred by other interventions, including reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling, sensory defect, germline deficiency, and impaired mitochondrial respiration.

The 5′-tRH-Gln extends lifespan by inhibiting translation in C. elegans

Next, we asked whether tRHs generated by DIS-3 affected DR-induced longevity. Because tRNAs interact with different processing enzymes in the nucleus and cytoplasm18, we determined the subcellular localization of DIS-3 by using mCherry-tagged DIS-3 (mCherry::dis-3) knock-in animals12. Under fed conditions, DIS-3 was mainly localized in the nuclei. We found that FD increased cytoplasmic DIS-3 protein levels (Fig. 2i). We confirmed the increased cytosolic translocalization of DIS-3 by FD using immunofluorescence, although the animals on FD exhibited small body and intestine sizes (Fig. 2j, k). We found that overexpression of cytoplasmic dis-3 using nuclear export signal (NES::dis-3) largely suppressed shortened lifespan of dis-3(L75Q) mutants (Fig. 2l), supporting the crucial role of DIS-3 in the cytoplasm. We then showed that both 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp were upregulated by FD but not by heat shock, an alternative stress that induces tRNA cleavage in mammalian cells (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 5a)2. Indeed, dis-3(L75Q) mutation did not alter heat stress resistance of animals (Fig. 2n). Surprisingly, overexpression of 5′-tRH-Gln (5′-tRH-Gln o/e) substantially increased lifespan, whereas the effects of 5′-tRH-Asp o/e were marginal (Fig. 2o and Supplementary Fig. 5b). We also found that transgenic expression of antisense RNAs specifically targeting 5′-tRH-Gln or 5′-tRH-Asp (anti-5′-tRH-Gln or anti-5′-tRH-Asp; Supplementary Fig. 5c–h) shortened lifespan (Fig. 2p, q). Based on these overexpression and antisense RNA experiments, we focused hereafter on our functional analysis on 5′-tRH-Gln. 5′-tRH-Gln o/e substantially increased the short lifespan of dis-3 mutants (Fig. 2r), consistent with our findings regarding the role of DIS-3 in the generation of 5′-tRH-Gln. We found that longevity caused by 5′-tRH-Gln o/e was not additive to that conferred by FD (Fig. 2s). We propose that DIS-3 generates 5′-tRH-Gln in the cytoplasm that contributes to longevity caused by DR.

We then sought to determine how 5′-tRH-Gln contributes to longevity. By performing an RNA pull-down assay using synthesized biotinylated 5′-tRH-Gln followed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), we identified 14 proteins that bound 5′-tRH-Gln (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Data 2 and 5). Among these proteins, seven were ribosomal proteins (Fig. 3b: bold). These data raise the possibility that 5′-tRH-Gln modulates translation by binding ribosomes19. Consistently, by measuring de novo protein synthesis rates utilizing fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) (Fig. 3c)20, we found that low translation rates in DR-mimetic eat-2 mutants21,22 were suppressed by dis-3 mutation (Fig. 3d). In addition, overexpression of the 5′-tRH-Gln reduced translation rates, measured by using surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay (Fig. 3e, f). We then focused on ribosomal protein L12/rpl-12, which encodes a ribosomal protein subunit, as we found that it reliably bound to 5′-tRH-Gln (Fig. 3g–i, and Supplementary Data 2). We also found that the 5′-tRH-Asp co-immunoprecipitated with RPL-12, further supporting interaction between ribosomal proteins and tRHs (Supplementary Fig. 5i). We further showed that under the Mg2+-supplemented condition, which stabilizes ribosomes, endogenous 5′-tRH-Gln was co-immunoprecipitated with RPL-12::HA, whereas ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), which disassembles ribosomes into subunits23, diminished the co-immunoprecipitation of 5′-tRH-Gln (Fig. 3j). To test whether the interaction between tRHs and ribosomes is generalizable to other ribosomal proteins, we performed additional co-immunoprecipitation experiments using rpl-29::GFP knock-in animals24 and obtained similar results (Fig. 3k). We then observed that RNAi targeting rpl-12, whose genetic inhibition reduces global protein synthesis in human cells and yeast25,26, extended lifespan, but did not further increase longevity caused by 5′-tRH-Gln overexpression (Fig. 3l). rpl-12 RNAi also suppressed the short lifespan of dis-3 mutants and that of eat-2; dis-3 double mutants (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Fig. 5j). Together, these data suggest that 5′-tRH-Gln generated by DIS-3 contributes to DR-induced longevity by inhibiting translation through binding ribosomal proteins.

DIS-3 acts with SKN-1/NRF for lifespan extension by DR

Next, we sought to identify factors that mediate dis-3-dependent DR-induced longevity. We analyzed the overlap between the genes upregulated in eat-2 mutants compared to wild-type and the genes downregulated by dis-3 mutation in eat-2 mutants. We found that eat-2 mutations elicited similar transcriptomic changes compared to wild-type and eat-2; dis-3 double mutants (Fig. 4a, b). These data indicate that mutations in eat-2 and dis-3 confer opposite effects on the transcriptome changes. Transcriptomic epistasis analysis also generated an epistasis coefficient of −0.96 between eat-2 and dis-3 (Fig. 4c). By analyzing overlaps between the genes upregulated in eat-2 mutants compared to wild-type and the genes upregulated in eat-2 mutants compared to eat-2; dis-3 double mutants, we observed that a significant number of genes (87, p-value = 2.38e−32) were dis-3-dependently upregulated in eat-2 mutants (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Data 3). We then compared these 87 upregulated genes in eat-2 mutants with those regulated by factors crucial for DR-induced longevity27, including PMK-1/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK), SKN-1/nuclear factor erythroid-related factor (NRF), helix-loop-helix protein 30 (HLH-30)/transcription factor EB (TFEB), PHA-4/forkhead box A (FOXA), AMP-activated kinase 2 (AAK-2)/AMPK, and HSF-1/heat shock factor 1. We found that there is a significant overlap between genes that were dis-3-dependently upregulated in eat-2 mutants and genes regulated by PMK-1, SKN-1, HLH-30, PHA-4, and AAK-2 (Fig. 4f). We then sought to determine if there are any genetic interactions between dis-3 and these five factors for DR-induced longevity. Genetic inhibition of skn-1 did not have an additive effect to the shorter lifespan caused by the dis-3(L75Q) mutations on DR-induced longevity (Fig. 4g), suggesting that they may function in the same pathway. In contrast, knockdown or mutations in pmk-1, hlh-30, pha-4, and aak-2 further decreased the lifespan of dis-3 mutants under DR conditions, suggesting these factors act in independent pathways from DIS-3 (Fig. 4h–k). Consistent with a role for SKN-1 in DIS3-mediated effects, we uncovered significant enrichment of a SKN-1 transcription factor-binding motif (AAAATGATGACAATT) in the promoter regions (−1 kb to +100 bp) of dis-3-dependently upregulated genes in eat-2 mutants (Fig. 4l, m).

To further determine how DIS-3 affects SKN-1 activity, we measured the mRNA levels of glutathione S-transferase 4 (gst-4), an established target of SKN-128–30. We found that dis-3(L75Q) mutation suppressed upregulation of gst-4 mRNA by eat-2 mutation (Fig. 4n). Overexpression of 5′-tRH-Gln also increased gst-4 mRNA levels (Fig. 4o). In addition, we found that 12 genes (H25K10.1, semo-1, Y51H4A.5, lipl-3, K09C4.1, tag-234, T01D3.6, aagr-1, asah-1, clec-265, clec-41, and clec-66) significantly overlapped between 87 dis-3-dependently upregulated genes in eat-2 mutants and 359 SKN-1 targets (p = 3.57e−12, Supplementary Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 3)29,30. We then determined whether the SKN-1 targets were upregulated by reduced translation via tRHs by analyzing the expression levels of these genes in translation-defective ifg-1/eIF4G mutants31. Six out of the 12 selected SKN-1 targets were also upregulated by the ifg-1 mutation (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Among the 12 SKN-1 targets, seven were upregulated by 5′-tRH-Gln o/e (Fig. 4p). Consistently, we found that the skn-1 mutation suppressed longevity conferred by 5′-tRH-Gln o/e (Fig. 4q), and RNAi knockdown of skn-1 decreased the lifespan of eat-2; dis-3 double mutants treated with rpl-12 RNAi (Fig. 4r). Together, these data suggest that SKN-1/NRF, which is upregulated upon reduced translation28,32, is activated by tRHs generated by DIS-3 to impact DR-induced longevity.

DIS3 plays conserved roles in the generation of tRHs and prevention of cellular aging in mammalian cells

We then sought to determine whether the role of DIS3 in the regulation of tRHs and aging was conserved in mammalian species, using cultured human lung fibroblasts IMR-90 expressing human telomerase reverse transcriptase (IMR-90-hTERT). We first measured the levels of 5′-tRH-Gln and 5′-tRH-Asp, the two representative targets of DIS-3 in C. elegans, using stem-loop qRT-PCR, but neither of the tRH levels was significantly changed by siRNA knockdown of DIS3 (siDIS3) (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). We then assessed whether human DIS3 generates translation-regulating tRHs, including 5′-tRH-Cys, 5′-tRH-Gly, and 5′-tRH-Ala33. We found that 5′-tRH-Cys and 5′-tRH-Gly were significantly downregulated by siDIS3 (Fig. 5a, see Supplementary Fig. 7b for C. elegans results). In comparison, siRNA knockdown of ANG, encoding the mammalian tRH-generating ribonuclease2, did not significantly affect the levels of 5′-tRH-Cys or 5′-tRH-Gly (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). We then performed in vitro cleavage assay using human tRNAs to determine the enzymatic activity of DIS3 on human tRNAs. DIS3 cleaved synthetic human Cys tRNA in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 7e–i). Consistently, both wild-type (WT) DIS3 and exoribonuclease-dead mutant DIS3(D487N) cleaved native human Cys tRNA to generate 5′-tRH-Cys in vitro (Fig. 5b, c). Next, we tested whether the role of DIS3 in aging was conserved in mammalian cellular senescence, which is crucial for organismal aging34. We showed in IMR-90-hTERT that short hairpin RNA (shRNA) knockdown of DIS3 (shDIS3) increased the activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) (Fig. 5d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 7k). shDIS3 increased the mRNA and protein levels of p16 and decreased Lamin B1 (LMNB1) level, all characteristics of senescent cells (Fig. 5f–i)35. shDIS3 also decreased the number of cells positive for EdU incorporation, which reflects senescence and the permanent stop of proliferation (Fig. 5j, k)36. We also utilized human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs), a model for replicative senescence, and found that shDIS3 upregulated SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 5l, m and Supplementary Fig. 7k) and the mRNA level of p16 (Fig. 5n).

We next sought to determine the role of tRHs in cellular senescence. Transfection of IMR-90-hTERT with synthesized 5′-tRH-Cys, which we showed to be a target of DIS3 (Fig. 5a–c and Supplementary Fig. 7e–i), partially suppressed upregulation of SA-β-gal by shDIS3 while the 5′-tRH-Gly did not have significant effects, as assessed by SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 5o, p and Supplementary Fig. 7o). Furthermore, shDIS3 substantially increased the translation of a reporter firefly luciferase mRNA in vitro, an effect that could then be suppressed by the addition of 5′-tRH-Cys (Fig. 5q). Together these results suggest that DIS3 prevents premature senescence in mammalian cells by contributing to the generation of tRHs, which inhibit translation.

Discussion

Our findings establish a direct and conserved role for specific tRHs in modulating aging, with 5′-tRH-Gln promoting C. elegans longevity and 5′-tRH-Cys delaying mammalian cellular aging. While earlier studies have primarily associated tRHs with chronic age-related pathologies such as cancer and neurodegenerative diseases4,8, their functional relevance in the aging process itself has remained unexplored. Recent work showing that 5′-tRH-Glu accumulates during aging and disrupts mitochondrial function hints at a broader regulatory potential of tRHs in aging, particularly in the context of mitochondrial metabolism9. Our study goes further by demonstrating that DIS-3, a ribonuclease that promotes longevity, generates functional tRHs, providing a mechanistic link between tRH biogenesis and lifespan regulation. Many tRHs are produced in response to cellular stress2,3, important modulators of aging, and their levels increase with age, implicating their functions in aging processes. By demonstrating that small and naturally abundant tRHs can directly delay aging trajectories, our work broadens the current understanding of RNA-based regulation of aging and opens up opportunities to explore tRHs as potential modulators or even interventions in aging-related processes.

Mutations in DIS3 are frequently identified in multiple myeloma, and DIS3 is implicated as a tumor suppressor in acute myeloid leukemia, metastatic melanoma, and nodular melanoma in humans37–39. The majority of pathological DIS3 mutations alter amino acids in the exoribonuclease domain40. In addition, the exoribonuclease activity of DIS3 contributes to proper regulation of DNA:RNA hybrid41 and snoRNA processing42, which could possibly affect aging43. Our data using the dis-3(L75Q) mutation, which alters L to Q in the endoribonuclease domain, suppressed longevity and downregulated tRHs, thereby supporting the functional importance of the endoribonucleolytic activity of DIS-3. Thus, against this background of related studies, our work here showing the role of DIS-3 as an endoribonuclease for generating tRHs in lifespan regulation raises the possibility that DIS3 may regulate aging or age-related diseases in humans in a conserved manner.

Previous studies have shown cytoplasmic functions of DIS344,45. However, the factors that regulate the subcellular localization of DIS3 are poorly understood. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Kap95 and Srp1 are importins that carry Rrp44/DIS3 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus46. However, factors that contribute to the nuclear export of DIS3 remain unknown. Because our work showed an important role of cytoplasmic DIS-3/DIS3 in the regulation of longevity, identifying factors that modulate the subcellular localization of DIS-3/DIS3 will provide further insights into how DIS-3/DIS3 affects aging and longevity.

Our study primarily focused on the lifespan-regulatory role of tRHs using the representative tRHs in C. elegans and humans. Nevertheless, the roles of tRHs may vary depending on their sequence, structure, and/or tRNA-cleaving enzymes involved. Understanding distinct regulatory functions of specific tRHs will be crucial for elucidating the roles of tRHs in specific biological contexts.

The lack of lifespan extension by dis-3 overexpression in contrast to the longevity caused by the 5′-tRH-Gln overexpression suggests that tRNA-cleaving activity of DIS-3 is constrained under normal physiological conditions. Because DIS-3 is a subunit of the RNA exosome complex47, efficient tRNA cleavage may require additional factors, including other exosome components. Another possibility is substrate limitation, as many tRNAs are already engaged in protein and RNA complexes such as aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and the translational machinery, restricting the amount of tRNAs accessible to DIS-3. Thus, overexpression of dis-3 may not be able to exert its effects on tRNA cleavage because the cleavage rate is limited by substrate availability.

Differences in target tRNAs between C. elegans DIS-3 and human DIS3 may be caused by distinct natures of tRNA biology. The relative abundance of individual tRNA isotypes varies among species48, which may influence tRNA substrate preference of C. elegans DIS-3 and human DIS3. In addition, tRNAs undergo extensive post-transcriptional modifications, which also differ between C. elegans and humans48. Such nucleotide modifications on tRNAs may influence sequence features used for substrate recognition by DIS-3/DIS3. Consistent with this idea, we did not identify the U-rich sequence motifs, preferred targets of DIS3 PIN domain44, within the anticodon loops of tRNAs cleaved by DIS-3/DIS3. Such species-specific differences in tRNA modifications may cause differential substrate preference of C. elegans DIS-3 and human DIS3, contributing to distinct translation inhibition mechanisms. C. elegans 5′-tRH-Gln inhibits translation by binding ribosome (Fig. 3j, k), whereas 5′-tRH-Cys in cultured mammalian cells inhibits translation by forming G-quadruplex that sequesters eIF4G33,49. Investigating DIS-3/DIS3-dependent changes in tRNA and tsRNA landscapes will provide insights into how DIS-3/DIS3 selectively cleaves tRNAs.

There are several limitations of this study. First, although overexpression and transfection of unmodified tRHs may mimic the effects of endogenous tRHs50,51, they may not fully reflect the same functions of endogenous tRHs. Second, northern blot would enable more precise detection of tRHs than the dye-based detection that we used in this study. Third, a detailed mechanism underlying the interaction between tRHs and ribosomes, such as the identification of precise tRH-binding sites within ribosomes, remains unknown, and it will be an interesting topic for future research.

Construction of a ribonuclease RNAi sub-library

Candidate C. elegans ribonucleases were selected by using gene ontology (GO) analysis (AmiGO 2 version: 2.5.12). Seventy genes that are associated with ribonuclease or endoribonuclease activity GO terms and nonu-1 that encodes an endoribonuclease55 were used for the RNAi screen. An RNAi sub-library containing the 71 putative C. elegans ribonucleases was then constructed from Julie Ahringer’s group RNAi collection56 and in-fusion cloning (639650, Clontech, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) for additional RNAi bacteria that were not available from the Julie Ahringer library (See Supplementary Data 4 for primer information). E. coli HT115 with empty L4440 vector was used as control RNAi bacteria.

RNAi induction

RNAi induction of bacteria was performed as previously described with minor modifications53,57. RNAi bacteria were cultured in Luria broth (LB) containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin (J63807.09, AlfaAesar, Haverhill, MA, USA) overnight at 37 °C, and 100 μL of bacteria were seeded on NGM plates containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin. After the incubation of seeded-RNAi bacteria overnight at 37 °C, 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (12481C50, Gold biotechnology, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to induce double-stranded RNA and incubated for 24 h at room temperature.

Stem-loop qRT-PCR for detecting tRHs of C. elegans

Stem-loop qRT-PCR was performed as previously described with modifications58,59. Approximately 2,000 synchronized young (day 1) adult animals that were cultured on RNAi plates at 20 °C from eggs by bleaching were harvested with M9 buffer for RNA isolation. Total RNA was isolated by using RNAiso plus (9109, Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), and 1 μg of RNA for each sample was reverse-transcribed by using ImProm-II Reverse Transcriptase (A3802, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and 20 pmol of target-specific stem-loop reverse transcription (RT) primers. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Power SYBRTM Green PCR Master Mix (4367659, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) on StepOne Real-Time PCR Systems (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Relative expression was analyzed by using the comparative CT method. The average of two technical repeats was used for each biological data point. The values of control snRNA (sn2343) and snoRNA (U18) were used for normalization. TOPO cloning kit (450030, Invitrogen, Waltham, CA, USA) was used for amplified PCR products prior to sequencing.

Purification of recombinant DIS3

Purification of protein was performed as previously described with minor modifications62. Human DIS3 cDNA was amplified from a cDNA synthesized using total RNA of Institute for Medical Research-90 cells expressing human telomerase reverse transcriptase (IMR-90-hTERT) and oligo d(T)20 primer (Cosmogenetech, Seoul, South Korea) and cloned into the pGEX6P1 vector using the restriction enzymes EcoRI and NotI. The DIS3(L66Q) was generated via site-directed mutagenesis, and the parental plasmid was digested using DpnI. The pGEX6P1-DIS3 vector was transformed into Rosetta2 (DE3) pLysS competent cells. Transformed cells were then cultured in LB medium containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C with shaking until the OD600 reached 0.6. The culture was then cooled on ice to 16 °C, and protein expression was induced by adding 0.25 mM IPTG. The cells were cultured overnight (16–18 h) at 16 °C, harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in DIS3 lysis buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.6, 350 mM NaCl, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME), 0.1% (v/v) IGEPAL, 10 µg/mL DNase I, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-free) (04693132001, F. Hoffmann-La Roche, Basel, Switzerland)]. The resuspended cells were lysed by sonication (40% amplitude, 10 s on, 20 s off cycles, for a total on-time of 3 min). The lysate was then cleared by centrifugation at 35,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was loaded onto a Glutathione Sepharose® 4 Fast Flow column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) equilibrated with binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.6, 350 mM NaCl, 1 mM BME). After incubation for 1 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation, the column was washed with 40 column volumes of the same buffer. On-column cleavage was performed by treating the column with PreScission Protease (400 units) in cleavage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)) and incubating overnight at 4 °C. The cleaved DIS3 protein was eluted and further purified using a HiTrap® Q Fast Flow column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with gradient elution. Buffer A contained 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, and 2 mM TCEP, and buffer B contained the same components with 1 M NaCl. The target fractions were concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Devices and buffer-exchanged to 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 350 mM NaCl, and 1 mM BME. The purified protein was aliquoted with 20% glycerol and stored at -80 °C until use.

Ribonuclease assay

Reactions were performed in a reaction buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM BME. The reactions were incubated at 37 °C and stopped by adding an equal volume of 1 × reaction stop solution (0.2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 99.8% formamide). For metal ion-dependent tRNA hydrolysis, the reactions included 500 nM tRNA substrate and 2 µM DIS3 in reaction buffer supplemented with either 1 mM EDTA as a metal-chelation control, 3 mM MgCl2, or 3 mM MnCl2, and were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. For time-dependent tRNA hydrolysis, reactions containing 500 nM tRNA substrate, 2 µM DIS3, and 3 mM MnCl2 in reaction buffer were incubated at 37 °C in a total volume of 50 μL. Aliquots of 10 µL were collected at specific time points (0, 1, 5, 15, and 30 min) and mixed with an equal volume of 1 × reaction stop solution. For enzyme concentration-dependent tRNA hydrolysis, the reactions were performed with 500 nM tRNA substrate and varying concentrations of DIS3 (0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 µM) in reaction buffer supplemented with 3 mM MnCl2 and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. All samples were separated on a 20% denaturing polyacrylamide gel (7 M urea) and visualized using 1 × GelStar® Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Model MP, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

A sequencing library was constructed using NEBNext ® Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (E7330L, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and paired-end RNA sequencing with 100 nucleotides read length using Illumina NovaSeqX platform was performed until ~2 Gb reads were generated (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea).

Isolation and cleavage of native tRNAs

Total RNAs were isolated from mixed-stage C. elegans cultured on high growth (HG) plates seeded with OP50 and from human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) using Trizol (#15596019, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Small RNAs below 100 nucleotides were enriched from total RNAs as previously described68. Specifically, small RNAs were enriched using LiCl precipitation (L7026, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). Isolation of tRNA was performed as previously described with minor modifications69. Specifically, antisense biotinylated DNAs were bound to Pierce Streptavidin Agarose (20353, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and hybridization was performed using 10 μg of small RNA and tetraethylammonium chloride (TEA-Cl, 86614, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) at 35 °C. Eluted tRNAs were precipitated using ethanol and dissolved in 20 μL of RNase-free water. Two μL of dissolved tRNA was used for the in vitro cleavage assay. Cleavage assays were performed in a reaction buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 3 mM MnCl2, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM BME]. The reactions were initiated by adding 250 nM of DIS3, DIS3(L66Q), or DIS3(D487N) in a final reaction volume of 10 μL and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The reactions were terminated by adding an equal volume of reaction stop solution (0.2% SDS and 99.8% formamide), followed by incubation at 70 °C for 3 min. Samples were separated on a 20% denaturing polyacrylamide gel (7 M urea) and visualized using SYBRTM Gold (S11494, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA).

C. elegans lifespan assays

The lifespan of adult C. elegans was measured as previously described with minor modifications57,70. OP50 bacteria were cultured in LB containing 10 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C overnight. One hundred μL of cultured OP50 was seeded on NGM plates and incubated 37 °C overnight. For RNAi experiments, RNAi plates were prepared as described above. Stage-synchronized embryos that were obtained from gravid adults were grown until reaching adulthood at 20 °C. Prefertile adults were then transferred to new NGM plates with E. coli containing 5 μM 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine (FUDR) (F0503, Merck, St. Louis, MO, USA) that prevents progeny from hatching. For heat stress resistance assay, the animals were incubated at 37 °C. The animals that did not respond to gentle touch with a platinum wire were counted as dead. The animals that burrowed, crawled off the plates, displayed vulval rupture or internal hatching were censored but included in the statistical analysis. Lifespan assays were performed by at least two independent researchers. Statistical analysis was performed using online application for the survival analysis portable (OASISp)71.

Design of antisense sequence targeting C. elegans tRHs

Biotinylated RNA pull-down and LC-MS/MS analysis

Biotinylated RNA pull-down was performed as previously described with minor modifications77. C. elegans were cultured on HG plates, harvested at mixed-stages, and washed with M9 buffer three times. The animals re-suspended with NET-2 buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 2 mM benzamidine, and 0.05% nonidet P-40 (NP-40)] were homogenized twice at 4 m/s for 40 s using FastPrep-24 homogenizer (MP Biomedical, South Korea). 5′-biotin-labeled scrambled and 5′-tRH-Gln RNAs (IDT, Newark, NJ, USA) were used for subsequent experiments (See Supplementary Data 4 for sequence information). The lysate was pre-cleared with streptavidin resin (Thermo Scientific) for one h at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 1 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was incubated with 10 μg of biotinylated RNAs in NET-2 buffer supplemented with 0.2 U/μL RNase inhibitor for 30 min at 30 °C and irradiated with ultraviolet light (UV; 254 nm 500 mJ/cm2) using a UVP crosslinker (CL-3000, AnalytikJena, Jena, Germany). Each extract was then incubated with 50 μL of streptavidin resin for 3 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 3000 × g for 1 min at 4 °C, the biotinylated RNA-bound resin samples were washed with the following order: buffer 1 [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM LiCl, 0.5% LiDS, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT], buffer 2 [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM LiCl, 0.1% LiDS, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT], buffer 3 [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, and 0.01% NP-40], and buffer 4 [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT]. The resin-bound proteins were eluted with 4 × LiDS sample buffer (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Proteins that interacted with the 5′-tRH-Gln were identified using LC-MS/MS (PROTIA Inc., Seoul, Korea).

Transcriptome epistasis analysis

The epistasis analysis of RNA seq data is composed of wild-type (WT) animals (control), single mutants (eat-2 and dis-3), and double mutants (eat-2; dis-3). Cells were stained using Click-iT® EdU staining Kit with Alexa FluorTM 488 dye (C10337, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The nuclei of the cells were subsequently stained using 1 μg/mL DAPI in PBS. The stained cells were analyzed using EVOS M5000 cell imaging system using a GFP channel for EdU and BFP channel for DAPI. The number of EdU-positive cells and DAPI signals was manually and double-blindly counted using ImageJ74.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR for cultured mammalian cells

Total RNA in cells was isolated by using Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNAs were synthesized by using ImProm-IITM Reverse Transcriptase (Promega) with 1 μg of total RNA and 500 ng of random hexamer primers or 20 pmol of stem-loop primers. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed by using 2 × SYBRTM Green mastermix and StepOne Real-time PCR systems (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The mRNA levels of GAPDH were used as an endogenous control for normalization and analyzed by using comparative CT method.

In vitro translation of firefly luciferase mRNA

For in vitro translation of firefly luciferase, firefly luciferase mRNA was transcribed using Riboprobe in vitro transcriptions systems (P1440, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Specifically, 0.5 mM of Ribo m7G Cap analog (P1711, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was supplemented and rGTP was reduced from 0.5 mM to 0.1 mM for capping of mRNA. After transcription, mRNAs were polyadenylated using E. coli poly(A) polymerase (M0276S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) for 10 min at 37 °C. In vitro translation of firefly luciferase was performed as previously described with minor modifications92. For preparing cell lysates, cells were cultured on a 150 mm plate and trypsinized. Cell pellets were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and re-suspended with ice-cold hypotonic buffer [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 10 mM potassium acetate, 1.5 mM magnesium acetate, and 2.5 mM DTT]. Cells were then incubated on ice for 30 min and homogenized with a 27G syringe needle. Homogenized cells were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and supernatant was used for in vitro translation. In vitro translation was performed with 10 μL of cell lysate containing 100 μg of protein, 3 μg of TM buffer [132 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 112.2 mM creatine phosphate, 0.66 μg/μL creatine phosphate kinase, 1 mM spermidine, 264 μM amino acid mixture, 6.6 mM rATP, 0.792 μM rGTP, and 13.2 mM DTT], 2 μL of HT buffer [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 400 mM potassium acetate, 5 mM magnesium acetate, 4 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol], 80 mM potassium acetate, 1 mM magnesium acetate, 8 U RNase inhibitor, 10 ng firefly luciferase mRNA, and 400 pmol of tRH in 20 μL of reaction. After 1 h of reaction at 37 °C, 50 μL of luciferase assay substrate (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was added, and luciferase was immediately assayed for 1 second using SPARK Multimode Microplate Reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).
