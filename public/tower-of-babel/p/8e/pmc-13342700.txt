Atypical Spread With Lung Shrinkage and Cystic Changes in Bilateral Lepidic Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Hiroto Takiguchi, Yoko Ito, Hiroshi Kajiwara, Makiko Kobayashi, Tsuyoshi Oguma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLepidic adenocarcinoma is a subtype of lung cancer that grows along the alveolar walls and frequently harbours epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations. We report a unique case of symmetrical lepidic adenocarcinoma that progressed to cystic changes and demonstrated a distinct discrepancy between the radiological and physiological findings during EGFR‐tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) treatment. A 44‐year‐old man presented with a right upper lobe mass and bilateral, symmetrical ground‐glass nodules (GGNs), both harbouring EGFR L858R mutations. Chemotherapy, including osimertinib, induced a stepwise transition from the initial GGN attenuation to extensive bilateral cystic degeneration accompanied by restrictive pulmonary dysfunction, which is attributed to the rapid tumour regression and subsequent architectural remodelling. This case underscores the importance of recognizing unique treatment‐related changes in the setting of diffuse lepidic tumour involvement. Clinicians should be aware of these atypical imaging manifestations in patients with extensive lepidic adenocarcinoma receiving potent EGFR‐TKIs.

Introduction

Lepidic adenocarcinoma is a subtype of lung cancer that grows along the alveolar walls [1] and frequently harbours epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations. EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as osimertinib, can elicit rapid responses and have dramatically improved the prognosis of these patients. However, the subsequent long‐term structural remodelling of the lung parenchyma during rapid tumour regression has not been fully characterized. Herein, we report a rare case of bilateral symmetrical EGFR‐mutated lepidic adenocarcinoma treated with osimertinib, carboplatin and pemetrexed. The patient presented a unique radiological change from ground‐glass nodules (GGNs) to fibrotic changes, and ultimately to extensive bilateral cystic degeneration. Notably, this emphysema‐like appearance contrasted with a restrictive dysfunction on pulmonary function test. This case highlights a dramatic radiological‐physiological discrepancy and underscores the importance of recognizing unique treatment‐related changes in the setting of diffuse lepidic tumour involvement.

Case Report

A 44‐year‐old man, an ex‐smoker with a smoking history of 8 pack‐years, presented with abnormal findings detected during a health checkup. He had a history of Kawasaki disease during childhood but had no respiratory symptoms or hypoxemia. High‐resolution chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a mass with spiculation in the right upper lobe and bilateral, symmetrical distribution of GGNs. Interlobular septal thickening surrounding the mass and granular lesions on the pleural surface were also observed (Figure 1A,B). No significant mediastinal lymphadenopathy was observed. His pulmonary function test results were normal. Transbronchial lung biopsies of the right mass and the left GGNs revealed lepidic lung adenocarcinoma (Figure 2). Both lesions were positive for the EGFR L858R mutation.

Chest computed tomography (1 mm slice thickness, 512 × 512 matrix) at initial presentation (A, B) revealed a mass with spiculation in the right upper lobe and bilaterally symmetrical centrilobular ground‐glass nodules (GGNs). Interlobular septal thickening (arrowhead) and granular lesions on the pleural surface (arrow) were observed. New infiltrative opacities and consolidation developed around and obscured the margin of the right upper lobe mass at 3 months after chemotherapy, including epidermal growth factor receptor‐tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy (C). The lung parenchyma was replaced by bilateral cystic changes at 1 year after the initial presentation (E, F).

Pathological findings of the transbronchial lung biopsies of the ground‐glass nodules in the left upper lobe. Haematoxylin and eosin staining revealed tumour cell proliferation replacing the alveolar epithelial cells. The underlying lung architecture was preserved, which is consistent with lepidic lung adenocarcinoma. These findings are consistent with those observed for the right upper lobe mass.

Combination therapy with osimertinib, carboplatin and pemetrexed was initiated to achieve a rapid and maximal therapeutic response due to the extensive tumour burden and diffuse bilateral distribution. New infiltrative opacities and consolidation developed around and obscured the margin of the right upper lobe mass at 3 months after initiating osimertinib. However, the bilateral GGNs were attenuated, and traction bronchiectasis had developed (Figure 1C,D). Maintenance chemotherapy with osimertinib and pemetrexed was continued after the completion of the induction course of combination chemotherapy. The lung parenchyma was replaced by bilateral cystic changes at 1 year after the initial presentation (Figure 1E,F). Follow‐up pulmonary function testing revealed restrictive pulmonary dysfunction with impaired diffusion capacity (Table 1).

Discussion

We observed a stepwise transition from unique bilaterally symmetrical GGNs representing lepidic adenocarcinoma to extensive cystic changes during the chemotherapy, accompanied by significant lung shrinkage. The pattern of tumour dissemination in the present case is considered as aerogenous spread, based on the centrilobular distribution of GGNs along the airways and the pathologic findings of lepidic growth [1]. Notably, a distinct discrepancy was observed between the radiological and physiological findings. While the final CT findings mimic emphysematous changes, the pulmonary function test showed a restrictive pattern with a normal residual volume/total lung capacity ratio, lacking evidence of lung hyperinflation.

The primary clinical implication of these observations is the importance of correct radiological interpretation and prevention of misdiagnosis. Clinician should be aware that these dramatic cystic changes can represent successful tumour regression rather than progressive disease or drug‐induced pneumonitis. Recognizing these sequential changes guides clinical decision‐making regarding the continuation of anticancer therapy.

The most plausible explanation for cyst formation and lung shrinkage is the rapid regression of lepidic adenocarcinoma cells lining the alveolar walls and small airways, which resulted in the formation of empty airspaces, manifesting radiologically as ‘cysts’. The simultaneous lung shrinkage is interpreted as a consequence of architectural remodelling following the disappearance of tumour cells in response to effective chemotherapies, including EGFR‐TKI.

In conclusion, the present case illustrates a rare, symmetrical stepwise transition to cystic degeneration during the effective chemotherapy including an EGFR‐TKI with a notable discrepancy between emphysema‐like imaging and restrictive physiological dysfunction. This case underscores the importance of recognizing unique treatment‐related changes in the setting of diffuse lepidic tumour involvement. Clinicians should be aware of these atypical imaging manifestations in patients with extensive lepidic adenocarcinoma receiving potent EGFR‐TKIs.
