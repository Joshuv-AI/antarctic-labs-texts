Introduction

Ectopic adrenocortical adenomas that mimic renal carcinoma are extremely rare [1, 2]. We present a patient with an ectopic adrenocortical adenoma located in the cortex of the upper pole of the right kidney. Following partial nephrectomy of this upper pole, which included removal of the adenoma, performed for a clinical diagnosis of renal cell carcinoma, the patient developed tertiary adrenal insufficiency.

Case Presentation

A 49‐year‐old woman was referred to our department after being diagnosed with a tumor in the upper pole of her right kidney. She received a diagnosis of high blood pressure at the age of 38, for which she had been receiving treatment to date. At the age of 43, she suffered a pubic fracture and was diagnosed with osteoporosis, for which she also had been receiving treatment. Furthermore, continuous treatment for depression had been initiated at 45 years old. Subsequently, an elevated level of γ‐GTP was detected during a health checkup, and detailed CT identified a right renal tumor, leading to the patient's referral to our department. At the time of referral, the patient had a history of hypertension and osteoporosis but no history of diabetes. The preoperative casual blood glucose level was 87 mg/dL. She was 150 cm tall, weighed 52.3 kg, and had a body mass index (BMI) of 23.24. The physical changes associated with the patient's Cushing's syndrome were not noticed preoperatively. CT and MRI revealed a suspected renal tumor measuring 20 mm in diameter, suggestive of renal cell carcinoma (Figure 1). CT scan showed no evidence of fatty liver disease. Laparoscopic right partial nephrectomy was performed following the clinical diagnosis of renal cell carcinoma involving the upper pole of the right kidney. During dissection of the kidney from the surrounding tissues and its separation from the liver, the normal adrenal gland was identified and preserved. The operation was performed by clamping the renal artery and vein, with a warm ischemia time of 20 min, a total operative time of 2 h and 50 min, blood loss of 50 mL, and no significant changes in vital signs throughout the procedure. Immediately after surgery, the patient experienced symptoms such as fatigue, loss of appetite, and dizziness; however, her postoperative course was considered normal, and she was discharged on the seventh postoperative day. After discharge, she continued to experience fatigue and loss of appetite, prompting her return to the hospital on the 14th postoperative day. The patient also exhibited decreased renal function (serum creatinine 1.42 mg/dL), elevated C‐reactive protein levels (9.40 mg/dL), and reduced serum cortisol (1.46 μg/dL) and serum ACTH (6.16 pg/mL) levels. Additionally, she showed hyponatremia (135 mEq/L) and hypoglycemia (casual blood glucose level 69 mg/dL), leading to suspicion of adrenal insufficiency. A rapid ACTH stimulation test confirmed central hypoadrenocorticism. Pathological findings revealed ectopic, microscopically normal adrenal tissue and an adrenocortical adenoma with no malignant findings continuous with the adrenal tissue present within the fibrous capsule of the kidney (Figure 2). This led to a diagnosis of tertiary adrenal insufficiency, which developed following removal of the ectopic adrenocortical adenoma. Thus, the patient started hydrocortisone therapy at 30 mg daily, with plans to gradually taper the dosage over time. After initiation of the therapy, the patient's symptoms, including loss of appetite, gradually improved, and laboratory results also showed improvement. Retrospectively, the patient exhibited signs of Cushing's syndrome, including a moon face, central obesity, a buffalo hump, thinning of the skin, and muscle weakness caused by proximal muscle atrophy.

Discussion

We present an extremely rare case of central adrenal insufficiency developing after a partial nephrectomy involving an ectopic adrenocortical adenoma that mimicked renal cell carcinoma, along with a retrospective diagnosis of adrenal Cushing's syndrome.

Adrenal insufficiency is typically classified as primary or secondary; however, tertiary adrenal insufficiency occurs when endogenous steroid levels abruptly decline following prolonged, excessive suppression of ACTH [3]. In the present patient, the tumor, initially suspected to be renal cell carcinoma, was definitively diagnosed as an ectopic adrenocortical adenoma. Surgical removal of the tumor led to a rapid decrease in endogenous steroid levels, resulting in tertiary adrenal insufficiency postoperatively.

The presence of ectopic adrenal tissue within the kidney is rare [1, 2]. Ectopic adrenocortical adenomas arising from the adrenal cortex that develop beneath the renal capsule or in the renal hilum can easily be mistaken for renal cell carcinoma on imaging studies. Consequently, partial nephrectomy or nephrectomy is sometimes performed [4, 5, 6]. In this case, the CT findings suggested a hypervascular tumor (Figure 1). The CT value of the tumor area on the plain CT scan was approximately 40 Hounsfield units, and the fat content of the tumor was not clearly visible on the CT images. Signal loss on T1‐weighted opposed‐phase MRI images compared to in‐phase images suggests the presence of intralesional fat. Capsule formation was not clearly observed. Based on the above findings, clear cell renal carcinoma was suspected; however, the absence of a discernible capsule was atypical. Retrospectively, these findings were consistent with an adrenocortical adenoma. Retrospectively, atrophy was observed in both sides of the adrenal glands.

Although most ectopic adrenocortical adenomas are non‐functioning, there are case reports of functional adenomas causing conditions such as Cushing's syndrome, androgen excess, and, rarely, aldosterone overproduction [7, 8, 9] In cases involving functional hormones that secrete cortisol or androgens, if hormone excess is diagnosed preoperatively, postoperative management can be effectively planned. In cases where the ectopic adrenocortical adenoma is functional, sudden cessation of the high‐cortisol environment following its removal can result in postoperative adrenal insufficiency. Therefore, there is a risk of developing tertiary adrenal insufficiency postoperatively, especially if preoperative adrenal overproduction is overlooked. If the patient's body habitus suggests Cushing's syndrome, it is important to conduct endocrinological evaluation, including cortisol screening, prior to surgery. Prolonged endogenous or exogenous hypercortisolism suppresses the pituitary CRH/ACTH axis, leading to atrophy of the adrenal cortex. If an ectopic adenoma or another tumor secretes excessive amounts of cortisol, the pituitary–adrenal axis will not recover adequately if cortisol supply is abruptly discontinued following removal. This can result in postoperative symptoms of hypocortisolism, such as fatigue, hypotension, nausea, and electrolyte imbalance. The required dosage of hydrocortisone replacement therapy after surgery depends on the patient's preoperative functional status. In many reports, short‐term hormone replacement is administered post‐surgery, with the patient gradually being tapered off while recovery is assessed using ACTH stimulation tests and morning cortisol measurements [7, 8, 9] The recovery period varies significantly from case to case, lasting anywhere from several weeks to several months, or even up to a year.

Conclusion

Ectopic adrenocortical adenomas can be challenging to differentiate from renal tumors. The present patient offered a rare example of tertiary adrenal insufficiency resulting from tumor removal. Adrenal insufficiency should always be considered when nonspecific symptoms arise following renal tumor surgery. Additionally, early postoperative monitoring and glucocorticoid supplementation should be performed if necessary.
