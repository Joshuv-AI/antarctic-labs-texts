Model-order-reduced spectral-element method for high-accuracy and fast 3-D transient electromagnetic forward modeling with SAI-Krylov
Authors: Ya’nan Fan, Kailiang Lu, Yuandi Huang, Jianhua Yue, Qinrun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the increasing demand for both accuracy and efficiency in transient electromagnetic (TEM) simulations, conventional 3-D forward modeling methods face growing challenges. This study presents a high-accuracy and high-efficiency 3-D forward modeling approach that combines the spectral-element method (SEM) with a model order reduction (MOR) scheme. High-order orthogonal basis functions are employed, and the computational domain is discretized in a finite-element manner to improve modeling accuracy. During element-level analysis, a reduced-integration strategy is introduced to enhance the sparsity of the double-curl and conductivity matrices, thereby reducing the computational time and memory consumption required for matrix assembly. For temporal treatment, a shift-and-invert Krylov (SAI-Krylov) subspace algorithm is adopted: the basis and projection matrices are constructed using only one matrix factorization and tens of back-substitutions, after which low-dimensional matrix exponential functions are evaluated to efficiently obtain electromagnetic responses at arbitrary times. Comparisons with other numerical methods demonstrate the superior efficiency and accuracy of the proposed approach. Finally, simulations on a 3-D sulfide ore-body model are performed to investigate TEM field propagation for both galvanic and loop sources, confirming the capability of the method to model electromagnetic responses in complex geological settings.

Introduction

TEM is an important geophysical exploration technique. Owing to its cost-effectiveness and operational efficiency, it has been widely used in metallic mineral exploration1, geohazard investigations2,3, and groundwater prospecting4. As a fundamental tool for understanding electromagnetic field diffusion, optimizing survey design, and enabling 3-D electromagnetic inversion, 3-D TEM forward modeling has achieved substantial progress over the past two decades5. However, as requirements for numerical accuracy and computational efficiency continue to increase, conventional forward modeling approaches are facing growing challenges.

At present, the main methods for 3-D TEM forward modeling include the finite-difference method6,7, the vector finite-element method8,9, and the mimetic finite-volume method10,11. These methods typically employ linear interpolation within each element/cell, which inherently limits their ability to further improve modeling accuracy. In recent years, the spectral-element method, a high-order weighted-residual technique, has attracted increasing attention12. SEM derives the weak form of the governing partial differential equations based on the Galerkin framework and combines the strengths of spectral and finite-element methods: orthogonal basis functions with spectral convergence are used as basis and test functions, while the computational domain is discretized in a finite-element fashion using structured or unstructured meshes13,14. With this strategy, SEM can significantly enhance simulation accuracy. Although SEM has been applied to numerical simulations of DC resistivity, frequency-domain electromagnetics, and time-domain electromagnetics15,16, its large number of degrees of freedom (DOFs) often leads to a substantial increase in computational cost, which hampers broader applications. This issue becomes particularly severe for 3-D TEM forward modeling: when the temporal term is discretized using the conventional backward Euler scheme, multiple matrix factorizations and hundreds of back-substitutions are typically required, resulting in markedly reduced efficiency.

To address this issue, model order reduction provides an effective route to improve computational efficiency. MOR seeks near-optimal solutions in a reduced subspace and can substantially decrease computational complexity17,18. In recent years, Krylov-subspace-based reduction methods have become mainstream techniques for efficiently solving large-scale linear systems and have demonstrated strong performance19,20. Among various Krylov-subspace algorithms, the SAI-Krylov method, due to its efficiency and scalability, has been widely used in 3-D TEM forward modeling11,21,22. By prescribing the subspace dimension and shift parameters, SAI-Krylov constructs the basis and projection matrices using only one matrix factorization and more than one hundred back-substitutions, and then evaluates the matrix exponential of the projected system to obtain electromagnetic responses at arbitrary times11. However, this approach often replaces the forward system with a diagonal matrix spanning a broad eigenvalue range, and requires precomputing both the subspace dimension and the optimal shifts; consequently, the subspace dimension is frequently overestimated, leading to additional computational cost22. To overcome this limitation, we develop a governing-equation-based SAI-Krylov algorithm. Instead of prescribing the subspace dimension in advance, the proposed approach controls the forward modeling process using a residual-based stopping criterion, thereby avoiding unnecessary computational overhead caused by an excessively large subspace. With this improvement, the proposed method further enhances efficiency while maintaining numerical accuracy.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first present the formulation of 3-D TEM forward modeling using SEM and discuss the characteristics of high-order SEM. We then compute TEM responses using both the backward Euler scheme and the SAI-Krylov algorithm and compare their results. Next, layered models are used to validate the advantages of the proposed approach through comparisons with other numerical methods. Finally, a 3-D sulfide ore-body model is employed to demonstrate the applicability of the proposed method to complex geological settings.

Element edge indexing

Vector FEM/SEM discretizations place DOFs on element edges, so a consistent edge-based indexing is required for assembling the discrete system. We use regular hexahedral elements and adopt a structured local numbering in which edge DOFs are grouped by direction (x, y, z) and ordered by a tensor-product traversal consistent with the nodal indexing (Figs. 1 and 2).

Reduced integration and discrete orthogonality

Because the GLL basis functions are high-order polynomials, the above integral terms generally do not admit simple analytical expressions. A key advantage of SEM is that exact integration can be replaced by GLL quadrature. Although an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(n+1\right)$$\end{document}-point rule is typically required to exactly integrate products of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2n$$\end{document}-th order polynomials, we adopt an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document}-th order GLL quadrature rule in which the quadrature points coincide with the interpolation nodes. This strategy is referred to as reduced integration. As verified by Lee et al., reduced integration introduces only a small numerical error without degrading the overall convergence order of SEM, while offering significant computational benefits12. In particular, by exploiting the discrete orthogonality of the GLL basis, the conductivity matrix becomes strictly diagonal14–16.

Algorithm analysis

Motivation for high-order SEM discretization

To motivate the use of high-order spectral-element discretization in the following EM simulations, we briefly examine the effect of p-refinement in SEM interpolation. Backward Euler scheme

The Fig. 8 shows the time-stepping schemes for both the explicit and implicit methods. Specifically, in the explicit scheme, the current field values, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{n}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{n}$$\end{document}, are computed directly from the previous time step values. This method is computationally efficient but may encounter stability issues, especially for stiff problems. In contrast, the implicit scheme requires solving a system of equations involving multiple time steps (such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{n-2}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{n-1}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{n}$$\end{document}), which enhances stability.

Equation (22) shows that the time step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta}t$$\end{document} directly enters the coefficient matrix. This feature creates a major computational bottleneck for time marching. If a very small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta}t$$\end{document} is used to control the truncation error and accurately approximate the underlying continuous differential equation, the number of time steps increases dramatically. If a variable-step strategy is adopted to reduce the number of steps, each change of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta}t$$\end{document} forces a costly re-factorization of the system matrix. Given the large number of DOFs produced by high-order SEM discretization, such conventional time-domain marching strategies are not well suited to efficient large-scale simulations.

Model order reduction based on an analytical expression

After the spatial discretization by SEM, the governing equation for the electric field can be rewritten as the following first-order system of ordinary differential Eqs10,11.:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial{E}}{\partial{t}}=-AE\\E\left(t=0\right)={E}_{0}\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{0}$$\end{document} is the initial electric field at current turn-off for a step-off waveform, and the system matrix is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{A}={\mathrm{M}}_{{\upsigma}}^{-1}{\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document}. The analytical solution of this initial-value problem is given by the matrix exponential:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\left(t\right)={e}^{-t\mathrm{A}}{E}_{0}$$\end{document}

In 3-D forward modeling, the dimension of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{A}$$\end{document} typically reaches hundreds of thousands or even millions. Owing to memory limitations and computational complexity, directly evaluating the large matrix exponential \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e}^{-t\mathrm{A}}$$\end{document} is extremely difficult and impractical. Although conventional implicit time-stepping methods are stable, they often require small time steps and expensive linear solves when applied to such large stiff systems.

To efficiently compute Eq. (24), we employ a Krylov-subspace exponential integrator to approximate the action of the matrix exponential on the initial vector. The standard Krylov method constructs the subspace from powers of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{A}$$\end{document}:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{K}}_{m}\left(\mathrm{A},{E}_{0}\right)=\mathrm{span}\left\{{E}_{0},\mathrm{A}{E}_{0},\cdots,{\mathrm{A}}^{\left(m-\mathrm{1}\right)}{E}_{0}\right\}$$\end{document}

However, for the diffusion-type system matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{A}$$\end{document} in electromagnetic problems, the spectrum typically spans a wide range, which leads to slow convergence of standard Krylov iterations.

To overcome this difficulty and accelerate convergence, we adopt a SAI-Krylov technique (Fig. 9). The key idea of SAI is to apply a spectral transformation. Instead of directly using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{A}$$\end{document}, the Krylov subspace is built with the transformed matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-1}$$\end{document}:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{K}}_{m}\left({\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-1},{E}_{0}\right)=\mathrm{span}\left\{{E}_{0},{\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-1}{E}_{0},\cdots,{\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-\left(m-\mathrm{1}\right)}{E}_{0}\right\}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is a prescribed shift parameter. This transformation maps eigenvalues of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{A}$$\end{document} that are far from the origin to a region closer to the origin in the transformed spectrum (as illustrated in Fig. 9, right). The resulting eigenvalue clustering substantially improves the convergence of Krylov iterations, especially for capturing the low-frequency modes that dominate long-time electromagnetic diffusion.

Using the Arnoldi process, the SAI-Krylov algorithm generates an orthonormal basis matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{V}}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times{m}}$$\end{document} and an upper Hessenberg matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{H}}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times{m}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document} is the SAI-Krylov subspace dimension and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} is the number of DOFs in the 3-D TEM forward model. Projecting the large-scale matrix exponential onto this low-dimensional subspace yields the following approximation of the electric field at time t11:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\left(t\right)\approx{E}_{\mathrm{m}}\left(t\right)={\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{m}}{e}^{-\frac{t}{\gamma}\left({\mathrm{H}}_{m,\mathrm{m}}^{-1}-I\right)}{\parallel{E}_{0}\parallel}_{2}{\mathrm{e}}_{1}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{e}}_{1}=[\mathrm{1,0},\dots,0{]}^{T}$$\end{document} is the first canonical basis vector of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document}. In this way, the expensive evaluation of a large-scale matrix exponential is reduced to operations on a much smaller matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{H}}_{m}$$\end{document} in a subspace with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m\ll{N}$$\end{document}, thereby substantially improving computational efficiency. The method requires only one matrix factorization and several hundred back-substitutions, which greatly accelerates high-order SEM-based TEM forward modeling.

Governing-equation-based model order reduction

In the method described in the previous section, both the SAI-Krylov subspace dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document} and the optimal shift \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} must be prescribed in advance. In practice, this often leads to an overly large subspace dimension and, consequently, additional computational cost. To address this issue, we propose a governing-equation-based model order reduction approach. This method does not require fixing the SAI-Krylov subspace dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document} a priori; instead, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document}is determined adaptively by a residual-based stopping criterion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\parallel{r}_{m}\parallel\le\mathrm{tol}$$\end{document}).

The basis matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{V}}_{m}$$\end{document} and the projected matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\stackrel{\sim}{\mathrm{H}}}_{m}$$\end{document} satisfy2228\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-1}{\mathrm{V}}_{m}={\mathrm{V}}_{m}{\stackrel{\sim}{\mathrm{H}}}_{m}+{\stackrel{\sim}{h}}_{m+1,m}{v}_{m+1}{e}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{e}}_{m}=[0,\dots,\mathrm{0,1}{]}^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$$\end{document}. Rearranging Eq. (28) gives29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{V}}_{m}={\mathrm{V}}_{m}{\mathrm{H}}_{m}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right){\stackrel{\sim}{h}}_{m+1,m}{v}_{m+1}{e}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}{\stackrel{\sim}{\mathrm{H}}}_{m}^{-1}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{H}}_{m}=\frac{1}{\gamma}({\stackrel{\sim}{\mathrm{H}}}_{m}^{-1}-\mathrm{I})$$\end{document}. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\stackrel{\sim}{h}}_{m+1,m}$$\end{document} is a very small value close to zero, Eq. (29) implies30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{H}}_{m}={\mathrm{V}}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{V}}_{m}$$\end{document}

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (23) by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{V}}_{m}^{T}$$\end{document} yields31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{V}}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\frac{\partial{E}\left(r,t\right)}{\partial{t}}=-{\mathrm{V}}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{V}}_{m}{\mathrm{V}}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}E\left(r,t\right)$$\end{document}

Applying the SAI-Krylov subspace MOR to the original system (23) and defining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{u}(\mathrm{r},t)={\mathrm{V}}_{m}^{T}\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{r},t)$$\end{document}, we obtain the reduced-order system32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\partial{u}\left(r,t\right)}{\partial{t}}=-{\mathrm{H}}_{m}u\left(r,t\right)$$\end{document}

The corresponding SAI-Krylov subspace is33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{K}}_{m}\left({\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-1},{u}_{0}\right)=\mathrm{span}\left\{{u}_{0},{\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-1}{u}_{0},\cdots,{\left(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}\right)}^{-\left(m-\mathrm{1}\right)}{u}_{0}\right\}$$\end{document}

Equation (32) has dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m\ll{N}$$\end{document}. The solution of the original governing equation can then be recovered by34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\left(r,t\right)={\mathrm{V}}_{m}u\left(r,t\right)$$\end{document}

Here \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document} denotes the final subspace dimension obtained when the stopping criterion is satisfied. After obtaining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\left(r,t\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial{B}\left(r,t\right)/\partial{t}$$\end{document} can be computed accordingly.

To assess whether the SAI-Krylov projection subspace is sufficiently accurate, we use a residual-based termination criterion, since the residual provides an inexpensive and effective error indicator. Following Botchev et al.23, the residual is computed as:35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{r}}_{m}=\frac{1}{\gamma}(I+\gamma\mathrm{A}){h}_{\mathrm{m}+1,m}{v}_{m+1}{\mathrm{e}}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathrm{H}}_{m}^{-1}u$$\end{document}

All quantities in (35) are obtained during the Arnoldi/SAI-Krylov construction, so evaluating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{m}$$\end{document} incurs negligible extra cost. With a prescribed tolerance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{tol}$$\end{document}, the subspace is expanded if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\parallel{r}_{m}\parallel>\mathrm{tol}$$\end{document}; otherwise, the current \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m$$\end{document}-dimensional subspace is accepted and the reduced-order solution is computed using (34). The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm 2.

Numerical simulations

The proposed solver is implemented in Fortran. All numerical experiments in this manuscript were run on a Windows 10 workstation. The code was compiled using Microsoft Visual Studio with the Intel Fortran compiler. For the sparse linear solves and matrix factorizations required by the workflow (including the shifted system in the SAI-Krylov MOR), we use the PARDISO sparse direct solver. In particular, the coefficient matrix is factorized with PARDISO and the resulting factors are reused for subsequent back-substitutions in the MOR stage.

In terms of accuracy, the governing-equation-based MOR method combined with SEM achieves mean relative errors of about 0.3%–0.6% for second order and above, whereas the backward Euler method yields mean relative errors of about 1%–2%. In terms of efficiency, under the same SEM order and integration strategy, MOR accelerates the forward modeling by approximately 16–20× for second to fourth order, while the memory consumption remains of the same order as that of the backward Euler method.

Therefore, considering both accuracy and efficiency, the governing-equation-based MOR method exhibits clear advantages over the conventional backward Euler scheme. When combined with reduced integration and high-order SEM (≥ 2), it can significantly improve the computational efficiency of 3-D TEM forward modeling while maintaining high accuracy.

In several time-domain EM benchmarks, the error reduction with increasing polynomial order shows an apparent plateau. This typically indicates a shift in the dominant error source: once p-refinement reduces the spatial discretization error to a sufficiently low level, the remaining discrepancy is governed by non-spatial error floors (e.g., time discretization, solver tolerances, and MOR algorithmic settings such as shift-parameter selection), and may also be influenced by solution regularity near the source and at early times. Therefore, under the present simulation settings, a clear spectral-like (near-exponential) error-decay trend with increasing polynomial order is often difficult to observe at the solution level in these EM simulations, even though higher-order SEM still improves the accuracy–efficiency trade-off relative to low-order baselines.

Figure 20a and c show the spatial distributions of the induced electromotive force in a homogeneous half-space excited by the galvanic source at 0.01 ms, 0.12 ms, and 2.0 ms. At 0.01 ms after current turn-off, the induced electromotive force is mainly concentrated near the source and forms a pair of opposite-polarity lobes on the two sides of the transmitter: a pronounced negative region on the left and a positive region on the right. The contours are densely clustered around the source, indicating that the early-time induced field is still confined to the shallow region close to the transmitter. At 0.12 ms, the positive and negative regions expand laterally, the zero-contour moves farther away from the source, and the overall amplitude decreases, suggesting that the induced currents diffuse over a larger scale and the field pattern gradually transitions from a strongly localized response to a more regional one. At 2.0 ms, the distribution approaches a slowly varying bipolar structure. Only a large-scale positive–negative gradient remains across the computational domain, reflecting the strong diffusion at late times and the decay of high-frequency components.

Figure 20d and f show the corresponding distributions for the loop source at the same times. At 0.01 ms, a pronounced positive core appears within the loop, surrounded by a ring of negative values near the loop perimeter. The contours are approximately concentric, forming a typical “positive core–negative ring” pattern, which reflects the circular induced-current system generated by the loop source. As time advances to 0.12 ms, the central positive region expands outward and its amplitude decays, while the surrounding negative ring is pushed farther away. The contours remain nearly axisymmetric about the loop center. By 2.0 ms, the contours become smoother, indicating that the induced currents have dispersed over a larger scale and the local confinement imposed by the loop source has weakened substantially.

A comparison of the two source types shows that, in a homogeneous half-space, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z$$\end{document}-component of the induced electromotive force generated by the galvanic (electric) source exhibits a pronounced bipolar pattern on the two sides of the transmitter, and its diffusion is mainly manifested as a gradual expansion of this bipolar feature. In contrast, the loop (magnetic) source produces a quasi-axisymmetric “positive core–negative ring” pattern centered on the loop plane, which gradually evolves into a smooth distribution dominated by a central monopole. These results demonstrate that the source type and geometry play a decisive role in the spatial distribution and temporal evolution of the induced electromotive force.

Figure 21a and c illustrate the evolution of the electromagnetic response excited by the galvanic source. At early diffusion times (Fig. 21a), the electromagnetic field is dominated by source effects and shallow formations. The response is characterized by very strong positive and negative anomalies tightly confined to the vicinity of the grounded wire. At this stage, the induced currents have not yet diffused to the target depth, the contours mainly expand outward in a quasi-concentric manner, and the conductive anomaly perturbs the field only weakly.

As time progresses to the intermediate stage (Fig. 21b), the field gradually diffuses downward and laterally. The field strength near the source decreases, and the induced currents begin to preferentially concentrate within the conductive zone. Notably, the positive-response center on the right side of the wire source no longer remains attached to the transmitter but shifts to the lower-right direction and gradually approaches the conductive anomaly, indicating that the anomaly starts to exert a significant influence on the electromagnetic field.

At late diffusion times (Fig. 21c), the induced currents in the background have largely decayed, and the remaining eddy currents are primarily concentrated within the conductive anomaly. The closed contour shapes closely match the anomaly outline, clearly delineating the horizontal position and geometry of the target, which suggests that the galvanic source provides good late-time resolution for conductive bodies.

Figure 21d and f show the response characteristics of the loop source. Unlike the galvanic source, the inductive source generates eddy currents that produce a predominantly monopolar response within and near the loop area.

At early times (Fig. 21d), the electromagnetic field is again controlled by the transmitter geometry. The induced currents are concentrated in the shallow region directly beneath the loop, the strongest surface signal occurs near the geometric center of the loop, and the contours form approximately symmetric concentric rings. The anomaly has little influence on the contour shapes, indicating that early-time signals cannot resolve the deep target.

In the intermediate stage (Fig. 21e), as the induced-current ring diffuses downward and outward in a “smoke-ring” manner, the influence of the anomaly becomes apparent. The maximum-response region deviates from the loop center, and the contours become distorted and elongate along the strike direction of the anomaly.

At late times (Fig. 21f), the induced eddy currents are mainly concentrated within the conductive anomaly. The induced electromotive force is then largely dominated by the anomaly, and the contours are well focused within the projected area of the target. The anomaly boundaries are outlined by the contours, demonstrating good lateral resolution and localization capability.

By comparing the propagation processes for the two transmitter types, we find that although their field patterns differ substantially, their responses to the conductive anomaly follow the same physical mechanism, namely a spatiotemporal evolution characterized by “source-dominated at early times, diffusion and migration at intermediate times, and target-dominated at late times”.

Notably, at 2.0 ms, the response excited by the galvanic source (Fig. 20c) appears to better conform to the elongated outline of the anomaly, particularly providing a clearer delineation of the left boundary. This is mainly because the conduction current generated by the galvanic source tends to accumulate within the conductive body, yielding a current-density distribution that more directly reflects the target geometry. In contrast, the purely inductive eddy currents excited by the loop source (Fig. 20f) tend to concentrate in the core of the anomaly, leading to a more compact response and a weaker sensitivity to the target edges.

Conclusions and outlook

This study proposes a high-accuracy and fast 3-D transient electromagnetic forward-modeling method that combines the spectral-element method with the SAI-Krylov subspace algorithm. Numerical experiments on a 1-D homogeneous half-space model, a layered model, and a complex 3-D sulfide deposit model demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves a favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off under the tested settings. For the reported benchmarks, high-order SEM coupled with SAI-Krylov improves simulation accuracy (with relative errors generally below 1% for the considered responses and time windows) while substantially enhancing computational efficiency, yielding a 16–20× speedup over the reduced-integration backward Euler scheme used in this work. We note that in practical time-domain EM simulations, the observable accuracy improvement with increasing spatial order may plateau once non-spatial error floors dominate; therefore, our main claim is the improved accuracy–efficiency trade-off relative to practical baselines rather than spectral convergence at the solution level.

Future work will focus on improving the adaptability of the proposed method to complex geological settings. This includes integrating non-structured mesh generation techniques, local adaptive meshing, and mesh-refinement strategies to better handle small-scale structures and irregular topography, which are common in geological models. Additionally, to address large-scale 3-D problems, we will explore the development of parallel computing frameworks and investigate combinations of multigrid techniques with model order reduction (MOR) to further improve efficiency and scalability. We also plan to conduct a dedicated comparison against representative high-order FEM/hp-FEM implementations to benchmark high-order discretizations under comparable accuracy targets and further evaluate their performance in complex geological scenarios.
