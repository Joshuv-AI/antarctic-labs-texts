Matrix stiffness-dependent regulation of immunomodulatory genes in human MSCs is associated with the lncRNA CYTOR
Authors: Justin J. Lim, Kyle H. Vining, David J. Mooney, Benjamin J. Blencowe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMechanotransduction, the translation of external physical cues to internal biochemical signals, influences cellular processes and behaviors. Here, we explore such mechanisms that underlie cellular responses to environmental stiffness using a 3D, tissue-mimetic culturing platform. We find that varying matrix stiffness differentially regulates cellular adhesion to the environment, which, partly through the activity of a long noncoding transcript the cytoskeleton regulator RNA (CYTOR), affects the immunomodulatory functions of perivascular mesenchymal stromal cells. These cells are widely studied for their use in cell therapies for the treatment of diseases affected by pathological inflammation. Overall, our findings further the understanding of mechanotransduction in 3D environments, and have potential implications for developing strategies to manipulate these pathways in therapeutic contexts.

Cells can actively perceive and respond to their physical environment through mechanotransductive processes that translate mechanical cues to impact a wide array of pathophysiological processes and basic cellular functions, including differentiation, migration, and proliferation (1, 2). Although such mechanobiological mechanisms have been well characterized in 2D tissue culture systems, the extent to which the resulting insights are directly applicable to 3D cultures remains unclear. The predominant model of mechanotransduction centers around the “molecular clutch” mechanism, wherein higher substrate rigidities generally stabilize the engagement of contractile actomyosin filament-adaptor protein–integrin complexes (clutches) with the extracellular matrix (ECM), forming focal adhesions (3, 4). Downstream signaling cascades subsequently modify cell phenotypes and behaviors through changes in gene regulation (2). However, the role of the molecular clutch mechanism in 3D is uncertain, given that cells in this environment often lack defined focal adhesions and their corresponding actin stress fibers (5–7). Cell–ECM interactions in 3D extend beyond those that are mediated by cellular traction forces. Encapsulated cells are mechanically confined, and they must mold their environment through protease-mediated degradation and/or cell-generated protrusive forces to expand, proliferate, and migrate (8). Accordingly, mechanosensory mechanisms, corresponding mechanotransduction pathways, and behavioral responses to changes in ECM mechanics do not always translate between 2D and 3D systems (9).

Moreover, despite indications of the regulatory intersection between long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) and mechanotransduction, their roles in cellular mechanoresponses are not well understood. lncRNAs are defined as transcripts that exceed 500 nucleotides in length and are thought to generally lack the capacity to encode functional protein products although many express short peptides (10). LncRNAs have important and wide-ranging roles in the control of gene expression through the regulation of chromatin structure, nuclear condensates, and sequestration of posttranscriptional regulatory machinery (11). Importantly, lncRNAs are modulators of the cytoskeleton and focal adhesions, which are essential components of the mechanosensory apparatus (12). As expected, recent studies have elucidated the critical involvement of lncRNAs in mediating functional responses to diverse mechanical cues across various cell types, thereby opening broad avenues for the exploration of mechanotransductive mechanisms (13–15).

The study of mechanotransduction in 3D has potential clinical implications, particularly in regenerative and immunomodulatory therapies that employ cell-laden hydrogels (16, 17). These hydrogels are currently being explored for the treatment of chronic kidney disease (18), spinal cord injury (19), and skin lesions (20, 21), among others. Recently, there has been a surge in efforts to rationally design the physical properties of such substrates, such as stiffness, to optimize the therapeutic efficacy of encapsulated cells by leveraging mechanosensing mechanisms (17). However, the outcomes of these efforts are inconsistent (22), raising concerns over the effectiveness and utility of this approach. Notably, there are conflicting reports that both soft and rigid matrices can enhance the secretion of immunosuppressive factors by mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) (23–28), which are employed in cell therapies for conditions typically characterized by pathological inflammation (17). Indeed, functional responses to matrix rigidity can vary widely under different chemical (29, 30) and physical (31) compositions of the matrix, cell densities (32), and tested ranges of stiffness (33). Such diversity in experimental conditions from study to study renders it challenging to form generalizable interpretations of the findings and predictions of functional outcomes in response to physical stimuli. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms that underlie cellular responses to matrix mechanics within different experimental systems would facilitate a better understanding how different confounding factors influence this mechanobiology.

In the present study, we have characterized stiffness-responsive behaviors, and their regulatory mechanisms, in bone marrow–derived MSCs. MSCs were encapsulated in a hydrogel that consists of an interpenetrating network (IPN) of alginate and fibrillar collagen type I (31). The stiffness of this system can be tuned independently of viscoelasticity and ligand density by modifying the extent of alginate crosslinking. We adjusted the biophysical parameters of the IPN to recapitulate the physiological range of the native bone marrow niche (34, 35), and performed a global-scale analysis of the stiffness-sensitive transcriptome using deep-coverage RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq). Our findings reveal that substrate rigidity modulates cell–ECM adhesions which determine the expression of functional networks of genes that regulate the composition and architecture of the ECM, and the secretion of immunoregulatory factors, specifically PGE2, in MSCs. Moreover, we detected stiffness-responsive lncRNAs and identified cytoskeleton regulator RNA (CYTOR) as the most highly expressed and differentially regulated lncRNA. CYTOR was further implicated in immunomodulatory mechanoresponses and its manipulation was shown to alter the expression of secreted cytokines that have previously documented therapeutic utility. Overall, our results reveal mechanisms underlying cellular responses to stiffness-driven changes in 3D collagen IPNs that are distinct from canonical mechanotransductive pathways observed on 2D substrates, and they further illuminate a mechanosensitive pathway involving an immunoregulatory lncRNA.

Results

Stiff Alginate-Collagen I IPNs Are Mechanically Confining and Inhibit Cell Elongation.

We isolated MSCs from human bone marrow aspirates sourced from three anonymous donors. Consistent with the guidelines for typing MSCs established by the International Society for Cell and Gene Therapy (36), the cells were positive for CD105, CD73, and CD90, but did not express the endothelial and T cell markers, CD34 and CD3, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We observed that MSCs derived from one particular donor maintained their proliferative capacity at later passages. For this reason, we chose to expand and utilize these cells for subsequent experiments. To assess the consistency of findings between different cell types, we repeated our experiments with primary BJ fibroblasts. We predicted that these cells would have similar responses to changes in matrix mechanics since dermal fibroblasts and MSCs are both mesenchymal cells that share many phenotypic characteristics (37). MSC and BJ cells were encapsulated in an IPN composed of alginate and collagen I that retains fibrillar collagen structures across a range of stiffnesses (31) (Fig. 1A). By adjusting the crosslinker [CaCO3] concentration to 9.5, 15, and 30 mM, we tuned the storage modulus (G’), or elasticity, of the hydrogel to approximately 150, 500, and 2,000 Pa, as confirmed by rheological time- and frequency-sweep experiments (Fig. 1 B and C). The loss tangent (tan δ), a measure of viscosity, remained consistent under these conditions (Fig. 1D). These values fall within the stiffness and viscoelastic range of physiological bone marrow (34, 35). Additionally, the viability of encapsulated MSC and BJ cells, as measured by trypan blue staining after cell retrieval, was unchanged across the varying stiffness conditions for at least 3 d (Fig. 1E).

Although cells are capable of mechanically deforming, expanding, and migrating through nanoporous and protease-resistant environments formed by polymers such as alginate (38), phalloidin and Hoescht staining of MSC and BJ cells demonstrated that they failed to elongate in the more rigid IPNs (Fig. 1 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). At 150 Pa, cell morphologies were heterogenous but reflective of mesenchymal migration cycles in 3D collagen gels with uniaxial protrusions tipped with small lamellipodial blebs (39, 40) (Fig. 1H). Since cell migration and spreading in 3D are dependent on matrix plasticity (38) and viscosity (41), we speculate that our hydrogels may be lacking in mechanical compliance, particularly at 2,000 Pa, hindering actin-based protrusions from physically reorganizing the matrix. Interestingly, while this mode of migration is dependent on the activity of contractile non-muscle myosin II (NMII) motors (39), the inhibition of NMII with blebbistatin, did not affect overall cell lengths (Fig. 1J). Previous studies have found that blebbistatin treatment does not completely abolish migration, but instead, reduces velocity (39, 40), which may not be evident in our snapshots of cell morphology. Thus, the extent of cell elongation does not necessarily correlate with migratory behavior. We then observed that cells at 2,000 Pa were significantly smaller in size, a property not inherently correlated with cell shape (Fig. 1 G and I). This reduction in volume is actively maintained by NMII-mediated contractions (Fig. 1J) that are likely localized to the cortex given that the cells possess a thick layer of actin filaments near the cell surface (Fig. 1K). Overall, these findings diverge from observations in 2D culture, where increasing stiffness promotes cell migration and spreading (42). Additionally, at 150 Pa, we detected perinuclear stress fibers that are normally associated with NMII-mediated mechanotransduction on rigid 2D surfaces (43) (Fig. 1K). Therefore, in contrast to previously documented observations of cellular mechanoresponses in 2D cultures (3), we anticipated that stiffness sensitivity of the transcriptome at 150 Pa, but not at 2,000 Pa, would be dependent on NMII activity, rendering it susceptible to blebbistatin treatment (43).

The Stiffness-Sensitive Transcriptome Is Enriched in ECM-Regulatory and Secreted Immunomodulatory Factors.

To globally investigate the mechanisms of mechanosensation, the role of NMII within this context, and the stiffness responsiveness of MSCs in our 3D substrates, we performed a poly(A)-enriched RNA-seq analysis of encapsulated MSCs. The cells were cultured in IPNs with stiffnesses of approximately 150, 500, and 2,000 Pa and with either DMSO or blebbistatin treatment. To minimize the proteolytic degradation of collagen fibers and disruption to the matrix architecture (44), we extracted cells at 1 and 2 d postencapsulation. Our study of these shorter timepoints would thus be expected to capture early changes in the transcriptome that are directly impacted by stiffness. We analyzed both gene expression and alternative splicing (AS) changes under these conditions. Previous studies have shown that matrix-mechanical properties can modulate AS through the regulation of splicing factors (45, 46); however, the extent to which this occurs on a transcriptome-wide scale has not yet been investigated. To study stiffness-dependent changes in AS, we first analyzed our dataset using Vertebrate Alternative Splicing and Transcription Tools (VAST-TOOLs), a software suite that aligns sequencing reads to a library of all possible annotated splice junctions (47–50). For each gene, VAST-TOOLs distinguishes and quantifies the fraction of transcripts that include a particular cassette exon, or alternative 3′/5′ splicing event, referred to as “percent-spliced-in” (PSI). Similarly, it quantifies a “percent-intron-retained” (PIR) value for intron retention events. We observed that, even by 2 d postencapsulation, there are relatively (compared to other cellular conditions previously analyzed) small numbers of differentially spliced events between stiffness conditions, with the majority of these changing events detected in pairwise comparisons between 150 and 2,000 Pa (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Furthermore, these events generally have minor changes or “deltas” in PSI (dPSI) or PIR (dPIR) (i.e., ≈10%) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). We did not detect enrichment for any Gene Ontology or Reactome pathways among the differentially spliced genes. Therefore, at least within the timescale and stiffness range of the experiment, matrix rigidity does not appear to have a substantial impact on the transcriptome at the level of AS.

Subsequently, we analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with DESeq2’s Likelihood ratio test (51) (Dataset S1). Most DEGs exhibited a monotonic change in abundance from 150 to 2,000 Pa, with an intermediate level of expression at 500 Pa (Fig. 2 A and B). This trend was consistent at both 24- and 48-h postencapsulation with DMSO-treated MSCs and similar gene expression patterns were observed following treatment with blebbistatin (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Notably, distinct Gene Ontology Biological Processes were associated with either low or high stiffness by the second day of hydrogel culture. At 150 Pa, genes related to substrate adhesion and ECM organization were up-regulated, whereas, at 2,000 Pa, we observed an enrichment for genes involved in the inflammatory response and activation of immune cells (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). We then inspected the expression of core matrisome and matrisome-associated genes (52) to better understand how stiffness affects the ability of the cells to modify their microenvironment (Fig. 2D). Both stiffness environments activated ECM regulators, such as secreted proteases or protease inhibitors; however, the soft matrix, specifically, up-regulates several lysyl oxidases. These enzymes catalyze the crosslinking of collagen and are critical for the integrity of collagen fibrils (53). In addition, levels of genes associated with focal adhesions, including ITGB1, ZYX, FLNA, ACTB, and ACTN4, were elevated at 150 Pa. It therefore appears that cells in soft hydrogels have a higher capacity to interact with and regulate the collagen matrix, which would be consistent with their migratory behavior (Fig. 1H). On the other hand, in agreement with our Gene Ontology enrichment analyses, more rigid matrices up-regulated the expression of secreted factors that can recruit and activate other ECM-regulatory cell types, namely immune cells (Fig. 2D). For example, IL16 and CCL7 can attract and activate macrophages that express and modify collagen (54–57). We also detected the upregulation of many immunomodulatory genes with known therapeutic functions, such as PTGS2 (58, 59), HGF (60), TNFAIP6 (61), LIF (62), and CHI3L1 (63–65) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). These genes are likely direct and early responders to stiffness as their differential expression was observed even at 24 h in hydrogel culture (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Using media conditioned by MSCs encapsulated in either 150 or 2,000 Pa IPNs, we quantified the secretion of two of the most differentially expressed immunoregulatory factors (TNFAIP6, PTGS2), or their enzymatic product, that also exhibited high levels of absolute transcript abundance. Although levels of TNFAIP6 were under the detectable limit of the assay, we found that prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a derivative of arachidonic acid produced by PTGS2, was significantly enriched in high stiffness conditions, presumably due to elevated PTGS2 levels (Fig. 2E). Importantly, BJ cells exhibited similar transcriptional responses to stiffness and enhanced secretion of PGE2 in rigid matrices (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S12).

Matrix Stiffness Modulates Pathways that Are Activated by Inflammatory Signaling In Vivo and Associated with NF-κB and AP-1 Pathways.

Given that our analyses highlighted the potential of matrix stiffness to activate functional gene networks in vitro, we asked whether these pathways reflect distinct cell states within mechanically heterogenous bone marrow in vivo (66). More specifically, we asked whether the stiffness-responsive gene signatures detected in vitro are also present in distinct homeostatic or disease-associated subpopulations of bone marrow MSCs. We aimed to study the physiological significance of our findings while identifying genes or pathways that may serve as potential mediators of stiffness responses in situ. To address this, using Seurat v4 (67), we combined and analyzed three single-cell RNA-seq datasets of bone marrow aspirates that are enriched for stromal cells by flow cytometry (68–70). These datasets consisted of samples derived from a total of 13 patients with multiple myeloma, a hematological malignancy associated with inflammation of the bone marrow (71), and 18 other “control,” patients with varying health statuses (healthy, heart disease, osteoarthritis). The aggregated dataset consisted of a total of 81,974 cells comprising those of both hematopoietic and stromal origins (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). We then independently clustered stromal cells that were identified by the expression of MSC markers, LEPR and CXCL12 (72) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Among these cells, we identified nine subpopulations: one endothelial, one osteogenic, three hematopoietic, and four MSC. The endothelial cluster expressed the classical markers of vascular cells, CDH5 and THBD (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Osteogenic cells were defined by the expression of the master osteogenic transcription factor, RUNX2, and the osteoblast markers, COL1A1, SPP1, and BGLAP (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). The three hematopoietic clusters were likely cell doublets or ECM-regulatory myeloid cells as they transcribed a mixture of hematopoietic, myeloid, and MSC markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 D and E). All MSCs, totaling 37,325 cells, expressed LEPR, VCAM1, CXCL12, ANGPT1, and KITLG (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8E).

To probe for functional differences between each MSC cluster, we found genes that were overrepresented in each population relative to all others and performed an enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology categories (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Interestingly, the MSC subpopulations (arbitrarily designated as MSC1-4) could be distinguished by genes associated with general cellular functions in protein translation, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, stress responses, and inflammation. MSC4 cells up-regulated a broad spectrum of ribosomal proteins which, in the absence of the coordinated expression of markers of lineage specification, suggests that they are potentially proliferative (73, 74) (Dataset S5). The MSC1 cluster appears to represent cells in close proximity to the vasculature (Dataset S2). They are characterized by the overexpression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, control of oxidative stress, and oxygen homeostasis, such as MT-ND1 or MT-CO3, and the hemoglobin subunits, HBB, HBA1, and HBA2 (75, 76). MSC3 cells express immediate-early response genes and components of the activator protein 1 (AP-1) pathway: IER2, IER5L, JUN, and FOS (77) (Dataset S4). Their transcriptional state could be attributed to stresses associated with the sample isolation and preparation process or an intermediate stage of cellular stimulation toward proliferation, differentiation, or even immunomodulation in response to some external trigger within the stromal niche. On the other hand, MSC2 cells are characterized by the expression of chemokines, collagens, angiogenic factors, and ECM regulators, indicating that these cells play an active role in modulating their tissue microenvironment (Dataset S3). Furthermore, MSC2 cells were specifically enriched among patients with multiple myeloma (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A).

After confirming that our integrative analyses could identify distinct subgroups of MSCs, we calculated module scores of enrichment for gene expression signatures that were up-regulated at either high (2,000 Pa) or low (150 Pa) stiffness (Dataset S1) from our bulk RNA-seq analysis (Figs. 2B and 3A). Contrary to our initial expectations, both stiffness signatures were detected in the same MSC cluster, MSC2 (Fig. 3 A and B). MSC2 cells constituted 76% and 61% of cells that scored highly for the low and high stiffness modules, respectively (Fig. 3C). We then found that the expression levels of these signatures were positively correlated with one another across all cells (Pearson, R = 0.76, P < 2.2e-16). In addition, all myeloma-derived MSCs were enriched for both gene signatures, when compared to the control cells, regardless of their cluster identity (Fig. 3D). Therefore, we were unable to correlate our stiffness-responsive gene sets with specific MSC subpopulations. We speculate that not all stromal cell clusters in vivo are represented in these single-cell datasets. Populations with low cell abundance and lacking marker expression would not be effectively enriched by flow cytometry (68–70). Nonetheless, matrix stiffness seems to impact specific molecular pathways that are associated with inflammation and malignancy (68), underscoring a potential interplay between mechanical cues and inflammatory signaling within the bone marrow microenvironment. Indeed, using EnrichR (78, 79), we detected a strong enrichment for TNFα signaling pathways (Odds Ratio = 11.88, adjusted P-value = 3.58e-27) among 125 stiffness-responsive genes that were up-regulated in myeloma (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). These findings were consistent with a univariate linear modeling analysis of transcription factor regulon activity (80, 81) where we found an activation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) in myeloma-associated MSCs (Fig. 3F). Moreover, the gene expression profiles of these cells correlated with the activity of transcription factors (TFs) within the AP-1 pathway, ATF1/2/4, JUN, and FOS (Fig. 3F). Considering that NF-κB and AP-1 TFs were also activated in rigid matrices (Fig. 4B), our findings imply that both substrate stiffness and myeloma-associated inflammatory environmental cues may operate via analogous pathways to yield similar immunomodulatory changes in MSCs.

The Stiffness-Dependent Activation of Immunomodulatory Genes Is Mediated by an Inhibition of Cell–ECM Adherence.

We next investigated how changes in matrix stiffness are transduced to regulate gene expression. To assess the role of intracellular contractility, we ran pairwise differential expression analyses between blebbistatin and DMSO-treated samples at 150, 500, and 2,000 Pa, and compared these results to genes that were stiffness-sensitive between 150 and 2,000 Pa. We focused our analyses on the endpoints of our stiffness range because genes are generally monotonically responsive to matrix rigidity (Fig. 2 A and B). Consistent with our observations of encapsulated cell morphology, blebbistatin affected a much larger number of genes in soft vs. stiff IPNs, and there were a significant number of DEGs that overlap with those that were stiffness-responsive (Hypergeometric test, P < 1e-21) (Fig. 4A). However, immunomodulatory genes and the regulons of NF-κB and AP-1 seemed to be mostly unaffected by myosin inhibition (Fig. 4B). Instead, blebbistatin-regulated DEGs were enriched in Gene Ontology Biological Process categories related to tissue morphogenesis and wound healing (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). This included growth factors, transcription factors, and some genes associated with ECM regulation and migration, such as LOX, LOXL2, MMP14, and ITGA5 (Dataset S6). Furthermore, overall patterns of gene expression changes across stiffness were not significantly different in blebbistatin-treated cells compared to DMSO-treated samples (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

In peptide-functionalized alginate hydrogels, matrix viscoelasticity regulates cell adherence (82). Cells within more elastic gels have diminished levels of peripheral integrin which impedes their ability to distort their surroundings and effectively induce ligand clustering (82). Considering that stiffer IPNs are less mechanically compliant (Fig. 1 F–J) and cells cultured in these environments transcriptionally down-regulate components of focal adhesions and the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 2C), we postulated that cells are also less adherent to the matrix in more rigid conditions. Since the recruitment and phosphorylation of FAK is a critical early step in focal adhesion maturation and assembly (83), we produced immunoblots to gauge the level of FAK activation in soft and stiff IPNs (Fig. 4C). These blots demonstrated that phosphorylation at Tyr397 is strongly inhibited at 2,000 Pa, providing support for our hypothesis. To ascertain whether this phenomenon directly contributes to mechanotransduction, we interfered with cell–collagen I adhesions in MSC and BJ cells using AIIB2 (84), an antibody specific for the collagen-binding integrin, ITGB1 (85). AIIB2 is function-blocking and has well-established applications in the inhibition of ITGB1 signaling, impeding adhesion-dependent processes, such cell invasion (86) or cancer metastasis (87) in culture and mouse models. Subsequently, we performed real-time qPCR reactions to assess the relative expression of 25 stiffness-responsive genes, including those with therapeutic relevance (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Remarkably, upon AIIB2 treatment in soft gels, immunomodulatory genes and factors involved in ECM regulation or substrate adhesion were up- and down-regulated, respectively, to levels observed at 2,000 Pa. In addition, AIIB2 exposure at 2,000 Pa does not induce additional changes in expression of most genes. Collectively, these findings offer compelling evidence that matrix stiffness works through the dynamics of cell–ECM adhesions, but not myosin activity, to direct the immune functions of encapsulated mesenchymal cells.

CYTOR Is a Mechanoresponsive lncRNA that Regulates Immunomodulatory Gene Expression in MSCs.

We next sought to identify mechanosensitive lncRNAs, regulated by adhesion signaling pathways, that facilitate the stiffness-driven expression of immunomodulatory genes. We performed a bulk RNA-seq experiment with ribosomal RNA (rRNA)-depleted libraries prepared from cells that were encapsulated in IPNs with storage moduli of 150, 500, and 2,000 Pa. This approach captures both polyadenylated and nonpolyadenylated transcripts. Confirming our earlier findings, we observed a strong positive correlation in the stiffness-dependent log2 fold change (pairwise comparison of 150 and 2,000 Pa conditions) in the expression of 20,284 overlapping coding and noncoding genes between our rRNA-depleted and poly(A)-enriched RNA-seq datasets (Pearson, R = 0.79, P < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 5A). We detected a total of 1,473 annotated lncRNAs (Dataset S7), of which three lncRNAs strongly responded to changes in environmental stiffness. These include CYTOR (~fourfold change in expression) and its paralog MIR4435-2 host gene (~2.5-fold), and MIR34-A host gene (~twofold) (Fig. 5B). CYTOR exhibited a monotonic downregulation in response to increasing matrix rigidity in both MSC and BJ cells (Fig. 5C). Moreover, exposure to AIIB2 reproduced this downregulation, establishing that the mechanoregulation of this gene follows the inactivation of focal adhesions and exhibits changes in parallel with other stiffness-sensitive genes (Figs. 4D and 5D).

CYTOR has been studied as a microRNA sponge and epigenetic modifier within the context of various cancers (88–90). Its expression is induced in muscle during resistance exercise (91), and it is enriched among pathogenic and inflammatory MSCs in multiple myeloma (68) and fibrosis (92). Given that CYTOR was also the most highly expressed transcript among our set of stiffness-sensitive lncRNAs, we hypothesized that it may functionally modulate some of the observed cellular mechanoresponses in MSCs, in particular those that are immunomodulatory.

To assess the function of CYTOR, we first investigated its subcellular localization by performing a chromogenic in situ hybridization assay with RNAscope probes (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Approximately 70 to 90% of CYTOR puncta were cytoplasmic in MSC and BJ cells, suggesting that it may have predominately cytoplasmic-associated functions, consistent with its reported role in miRNA sponging (88, 93). Next, we performed a knockdown (KD) of CYTOR transcripts using RNase H-activating antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) with flanking locked nucleic acids (LNAs) that improve binding affinity and protect the oligos from exonucleases (94). We designed two ASOs that target distinct sequences in the same exon within CYTOR to control for off-target effects. These oligos, along with a nontargeting control, and ASOs that target MALAT1 or ZFAS1, as stiffness-independent specificity control lncRNAs, were delivered into cells using lipid-based transfection. RNA was extracted 3 d later and changes in the expression of the targeted lncRNAs were quantified using qPCR. Transfection of ASOs resulted in a specific and robust (twofold to fivefold) KD of either MALAT1, ZFAS1, or CYTOR (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Subsequently, we measured the relative expression of 11 mechanoresponsive immunomodulatory genes with detectable expression in MSCs grown on tissue culture plastic (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Strikingly, the relative abundances of these genes were strongly up-regulated, specifically following CYTOR KD (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, a co-transfection of both CYTOR-targeting ASO yielded an additive enhancement in gene activation (Fig. 5E). Remarkably, the magnitude of gene expression changes was significantly greater than observed through stiffness signaling alone (Dataset S1). Taken together, these results suggest that the immunogenic response to environmental stiffness is, in part, mediated by the mechanoresponsiveness of the lncRNA CYTOR, and furthermore that this lncRNA can be experimentally manipulated to modulate this cell behavior.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the stiffness-sensitive transcriptome of MSCs encapsulated in bone marrow-mimetic alginate-collagen I IPNs to unveil mechanosensitive pathways and cellular functions relevant to 3D cultures. Increasing matrix stiffness from 150 to 2,000 Pa restricts cell elongation and interferes with cell–ECM adhesion, triggering the expression and secretion of immunomodulatory genes known for their therapeutic potential in cell-based therapies. In contrast, encapsulation in softer matrices leads to elevated levels of ECM regulators, particularly those that specifically modulate the collagen architecture. Although gene signatures associated with each stiffness condition are not differentially expressed among detected bone marrow stromal populations, they are enriched in MSCs that inhibit the inflammatory niche in patients with myeloma. Additionally, we define CYTOR as a mechanosensitive lncRNA and demonstrate that its modulation contributes to stiffness-driven changes in immunomodulatory gene expression.

Consistent with our findings, murine MSCs cultured within peptide-functionalized alginate hydrogels have been observed to up-regulate immunosuppressive factors in stiff matrices (3 vs. 18 kPa) (23). Conversely, human MSCs up-regulate genes with similar functions in soft gelatin methacrylate hydrogels (GelMA) (2.4 vs. 31.4 kPa) (28), and on soft fibronectin-coated polyacrylamide surfaces (0.5 vs. 200 kPa) (25). The discrepancies between these studies and ours could be attributed to variations in hydrogel characteristics. Unlike our IPNs, GelMA and polyacrylamide gels are more elastic than viscoelastic (28). Viscoelastic materials, like physiological tissues, exhibit a time-dependent dissipation in input energy, and changes to this property not only trigger mechanotransduction but also impact cellular responses to matrix rigidity. Indeed, MSCs exhibit a stiffness-dependent response to inflammatory priming within viscoelastic gels, whereas, in more elastic matrices, this association is not observed (31). Furthermore, GelMA substrates are made of methacrylamide-functionalized units of denatured collagen. Consequently, the resulting scaffolds have an architecture distinct from that of collagen (95), which can profoundly influence cellular mechanosensing. Fibrillar hydrogels, consisting of collagen or fibronectin, promote the maturation of defined focal adhesions and the recruitment and phosphorylation of FAK and paxillin (29), whereas nonfibrillar matrices, generally do not (30). Evidently, comparisons between studies are challenging, highlighting the need for a more systematic investigation of the interplay between matrix composition and mechanics, and their integrated effects on cell functions. As we demonstrate in the present study, gaining insight into the pathways and mechanisms underlying mechanoresponses facilitates an understanding of these complex interrelationships and is essential for the application of these biomaterial systems in cell therapies.

We show that increases in matrix rigidity inhibit cell–ECM adhesions mediated by ITGB1, which leads to an upregulation in functional networks of genes involved in ECM modulation or immune regulation. Similar changes to cell-substrate adhesion have been documented in response to viscoelasticity and plasticity (82, 96). These studies suggest that the dissipation of cellular traction forces through viscosity interferes with adhesion, unless the substrate is permanently deformable which enables the clustering of integrin ligands and subsequent formation of focal adhesions. Given that viscoplastic materials, like our IPNs, deform reversibly under small amounts of strain, we speculate that, within stiffer environments, cells are unable to adequately displace the surrounding matrix to induce lasting changes in structure. As a result, integrins fail to cluster and adhesion is inhibited. Accordingly, MSCs encapsulated in our stiff IPNs are more rounded in morphology, lack actin stress fibers, and exhibit low levels of FAK activation. Although MSCs are anchorage-dependent, we found no evidence of programmed cell death, or anoikis (97). Integrins are presumably only partially inhibited in high stiffness conditions and the interactions of collagen with other cell surface receptors may be critical in mediating this stiffness response while also supporting cell viability (98).

In our examination of native MSC populations in the bone marrow stroma, we found that stiffness-responsive genes, whether triggered by soft or rigid substrates, are enriched in myeloma but not in specific subpopulations of MSCs. Additionally, our analyses of transcription factor regulon activity demonstrated that NF-κB and AP-1, which are stimulated by inflammatory signaling (99, 100), are activated in myeloma samples and stiff matrices. Interestingly, around 30 to 40% of patients with multiple myeloma are also diagnosed with bone marrow scarring, or myelofibrosis, resulting in worse survival and higher incidence of metastasis (101, 102). Our findings suggest that matrix stiffness may exacerbate inflammatory processes in resident stromal cells, and thus contribute to the progression of malignancy. Recent studies have similarly shown that changes in matrix stiffness or viscoelasticity up-regulate targets of the AP-1 and NF-κB pathways (28, 103, 104). Although it is not well understood how these mechanical cues modulate their activity, there is some evidence that NF-κB and AP-1 are, at least in part, regulated by the mitogen-activated protein kinases MAPK1/3 and p38, as well as Rho guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases) (104–107). These findings are consistent with our morphological observations of MSCs encapsulated in rigid IPNs. We observe that their actin cytoskeleton consists of contractile fibers that regulate cell volume and are arranged around a rounded cell cortex. This ring of actin filaments resembles the cortical actin networks found in detached mitotic cells, suspended cells, or cells cultured on small adhesive islands (105, 108). In these contexts, RhoA and its effectors are required for the maintenance of cortical tension and generation of the rounded morphology (105, 109). Therefore, we speculate that the inhibition of cell–ECM adhesion in rigid IPNs activates Rho GTPase-associated signaling cascades that reorganize the actin cytoskeleton and regulate the activity of NF-κB, AP-1, and other stiffness-dependent pathways.

We further observe that CYTOR is the most abundantly expressed stiffness-responsive lncRNA in MSCs. Similar to other abundant lncRNAs, it has a range of previously documented functional associations. Notably, CYTOR has been reported to regulate myoblast differentiation (91), apoptosis in myeloid cells (110), and metabolism in cancer cells (111), among other roles (89). In the present study, through ASO-mediated KD experiments, we found that the CYTOR modulates a network of secreted bioactive factors with immunomodulatory activity. CXCL6 and CXCL8, in particular, exhibited a significant upregulation by around 20 to 60-fold. CXCL6/8 are Glutamine-Leucine-Arginine positive (ELR+) chemokines that predominantly function as neutrophil chemoattractants; however, these factors also induce endothelial chemotaxis and promote neovascularization (112, 113). Intriguingly, CXCL8 has been implicated in the trafficking and polarization of immunosuppressive (M2) macrophages that facilitate immune evasion in tumors (114–116). Therefore, CYTOR may function as a mechanotransductive modulator of the crosstalk between MSCs and various populations of immune cells.

In summary, we employed transcriptome profiling to define stiffness-sensitive behaviors in MSCs. We show that immunomodulatory and ECM-regulatory gene expression is driven by changes in cell–ECM adherence. Specifically, rigid and noncompliant matrices impede the formation of ITGB1-based focal adhesions, eliciting a rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton into a spherical, cortical, and contractile shell reminiscent of actin morphologies in suspended cells. This results in the repression of CYTOR which facilitates the stiffness-driven induction of factors with known immunosuppressive activity. Importantly, KD of CYTOR results in changes in the expression of these factors to a greater extent than observed through stiffness signaling. Our study highlights the importance of elucidating mechanotransductive pathways, particularly those associated with lncRNAs whose functions are still unclear. Unraveling these mechanisms has potential to offer insights into 3D mechanotransduction and the regulation of clinically desired behaviors in MSCs through hydrogel encapsulation.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Alginate-Collagen I IPN Hydrogels.

IPN hydrogels were prepared with alginate (4200501, Pronova) and rat tail collagen I (354249, Corning) in a 1.5% and 6% w/v concentration, respectively. Subsequently, a CaCO3 slurry and a solution of 0.4 g/mL glucono-delta-lactone were added to the mixture and the hydrogels were incubated at 37 °C to cross-link and equilibrate. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Rheological Characterization.

The rheological properties of the resulting IPNs were measured on the AR-G2 rheometer (TA Instrument). 100 µL of the hydrogel solution was aliquoted onto the rheometer loaded with a 20 mm flat plate at 37 °C. Excess solution was wiped off and a solvent trap (sealed with mineral oil) was added to prevent evaporation during the rheometric tests. At an oscillation of 1 Hz and strain of 1%, the storage modulus (G’) and loss tangent (tan δ) were recorded until equilibrium was achieved (1 to 2 h), after which a frequency sweep was performed (0.1 to 10 Hz, 1% strain).

Cell Culture and Hydrogel Encapsulation.

MSCs were isolated from human bone marrow aspirates (Lonza) as previously described (31). BJ fibroblasts were purchased from ATCC (CRL-2522). The cells were added to the hydrogel mixture at a final concentration of 2E06 cells/mL. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Flow Cytometry.

Cell surface marker analysis of passage five MSCs were performed with antibodies targeting CD105, CD90, CD73, CD3, and CD34. The samples were analyzed using a BD LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences) flow cytometer at the University of Toronto Flow Cytometry core facility. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Immunofluorescence Image Acquisition and Analysis of Encapsulated Cell Morphology.

MSC and BJ cells were cultured within the hydrogels for 48 h and subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Phalloidin-iFuor 594 (ab176757, Abcam) and Hoescht 33342. The samples were imaged on the Leica SP8 lightning confocal microscope and a 20× water-immersion objective at the SickKids Imaging Facility. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Cell Retrieval from Hydrogels, Viability Measurements, and RNA Isolation.

Cells were extracted from IPNs using an enzymatic solution consisting of 23 U/mg alginate lyase (A1603, Sigma-Aldrich) and 300 U/mL collagenase (C0130, Sigma-Alrich). RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Mini kit (74106, Qiagen) or the mirVana kit (AM1560, Invitrogen). More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Bulk RNA-seq.

Total RNA samples of encapsulated MSCs (n = 3) were submitted to the Donnelly Sequencing Centre for complementary DNA (cDNA) library generation and RNA-seq. The cDNA libraries for poly(A)-enriched and ribo-depleted RNA-seq were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA kits (New England Biolabs) and were run on the Novaseq system. Details on the sequencing analysis can be found in SI Appendix.

Conditioned Media and ELISAs.

Conditioned medium was collected from MSCs and BJ cells that have been encapsulated in IPNs for a total of 3 d. PGE2 levels in the media were quantified in an ELISA (KGE004B, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Single-Cell RNA-seq Data Integration and Analysis.

Single-cell sequencing datasets of stromal cells in bone marrow aspirates were acquired from GSE190965 (69), GSE147287 (70), and E-MTAB-9139 (68). Data were processed and integrated with Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) and Seurat v4 in R (67). More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Protein Extraction and Immunoblotting.

After 48 h of encapsulation, the MSCs were retrieved from the IPNs as described above. After counting, they were lysed on ice for 30 min in a lysis buffer consisting of 150 mM sodium chloride, 1% NP-40, and 50 mM Tris pH 8.0 at a concentration of 5E06 cells per mL of buffer. The samples were processed for Western blotting and protein was detected with chemiluminescence (NEL120001EA, Perkin Elmer). More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Blocking ITGB1 Adhesions.

Immediately after IPNs reached equilibrium, encapsulated MSC or BJ cells were treated with the ITGB1-blocking antibody, AIIB2 (MABT409, Sigma-Aldrich), or an isotype control (MABF1786, Sigma-Aldrich). The antibodies were replenished 24 h later along with a media change and cells were extracted 48 h postencapsulation for downstream experiments.

cDNA Synthesis and RT-qPCR Assays.

cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the Maxima H Minus First Strand kit (K1651, Thermo-Fisher). qPCRs were performed with the SensiFAST SYBER No-ROX Kit (BIO-98005, Meridian Bioscience). All primers used in this study are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

In Situ Hybridization with RNAscope.

CYTOR-targeting RNAscope probes were designed by the team at Advanced Cell Diagnostics and used with the RNAscope 2.5 HD-RED kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) to label and image the localization of the transcript in MSC and BJ cells. Images were acquired by the Sickkids Imaging Facility with the 3DHistech Pannoramic Flash II Slide Scanner. More details can be found in SI Appendix.

CYTOR KD with ASOs.

KD of CYTOR and ZFAS1 were performed with ASOs customized by Qiagen (339511, 339515, Qiagen). They were transfected into MSCs using the RNAiMAX lipofectamine reagent (13778075, Invitrogen). More details can be found in SI Appendix.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (10.0; GraphPad Software) and R. All experiments were conducted with three independent replicates.
