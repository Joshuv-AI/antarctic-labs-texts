Management of Acute Right Ventricular Failure After High-Risk Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement
Authors: Andrew C Cooper, Sarah G Stafford, Charlene L Rohm, Kashish Goel, Barinder S Hansra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter tricuspid valve replacement using the EVOQUE system is approved for patients with symptomatic severe tricuspid regurgitation refractory to optimal medical therapy. History of Presentation

An 82-year-old woman with severe symptomatic tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and NYHA functional class III symptoms presented for elective transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) using the EVOQUE Tricuspid Valve Replacement System. Six weeks earlier, she had undergone attempted tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair targeting the commissural aspect of the anterior-septal leaflet (Figure 1), which failed to reduce TR severity. Additional leaflet approximations were attempted but ultimately deferred due to persistence of severe TR and inability to grasp sufficient tissue at the leaflets and preserve future options for TTVR. In particular, because the clip was not placed in a central location in the tricuspid valve, she was still eligible for a TTVR, which would result in pushing the clip peripherally toward the tricuspid annulus. However, TTVR in the setting of a preexisting TriClip poses technical challenges and can lead to device-device interactions that can interfere with deployment or functioning of the valve.

The patient continued experiencing debilitating fatigue, anorexia, and NYHA functional class III symptoms and was referred to our institution for consideration of TTVR with EVOQUE. Clinically, the patient was asymptomatic and near her discharge weight from the outside hospital; therefore, no further preprocedural medical optimization was pursued. TTVR was successful (Videos 1 and 2), confirmed by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography showing stable valve positioning, no paravalvular leak, and complete resolution of TR. Notably, the right ventricular (RV) lead from the biventricular pacemaker was jailed, but serial electrocardiograms confirmed that the device was functioning appropriately. On postprocedure day 1, a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed a well-seated EVOQUE valve without TR, severely dilated RV with moderate-to-severe systolic dysfunction, and elevated right-sided filling pressures (Figure 2). The patient was subsequently started on intravenous bumetanide. That night, she became acutely hypotensive with a blood pressure of 70-79/50-59 mm Hg and progressive lactic acidosis. Hemodynamic instability persisted despite intravenous fluid resuscitation, necessitating transfer to the medical cardiovascular intensive care unit for vasopressor and inotropic support.

Past Medical History

The patient has a history of atrial fibrillation requiring ablation, sick sinus syndrome with biventricular pacemaker placement, right carotid endarterectomy, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, moderate pulmonary hypertension (RV systolic pressure 50 mm Hg), and mild RV dysfunction. She also has a history of scleroderma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, and obstructive sleep apnea.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for the patient’s hypotension included cardiogenic shock, hemorrhagic shock, cardiac tamponade, overdiuresis, tension pneumothorax, TTVR malfunction, and acute coronary syndrome.

Investigations

On arrival to the cardiovascular intensive care unit, the patient was afebrile, blood pressure was 83/52 mm Hg (mean arterial pressure 62 mm Hg), heart rate was 60 beats/min, and oxygen saturation was 91% on 2 L per minute of supplemental oxygen via a nasal cannula. Physical examination revealed a holosystolic murmur loudest over the left upper sternal border, elevated jugular venous pressure estimated at 15 cm H2O, 1+ bilateral lower extremity pitting edema, and no signs of respiratory distress or crackles on auscultation. No hematomas were observed near access sites. The electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm without evidence of ischemia. Chest radiography demonstrated small pleural effusions without pulmonary edema. Point-of-care ultrasonography excluded pericardial effusion. An urgent TTE confirmed persistent severely dilated RV with moderate-to-severe systolic dysfunction (Video 3), and the interventricular septum was flattened, consistent with RV pressure-volume overload (Video 4). The EVOQUE valve remained well positioned without structural abnormality, paravalvular leak, or residual TR.

Management

The patient was diagnosed with RV-predominant cardiogenic shock as evidenced by new severe RV dilation with moderately reduced systolic function, elevated right-sided filling pressures on echocardiogram, lactic acidosis, and systemic hypotension by noninvasive blood pressure measurement. She was initiated on inotropic support with dobutamine titrated to 5 μg/kg/min along with norepinephrine ranging from 5 to 20 μg/min, with resulting mean arterial pressures (MAPs) improving from 55 mm Hg to a goal of >65 mm Hg. An arterial line and a right internal jugular central line were placed to facilitate invasive hemodynamic monitoring and administration of vasoactive agents. Placement of a pulmonary artery catheter was attempted but was aborted due to difficulty passing the catheter past the TTVR. On postprocedure day 2, whole-blood lactates had normalized, indicating improved perfusion. Due to MAPs maintaining >70 mm Hg, dobutamine was weaned to 2.5 μg/kg/min with MAPs reassuringly remaining >65 mm Hg. Norepinephrine was transitioned to vasopressin at 0.04 units/min, and inhaled epoprostenol was initiated at 50 ng/kg/min to reduce pulmonary vascular resistance in the context of known pulmonary hypertension. MAPs maintained >70 mm Hg with this regimen. Over the next 3 days, intermittent intravenous bumetanide was administered to a target central venous pressures of 10 to 12 mm Hg, which were transduced using the central line. With gradual clinical improvement, vasoactive support was sequentially weaned beginning with vasopressin, followed by dobutamine, and ultimately inhaled epoprostenol. Home bumetanide 2 mg by mouth twice daily and spironolactone 25 mg daily were reinitiated before discharge and were well tolerated.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At a telehealth follow-up visit 1 month post-discharge, the patient reported marked improvement in overall well-being, with resolution of exertional dyspnea and chest discomfort. Her symptoms had improved from NYHA functional class III to NYHA functional class I with no hospital readmissions. A repeat TTE performed 7 weeks after discharge demonstrated persistently severely enlarged RV but with normalized RV systolic function and an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of 41 mm Hg.

Discussion

The use of EVOQUE for patients with severe TR has yielded positive outcomes regarding quality of life and NYHA functional class,1 and clinical trials report high rates of procedural success.2 Furthermore, reverse right heart remodeling—characterized by reductions in RV dimensions and inferior vena cava size—has been observed after TR elimination.2,3 However, pivotal device approval studies have excluded patients with severe pulmonary hypertension as well as previous tricuspid surgery or intervention.1 Specifically, patients with pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) >60 mm Hg by echocardiogram or >70 mm Hg by right heart catheterization were excluded, limiting generalizability to higher risk populations. Our patient underwent TriClip placement at a referring hospital, where the initial investigation via TTE showed a PASP of 72 mm Hg, which improved to 51 mm Hg after diuresis.

In this context, the patient’s development of acute RV cardiogenic shock post-TTVR was likely precipitated by pressure overload. With the abrupt elimination of TR, the RV, which was previously decompressed by the low-resistance regurgitant orifice, was now suddenly forced to eject against a high-resistance pulmonary circuit. This mismatch likely contributed to the rapid onset of hypotension, lactic acidosis, and echocardiographic findings of RV dilation, systolic dysfunction, and septal flattening consistent with pressure and volume overload.

At our institution, the standard protocol involves overnight monitoring after TTVR. Notably, our patient began exhibiting signs of RV failure approximately 20 hours post-procedure, with progressive hypotension and biochemical evidence of hypoperfusion developing within 30 hours. The delayed clinical deterioration highlights the need for extended monitoring in patients with preexisting RV dysfunction or elevated pulmonary pressures. Invasive monitoring with a pulmonary artery catheter, though unsuccessful in this case due to difficulty crossing the prosthetic valve, may facilitate earlier detection of hemodynamic compromise in similar high-risk patients. In similar cases where invasive hemodynamic monitoring is unsuccessful, the diagnosis of RV failure may be facilitated by measuring the ratio of the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion and the PASP, which can be readily obtained using TTE and is a validated surrogate for RV-PA coupling.4 The tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion:PASP ratio can also be used in the preprocedural setting to identify patients who are at a higher risk for decompensation, specifically those patients who have a significant mismatch between RV contractility and pulmonary arterial afterload at baseline. Although this parameter was unavailable for our patient, it could be a useful tool to risk-stratify patients undergoing TTVR and to help diagnose RV failure.

In our patient, initial stabilization focused on restoration of systemic vascular resistance with norepinephrine to support left ventricular perfusion, particularly given TTE evidence of RV septal bowing. Norepinephrine was later transitioned to vasopressin, which offers selective systemic vasoconstriction with dose-dependent pulmonary vasodilation.5 Inhaled epoprostenol was added to further reduce pulmonary vascular resistance. Inotropic support with dobutamine was initiated early to augment RV contractility. Diuresis was guided by central venous pressure, for a target range of 10 to 12 mm Hg. Options for mechanical circulatory support were briefly discussed for the patient but were ultimately not needed given her clinical improvement. The most likely mechanical circulatory support of choice, if clinically indicated, was determined to be extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, as placing a right-sided Impella could compromise the TTVR.

After the patient’s hemodynamics stabilized, vasoactive support was weaned sequentially, starting with vasopressin, dobutamine, and lastly inhaled epoprostenol. The patient was off all vasoactive support by postprocedure day 4 and discharged home on postprocedure day 6. At the 1-month follow-up appointment, the patient reported NYHA functional class I symptoms. She had no interim readmissions. TTE 7 weeks after discharge revealed normalization of RV systolic function.

Conclusions

Despite high rates of procedural success, there are currently no guidelines on the management of acute RV failure after TTVR. We present a case of acute RV failure after TTVR in a patient with pulmonary hypertension and prior tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, which required inotropes and vasopressors but was ultimately associated with a positive outcome at 1-month post-procedure. Further study is needed to identify risk factors for decompensation after EVOQUE placement and to establish how to optimally manage patients who develop RV failure.
