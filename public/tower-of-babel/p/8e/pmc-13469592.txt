A low-temperature, water-free fabrication route to Mg-based micro thermoelectric coolers for thermal management
Authors: Jiawei Yang, Rui Zhu, Ming Li, Zengxia Mei, Ling Chen, Li-Ming Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustainable thermal management of high-heat-flux electronics beyond Moore’s Law requires micro thermoelectric coolers (μ-TECs) that are both high-performance and environmentally sustainable. However, state-of-the-art μ-TECs rely on Bi2Te3-based materials, whose tellurium scarcity, toxicity concerns and limited mechanical robustness hinder widespread adoption. Mg-based thermoelectric materials offer a promising alternative but remain challenging to integrate into microscale devices. Here we show that a low-temperature, water-free fabrication strategy enables tellurium-minimized μ-TECs based on n-type Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 and p-type MgAgSb. Using magnetron sputtering as a cold bonding approach, we fabricate compact 12-pair microdevices (2.95 × 4.35 × 1.4 mm3), with thermoelectric legs approximately 3% the size of previously reported Mg-based devices. The resulting μ-TECs achieve a power density of 4.34 W cm−2 and a packing density of 93.5 pairs cm−2. Our work establishes a scalable route for sustainable thermoelectric cooling and a viable alternative to conventional Bi2Te3 technologies.

Introduction

The relentless miniaturization of high-heat-flux electronic systems in the post-Moore’s Law era has placed stringent demands on sustainable thermal management technologies, with microthermoelectric coolers (μ-TECs) emerging as a key solution for localized, energy-efficient cooling. Conventional Bi2Te3-based μ-TECs, however, face insurmountable barriers to widespread application: tellurium’s inherent toxicity and resource scarcity conflict with global sustainability goals, while the material’s mechanical fragility renders it incompatible with precise microfabrication processes. In response, n-type Mg3(Bi, Sb)2 and p-type MgAgSb have emerged as promising tellurium-free alternatives, but their translation into microdevices has remained elusive due to the materials’ extreme sensitivity to moisture and limited compatibility with high-temperature processing. To bridge this gap, we develop a low-temperature and water-free route that circumvents these intrinsic material limitations, enabling the fabrication of high-performance Mg-based μ-TECs and unlocking their potential for electronics.

Thermoelectric materials enable direct conversion between heat and electricity, providing scalable solid-state solutions for power generation and active cooling1,2. Their microscale implementation—micro-thermoelectric coolers (μ-TECs)—delivers exceptional cooling power density and localized thermal control, addressing critical heat management challenges in optoelectronic and microelectronic systems3,4. Current μ-TECs predominantly use Bi2Te3, a material plagued by mechanical fragility and environmental concerns due to its tellurium content—specifically because of Te’s toxicity and low terrestrial abundance, limiting further device miniaturization3–5. While numerous high-zT materials (e.g., Mg3(Bi,Sb)25–7, MgAgSb8–10, SnSe11–13, PbSe14,15, AgSbTe216,17, Ag2Se18–20, and half-Heuslers21) have been developed, their integration into functional microscale devices remains a key bottleneck.

Developing μ-TECs from emerging materials faces two major challenges. First, the R&D cycle for device fabrication far exceeds that for material synthesis. Miniaturization—for instance, reducing Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 leg volumes from macro scales (>1.5 × 1.5 × 2 mm3) to μ-TEC dimensions (~0.3 × 0.3 × 0.4 mm3)—dramatically increases difficulties in positioning, cutting, and soldering3,22. Second, interfaces for emerging thermoelectric materials are typically prepared via one-step hot-pressing, producing thick, non-uniform layers under manual control, thereby hindering independent optimization of thermoelectric and interface properties9,10,22–25. This is particularly critical for materials like Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 and MgAgSb, under high-temperature sintering conditions, the volatilization of ingredients, phase transition, and material decomposition are accelerated. Moreover, the high-temperature treatment used to improve grain size and carrier mobility also accelerates interfacial diffusion26–29. Addressing these issues requires both theoretical interface design and advanced fabrication strategies.

We report the development of high-performance, tellurium-minimized μ-TECs based on n-type Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 and p-type MgAgSb for near-room-temperature applications. Employing room-temperature cold interface bonding via magnetron sputtering, a low-temperature and water-free route, we fabricated Cu/Mo and Ag/Mo multilayer interfaces on Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 and MgAgSb, achieving robust adhesion and uniform morphology; notably, the Cu/Mo/Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 junction exhibited an ultralow interfacial resistivity of 5.9 μΩ cm2. Leveraging this strategy, we demonstrate a tellurium-minimized 12-pair Mg-based μ-TEC that is only 3% the size of previously reported normal-sized devices (leg size shrinking from ~1.5 × 1.5 × 2 mm3 to 0.48 × 0.48 × 0.56 mm3) and a packing density of 93.5 pairs cm−2, delivering a maximum cooling power density of 4.34 W cm−2—a fourfold enhancement over prior Bi2Te3-free Mg-based devices. These results highlight the potential of environmentally friendly, high-performance miniaturized thermoelectric modules to advance sustainable thermal management in electronics and provide a route towards the miniaturized integration of emerging thermoelectric materials.

Results

Interfacial engineering technology for Mg-based materials

We first conducted a comprehensive evaluation of interface fabrication techniques—including hot pressing (HP), electroplating (EP), chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and sputtering—highlighting differences in operating temperature and the ability to decouple the synthesis of thermoelectric materials and thermoelectric interface materials (TEiMs), as shown in Fig. 1a. For microscale Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 and MgAgSb devices, fabricating a sandwich structure consisting of interface powders or foils and thermoelectric legs via HP poses a major limitation: poor control over interface thickness and uniformity. Layers often reach several hundred micrometers—comparable to the leg length—and post-sintering treatments like polishing or cleaning exacerbate non-uniformity, hindering precise device fabrication22–25,27,28. High room-temperature performance in Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 requires sintering at ~973 K for grain growth and reduced grain boundary scattering, but high-temperature processing induces elemental diffusion that degrades thermoelectric performance26,29. Fe, a commonly used TEiM for Mg3(Bi,Sb)25,27, was shown by Qu et al. to cause a 75% conductivity drop and n-type-to-p-type conduction reversal when an Fe/Mg3(Bi,Sb)2/Fe stack was hot-pressed at 1073 K27. Similarly, Wang et al. reported reduced power generation efficiency in single-leg Ni/Mg3(Bi,Sb)2/Ni devices after annealing at 573 K30. In MgAgSb, the α-to-β-phase transition near 573 K induces abrupt carrier transport changes8, making hot-pressed interface layers with high-melting-point metals (such as Ti, V, and Cr) difficult to fabricate31. Typically, the hot-press sintering temperature is maximized without exceeding the melting or phase-transition point of the thermoelectric substrate—for example, ~700 K for Bi2Te3/Ni32, ~800 K for PbTe/Co0.8Fe0.233, ~900 K for Mg3(Bi,Sb)2/Fe, Co or Cr5,34, and below 573 K for MgAgSb/Ag, Co, or MgCuSb9,24. Interface-layer sintering temperature’s dependence on the substrate underscores the need to decouple thermoelectric material fabrication from TEiM fabrication. Unlike hot pressing, thin-film deposition methods enable decoupled optimization of thermoelectric materials and interface layers—opening a viable path to fabricate interface layers for microscale devices. However, variations in processing temperature and chemical reactivity restrict the suitability of some techniques. Apart from its high processing temperature, CVD is further constrained by complex gas handling and limited precursor availability, which hinder process control and may trigger side reactions35. EP operates at low temperatures (<373 K) but risks corrosion from acidic/basic electrolytes36. Compared with conventional HP- and EP-based interface fabrication strategies, sputtering offers a more suitable route for Mg-based micro-TECs because it enables low-temperature, solvent-free, and thickness-controllable interface construction37,38. Although sputtered interfaces have been widely explored in Bi2Te3-based devices4, their application to Mg-based thermoelectric materials remains scarce, and direct transfer of these established interface systems is limited by the distinct physicochemical properties and interfacial stability requirements of Mg-based materials. Accordingly, we adopted magnetron sputtering to develop material-specific interfacial layers for Mg-based thermoelectric materials, addressing a rarely explored interface-fabrication route for Mg-based micro-TEC integration.

To implement this strategy, we deposited bilayer thermoelectric interfaces via room-temperature cold magnetron sputtering: Mo/Cu on n-type Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 and Mo/Ag on p-type MgAgSb. Focused ion beam (FIB) cross-sections of randomly selected regions (Fig. 1b) reveal compact, uniform structures with distinct layering and strong adhesion to thermoelectric substrates; their thicknesses are ~0.5/1 μm (Mo/Cu) and 0.5/2 μm (Mo/Ag), respectively. For comparison, Fig. 1c summarizes the combined thicknesses of the interface layers (hot-pressed) at both hot and cold sides—along with their contact resistivities—for Mg3(Bi,Sb)2. Our sputtered Mo/Cu bilayer (fabricated in this work) exhibits a contact resistivity of 5.9 μΩ cm2—markedly lower than that of hot-pressed pure metals like Fe and Ni, and comparable to values reported for multicomponent alloys22,25,27,30,39–42. Notably, its thickness is nearly two orders of magnitude smaller than hot-pressed counterparts (often >200 μm), whose thicknesses approach the leg heights of mini-bulk or macro-scale thermoelectric modules22,25,27,30,39–42. By contrast, our sputtered layers are only a few micrometers thick—comparable to the leg lengths of MEMS-fabricated micro-film modules43. Building on this optimized interface strategy, we fabricated 12-pair Mg3(Bi,Sb)2/MgAgSb-based μ-TECs with ultra-compact thermoelectric legs of 0.48 × 0.48 × 0.56 mm3 (Fig. 1d). Specifically, the overall module dimensions are 2.95 × 4.35 × 1.4 mm3, resulting in a high leg fill factor of 43.1% and a packing density of 93.5 pairs cm−2. Cold interfacial bonding

To enable cold interfacial bonding, we identified Cu as a suitable sputtered interfacial material because its thermal expansion coefficient (~20 × 10−6 K−1) closely matches Mg3Sb2 (~17 × 10−6 K−1), and Cu/Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 interfacial bonding strengths up to 43 MPa have been reported22,34. Similarly, Ag and MgAgSb share comparable expansion coefficients (20 vs. 21 × 10−6 K−1), and Ag forms strong bonds with MgAgSb via hot pressing9,10. Accordingly, we first attempted Cu/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 and Ag/MgAgSb interfacial structures, depositing layers via room-temperature magnetron sputtering. However, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed weak bonding at the Cu/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 interface, with numerous voids (Supplementary Fig. 1), while the Ag/MgAgSb interface showed even poorer adhesion and could be manually delaminated (Supplementary Fig. 2). Given such poor interfacial bonding, we turned to the literature to identify a viable solution. Wu et al. studied element diffusion in Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 annealed at 500 °C for 15 min, showing that Cu and Ni diffuse relatively rapidly, whereas Mo, Cr, Nb, and W exhibit much lower diffusivity34. However, their high-melting points hinder effective bonding with Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 at thermoelectric-material-compatible sintering temperatures. We therefore used Mo, which combines low diffusivity with a relatively low work function and higher sputtered-particle energy than Cu and Ag50, as a transition interlayer in a bilayer interface strategy: introducing Mo as a transitional interlayer to form Cu/Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 and Ag/Mo/MgAgSb interfaces for the thermoelectric legs. A Cu/Mo bilayer was sputtered onto an Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 pellet (ϕ 12.7 mm, thickness 2–3 mm; Supplementary Fig. 3a). SEM and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analyses confirm high interfacial uniformity, density, and structural continuity (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). For analysis, a polished cross-section of the Cu/Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 interface was examined via SEM and EDS, revealing that the Mo interlayer significantly enhanced interfacial integrity—no voids were detected at either the Cu/Mo or Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 4). High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images of the Cu/Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 junctions, combined with elemental mapping, reveal a uniform distribution of elements across the Cu, Mo, and Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 layers without any obvious segregation (Fig. 2a). The Mo and Cu layers are clearly distinguishable, with thicknesses of ~0.5 μm and ~1 μm, respectively. Elemental line scans reveal sharp compositional transitions at the interfaces without plateau regions (Fig. 2b), indicating the absence of intermediate reaction products. Consistently, the Mg-Bi-Mo ternary phase diagram shows no Mo-containing compounds within this system, apart from Mg3Bi2 and Mg149Bi (Supplementary Fig. 5). The HAADF-STEM and bright-field (BF) images, along with EDS analyses of both the Cu/Mo and Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 interfaces, further confirm these observations (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Furthermore, as shown in the Cu-Mo binary phase diagram (Supplementary Fig. 8), no intermetallic compounds are formed between Cu and Mo. Together with the Mg-Bi-Mo ternary phase diagram, this suggests that Mo can act as an effective diffusion barrier at high temperatures, improving the service stability of the interface. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals that the Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 interface is largely disordered and rough, with pronounced phase-contrast variations (Fig. 2c), indicative of incoherent characteristics. The rough interfacial morphology and phase-contrast variations suggest local structural disorder generated during heterogeneous atomic-scale bonding between the sputtered Mo layer and the Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 substrate. Owing to the differences in crystal structure, bonding characteristics, and atomic arrangement between Mo and Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5, incoherent interfacial regions may form during sputter deposition, leading to the observed contrast fluctuations. These features indicate effective Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 interfacial bonding and support the feasibility of magnetron sputtering for constructing Mg-based thermoelectric interfaces. Electrical contact resistivity and thermal stress

As shown in Fig. 3a, the Cu/Mo/Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 junction exhibits a low contact resistivity (ρc) of ~5.9 μΩ cm2, measured via the four-probe method. To assess the thermal stability of the sputtered interface, three Cu/Mo/Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 junction samples were prepared using the same deposition process and subjected to different aging conditions: unannealed, vacuum-annealed at 250 °C for 71 h, and vacuum-annealed at 250 °C for 166 h. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, the interface remained sharp and continuous after aging, with no detectable cracks or interfacial gaps. Moreover, the contact resistivity also showed negligible change after annealing, further confirming the excellent bonding integrity and thermal stability of the junction below 300 °C. In contrast, the Ag/Mo/MgAgSb junction shows a higher contact resistivity of 51.7 μΩ cm2, indicating that further optimization is required (Supplementary Fig. 14). The ratio of contact to total resistance (Rc/Rtotal) of our n-type leg was derived as a function of leg length and contact resistivity, assuming a bulk resistivity of ~14 μΩ m (Fig. 3b), as the thermoelectric leg length is greatly reduced. The contact resistance contributes ~15.3%, 4.3%, and 2.9% at leg lengths of 0.5, 2, and 3 mm, respectively. At a leg length of 0.5 mm, the Cu/Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 junction exhibits a 44.9% lower contact resistance fraction than the two-step hot-pressed Cu/Mg2Cu/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 counterpart22,44. Mechanical testing revealed tensile strengths of 3.9 MPa and 4.1 MPa for the Cu/Mo/Mg3Bi1.5Sb0.5 and Ag/Mo/MgAgSb junctions, respectively, both with a cross-sectional area of 3.5 × 4.1 mm2 (Supplementary Fig. 15). To study the effect of the Mo layer on device thermal stress, we performed finite element simulations of four interface configurations under constant material and boundary conditions (Supplementary Table 1). The fourth case corresponds to the experimentally fabricated μ-TECs, with the structure illustrated in Fig. 3d and parameters detailed in Supplementary Table 2. A thermal load corresponding to the device’s maximum cooling power at ΔT = 0 K was applied at the cold end to simulate actual operating conditions. Given that the total height of the thermoelectric leg was fixed, the increased thickness of the interface layers in Case 1 and Case 3 effectively reduced the height of the thermoelectric material by ~50%, thus halving the device’s cooling power. Accordingly, a maximum heat load of 5 W cm−2 was assigned to Cases 1 and 3, while 10 W cm−2 was applied to Cases 2 and 4. In all cases, the applied maximum heat load at the cold side resulted in a peak cooling temperature difference below 1 K (Supplementary Fig. 16). Consequently, the small temperature gradients in the device gave rise to uniform and low thermal stress levels (<10 MPa), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 16. To evaluate stress within the thermoelectric legs, multiple cross-sections were extracted for all four cases (Supplementary Figs. 17–20). In all cases, the p-type legs exhibited higher stress than the n-type legs. Interfacial stress increased markedly as interface layer thickness decreased, regardless of Mo presence, with pronounced stress concentrations at edges and corners. Axial stress profiles were quantified by tracking the maximum and minimum stresses along the leg axis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, maximum thermal stress is not determined solely by the coefficient of thermal expansion; reducing the interface layer thickness markedly increases stress in both the interface and thermoelectric materials (Case 1 vs. 2, Case 3 vs. 4). When the interface layer is comparable in thickness to the thermoelectric leg, Mo raises maximum stress in both the interface and thermoelectric materials (Case 1 vs. 3). For very thin interfaces, Mo increases stress in the interface while slightly reducing it in the thermoelectric material (Case 2 vs. 4). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, minimum thermal stress in both the interface and thermoelectric material decreases with reduced interface thickness (Case 1 vs. 2, Case 3 vs. 4). Introducing a Mo layer raises the minimum stress in the interface but simultaneously lowers the minimum stress in the thermoelectric material (Case 1 vs. 3, Case 2 vs. 4). As shown in Fig. 3e, thinning the interface layer without altering its composition has a relatively minor impact on the average thermal stress along the thermoelectric leg (Case 1 vs. 2). With the addition of the Mo layer, the average thermal stress increases from 4.5 to 14.5 MPa in the interface layer and from 3.5 to 4.0 MPa in the thermoelectric layer (Case 1 vs. 3, Fig. 3e, f). With a substantial reduction in interface thickness (Case 3 vs. 4), the average stress decreases to 12.7 MPa in the interface layer and 3.0 MPa in the thermoelectric layer, accompanied by a more uniform stress distribution across the latter (Fig. 3f). In addition, thermal stress distributions were simulated for the four cases under a temperature difference of ~32 K (Supplementary Figs. 23–28). Compared with the maximum-cooling-power condition, a larger temperature gradient raised the maximum stress to around 300 MPa, while the average stress remained below 200 MPa. Thermal stress was primarily concentrated at the edges and corners, and the overall magnitude still stayed lower than the typical stress levels if conventional TEG modules, which can exceed 600 MPa51. Overall, thermal stress is governed not only by thermal expansion mismatch but also by interface layer thickness, device geometry, and the mechanical properties of thermoelectric materials. Therefore, although the Mo layer elevates local interfacial stress, it does not increase stress within the thermoelectric legs, nor does it counteract the performance advantages brought by optimized interfacial transport and diffusion barrier effects.

Cooling performance of μ-TECs

This is slightly below the single parabolic band model (SPB) prediction, likely due to neglect of grain boundary scattering29,44 (Supplementary Fig. 30). Prior to fabrication, calculations using the parameters in Supplementary Table 4 were conducted to determine the effect of leg dimensions on maximum temperature difference (ΔTmax), cooling power density (qc), and coefficient of performance (COP). Short leg lengths in μ-TECs make contact resistance a significant contributor to total device resistance, strongly degrading performance. Consequently, the maximum cooling temperature difference (ΔTₘₐₓ) was calculated as a function of the n/p leg area ratio (An/Ap) for varying leg lengths (Fig. 4b). The optimal An/Ap increases with leg length, ranging from 0.43 to 0.75 as the leg height varies from 0.2 mm to 10 mm, with Aₚ fixed at 0.25 mm2. For a 0.5 mm leg, the optimal ratio is ~0.53, corresponding to an n-type leg area of 0.36 × 0.36 mm2. For all leg lengths, qcmax grows with An/Ap (Fig. 4c), and shorter legs amplify this effect. Specifically, for a thermoelectric leg length of 0.5 mm, increasing An/Ap from 0.53 to 1.0 reduces ΔTₘₐₓ by 6.0% while raising qcmax by 24.6%, demonstrating that a larger n/p area ratio enhances qcmax. To prevent short-circuiting during soldering, μ-TEC thermoelectric legs were designed with a 0.48 mm cross-section, just below the Cu electrode width (~0.55 mm), achieving a high fill factor of 43%. A schematic of Mg-based μ-TEC fabrication is shown in Supplementary Fig. 34. The high adhesion of the Mo layer enabled successful interfacial layer deposition (Supplementary Fig. 35) and precise cutting of thermoelectric legs (Supplementary Fig. 36). Figure 4d shows measured ΔT, qc, and COP versus current (I). At I = 0.8 A, ΔT reached 32 K and 42 K for hot-side temperatures of 300 K and 325 K. At I = 0.43 A, with a hot-side temperature of 353 K and a ΔT of 40 K, the device achieved a COP of 0.24. Notably, qcmax measured at 300 K reached 4.34 W cm−2, slightly below the simulated values. The difference between the experimental and theoretical values is likely due to additional resistance introduced during soldering. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, the resistance contributions from different components of the device were calculated. The discrepancy likely arises from soldering-induced resistance and the unaccounted contribution of the 25-μm-thick Cu electrodes, leading to a measured resistance of 1.78 Ω, slightly above the theoretical value of 1.56 Ω. Despite this deviation, the device exhibits good reproducibility, with coefficients of variation (CVs) of 1.38% for resistance and 1.78% for ΔTmax across multiple measurements (Supplementary Table 6). We further evaluated qcmax as a function of leg length and fill factor and compared with reported Mg-based devices22,44–46. In most prior MgAgSb- or Mg3(Bi,Sb)2-based devices, leg length exceeds 2 mm, and fill factor is <25%, limiting qcmax to ~1 W cm−2. Our device achieves 4.3 W cm−2—3–4 times higher—at a leg height of 0.56 mm and fill factor of 43% (Fig. 4e). In addition, simulations of qcmax as a function of ceramic plate thickness (hc) and p-type contact resistivity (Fig. 4f) indicate that lowering the resistivity to 5 μΩ cm2 and hc to 0.2 mm could raise qcmax to 9.5 W cm−2. Further optimization of soldering, reduced contact resistivity of MgAgSb interface layers, and higher power factor could increase the maximum cooling power density of Mg-based μ-TECs, potentially reaching 20 W cm−2. Owing to the lack of experimental setups for power generation, performance was evaluated by calculation (Supplementary Fig. 37). At ΔT = 150 K, the device delivers a maximum power (Pmax) of ~0.095 W, corresponding to a power density (ωmax) of 0.73 W cm⁻2 and an efficiency of ~5%.

In summary, using a low-temperature and water-free fabrication route, we produced Cu/Mo/Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 and Ag/Mo/MgAgSb junctions with high bonding strength, achieving 12-pair MgAgSb/Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 μ-TECs with a packing density of 93.5 leg pairs per cm2, a cold-side area of 2.95 × 4.35 mm2, a fill factor of 43%, and a cooling power density of 4.34 W cm−2, substantially surpassing previous reports on Mg-based thermoelectric devices. These results establish a scalable, low-temperature and water-free route to fully exploit emerging thermoelectric materials in micro-cooling and thermal management. Looking forward, overcoming bonding challenges is essential for high-quality interfaces and the widespread deployment of reliable, high-performance micro-thermoelectric modules based on emerging materials.

Methods

Material syntheses

Mg turnings (Western Elements, 99.99%), Sb shots (Aladdin, 99.99%), Bi shots (Aladdin, 99.99%), Ag shots (Macklin, 99.99%), and Te shots (Aladdin, 99.99%) were weighed according to the nominal stoichiometry of Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 and MgAgSb. Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 powder was prepared by high-energy ball milling for 12 hours under an argon atmosphere using a SPEX 8000D Mixer/Mill (SPEX, USA). Subsequently, pellets of Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025, approximately 12.7 mm in diameter and 11 mm thick, were prepared by hot pressing at 1053 K for 10 minutes. A two-step milling process was employed for MgAgSb. Mg and Ag were ball-milled at an atomic ratio of 1:1 for 10.5 h under an Ar atmosphere using a SPEX 8000D Mixer/Mill (SPEX, USA). Subsequently, Sb was added to achieve a final stoichiometry of Mg:Ag:Sb = 1:1:1, followed by an additional 10.5 h of milling. MgAgSb pellets with a diameter of 12.7 mm and a thickness of approximately 9 mm were hot pressed at 553 K for 1 hour.

Interface layer preparation

The sintered ingots, 12.7 mm in diameter, were cut into 0.56 mm-thick slices using wire electrical discharge machining. For the Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 samples, Cu/ Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 junctions were prepared by directly sputtering Cu at 60 W for 80 min under a pressure of 0.4 Pa, while Cu/Mo/Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 junctions were fabricated by first depositing Mo at 50 W or 70 W for 40 min under 0.4 Pa, followed by Cu deposition at 60 W for 80 min under the same pressure. For the MgAgSb samples, Ag/MgAgSb junctions were prepared by directly sputtering Ag at 60 W for 1 h under 0.4 Pa, while Ag/Mo/MgAgSb junctions were fabricated by first depositing Mo at 45 W for 40 min or 65 W for 35 min under 0.4 Pa, followed by Ag deposition at 60 W for 1 h under the same pressure.

Fabrication of micro-thermoelectric modules

The thermoelectric slices with deposited interface layers, 0.56 mm thick, were diced into square blocks measuring 0.48 × 0.48 mm2 using wire electrical discharge machining. Subsequently, the n-type Mg3.2Bi1.4975Sb0.5Te0.0025 and p-type MgAgSb thermoelectric legs were soldered onto a 0.4 mm-thick aluminum nitride (AlN) ceramic substrate using Sn63/Pb37 solder. The AlN ceramic substrate (2.95 × 4.35 mm2) featured copper electrodes (0.55 × 1.25 mm2) with an inter-electrode gap of 0.15 mm.

All cooling performance tests, including temperature difference (ΔT), cooling power (Qc), and coefficient of performance (COP), were conducted under vacuum conditions.

Temperature difference measurement: The hot side of the device was mounted on a temperature-controlled stage and maintained at ~25 °C, while the cold side was thermally insulated to achieve near-adiabatic conditions. An optimized current was applied, and ΔT was measured using thermocouples positioned on both sides of the device.

Qc and COP measurement: The hot side was fixed at ~25 °C. A micro-heater attached to the cold side provided a heat load assumed to be fully transferred to the device (Qc = Pheater), neglecting heat losses. The heater power was adjusted until ΔT = 0, at which point Qc was obtained.

The cooling power density (qc) was calculated as qc = Qc / A, where A is the cold-side area. The COP was calculated as COP = Qc / P, where P is the electrical input power.
