Targeting bone marrow adipocyte-driven fatty acid metabolism to overcome drug resistance in lung cancer bone metastasis
Authors: Jian Dong, Huaze Ding, Shuoer Wang, Shengzhe Ruan, Jianxin Ye, Peng Hu, Tong Meng, Wangjun Yan, Lei Zhou, Changping Wang, Dianwen Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with bone metastasis presenting a significant challenge due to its association with severe skeletal complications and therapy resistance. This study investigates the role of bone marrow adipocytes (BMAs) in modulating fatty acid metabolism within the bone metastatic niche of lung cancer. Utilizing single-cell sequencing and in vitro co-culture models, we identified critical interactions between BMAs and metastatic lung cancer cells that enhance fatty acid metabolism, promoting tumor survival and drug resistance. To target this metabolic axis, we screened a library of fatty acid synthesis inhibitors, and developed a nanoparticle system encapsulating kaempferol, and cisplatin, surface-modified with poly-aspartic acid for efficient bone targeting. The nanoparticles release their therapeutic payload in the acidic tumor microenvironment, disrupting fatty acid metabolism and overcoming chemoresistance. Our findings highlight the metabolic reprogramming driven by BMAs in bone metastasis and propose a novel therapeutic strategy to improve outcomes for patients with metastatic lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03792-2.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most prevalent and lethal malignancy globally, imposing a substantial burden on public health [1]. In 2022, lung cancer was responsible for an estimated 2.5 million new diagnoses and 1.8 million deaths, representing approximately 12.4% of all new cancer cases and 18.7% of cancer-related mortality worldwide [2]. Despite substantial advances in targeted therapies, immunotherapy, and novel chemotherapeutic agents, the prognosis for patients with lung cancer, particularly those with advanced-stage disease, remains dismal [3]. One of the most critical factors influencing survival is metastasis, with bone metastasis being one of the most common and debilitating forms of distant spread [4]. Bone metastases affect 30–40% of patients with advanced lung cancer, leading to severe skeletal complications, including pain, fractures, and loss of mobility [5]. Furthermore, these metastatic lesions often exhibit resistance to conventional therapies, exacerbating clinical outcomes and significantly shortening patient survival [6]. Thus, overcoming drug resistance in the context of bone metastasis presents a major therapeutic challenge that demands the development of novel strategies to improve patient outcomes and extend survival.

The bone microenvironment represents a unique and highly dynamic niche that plays a pivotal role in the progression of bone metastasis [7]. Of particular interest is the increasing prevalence of BMAs, which have been shown to significantly influence cancer progression [8, 9]. As individuals age and obesity rates rise, the population of BMAs increases substantially, occupying up to two-thirds of the bone marrow space [10, 11]. These adipocytes, which serve as major reservoirs for triglycerides, are integral to regulating fatty acid metabolism and influencing bone remodeling through the secretion of adipokines and cytokines such as leptin and adiponectin [12]. In the context of bone metastasis, BMAs play a critical role in modulating tumor cell behavior. They secrete interleukin-6 (IL-6) and other factors that enhance epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in metastatic tumor cells, promote osteoclast activation, and contribute to the formation of osteolytic lesions [13]. Additionally, BMAs release pro-angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which facilitates the vascularization of metastatic lesions [14]. This bidirectional interaction between BMAs and metastatic tumor cells has emerged as a crucial driver of cancer progression, particularly in lung cancer bone metastasis.

These interactions highlight the critical role of fatty acid metabolism in the context of bone metastasis, where BMAs and tumor cells jointly influence metabolic pathways that drive cancer progression. Fatty acid metabolism has emerged as a critical regulator of cancer cell biology, influencing tumor progression through multiple mechanisms. Fatty acids are essential components of membrane lipids, energy substrates for mitochondrial function, and signaling intermediates involved in key pathways such as phosphatidylinositol (4,5)-bisphosphate (PIP2) and diacylglycerol (DAG) production, all of which promote cellular proliferation and oncogenic signaling [15]. Beyond membrane biogenesis, fatty acids contribute to mitochondrial ATP and NADH production, lipid modification of signaling proteins, and regulation of cellular oxidative stress responses [16]. In cancer, particularly in metastatic cells, fatty acid metabolism is often dysregulated [17]. Tumor cells display altered lipid compositions, with increased levels of saturated and monounsaturated acyl chains in glycerophospholipids [16]. This metabolic shift is associated with enhanced tumor cell survival, increased metastatic potential, and resistance to therapies, suggesting that fatty acid metabolism is a key metabolic vulnerability that could be therapeutically targeted in the context of metastasis [18–20]. Moreover, the desaturation of fatty acids is critical for tumor cell survival by preventing lipotoxicity and ferroptosis, and may also contribute to resistance to chemotherapy by reducing membrane permeability [21]. Thus, the interplay between BMAs and tumor cells in regulating fatty acid metabolism is not just a consequence of tumor growth but a driving force behind metastatic progression and therapy resistance. Targeting this metabolic axis represents an innovative therapeutic approach, particularly in the context of bone metastasis, where dysregulated lipid metabolism underpins tumor aggressiveness and chemoresistance. By disrupting key components of fatty acid metabolism, it may be possible to reverse drug resistance and improve treatment outcomes in metastatic lung cancer, thereby offering a novel avenue for therapeutic intervention in this challenging disease.

Despite the promise of targeting cancer metabolism, one of the major challenges in this approach is the selective targeting of tumor cells while minimizing damage to healthy tissues [22]. Cancer cells and normal cells share many of the same metabolic pathways, including those involved in fatty acid metabolism, making it challenging to develop therapies that selectively target tumor cells without affecting normal tissues [20, 23, 24]. This issue is especially pertinent in the bone metastasis setting, where the tumor microenvironment is composed not only of cancer cells but also a variety of stromal and immune cells that are essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis [25]. As a result, metabolic-targeted therapies often face a narrow therapeutic window, with the potential for significant off-target effects and toxicity to healthy tissues. Thus, while targeting metabolism holds great promise, developing strategies to selectively modulate tumor cell metabolism without disrupting normal tissue function remains a critical challenge in the field of cancer therapy.

This study aims to address these gaps in knowledge by exploring the role of BMAs in modulating fatty acid metabolism in the bone metastatic niche of lung cancer. By leveraging single-cell sequencing of bone metastasis samples from lung cancer patients and in vitro co-culture models (Fig. 1a), we have identified key interactions between BMAs and lung cancer cells that amplify tumor lipid metabolism and contribute to drug resistance in the bone metastatic microenvironment. We hypothesize that BMAs actively participate in the metabolic reprogramming of metastatic tumor cells, promoting their survival and resistance to treatment. To explore potential therapeutic strategies targeting this interaction, we have developed a library of small-molecule fatty acid synthesis inhibitors and selected kaempferol (KAE), a natural flavonoid compound known to inhibit key lipid synthesis enzymes such as fatty acid synthase [26] (FAS) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase [27] (ACC). We have formulated KAE in combination with cisplatin (CDDP) into stable nanoparticles (KACPNP@Asp), which are surface-modified with poly-aspartic acid (PAsp) to form a core-shell structure (Fig. 1b). This design enables efficient targeting of bone metastases, with the nanoparticles shedding their shell in the acidic tumor microenvironment to penetrate cancer cells. Once inside the cells, the system releases CDDP and KAE, inhibiting lipid metabolism and overcoming drug resistance. Through this approach, we aim to slow the progression of lung cancer bone metastasis and provide a new avenue for therapeutic intervention in metastatic lung cancer.

Results

BMA – cancer cell interaction-induced lipid metabolism elevation promotes tumor resistance and DNA damage repair

Our investigation into the role of lipid metabolism in lung cancer bone metastasis utilized single-cell sequencing of tumor samples from patients with lung cancer bone metastasis and primary lung cancer (Fig. 1a). The analysis revealed a significant upregulation of genes related to lipid metabolism and cisplatin resistance in metastatic lung cancer cells compared to primary lung cancer cells (Fig. 2a-c, Figure S1a-c). This suggests a link between metabolic reprogramming and treatment resistance in metastatic cells. Given the importance of DNA repair mechanisms in response to platinum-based chemotherapy [28, 29], we further examined the expression of DNA damage repair genes. These genes were also significantly upregulated in metastatic tumor cells (Fig. 2d, Figure S1d and S2), indicating a potential connection between lipid metabolism, cisplatin resistance, and enhanced DNA repair in the context of lung cancer bone metastasis [30, 31]. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis confirmed increased expression of ELOVL1, ERCC1, and PARP1 in bone metastatic tumors, implicating their roles in lipid metabolism and DNA damage repair (Fig. 2e).

To substantiate these findings, we established an obese mouse model by feeding mice a long-term high-fat diet (HFD) and implanted Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells into the tibia to simulate bone metastasis. IVIS fluorescence imaging and transcriptome sequencing of tumor tissues after 14 days showed accelerated tumor progression in the HFD group, with KEGG enrichment analysis highlighting fatty acid metabolism pathways and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 2f and g). These pathways are known to regulate fatty acid synthesis and have been linked to enhanced tumor cell resistance to chemotherapy by supporting DNA repair mechanisms. Clues within these pathways pointed to the critical role of the PPAR signaling pathway and biosynthesis of fatty acids. The PPAR signaling pathway is closely associated with fatty acid synthesis and regulates the transcription of genes related to this process by binding to its ligands, such as free fatty acids and prostaglandin derivatives [32]. Previous studies have shown that fatty acid synthesis can enhance tumor cell resistance to chemotherapeutic agents by facilitating DNA damage repair [33, 34]. Building on this, GSEA analysis of GO-BP terms associated with fatty acid synthesis revealed a marked upregulation in tumor tissues of HFD mice, implying that obesity-driven enhanced fatty acid synthesis in the tumor microenvironment might promote tumor progression (Figure S3). Notably, the expression levels of fatty acid binding protein (FABP) were also elevated in the HFD group (Figure S4). FABP family primarily functions in the transport and metabolism of fatty acids [35], which further suggested that obese tumor-bearing mice might have more fatty acids transported into tumor cells. In summary, these enrichment results revealed that obesity promoted fatty acid synthesis within the tumor microenvironment, potentially accelerating tumor progression by supporting DNA repair mechanisms and reinforcing drug resistance.

To further explore the mechanistic relationship between fatty acid synthesis, cisplatin resistance, and DNA damage repair, we investigated the interactions between adipocytes and A549 lung cancer cells. After differentiating 3T3-L1 pre-adipocytes into mature adipocytes, characterized by the accumulation of lipid droplets (Figure S5), we established a co-culture system in which A549 cells were exposed to the adipocytes (Fig. 2h). Oil Red O staining revealed a significant increase in lipid content in Adi-A549 cells, demonstrating that adipocyte-tumor cell interactions enhance fatty acid synthesis in the tumor cells (Fig. 2j and k). To investigate the metabolic alterations in Adi-A549 cells, we performed broad-targeted metabolomic profiling (Fig. 2i and Figure S6-8). Xanthine, xanthosine, and ribosyladenosine—intermediates of purine metabolism—were markedly upregulated in Adi-A549 cells (Figure S7). As key substrates in nucleotide synthesis and salvage pathways, their accumulation suggests enhanced nucleic acid metabolism, potentially supporting increased DNA synthesis and repair capacity. Pathway enrichment of significantly altered metabolites revealed pronounced upregulation of nucleotide and purine metabolism, pointing to enhanced DNA synthesis and repair. Additionally, increased choline metabolism and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis suggest intensified membrane lipid turnover, potentially stabilizing drug efflux pumps and improving membrane fluidity, which together may underlie the observed drug-resistant phenotype (Fig. 2i).

Additionally, to investigate the impact of adipocyte-cancer cell interactions on cisplatin resistance, we compared the viability of A549 cells, Adi-A549 cells, and cisplatin-resistant A549 (A549/CDDP) cells under varying concentrations of CDDP (Fig. 2l). Remarkably, Adi-A549 cells exhibited significantly higher resistance to CDDP than both A549 cells and A549/CDDP cells, maintaining substantial viability even at high CDDP concentrations (~ 200 µM). This suggests that adipocyte-induced lipid metabolism enhances tumor cell resistance to CDDP, potentially through metabolic reprogramming and cellular adaptation to chemotherapy-induced stress. These results implicate lipid metabolic pathways as a key contributor to the drug-resistant phenotype in metastatic lung cancer. At the molecular level, we further compared the expression levels of SCD-1, ELOVL1, and GPX4 proteins between Adi-A549 cells and A549 cells. Consistent with the single-cell sequencing results, the expression levels of SCD-1, ELOVL1, and GPX4 were all increased in Adi-A549 cells (Fig. 2m-o and Figure S9), supporting the notion that lipid metabolism is upregulated in adipocyte-associated tumor cells, contributing to their enhanced survival and resistance.

To emphasize the necessity of fatty acid synthesis in tumor resistance and DNA damage repair, we treated both Adi-A549 cells and A549 cells with C75, an inhibitor of fatty acid synthase [36]. Western blot analysis revealed a significant downregulation of SCD-1 in both cell types, indicating a reduction in fatty acid synthesis (Figure S10). Notably, C75 treatment reversed the upregulation of ERCC1 and PARP1 in Adi-A549 cells, restoring their sensitivity to CDDP and impairing DNA repair mechanisms (Fig. 2p). These results underscore the critical role of fatty acid synthesis in maintaining tumor resistance and promoting efficient DNA repair in the bone metastatic microenvironment.

The findings highlight the complex interplay between lipid metabolism, chemoresistance, and DNA damage repair in the context of lung cancer bone metastasis. The upregulation of lipid metabolism-related genes and DNA repair pathways in bone metastatic lung cancer cells suggests a metabolic reprogramming that supports tumor cell survival and resistance to CDDP. Specifically, BMAs-induced fatty acid synthesis provides metabolic substrates that enhance tumor cell invasiveness, suggesting that lipid metabolic reprogramming in the bone metastatic niche is a key driver of therapeutic resistance. Furthermore, the data implicates fatty acid synthesis inhibitors as promising therapeutic agents that could potentially sensitize bone metastatic tumors to chemotherapy by disrupting the metabolic adaptations that confer drug resistance.

Synthesis and preparation of KACPNP@Asp

Given the critical role of fatty acid synthesis in promoting tumor resistance in bone metastasis microenvironment, we constructed a library of 14 fatty acid synthesis inhibitors to identify compounds that could enhance cisplatin sensitivity in A549/CDDP cells (Fig. 3a and b). Among these compounds, KAE, when combined with CDDP, exhibited the most potent cytotoxic promotion effect, suggesting that KAE not only enhanced cisplatin sensitivity but also potentially modulated fatty acid metabolism to overcome chemotherapy resistance in tumors. Consequently, we selected KAE as a key building-block to develop a novel nanoparticle system capable of co-delivering both CDDP and KAE for enhanced therapeutic efficacy. The KACPNP@Asp nanoparticle formulation was constructed through the supramolecular assembly of KAE and CDDP at a 1:1 molar ratio, with the biocompatible polymer PAsp serving as a stabilizing and targeting agent(Fig. 1b and Table S1) [37, 38]. PAsp’s strong affinity for hydroxyapatite (HA), a major constituent of bone tissue, endowed KACPNP@Asp with enhanced bone-targeting capabilities, particularly important for treating bone metastases [39].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image confirmed the uniform formation of nanoparticles with distinct electron-dense features (Fig. 3c), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements indicated an average particle size of 274.25 nm, ideal for cellular uptake and prolonged circulation (Fig. 3d). The stability of the KACPNP@Asp formulation was evaluated over a 72-h period. While KACPNP exhibited significant aggregation over time, leading to particle size increase up to ~ 1600 nm, the PAsp-coated KACPNP@Asp remained stable, with no notable changes in size distribution (Fig. 3e). This remarkable stability is attributed to the protective role of PAsp, which prevents aggregation and enhances the dispersion and circulation time of the nanoparticles in vivo. Such stability is critical for ensuring consistent drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, elemental analysis via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed the successful incorporation of KAE and CDDP into the nanoparticles (Fig. 3f and g). And complementary UV-vis absorption spectra analysis also supported these findings (Fig. 3h), establishing their suitability for co-delivery of the two therapeutics.

To exploit the acidic TME for targeted drug release, we assessed the pH-sensitive behavior of KACPNP@Asp. In simulated intracellular conditions (pH = 5.0), representative of endosomal or lysosomal compartments [40], KACPNP@Asp released significant amounts of CDDP within 2 h, whereas negligible release was observed at neutral condition (Fig. 3i). This pH-responsive release profile ensures that the therapeutic agents are primarily released within the tumor cells, minimizing systemic toxicity and enhancing localized therapeutic effects. Finally, the bone-targeting potential of KACPNP@Asp was assessed using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled nanoparticles and bovine bone slices. KACPNP@Asp showed significantly higher retention on the bone slices compared to non-targeted KACPNP, highlighting the contribution of PAsp in facilitating bone-specific targeting (Fig. 3j).This enhanced bone affinity is especially valuable for treating bone metastases, a common and debilitating complication in advanced cancer.

KACPNP@Asp inhibits tumor lipid metabolism in vitro

The inhibitory effects of KACPNP@Asp on lipid metabolism and its potential to overcome CDDP resistance were evaluated in A549/CDDP cells. Our findings reveal that KACPNP@Asp significantly inhibits lipid droplet formation, as evidenced by reduced Nile Red fluorescence, indicating disruption of intracellular lipid storage (Fig. 4a). This observation is further supported by the substantial reduction in intracellular lipid content following treatment with both KAE and KACPNP@Asp, compared to the control and CDDP groups (Fig. 4b). Lipidomic profiling revealed that KACPNP@Asp treatment led to a marked reduction in the monounsaturated fatty acid content of various glyceryl phosphatides (Fig. 4c), particularly lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) and plasmenylethanolamine (PE-O). Quantitative results revealed a significant decrease in the ratio of monounsaturated to polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in PE-O, as well as in the ratio of monounsaturated to saturated fatty acids (SFAs) in LPI (Fig. 4d and e). These alterations in membrane lipid composition indicate that KACPNP@Asp may affect membrane structure and fluidity. Such changes are likely to enhance drug sensitivity and reduce tumor cell resistance to chemotherapy. Additionally, KACPNP@Asp caused a significant reduction in the monounsaturated fatty acid content of sphingomyelin (SM) and cholesteryl esters (CE) (Figure S11 and S12), which are essential for maintaining membrane fluidity [41].

KACPNP@Asp also suppressed palmitic acid production, a key end product of de novo fatty acid synthesis (Fig. 4f), leading to a reduction in the synthesis of diacylglycerols and triacylglycerols—crucial components of lipid storage and energy supply (Fig. 4g and h). This inhibition of fatty acid biosynthesis is particularly relevant, as fatty acid biosynthesis is often linked to enhanced DNA repair and chemoresistance in cancer cells [33, 34, 42]. Consistent with this, we observed a reduction in the expression of key fatty acid metabolic enzymes, including SCD-1 and ELOVL1 (Fig. 4i-k), further supporting the role of KACPNP@Asp in modulating lipid metabolism. Notably, inhibition of SCD-1 activity, which converts saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) [9, 43], was confirmed by a reduction in the MUFA/SFA ratios of key lipid species, such as Carnitine-OH, LPS, and SPH (Figure S13). This suggests that KACPNP@Asp effectively alters lipid metabolism in A549/CDDP cells.

To further investigate the impact of KACPNP@Asp on membrane integrity and permeability, we performed DiI staining to visualize the membrane structure (Fig. 4l). The results revealed that KACPNP@Asp treatment significantly disrupted membrane integrity and permeability, as indicated by the reduced fluorescence intensity and the loss of clear membrane delineation. In contrast, cisplatin-treated cells exhibited only a modest decrease in fluorescence intensity, with their membrane morphology remaining largely intact. This structural disruption likely contributes to enhanced drug uptake, as evidenced by the increased cellular internalization of KACPNP@Asp compared to CDDP (Fig. 4m). This was further corroborated by measuring intracellular platinum concentrations, which were higher in the KACPNP@Asp-treated group (Fig. 4n).

Taken together, these findings suggest that KACPNP@Asp exerts its therapeutic effect by modulating lipid metabolism and membrane properties. The inhibition of lipid droplet formation, suppression of fatty acid biosynthesis, and alteration of membrane fluidity are key mechanisms through which KACPNP@Asp enhances CDDP uptake and reverses drug resistance. By disrupting lipid synthesis and storage, which may potentially disrupt tumor cell DNA damage repair, and enhancing drug permeability, KACPNP@Asp represents a promising strategy to improve chemotherapy efficacy and overcome resistance in cancer cells.

Anti-tumor effects and resistance reversal of the KACPNP@Asp in vitro

Subsequently, we explored the efficacy of KACPNP@Asp in killing tumors in vitro. KACPNP@Asp demonstrated a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect in A549/CDDP cells, outperforming both KAE and CDDP (Fig. 5a), even at low concentrations (10 µM). This was further validated by flow cytometric analysis, which confirmed that KACPNP@Asp induced a significantly higher rate of apoptosis compared to the other treatments (Fig. 5b). In line with these findings, fluorescence microscopy revealed that KACPNP@Asp caused the most pronounced cell death, as evidenced by a marked reduction in cell viability relative to the KAE and CDDP groups (Fig. 5c and Figure S14). Western blot analysis revealed that KACPNP@Asp treatment significantly upregulated key pro-apoptotic proteins, such as p53 and cytochrome c (Cyto C), and downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 in A549/CDDP cells (Fig. 5d-g). These changes in apoptosis-related proteins suggest that KACPNP@Asp enhances tumor cell death through the activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways.

Building on previous results indicating that KACPNP@Asp modulates lipid metabolism and affects DNA damage repair, we next explored the expression of DNA damage repair-related proteins. The critical DNA repair protein ERCC1, which plays a pivotal role in tumor drug resistance, showed significant downregulation following KACPNP@Asp treatment (Fig. 5h and i). Interestingly, ERCC1 levels in A549/CDDP cells increased after CDDP treatment, suggesting that A549/CDDP cells developed resistance during CDDP treatment. KACPNP@Asp, however, circumvented this resistance, likely by enhancing CDDP uptake and overcoming the mechanisms that typically contribute to DNA repair. Additionally, KACPNP@Asp downregulated GPX4 (Fig. 5m and n), a critical protein involved in lipid peroxide detoxification and the reinforcement of tumor cell resistance [44]. Furthermore, cellular glutathione (GSH), which play a pivotal role in the reduction of lipid peroxides, also showed a significant reduction as shown by GSH assays (Fig. 5o). By reducing GPX4 expression and GSH levels, KACPNP@Asp may enhance oxidative stress in tumor cells, sensitizing them to apoptosis. This suggests that KACPNP@Asp can reverse drug resistance through both direct inhibition of DNA repair proteins and modulation of cellular antioxidant defenses.

We also investigated DNA damage in response to KACPNP@Asp treatment. The expression levels of γH2A.X and p-Chk1, markers of DNA damage, were significantly elevated following treatment with KACPNP@Asp (Fig. 5h, j and k). These findings indicate that KACPNP@Asp induces substantial DNA damage, further contributing to its therapeutic efficacy. Immunofluorescence staining for γH2A.X confirmed these results, with KACPNP@Asp exhibiting the strongest DNA-damaging effects compared to other treatments (Fig. 5l and Figure S15). Moreover, KACPNP@Asp demonstrated an inhibitory effect on osteoclast differentiation, a critical process in the osteolytic progression of bone metastasis (Figure S16 and S17). Given the osteolytic nature of cancer bone metastases, this suggests that KACPNP@Asp may have additional therapeutic potential in targeting lung cancer bone metastasis.

Anti-tumor effects of the KACPNP@Asp in vivo

To investigate the anti-tumor effect of KACPNP@Asp in vivo, we established an in vivo A549/CDDP cell tibial tumor model using nude mice to simulate lung cancer bone metastasis. The mice were intravenously administered PBS, CDDP, KAE, or KACPNP@Asp every two days for a total of five times. The in vivo imaging system (IVIS) was utilized to measure the fluorescence intensity at the tumor site on day 0, day 6, and day 15 following the initiation of treatment (Fig. 6a). In comparison to other groups, the KACPNP@Asp group showed the most effective tumor suppression (Fig. 6b, Figure S18), with the most significant effect observed on day 15. Furthermore, throughout the treatment period, no deaths occurred in the KACPNP@Asp group (Fig. 6c), and there was no significant weight loss (Fig. 6d). Conversely, the CDDP and KAE groups both exhibited a 50% mortality rate during the treatment, with the CDDP group displaying marked weight loss and inadequate tumor suppression; we hypothesized that this might be linked to tumor resistance caused by CDDP.

Moreover, we conjugated CDDP and KACPNP@Asp with the Cy5 fluorescent group and administered them via tail vein injection into the mice, observing the fluorescence distribution in the major organs and tumors (Figure S19). The results showed that KACPNP@Asp exhibited pronounced accumulation in tumors, with strong fluorescence signals persisting up to 48 h and gradually declining thereafter, while CDDP showed weaker tumor enrichment and more rapid clearance. In addition to tumors, CDDP displayed stronger renal accumulation, consistent with its renal excretion characteristics, whereas KACPNP@Asp demonstrated more prominent hepatic distribution, reflecting nanoparticles uptake by the reticuloendothelial system. To verify whether KACPNP@Asp could be taken up within the bone microenvironment, we injected Cy5-labeled KACPNP@Asp into tumor-bearing mice and performed fluorescence imaging of bone sections (Figure S20). Notably, strong Cy5 signals were observed in the bone microenvironment 6 h post-injection, confirming the efficient accumulation and uptake of KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles in situ. To further assess the circulation profile, ex vivo fluorescence imaging of serum samples was performed at 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-administration (Figure S21). The results revealed that KACPNP@Asp maintained relatively high fluorescence intensity within the first 6 h, which gradually decreased at 12 h and was markedly diminished by 24 h, reflecting a favorable circulation half-life and subsequent clearance process. Next, we evaluated the in vivo safety of KACPNP@Asp, and the hematoxylin and eosin (HE) sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys from mice treated with KACPNP@Asp showed no noticeable abnormalities compared to the PBS group (Figure S22). Furthermore, the blood routine and biochemical analyses from different intervention groups showed that KACPNP@Asp did not cause any increases in values, whereas CDDP significantly elevated the alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels (Figure S23), indicating that long-term use of CDDP caused liver and kidney damage in the mice.

Micro-CT analysis was then used to analyze the microarchitecture of the distal femur (Fig. 6e and f). The KACPNP@Asp group showed improved bone microarchitecture including higher bone volume, trabecular thickness, trabecular number and bone mineral density compared to other groups. To further clarify the levels of lipid metabolism, drug resistance, and DNA damage at the tumor site, we performed immunofluorescence on tumor sections from mice treated with PBS, CDDP, KAE and KACPNP@Asp (Fig. 6g and h). TUNEL staining revealed a marked increase in apoptosis at the tumor site after KACPNP@Asp treatment. The IF results for ERCC1, γH2A.X, and SCD-1 indicated that KACPNP@Asp led to decreased lipid metabolism and drug resistance levels at the tumor site, while increasing DNA damage levels, which were consistent with the results of our in vitro experiments. To explore the impact of different intervention conditions on tumor secondary metastasis, we meticulously compared the HE sections of the liver and lungs across all intervention groups. The HE sections of the lungs and liver in the KACPNP@Asp intervention group displayed well-defined structures with no detected metastasis, whereas the other groups exhibited varying degrees of metastasis (Fig. 6i). Our results prove that KACPNP@Asp can significantly inhibit tumor progression and metastasis in vivo, while also exhibiting safety and targeting properties.

Discussion

In this study, we have elucidated the pivotal role of BMAs in modulating fatty acid metabolism within the bone metastatic niche of lung cancer, highlighting their critical involvement in promoting tumor progression and chemoresistance. Our findings underscore the metabolic reprogramming that occurs in metastatic lung cancer cells, where fatty acid metabolism is dysregulated and contributes to both enhanced cell survival and resistance to conventional therapies. By developing a novel nanotherapeutic system, we demonstrate the potential of targeting fatty acid metabolism as a strategy to overcome drug resistance in bone metastasis. Through screening multiple FAS inhibitors, we formulated KAE in combination with CDDP into stable nanoparticles, surface-modified with PAsp to form a core-shell structure. This formulation effectively disrupts fatty acid metabolism in bone metastatic cells, thereby enhancing their sensitivity to chemotherapy. This approach not only addresses the metabolic vulnerabilities in tumor cells but also offers a promising strategy for selectively targeting the bone metastatic microenvironment, minimizing off-target toxicity. Although fatty acid metabolism is essential for many normal tissues, our short-term in vivo data showed no obvious systemic toxicity of KACPNP@Asp. Nonetheless, potential off-target effects, including long-term or species-specific toxicity, cannot be excluded, and future studies with extended observation and human-relevant models will be required to fully establish safety. Given the increasing prevalence of bone metastases in lung cancer patients, our study provides a compelling rationale for the development of lipid metabolism-targeted therapies as a means to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy and improve clinical outcomes. Future clinical translation of these strategies holds great promise for advancing treatment options for patients with metastatic lung cancer, particularly in overcoming the therapeutic challenges posed by the bone microenvironment.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the patient sample size was relatively limited, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Second, although short-term pharmacokinetic and biodistribution analyses were performed, long-term safety, clearance, and potential off-target effects remain to be fully evaluated. Third, while our mechanistic investigations provide supportive evidence, additional in-depth validation will be required to fully delineate the underlying molecular pathways. Future studies addressing these aspects will be essential to strengthen the translational relevance of our findings.

Conclusion

This study identifies bone marrow adipocytes as key modulators of fatty acid metabolism in the bone metastatic niche of lung cancer, driving tumor progression and chemoresistance. We developed a PAsp-modified nanotherapeutic system co-delivering KAE and CDDP, which effectively disrupts fatty acid metabolism and restores chemosensitivity in metastatic cells. By targeting metabolic vulnerabilities within the bone microenvironment, this approach highlights a promising strategy to enhance chemotherapy efficacy while minimizing off-target toxicity. These findings provide a strong rationale for further preclinical and translational development of lipid metabolism–targeted therapies to improve outcomes in lung cancer patients with bone metastases.

Cell lines and animals

Human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells, Lewis Lung Carcinoma (LLC) cells, 3T3-L1 pre-adipocytes and cisplatin-resistant A549 cells (A549/CDDP) were obtained from Fudan University (Shanghai, China). Female BALB/c-Nude mice and male C57BL/6J mice, 4 weeks old were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., LTD (Jiangsu, China). We used male C57BL/6J mice for high-cholesterol diet feeding for 12 weeks to induce obesity in the HFD mouse model. The mice were monitored for one week to ensure a suitable survival environment and assess their health status. They were housed in pathogen-free conditions with BALB/c-Nude mice equipment at a controlled room temperature of 26–28 °C and maintained humidity levels of 40–60%. A daily light/dark cycle of 10 h of light and 14 h of darkness was implemented. Sterilized food and water were provided ad libitum. Health checks were conducted for both BALB/c-Nude and C57BL/6J mice during this period.

Synthesis of NPs

Cisplatin (CDDP, 15 mg, 0.05 mmol) and silver nitrate (16.99 mg, 0.1 mmol) were dissolved in 2 mL deionized water and stirred at room temperature in the dark for 24 h. The reaction mixture was centrifuged to separate and remove the silver chloride precipitate, after which the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, the supernatant was filtered through a membrane with a pore size of 0.22 microns to obtain hydrated cisplatin (7.5 mg/ml). kaempferol (50 mg) was dissolved in 5mL DMSO to obtain kaempferol solution (10 mg/ml). A bone targeting polymer poly-(α, β)-DL-aspartic acid (PAsp,5 mg) was dissolved in 5mL deionized water to obtain PAsp solution(1 mg/ml). Hydrated cisplatin (7.5 mg/ml) and kaempferol solution (10 mg/ml) were ultrasonicated (100 Hz) under alkaline conditions (pH = 11) at a molar ratio of 1:1 in 2 mL deionized water solutions containing hydrated cisplatin (375.06 µg/ml) and kaempferol (357.79 µg/ml) for 3 min at room temperature in the dark to obtain KACPNP. Then, an equimolar amount of PAsp (1 mg/ml) was added with KACPNP, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 3 min at room temperature in the dark to obtain KACPNP@Asp. The obtained KACPNP and KACPNP@Asp nanoparticle solutions were kept at room temperature before characterization and further use.

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Lung cancer bone metastatic tumor tissues undergo dissociation to generate single-cell suspensions. Enzymatic digestion was performed using an optimized combination of collagenase IV (1 mg/mL), DNase I (0.1 mg/mL), and trypsin/EDTA, followed by filtration through a 40 µm cell strainer. Single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium system, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, single cells were encapsulated into Gel Beads-in-Emulsion (GEMs), where individual cells were lysed, and RNA molecules were barcoded with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). Reverse transcription was performed to generate barcoded cDNA, which was subsequently amplified via PCR. The amplified cDNA was used to construct sequencing libraries using Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kit v3. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with paired-end sequencing 150 bp reads, targeting an average of 50,000–100,000 reads per cell to ensure sufficient coverage and depth. Base calling and demultiplexing were performed using the Illumina pipeline to generate FASTQ files.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from 17 lung cancer samples, including 6 self-sequenced bone metastasis samples and 11 primary tumor samples from the GSE131907 dataset, were integrated for analysis. Each dataset (primary and metastasis cohorts) was processed and annotated independently. Within each dataset, BBKNN was applied to remove library- and patient-level technical variation for visualization and clustering. Malignant epithelial cells from the two datasets were then merged; however, cross-dataset batch correction was not performed, as such methods can attenuate biologically meaningful variation in malignant cells, which is largely driven by copy-number alterations and clonal architecture. Raw UMI counts were filtered (cells with < 200 genes or > 20% mitochondrial RNA removed; genes detected in < 3 cells excluded). Counts were library-size normalized to 10,000 counts per cell and log-transformed (log1p). For visualization we regressed out total counts, %mitochondrial RNA and cell-cycle scores (S and G2/M). Highly variable genes (≈ 2–3k per dataset) were selected; PCA (50 PCs), k-NN graph (k = 30, cosine), and UMAP (min_dist = 0.3) were computed.

For the merged malignant object, we computed PCA/UMAP on the same normalization without cross-dataset batch correction. Differential expression between primary vs. metastasis was performed at the patient level using pseudo-bulk counts with sc.tl.rank_genes_groups, modeling group and batch (and patient for paired samples). Functional enrichment analyses, including KEGG and GO analyses, were performed on the differentially expressed genes. Gene sets related to lipid metabolism, cisplatin resistance, and DNA damage repair within the metastatic lesions were analyzed using AUCcell. These gene sets were projected onto UMAP plots to visualize and compare their differential expression between the bone metastasis and primary tumor groups.

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA sequencing was conducted by LC-Bio Technology Co., LTD. (Hangzhou, China). Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596018), and mRNA was subsequently purified with Dynabeads Oligo (dT) (Thermo Fisher). Fragmentation of the mRNA was achieved by exposure to divalent cations at 94 °C for 5–7 min (NEB, E6150). The fragmented RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen), followed by second-strand synthesis using E. coli DNA polymerase I, RNase H, and dUTP (Thermo Fisher). After A-tailing, dual-index adapters were ligated, and size selection was carried out with AMPure XP beads. U-labeled second-strand DNA was degraded using heat-labile UDG (NEB). The library was then amplified through 8 PCR cycles, producing cDNA fragments with an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology, Hangzhou, China), following standard manufacturer protocols.

Differential gene expression analysis between the HFD and CON groups was carried out using DESeq2 software. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and an absolute fold change of ≥ 2 were identified as differentially expressed. These genes were further analyzed for enrichment in KEGG pathways.

Metabolomics analysis

Broad-targeted metabolomic profiling of Adi-A549 and control A549 cells was performed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cell pellets were extracted with 80% methanol containing internal standards, subjected to repeated freeze–thaw cycles, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. Metabolites were analyzed using UPLC coupled with high-resolution QTOF-MS and triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP) MS systems (Sciex). Both positive and negative ion modes were applied. Chromatographic separation was performed using C18 and HILIC columns under optimized gradients. MS data acquisition was conducted in both IDA and MRM modes to ensure broad metabolite coverage and sensitivity. Instrument calibration and quality control were carried out according to the standard operating procedures of the service provider.

Widely targeted lipidomics assay

Widely targeted lipidomics assay was performed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cell pellets were thawed on ice, resuspended in 100 µL ultrapure water (with protease inhibitors), and divided into two parts. For lipid extraction, 50 µL of suspension was mixed with 500 µL methanol/MTBE/internal standard solution, vortexed, followed by 100 µL water, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (4 °C, 10 min). The supernatant was concentrated, reconstituted, and stored at −80 °C for LC-MS/MS analysis. The remaining 50 µL of the sample was subjected to freeze-thaw cycles, followed by centrifugation, and used for protein quantification using the BCA assay. Lipid extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (QTRAP® 6500+) equipped with a Thermo C30 column. Separation was performed with a binary gradient of solvent A (ACN/water, 0.1% FA, 10 mmol/L AmFA) and solvent B (ACN/IPA, 0.1% FA, 10 mmol/L AmFA) at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min, a column temperature of 45 °C, and an injection volume of 2 µL. ESI-MS/MS analysis was conducted in both positive and negative ion modes with optimized source parameters and MRM conditions.

Differential metabolites for two-group comparisons were determined using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP > 1) combined with statistical significance (P < 0.05, Student’s t-test). VIP values were calculated from OPLS-DA, which incorporates score plots and permutation plots, generated through the MetaboAnalystR package in R. Data preprocessing involved log2 transformation and mean centering before applying OPLS-DA. A 200-iteration permutation test was performed to control overfitting.

Cell cultures and adipocyte differentiation

A549 cells and cisplatin-resistant A549 cells were cultured in F-12 K medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. 3T3-L1 cells were grown in DMEM containing 10% newborn calf serum (NBCS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cell cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% air. To induce differentiation, 3T3-L1 preadipocytes were first harvested and grown to 100% confluence. Two days after reaching confluence, differentiation was initiated by treating the cells with a hormonal cocktail comprising 10 µg/mL insulin, 1 µM dexamethasone, and 0.5 mM IBMX in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After another two days, the induction medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% FBS and 10 µg/mL insulin. The culture medium was refreshed every two days thereafter.

Oil red-O staining

The differentiated adipocytes, A549 cells, and adipocyte-associated A549 cells were rinsed twice with 500 µL of PBS and fixed with 400 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature (RT) for 30 min. After fixation, the cells were washed twice with PBS before 400 µL of Oil Red-O staining solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at RT. Excess stain was removed by washing with 60% isopropyl alcohol, and the cells were then examined under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan; magnification, 400x).

Indirect co-culture

Adipocytes were indirectly co-cultured with A549 cells using a Transwell culture system (pore size: 0.4 μm; Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) in F-12 K medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C. To study adipocyte biology, preadipocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL in the upper chamber and cultured until they reached full confluency and underwent differentiation. Mature adipocytes were harvested for 8 days post-differentiation. These intact adipocytes were then co-cultured with A549 cells, which were seeded in the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL. We refer to these A549 cells as adipocyte-associated A549 cells. The time of co-culture was 9 days. A549 cells were cultured alone served as controls.

Intracellular lipid detection

A549 cells and adipocyte-associated A549 cells were stained with Oil Red-O and the dye retained in the cells was eluted into isopropanol. Absorbance at 496 nm was then determined to quantify the lipid contents.

The in vitro cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxicity was assessed using the MTT assay. A549/CDDP cells were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was then replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing various concentrations of lipid metabolism inhibitors, CDDP, KACPNP, or KACPNP@Asp. After 48 h of incubation, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by an additional 4 h of incubation. The medium was subsequently replaced with 100 µL of DMSO to dissolve the formazan crystals, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (50 µg) from the cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) BSA at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting GPX4, SCD-1, p53, Cyto c, Bcl-2, γH2A.X, PARP1, p-Chk1, ELOVL1 and ERCC1 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at appropriate dilutions.Following primary antibody incubation, the membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated with a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After additional washes with TBST, the protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Millipore).

Characterization of NPs

Pt, kaempferol and PAsp concentrations in KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles were characterized by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, Thermoscientific, USA). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were taken on an electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan). HAADF-STEM images, and the EDX mapping were obtained using a JEOL electron microscope (JEM2100, JEOL, Japan). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectrum of CDDP, PAsp, kaempferol, and KACPNP@Asp were achivevd by using UV–vis spectra (UV2700, Shimadzu). Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of nanoparticles were measured by Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK). The loading efficiencies of CDDP and KAE in KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles were determined according to the physicochemical properties of each compound. The CDDP content was quantified based on the platinum concentration measured by inductively coupled plasma spectrometry, while the KAE content was determined using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at the characteristic absorption peak of KAE (370 nm). After measurement, the nanoparticle suspensions were lyophilized into powder and accurately weighed. The drug loading efficiency was calculated as the ratio of the weight of drug loaded in nanoparticles to the total weight of the nanoparticles. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure accuracy.

In vitro drug release from NPs

The KACPNP@Asp nanoparticle solutions were transferred into 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes, and each tube was filled with 2 mL deionized water buffer (pH 7.4 or 5.0). At each indicated time point, the centrifuge tube was centrifuged (15000 rpm, 20 min), and then 50 µl sample in the supernatant was collected and 50 µl of fresh deionized water were replenished in the centrifuge tube. The platinum concentrations in the collected samples were measured by ICP. Three repeats were conducted for each sample.

Synthesis of FITC-labeled NPs and CY5-labeled NPs

The FITC-labeled KACPNP and KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles were synthesized by slmply mixing the obtained KACPNP and KACPNP@Asp nanoparticle with FITC (dissolved in DMSO) at a molar ratio of 10:1 (CDDP: FITC) respectively. The mixture was stirred in the dark at room temperature for 24 h. The product was intensively dialyzed against deionized water (3000 Da). CY5-labeled KACPNP@Asp was synthesized by the same procedure.

In vitro bone targeting assay

Bovine bone slices (6 mm in diameter and 1 mm in height, gifts from prof. Kaili Lin, Shanghai Jiaotong University) were placed into 96-well plates and incubated with KACPNP (25 mM Pt) and KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles (25 mM Pt) which were labeled with FITC for 24 h. The incubation solutions were removed, and tablets were rinsed in deionized water for 30 min and dried at room temperature. IVIS was used to record the luminescence intensity of the bovine bone slices.

Cell apoptosis and calcein-AM/PI assay

The apoptotic effects of PBS, CDDP, KACPNP, and KACPNP@Asp on A549/CDDP cells were quantified using the Annexin V-FITC/7AAD kit. A549/CDDP cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with PBS, CDDP, KACPNP, or KACPNP@Asp at a concentration of 30 µM. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were harvested and assessed for apoptosis using Annexin V-FITC staining and flow cytometry. For Calcein-AM/PI staining, the cells underwent the same treatment protocol as in the apoptosis assay. They were then stained with Calcein-AM and PI using the respective detection kit and observed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following a 1-hour blocking step, the fixed cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against γH2A.X (Abcam, ab81299) diluted in blocking buffer. After washing, the cells were incubated with a secondary antibody (Jackson) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAPI staining to visualize nuclei. Immunofluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica SP5, Germany). Nile red was used to determine the lipid content in A549/CDDP cells. Intracellular lipid droplets were stained by Nile red (Beyotime, 1 : 1000, 37 °C, 20 min). Upon treatment with PBS, CDDP, KAE and KACPNP@Asp (30 µM) for 48 h. The A549/CDDP cells were washed by PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. Hoechst 33,342 was used to stain the nucleus and the cells were scanned using the confocal microscope. Nile: λex = 485 nm, λem = 535 nm. Hoechst 33,342: λex = 350 nm, λem = 461 nm.

Membrane staining

A549/CDDP cells were seeded into 35 mm confocal dishes and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Upon treatment with PBS, CDDP, KAE and KACPNP@Asp (30 µM) for 48 h. The cells were washed with PBS (4 °C) and stained with Dil (Beyotime, 1 : 1000, 37 °C, 20 min) and Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, 37 °C, 10 min) after treatment. The images were evaluated using a confocal microscopy. Dil: λex = 549 nm, λem = 565 nm.

In vitro GSH test

The A549/CDDP cells were seeded in six-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well). Following overnight incubation, the cells were treated with PBS, CDDP, KACPNP and KACPNP@Asp respectively (concentration: 30µM) for 48 h. Subsequently, an equal number of cells from each group was collected, and the cellular GSH content was measured using the Total Glutathione Assay Kit.

Cellular uptake

A549/CDDP cells were seeded into a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and incubated for 12–16 h. Then the fresh medium was replaced, and the cells were treated with 30 µM FITC-labeled CDDP or KACPNP@Asp for 24 h. DAPI was applied to stain the nuclei, and the cells were imaged using a confocal microscope. The excitation and emission wavelengths were as follows: FITC (λex = 494 nm, λem = 520 nm) and DAPI (λex = 364 nm, λem = 454 nm). After imaging, the cells were washed with cold PBS (1×) and harvested. The harvested cells were digested with 100 µL of nitric acid at 95 °C for 2 h. To the digested solution, 50 µL of H₂O₂ and 100 µL of HCl were added to achieve a uniform mixture. The platinum (Pt) content in the sample was quantified using ICP-MS after appropriate dilution.

Differentiation of primary bone marrow monocytes

6-week-old C57BL/6 mice were sacrificed, the tibia and femur of the mice were sterilized using 75% ethanol solution. After rinsed for three times with PBS, the ends of tibia and femur were removed to expose the bone marrow cavity. A syringe filled with α-MEM medium was used to blow out the bone marrows. The collected cells were cultured in a 6-cm culture dish (containing 10% FBS and 5 ng/mL M-CSF), and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After 24 h incubation, BMSCs were adhered on the plate while BMMs were suspended in the culture medium. For BMMs, the culture medium was carefully collected from the petri dish, and the supernatant solution was filtered with a 40 μm sieve. The filtrate was collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min. The obtained BMMs were suspended with 4 mL α-MEM medium containing 10% FBS and 10 ng/mL M-CSF, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The differentiation of BMMs were conducted in a 96-well plate (~ 8000 cells per well). The culture media were added with 50 ng/mL M-CSF and 100 ng/mL RANKL to stimulate osteoclastogenesis. The cells were incubated with PBS, KAE (10 µM) or KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles (10 µM KAE) for seven days. The medium was replaced with fresh one every two days. Each time the medium was changed, PBS, KAE and KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles were re-added. The differentiated osteoclasts were stained by a TRAP staining kit and photographed by a microscope, and the number of osteoclasts with nuclei more than three was counted.

RT-PCR assay

BMMs were cultured in a 6-well plate with 20,000 cells per well. The culture media were added with 50 ng/mL M-CSF and 100 ng/mL RANKL. The cells were then treated with PBS, KAE (10 µM) and KACPNP@Asp NPs (10 µM KAE) for seven days, and the media were replaced every two days. The differentiated osteoclasts were lysed with 1 mL Trizol for 10 min. Then, 200 µL chloroform was added and the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 ℃ for 15 min. The supernatant was added with 500 µL isopropanol for 10 min, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 15 min. 1 mL 75% ethanol solution was added to wash the sediment, and the obtained RNA was reversed transcribed into cDNA by a reverse transcription kit (Takara). RT-PCR was performed in a CFX96 Connect Real-time PCR Detection System (Thermo-Fisher Scientific). Each PCR reaction mixture (20 µL) contained 2 µL diluted cDNA, 10 µL SYBR Green II Mix (2x), 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 7 µL of double distilled water. The amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min to pre-denaturation, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 15 s. Each reaction was repeated for three times.

The primer sequences for NFATc1, Cathepsin K, TRAP and TRAF6 are listed as follows:

NFATc1: Forward primer GGTGCCTTTTGCGAGCAGTATC.

Reverse primer CGTATGGACCAGAATGTGACGG.

Cathepsin K: Forward primer AGCAGAACGGAGGCATTGACTC.

Reverse primer CCCTCTGCATTTAGCTGCCTTTG.

TRAP: Forward primer GCGACCATTGTTAGCCACATACG.

Reverse primer CGTTGATGTCGCACAGAGGGAT.

TRAF6: Forward primer TTTCCCTGACGGTAAAGTGCCC.

Reverse primer ACCTGGCACTTCTGGAAAGGAC.

Animal experiments

Establishment of a lung cancer bone metastatic animal model

A murine lung cancer bone metastatic model was established to perform in vivo experiments. BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old, female) and C57BL/6J mice (16 weeks old, male) were anesthetized using 3% isoflurane. A549/CDDP cells and LLC cells stably expressing luciferase (1 × 106 cells in 20 µL matrigel matrix) were injected into the cavum medullare of tibias, with A549/CDDP cells injected into BALB/c nude mice and LLC cells into C57BL/6J mice, respectively. To monitor the growth of tumors in the tibias, D-luciferin, a substance of luciferase was administrated via intraperitoneal injection (150 mg/kg mice), and the mice were imaged by in vivo imaging system (IVIS, Lumina-II, Caliper Life Sciences). For BALB/c nude mice, those who with luminescence signals at the tibias were chosen for subsequent in vivo therapeutic experiments.

In vivo anti-tumor efficiency

For the therapeutic experiments, the mice bearing A549/CDDP tumors in the tibias were divided into four groups with six animals in each group. The mice were intravenously injected with PBS, CDDP, KAE or KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles (3 mg Pt/kg mice), respectively. The treatments were repeated every two days with a total number of five injections. After five injections, we observed the mice until day fifteen, after which they were sacrificed. IVIS was used to record the luminescence intensity of tumors at the sixth and fifteenth days. The body weight of mice was recorded every other day. After mice were sacrificed, tumor-bearing legs and major organs were immediately extracted, soaked in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and used for subsequent experiments. And the whole blood from the treated mice were collected for hematological analysis using a hematology analyzer (HEMAVET-950, Drew Scientific). The serum from the treated mice were also collected for liver function and renal function analysis using a fully automatic chemistry analyzer (Chemray 800).

To assess distant metastasis of the tumor, liver and lung sections from each group were examined via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Tumor sections from both the PBS and KACPNP@Asp groups were stained with antibodies consisting of anti-SCD1 (Affinity Biosciences, DF13253,1:200), anti-γH2A.X (Abcam, ab81299,1:200) and anti-ERCC1 (Proteintech,14586-1-AP,1:100) for immunofluorescence. TUNEL staining was also performed to evaluate the cellular apoptosis. Upon staining, the tumor sections were observed using a fluorescence microscope. For biodistribution analysis, the mice bearing A549/CDDP tumors in the tibias were intravenously injected with CY5-labeled CDDP or CY5-labeled KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles (3 mg Pt/kg mice), and the mice were sacrificed after 6 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 7days. The main organs and tissues including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor-bearing legs were harvested, and imaged by IVIS. At 6 h post-injection, bone tissues were harvested from mice, sectioned, and stained with DAPI. The distribution of KACPNP@Asp within the bone microenvironment was then visualized using fluorescence microscopy. At predetermined time points (2, 6, 12, and 24 h) after intravenous injection of Cy5-labeled KACPNP@Asp nanoparticles, blood was collected from tumor-bearing mice and centrifuged to obtain serum. The fluorescence signal of serum samples was measured using an in vivo imaging system.

Micro-CT analysis

The tumor-bearing tibias were excised and scanned by a micro-CT equipment (SkyScan 1276, Bruker). The 3D reconstruction of tumor-bearing tibias was conducted using the CTVox program. CTAn software was used for further analysis of the scanning profiles, including bone volume (BV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and bone mineral density (BMD) results.
