Effect of foot orthotics on running kinetics in adults with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction: A controlled laboratory study
Authors: Ebrahim Piri, AmirAli Jafarnezhadgero, Mahrokh Dehghani, Heidar Sajedi, Valdeci Carlos Dionisio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Individuals with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and pronated feet (ACLR-PF) often display abnormal running ground reaction forces (GRFs), which are associated with higher re-injury risk and post-traumatic osteoarthritis. While foot orthoses (FOs) are used to influence biomechanics, their effectiveness at various post-operative stages in this group remains unknown. This study aimed to evaluate the immediate impact of different FOs on GRFs during running in males with ACLR-PF at 6-, 12-, and 18-months post-operative, compared to a healthy control group. This controlled laboratory study with randomized repeated orthotic conditions included males with ACLR-PF at 6, 12, and 18-months post‑operative, as well as healthy controls. Forty-five right-footed males with ACLR-PF were divided into three groups based on post-operative duration: 6 ± 1 months (group A, n = 15), 12 ± 1 months (group B, n = 15), and 18 ± 1 months (group C, n = 15). A matched health control group (group D, n = 15) was also included. Participants ran at 3.2 m/s under four randomized footwear conditions: control shoe, placebo orthotic, double-density orthotic, and arch-support orthotic. Three-dimensional GRFs were collected using a force plate. ACLR-PF groups at 12- and 18-months showed significantly higher impact vertical peaks and vertical loading rates than the healthy group. The 6-months ACLR-PF group had a longer time-to-peak for the impact vertical peak. Peak positive free moment was higher in the 6- and 18-months ACLR-PF groups compared to controls. A significant main effect of FOs was detected, with the double-density orthotic significantly decreasing the peak positive free moment relative to the control shoe. Significant group-by-FOs interactions indicated that the impact of specific orthoses on peak posterior force, loading rate, and stance time depended on the post-operative stage. Abnormal loading patterns continue up to 18-months after ACLR in individuals with PF, highlighting a critical window for intervention. FOs, especially double-density designs, can immediately adjust specific maladaptive GRFs, such as lowering excessive rotational moments. An orthotic prescription should be customized to the individual’s specific biomechanical deficits and the stage in the rehabilitation process. In this controlled laboratory study, double-density and arch-support FOs acutely modified selected GRFs in males with ACLR and PF across post-operative stages. These findings suggest that FOs may provide immediate biomechanical adjustments during running; however, the long‑term clinical relevance of these acute changes remains uncertain and requires longitudinal investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55763-8.

Introduction

Running is a fundamental human locomotion pattern, but it imposes significant biomechanical loads on the lower extremities, with peak vertical ground reaction forces (vGRF) often exceeding 2.5 times body weight during the stance phase1. Despite its benefits, running-related injuries are common, affecting millions each year, and their prevalence varies widely, ranging from 3% to 85%. The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is among the most frequently injured ligaments, with an estimated incidence of 35 to 85 per 100,000 person-years in active populations2. While ACL reconstruction (ACLR) is the standard surgical method for restoring knee stability, it does not guarantee a return to pre-injury biomechanical function. For instance, a systematic review shows that only 55–65% of athletes return to their pre-injury sport level, and reinjury rates can reach 20–25% within two years of returning to sport3.

The patterns of ground reaction forces (GRFs) serve as a key biomarker for assessing functional recovery and reinjury risk. Post-ACLR, individuals frequently exhibit abnormal GRFs patterns, including elevated peak vGRF, increased loading rates, and asymmetrical limb loading. These patterns significantly increase the risk of post-traumatic osteoarthritis, affecting over 50% of ACLR patients within 10–15 years4. These persistent loading abnormalities are driven by complex biomechanical deficits, influenced by various intrinsic factors.

A key intrinsic factor is a pronated foot (PF). Excessive foot pronation, characterized by rearfoot eversion and tibial internal rotation, disrupts the entire lower extremity kinetic chain. This biomechanical misalignment can increase knee valgus and anterior tibial shear forces, thereby increasing rotational and translational stress on the healing graft5. Consequently, excessive pronation can sustain abnormal loading patterns long after surgery. During the critical return-to-sport phase, typically between 6 and 9 months post-ACLR, individuals often display a “stiff-legged” gait pattern, marked by reduced knee flexion and increased peak vGRF6. These compensatory strategies, initially protective but often persistent, lead to significant limb loading asymmetries7, including uneven vGRF and high loading rates, which were observed even 18–24 months post-operative. The persistence of these deficits raises the risk of graft reinjury and accelerates post-traumatic osteoarthritis8.

To reduce these risks, foot orthoses (FOs) are a typical treatment used to control excessive pronation and modify GRFs. FOs can effectively decrease peak vGRF (by 4–7%) in individuals with PF, improving foot alignment and shock absorption9 and enhancing running mechanics10–12. However, while FOs can control excessive pronation13, the effectiveness of various FOs, such as placebo, arch-support, and double-density, varies, as reflected in their impact on GRFs and their timing characteristics, particularly Time-to-Peak (TTP)14,15. Arch-support orthotics primarily stabilize the medial longitudinal arch, resist excessive rearfoot eversion, and reduce peak posterior force and anterior tibial shear force 14,15, thereby helping to reduce stress on the healing ACL graft10. In contrast, double-density FOs, with their dual-stiffness design16, provide a broader damping effect, effectively reducing peak vertical GRF and promoting smoother force application during running. The varied biomechanical outcomes associated with these distinct designs underscore the need to investigate their specific effects14.

Despite the known benefits of FOs for general pronation, their specific influence on GRFs in individuals with concurrent ACLR and pronated feet (ACLR-PF) remains unexplored across critical rehabilitation milestones (6-, 12-, and 18-months post-operative). This gap is significant because the combination of ACLR and PF can exacerbate abnormal loading and rotational stress on the knee. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the immediate effects of different FO conditions on GRFs in males with ACLR-PF at these key rehabilitation stages, to determine whether specific orthotic designs might be more appropriate at distinct phases of post-ACLR recovery, rather than assuming a uniform orthotic strategy.

We hypothesized that FOs would significantly decrease peak vertical and posterior GRFs across all time frames, with the force-reducing effect becoming more pronounced at 12- and 18- months post-operative compared to 6-months post-operative, reflecting improved neuromuscular and biomechanical adaptation over time.

Methods

Participation

This was a controlled laboratory study with a cross‑sectional grouping by post-operative time (6-, 12-, and 18-months after ACLR-PF) and a healthy control group. Within each participant, the four orthotic conditions (control shoe, placebo orthoses, arch-support orthoses, and double‑density orthoses) were applied in a randomized order using a balanced Latin square design. To ensure a sufficiently powered sample, a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.2). This analysis, based on an F-test for a within-between interaction in a repeated-measures ANOVA, determined that 16 participants were required to detect a large effect size (d = 0.93) with a statistical power of 0.8 and a significance level of 0.05 for the TTP of the impact vertical peak14. To maintain the double-blinded nature of the study, both participants and the biomechanist responsible for processing the raw kinetic data were blinded to the specific type of FOs being tested in each trial. Participants were informed they would be testing different types of commercially available FOs designed for comfort and support, without being told the specific functional hypothesis or the differences between the placebo, arch-support, and double-density designs. This approach prevented participant expectations and data processing from being influenced by knowledge of the intervention. Although participants were blinded to the specific orthotic condition labels and study hypotheses, we acknowledge that they could still perceive differences in stiffness and shape between the orthoses. This study was a double-blinded study that conformed to the ethical guidelines of the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Mohaghegh Ardabili, Iran (IR.UMA.REC.1404.019) and was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) under the registration number IRCT20220129053865N2, first registered on 06/07/2025 (Trial ID: 83460, Membership number: 53865). We obtained the written informed consent from all participants before enrollment.

This study included 60 right-footed males aged 18–45 years from Ardabil city, Iran. All participants were right-leg dominant, as determined by their preferred kicking leg. The experimental group comprised 45 individuals with PF who had undergone unilateral right ACLR. These patients were further stratified into three distinct post-operative phases to examine the influence of surgical recovery time: Group A (6 ± 1 months post-surgery, n = 15), Group B (12 ± 1 months post-surgery, n = 15), and Group C (18 ± 1 months post-surgery, n = 15). A healthy control group (Group D, n = 15) of matched healthy males was also recruited. Foot orthoses were administered as immediate interventions during a single laboratory session at each post-operative stage. All data collection was performed at the Sports Biomechanics Laboratory of the University of Mohaghegh Ardabili. Participant eligibility was rigorously defined for the ACLR-PF groups, requiring: (1) PF posture objectively confirmed by a Foot Posture Index (FPI) score of 6–10 (as evaluated by an experienced podiatrist)17, (2) a navicular drop greater than 10 mm17,18, and (3) a rearfoot eversion angle exceeding 4º19,20. All participants were heel strikers, and no test-related injuries were reported during the study. As shown in Table 1, there were no significant baseline differences in demographic and anthropometric data between groups.

Participants also completed the Tegner Activity Scale (21) to assess current physical activity levels. All ACLR participants received hamstring tendon autografts, and a uniform anteromedial portal surgical technique was applied across all patients. Tegner scores range from 0 (indicating disability/bedridden) to 10 (indicating elite athletes) (Table 1). All ACLR procedures were performed by the same experienced orthopedic surgeon using a consistent surgical team and a standardized operative protocol. All surgical procedures utilized the anteromedial portal technique (AMT) to ensure precise, anatomic femoral tunnel placement independent of tibial alignment. This was particularly relevant given that the excessive tibial internal rotation characteristic of PF can compromise tunnel accuracy with non-independent drilling methods21. Unlike the traditional transtibial technique, in which the tibial tunnel constrains femoral tunnel placement, AMT allows independent, anatomic femoral tunnel drilling, thereby improving rotational stability and long-term functional outcomes22. The AMT technique is widely recognized for its ability to achieve anatomic tunnel placement and favorable long-term clinical outcomes following ACLR. Post-operative radiographic measurements confirmed appropriate coronal tibial and sagittal femoral tunnel angles23, which are critical parameters, as the altered lower-limb kinematics from PF can influence graft loading24. Femoral tunnel clock position describes the anatomic placement of the femoral tunnel during ACLR, quantified using a clock face reference. The reported values (e.g., 10.1 ± 0.3) represent the mean position and variability for each group. This anatomic range has been consistently reported in previous anatomical and biomechanical studies of femoral tunnel placement during ACLR25,26. The high p-value (p = 0.432) indicates no statistically significant difference in femoral tunnel placement between the ACLR-PF groups, confirming the consistency and comparability of the surgical technique across all patients27.

Assessment of running kinetics

All testing was conducted in a controlled laboratory environment, maintained at 22–24 °C and 40–50% relative humidity. Participants were instructed to run at a consistent speed of approximately 3.2 m/s along an 18-meter runway. Running cadence was guided by an electronic metronome that was individualized for each participant based on preliminary speed trials. Actual running speed was monitored using timing gates positioned 3 m before and 3 m after the force plate. Trials were accepted if the running speed was within ± 5% of the target speed; otherwise, the trial was discarded and repeated. This procedure ensured that all recorded footfalls were obtained under a consistent running speed. All participants wore the same standardized running shoe model during testing to eliminate footwear-related confounding effects28. A 15-minute familiarization period preceded data collection to ensure proper technique and consistency. Participants completed three practice trials before performing five test trials, with a 1-minute rest period between each practice and test trial. For inclusion in data analysis, speed variation within running trials had to remain within a 5% range, controlled and monitored using a metronome. Test-retest reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), which consistently exceeded 0.94 across groups and overall. Similar ICCs values have been previously reported for GRF measurements during running, indicating excellent reliability29.

The order of the four footwear conditions (arch-support, double-density, placebo orthotics, and control shoe [without FOs]) was randomized for each participant using a balanced Latin square design to mitigate order effects30.

A force plate (Bertec Corporation, Columbus, OH, USA) was used to collect GRFs at 1,000 Hz during running under the four footwear conditions: arch-support, double-density, placebo FOs, and a control shoe (Runfalcon 5 running shoes, adidas). Kinetic data were processed according to methods previously outlined by Jafarnezhadgero et al.31. GRF signals were low-pass filtered at 20 Hz using a 4th-order Butterworth filter with zero lag. Specific running events, such as heel strike and toe-off, were identified from force data using a 10 N threshold to detect the stance phase of the running cycle. The following dependent variables were extracted: vertical impact and active peaks, peak posterior and anterior forces, and lateral and medial peaks (23). GRF amplitudes were normalized to body weight (BW) and expressed as a percentage of BW (%BW). The TTP for the impact vertical peak was defined as the time interval from initial heel contact to the first peak in the vGRF during the stance phase. Vertical loading rates were computed as the average slope across 20%-80% of the vGRF at the point of interest32. The foot’s free moment (FM) was also computed, with FM amplitudes normalized by BW × height. All running variables were averaged across the three test trials33,34.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A mixed-model ANOVA with a 4 (FOs condition) × 4 (group) design was performed to investigate main and interaction effects on dependent variables. When significant main effects were identified, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni adjustments. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (ηp²) obtained from the ANOVA output, interpreted according to established guidelines: ηp² ≥0.01 for small effects, ≥ 0.06 for medium effects, and ≥ 0.14 for large effects35. The alpha level for all statistical tests was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were executed using SPSS version 26.

Results

Group effects

Significant main effects of group were observed for several kinetic and temporal variables (Tables 2 and 3; Figs. 2, 3 and 4): peak medial force (p = 0.036; η²=0.140), peak posterior force (p < 0.001; η²=0.470), peak anterior force (p = 0.004; η²=0.209), impact vertical peak (p = 0.001; η²=0.259), active vertical peak (p = 0.014; η²=0.171), TTP for impact vertical peak (p < 0.001; η²=0.290), negative FM (p = 0.027; η²=0.150), positive FM (p < 0.001; η²=0.398), loading rate (p < 0.001; η²=0.355), and stance time (p = 0.001; η²=0.252) .

Compared with the healthy group, the ACLR-PF (12-months) group demonstrated significantly greater peak medial force (p = 0.025, d = 1.05, ~ 4.07%BW). In contrast, ACLR-PF (6-months) group showed significantly lower peak posterior (p < 0.001, d = 1.51, ~ 7.11%BW), peak anterior (p = 0.002, d = 1.34, ~ 5.19%BW), active vertical peak (p = 0.043, d = 0.98, ~ 27.53), and longer stance time (p = 0.005, d = 1.25, ~ 67.35 ms). In addition, compared with the healthy group, the ACLR-PF (18-months) group demonstrated significantly greater impact vertical peak (p = 0.008, d = 1.19, ~ 28.22%BW) and vertical loading rate (p = 0.006, d = 1.22, ~ 7.90 BW/s) and ACLR-PF (12-months) group showed greater impact vertical peak (p = 0.014, d = 1.12, ~ 26.63%BW) and vertical loading rate (p = 0.026, d = 1.05, ~ 6.79 BW/s). In contrast, compared with the healthy group, the ACLR-PF (6-months) group demonstrated significantly longer TTP for the impact vertical peak (p = 0.002, d = 1.36, ~ 9.38 ms). In addition, compared with the healthy group, the ACLR-PF (6-months) (p < 0.001, d = 2.12, ~ 5.53%BW*height) and ACLR-PF (18-months) (p = 0.018, d = 1.10, ~ 2.86%BW*height) groups showed significantly greater peak positive FM.

FOs effects

Significant main FOs effects were observed for peak lateral force (p = 0.010; η²=0.189), peak medial force (p = 0.003; η²=0.224), peak posterior force (p = 0.014; η²=0.177), peak anterior force (p < 0.001; η²=0.370), peak negative FM (p = 0.014; η²=0.176), and peak positive FM (p = 0.002; η²=0.232). Compared with control condition, paired-wise comparison demonstrated significantly lower peak lateral force at placebo, double-density, and arch-support conditions (p = 0.009–0.020, d = 0.83–0.95, ~ 1.22–1.34%BW), peak medial force at placebo condition (p = 0.002, d = 1.34, ~ 1.70%BW), peak posterior force at placebo condition (p = 0.047, d = 0.69, ~ 2.22%BW), peak anterior force at placebo and arch-support conditions (p < 0.001, d = 1.25–1.72, ~ 2.60–3.55%BW), peak negative FM at placebo condition (p = 0.010, d = 0.81, ~ 0.77%BW), and peak positive FM at double-density condition (p = 0.001, d = 1.45, ~ 1.74%BW) (Tables 2 and 3; Figs. 3 and 4).

Interaction effects

Significant “group-by-foot orthoses” interactions were detected for peak posterior force (p = 0.027; η²=0.104), TTP for impact vertical peak (p = 0.008; η²=0.122), loading rate (p = 0.018; η²=0.110), and stance time (p = 0.001; η²=0.145) (Tables 2 and 3).

In the ACLR-PF (12-months) group (but not in other groups), paired-wise comparisons showed greater peak posterior force at the arch-support condition than at the placebo condition. Compared with the footwear condition, the ACLR-PF (18-months) group (but not the other groups) showed lower vertical loading rates at placebo and double-density conditions, and the ACLR-PF (12-months) group (but not the other groups) showed greater stance time at placebo and double-density conditions.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that short-term application of FOs can significantly modify abnormal GRFs in individuals with ACLR-PF. Our findings revealed significantly greater impact of vertical peak and vertical loading rates in both the ACLR-PF (12-months) and ACLR-PF (18-months) groups compared to the healthy group. This is concerning as it suggests a biomechanical adaptation that manifests in the later stages of recovery. The clinical significance of these findings is substantial, given that elevated impact vertical peak and vertical loading rate are recognized risk factors for the development of post-traumatic osteoarthritis (OA)36. This risk is further contextualized by meta-analytic evidence from Culvenor et al.37, who synthesized data from 17 studies. Their work revealed a median prevalence of radiographic patellofemoral joint OA at 36% within 2–15 years post-ACLR. Importantly, their analysis showed that isolated patellofemoral joint OA (~ 26%) is more prevalent than isolated tibiofemoral joint OA (~ 15%), establishing the patellofemoral joint as a primary site for post-ACLR degeneration. This result underscores that the abnormal loading patterns observed disproportionately affect the patellofemoral joint, a compartment with documented high vulnerability to long-term structural decline. Consequently, this highlights the patellofemoral joint as a critical target for prophylactic rehabilitation strategies. Because the present study employed a cross‑sectional design with separate groups at 6-, 12-, and 18-months post‑operative, these findings should be interpreted as differences between groups at distinct post-operative stages rather than as longitudinal progression within the same individuals.

Conversely, our results indicated a significantly longer TTP for the impact vertical peak in the ACLR-PF (6-months) group compared to the healthy group. Mafi and colleagues33 reported that a prolonged TTP for the vGRF is associated with a reduced injury rate, supporting the notion that this delayed loading may reflect a protective loading pattern for the recovering knee. Thus, the prolonged TTP vertical force observed at 6-months may represent a strategy that reduces loading on the recovering knee14.

Furthermore, significantly greater peak positive FM was observed in both the ACLR-PF (6-months) and ACLR-PF (18-months) groups compared to the healthy group. While a previous study found individuals with PF exhibited lower peak negative FM, interpreted as a potential compensatory mechanism to reduce torsional stress at the knee and hip joints during running14, the ACLR-PF groups here displayed an elevation in peak positive FM. Since FM reflects vertical torque around the center of pressure and is associated with lower-limb torsional stress and tibial stress fractures in runners38,39, this increase in ACLR patients indicates a loading pattern that could heighten mechanical stress on the knee. The altered rotational forces may increase stress on the knee joint, placing additional demands on the healing graft and other passive stabilizers, with implications for long-term joint health and reinjury risk.

Our findings indicated a significantly lower peak positive FM in the double-density FO condition than in the control footwear condition. This reduction suggests that specific orthotic designs can differentially influence lower-limb rotation. The double-density insole may have influenced lower-limb loading through altered plantar sensory feedback, which may be associated with reduced internal tibial rotation40. In our study, the double-density orthoses significantly reduced positive FM compared with the control shoe and other orthotic conditions. A lower positive FM may reflect reduced transverse plane torque at the foot–ground interface, which has been hypothesized to decrease rotational loading at the knee. However, these findings reflect immediate changes during a single laboratory running session, and it is unknown whether such acute reductions in FM translate into sustained alterations in loading patterns or a reduced risk of reinjury or degenerative changes. Therefore, the clinical implications of these FM modifications should be interpreted with caution, and future longitudinal studies are required to determine their long‑term significance. Additionally, regarding interaction effects, the ACLR-PF (12-months) group (but not the other groups) demonstrated a greater peak posterior force with the arch-support FO than in the placebo condition. This result highlights the nuanced, group-specific responses to different orthotic interventions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample included only males with ACLR-PF, which limits the generalizability of our findings to females or individuals without PF. Second, the design was cross-sectional, with separate groups tested at 6-, 12-, and 18-months after surgery; therefore, we cannot make conclusions about long-term recovery patterns within the same individuals. Third, the assessment focused solely on immediate biomechanical responses during one laboratory running session. We did not collect joint kinematics or electromyography data, so the underlying movement patterns and neuromuscular strategies associated with the observed changes in GRFs remain speculative. Finally, the long-term clinical importance of these short-term changes in GRF and FM remains unknown; future long-term intervention studies are needed to determine whether orthotic-induced changes in GRF lead to fewer reinjuries, better function, or a lower risk of degenerative joint issues. Moreover, despite our blinding procedures, participants may have been able to distinguish between orthotic conditions based on perceived differences in stiffness and contour, which could have affected their running behavior.

Conclusions

In summary, double-density and arch-support FOs acutely modified selected maladaptive GRFs variables during running in males with ACLR-PF at different post-operative stages. In particular, double-density orthoses reduced positive FM and altered vertical and anterior–posterior loading patterns in some ACLR‑PF groups. These findings suggest that FOs can be used to achieve immediate biomechanical adjustments; however, their long‑term effects on clinical outcomes remain unclear. Given the heterogeneity in responses across post-operative stages and between individuals, orthotic prescription after ACLR should be carefully individualized rather than based on a uniform approach.
