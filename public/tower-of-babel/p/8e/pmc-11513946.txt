Acceleration is the key to drag reduction in turbulent flow
Authors: Liuyang Ding, Lena F. Sabidussi, Brian C. Holloway, Marcus Hultmark, Alexander J. Smits
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceReducing friction drag is critical to improving the performance of transportation and energy systems such as airplanes, ships, and wind turbines. Spanwise oscillation of surface elements has been shown to be one of the most promising approaches, yielding benefits up to 50%. Finding the optimal drag reduction, however, has been thwarted by the broad parameter space defining the surface movement. Our experimental strategy has now identified the acceleration of the spanwise motion to be the key parameter to scale the drag reduction. This greatly reduces the complexity of the phenomenon and opens potential avenues for reducing energy costs in large engineering systems.

In conditions encountered by, for example, airplanes, ships, wind turbines and pipelines, turbulence generates skin-friction drag that constrains both speed and efficiency. Even modest reductions in drag could immediately improve performance enough to yield significant economic and environmental benefits, such as improvements to the fuel economy of large vehicles and the handling capacity of industrial pipelines. One of the most promising and most explored candidates for significant drag reduction is spanwise oscillation of surface elements, with drag reduction up to 50% possible under some circumstances (1, 2).

Here, we consider the case where the entire surface oscillates purely in time t with a velocity given by[1]ws(t)=Asin(2πt/T),

in which A is the amplitude of the spanwise velocity, and T is the period of the oscillation. We achieve this motion in a pipe flow by oscillating the pipe in the azimuthal direction. The surface motion induces a spanwise velocity component in the fluid very near the surface, well described by a Stokes layer (3), and this momentum injection interferes with the turbulence production, specifically the streak-like structures found close to the surface, in such a way as to reduce drag.

To relate our laboratory study to a full-scale application, we need to maintain dynamic similarity. That is, we need to be able to scale test result to the full scale, and the crux of the current paper is to demonstrate that this scaling problem has an underlying simplicity which has not been appreciated before.

Consider that the local drag per unit area, averaged across the surface or “wall,” τw, has a functional dependence g1 given by[2]τw=g1(U¯,R,ρ,ν,T,A),

where U¯ is the bulk velocity, R is the radius of the pipe, and ρ and ν are the fluid density and kinematic viscosity, respectively. The drag reduction, DR, is defined as the fractional decrease in τw, and dimensional analysis then gives[3]DR=g2(A+,T+,Reτ),

where uτ0=τw0/ρ, and Reτ=Ruτ0/ν is the Reynolds number, which for full-scale applications is typically very large, that is, Reτ≫103. The subscript “0” denotes the quantities measured in the nonactuated case, that is, over the stationary surface. The nondimensional velocity A+=A/uτ0, and the nondimensional period T+=Tuτ02/ν.

Flows with spanwise oscillating surfaces have been studied extensively in channel flows at low Reynolds numbers (Reτ≤2,000) using Direct Numerical Simulations (DNS) (see, for example, refs. 4–9). In this respect, Yao et al. (9) showed that at all Reynolds numbers investigated (Reτ<2,000), the drag reduction increased with T+ up to a peak value and then gradually decreased at higher values of T+. As the Reynolds number increased from 200 to 2,000, the peak DR decreased from a maximum of about 35% to about 23%, and the optimal value of T+ shifted from 100 to 80. The scaling with A+ was not investigated, as all these computations were performed at a fixed value of A+=12.

A recent computation for pipe flow with azimuthal oscillation reported 22.9% DR at Reτ=720 (A+=10, T+=100) (10), and as far as the authors know there is only one previous experiment reported in the literature for this configuration, that by Choi and Graham (11). The drag reduction was measured at Reτ= 649 and 995 over a relatively wide range of values A+ and T+, and it displayed a very similar behavior to that found using DNS in a channel at comparable Reynolds numbers. That is, the maximum DR reached approximately 25% at T+≈100, maintaining this value to T+≈50, the lowest value explored in these experiments. The authors concluded, without much justification, that the results scaled on T+.

All of these studies were for Reτ≤2,000. To examine the behavior at higher Reynolds numbers and to investigate more fully the scaling on A+ and T+, we cover Reynolds numbers from approximately 800 to 6,500, and use a strategy of controlling the water temperature to vary A+, T+ and Reτ independently. As seen in Fig. 1, neither A+ nor T+ collapse our data. Although some of our results in these scalings agree with the data of Choi and Graham (11), other results do not. This is particularly true for the dependence on T+.

Is There a Better Parameter Choice to Characterize the Drag Reduction?.

Early investigations on 3D turbulent boundary layers provide some insights. Bradshaw and Pontikos (12) studied an initially 2D turbulent boundary layer (TBL) as it passed through a section with curved lateral walls so that a transverse shear component dW/dy was induced in the flow in addition to the 2D mean shear dU/dy. Their measurement showed that, upon entering the curved section, both the turbulent kinetic energy and the turbulent shear stress (−uv¯) were suppressed, and accordingly a lower skin friction was observed. A few later studies, including DNS of channel flow with a suddenly imposed transverse pressure gradient (13) and experiments on a 2D TBL approaching a wedge (14), reported similar suppression of turbulence following the onset of three-dimensionality. This is a transient response, in that the flow is expected to eventually adjust to the newly established mean shear direction given by (dU/dy,dW/dy) (or ∂/∂y, where it applies) and that the skin friction would recover its canonical 2D value. This behavior leads to a hypothesis that suppression of turbulence and hence reduced skin friction are associated with the rate of change of the transverse shear dW/dy, which is high only during the transitional phase. Bradshaw and Pontikos (12) suggest that the rate of change of dW/dy distorts the “eddy structure” so that large eddies become less efficient in extracting energy from the mean shear. Indeed, the observation of transient drag reduction inspired Jung et al. (15), for the first time, to employ wall oscillation to achieve a sustained transient state, which was a great success for drag reduction and has since spurred numerous research efforts. Also worth noting is that Agostini et al. (16) in a DNS study of channel flow at Reτ=1,000 showed that the phase-wise decrease of skin friction occurred when the rate of change of ∂W/∂y is high.

For wall oscillation, the rate of change of ∂W/∂y in the Stokes layer scales with A+/T+, as can be shown analytically from the Stokes solution (4). Therefore, we put forward the nondimensional acceleration a+=A+/T+=Aν/(Tuτ03) as a candidate scaling parameter. When we plot our DR data in terms of a+, as in Fig. 2A for T+≥100, we immediately see a convincing collapse, and our data now also agree well with that of Coxe et al. (10) and Choi and Graham (11). This is in marked contrast to previous scalings based on A+ or T+, or on other parameters such as the influence range of the Stokes layer (4, 17, 18). Hence, for T+≥100, the data indicate that[4]DR=g(a+).

Crucially, compared to the initial problem described by Eq. 3, this result represents a significant reduction in complexity.

The range T+≥100 is important from a practical point of view because past channel flow simulations have suggested that the maximum DR is achieved at a value of T+≈100, with reported values ranging from about 80 to 130 (4, 7, 9). In addition, the power required to move the fluid scales approximately as (T)−2.5 (3), and so there are great benefits to operating at low frequencies (that is, T+≥100). Such a low-frequency, large-period actuation appears to be the only pathway to energy-efficient drag reduction at high Reynolds number (19, 20). The collapse of the data with a+ demonstrated here includes, therefore, the most important data in terms of DR. In addition, g(a+) serves as a powerful design tool for achieving maximum levels of DR. In terms of the oscillation frequency f+ and amplitude d+, a+∝(f+)2d+, and (f+)2 and d+ have equivalent effects on achieving maximum DR.

What Happens When T+ < 100?

In Fig. 2B we plot the same data as in Fig. 2A for all values of T+. For T+<100, our data and those of Choi and Graham (11) diverge from a pure a+ scaling and exhibit different levels of maximum DR. The divergence begins at T+≈100, where, for our data (at Reτ≈808) a+≈0.3, corresponding to A+≈30, while for Choi and Graham’s data it occurs at a+≈0.15, corresponding to A+≈15. That is, the departure for pure a+ scaling near the maximum DR is due to a dependence on A+ (since T+ is fixed at about 100). The A+ dependence of maximum DR was also found in previous pipe and channel DNS, although these were performed at Reτ≤400 (4, 17).

What About Channel Flows?

Several channel flows with spanwise wall oscillation have been investigated (5, 7, 9, 21, 22). These studies were all performed using DNS, and consequently, they were limited to a maximum Reynolds number of 2,000 (9). We present all the available data for T+≥100 in Fig. 3A. Data for Reτ⪅400 are excluded since it is well known that they exhibit low Reynolds number effects (23). All these computations were performed with A+=12, so that the collapse with a+ is not surprising. Nevertheless, the data agree well with the pipe results, although the channel data rise a little quicker with increasing a+.

Comparing this graph with the pipe flow results shown in Fig. 2B, we see that the channel flow DR also reaches a maximum at T+≈100 and then decreases at higher values of T+. There is a noticeable Reynolds number dependence close to T+=100, which is most evident in the results obtained by Yao et al. (9). However, because all the channel flow data were obtained at a fixed value of A+ we cannot discern the A+ dependence.

What Happens at High Reynolds Number?

Although our data do not manifest a Reynolds number dependence (SI Appendix, Text and Fig. S2), previous studies have suggested otherwise. For example, the DNS channel flow results (6–9) showed that the maximum DR decreased with increasing Reτ, approximately as Reτ−0.2. Hence, the maximum DR is expected to decrease only slowly at high Reynolds numbers. In addition, the channel flow data of Yao et al. (9) suggests that the dependence on Reτ only affects DR over a range of T+ near T+=100, and that this range shrinks as Reτ increases. In our measurements, T+>400 for all data with Reτ>2,000, a range where we would expect only a weak dependence Reτ. This observation seems to reconcile the seemingly contradicting observations regarding Reynolds number dependence of our data compared to that from channel flow DNS.

In conclusion, we have shown that the nondimensional wall acceleration a+ is the correct scaling parameter for the drag reduction in pipe and channel flows with spanwise wall oscillation where T+≥100. This range of T+ includes the point where the maximum drag reduction is achieved and covers the most practical energy-efficient pathway for drag reduction at high Reynolds numbers. Since the acceleration is the key parameter, it may well be advantageous to use nonsinusoidal waveforms where the cycle-integrated acceleration can be maximized. As noted in refs. 5, 16, and 24, the attenuation of the near-wall streaks only occurs during certain “active” phases of the wall-oscillation cycle when the orientation of (∂W/∂y) changes rapidly. Therefore, nonsinusoidal waveforms might achieve drag reduction even more efficiently than sinusoidal waveforms.

Materials and Methods

The experiments were conducted in a recirculating water pipe facility with an inner diameter (2R=D) of 38.1 mm. Transverse momentum injection was implemented by oscillating the pipe around its longitudinal axis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The oscillating pipe section is 1.21 m long (≈32D) connected to a crank-slider mechanism driven by a motor via a T-slot that allows for changes in the oscillation amplitude. The slider translates linear motion to azimuthal oscillation of the pipe via a timing belt. The length ratio of the crank to the rod connecting the crank to the slider is less than 0.05, so that the oscillation closely approximates a sinusoidal motion. Oscillation azimuthal amplitudes (d) up to 12.8 mm and oscillation frequencies (f=1/T) up to 20 Hz were tested. The test section was placed 100D downstream of the inlet to ensure fully developed flow at the entrance to the test section. Experiments were performed at bulk velocities ranging from 0.4 to 4.2 m/s, and the water temperature was adjusted from ambient values up to 57 °C. The Reynolds number was varied from approximately 800 to 7,000. SI Appendix, Table S1 summarizes our various test parameters, together with those from the oscillating pipe experiment by Choi and Graham (11) and the DNS study by Coxe et al. (10).

To measure drag reduction, the friction factor was found by measuring the time-averaged pressure drop using a pair of pressure taps located 135 mm upstream and downstream of the oscillating pipe section. A differential pressure sensor, Validyne DP103, was used and was regularly calibrated throughout our measurement campaign to ensure accuracy within 1 to 2% compared to the friction factor correlation reported by McKeon et al. (25). Pressure difference data were adjusted by subtracting the pressure drop in the upstream and downstream stationary sections, assuming drag alteration occurred entirely within the oscillating section. DR was then computed as the percentage difference of the friction factor, using the results from McKeon et al. (25) for the nonoscillating case at matching values of Reτ. For each combination of [A+,T+,Reτ], we acquired a total of 2,000 to 3,000 samples, in one or two trials, at a sampling rate of about 5 Hz, in a nonphase-locked way with respect to the pipe oscillation. As the sampling frequency (5 Hz) is close to the minimum pipe oscillation frequency (4 Hz), our data covered a sufficient number of oscillation periods (≈2,000 to 3,000).

We attribute the error in the measured mean pressure drop to two categories of contributions. First, the differential pressure sensor (Validyne DP103) introduced a systematic bias, which was characterized and monitored throughout our measurement campaign by comparing the pressure drop in the flow of the nonoscillating pipe to the friction factor correlation reported in ref. 25. Recalibration with a digital manometer (Sper Scientific 840080) or a dual micrometer fluid-filled manometer (Hass Instrument) was conducted whenever necessary to ensure that this bias error was within 1 to 2% of the total mean pressure drop.

The second category encapsulates the mechanisms that cause fluctuations in pressure readings, which, in turn, contribute to an error in the mean pressure difference due to statistical convergence. The fluctuations arise from a combination of phase-dependent variations, turbulent fluctuations, electrical noise in the data acquisition system, and vibration of the test section table. Given that our instrumentation did not allow the discrimination of different sources of fluctuations, we used a lumped estimate, Δprms/Ns, to quantify the convergence error. Here, Δprms is the root-mean-squared pressure difference, and Ns=2,000to3,000 is the total number of pressure difference readings. The result is plotted against T+ in its dimensional form (in Pascals) in SI Appendix, Fig. S3A and as a fraction of the total mean pressure drop, Δp¯, in SI Appendix, Fig. S3B. It is clear that Δprms/(Δp¯Ns) is almost always below 0.2%, a small fraction of the sensor bias error. The amplification seen in SI Appendix, Fig. S3B at smaller T+ is due to reduced Δp¯ at smaller Reτ.

We therefore estimate an uncertainty of ±2% for all of our DR data (DR± 2%, not 2% of measured DR). This estimate is corroborated by the collapse of DR with a+ within the uncertainty limits of ±2% (Fig. 2).
