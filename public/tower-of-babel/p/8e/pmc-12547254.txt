Does shoe cushioning space affect patellofemoral pain risk? A study based on the integrated finite element model
Authors: Qincheng Ge, Huiyu Zhou, Datao Xu, Zanni Zhang, Xiuye Qu, Yi Yuan, Yaodong Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryVariations in toe box space (TBS) of shoes during stop-jumping landings may increase knee injury risk, especially patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS). This study aimed to investigate the potential influence of TBS on PFPS during stop-jumping landings. Twenty-eight male basketball athletes were recruited, wearing tight-fitting (TF) and loose-fitting (LF) shoes. Three-dimensional patellar displacement was measured using a high-speed dual fluoroscopic imaging system (DFIS). An integrated foot-ankle-shoe-knee finite element model was used to estimate internal knee stresses. The TF group demonstrated a restricted ankle dorsiflexion range of motion (p < 0.001) and increased knee adduction angle and moment (p < 0.001). Earlier activation of vastus lateralis (VL) relative to vastus medialis (VM) in the TF group contributed to greater lateral patellar displacement and higher Von-Mises stresses in the lateral patella. TF shoes may cause abnormal patellar tracking, elevating the likelihood of PFPS. This study may offer new insights into basketball footwear selection and theoretical support for reducing the risk of PFPS.Subject areas: sports medicine, Biological sciences, Bioengineering

Introduction

Basketball involves numerous high-intensity movements, including stop-jumping, change of direction, landing, and lateral cutting, all of which impose significant stress on the lower extremity and are highly prone to causing sports injuries. Among them, the stop-jumping maneuver is widely used in scenarios such as transition play, driving layups, and shot-blocking and is generally regarded as a high-risk movement. This task typically consists of an approach run, deceleration, horizontal landing, take-off, and vertical landing. Of these, the final landing phase is the most critical part, as it involves impact absorption and body posture control.1 Improper landing mechanics, especially after explosive stop-jump actions, are closely associated with common lower limb injuries such as anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears, patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS), and ankle sprains.2,3

PFPS is a prevalent knee disorder characterized by chronic anterior or peripatellar pain that typically exacerbates during knee flexion activities (e.g., stair climbing, squatting, running, jumping, or standing up after prolonged sitting).4 The global prevalence of PFPS is estimated to range from 15% to 45%.5 It predominantly affects adolescents, young athletes, and physically active individuals. Notably, females demonstrate a greater susceptibility to this condition.6 The etiology of PFPS is multifactorial, and there is currently no unified explanatory theory. Most studies suggest that its pathogenesis is closely related to the abnormal movement of the patella within the femoral trochlear groove (i.e., patellar tracking disorder). This abnormality typically results from an imbalance in quadriceps strength, with key contributing factors including weakness of the vastus medialis (VM), relative overactivity of the vastus lateralis (VL), gluteus medius (GM) weakness, and lower limb misalignment (such as femoral internal rotation or tibial valgus).7 As the medial stabilizer of the patella, VM plays a crucial role in maintaining its dynamic stability. When the activation patterns of the VM and VL are imbalanced, it may lead to the lateral displacement of the patella, thereby increasing pressure on the patellofemoral cartilage. When dysfunctional, the gluteus medius, responsible for controlling femoral adduction and internal rotation, may cause compensatory tibial abduction, further alter knee joint mechanics, and disrupt normal patellar tracking.8

While much of the existing literature primarily focuses on proximal factors contributing to PFPS, such as quadriceps muscle imbalances, recent studies suggest that limited ankle dorsiflexion range of motion (DF-ROM) may contribute to patellofemoral joint dysfunction. Hassan et al. conducted a case-control study comparing 70 patients with PFPS with 70 asymptomatic individuals and found that the PFPS group exhibited significantly reduced weight-bearing ankle DF-ROM when measured with the knee flexed.9 In contrast, Coelho et al. found no significant differences in knee pain or dynamic valgus among patients with PFPS with varying degrees of restriction in ankle DF-ROM.10 In conclusion, while restricted ankle DF-ROM may be a contributing factor to PFPS, its precise role remains unclear, and further research is needed to clarify this relationship.

In basketball, athletes exhibit varying preferences regarding the TBS of basketball shoes. Some prefer tight-fitting (TF) footwear, while others opt for loose-fitting (LF) designs. Currently, it remains unclear whether variations in TBS influence the lower extremity injury risk, particularly in relation to PFPS. A tight toe box may restrict the natural splaying of the toes, leading to compression and altered foot alignment, which could subsequently affect ankle joint control and the mechanical transmission along the kinetic chain. This may ultimately disrupt knee joint kinematics and muscle coordination, resulting in compensatory movement patterns. Conversely, a loose toe box may compromise forefoot stability and reduce movement control, which may also elevate patellofemoral stress. Therefore, whether the toe box is tight or loose, both conditions may influence foot function and the associated movement mechanics along the kinetic chain, potentially contributing to the onset or exacerbation of PFPS. However, this hypothesis warrants further empirical investigation.

To further investigate how changes in TBS affect foot structure and subsequently influence patellofemoral joint function through the kinetic chain, finite element analysis (FEA) serves as an advanced modeling technique to address this research question effectively.11 FEA is a computational method based on anatomical structure and material properties, capable of simulating stress distribution, joint contact forces, and deformation within the knee joint under complex boundary conditions, thereby providing a high-precision quantitative assessment. The use of high-resolution medical imaging data (e.g., CT and MRI), particularly Helium-Free MRI, allows for the efficient and non-invasive construction of personalized finite element models (FEMs). Numerous studies have employed FEA as a research tool. For example, some have simulated stress distribution at the suture site after meniscal repair,12 while others have used FEM of unicompartmental knee arthroplasty to assess the impact of insert thickness on patellofemoral joint stress.13 In addition, several studies have developed integrated FEMs. For example, Zhou et al. established a foot-sole FEM to investigate the effects of different contact angles on the foot bones.14 Xu et al. constructed a foot-ankle-knee FEM to explore how different ankle plantarflexion angles affect the risk of knee joint injuries.15 However, no existing study has incorporated the shoe into a foot-ankle-knee FEM. Therefore, this study proposes a novel foot-ankle-shoe-knee FEM based on previous research, aiming to reveal how foot biomechanics under realistic shod conditions may influence knee joint function through the kinetic chain.

This study aimed to establish an integrated foot-ankle-shoe-knee FEM to systematically investigate the impact of different basketball shoe TBSs on the potential pathogenesis of PFPS during stop-jumping landing maneuvers. The study hypothesized that the TF group may exhibit restricted ankle DF-ROM and increased knee adduction moment. This biomechanical alteration could cause delayed activation of the VM relative to the VL, ultimately leading to the increased lateral displacement of the patella and elevated lateral stress concentration, consequently increasing PFPS risk.

Results

Ankle and knee joint angles and moments

Figure 1 represents the SPM results and peak values of ankle and knee joint angles and moments. As illustrated in Figure 1A, wearing tight-fitting (TF) and loose-fitting (LF) basketball shoes led to significant differences in ankle and knee joint angles and moments. Specifically, in the sagittal plane, the TF group exhibited significantly smaller ankle plantarflexion and dorsiflexion angles (0–7% gait cycle, p = 0.045; 24–100%, p < 0.001), and plantarflexion moments (0–90%, p < 0.001), as well as significantly reduced knee extension moments (3–100%, p < 0.001) but increased flexion angles (0–100%, p < 0.001). In the frontal plane, the TF group showed significantly greater ankle inversion angles (43–100%, p < 0.001) and inversion moments (0–48%, p < 0.001; 79–100%, p = 0.014), but smaller eversion angles (0–13%, p = 0.025). For the knee joint, the TF group demonstrated significantly increased abduction angles (18–100%, p < 0.001) and adduction moments (9–100%, p < 0.001). As shown in Figure 1B, comparisons of peak joint angles and moments between the two groups further revealed that, except for the peak knee abduction angle and moment, all other biomechanical parameters differed significantly between them.

Muscle activation and patellar displacement

Figure 2 presents the peak values and SPM results of the VL, VM, GMAX, and GMED alongside patellar displacement. As shown in Figure 2A, TF and LF groups demonstrated significant differences in muscle activation, including VL, VM, GMAX, and GMED. For the thigh muscles, compared with the LF group, the TF group showed greater VL muscle activation at 18–34% (p = 0.002) but smaller activation at 35–71% (p < 0.001). For the VM muscle, TF group demonstrated smaller activation at 18–41% and 77–100% (p < 0.001; p < 0.001), while showing greater activation at 46–65% (p < 0.001). Regarding the gluteal muscles, compared with the LF group, the TF group exhibited greater GMAX muscle activation at 7–42% (p < 0.001) and smaller activation at 49–60% (p = 0.006). Furthermore, the TF group showed smaller GMED muscle activation at 31–43% and 82–97% (p = 0.011; p = 0.004), while demonstrating greater activation at 48–61% (p = 0.009). The activation timing analysis revealed that when wearing TF, the VL activated earlier and reached peak activation before the VM, whereas there was no notable difference in activation sequence between them when wearing LF. Moreover, significant differences were observed between TF and LF groups in both maximum muscle activation of VL, VM, GMAX, and GMED (all p < 0.05), as well as in minimum activation for all muscles except VM and GMED (Figure 2B).

As illustrated in Figure 2C, the patellar displacements in the anterior-posterior, proximal-distal, and medial-lateral directions were analyzed. The TF group exhibited significantly greater distal displacement than the LF group during 30–90% of the landing phase. Additionally, lateral displacement was significantly larger in the TF group compared to the LF group throughout 10–100% of the landing phase. However, there were no significant differences in the anterior-posterior direction.

Finite element analysis results

Figures 3A–3H illustrate the distribution and peak values, along with the percentage variations, of von Mises stress in the cartilage, ligaments, and bones of the TF and LF groups during the landing phase of the stop-jump task. For the ACL, the TF group exhibited higher average (13.896 MPa) and peak (27.519 MPa) stresses compared to the LF group (11.415 MPa and 24.125 MPa, respectively), with stress concentrations primarily in the mid-to-lower portion of the ACL. In the PCL, the TF group also showed greater average (11.546 MPa) and peak (23.685 MPa) stresses than the LF group (10.627 MPa and 21.754 MPa), with stress mainly localized at the upper and lower ends. For the MCL, the TF group displayed higher average (4.127 MPa) and peak (8.754 MPa) stresses than the LF group (3.346 MPa and 7.685 MPa), with stress concentrated in the mid-to-upper region. The LCL demonstrated elevated stress levels in the TF group (average: 7.035 MPa, peak: 12.793 MPa) compared to the LF group (average: 5.413 MPa, peak: 10.246 MPa), with stress distributed across the mid-to-upper and mid-to-lower regions. In the Patella, the TF group had a higher average (5.230 MPa) and peak (9.508 MPa) stresses than the LF group (3.407 MPa and 5.997 MPa). Stress was primarily concentrated at the lower region and slightly toward the lateral side. In the PL, the TF group exhibited greater average (14.162 MPa) and peak (27.846 MPa) stresses than the LF group (10.665 MPa and 21.159 MPa). Stress was primarily concentrated in the lower region. In the MM, the TF group showed greater average (10.553 MPa) and peak (20.624 MPa) stresses than the LF group (8.124 MPa and 16.259 MPa), with stress mainly distributed at the upper and lower ends. For the LM, the TF group displayed higher average (9.953 MPa) and peak (19.624 MPa) stresses than the LF group (8.224 MPa and 15.259 MPa), with stress concentrated in the central region.

The patella showed the most substantial decrease, decreasing by 36.92%, followed by the PC with a 24.02% reduction. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to systematically investigate the influence of different basketball shoe TBSs on the pathogenesis of PFPS during stop-jumping landing maneuvers based on an integrated foot-ankle-shoe-knee FEM. We hypothesized that, compared to the LF group, the TF group may exhibit reduced ankle DF-ROM and increased knee adduction moment. During stop-jumping landings, the VL in the TF group may activate earlier and reach peak activation sooner. Furthermore, this group may show significantly greater lateral patellar displacement and elevated lateral stress. The results of our study support our original hypothesis.

Restricted ankle DF-ROM may influence the development and progression of PFPS by altering knee joint kinematics. This study compared differences in sagittal plane angles and moments of the ankle and knee joints between the TF and LF groups. The results demonstrated significantly smaller peak plantarflexion and dorsiflexion angles in the TF group, indicating restricted ankle DF-ROM that may predispose this group to PFPS development. These findings align with Hassan et al.’s research, which compared ankle DF-ROM between patients with PFPS and asymptomatic individuals during knee flexion and suggested that DF-ROM restriction exacerbated dynamic knee valgus, potentially serving as an important pathogenic factor for PFPS.9 Furthermore, multiple studies have identified a significant association between gastrocnemius tightness-induced ankle dorsiflexion limitation and PFPS.16 However, other research has shown that restricted ankle DF-ROM does not significantly affect dynamic knee valgus or knee pain, a phenomenon that may relate to the inherent characteristics of closed kinetic chain tasks such as step-down tests.10

According to our results, the TF group demonstrated significantly greater peak knee flexion angles as well as higher peak valgus angles and moments during stop-jumping landing maneuvers. This supports the notion that limited ankle DF-ROM may exacerbate dynamic knee valgus. Yang et al.’s research on recreational runners showed that patients with PFPS exhibited greater knee valgus angles during pain-free running, which is highly consistent with our findings.17 However, their study did not detect significant differences in valgus moments, whereas our results observed greater valgus moments. This disparity might be explained by variations in impact loading between the movements, as the greater impact forces generated during stop-jumping movements likely amplified the effect of restricted ankle dorsiflexion on the knee joint, resulting in more pronounced differences in valgus moments. Furthermore, Willson and Davis reported increased knee valgus angles in patients with PFPS during progressive loading tasks.18 This finding was further supported by Holden et al., who found that among adolescent athletes, those with greater knee valgus angles had a 2.3-fold increased risk of developing PFPS.19

PFPS is a chronic sports-related injury with a multifactorial etiology. Current consensus suggests that its pathogenesis is closely associated with abnormal patellar tracking.8 In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the role of proximal hip muscles in the lower extremity kinetic chain. As a primary hip abductor, the insufficient activation of the GMED may lead to femoral internal rotation and hip adduction, thereby exacerbating knee valgus and increasing patellofemoral joint stress, which may induce or aggravate PFPS. Syed et al. found that patients with PFPS exhibited significantly lower GMED activation levels than healthy controls during weight-bearing tasks such as squatting.4 Willson further demonstrated delayed GMED activation in patients with PFPS during running and stair negotiation.20 Glaviano and Saliba reported that during single-leg squats, PFPS individuals showed significantly reduced surface electromyography (sEMG) signal amplitudes in both GMED and GMAX compared to controls.21 Nascimento et al. observed similar findings during functional single-leg tasks and noted that the insufficient activation of GMED and GMAX was significantly correlated with increased knee valgus angles and moments, suggesting that impaired proximal control may be a key factor in lower extremity kinetic chain dysfunction.22 Our findings partially supported these observations, demonstrating significantly decreased peak activation of GMED and GMAX in the TF group with restricted ankle DF-ROM. However, no delayed GMED activation was observed in our study, which may be attributed to task-specific demands. Compared to running or stair negotiation, the stop-jumping task may require more rapid GMED activation, potentially masking differences in onset timing.

On the other hand, in addition to proximal hip muscle dysfunction, the imbalance in medial-lateral quadriceps recruitment is also a critical feature of PFPS. Chen et al. demonstrated that patients with PFPS exhibited earlier activation onset of the VL compared to the VM during several movements, along with significantly reduced percentage of maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) in the GMED during closed-chain movements relative to healthy controls.23 Kim et al. reported through muscle-tightness assessment that patients with PFPS showed significantly elevated tightness in both VL and VM on the affected side, which may further compromise patellar stability and exacerbate patellofemoral joint loading.24 This study corroborated and extended these findings that in the TF group, VL demonstrated earlier activation onset compared to VM and significantly higher peak amplitude compared to the LF group, whereas VM exhibited markedly reduced peak activation. This recruitment pattern not only reflected dynamic imbalance between medial and lateral quadriceps but also highlighted a combined influence of proximal hip muscle dysfunction and distal ankle dorsiflexion limitation. Collectively, muscular abnormalities in PFPS manifested both temporal and intensity disparities between VM and VL recruitment and insufficient activation of GMED/GMAX. These factors acted synergistically to disrupt the kinetic chain from proximal to distal segments.

DFIS is a non-invasive skeletal motion measurement tool that integrates real-time X-ray imaging, medical imaging, and the alignment of three-dimensional models with two-dimensional models. It enables submillimeter accuracy capture of 6° of freedom (6DOF) skeletal motion during dynamic activities, effectively avoiding soft tissue artifact interference commonly associated with traditional skin marker methods. This technique employs dual-plane X-ray emitters coupled with image intensifiers to synchronously acquire skeleton motion images, which are then dynamically registered with 3D bone models reconstructed from CT scans to achieve the precise quantification of intra-articular motion. Although DFIS has been widely applied in studies of foot, ankle, and overall knee joint kinematics,25 no prior research has systematically quantified three-dimensional patellar displacement during dynamic tasks. This study represented the first application of DFIS to measure the spatial trajectory of the patella during the landing phase of a stop-jumping task. We measured the anterior-posterior, medial-lateral, and proximal-distal displacements of the patella and found that the TF group exhibited significantly greater lateral displacement than the LF group throughout the landing phase. This abnormal outward shift of the patella may increase contact stress between the patella and femoral trochlea, potentially elevating the risk of PFPS. In contrast to previous studies focused on foot arch deformation and ankle joint stability,26 our study extended the application of DFIS to the anterior knee by focusing on the patella, providing a novel perspective for detecting patellar displacements.

The FEA results provided further insight into the potential mechanism of PFPS in the stop-jumping landing maneuvers. This study demonstrated that the TF group exhibited significant lateral patellar displacement and stress concentration, which was highly consistent with previous research. Ho et al. reported that patients with PFPS exhibited significantly increased lateral patellar stress using FEA, which was also associated with reduced cartilage thickness.27 Similarly, Lee et al.’s in vitro experiments showed that abnormal tibiofibular rotation alters patellar contact mechanics.28 Our study further extended these findings by demonstrating that knee valgus not only led to stress concentration in the lower lateral region of the patella and PL but also induced abnormal stress distribution in joint stabilizing structures, including MCL, LCL, menisci, and cruciate ligaments. These results supported Huberti and Hayes’ theory on patellofemoral contact pressure distribution,29 providing new evidence for understanding the biomechanical mechanisms of PFPS. This research examined the impact of basketball shoe TBSs on the potential pathogenesis of PFPS during stop-jumping landing maneuvers, based on an integrated foot-ankle-shoe-knee FEM. The results indicated that compared to LF shoes, wearing TF basketball shoes led to restricted ankle DF-ROM, increased knee adduction moment, greater lateral patellar displacement and stress, as well as earlier activation of the VL relative to the VM. These findings collectively suggest TF shoes may elevate abnormal patellar tracking risk and the likelihood of PFPS development. This study may offer new insights into basketball footwear selection and theoretical support for reducing the risk of PFPS.

Another methodological consideration relates to the material properties assigned to soft tissues in our FE model. While this study adopted parameters validated in previous biomechanical analyses, recent advances in shear wave elastography (SWE) offer an opportunity to refine these assumptions by providing in vivo, subject-specific mechanical data for living muscles, tendons, and skin. SWE, an ultrasound-based technique, quantifies tissue stiffness by analyzing the propagation velocity of induced shear waves and has shown strong agreement with traditional materials testing methods.30 Furthermore, SWE has been increasingly applied in musculoskeletal research to evaluate tissue elasticity, offering a real-time and non-invasive assessment compared to MRI-based elastography.31,32 Future research should integrate SWE-derived elastic properties into FE modeling frameworks to improve physiological fidelity and capture tissue heterogeneity under dynamic loading.

Limitations of the study

This research presents several limitations that need to be recognized. First, only male participants were recruited. Given the physiological and anatomical differences between males and females, the generalizability of the findings to female populations remains uncertain and requires further validation. These sex-based differences may influence lower limb kinematics and joint loading responses during landing, particularly under different footwear conditions. Future studies should include female participants to determine whether similar biomechanical patterns and injury risks are present across sexes. Second, we only compared TF and LF basketball shoes, with one EUR size difference (approximately 5 mm) between them. Future research should incorporate a wider range of shoe types and size combinations. Third, due to computational constraints, the FEA was based on a single standard subject model. Considering individual variability, future studies should adopt subject-specific modeling to improve the representativeness of the results. Additionally, to simplify the model, the MCL and LCL of the knee joint were assumed to be isotropic materials. Future studies could consider incorporating anisotropic or hyperelastic material properties to improve the accuracy of biomechanical simulations. Finally, the present study did not incorporate material properties for soft tissues derived from SWE. SWE provides more physiologically realistic, in vivo estimates of skin and muscle elasticity, which could significantly enhance the accuracy and relevance of FE simulations. Future studies should integrate SWE-derived data into subject-specific models to address this limitation and better capture tissue heterogeneity under dynamic loading conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study participant details

Building on prior related research,1 this study utilized G-Power software (version: 3.1.9.7; Henry University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the required sample size. A total of 28 male basketball players (age: 24.20 ± 1.92 years; height: 182.80 ± 8.07 cm; body weight (BW): 80.60 ± 8.59 kg) from Ningbo University were recruited, with calculations based on an effect size of 0.51, a power of 0.8, and a significance level of 0.05. The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) all participants were amateur basketball players from Ningbo University; (2) each participant engaged in basketball activities at least three to four times per week, with a minimum duration of one and a half hours per session; (3) no lower limb injuries had been sustained in the past six months; and (4) no history of surgical procedures on the lower limbs.33 All participants were clear about the study purpose, requirements, and experimental procedures and signed a subject informed consent form. Before conducting the experiment, the Ethics Committee of Ningbo University approved this study (Approval Number: TY2025044).

Method details

Experimental protocol

All experimental tasks in this study were performed in the Exercise Biomechanics Lab of Ningbo University. Based on previous research, we fixed 38 reflective markers (each with a diameter of 12.5 mm) on the upper and lower limbs of each subject to track the movement trajectories of subjects.33 The Vicon Motion Capture System (Vicon Metrics Ltd., Oxford, United Kingdom) equipped with eight infrared cameras was used to collect kinematic data (sampling frequency: 200 Hz), while kinetic data were collected using two force plates (AMTI, Watertown, MA, United States) (sampling frequency: 1000 Hz).34,35 Surface muscle activation of the lower extremity was collected by electromyography (EMG) system (Figure 4A). EMG sensors were placed on the subjects’ gluteus medius (GMED), gluteus maximus (GMAX), vastus lateralis (VL), vastus medialis (VM), tibialis anterior (TA), and peroneus longus (PL). Subsequently, maximum voluntary contractions (MVC) of the six muscles were collected to normalize muscle activation. Prior to surface EMG data acquisition, body hair at the electrode placement sites was removed, and the skin was cleaned with alcohol to reduce impedance between the skin and electrodes.36 OpenSim 4.5 was employed to construct musculoskeletal models of the participants. As shown in Figure 4A, the simulated muscle activation results from OpenSim 4.5 were compared with the surface EMG data to validate the accuracy of the musculoskeletal models.

Experimental procedure

Prior to the initiation of experimental procedures, all subjects completed a standardized warm-up protocol under the supervision of a certified athletic trainer. The regimen consisted of 10 minutes of treadmill jogging at 9 km/h while wearing uniform compression tights and basketball shoes, followed by compressive muscle stretching exercises. To mitigate potential fatigue effects, a 5-minute rest period was implemented before initiating experimental measurements. Participants then engaged in familiarization trials until demonstrating proficiency with the testing protocols. During this acclimatization phase, subjects performed multiple practice repetitions of the required stop-jumping maneuvers under standardized verbal instruction to ensure proper movement execution. For baseline data acquisition, participants were positioned in a standard anatomical stance at the center of the force platform, maintaining complete stillness for static calibration measurements.36

As illustrated in Figure 4B, participants began accelerating from the starting position adjacent to the force platform, executing a four-step linear approach at self-selected velocity toward the measurement area. Upon platform contact, subjects performed a deceleration maneuver characterized by sequential heel strikes (non-dominant limb preceding dominant limb), immediately followed by maximal vertical propulsion and subsequent landing. Data acquisition specifically targeted the dominant limb, operationally defined as the preferred kicking leg. Trials were considered valid when they met the following criteria: (1) complete execution of both braking and propulsion phases within the force platform boundaries; (2) maintenance of vertical trunk alignment during takeoff; and (3) post-landing static equilibrium sustained for at least 2 seconds. Each participant completed eight successful trials per footwear condition (loose-fitting and tight-fitting basketball shoes), yielding 16 total data sets. To minimize the potential influence of fatigue on experimental outcomes, a standardized 1-minute recovery period was implemented between consecutive trials.

Biomechanical parameter collection and processing

The initial ground contact was defined as the time point when the vertical ground reaction force (VGRF) first exceeded 10 N, with the landing phase spanning from initial contact to maximum knee flexion.37 The Vicon Nexus motion capture system was employed to acquire the original kinematic and kinetic data, then exported in C3D format. Marker data preprocessing included: 1) standard anatomical labeling of reflective markers; 2) gap filling for missing markers; and 3) removal of erroneous markers. The data were processed in MATLAB R2024a (MathWorks, Massachusetts, USA) with a fourth-order zero-phase lag Butterworth filter, applying cutoff frequencies of 10 Hz for kinematic data and 20 Hz for kinetic data based on optimal signal-to-noise ratio calculations. Filtered data were converted to the trc format and imported into OpenSim 4.5 for subsequent musculoskeletal modeling. Kinematic and kinetic analysis were subsequently performed in OpenSim 4.5 using inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics methods. Subsequently, static optimization was performed in OpenSim 4.5 to calculate individual muscle activation.

To eliminate artifacts and noise while ensuring data reliability, surface electromyography (EMG) signals were initially processed using a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter with cut-off frequencies set at 10 Hz and 400 Hz. The filtered signals underwent full-wave rectification, followed by low-pass filtering at 6 Hz to derive the smoothed EMG envelope, based on optimal signal-to-noise ratio considerations. The resulting envelopes were standardized to their maximum amplitude. To determine maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) levels, each muscle underwent three 5-second isometric contractions against manual resistance, with the maximum value of root mean square (RMS) used as the normalization benchmark.38

Subsequently, the normalized EMG signal ei(t) was input into a second-order nonlinear differential equation model to estimate muscle activation ai(t).39 These EMG-derived activation patterns were then compared with simulated muscle activations from OpenSim, serving as the basis for evaluating the musculoskeletal model’s accuracy.

The nonlinear coefficient Ai characterizes the degree of nonlinearity between neural activation ui(t) and muscle activation ai(t).15 In this study, Ai was assigned a value of 1.5, and the electromechanical delay was set to 10 milliseconds. All data were resampled into 101 points, representing 0% to 100% of the landing phase, using custom scripts of MATLAB R2024a. As shown in Figure 4A, the muscle activation patterns calculated by OpenSim were generally consistent with those derived from processed EMG data, supporting the validity of the musculoskeletal model used in this study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Geometric data collection

According to the mean height and weight of the 28 participants, we chose a representative subject (height: 180 cm; weight: 80 kg; age: 24 years) for this study. Using Helium-Free MRI (Spin MRI, XGY-Spin MRI-CS001, Xingaoyi Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) and CT scanning with a 2 mm interval, we acquired comprehensive imaging data of the lower limb and basketball shoes with varying TBSs. The subject’s dominant leg was maintained in a shod condition and fixed in a neutral position using an ankle-foot orthosis to establish a foot-shoe-ankle-knee finite element model (Figure 5).

Foot-shoe-ankle-knee integrated finite element model construction

In this study, tight-fitting (TF) shoes were defined as basketball shoes that were one full size smaller than each participant’s regular size, while loose-fitting (LF) shoes were defined as one full size larger. Based on the regular shoe sizes of the 28 participants (EU 40 to 44), six sizes of basketball shoes (EU 39 to 45) were prepared to ensure coverage of both TF and LF conditions for all subjects. All test shoes were identical in brand, model, outsole structure and material, upper design, and lacing system. Shoe size was the only variable adjusted, which helped ensure that any differences in biomechanical outcomes could be primarily attributed to changes in forefoot space rather than to confounding factors such as structure, material, or cushioning (Figure 5C). To further characterize the variation in forefoot space between the TF and LF conditions, we introduced the toe box space (TBS) as a reference parameter. TBS was defined as the shortest distance between the tip of the first distal phalanx and the inner front end of the shoe. Although TBS was not directly measured, standard last data for basketball shoes indicate that a one-size increase typically extends the toe box by approximately 5 mm. Thus, TBS was indirectly modulated through shoe size manipulation.

The finite element modeling procedure consisted of the following four steps, as illustrated in Figures 5A and 5B: (1) Importing the two-dimensional medical images in DCM format into Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) to segment three-dimensional models of bones, soft tissues and basketball shoes. The shoe model was divided into three components: the upper, the insole, and the sole while ensuring the shoes’ shape and thickness accurately reflected real dimensions; (2) These models were then exported in STL format to Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems. Rock Hill, SC, USA) for operations including smoothing, patching, filling, and creating precise surfaces to obtain NURBS (Non-Uniform Rational B-Splines) surfaces; (3) the models were exported in STP format to SolidWorks 2021 (SolidWorks Corporation, Waltham, MA, United States) for solid conversion and model assembly operations, and saved in X_T format; (4) meshing and von Mises stress analysis were performed in Workbench 2021 (ANSYS, Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA). To ensure solution convergence and computational reliability, a mesh convergence test was conducted by gradually reducing the element size until the difference in displacement at the knee joint between two meshes was within 3%.40 For example, the cartilage mesh size was reduced from 3.5 mm to 2.0 mm, resulting in less than 3% variation in displacement, and further refinement to 1.5 mm showed negligible improvement. Based on these results, the final mesh sizes were selected to balance accuracy and efficiency. The solid models were discretized using tetrahedral elements with the following size specifications: 5.0 mm for footwear components (upper, sole, insole), soft tissue, and plate41; while a finer mesh of 3.5 mm was used for the bones, and an even finer 2.0 mm mesh was designated for cartilage. This finite element model incorporated 30 bones (including distal femur, patella, tibia, fibula, 7 tarsals, 5 metatarsals, and 14 phalanges). It also included 4 cartilages of the knee joint, 29 cartilages between the bones of the foot and ankle, the medial and lateral meniscus, and 5 plantar fasciae. Additionally, to optimize computational efficiency without compromising accuracy, 17 ligaments relevant to this study were included to connect the bones and ensure structural integrity.

Boundary and loading conditions

The FEA utilized kinematic and kinetic data collected at the peak vertical ground reaction force (VGRF) time point during gait as the loading conditions (Table 2). These inputs included ankle and knee joint angles and moments, ground reaction forces in three directions, as well as estimated Achilles tendon force (ATF). The sagittal plane angles of the ankle and knee joints were calculated through modifying the angles formed between the femoral axis, tibial axis, and the foot’s longitudinal axis. The ground plate was rotated to reproduce the foot-ground angle at the peak VGRF. The proximal surface of the femur and the ground plate were constrained in all degrees of freedom, serving as fixed supports, while a remote displacement (fixed) was applied to the shoe’s upper surface to allow for deformation.

Model validation

This study employed two classical experimental methods (anterior drawer test and axial stress test) to validate the finite element model (FEM).55 With the knee in full extension (0° angle between the femoral axis and tibial axis in the sagittal plane), a 134 N backward load was applied at the midpoint of the femoral condyle midline to verify the reliability of the foot-shoe-ankle-knee integrated FEM. The FEA yielded anterior-posterior, proximal-distal, and medial-lateral displacement of 4.23 mm, 0.41 mm, and 0.55 mm, respectively. As shown in Table 3, these values closely matched previous research findings by Song, Xu, and Gabriel, thereby fully demonstrating the validity of the established FEM.36,55,56

Additionally, the accuracy of the FEM was verified using the high-speed dual fluoroscopic imaging system (DFIS). This system enables the acquisition of dynamic fluoroscopic images of the knee joint, allowing for the calculation of its displacement, which was then compared with FEA results to validate the FEM. Figures 5C and 5D illustrate the displacement calculation process based on DFIS and the comparison with FEA outcomes respectively. The DFIS parameters were meticulously calibrated: source-to-detector distance 1350 mm, operating voltage 70 kV, tube current 560 mA, single-frame exposure time 3 s; laser positioning system wavelength 650 nm with 3 mW power; with a pixel matrix of 3072 by 3072, the flat-panel detector provides an imaging coverage of 427 mm by 427 mm; with auto-exposure control range of 45 μGy; X-ray tube nominal voltage 150 kV, with dual-source orthogonal configuration at 90°.

Building upon the DFIS configuration, the dynamic knee analysis was conducted through the following process: initial fluoroscopy mode for baseline data, followed by dynamic sequence mode during the landing phase of stop-jumping maneuvers. Dual X-ray sources synchronously emitted beams through the knee joint, with projections recorded by flat-panel detectors to generate dynamic fluoroscopic images. The post-processing workflow involved: (1) keyframe extraction from dynamic sequences; (2) import of STL-format 3D knee models; (3) 2D-3D registration (including marking feature point, drawing normal vector and establishing a coordinate system); (4) displacement quantification in anterior-posterior, proximal-distal, and medial-lateral directions.

Figure 5D illustrates the displacement measurement procedure using the DFIS and its comparison with the FEA results. The knee joint displacements in the anterior-posterior, proximal-distal, and medial-lateral directions obtained from DFIS while wearing the TF shoes closely matched the corresponding FEA results. A similar level of agreement was also observed under LF conditions. These results further validate the accuracy and reliability of the integrated foot-shoe-ankle-knee FEM developed in this study.

Statistical analysis

The normality of each variable was tested through the Shapiro-Wilk test before the statistical analysis. If the data did not meet the assumption of normality, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied as a non-parametric approach. For statistical parametric mapping (SPM), full time-series data during the landing phase were extracted and time-normalized to 101 data points (representing 0% to 100% of the phase) using custom MATLAB scripts. Subsequent statistical testing was performed using the open-source SPM1d toolbox with a significance threshold of 0.05. Additionally, paired sample t-tests in SPSS 27.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) were used to compare peak variable differences between TF and LF basketball shoes, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
