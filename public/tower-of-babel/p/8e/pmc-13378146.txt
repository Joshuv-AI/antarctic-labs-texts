Peripheral skin temperature during hospitalization and allergic disorders in non-NICU neonates >35 weeks
Authors: Huiling Cao, Qin Tan, Chengjun Yu, Yuan Shi, Long Chen, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study explored the relationship between peripheral skin temperature during hospitalization (PSTDH) and long-term allergy-related diseases. A prospective cohort study measured PSTDH in non-NICU human neonates >35 weeks gestation (n = 577), and followed them for 4–5 years. Quartile grouping and restricted cubic splines were used to determine cutoff values. Four analytical methods-chi-square test, logistic regression, propensity score matching, and inverse probability of treatment weighting-were applied to assess associations between lower PSTDH and outcomes including bloody stool, necrotizing enterocolitis, cow’s milk protein allergy, asthma, rhinitis, food allergy, and drug allergy. Neonates with higher PSTDH showed lower incidences of bloody stool (aOR 0.28, 95% CI 0.14–0.56), rhinitis (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.38–0.88), allergic rhinitis (aOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.18–0.90), allergic dermatitis (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.24–0.91), and food allergy (aOR 0.59, 95% CI 0.38–0.91). These findings suggest that both axillary and peripheral skin temperatures deserve equal attention in neonatal care.

Introduction

Temperature is a trustworthy quantitative indicator for determining an adult’s health status. Cold exposure raises the chance of hospitalization for cardiovascular conditions, according to a meta-analysis of 64 studies.1 One additionally, a multi-country observational analysis also demonstrates that cold exposure was responsible for the majority of the temperature-related mortality burden.2,3 Since Silverman et al. first report that controlling the thermal environment (cold exposure) during the first five days after birth significantly reduces mortality in preterm neonates,4 admission hypothermia has been linked to an increased incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) in multiple studies.5,6 A strong correlation was also found between a decrease in ambient temperature and a higher chance of developing allergic disorders, such as atopic dermatitis and asthma.7,8 To sum up, low body temperature (BT) or cold exposure appears to be harmful to human health.

Common allergic conditions include cow’s milk protein allergy (CMPA), eczema, asthma, rhinitis, food and drug allergies, allergic conjunctivitis, and so forth. in early childhood. CMPA is the most common food allergy disease in newborn.9 Bloody stool is one of the main symptom of CMPA, and the primary diagnostic criteria include clinical history, effective dietary elimination, and positive food challenge test.10 A growing number of children worldwide suffer from asthma and allergic rhinitis, two of the most prevalent chronic illnesses that have a major influence on healthcare systems and quality of life. Studying these disorders is essential for minimizing atopic advancement or allergic march, enhancing long-term health outcomes, and comprehending immunological development in infancy.11,12 In 2012, the American Academy of Pediatrics distributed guidelines recommending controlling environmental temperature to balance an axillary temperature range of 36.5°C–37.4°C prior to discharge for an infant in an open crib.13 However, the guideline does not recommend a range of peripheral skin temperature during hospitalization (PSTDH). Actually, three temperature conditions can appear during neonatal hospitalization, including axillary temperature range of 36.5°C–37.4°C with relatively warmer peripheral skin temperature, axillary temperature range of 36.5°C–37.4°C with relatively cooler peripheral skin temperature and abnormal axillary temperature (<36.5°C or ≥37.5°C). So far, no one has explicitly proposed a threshold for peripheral skin temperature, later in discussion which adverse consequences would occur, let alone its relationship with allergic-related diseases.

In this context, we hypothesized that the incidence of short-term and long-term outcomes might differ in patients with relatively warmer PSTDH compared with those with relatively colder PSTDH, in which patients in both groups were shown the axillary temperature range of 36.5°C–37.4°C. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a prospective cohort study evaluating the associations between peripheral skin temperature and short-term outcomes (bloody stools, NEC, CMPA) as well as long-term allergic outcomes (asthma, allergic asthma, rhinitis, allergic rhinitis, food allergy, and drug allergy) in early childhood.

Results

Study population

A total of 1,931 neonates were admitted to the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. We excluded 1,354 infants, including 345 of GA < 35 weeks, 11 of corrected GA > 44 weeks, 45 of needing invasive ventilation, 259 of needing noninvasive ventilation, 59 of major congenital anomalies, 23 of anal fissure, 19 of chromosomal abnormalities, 224 of suspected NEC, 255 of suspected sepsis, and 114 of parents’ refusal. A total of 577 met the inclusion criteria and underwent further assessment during hospitalization. 73 patients had bloody stool during their hospital stay. Patients with bloody stool were monitored at 6 months of age to determine if CMPA could be diagnosed. We performed a further follow-up when the 577 kids were 4–5 years old, and 44 patients (7.6%) were lost to follow-up in all. The final long-term outcome analytic population consisted of 533 patients. The long-term follow-up results showed that thirty-five participants (6.6%) had a clinical diagnosis of asthma. Of them, fifteen instances were diagnosed as allergic asthma. A total of 191 patients (35.8%) had rhinitis. Runny noses (53.4%), nasal obstruction (18.6%), and sneezing (12.9%) were the primary clinical symptoms, and interestingly, six individuals (2.2%) had nosebleeds as the primary symptom of rhinitis. Among them, 66 patients were considered to have allergic rhinitis. A history of food protein allergy was present in 153 patients (28.7%). Milk protein was the most common allergy (81 cases), followed by shellfish (23 cases), mango (23 cases), and so on. Thirteen patients (2.4%) had a history of medication allergies, including three cases of penicillin allergy, two cases of cephalosporin allergy, and one incidence of each of ibuprofen and Yanhuning allergy. Demographic characteristics

Firstly, when we divided the subjects into four groups based on the quartiles of the lowest peripheral skin temperature, as shown in Table 1. Regarding continuous variables, age increased progressively across the quartiles (median [P25, P75]: Q1: 3.96 [1.90, 8.85]; Q2: 5.29 [2.80, 10.13]; Q3: 6.94 [3.67, 14.24]; Q4: 8.33 [4.96, 14.92] days; p = 0.009). Admission blood glucose levels were higher in the higher temperature groups (Q1: 4.60 [3.88, 5.20]; Q2: 4.50 [3.90, 5.10]; Q3: 4.70 [3.90, 5.20]; Q4: 4.80 [4.40, 5.30] mmol/L; p = 0.001), while the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (N/L) decreased significantly (Q1: 1.36 [0.75, 2.25]; Q2: 1.22 [0.81, 2.12]; Q3: 1.09 [0.72, 1.81]; Q4: 1.01 [0.68, 1.57]; p = 0.013). For categorical variables, the proportion of patients with pneumonia decreased significantly from Q1 to Q4 (Q1: 47.1%; Q2: 39.7%; Q3: 42.6%; Q4: 29.1%; p = 0.013). Similarly, the proportion of patients presenting with concurrent jaundice and pneumonia also showed a marked decline across the quartiles (Q1: 32.1%; Q2: 22.7%; Q3: 21.6%; Q4: 12.8%; p = 0.001). No other baseline variables differed significantly among the four groups (all p > 0.05). Subsequently, we applied restricted cubic splines, treating the minimum peripheral skin temperature as a continuous variable, to explore the dose-response relationship between this variable and the primary outcome indicators, namely asthma, rhinitis, and food allergy. As shown in Figure 3, when the minimum peripheral skin temperature >32.4°C, it exerts a protective effect against the development of asthma, rhinitis, and food allergies. Based on the results obtained from Figure 3, the newborns were divided into two groups: the lower PSTDH group (n = 294) and the higher PSTDH group (n = 283). A comparison of the baseline data between the two groups revealed that there were differences in the baseline data, as shown in Table 2. Therefore, in the subsequent study, to make the baseline comparable, we conducted propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analyses. After PSM matching, 235 patients were successfully matched in each group, and most of the baseline data were well balanced, except for small for gestational age (SGA) (standardized mean difference (SMD) = 0.182, p = 0.091) and Buffer excess (BE) (SMD = 0.103, p = 0.161) (please refer to Table 3). After IPTW, the SMD of GAs was greater than 0.1 (SMD = 0.108, p = 0.243), while the SMD of the other baseline data was less than 0.1, indicating that the baseline was well balanced (please refer to Table 4). In Figure S1, the changes in the SMD of covariates before and after PSM and IPTW are presented. As shown in the figure, all covariates have been well balanced. It is worth noting that the treatment and status during hospitalization indicators presented in Tables 2, 3, and 4 were recorded both before and after matching and weighting. Outcomes

To evaluate the impact of lower PSTDH on allergy-related diseases and to ensure the robustness and reliability of the findings, we employed two grouping strategies (quartile-based grouping and the cut-off value derived from restricted cubic spline analysis) and applied multiple analytical approaches, including chi-square tests, regression analyses, PSM, and IPTW. Additionally, adjustments for potential confounders were performed in the analyses. The results for outcomes are summarized later in discussion.

Short-term outcomes

As shown in Table 5, the proportions of bloody stools decreased progressively across the quartile groups from Q1 to Q4 (25.7% vs. 12.8% vs. 8.8% vs. 4.1%; p < 0.001). Long-term outcomes

As shown in Table 5, the proportions of rhinitis decreased progressively from Q1 to Q4 (46.8% vs. 40.3% vs. 28.3% vs. 29.3%; p = 0.003). Similarly, allergic rhinitis showed a declining trend (49.2% vs. 30.8% vs. 30.8% vs. 22.0%; p = 0.029). Food allergy also demonstrated a significant decreasing trend (37.3% vs. 28.7% vs. 21.7% vs. 27.9%; p = 0.049). Allergic dermatitis was more frequent in Q1 (13.5%) compared with Q3 (3.6%; p = 0.022). Airway hypersensitivity (composite of asthma and rhinitis) decreased significantly across quartiles (48.4% vs. 43.4% vs. 31.9% vs. 29.3%; p = 0.003), as did gastrointestinal hypersensitivity (38.8% vs. 29.5% vs. 23.2% vs. 28.6%; p = 0.047). The allergy score was highest in Q1 (1.4 ± 1.23) and decreased significantly across groups (p < 0.001). No significant trends were observed for asthma, allergic asthma, eczema, urticaria, allergic conjunctivitis, drug allergy, or skin hypersensitivity.

In the binary logistic regression analyses (Table 6), compared with Q1, the odds ratios for rhinitis in Q3 were 0.447 (95% CI: 0.269–0.745) in Model 1, 0.438 (95% CI: 0.260–0.737) in Model 2, and 0.427 (95% CI: 0.249–0.733) in Model 3, with significant trends across all models (p = 0.004, 0.005, and 0.007, respectively). For allergic rhinitis, the OR for Q4 versus Q1 was 0.291 (95% CI: 0.118–0.715) in Model 1, 0.277 (95% CI: 0.109–0.706) in Model 2, and 0.259 (95% CI: 0.096–0.702) in Model 3 (p = 0.033, 0.028, and 0.020, respectively). Food allergy showed a significant decreasing trend in adjusted models (Model 2: OR for Q3 = 0.407, 95% CI: 0.232–0.712, p = 0.017; Model 3: OR for Q3 = 0.417, 95% CI: 0.233–0.745, p =0.032). Airway hypersensitivity was significantly lower in Q3 and Q4 across all models (Model 3: OR for Q3 = 0.399, 95% CI: 0.215–0.737; OR for Q4 = 0.387, 95% CI: 0.205–0.731; p = 0.005). Gastrointestinal hypersensitivity also demonstrated a significant protective effect in higher quartiles (Model 3: OR for Q3 = 0.441, 95% CI: 0.249–0.778; p = 0.044). No consistent significant associations were observed for asthma, allergic asthma, eczema, urticaria, allergic conjunctivitis, drug allergy, or skin hypersensitivity in the regression models.

After PSM (Table 7), the higher PSTDH group had significantly lower proportions of rhinitis (29.9% vs. 43.3%; p = 0.003), with an adjusted OR of 0.58 (95% CI: 0.38–0.88; p = 0.012). Allergic rhinitis was also less frequent in the higher PSTDH group (22.4% vs. 43.0%; p = 0.008), with an adjusted OR of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.18–0.90; p = 0.027). Food allergy occurred in 25.9% of the higher temperature group compared with 36.3% of the lower temperature group (p = 0.018), with a consistent adjusted OR of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.38–0.91; p = 0.017). Allergic dermatitis was significantly less common in the higher PSTDH group (7.1% vs. 13.5%; p = 0.032), with an adjusted OR of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.24–0.91; p = 0.025). Airway hypersensitivity was reduced in the higher PSTDH group (18.8% vs. 27.9%; p = 0.029), with an adjusted OR of 0.53 (95% CI: 0.33–0.85; p = 0.010). Gastrointestinal hypersensitivity was also significantly lower in the higher temperature group (27.2% vs. 37.2%; p = 0.025), with an adjusted OR of 0.62 (95% CI: 0.40–0.96; p = 0.031). The allergy score was significantly lower in the higher PSTDH group (0.97 ± 1.10 vs. 1.41 ± 1.31; p < 0.001), with an adjusted mean difference of −0.452 (95% CI: −0.694 to −0.210; p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed for asthma, allergic asthma, eczema, urticaria, allergic conjunctivitis, drug allergy, or skin hypersensitivity after PSM.

In the IPTW analysis (Table 8), the weighted results were consistent with the PSM findings. The higher PSTDH group had significantly lower weighted proportions of rhinitis (28.8% vs. 43.3%; p = 0.001), with an adjusted OR of 0.54 (95% CI: 0.36–0.81; p = 0.003). Allergic rhinitis showed a lower proportion in the higher PSTDH group (25.7% vs. 41.1%; p = 0.046), though the association became non-significant after full adjustment (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.29–1.23; p = 0.158). Food allergy was significantly less frequent in the higher PSTDH group (23.0% vs. 33.0%; p = 0.013), with a consistent adjusted OR of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.40–0.92; p = 0.018). Allergic conjunctivitis was also less common in the higher PSTDH group (2.2% vs. 5.8%; p = 0.049), with an adjusted OR of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.13–0.97; p = 0.043). Airway hypersensitivity remained significantly lower in the higher PSTDH group (17.7% vs. 28.9%; p = 0.004), with an adjusted OR of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.30–0.72; p = 0.001). Gastrointestinal hypersensitivity also showed a significant reduction (24.2% vs. 34.1%; p = 0.015), with an adjusted OR of 0.62 (95% CI: 0.41–0.94; p = 0.024). The allergy score was significantly lower in the higher PSTDH group (0.93 ± 1.07 vs. 1.36 ± 1.26; p < 0.001), with an adjusted mean difference of −0.45 (95% CI: −0.66 to −0.23; p < 0.001). No consistent significant differences were observed for asthma, allergic asthma, eczema, urticaria, allergic dermatitis (which became non-significant after full adjustment, p = 0.062), drug allergy, or skin hypersensitivity after IPTW.

In summary, across multiple analytical strategies, a lower peripheral skin temperature during neonatal hospitalization was consistently associated with a higher risk of long-term allergic outcomes, including rhinitis, allergic rhinitis, food allergy, allergic dermatitis, allergic conjunctivitis, airway hypersensitivity, gastrointestinal hypersensitivity, and a higher overall allergy score. In contrast, no consistent associations were found for asthma, allergic asthma, eczema, urticaria, drug allergy, or skin hypersensitivity.

Sensitivity analysis

During the follow-up for long-term outcomes, some patients did not attend outpatient visits at our hospital. For these patients, only telephone follow-up with their parents or guardians was feasible. Therefore, two inclusion criteria were adopted for asthma and rhinitis: (1) doctor-diagnosed alone, and (2) combined diagnosis (doctor diagnosis plus parental report). Sensitivity analyses were performed to compare these two definitions. The results demonstrated that for rhinitis and airway hypersensitivity, the findings remained robust across all three analytical models under both diagnostic criteria. Detailed results are presented in Figure S2.

Second, during the follow-up for long-term outcomes, 44 patients (7.6%) were lost to follow-up. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with missing follow-up data and those with complete follow-up data, and all p values were >0.05 (Table S1). The missing data were verified to be completely at random (MCAR), and the missing rate was less than 10%. Therefore, a complete case analysis was adopted. To validate the appropriateness of the complete case approach, we performed inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to assess whether the results were stable between the two methods for handling missing data. As shown in Figure S3, the results for the study outcomes, including asthma, allergic asthma, rhinitis, allergic rhinitis, bloody stools, food allergy, and drug allergy and so forth—were consistent between the two methods. Only the results for allergic dermatitis were inconsistent. Therefore, we consider that the use of complete case analysis did not materially affect the findings regarding loss to follow-up data.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we aimed to explore whether PSTDH was related to the increased short-term outcomes, including bloody stool, NEC ≥ 2nd, and CMPA, and long-term outcomes related to allergic disorders, such as asthma, rhinitis, food, and drug allergy. After adjustment for infant characteristics, patients with higher PSTDH had lower incidences of bloody stool (aOR 0.28, 95% CI 0.14–0.56), rhinitis (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.38–0.88), allergic rhinitis (aOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.18–0.90), allergic dermatitis (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.24–0.91), and food allergy (aOR 0.59, 95% CI 0.38–0.91) as compared with those with colder PSTDH. No differences were shown in NEC ≥ 2nd, CMPA, and drug allergy between the two groups. Our study evaluated the connection between allergic disorders and PSTDH, and identified a threshold for peripheral skin temperature below which the risk of allergic-related diseases appears to increase. This finding has the potential to guide early clinical monitoring and intervention strategies in the neonatal period. The findings give us a fresh approach to caring for newborns during hospital stay: axillary temperature and PSTDH should be given equal weight.

In the present study, lower-PSTDH is associated with an increase in bloody stool in the neonatal period. Although there are no clinically relevant reports to support our research, several basic studies and indirect evidence have provided a theoretical basis for the present conclusion. The study by Chen et al. suggests that a cold environment could cause mesenteric vasoconstriction, reducing intestinal blood flow,14 the intestinal mucosal epithelial cells undergo energy depletion due to ischemia and hypoxia, resulting in the structural disruption of tight junction proteins (such as occludin and ZO-1), a significant increase in intestinal barrier permeability, and ultimately leading to bloody stool.15 A study explored the effects of hypothermia and acidosis on coagulation function and found that hypothermia and acidosis significantly impaired platelet aggregation function and increased the risk of gastrointestinal mucosal bleeding.16 Finally, and most importantly, chronic cold exposure activates the TLR4/MyD88-mediated NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and COX-2. These inflammatory mediators can indirectly damage microvessels and increase bleeding tendency. Furthermore, chronic cold exposure induces the cleavage of Gasdermin D (GSDMD) via the NLRP3/ASC/caspase-1 and caspase-11 pathways, triggering pyroptosis - a lytic, pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death.17 Consistent with this, Jiang et al. further demonstrated that cold stress upregulates NLRP3/caspase-1 signaling, and that the inhibition of this pathway alleviates cold-induced intestinal injury.18 These studies provide theoretical evidence that hypothermia can lead to an increase in bloody stool. Bloody stool is an emergency clinical condition and needs to further exclude other causes, including NEC, CMPA, and other conditions, which can increase the hospital stay. In the present study, patients with lower PSTDH group had longer hospital stay (aMD −2.2, 95%CI [−3.8]−[−1.5]) as compared with those with higher PSTDH group. No differences were shown in NEC ≥ 2nd and CMPA between the two groups after adjustment for infant characteristics.

NEC, one of the causes of bloody stool in newborns, was not found to be significantly associated with lower-PSTDH in this study. However, a study in 2023 showed a higher probability of developing NEC in premature infants (GA <32 weeks, birth weight <1500 g) with a lower BT at admission.19 The cause was the relatively low incidence of NEC in newborns with body weight >2500 g or/and ≥35 weeks’ gestation. In the future, multicenter randomized controlled studies or larger cohorts are needed to verify the results.

Normal human BT has long been considered to be 37.0°C. But BT has declined over the past 20 years in the United States, and nowadays the BT is about 36.5°C–36.6°C.20,21,22 Conversely, allergic disorders are gradually increasing according to the survey of the World Allergy Organization.23 It is a plausible speculation that the rising allergic disorders might be due to declining BT. The study by Belachew et al. found that the risk of new-onset asthma increased by 7% for every 1°C drop in the average winter temperature (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13).24 Systematic review with meta-analysis included 26 studies (combined total number of subjects >26 million) was performed to evaluate the effect of temperature drop on risk of asthma, and the results indicated that the OR estimate between temperature drop and asthma was 1.05 (95% CI 1.02–1.08), especially in children (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.03–1.15). Dose-effect analyses also showed stronger associations in asthma risk for each 1°C decrement in short-term temperature (OR 1.055, 95% CI 1.00–1.11).7 Our study did not identify a significant link between the lower-PSTDH group and increased asthma risk. However, given that most children were around four years of age, when asthma diagnosis is often inconclusive without pulmonary function tests, longer follow-up is needed. Future studies that include bronchial provocation testing in older children may provide a more accurate assessment of this relationship. Otherwise, Cheng et al. indicated that a decrease in temperature is associated with an increased risk of allergic rhinitis in children.25 The conclusions are consistent with our research, in which the incidence of rhinitis (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.38–0.88) and allergic rhinitis (aOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.18–0.90) decreased in the higher-PSTDH group than that in the lower-PSTDH group. Several mechanisms may explain the observed association between lower peripheral skin temperature and increased risk of rhinitis and allergic rhinitis.

First, cold-sensitive TRP channels mediate neurogenic inflammation. TRPM8, the primary receptor for cool temperatures, is expressed on trigeminal sensory nerve endings as well as in the nasal epithelium, submucosal glands, and perivascular regions.26 Upon cold exposure, TRPM8 activation induces Ca2+ influx, triggering nerve impulse conduction and local neuropeptide release. Activation of TRPM8/TRPA1 promotes the release of neuropeptides, including substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), neuromedin U (NMU), and neuropeptide Y (NPY). These neuropeptides directly act on nasal mucosal vessels and glands: SP and CGRP induce vasodilation and increased vascular permeability; VIP promotes glandular secretion; collectively, they produce typical rhinitis symptoms such as nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and sneezing. More importantly, neuropeptides amplify inflammatory responses through direct crosstalk with immune cells. SP and NMU activate mast cells, promoting degranulation and histamine release; CGRP activates type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), promoting the production of type 2 cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which further recruit and activate eosinophils, establishing a persistent inflammatory state. Conversely, inflammatory mediators (e.g., histamine, cytokines) released by immune cells can sensitize TRP channels on sensory neurons, lowering their activation threshold and rendering the host more sensitive to subsequent cold stimuli. This positive feedback loop underlies the persistence and chronicity of nasal hyperreactivity.

Second, cold exposure induces an autonomic nervous system imbalance. Peripheral cold stimulation not only activates local neural reflexes but also triggers systemic autonomic responses via the central nervous system. A classic study by Numata et al. (1999) provided direct evidence for this mechanism. Using acoustic rhinometry to assess nasal mucosal responses to foot cold stimulation in patients with idiopathic rhinitis (vasomotor rhinitis), allergic rhinitis, and healthy controls, the study found that in healthy individuals, foot cold stimulation induced sympathetic-mediated nasal mucosal vasoconstriction. In contrast, patients with rhinitis exhibited significantly suppressed sympathetic responses, with cold stimulation instead causing nasal mucosal swelling and increased secretion.27 This autonomic imbalance, characterized by sympathetic hyporesponsiveness and relative parasympathetic predominance, represents a key pathophysiological basis for cold-induced symptom exacerbation in rhinitis patients.

Third, cold exposure impairs nasal epithelial barrier function. Biopsy specimens from allergic rhinitis patients have shown significantly reduced the expression of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin.28 Ex vivo tissue studies have demonstrated that nasal mucosa from allergic rhinitis patients exhibits markedly decreased transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and approximately 2-fold increased paracellular flux of macromolecules (FD4), with symptom severity scores negatively correlated with barrier function. Cold stimulation may compromise epithelial barrier integrity through the following mechanisms: (1) Oxidative stress: TRPA1 activation induces reactive oxygen species (ROSs) generation, and ROSs are known to damage tight junction proteins.29 (2) Mast cell mediators: Cold-induced mast cell activation releases tryptase, which can degrade tight junction proteins. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and TNF-α have been reported to downregulate tight junction protein expression in allergic rhinitis. Collectively, these mechanisms create a permissive environment for allergen penetration and subsequent immune activation.

We also demonstrated that the incidence of food allergy in lower-PSTDH was higher than that in higher-PSTDH (aOR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.38–0.91). This finding is consistent with previous epidemiological evidence showing that infants born in winter (December–February) had a significantly higher risk of developing food allergies before the age <5 years than those born in summer (June–August) (aOR 1.53, 95% CI 1.22–1.92).30 The prevalence rate of food allergy among children was higher in high-latitude cold regions than that in warm regions (RR 1.81, 95%CI 1.66–1.98).31 Collectively, these data suggest that cold exposure, particularly as reflected by peripheral skin temperature, may represent an underrecognized environmental determinant of food allergy.

First, cold stress promotes food allergy through intestinal IL-33/Th2 axis activation. Animal study also showed that cold stress induced food allergy by promoting the expression of IL-33 in intestinal epithelial cells via the ACTH/cortisol axis, and IL-33 knockdown abrogates cold stress-induced food allergy.32 IL-33, as a key alarmin, activates ILC2s and Th2 cells, driving IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 production, thereby facilitating IgE class switching. This provides a direct molecular pathway linking cold exposure to allergic sensitization.

Second, cold stress compromises intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating allergen translocation. Guo indicates that cold exposure decreases the protein expression levels of claudin-3 and occludin by 60%, and leads to impaired intestinal barrier function and increased intestinal permeability. Finally, the disruption of intestinal barrier function made allergens such as β-lactoglobulin more likely to pass through the intestinal barrier and enter the bloodstream and trigger allergic reactions.33 The combination of Th2 polarization and barrier disruption creates a permissive environment for food allergy development.

Third, mast cell activation and neuro-immune crosstalk may amplify allergic responses. Cold stimulation directly induces mast cell degranulation, as evidenced by elevated histamine levels in cold urticaria patients.34 Moreover, recent work has revealed a mast cell-thermoregulatory neuron circuit: mast cell-derived chymase activates TRPV1+ sensory neurons via PAR1, suppressing thermogenesis and causing hypothermia during anaphylaxis. This feedback loop suggests that cold exposure may both pre-activate mast cells and sensitize TRP channels (e.g., TRPM8, TRPA1), lowering the threshold for food allergen-induced reactions.35

In summary, these mechanistic insights-spanning intestinal IL-33/Th2 axis, barrier dysfunction, mast cell-neural crosstalk-collectively support a causal link between peripheral skin hypothermia and increased food allergy risk. Further studies are warranted to verify these pathways in human cohorts and to explore potential interventional strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study is an exploratory investigation that primarily examines whether lower peripheral skin temperature increases the risk of adverse outcomes in pediatric patients, particularly allergy-related outcomes. The study was consistent with the established standard protocol, diagnostic criteria, and ethical guidelines, while incorporating a comprehensive collection of variables, as well as long-term outcomes follow-up. However, there are limitations that need to be considered. Firstly, not all patients were followed up with at the 6-month follow-up; only those with bloody stool were followed up. Nevertheless, we compensated for this shortcoming in the ensuing follow-up with children aged 4–5 years; Secondly, three times temperature monitoring may be inadequate, and more or continuous monitoring of axillary and PSTDH may affect the assessment of these outcomes; Thirdly, observational design of the present study did not supply us with causal claims, this result should be investigated in further randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Fourthly, since the incidence of NEC is very low, more samples are needed to explore the relationship between peripheral skin temperature and NEC. Moreover, asthma was diagnosed clinically, and pulmonary function provocation tests were performed on a subset of pediatric patients. A longer follow-up was required to determine the incidence of asthma and its relationship with peripheral skin temperature. Lastly, a primary limitation of this study is the lack of an external validation cohort. This is largely due to the unique nature of our single-center, prospective cohort. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings requires further investigation. Future multicenter, prospective RCTs for peripheral skin temperature measurement and allergy outcome assessment are needed to establish a validation cohort and confirm our results.

In summary, lower peripheral skin temperature was associated with higher rates of hematochezia, rhinitis, allergic rhinitis, and food allergies. This study innovatively proposes a cutoff value of 32.4°C to identify at-risk infants. Future studies should be performed to verify the results in the smaller preterm infants and/or in larger RCTs and prospective cohorts.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study population

This was a single-center, prospective cohort study conducted at Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between 2021 and 2022. Eligibility requirements for neonates were: (1) an infant’s body weight >2500 g or/and ≥35 weeks’ gestational age (GA), and ≤28 days after birth. (2) neonate is diagnosed with hyperbilirubinemia and/or pneumonia without need of noninvasive or invasive ventilation and (3) admission in non-neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Exclusion criteria were: (1) major congenital anomalies; (2) chromosomal abnormalities; (3) parents’ rejection of enrollment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. 2020204). Informed parental written consents were obtained before data collection. The medical records for all enrolled newborn infants were collected into a database according to the protocol. The trial was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the participating institutions. A total of 577 neonates met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. The long-term outcome analytic sample comprised 533 participants after excluding 44 cases with missing outcome data.

Participant characteristics

Information on patient demographic characteristics (Gestational age, Gender, Birth weight, Admission weight, Breast milk feeding, Admission temperature, Ages and etc.), primary diagnosis (Pneumonia/Jaundice), clinical manifestation, examination and test results during hospitalization, disease extent, treatment details, and in-hospital and follow-up outcomes and maternal information should be recorded. These characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Sex was assigned at birth based on biological characteristics (male/female). Because the study population consisted exclusively of neonates (non-verbal, pre-verbal), gender identity was not applicable and therefore not assessed. No sex-based analysis was required beyond the comparison of outcomes between male and female infants, which is reported in Tables 1 and 2. This study did not collect data on ancestry, race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status because the study was conducted in a single-center setting within a homogeneous geographic and ethnic region (Chongqing/China). Consequently, analyses based on these variables were not performed. The absence of these data is acknowledged as a limitation to the generalizability of our findings to more diverse or multi-ethnic populations.

Method details

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are specified in Section 1 (Experimental model and study participant details). No data points were excluded after analysis except for lost for follow up.

Exposure measurement: Peripheral skin temperature monitoring and grouping

When a neonate had fulfilled the eligibility criteria, peripheral skin temperature was recorded by Doctor Tan at 19:00 every day during hospitalization. Because the area of the sole of a newborn’s foot is larger than that of the palm and the temperature of the foot is the part with the lowest body surface temperature in the human body,36 the center sole of the foot was selected as the measurement site for the extremity temperature of the newborn. Foot skin temperature was measured using an OMRON MC-872J Thermometer (Japan). Before each measurement session, the device was calibrated against a mercury thermometer; measurements proceeded only when the difference between the two was<0.2°C. Three consecutive foot temperature readings were obtained while the infant was quiet and the foot skin was clean, dry, sweat-free, and uncovered. The three values were averaged, and the mean was recorded as the final foot temperature. Axillary temperature was recorded simultaneously. The primary exposure variable was defined as the minimum peripheral skin temperature recorded during each neonate’s hospitalization.In the primary analysis, participants were categorized into four groups based on the quartiles of this minimum temperature distribution: Q1:<30.7°C, Q2:30.7°C–32.4°C, Q3:32.4°C–34.9°C, Q4:≥34.9°C. Baseline characteristics and outcome data were compared across these four quartile groups. In subsequent exploratory analyses, we employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to investigate the dose-response relationship between the minimum peripheral skin temperature and the primary allergic outcome. The RCS model identified a non-linear relationship and suggested a potential cut-off value of 32.4°C. Based on this data-driven cut-off, the study population was reclassified into two groups for further outcome analyses: lower PSTDH Group: ≤32.4°C, higher PSTDH Group: >32.4°C. All subsequent analyses (e.g., Propensity Score Matching, Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting) comparing allergic outcomes were conducted using this binary classification. Other cares including the setting and adjustment of the incubator temperature followed the guideline and suggestion in China.37

Follow-up

Follow-up was conducted via office visits or telephone contact. Short-term follow-up was conducted for patients with bloody stool during hospitalization at the age of 6 months to determine whether the infants could be diagnosed with CMPA by conducting a standard oral provocation test. The long-term follow up was conducted on all the 577 patients included in the cohort and extended to March 2026, to ensure that all patients had an opportunity for at least 4–5 years of follow-up. The contents of long-term follow-up include: whether asthma, allergic asthma, rhinitis, allergic rhinitis, eczema, urticaria, food allergy, drug allergy, allergic conjunctivitis, etc. have been diagnosed. Specific symptoms, allergens, etc. To ensure the accurate assessment of the clinical endpoints, additional information was obtained from telephone contacts with parents of the patients and from medical records obtained from our hospitals, as necessary.

Definition and diagnosis

Hematochezia, is defined as gastrointestinal bleeding due to a variety of causes involving digestive tract disorders and comorbidity of some critical illnesses, manifesting as visible bloody stool or positive fecal occult blood test (FOBT).38 CMPA is defined as an immunological reaction to one or more milk proteins. The diagnosis of CMPA requires a combination of clinical history, elimination diet, and oral food challenge (OFC) or double-blind placebo-controlled food challenge (DBPCFC) (gold standard). which is based on the International Consensus Guidelines.39 OFC test was not performed during hospitalization due to the short duration of hospitalization, all of them were therefore diagnosed with suspected CMPA during hospitalization. The oral food challenge test was usually done during outpatient follow-up within six-month-old after birth. Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by variable airflow obstruction, bronchial hyper-responsiveness, and recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing. Clinical asthma was defined by the presence of recurrent wheezing with documented responsiveness to bronchodilators and/or inhaled corticosteroids, combined with/without positive results on pulmonary function testing (e.g., bronchodilator reversibility or bronchial provocation testing). Allergic asthma was further diagnosed when, in addition to fulfilling the criteria for asthma, at least one positive allergen sensitization was confirmed by either skin prick testing or serum-specific IgE testing.40 Rhinitis was diagnosed when a patient met at least two of the following typical symptoms: nasal itching, paroxysmal sneezing, clear rhinorrhea accompanied by nasal congestion. Nasal endoscopy was required to show pale and swollen nasal mucosa with clear secretions.When a patient met the diagnostic criteria for rhinitis and had at least one positive allergen test result (either skin prick testing or serum-specific IgE testing), a diagnosis of allergic rhinitis was made. In the absence of positive allergen test results, most patients were diagnosed with rhinitis alone.41,42 Food allergy refers to an adverse reaction to specific food proteins mediated by immune mechanisms, usually caused by IgE-mediated (immediate type) or non-IgE-mediated (delayed type). The diagnostic criteria for food allergies are based on European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) guidelines.43 Similarly, Drug allergy is an adverse drug reaction mediated by specific immune mechanisms (IgE- or non-IgE-mediated), characterized by reproducible symptoms upon re-exposure to the causative medication, ranging from mild cutaneous manifestations to life-threatening systemic reactions such as anaphylaxis. EAACI serves as the foundation for the drug allergy diagnostic criteria.44 To facilitate further exploration, the researchers defined several custom composite indicators. Airway hypersensitivity: asthma or allergic rhinitis (positive). Gastrointestinal hypersensitivity: food or drug allergy (positive). Skin hypersensitivity: eczema, urticaria, allergic dermatitis, or allergic conjunctivitis (any positive). Allergy score: sum of positive outcomes among eight conditions (asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema, urticaria, allergic dermatitis, allergic conjunctivitis, food allergy, drug allergy; 1 point each).

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study included the following disorders

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) of Bell’s stage ≥2nd, according to the modified Bell’s staging criteria for NEC,45 Rhinitis, Asthma, Food allergy, Drug allergy. It is worth noting that in our subsequent analysis, the diagnosis of rhinitis, allergic rhinitis, asthma, food and drug allergies, are partly based on the reports provided by the parents.

The secondary outcomes comprised the following conditions

Allergic rhinitis, Allergic asthma, Cow’s milk protein allergy (CMPA), rash, eczema, urticaria, allergic dermatitis, allergic conjunctivitis, hospital stay and weight at discharge during hospitalization. Composite outcome: airway hypersensitivity, gastrointestinal hypersensitivity, skin hypersensitivity and allergy score. Due to the limited number of cases for allergic rhinitis and allergic asthma (n = 66 and n = 15, respectively), these outcomes were analyzed as exploratory secondary outcomes. Results for these outcomes should be interpreted with caution owing to limited statistical power.

Randomization and blinding

This was an observational cohort study; therefore, no randomization or blinding was applied to the exposure.

Sample size estimation

The sample size estimation was calculated according to primary outcomes byPASS 2021(version 21.0.3) with a 1-sided significance level of 0.025 and 90% of power, 1:1 design and the loss to long-term follow-up rate was 20%. We hypothesize that the group with higher acral temperature has a lower probability of developing allergy-related diseases. We calculate the primary long-term outcome measures according to the report of Panzner et al.,41 the incidence of rhinitis in children worldwide ranges from 10% to 40%. We assume that the incidence of rhinitis in the higher-PTSDH group is 20% and that in the lower-PTSDH group is 40%, 133 patients in each group should be involved in this cohort. According to the study about the food allergy,46 the average incidence of food allergy in children is 10%. Interestingly, the global incidence rate of asthma is also about 10%,47 we assume that the incidence of asthma/food allergy in the lower-PTSDH group is 15% and that in the higher-PTSDH group is 5%, 230 cases in each groups respectively. The most stringent requirement (asthma/food allergy) determined the final sample size: there are at least 230 cases in each groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical software

We used GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) for graph drawing. PASS 2021(version 21.0.3) was used for sample size calculation. The R package (4.5.0) was used for statistical analysis, propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting and restricted cubic spline.

Descriptive analysis

Categorical variables were reported as the number of positive cases with corresponding percentages (%). For continuous variables, normality was assessed prior to analysis. Variables following a normal or approximately normal distribution were described using mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas variables with a nonnormal distribution were summarized using median with the 25th and 75th percentiles (P25, P75).

Preliminary analysis: Quartile-based grouping

To avoid arbitrary cut-off selection, minimum peripheral skin temperature was first divided into quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4). Quantitative variables would be firstly checked for normality using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For binary categorical variables, group comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test, reporting the χ2 statistic and Cramer’s V(φc) as the effect size. For continuous variables following a normal or approximately normal distribution, group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA, reporting the F statistic and η2 as the effect size. For continuous variables following a nonnormal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used, reporting the H statistic and ε2 as the effect size.

Logistic regression

We performed binary logistic regression to assess the association between each quartile (with Q1 as reference) and primary and secondary outcome (Table 6), To examine the association between peripheral skin temperature groups and each outcome, binary logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted. Model 2: Adjusted for age, pneumonia (yes/no), jaundice with pneumonia (yes/no), blood glucose, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (N/L). Model 3(represents a fully adjusted model incorporating all available clinically relevant covariates to minimize potential confounding.): Additionally adjusted for exchange transfusion, formula feeding, change in formula, antibiotic use, late-onset sepsis (LOS), cardiac acceleration, and diarrhea. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR or β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and baseline characteristics that differed significantly across the four temperature quartile groups (p < 0.05 in ANOVA or χ2 test or Kruskal-Wallis H test). Because the exposure was categorized into four groups (Q1-Q4), univariable regression analyses for covariate selection were not performed, as many variables lacked statistical significance in such analyses due to limited outcome events.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis

To explore the dose-response relationship between the minimum peripheral skin temperature during hospitalization and allergic outcomes (rhinitis, asthma, and food allergy), we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression using the rms package in R software. A Cox proportional hazards model was fitted with the minimum skin temperature as the exposure variable, using an RCS transformation with three knots (placed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the temperature distribution). The overall significance of the spline term was assessed using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) function. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated across the range of minimum skin temperature, with the reference value set at the median temperature. Based on the RCS analysis, a non-linear relationship was observed, and a potential cut-off value of 32.4°C was identified. This cut-off was subsequently used to reclassify the study population into two groups for further analyses: lower-PSTDH group (≤32.4°C) and higher-PSTDH group (>32.4°C).

Propensity score matching (PSM)

Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to assemble a cohort of patients with similar baseline characteristics. Propensity score matching (1:1 nearest-neighbor matching, caliper = 0.2 SD of logit propensity score) was performed using the MatchIt package in R. Covariates included pneumonia, the neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio, male, box temperature, ages, blood-sodium, jaundice with pneumonia, blood glucose, platelet count, admission weight.. Balance was assessed using SMD (SMD <0.1 considered balanced). Post-matching analyses used weighted logistic/linear regression with three adjustment models: unadjusted, adjusted for post-matching imbalanced covariates (SMD >0.1), and fully adjusted for additional clinical covariates. Sensitivity analyses using alternative diagnostic criteria for asthma, rhinitis, and airway hypersensitivity were visualized with forest plots.

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)

To further control for confounding and estimate the average treatment effect, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was performed using R software with the survey package. The propensity score (PS) was defined as the predicted probability of being in the lower-PSTDH Group (≤32.4°C, coded as Group4 = 0) versus the higher-PSTDH Group (>32.4°C, coded as Group4 = 1). A logistic regression model was fitted with the binary exposure variable (Group4) as the outcome and the following baseline covariates as predictors: pneumonia (P), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NL), male sex (male), birth weight (W), jaundice with pneumonia (P_J), blood glucose (Glu), platelet count (PLT), temperature of the incubator (XW), age at admission (Ages), and sodium (Na). For each participant, the IPTW weight was calculated as: For the control group (Group4 = 0): weight = 1/PS. For treatment group (Group4 = 1): weight = 1/(1-PS). No truncation or stabilization of weights was applied in the primary analysis. Balance was assessed using SMD (SMD <0.1 considered balanced). After IPTW, the association between the low skin temperature group and each outcome was examined using weighted logistic regression (for binary outcomes) or weighted linear regression (for continuous outcomes) within a survey design framework using the svyglm function. Three sequential models were constructed:Model 1 (Unadjusted): Adjusted only for the exposure variable (Group4). Model 2 (Adjusted for post-weighting imbalanced covariates): Additionally adjusted for covariates that remained imbalanced after IPTW (SMD>0.1). Model 3 (Fully adjusted): Further adjusted for additional clinically relevant covariates that remained imbalanced after IPTW. The proportion of missing data for each variable was assessed using the plot_missing function from the DataExplorer package. Little’s MCAR test was performed to evaluate whether missing data were completely at random (MCAR). The primary analysis was conducted as a complete case analysis restricted to participants with complete follow-up data (followup_status = 1). To assess the robustness of the findings to missing data, a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) was performed, with censoring weights derived from a logistic regression model predicting follow-up completion based on Group status. Sensitivity analyses using alternative diagnostic criteria for allergic outcomes were visualized with forest plots.

Significance definition

All tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reporting of n and precision measures

For all analyses, each individual infant was considered one biological unit (n). Sample sizes (n) are provided in the first row of each table. Precision measures were reported as follows: normally distributed continuous variables as mean ± SD; non-normally distributed continuous variables as median (P25, P75); categorical variables as counts (percentages). Effect estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI).
