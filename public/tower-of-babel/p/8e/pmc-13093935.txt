Mucopolysaccharidosis II with diverse genetic origins in a single family: a case series and literature review
Authors: Ruo-Yan Liu, Yang-Li Dai, Chao-Chun Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo highlight the mutations of the iduronate-2-sulfatase (IDS) gene in one family with different origins.Case presentationThe proband (case 1) was a 4-year and 7-month-old boy who presented for "inability to fully extend his fingers for over 2 years". His developmental milestones were unremarkable. However, his fingers could not be extended straight when he was about 2 years old. Snoring was also reported. Physical examination showed dense eyebrows and hair, macrocephaly, coarse facial features, a collapsed nose bridge, hypertelorism, big ears, full lips, a short neck, a lot of Mongolian spots, hypertrichosis, short fingers, and joint contracture in fingers. X-ray showed dysostosis multiplex and brain magnetic resonance showed enlarged ventricles and widened sulci. A high ratio of urea glycosaminoglycans and creatinine (GAGs/Cr) and decreased activity of iduronate-2-sulfatase (IDS) were found. Whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed a hemizygous c.593 A > C (p.D198A) variant of the IDS gene originated from his mother. Furthermore, high urea GAGs/Cr, decreased activity of IDS, and a similar mutation were also noted in his younger brother (case 2, 1.5 years old), who had slight coarse facies and a lot of Mongolian spots. Moreover, the MPS II screen found that a distantly related cousin of case 1 (case 3, 7-month-old) had high urea GAGs/Cr and decreased activity of IDS. It was notable that WES analysis revealed a novel hemizygous c.1403G > A (p.Arg468Gln) variant in the IDS gene, which was de novo and distinct from cases 1 and 2.ConclusionIn children with coarse faces, joint contracture, Mongolian spots, or hirsute, MPS should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Two completely different mutations may independently exist in a family. Hence, only the known mutation verified in family members may result in a missed diagnosis, urea GAGs and IDS activity measurement may reduce this condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06630-y.

Introduction

Mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS), a significant kind of lysosomal storage disorder (LSD), is a group disease caused by mutations in hydrolytic enzymes within lysosomes that degrade glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in human cells. These mutations result in the loss of enzyme activity, preventing the degradation and metabolism of GAGs, which ultimately leads to their accumulation in the body [1, 2]. MPSs can be divided into 8 types including types I, II, III, IV, VI, VII, IX, and X [1, 3, 4]. The incidence, clinical features, and accumulated GAGs of different types were various [1, 2].

MPS II (OMIM 309900), also refer Hunter syndrome, is caused by pathogenic variants in the IDS gene (OMIM 300823) located on chromosome Xq28. This disorder presents with progressive multi-organ dysfunction, including coarse facial features, dysostosis multiplex, recurrent respiratory infections, organomegaly, hernias, Mongolian spots, short stature, cardiovascular disease, and hearing impairment [5–7]. The incidence of MPS II varies by region and ethnicity. Although MPS I or MPS III are the common type in Europe and North America [2], MPS II is the most common type of MPS in East Asia (including China, Japan, and South Korea) and Brazil, with an incidence of (0.48–0.84)/100,000 live births, accounting for 30%-55% of all diagnosed MPS cases [8–11]. MPS II is the only X-linked recessive genetic disease due to IDS gene mutation while the other types are autosomal recessive inheritance. The majority of patients are male, although a small number of female cases have been reported, typically due to nonrandom inactivation of the paternal X chromosome [12–14]. Usually, the patients within a family were inherited from the "same mother". Herein, we reported 3 pediatric patients early diagnosed with MPS II from different origins in one family to emphasize the diagnosis of similar conditions.

Case presentation

Case 1 was a 4-year and 7-month-old boy (born in February 2020), who was referred to our unit in October 2024 for evaluation of "inability to fully extend his fingers for over 2 years". He was the first child (G1P1) of healthy, non-consanguineous parents. He was delivered naturally at 40 weeks of gestation with an unremarkable prenatal and perinatal history. His birth weight and length were 3.4 kg and 50 cm, respectively. His developmental (e.g., start speaking, walk alone, height and weight) milestones were unremarkable. He achieved stable head control at 3 months, could sit independently at 7 months, and began walking independently around 1 year old. He could run and jump currently but exhibits mild out-toeing gait. After diagnosis, he completed a 6-minute walk test with a result of 397 meters. He demonstrates stable grasping ability. At 8 months, he started saying "dad" and "mom", began using words at 1.5 years old, and progressed to speaking short sentences by age 2. He attended kindergarten normally. He could complete simple communication, and had age-appropriate social skills. However, his parents noticed that he was unable to fully extend his fingers when he was about 2 years old. Snoring was also observed since 3 years old. Unfortunately, the cause and diagnosis remained unclear after consulting the Department of Pediatrics, Orthopedics, and Neurology in a local hospital.

Physical examination showed a height of 107 cm (about P50), weight of 20 kg, and head circumference of 52.5 (over P50). Dense eyebrows and hair, prominent forehead, coarse facies, collapsed nose bridge, hypertelorism, big ears, full lips, short neck, and pectus excavatum were noted. The liver was palped about 2 cm under the rib margin while the spleen was not palpable in the subcostal region. Notably, a lot of large Mongolian spots were observed in the back and hips (Fig. 1A). Hypertrichosis was prominent, especially on the posterior neck and back. Short fingers and finger-joint contractures were found on both hands (Fig. 1B). Mild knee-joint contracture and X-sharped legs were also observed. No visual or hearing impairments were detected. Auscultation of the heart and lungs revealed no murmurs or abnormal sounds. There was no evidence of kyphosis, hernias, or lymphadenopathy. His neurodevelopmental history reported no abnormalities in gross motor, fine motor, or social milestones. Language development was essentially normal: at the time of presentation, the child was able to speak in clear, complete sentences, had age-appropriate communication skills, and was doing well in kindergarten, with no specific learning difficulties identified at that time. Unfortunately, no standardized neurodevelopmental assessment (e.g., Gesell Developmental Scale) had been performed. Neurological examination showed the child to be alert and cooperative, with a complete gross test of cranial nerve function (visual tracking, facial activity, normal gross hearing), normal generalized muscle tone, and normal muscle strength in all limbs, except for a bilateral limitation of active extension of the finger joints. Ataxia was well accomplished at an age-appropriate level, with slightly slower but rhythmic rotation movements. Deep tendon reflexes were symmetrical and normally active (2+), and gait was stable with mild knee valgus. No abnormal motor or sensory deficits were noted.

The ratio of urinary GAGs and creatinine (GAGs/Cr) was 65.67 mg/mmol (normal range, < 13.2) measured by dimethyl methylene blue (DMB). The complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, thyroid function tests, and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) were all within normal range.

Based on the findings of coarse facial features, joint contracture, elevated urinary GAGs levels, and reduced IDS activity, MPS II was considered. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed and identified a hemizygous c.593 A > C variant in exon 5 of the IDS gene (NM_000202.8). This variant was also detected in his mother, causing an aspartic acid replaced by alanine (p.Asp198Ala). (Fig. 3) This variant was a missense variant and had not been reported in the literature or the large population frequency database like gnomAD. A search of the DECIPHER database showed that the IDS gene had a pLI value of 1.0 and a LOEUF value of 0.18, which supported a single-dose sensitivity gene and was annotated as 'X-chromosome hemizygous gene product deletion associated with Mucopolysaccharide Storage Disease Type II'. Protein function prediction by SIFT, Polyphen, and MutationTaster suggested that it was a 'high hazard'. The ClinVar database did not contain this variant, but did contain c.593 A > G (p.D198G) at the same amino acid position [15], which was annotated as 'Likely Pathogenic'. Based on the available evidence, this variant was defined as a suspected pathogenic variant according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria (Fig. 4). The diagnosis of MPS II was confirmed. Following confirmation of the diagnosis, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching was performed, and the patient is currently awaiting hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

Case 2 was a 1-year and 6-month-old boy (born in April 2023) and the younger brother of case 1. He was born at full term with a birth weight of 3.65 kg and a birth length of about 50 cm, respectively. His global developmental milestones were normal. He achieved stable head control at 3 months, could sit at 8 months, and began walking independently at 1 year old. His walking posture showed no significant abnormalities. He started saying "dad" and "mom" at 8 months old and was able to use two-word phrases with ease. Fortunately, the parents brought this patient together when they brought Case 1 to our clinic. We suggested a urea GAGs screen as the presence of mild coarse facial features.

Case 2 showed a height of 88 cm (about P50) and a weight of 11.5 kg. Dense eyebrows and hair, slight coarse facies, collapsed nose bridge, hypertelorism, big ears, full lips, and short neck were also observed. The liver was palped about 1–2 cm under the rib margin. Mongolian spots, which were larger and darker in color compared to his brother, were also noted on the back and hips (Fig. 1C). Hypertrichosis and short fingers were also noted without joint contracture. There was no murmur on the auscultation of the heart and lungs. His overall developmental milestones were normal. At the time of presentation, he was able to speak approximately 2-word vocabulary, understand simple commands, and play appropriately for his age. Standardised assessments (e.g. Bayley Scale) were not yet available. A brief neurological examination found him to be alert and able to interact. Cranial nerves were grossly normal. Gross tests of muscle tone and strength were normal, and he was able to walk independently with no obvious gait abnormalities.

Chest X-ray showed normal in Nov. 2023 (Fig. 2E). Urinary GAGs/Cr was 116.14 mg/mmol (normal range, < 17.3) by DMB and the activity of IDS was 1.16 nmol/4hr/mg protein, as shown in Table 1. ECG and echocardiographic measurements were all normal. Blood cell counts, and liver and kidney function were also within normal ranges. Family verification of the c.593 A > C variant of the IDS gene was performed for this patient and his aunt. This variant was confirmed in this patient but was absent in his aunt (Fig. 4F and G). The diagnosis of MPS II was confirmed and he is waiting for HSCT as well.

Case 3 was a 7-month-boy (born in March 2024). He was a distantly related cousin of case 1, and his grandfather was the brother of the maternal grandmother of case 1 (Fig. 5). He was G1P1 of healthy and non-consanguineous parents. He was born naturally at 39 weeks of gestation with a birth weight of 3.65 kg and a birth length of about 50 cm. His global developmental milestones were also normal till now. He achieved stable head control at 2 months and could sit independently by 6 months. At that time, he had not yet begun to crawl. He could respond to his name but did not yet consciously say "mom" or "dad". Fortunately, the parents requested screening for MPS II as the diagnosis of cases 1 and 2.

Physical examination showed a height of 76 cm (over P50) and weight of 10.3 kg. Multiple Mongolian spots were observed on the back, which were lighter in color than those in cases 1 and 2 (Fig. 1D). The liver was palped about 2 cm below the rib margin without a palpable spleen. Moreover, a unilateral inguinal hernia was noted. No dysmorphic features such as dense eyebrows, coarse facial features, hypertelorism, full lips, short neck, hypertrichosis, or joint contractures were observed. There was no murmur on auscultation of the heart and lungs, also no obvious visual or hearing impairment. His developmental milestones were reported to be normal: he could sit supported, reach for objects, babble, smile socially, and respond to his name. A brief neurological examination found him to be alert and responsive. Gross cranial nerve testing was intact. Muscle tone, muscle strength, and primitive reflexes were age-appropriate.

Urinary GAGs/Cr was 161.33 mg/mmol (normal range, < 23.3), and the activity of IDS was 1.23 nmol/4hr/mg protein (Table 1). Blood cell counts, liver and kidney function and echocardiography were all normal. Although a chimera status in his grandfather and a hemizygote in his mother was initially speculated based on the family history, urea GAGs, and activity of IDS, WES was performed to confirm this speculation and also exclude other conditions. It was notable that a novel hemizygous variant in the IDS gene, distinct from the mutation found in cases 1 and 2, was detected. This variant, c.1403G > A in exon 9(Fig. 3), results in a missense mutation (p.Arg468Gln) and was classified as pathogenic according to the ACMG criteria. The overall incidence of this variant in gnomAD was 8.272e-7. The REVEL score was 0.952 and the PolyPhen score was 0.999, suggesting 'highly pathogenic'. This variant had been reported in several patients with MPS II [16]. In addition, other variants in amino acid residues at the same site (p.Arg468Gly, p.Arg468Leu, p.Arg468Trp, and p.Arg468Pro), had also been reported in patients with MPS II [17]. This mutation was a de novo mutation and was not found in his father or mother (Fig. 6). The diagnosis of MPS II was confirmed and he was waiting for HSCT as well.

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) was administered for case 1 while HLA typing for HSCT was performed for all 3 cases. Genetic counseling was also provided. 50% of the sons were affected and 50% of the daughters were carriers when cases 1 and 2' mother (c.593A > C carriers) reproduced; Case 3 parents were tested and confirmed that neither of them carried the c.1403G > A variant, but there was a very low likelihood of gonadal chimerism (empirical risk < 1%), and the overall risk of reproduction was close to the population background level. Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) was recommended for all families: preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic (PGT-M) was required for cases 1 and 2' parents to block the transmission of disease-causing genes, and PGT-SR (gender screening) was preferred for cases 3's parents to transfer a female embryo to avoid the theoretical risk or to verify the health of the fetus in conjunction with prenatal diagnostics (chorionic villus/amniotic fluid testing). The final decision was made on the basis of the family’s wishes and technical feasibility.

Discussion

The IDS gene contains 9 exons and encodes IDS, a 550-amino acid enzyme that participates in the first step of the degradation of dermatan sulfate (DS) and heparan sulfate (HS) in lysosomes [18]. IDS is widely expressed in various tissues of the human body, with the highest expression level in the brain [19]. Pathogenic variants in the IDS gene cause reduced or absent enzyme activity, preventing the breakdown of GAGs and leading to their gradual accumulation in lysosomes [1, 2]. Key indicators of efficacy assessment include reductions in urinary glycosaminoglycan (uGAG) levels, reductions in visceral organ volume, and motor function test results such as the six-minute walk test [20]. Studies had shown that patients who undergo HSCT at an early stage (e.g., less than 5 years of age) had higher scores on the ability to perform activities of daily living; similarly, more significant clinical benefits were observed when ERT was initiated at an early stage of the disease [21-25]. However, it was very difficult to early diagnose MPS II without multiple organ dysfunctions (e.g., coarse facial features, organomegaly, hernias, Mongolian spots, hearing impairment, joint contracture, intellectual and behavioral regression) [26]. For example, in Case 1, the diagnosis was only confirmed after the presentation of coarse facial features and joint contractures, despite the early presence of Mongolian spots and hypertrichosis. Hence, in children with coarse facial features and joint contracture, MPS should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Moreover, in children with other suggestive signs such as Mongolian spots and hirsute, screening tests for MPS (e.g., urinary GAGs analysis) should also be suggested.

Fortunately, case 2 and case 3 were diagnosed as less than 2 years old following the confirmation of case 1. Notably, the mutations among the 3 cases had different origins. The c.593A>C variant in cases 1 and 2 was inherited from their mother. Although it was regarded as 'Likely Pathogenic' according to ACMG criteria, the clinical feature, elevated urinary GAGs levels, and decreased activity of IDS in both cases 1 and 2 suggest its pathogenicity. Unlike our previous suspicion, the c.1403G>A mutation of case 3 was de novo, not originated from his mother or maternal grandfather. It was completely different from cases 1 and 2, which highlighted the possibility of two distinct mutations independently existing within the same family. Only the known mutation verified in family members, but not WES, may result in a missed diagnosis. However, although WES can provide comprehensive genetic information, its single-sample cost is as high as approximately $1,000. In the initial screening, biochemical screening has an economic advantage. The cost of urine GAGs/Cr combined with IDS activity detection is less than $100. For family members with abnormal biochemical screening results, conducting WES can reduce the rate of missed diagnosis while controlling the screening cost, which is conducive to early diagnosis and treatment and reducing long-term medical costs. It should be noted that female members of the family, even if biochemical screening is negative, should also be aware of the possibility of being a carrier and X-chromosome inactivation skewing.

Mutations in the IDS gene are highly heterogeneous, with more than half of the mutations (57.4%) reported in only one family [5]. The c.593A>C variant in cases 1 and 2 was the first reported and was located in exon 5, which is not a common mutation site, but other variants at this amino acid site are associated with milder phenotypes [15]. The c.1403G>A mutation in case 3 (which was usually considered associated with severe phenotypes) was the second most common mutation worldwide[5, 27]. However, the clinical presentation of MPS II is highly heterogeneous. With age, progressive deposition of glycosaminoglycans in the skeleton and nervous system leads to a gradual worsening of clinical manifestations. Data from a follow-up cohort of Chinese children showed that cognitive regression usually occurred before 6 years of age (94.3%) [28]. Case 1 (4.5 years old) could communicate normally and attended kindergarten, but had significant skeletal deformities, while the younger cases 2 (1.5 years old) and 3 (7 months old) exhibited relatively mild phenotypes. This difference was likely attributable to the significant age gap. As an X-linked recessive disorder, MPS II has a low incidence in females. This may be due to random X-chromosome inactivation, which renders most female carriers of pathogenic mutations either asymptomatic or present with only mild symptoms[29]. Conversely, since males require only a single pathogenic mutation to cause the disease due to lack of allelic compensation, de novo mutations contribute significantly to X-linked disorders[30]. Approximately 22.5% of MPS II cases involved de novo mutations in the IDS gene[31].

It was reported that the c.1122C>T (p.Gly374Gly), c.1472C>A (p.Ser491Tyr), and codon 468 may be common mutation sites in China [28, 32, 33]. Small in-frame deletions or insertions were also reported. It was also notable that there is a pseudogene of the IDS gene, named IDS pseudogene (IDSP1), near the IDS gene. Intrachromosomal homologous recombination occurring between the homologous regions of the IDS gene and IDSP1 gene was also reported[18, 34, 35]. Hence, advanced diagnostic techniques such as multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), long-range PCR, or RNA sequencing, may be helpful for the diagnosis in some patients[18, 36-38].

Intravenous infusion of recombinant IDS enzymes can significantly reduce urinary GAGs, decrease the volume of the liver and spleen, and improve functional outcomes such as the 6-minute walking test score, growth, and lung function [21, 22, 39]. However, its benefits are limited in addressing skeletal abnormalities, hearing loss, cardiac valve disease, and neurological symptoms (as recombinant IDS cannot cross the blood-brain barrier). The annual cost of nearly one million RMB is also a heavy burden for families [40]. HSCT can cause enzyme-producing donor cells to enter the blood circulation. These cells produce the enzyme and correct substrate accumulation. Moreover, these cells can cross the blood-brain barrier to produce enzymes in the brain, improving neurological symptoms[21-25]. Some clinical studies suggest that HSCT was not only more effective in reducing GAG levels but may also improve or stabilize MRI imaging of the brain[41, 42]. HSCT has been listed as one of the therapeutic choices for MPS II in countries such as Japan, China, and Brazil; however, due to the high risks associated with transplantation, its use in countries such as the United States was relatively limited[41] and was more suggested for younger patients with or suspected of several symptoms[25]. Currently, there is no widely accepted international standard for the indication of HSCT and a unified transplantation protocol. Recent research data shown that the seven-year overall survival (OS) rate for MPS II treated with HSCT without irradiation preconditioning regimen could reach 62.5%[43]. For our 3 patients, the parents opted for HSCT, and all three patients are currently awaiting HLA-matched donors. Parental hemi-compatible stem cell transplants are also being considered.

We reported a unique MPS II pedigree. However, due to the short observation period, the therapy outcome of the cases remained difficult to assess, and comprehensive developmental and neurological evaluations could not be performed. Although we speculated that the maternal grandmother of the proband may be a carrier, we were unable to perform genetic verification of the other family members because of financial constraints. Furthermore, as only peripheral blood genetic testing was performed, the possibility of gonadal chimerism could not be excluded. The underlying mechanism and potential familial genetic predisposition of the existence of two independent mutations in one family remained to be investigated.

Conclusion

In summary, in children with coarse facial features, joint contracture, Mongolian spots, or hypertrichosis, MPS should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Completely different mutations may independently exist in a family. Hence, only the known mutation verified in family members may result in a missed diagnosis, screening for MPSII like urinary GAGs analysis and enzyme activity measurement is essential to reduce this condition and improve early detection.
