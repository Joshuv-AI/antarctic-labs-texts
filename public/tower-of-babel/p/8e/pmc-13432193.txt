Positively Charged Polymer‐Brush MOFs for Large‐Area, Pressure‐Resistant Gas Separation Membranes
Authors: Yi Yang, Ye Yuan, Yuxiu Sun, Fei Shi, Chenyang Song, Weifan Wang, Qinghua Li, Menglong Sheng, Zhihua Qiao, Jingwei Hou, Anthony K Cheetham, Zhi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTScalable fabrication of high‐performance gas separation membranes remains a major challenge for energy‐efficient gas purification. Industrial translation of pressure‐resistant mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) is largely impeded by the dispersion instability of nanofillers under rapid, non‐equilibrium manufacturing conditions, which leads to uncontrollable aggregation and interfacial defects during processing. Here, we present a universal “pre‐occupation and post‐activation” strategy to construct positively charged polymer‐brush metal–organic frameworks (MOFs). This approach endows the fillers with a dual‐stabilization mechanism: electro‐steric effects ensure static dispersion stability, while a hydrogen‐bonding‐driven interfacial interlocking mechanism maintains stability during dynamic processing. Together, these mechanisms synergistically facilitate the seamless integration of nanofillers within ultrathin selective layers. This design allows roll‐to‐roll production of 1 m‐wide, pressure‐resistant mixed matrix composite membranes (MMCMs) with tunable CO2 separation performance. The resulting MMCMs deliver outstanding CO2/CH4 and CO2/N2 separation under industrially relevant pressures, reducing the required membrane area by more than an order of magnitude compared with laboratory‐scale membranes. A spiral‐wound module with an effective area of 0.4–2 m2 further validates the scalability and operational robustness. This work overcomes a long‐standing barrier in MMM processing, marking a significant step toward industrial implementation of MOF‐based, energy‐efficient gas separation technologies.Keywords: CO2 separation, metal‐organic frameworks, mixed matrix composite membranes, roll‐to‐roll, scale‐up fabrication

Introduction

Chemical separations consume nearly 50% of global industrial energy [1, 2]. Membrane technology offers a more energy‐efficient alternative to conventional methods like cryogenic distillation and adsorption, with advantages in flexibility and compact design [3, 4]. Despite its promise, gas separation membrane remains underused compared to their liquid separation counterparts, largely because scalable, energy‐efficient manufacturing technologies are lacking. Moreover, there is an urgent need for high‐pressure‐resistant gas separation membranes, as operating under moderate‐to‐high pressure reduces capital costs and spatial needs for membrane processes [5, 6]. Operating at pressure also enhances mass transfer and improves compatibility with other process units, helping to reduce energy losses from recompression. Thus, developing membranes that combine high‐permeance with pressure‐stability is essential for cost‐effective, energy‐efficient gas separation.

Polymer membranes are widely used in gas separation due to their ease of processing, but their performance and stability remain limited [7, 8, 9, 10]. A common approach is to embed high‐performance microporous nanofillers into a polymer matrix to form mixed matrix membranes (MMMs), or more preferably mixed matrix composite membranes (MMCMs), where a thin mixed matrix separation layer (100–200 nm) is coated on top of a substrate to maximize the membrane permeation [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. As Baker et al. noted, industrial‐scale gas separations require membrane areas ranging from thousands to millions of square meters [19]. While recent advances have demonstrated proof‐of‐concept MMCMs, the largest reported membrane area to date is only about several thousand square centimeters [15, 16], far short of what is needed for practical deployment. One of the central challenges in the large‐scale production of gas separation MMCMs lies in the fundamental mismatch between the static dispersion stability of nanofillers and the rapid kinetics of membrane formation during processing [20, 21, 22]. Specifically, under the mild, “quasi‐equilibrium” conditions typical of laboratory settings, achieving a uniform filler distribution in the membrane usually only requires maintaining static dispersion; this has led to the critical influence of membrane‐forming kinetics being long overlooked. However, during the rapid, non‐equilibrium dynamics inherent to industrial manufacturing, the dispersion equilibrium established under static conditions is completely disrupted. This triggers a sharp deterioration in filler behavior, leading to uncontrollable aggregation and multi‐scale interfacial defects—a systemic failure conceptualized here as “non‐equilibrium processing‐induced filler destabilization” [23]. During industrial coating, intense convective disturbances, such as capillary and Marangoni flows driven by rapid solvent evaporation, are far more violent than those in static casting [24, 25, 26]. These convective effects not only exacerbate filler clustering but also induce localized stress concentrations that introduce non‐selective defects, fundamentally undermining the reliable continuous membrane preparation. This helps to explain why many advanced small‐scale mixed matrix membrane technologies remain difficult to translate into large‐scale, continuous fabrication processes.

The key to addressing this challenge lies in developing high‐performance nanofillers with dual static and dynamic stabilities that are compatible with rapid, non‐equilibrium continuous processing. We therefore propose a preoccupation and post‐activation (POPA) strategy for the structural design and modification of nanofillers, enabling their seamless integration into thin separation layers under industrial membrane fabrication conditions. Metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs) are considered as one of the most promising nanofiller candidates featuring tailorable structures and facile functionalization. Here, we applied the POPA strategy to the reported MOF platform, engineering a novel class of MOF nanofillers integrating three critical features: a highly positively charged framework, free‐stretching surface‐grafted polymer brushes, and preserved porous structure. This highly positively charged polymer‐brush MOF effectively addresses the persistent issue of pore blockage post‐modifications. Moreover, it confers exceptional static dispersion stability via an electro‐steric effect, thereby achieving a uniform and stable dispersion of high‐performance nanofillers in the coating solution. More critically, when subjected to intense convective disturbances caused by rapid solvent evaporation under industrial manufacturing conditions, the free‐stretching polymer brushes on the MOF surface maintain their dynamic stability through a hydrogen bond‐driven interfacial interlocking mechanism. This mechanism enables self‐ adaptive counteraction against various aggregation‐inducing forces, ensuring the continuous production of large‐area, pressure‐resistant MOF‐based MMCMs. Based on this strategy, we achieved the roll‐to‐roll production of 1‐meter‐wide, pressure‐resistant MMCMs with tunable CO2 separation performance for the first time. These MMCMs exhibit exceptional performance for natural gas decarbonation and post‐combustion CO2 capture under industrially relevant pressures. Process simulations demonstrate that our scaled‐up MMCMs significantly reduce the required membrane area by more than an order of magnitude compared to lab‐scale membranes with similar separation targets. Furthermore, the successful fabrication and performance validation of a spiral‐wound module with an effective area of approximately 0.4–2 m2 further demonstrates its promising potential for industrial application. Beyond achieving industrial‐scale continuous fabrication of pressure‐resistant gas separation MMCMs, this work represents a fundamental paradigm shift from laboratory‐scale static batch fabrication to industrial dynamic continuous manufacturing. This work breaks through key challenges in mixed matrix membrane scalable processing, representing a significant advancement in transitioning MOF‐based, energy‐efficient membrane separation technologies from laboratory innovation toward practical application.

Results and Discussion

Specifically, we used MIP‐202 (MIP stands for the Materials of the Institute of Porous Materials, Paris), a 12‐connected Zr6(µ3‐O)4(µ3‐OH)4(COO−)12 cluster coordinated with L‐aspartic acid, as a representative MOF for the fabrication of the membrane nanofillers. The POPA strategy was applied to introduce the highly positively charged surface chains without compromising the micropore accessibility (Figure 1a,b). MIP‐202 synthesis was modulated with HCl to fully protonate the ‐NH2 groups within its channels, forming ‐NH3 +Cl− complexes (the sample was named MIP‐202P, with P standing for preoccupation). The MOF was then surface functionalized with a silane coupling agent (KH‐560) and linear polyvinylamine (PVAm) (the sample was named MIP‐202P‐KP, with KP standing for modification using KH‐560 and PVAm). Pre‐occupied ‐NH3 +Cl− clusters direct PVAm chains to the external MOF surface, preventing pore blockage. Such a structure allows the subsequent deprotonation treatment using anion exchange resins to restore the chemical activity of amine groups by converting ‐NH3 +Cl− back to ‐NH2 within the channels (the sample was named MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA, with PA standing for post‐activation). This step also reopens the internal pores of the framework, enhancing pore accessibility. In addition, increased positive charge density promotes the extension of surface‐grafted polymer chains through electrostatic repulsion, fully exposing the interaction sites, allowing subsequent interlocking between MOF and the polymer matrix.

Powder X‐ray Diffraction (PXRD) (Figure 2a) confirms that the MOF retains its crystalline integrity throughout the synthesis process. Complementary techniques, including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR) (Figure 2b) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Figures S1 and S2), confirm the conversion of ‐NH3 +Cl− to ‐NH2 within the MOF channels and the successful modification with PVAm [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Ar adsorption isotherms at 87 K, along with the Brunauer‐Emmett‐Teller (BET) surface area and pore volumes calculations, were normalized to the MOF mass without polymer loading, as determined from TGA profiles (Figures S3–S5 and Table S1). The MOF modification has minimal impact on the normalized pore volume and results in only a slight reduction in the most probable micropore size (Figure 2c), indicating that protonated species effectively prevent pore blockage and ensure polymer modification primarily at the MOF surface. Deprotonation restores amine functionality, leading to a significant increase in micropore volume, micropore size, and CO2 adsorption capacity (Figure S6). MOFs processed via POPA exhibit a progressive increase in zeta potential (from −8.4 mV to +45.5 mV) and a concurrent decrease in Z‐average size (Figure 2d). This shift is primarily attributable to the attenuated Cl‐ screening effect and the opened porous environment, which fully exposes the intrinsic electropositivity of ‐NH2 sites. Such characteristics significantly enhance the colloidal stability in aqueous media (Figure S7) and promote uniform nanoparticle distribution during membrane manufacturing.

Rheological analysis reveals that the POPA method induced an exponential increase in both the yield point (G′/G″ crossover) and viscoelastic moduli of the MOFs (Figure 2e). This reinforcement arises not only from the polymeric functionalization but more significantly from the increasing positive charge density, which promotes polymer chain extension and enhanced interparticle brush‐brush interactions [35]. The high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) image (Figures 2f; Figure S8) further demonstrates changes in the polymer shell state during the POPA process. Combined with rheological data, these observations establish a direct intrinsic relationship between increased positive charge and the extension of polymer brushes. In addition, structural information from selected‐area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns further supports the preservation of the MOF crystallinity during the POPA process. To further probe the conformation of surface polymer chains under solvated conditions, we employed liquid‐phase atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Figure S9) and quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM‐D) (Figure S10). Salt‐sensitive long‐range repulsive forces captured by AFM force‐separation curves, along with reversible pH‐responsive swelling‐deswelling transitions monitored via QCM‐D, collectively substantiate the highly extended conformation and dynamic responsiveness of the grafted polymers in solution [36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. These results converge with our rheological and microscopic analyses to form a comprehensive chain of evidence—bridging microscopic morphology and solution‐state behavior—that confirms the successful engineering of extended polymer‐brush architectures on the MOF surfaces.

An industrial‐scale continuous roll‐to‐roll production line for MMCMs (Figure 3) was developed, integrating two cycles of dip‐coating, blade‐coating, and thermally driven interfacial self‐assembly units for fabricating the gutter layer and separation layer, respectively. Specifically, the commercial Polysulfone (PSf) substrate was first coated on one side in the first coating trough containing pre‐crosslinked polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), which served to partially eliminate micrometer‐scale thickness fluctuations and underlying defects, while also reducing the pore penetration of the subsequent mixed matrix coating solution (Figure S11). Guided by rollers, the substrate with the PDMS‐coated gutter layer was then re‐immersed on the same side into the mixed matrix coating solution containing uniformly dispersed MOFs, and directed into a drying tunnel for rapid formation, with the entire process completed in only approximately 2 min.

Schematic illustration of the industrial‐scale roll‐to‐roll continuous production line for MMCMs based on the integrated dip‐blade coating process.

The thermal‐driven uniform formation of the mixed matrix coating is more challenging than that of the purely polymeric PDMS gutter layer, as it requires overcoming instability induced by intense fluid shear and dynamic interfacial conditions under non‐equilibrium processing to achieve highly matched integration of nanofillers within the polymer matrix. After a wet mixed matrix coating of specific thickness is applied via the second coating unit, the substrate is transferred to a drying tunnel for final consolidation. Initially, during the solvent‐retention stage (Figure 4a(i)), MOFs with highly positively charged polymer brushes are uniformly and stably dispersed in the coating solution through electro‐steric effect. Subsequently, during the rapid solvent evaporation stage (Figure 4a(ii)), hydrogen bonding between the polymer brushes on the MOF surface and the surrounding polymer matrix enhances molecular‐level entanglement. This strong interlocking at the interface helps stabilize the membrane structure by dissipating energy and counteracting dynamic instabilities that arise from the redistribution of multiple forces–including gravity and flow fields–mediated by intrachain entropic elasticity and dynamic damping from polymer entanglement. Through this mechanism, the highly interlocked structure, driven by the dense hydrogen bonding interaction between MOFs and matrix polymer via high site accessibility of flexible polymer brushes, effectively stabilizes the MOFs and suppresses their aggregation during continuous MMCM fabrication. Finally, during the solidification stage (Figure 4a(iii)), MOFs establish tightly bound interfaces with the polymer matrix while maintaining a uniform distribution within the thin mixed matrix separation layer. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure 4b; Figure S12), TEM (Figure 4c; Figure S13), FT‐IR (Figure S14a), and XRD (Figure S14b) measurements confirm the uniform distribution of crystalline MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA nanoparticles within the separation layer. Positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) data (Figure 4d; Table S2) show that micropore sizes corresponding to the third and fourth lifetimes of MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA‐PVAm membrane align with the pure polymer and intrinsic MOF pores, respectively, indicating excellent interfacial compatibility and well‐preserved pore structure of the MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA. This structural integrity arises from hydrogen bond‐driven molecular interlocking between the extended polymer brushes on the MOF surface and the polymer matrix, ensuring excellent interfacial compatibility and preventing polymer intrusion into MOF channels during MMCM separation layer formation. The observed redshift in amine‐specific infrared absorption peaks of the mixed matrix membrane, coupled with pronounced temporal viscosity evolution of the coating solution during solvent evaporation, collectively provides robust evidence for the formation of hydrogen‐bonding networks (Figures S14a and S15). These findings are further corroborated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figure S16), which reveal a high degree of temporal synchronization between the increasing density of hydrogen bonds and the physical interpenetration of polymer chains, identifying these synergistic interactions as the primary kinetic driver for the interfacial interlocking. Furthermore, the interfacial enhancement driven by this molecular interlock is directly reflected in the macroscopic thermo‑mechanical properties of the composite. Dynamic scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Figure 4e; Figure S17) and dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) (Figure 4f; Figures S18 and S19) show a significant increase in both the glass‑transition temperature (Tg ) and the mechanical strength of the membrane. This improvement, resulting from the strengthened interfacial entanglement between POPA‑processed MOF nanoparticles and the polymer matrix, provides a solid foundation for stable membrane operation under medium‑ to high‑pressure conditions.

Utilizing this production line, we achieved continuous fabrication of 1 m‐wide MMCMs (Figure S20). Consistent separation layer thickness and stable CO2/CH4 and CO2/N2 separation performances at 12 randomly sampled locations across both transverse and longitudinal directions confirm the exceptional scalability and uniformity of the MMCMs under industrial membrane‐fabrication conditions (Figure 5a,b; Figures S21 and S22a). Furthermore, the highly concentrated performance distributions and minimal regional deviations observed among 72 membrane samples harvested from 6 independent production batches underscore the rigorous consistency and reproducibility of this large‐scale manufacturing process (Figures S23 and S24). The fabricated membranes exhibit a characteristic decline in performance with increasing pressure—a hallmark of carrier saturation in facilitated transport mechanisms [32]. Nevertheless, they maintain superior pressure‐resistant CO2 separation performance and anti‐plasticization performance (Figures S25 and S26) across a broad pressure range, as demonstrated by pressure‐cycling tests up to 2.0 MPa (Figure 5c; Figures S22b, S27–S29). More importantly, the MMCMs exhibited remarkable separation stability even after 168 h of continuous operation under a high pressure of 4.0 MPa (Figures S30–S32). This performance arises from the hydrogen bonding‐induced interlocking structure, which ensures stress delocalization at the interface under high feed pressures. For comparison, non‐modified MOFs exhibit pronounced aggregation during rapid membrane formation, resulting in membranes that are prone to extensive, non‐selective defect formation (Figures S12 and S33). Furthermore, we synthesized a series of MOFs via direct polymer modification without the pore preoccupation process, followed by post‐activation. Structural characterization of the MOFs and separation performance measurements of the resulting MMCMs clearly demonstrate the critical importance of the synergistic interplay between pore preoccupation and post‐activation (Figures S34–S46). Maxwell model analysis (Figure 5d; Figures S47–S53) confirms that the POPA‐processed MOF materials enhance gas permselectivity by maintaining unobstructed pore channels for rapid gas transport while creating an amine‐rich pore environment for CO2‐selective adsorption. Notably, this strategy is also applicable to other MOF materials, such as MIP‐206‐NH2 and Tb‐MOF76‐NH2 (Figures S54 and S55). Compared to the state‐of‐the‐art membranes, which are mostly fabricated in lab settings (Figure 5e; Table S3), our industrially fabricated membrane demonstrates superior CO2 permeance at medium‐to‐high operational pressures, while maintaining competitive selectivity.

To assess the industrial viability of the manufactured membranes, a process simulation was performed for a natural gas decarbonization system processing 35 million standard cubic feet per day (MMSCFD) of feed gas (Figure S56, Tables S4 and S5). As illustrated in Figure 5f, our pressure‐resistant MMCMs significantly reduce the required membrane area while maintaining low specific energy consumption. Furthermore, these membranes demonstrate exceptional performance in post‐combustion carbon capture scenarios (Figures S22c,d, S57 and Tables S6–S8), offering superior fixed investment‐to‐energy consumption trade‐offs compared to conventional polymeric membranes. ​Additionally, membrane modules are critical for deploying membrane technology. Spiral‐wound modules, favored for their straightforward fabrication and high achievable performance, are extensively utilized across diverse industrial sectors, including water treatment and gas separation [42, 43, 44]. Leveraging a previously established fabrication technique [45], we produced the spiral‐wound module with an effective membrane area of 0.4–2 m2 (Figure 5g; Figure S58). While concentration polarization and stage‐cut effects [45] compromised both permeance and selectivity by approximately 35%, the module still maintained a robust performance level under high pressures and exhibited exceptional stability during continuous operation (Figure 5h; Figures S59–S63). These results underscore the promising potential of these MMCMs for practical implementation.

Conclusion

In summary, to address the long‐standing challenge of scaling up mixed matrix membranes—rooted in the fundamental mismatch between the static dispersion stability of nanofillers and the rapid kinetics of membrane formation during processing—we developed a versatile POPA strategy. This approach enabled the engineering of a novel class of MOF nanofillers featuring a highly positively charged framework, free‐stretching surface‐grafted polymer brushes, and preserved porous structure. Leveraging this dual‐stabilization MOF, we achieved the first stable industrial‐scale continuous production of 1‐meter‐wide MMCMs, showcasing the pressure‐resistant CO2 separation. Furthermore, the successful fabrication and performance validation of spiral‐wound gas separation membrane modules underscore their practical applicability. Beyond achieving industrial‐scale continuous fabrication of pressure‐resistant gas separation MMCMs, this work represents a fundamental paradigm shift from laboratory‐scale static batch fabrication to industrial dynamic continuous manufacturing. By resolving the critical bottleneck in scalable processing, it provides a viable solution for efficient energy gas purification and post‐combustion carbon capture. Importantly, it marks a significant advancement in translating MOF‐based gas separation membranes from laboratory research to real‐world applications.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of MOF Particles

Synthesis of MIP‐202P: The synthesis was based on the reported procedures [27, 46] with some modifications. First, a mixture of ZrCl4 (345 g) and L‐AA (420 g) was completely dissolved in 3000 mL of deionized water, and 300 mL of HCl aqueous solution was added. At this point, the solution took on a slight pink color. Second, the solution was transferred to a 5 L MOF automated synthesis device with a jacket‐coil dual temperature control system (Figure S64) and heated at 130°C for 24 h. After the reaction was completed and cooled to room temperature, the precipitate was obtained by vacuum filtration and washed with DI water. Subsequently, the precipitate was immersed in 10 L of EtOH and left to stand for 24 h, during which time the EtOH was renewed every 8 h. Finally, the white powder (Figure S65) was collected by vacuum filtration and dried in a vacuum oven for 8 h at room temperature for subsequent use. A typical single‐batch output was ≈430.7 g, achieving a yield of 89.4%.

Synthesis of MIP‐202P‐KP: The synthesis procedure was optimized based on our previous work [32]. Firstly, MIP‐202P particles (10 g) were dispersed in 100 mL of DI water, sonicated, and stirred in a beaker for 2 h each at room temperature. Subsequently, the excess KH‐560 was added drop by drop to the stirred mixture, followed by the addition of PVAM aqueous solution (2 wt.%). The mixture solution was stirred and sonicated, and then heated at 80°C for 24 h. After the reaction, the suspension of modified nanofillers was recovered by centrifugation, and the unreacted KH‐560 and polymer were removed by washing with DI water and EtOH (3 times). Finally, the MIP‐202P‐KP particles were vacuum‐dried at 25°C for use. A typical single‐batch output was ≈11.0 g, achieving a yield of 92.0% (based on MIP‐202P).

Synthesis of MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA: Firstly, MIP‐202P‐KP particles were loaded into a cellulose filter thimble moistened with DI water. Subsequently, they were placed in a Soxhlet extractor containing DI water and quaternary ammonium anion exchange resin and treated for 3 h at 60°C. At the end of the process, the particles in the cellulose filter thimble were collected, washed, and vacuum‐dried for use. A typical single‐batch output was ≈10.1 g, achieving a recovery of 91.8%.

Synthesis of MIP‐202Ref‐KP‐PA (Ref standing for the virgin MOF synthesis directly followed by the reported method without modification). First, a mixture of ZrCl4 (11.5 g) and L‐AA (14 g) was completely dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water. Then, the solution was transferred to a 500 mL PTFE reactor and heated at 130°C for 24 h. After the reaction was completed and cooled to room temperature, the precipitate was obtained by centrifugation and washed with deionized water. Subsequently, the precipitate was immersed in 1 L of ethanol and left to stand for 24 h, during which time the ethanol was renewed every 8 h. Finally, the white powder (MIP‐202Ref) was collected by centrifugation and dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 8 h. MIP‐202Ref‐KP‐PA, with reference to the synthesis of MIP‐202P‐KP and MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA.

Scale‐Up Membrane Manufacturing

An industrial‐scale production line was independently designed and constructed for preparing large‐area high‐performance MOF‐based mixed matrix composite membranes. Specifically, the production line consists of two sets of independent dip‐blade combined coating units connected in series. The first coating unit was used to prepare the Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) gutter layer, while the second coating unit can be used to coat the separation layer solution on the gutter layer. During operation, the PSf support layer with water contained in the pores was released from the continuous unwinding module with an independent rotatable double‐station switching system. The overall operating line speed was maintained at 5 m/min. Then, the support layer was unifacially immersed in the coating trough filled with PDMS solution under the control of surface tension by the back rollers. The principle of this process was that the PDMS solution cannot penetrate the membrane pores by dissolving in water inside the pores, thereby maintaining overall gas permeability. Then, the support layer dipped with the excess solution was flattened and a controlled thickness at about 200 µm by the blade scraper before being sent to the Drying Tunnel I. The drying tunnels were divided into seven independently adjustable areas, where the temperature and air velocity can be adjusted separately. More specifically, the temperature in Drying Tunnel I was set to gradually rise to 60°C from the room temperature and overall air velocity at 5 m/min. The prepared membrane was named modified polysulfone (mPSf) after drying. The coating operation of the membrane in the second coating unit was similar to that in the first unit, only differing in the type of coating solution (mixed matrix coating solution), wet coating thickness (100 µm), drying temperature (95°C), and air velocity (5 m/min). The dried product was then pulled to the winding unit for subsequent experiments.

The PDMS solution was prepared by pre‐crosslinking and a dilution process [47]. During the pre‐crosslinking process, the silicone rubber prepolymer was mixed with tetraethyl silicate crosslinking agent and dibutyltin dilaurate catalyst at the mass ratio of 5:4:4 and then triggered the dealcoholization crosslinking reaction in a humid environment until the solution viscosity reaches about 600 mPa s. Then the solution was diluted by heptane to a concentration of 0.40 wt.% and stored in a double‐layer cooling kettle at ‐5°C.

The main component of the benchmark mixed matrix solution mentioned above was 0.075 wt.% PVAm, with the addition of 10 ppm SDS as surfactants, 20 ppm PVA as water‐retaining agents, and 5 ppm PAAS as thickeners, respectively, to facilitate the uniform spreading of the water‐based coating solution on the hydrophobic gutter layer surface [48]. The solution needs to be filtrated by membranes with a pore size of 10 microns to remove flocculent substances that precipitate due to the cross‐linking of high molecular weight polymers and prevent depositing on the membrane surface and causing defects. The mixed matrix coating solution was obtained by adding nanofiller dispersion solution dropwise into the pretreated coating solution. The prepared membranes were systematically designated as “MOF‐PVAm/mPSf” according to the specific MOF type incorporated during fabrication. Characterization

A field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan) was used to analyze the morphologies of MOF particles, composite membrane surfaces, and cross‐sections with the accelerating voltage of 3 kV. To obtain the cross‐sectional SEM image, the membrane was cryogenically fractured in liquid nitrogen to preserve its microstructures. High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM, JEM‐F200, JEOL, Japan) assembled with a Gatan Oneview camera was used to analyze the morphology and structure of the MOF particles and their dispersion state in the composite membrane separation layer with the accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Ultrathin sections with a thickness of 70 nm were cut by a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) mode was applied by retrofitting a Gatan model 698 cryo transfer tomography holder to investigate the preservation of MOFs’ crystal structure during the POPA process at 100 K. The structure about the MOF crystal surface corresponding to such a pair of diffraction spots can be inferred by the Bragg equation from the distance between the symmetric electron diffraction spots.

A differential scanning calorimeter (DSC214, Netzsh, Germany) was used to determine the glass transition temperature (Tg) of mixed matrix self‐supporting membranes. The samples were tested at 10°C·min−1 from 30°C to 250°C under the N2 atmosphere. A thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA 550, TA Instruments, USA) was performed to evaluate the polymer loading on the MOFs [35]. The samples were firstly heated to 150°C at the rate of 20°C/min and retained at that temperature for 30 min under an N2 atmosphere to remove the residual solvent. The temperature rose to 800°C at a rate of 20°C/min under an O2 atmosphere and was kept at 800°C for 5 min. The pore structure parameters were calculated via the original Horvath‐Kawazoe method from the Ar adsorption isotherm characterized at 87 K by a specific surface area and pore size distribution analyzer (Autosorb IQ, Quantachrome, USA). The MOFs were loaded into a preweighed special glass tube and heated at 120 °C for 12 h under a dynamic vacuum. High‐pressure CO2, N2, and CH4 adsorption tests of MIP‐202P‐KP‐PA and MIP‐202Ref‐KP‐PA were carried out using a high‐pressure gas sorption analyzer (BSD‐PH, Beishide, China) at 298 K with the pressure range of 0–20 bar. All of the samples were degassed at 120°C for 12 h under dynamic vacuum. The rheology properties of MOFs were performed on a rotational rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar, Austria) with a diameter (25 mm) of cone‐plate geometry by the oscillatory mode with a fixed angular frequency ω of 10 rad/s. The shear strain was set as 0.1%–100%.

Positron annihilation lifetime spectra were employed to detect the free volume size and free volume distribution of mixed matrix materials used to prepare membrane separation layers. A 22Na radioactive source was used as the positron source with a source intensity of about 13 microcuries. For testing, the self‐supporting membrane (prepared by different mixed matrix materials) was folded into a 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 mm block, and two identical samples were sandwiched between the two sides of the 22Na source, forming a typical “sandwich” geometry. The Fabrication Procedure of Spiral‐Wound Membrane Module

Figure S66 illustrates each constituent unit of the spiral‐wound module. The detailed fabrication procedure of the membrane module followed our previous work and was described as follows [36]:

First, the as‐prepared MMCMs were cut to a width matching the module length and folded inward after inserting the feed spacers to prepare membrane leaves. To protect the mixed‐matrix membrane interface from shear forces during the winding process, a highly permeable non‐woven fabric was incorporated as an additional protective spacer between the membrane and the feed spacer. Meanwhile, the inner edges of the folded membranes were sealed with airtight tape to prevent leakage at the creases. Subsequently, the membrane leaves were stacked in sequence with fixed intervals at the folded ends, with permeate spacers inserted between each membrane leaf. Then, the membrane envelopes were prepared by welding the edges between the membrane leaves with polyurethane sealant, ensuring smooth flow of permeate gas only within the channels formed by permeate spacers. Finally, the membrane envelopes were wrapped tightly around the perforated permeate tube with a specific tension and secured externally with tape. Following a 24‐h curing period for the sealant, the membrane module was produced by precision trimming at both ends. After installing Y‐ring seals, the module was loaded into a custom‐made stainless steel membrane housing for testing (Figure S67).

To further visualize this process, a schematic animation of the module fabrication and operation has been provided in Video S3.

Gas Permeation Measurements

The gas permeance measurement in this work was conducted according to the Wicke‐Kallenbach (W‐K) method. The test platform mainly includes a humidification unit, a membrane cell, a pressure control unit, a gas chromatograph, and a constant temperature chamber (Figure S68). The pressure control unit was composed of a pressure transmitter and a back pressure valve, while the humidification unit was composed of a series connected humidification tanks and a dehumidification tank. The humidification tank can humidify the feed gas to the saturation humidity at the corresponding temperature. Multiple water‐blocking plates were welded in the dehumidification tank to prevent liquid water from entering the membrane cell and causing a decrease in the effective membrane area. The feed gas was drawn out from a pre‐configured cylinder and entered the humidification unit, following with the feed side of the membrane cell. After the feed gas passes through the membrane sample, the CO2‐rich gas was collected on the permeate side and carried by He sweep gas into the gas chromatography (Agilent 7890B, USA) for analysis of composition. The effect of back‐diffusion of sweep gas has already been proven as being negligible [51]. The CO2‐poor gas was discharged from the penetration side through a back‐pressure valve. During testing, the stage cut (the ratio of permeate gas flow rate to feed gas flow rate) should not exceed 0.015, so that the feed side CO2 partial pressure was much greater than the permeate side CO2 partial pressure to ensure the membrane was in the ideal state. When three measured consecutive compositions remained unchanged, the membranes were considered to have reached a steady state at this pressure. The average values and standard deviations were calculated from measurements of three separate membranes prepared under the same conditions.

The separation stability of the fabricated membrane toward CO2/N2 and CO2/CH4 mixed gases was systematically examined through a 120‐h continuous test under constant feed pressure (1 MPa). The experimental protocol comprised three consecutive stages: (1) During 0–40 h, the separation stability was assessed using CO2/N2 (15/85 vol%) and CO2/CH4 (10/90 vol%) feed gases; (2) From 40–80 h, simulated flue gas (14.5 vol% CO2, 6.5 vol% O2, 140 ppm SO2 and balanced by N2) and simulated natural gas (1 vol% H2S, 9vol% CO2 and 90vol% CH4) were introduced to evaluate impurity resistance stability under industrially relevant poisoning conditions; (3) In the final 80–120 h phase, reverting to the original CO2/N2 or CO2/CH4 feeds enabled quantification of the membrane's performance recovery capability after exposure to aggressive environments.

The gas separation performance of the spiral‑wound membrane module was evaluated following the procedure described in our previous work [36].
