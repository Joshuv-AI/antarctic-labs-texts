Complementary and alternative therapies in the management of menopausal symptoms: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Eda Yakit AK, Zeynep Ogul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis meta-analysis evaluated the effects of complementary and alternative therapies on menopausal symptoms.Materials and methodsRelevant randomized controlled trials published after 2020 were systematically searched in the ASSIA, CENTRAL, CINAHL, LILACS, PubMed, Embase, and WOS databases. A total of 24 studies were included, and analyses were conducted using RevMan and JASP software. Heterogeneity and publication bias were assessed using various statistical methods. Results Twenty-four randomized controlled trials were synthesized. Evidence from the included studies indicates that complementary and traditional interventions may contribute to improvements in menopausal symptoms; however, effect estimates varied substantially across studies. A random-effects meta-analysis identified an average effect estimate, but substantial heterogeneity (τ2 = 0.362) and a wide 95% prediction interval (−0.228 to 2.340) suggest considerable uncertainty in the magnitude and direction of effects across different settings. Meta-regression analyses showed that larger sample sizes were associated with smaller observed effects (β = −0.005, p = .014), indicating potential small-study effects. Intervention duration significantly influenced outcomes, whereas intervention type, dose, and formulation were not significant moderators. Subgroup analyses suggested beneficial trends for both herbal and non-herbal interventions, although prediction intervals remained wide, reinforcing the variability of findings.ConclusionImprovements were most consistent for hot flashes, while evidence for overall symptom severity and quality of life was less consistent. Although no statistically significant publication bias was detected, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and observed heterogeneity. Integrating evidence-based complementary and alternative approaches into clinical guidelines could enhance holistic women’s healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00404-026-08517-w.Keywords: Complementary medicine, Alternative therapy, Menopause, Meta-analysis, Traditional medicine, Menopausal symptoms

What does this study add to the clinical work?

This systematic review and meta-analysis shows that evidence-based complementary and alternative therapies, particularly herbal interventions, may help reduce menopausal symptoms, especially hot flashes. Healthcare professionals may consider these approaches as supportive options within individualized, evidence-informed menopause care, while recognizing the need for higher-quality evidence for some outcomes.

Introduction

Menopause is the cessation of menstruation, either physiologically or surgically, due to a decline in ovarian function. The onset of menopause is defined as the absence of menstrual periods for twelve consecutive months without any other underlying cause. Typically, the menopausal period occurs between the ages of 45 and 56. Menopause is a natural process in every woman’s life and is considered the end of female reproductive life [1, 2].

Menopausal symptoms refer to a range of manifestations that occur during the perimenopausal and early postmenopausal periods. Menopause is a variable process that reflects the complex interaction between biological, psychological, and social factors. Approximately 80% of women experience menopausal symptoms to varying degrees, which in some cases may require clinical intervention [3]. These symptoms arise due to a decline in estrogen secretion from the ovaries, leading to autonomic nervous system dysfunction and resulting in hot flashes and sweating, mood disturbances, insomnia, fatigue, headaches, dizziness, palpitations, nausea, musculoskeletal pain, and genitourinary symptoms [4]. Most vasomotor symptoms, which are among the most commonly reported menopausal complaints such as hot flashes and night sweats, last less than seven years after the final menstrual period, although in some cases they may persist for more than ten years [5]. Although numerous treatment options exist for menopausal symptoms, the risks associated with current therapies and a lack of knowledge about available treatment options remain barriers to care for many women. The costs of pharmacological treatments and dissatisfaction with healthcare services have led women to seek more natural approaches [6].

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is considered one of the primary treatments for alleviating menopausal symptoms. However, the benefits of HRT should be weighed alongside its potential risks, as some women undergoing HRT may experience adverse effects such as breast cancer, cervical cancer, and cardiovascular problems [7]. Due to these side effects, women increasingly use various natural products as alternatives to HRT to alleviate menopausal symptoms, owing to their presumed health benefits and lower risk of adverse effects [8]. Nowadays, women turn to numerous complementary and alternative treatment methods to cope with menopausal symptoms. Complementary medicine is defined as a cultural system of theories, beliefs, and practices aimed at treating diseases and maintaining health, whereas alternative medicine involves the use of health practices believed to be beneficial in treatment [9]. Women often learn these complementary therapies methods from family, relatives, and religious sources and utilize them in efforts to manage menopausal symptoms. Women obtain the complementary and alternative methods they use from spice vendors, medical stores, and certain healthcare centers [10]. The complementary and alternative practices applied can vary between countries. For example, a study conducted in Türkiye found that women used herbal therapy, reflexology, prayer, occupational therapy, thermal spa treatments, cupping, and leech therapy as complementary approaches to manage menopausal symptoms [11]. In Alexandria, another study reported the use of cupping, religious rituals, and herbal remedies [10]. Studies conducted in different regions to cope with menopausal symptoms indicate the use of alternative treatments such as reflexology, yoga, acupuncture, bee pollen extract, vitamins, aromatherapy, black cohosh, evening primrose oil, yam root, Chinese angelica, pomegranate, curcumin, flaxseed, and phytoestrogens [8, 12, 13].

Understanding the complementary and traditional therapies used by women during menopause is essential for delivering holistic and evidence-informed care. Women frequently use a range of non-pharmacological interventions—such as herbal preparations, acupuncture, and mind–body practices—to manage menopausal symptoms. However, the concurrent use of these approaches with conventional treatments may influence therapeutic outcomes and safety profiles. Although numerous randomized controlled trials have investigated complementary and traditional interventions for menopausal symptoms, the evidence remains fragmented regarding which specific therapies are effective for particular symptom domains [14, 15]. The lack of symptom-specific synthesis limits clinical applicability and decision-making. Therefore, a systematic and methodologically coherent synthesis of randomized controlled trials examining clearly defined intervention–outcome pairs is warranted.

In this context, the present study was conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate pooled effects and evaluate the safety profile of complementary and traditional interventions for menopausal symptom outcomes.

Literature review and information sources

A systematic literature search was conducted in the Applied Social Sciences Index & Abstracts (ASSIA), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS), PubMed (Medline), Embase, and Web of Science (WOS) databases, yielding a total of 1151 records. During the initial screening, 116 duplicate records were removed, leaving 1035 records. Of these, 912 were excluded based on the eligibility criteria, and access to 1 study could not be obtained, resulting in 115 reports for full-text assessment. Upon evaluation, 18 studies were excluded due to methodological inconsistencies, and 73 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 24 studies were included in the meta-analysis.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed using a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to menopause, complementary and herbal medicine, menopausal symptoms, and randomized controlled trials. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied appropriately. Searches were limited to randomized and clinical trials published from January 2020 onward. The complete search strategy for PubMed/Medline is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full-text articles of potentially relevant records were assessed independently by the same reviewers according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by two reviewers using a standardized and piloted data extraction form. Extracted variables included study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details, comparator information, outcome measures, and effect size data. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Studies excluded after full-text review were documented together with their primary reasons for exclusion. Studies were excluded for not meeting the predefined eligibility criteria (e.g., ineligible population, absence of a complementary or alternative intervention, lack of a control or comparison group, non-randomized design, or failure to report relevant outcomes such as quality of life). Additional exclusions were due to methodological inadequacy, including non-randomized or quasi-experimental designs misclassified as RCTs, incomplete outcome data preventing effect size calculation, or insufficient reporting of essential methodological details.

Results

The random-effects meta-analysis of 24 randomized controlled trials revealed substantial between-study variability. The residual heterogeneity test was statistically significant (Qₑ = 754.905, df = 23, p < .001), indicating considerable inconsistency across studies. The estimated between-study variance was moderate to high (τ = 0.602; τ2 = 0.362), reflecting dispersion in effect sizes across trials.

The 95% prediction interval ranged from −0.228 to 2.340, indicating that intervention effects varied across different settings and populations. This interval includes values close to zero, suggesting that some studies may yield limited or non-significant effects.

These findings highlight the presence of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included interventions. Therefore, while the pooled effect provides an average estimate, it should not be interpreted as uniformly applicable across all contexts (Fig. 2).

Forest plot for all studies

Meta-regression analyses by ındependent variables

Meta-regression analysis showed that sample size was a significant predictor of effect size (F(1,13) = 7.977, p = .014). The regression coefficient was negative (β = −0.005, 95% CI [−0.008, −0.001]), indicating a tendency for smaller studies to report larger effect sizes.

Intervention duration significantly contributed to the model (F(5,13) = 90.863, p < .001). Compared to the reference category, 4 week (β = 1.248, p < .001), 8 week (β = 0.943, p < .001), and 26 week interventions (β = 1.009, p = .003) were associated with significantly higher effect sizes. No significant differences were observed for 24 week (p = .841) and 5 week (p = .982) interventions.

Intervention type did not significantly moderate the effect size (F(4,13) = 2.104, p = .139). None of the categories (Herbal, Homeopathic, Mind-Body, or Nutritional Supplement) differed significantly from the reference category (all p > .05).

Meta-regression analysis indicated that total daily administered dose was not a significant predictor of effect size (β = −4.408 × 10−5, SE = 4.306 × 10⁻5, p = 0.328). Similarly, formulation type did not significantly moderate the pooled effect (F = 1.235, p = 0.356).

These findings suggest that neither variation in daily dosage nor differences in pharmaceutical form (e.g., granule, liquid, syrup, tablet, topical) significantly explained the between-study heterogeneity observed in the overall analysis.

To assess publication bias within the scope of this meta-analysis, a funnel plot asymmetry test was conducted. The meta-regression-based funnel plot asymmetry test did not indicate statistically significant asymmetry (z = −0.934, p = 0.350). Given the number of included studies, the statistical power of this test may be limited; therefore, publication bias or small-study effects cannot be definitively ruled out. However, given the limited number of included studies, the statistical power of this test may be insufficient to reliably detect asymmetry. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution and does not definitively rule out the presence of publication bias or small-study effects.

Subgroup analyses

Herbal interventions

The classical meta-analysis of herbal interventions, based on 18 studies, revealed substantial residual heterogeneity (Qₑ = 582.845, df = 17, p < .001), indicating considerable inconsistency across studies. The between-study variance was moderate to high (τ = 0.620; τ2 = 0.385), further supporting the presence of marked dispersion in effect sizes.

Under the random-effects model, a statistically significant positive effect was observed (ES = 1.002, SE = 0.177, t(17) = 5.653, p < .001; 95% CI 0.628 to 1.376). However, this estimate represents an average effect and should be interpreted in the context of substantial between-study heterogeneity. The 95% prediction interval (−0.359 to 2.364) was wide and included values close to or below zero, suggesting that the magnitude and even the direction of the effect may vary across different contexts.

Taken together, while herbal interventions may be associated with improvements in menopausal symptoms, the substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction interval indicate that these findings should be interpreted with caution. The variability across studies underscores the importance of considering intervention-specific characteristics and contextual factors when evaluating their effectiveness (Fig. 3).

Forest plot and funnel plot for herbal interventions subgroup

Funnel plot asymmetry assessed using Egger’s test with multiplicative overdispersion (t = −1.965, df = 16, p = 0.067) did not reach statistical significance. Given the number of included studies, the statistical power of this test may be limited. Therefore, publication bias or small-study effects cannot be definitively ruled out.

A statistically significant overall effect was observed; however, substantial between-study variability was also present, indicating that effect sizes varied across studies.

Hot flashes

A random-effects meta-analysis including seven studies indicated substantial between-study heterogeneity (Qₑ = 394.338, df = 6, p < .001; τ2 = 0.472), suggesting considerable variability in effect estimates across studies. Although a statistically significant positive effect was observed (Estimate = 1.057, SE = 0.279, p = 0.009; 95% CI 0.375 to 1.740), this estimate represents an average effect and should be interpreted in the context of substantial heterogeneity. The 95% prediction interval (−0.756 to 2.871) was wide and included values below zero. This finding indicates that the magnitude and direction of the effect may vary across different populations and settings, and that some studies may observe limited or no benefit.

Overall, while herbal interventions may be associated with improvements in hot flashes, the substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction interval warrant cautious interpretation, emphasizing the need to consider study-specific and intervention-specific factors (Fig. 4).

Forest plot and funnel plot for hot flashes

The Egger test (t = −1.175, df = 5, p = 0.293) did not reach statistical significance. However, given the relatively small number of included studies, the statistical power of this test may be limited, and the absence of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, publication bias or small-study effects cannot be definitively ruled out within this subgroup.

Menopausal symptom severity

A random-effects meta-analysis of three studies evaluated the effects of herbal interventions on overall menopausal symptom severity. Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate and not statistically significant (Qₑ = 4.575, df = 2, p = 0.102; τ = 0.348), although this should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of studies and the low power of heterogeneity tests. Although the estimated effect size was moderate (ES = 1.084, SE = 0.255), it did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance (p = 0.051), and the 95% confidence interval (−0.011 to 2.180) included values close to zero. The 95% prediction interval (−0.772 to 2.940) was wide, indicating uncertainty in the expected effects of future studies and the possibility of no effect.

Given the small number of included studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and the current evidence remains insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding the effectiveness of herbal interventions on overall menopausal symptom severity.

Egger’s test for the three studies examining herbal interventions on menopausal symptom severity was not statistically significant (t = −1.489, df = 1, p = 0.376). However, given the extremely small number of included studies, the statistical power of this test is very limited, and the absence of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, publication bias or small-study effects cannot be definitively ruled out (Fig. 5).

Quality of life

A random-effects meta-analysis of three studies evaluated the effects of herbal interventions on quality of life. Substantial between-study variability was observed (τ2 = 0.580), indicating notable dispersion in effect estimates across studies. Although a large effect size was estimated (ES = 2.031), this estimate should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of studies and the associated uncertainty. It did not reach conventional statistical significance (p = 0.062), and the limited number of studies restricts the robustness of this finding. The 95% prediction interval (−1.959 to 6.021) was wide and included values below zero, suggesting considerable uncertainty regarding the direction and magnitude of effects in future studies.

Taken together, these findings indicate that while herbal interventions may be associated with improvements in quality of life, the evidence remains inconclusive. The small number of studies and substantial variability warrant cautious interpretation. Egger’s test (t = −0.271, p = 0.831) did not indicate statistically significant asymmetry; however, given the very small number of studies, this test is likely underpowered and the absence of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 6).

The classical meta-analysis of non-herbal complementary interventions (including acupuncture, nutritional supplements, mind–body approaches, and homeopathic treatments) was based on six studies. The residual heterogeneity test was not statistically significant (Qₑ = 10.050, df = 5, p = .074); however, given the small number of studies and the limited power of the Q test, this finding should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence of homogeneity. The estimated between-study variance (τ = 0.572; τ2 = 0.327) suggests potential variability across studies, although this estimate should be interpreted with caution given its limited precision.

Under the random-effects model, a statistically significant positive effect was observed (ES = 1.268, SE = 0.307, t(5) = 4.137, p = .009; 95% CI 0.480 to 2.056). However, this estimate represents an average effect and should be interpreted in the context of the observed heterogeneity and limited number of studies. The 95% prediction interval (−0.400 to 2.937) was wide and included values below zero, indicating substantial uncertainty regarding the magnitude and direction of effects in future studies.

Overall, while non-herbal complementary interventions may be associated with improvements in menopausal symptoms, the limited number of studies, potential imprecision in heterogeneity estimates, and wide prediction interval warrant cautious interpretation. These findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted in light of intervention-specific and contextual differences (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In the present analysis, substantial between-study variability was observed, as reflected by significant residual heterogeneity and a wide prediction interval spanning both clinically meaningful benefit and negligible effects. This indicates that the effectiveness of complementary and traditional interventions is not uniform but highly context-dependent across populations and intervention designs. The inclusion of values close to zero further suggests that, under certain conditions, these interventions may yield limited or non-significant effects.

This variability may be explained by differences in intervention characteristics (e.g., content, standardization, delivery) and population-level factors such as baseline symptom severity, cultural practices, and adherence patterns, consistent with previous meta-analyses [40, 41]. While prior literature attributes effectiveness to mechanisms such as hormonal modulation or neuroendocrine regulation [42, 43], the present findings suggest these explanations may be overly generalized, with effects likely conditional and setting-specific.

A key meta-regression finding was the inverse association between sample size and effect size, indicating that smaller studies tended to report larger effects. This pattern may reflect small-study effects arising from selective reporting, methodological limitations, or increased variability. Although funnel plot asymmetry was not statistically significant, limited power means publication bias cannot be excluded [44, 45].

Intervention duration emerged as the most consistent moderator, with certain durations (4, 8, and 26 weeks) showing larger effects. However, the absence of a clear dose–response pattern limits causal interpretation, suggesting that these findings may reflect contextual or study-level factors rather than true temporal mechanisms [45–47]. In contrast, intervention type, formulation, and daily dose did not explain heterogeneity, consistent with broader reviews highlighting the role of unmeasured factors [48, 49].

Mechanistic interpretations should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. While physiological or behavioral adaptation processes may be relevant, they are not directly supported by this analysis, and causal inferences should be avoided [46, 47]. Overall, study size and methodological factors appear critical, emphasizing the need for adequately powered trials and standardized interventions.

For herbal interventions, pooled estimates suggested improvement in menopausal symptoms; however, substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals indicate inconsistent effects across settings [7, 48, 51]. Similar variability has been reported in recent meta-analyses [52, 53]. Mechanistic explanations such as phytoestrogenic or anti-inflammatory effects remain speculative and inconsistently supported [54, 55]. Instead, variability in composition, dosage, and participant characteristics likely plays a more decisive role [7, 56].

Regarding hot flashes, herbal interventions showed potentially beneficial effects, but substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals indicate limited reproducibility [7, 50, 51, 57]. Similar variability has been reported for specific interventions such as pollen extracts and Salvia officinalis [58]. Mechanistic interpretations (e.g., phytoestrogenic or thermoregulatory effects) remain inconsistent with clinical outcomes [59, 60], and observed effects likely reflect variability in formulations and populations [51, 61].

For overall menopausal symptom severity, findings suggested a potential effect, but evidence remains inconclusive. The small number of studies limits statistical power and the reliability of heterogeneity measures [62]. Wide prediction intervals further indicate substantial uncertainty [63–65], supporting the need for larger, methodologically robust studies [66, 67].

Similarly, although a large effect was estimated for quality of life, the lack of statistical significance and wide confidence and prediction intervals indicate imprecision and instability [68]. These findings are therefore better considered hypothesis-generating, with limited generalizability [63].

For non-herbal complementary interventions, statistically significant effects were observed; however, interpretation is constrained by methodological limitations. The non-significant Q test likely reflects low power rather than homogeneity [69, 70], and τ2 indicates underlying variability [71, 72]. Wide prediction intervals further suggest limited generalizability [73]. Given the diversity of interventions, mechanistic explanations remain speculative.

Overall, the findings should be considered indicative rather than definitive. The consistent presence of heterogeneity, uncertainty, and context dependency underscores the need for standardized intervention protocols, improved reporting, and adequately powered studies to enhance the reliability and generalizability of evidence.

Limitations

This meta-analysis study has certain limitations that must be considered. Significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies included in this study, and the confidence intervals were wide; this indicates that effect sizes may vary considerably across settings. Although meta-regression analyses were performed, a significant portion of the variability could not be explained. Some subgroup analyses, such as overall menopausal symptom severity and quality of life, were based on a small number of studies. This may reduce statistical power and the precision of pooled estimates. Furthermore, most of the included studies had relatively small sample sizes, which may have affected effect size estimates.

In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted using relatively broad categories (e.g., intervention type and duration), which may have masked more nuanced differences between specific interventions and contexts. More detailed subgroup analyses were not feasible due to the limited number of studies and variability in reporting across studies.

Differences in intervention types, durations, and outcome measurement tools may have contributed to methodological and clinical heterogeneity. Although all included studies were randomised controlled trials, some studies may have had biases, particularly in reporting and randomisation procedures. Finally, the search was limited to studies published from 2020 onwards. While this ensured the inclusion of recent evidence, earlier relevant studies may have been missed. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and there is a need for better-designed, large-scale randomised trials.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that complementary and alternative interventions may be associated with improvements in vasomotor symptoms, overall menopausal symptoms, and quality of life in menopausal women. Herbal therapies were the most frequently studied interventions and showed potentially beneficial effects, particularly for hot flashes.

However, the substantial heterogeneity across studies, wide prediction intervals, and low certainty of evidence for several outcomes limit the confidence and generalizability of these findings. Therefore, the current evidence should be interpreted cautiously, and the findings are better considered exploratory rather than definitive for clinical practice.

Further large-scale, methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trials using standardized intervention protocols and outcome measures are needed to clarify the effectiveness and clinical applicability of complementary and alternative therapies in menopausal care.
