Calibrating limitation features and dependence levels in basic activities of daily living among individuals with dementia: an item response theory-based analysis
Authors: Jinni Wang, Xiangyi Chen, Xiaomei Lin, Hua Ye, Xiaoyan Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvaluating basic activities of daily living (BADL) is essential for monitoring disease progression and determining appropriate care plans for individuals with dementia. Existing dementia-specific BADL scales emphasize specific limitations without adequately depicting the spectrum of dependence. Discrepancies in how these limitations and the required assistance are described and interpreted across scales highlight the urgent need for a standardized approach to accurately map limitation features to their appropriate levels of dependence.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. Participants with dementia were enrolled between May and September 2022 from a 274-bed dementia unit at a nursing home in Guangzhou, China. Limitation features were classified into eight categories from “no limitations” to “resistance to care”. Dependence was rated on an 11-point scale, from complete independence (0) to complete dependence (10). The calibration of limitation features and dependence levels was based on item response theory (IRT) analysis.ResultsIn total, 215 subjects were included (mean age 85.2 ± 7.4; 70.7% female). Hierarchically, the average measures (logit) increased from “no limitations” (-3.6 to -1.8), “initiation deficits” (-2.7 to -0.6), “planning deficits” (-2.2 to 0), “organizational deficits” (-1.6 to 0.4), “partial execution deficits” (-1.2 to 0.8), “inability with cooperation” (0.3 to 1.3), to “complete inability” (1.4 to 2.1). IRT location parameters revealed that deficits in initiation, planning, and organization fell within mild dependence (average θ: -1.1 to 0) across most of the items, although organizational deficits in grooming aligned more closely with moderate dependence. Partial execution deficits and inability with cooperation corresponded to moderate (average θ: -0.4 to 0.5) and severe dependence (average θ: 0.3 to 0.8), respectively. “Resistance to care” was disordered and fell between “inability with cooperation” and “complete inability”. The item characteristic curves supported these mappings.ConclusionsThe findings offer valuable insights into the hierarchical nature of dementia-specific BADL limitations and gradation of dependence in BADL of persons with dementia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07251-x.Keywords: Basic activities of daily living, Dementia, Dependence, Item response theory, Measurement

Introduction

Dementia is a leading contributor to disability and dependence among older adults and poses a significant public health challenge worldwide [1]. It is characterized, in part, by impairment in activities of daily living (ADL), which constitutes one of the core diagnostic criteria for dementia [2]. ADL are commonly divided into instrumental ADL (IADL)—complex activities necessary for independent living, such as shopping and meal preparation—and basic ADL (BADL)—fundamental personal care tasks, such as bathing, grooming, and dressing. Typically, IADL decline emerges in the early stages of dementia, while BADL impairment manifests during more advanced stages [3]. The decline in BADL ultimately requires substantial caregiving support, which compromises the autonomy and quality of life of those living with dementia, increases caregiver burden, and frequently results in institutionalization [4]. Therefore, accurate assessment of BADL is critical for monitoring disease progression and determining appropriate levels of support.

A progressive deterioration in cognitive function often interferes with ADL independence [5] and plays a major role in the loss of autonomy [6], with evidence suggesting that cognitive impairment remains a strong influencing factor even in severe stages of dementia [7]. Previous studies have highlighted that IADL are particularly sensitive to cognitive decline, while BADL limitations can arise from a combined effect of cognitive deterioration, motor dysfunction, and neuropsychiatric symptoms [8, 9]. Despite being physically capable, individuals with dementia often experience difficulties in executing BADL; their cognitive deficits can interfere with their ability to do so [3]. For example, they may lack daily structure and consequently do not know what to do in a particular moment or may be unable to select clothing appropriate to the weather and occasion because of deficits in recognizing objects [3]. These observations indicate that BADL assessments for cognitively impaired individuals should focus on functional limitations specific to cognitive impairment.

Importantly, many traditional ADL scales cannot address dementia-specific challenges [10]. Numerous assessments have been specifically developed for patients with dementia by accounting for the impact of physical, cognitive, and/or neuropsychiatric changes [11–15]. The dual-processing hypothesis, which posits that both controlled and automatic processing are involved, serves as a key framework for elucidating dementia-specific ADL limitations [12, 16]. Controlled processing involves active attention to initiate, plan, and organize ADL tasks, while automatic processing is triggered by appropriate stimuli and then proceeds with minimal conscious effort to execute well-practiced routine activities [17]. Deficits in initiation, planning, organization, and execution are considered central to dementia-specific disability [12].

Existing scales that assess ADL in individuals with dementia often emphasize specific limitations without adequately depicting the spectrum of dependence [14], or they simply amalgamate limitation features and dependence levels into item options [13]. However, the associations between specific limitations and corresponding dependence levels are not well defined. Moreover, there is a lack of consistency in how such limitations are described across different scales tailored for dementia. The Bristol Activities of Daily Living Scale (BADLS) [13], for instance, describes organizational deficits in dressing as “puts clothes on in wrong order and/or back to front and/or dirty clothing,” contrasting with the Activities of Daily Living Questionnaire (ADLQ), which states, “wrong sequence, forgets items” [15].

Additionally, interpretations of dependence and the need for assistance vary across scales. For example, the Cleveland Scale for Activities of Daily Living (CSADL) focuses on the frequency of “direction or help” needed for each item, which fails to distinguish between the need for simple guidance and actual physical assistance [12]; the Alzheimer’s Disease Functional Assessment and Change Scale (ADFACS) designates “resistance to care” as the highest level of disability [18]. These discrepancies highlight the urgent need for a standardized approach to calibrate limitation features with their appropriate levels of dependence—a research gap that remains largely unaddressed.

Increasingly, researchers in clinical fields are using methodologies based on item response theory (IRT) to link scores between different instruments that measure similar constructs [19]. Once scores are linked to a common metric, a crosswalk table can be constructed that associates scores from one scale with corresponding scores on another [20]. The same methodologies can be applied to calibrate dementia-specific BADL limitations with levels of dependence. This approach can also place response categories on a single continuum [21], thereby enabling comparisons of different limitation profiles.

This study aimed to calibrate dementia-specific BADL limitations with corresponding levels of dependence using an IRT-based approach.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional study design was used to collect data for this research. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from May to September 2022 from a 274-bed dementia unit at a nursing home in Guangzhou, China. The inclusion criteria were: (1) 60 years or older, which aligns with the common administrative definition of older adults in China [22]; (2) meeting the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) criteria for dementia [23] or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th Edition (DSM-5) criteria for Major Neurocognitive Disorder [2]; (3) consenting to participate. The exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of comorbidities affecting BADL, such as hemiplegia, paralysis, joint or bone disease, and Parkinson’s disease; (2) having deafness and/or blindness. These conditions were excluded to minimize the confounding effect of primary physical limitations on the assessment of dementia-specific BADL limitations, allowing us to focus on functional deficits primarily attributable to cognitive impairment.

Eligible informants were those who: (1) worked full-time and cared for the same residents over their three-shifts per day (including day and night shifts); (2) maintained regular contact with the residents at least three times a day during the past month [24].

Sample size calculations

According to recommendations by Linacre [25], a sample size of 150 (n range, 108–243) would provide 99% confidence for item calibration within ± 0.5 logits. In this study, 215 older adults with dementia were included after screening 263 residents for eligibility; 48 were excluded due to congenital intellectual disability.

Measures

Features of BADL limitations

BADL tasks that are consistently represented across existing dementia-specific ADL scales were selected: bathing, grooming, toileting, dressing, and eating. The limitation features were drawn from the scales (see Supplementary Table S1) and classified into the following categories: A (no limitations), B (initiation deficits), C (planning deficits), D (organizational deficits), E (partial execution deficits), F (inability with cooperation), G (complete inability), and UR (resistance to care). Categories A through UR were numerically coded from 0 to 7 to facilitate ordinal data analysis. Notably, in existing scales, the planning deficit category is not considered applicable for the item ‘Toileting’ (see Supplementary Table S1).

Formal caregivers who were directly involved in caring for participants reported the observed limitations during the preceding week. A researcher (Wang) observed the basic activities of some of the participants (n = 70) for 1.5 months to test the reliability of the formal caregivers’ reports.

Levels of dependence in BADL

Formal caregivers were also asked to rate dependence levels for each BADL task using an 11-point numerical rating scale. This scale is considered useful for evaluating dependence levels resulting from aging-related disabilities [26] and can be conceptualized as a unidimensional construct for the Rasch model [27]. The continuous score values of 0–10 were divided into five levels, with 0 standing for complete independence, 1–3 for mild dependence (i.e., requiring either assistive devices or supervision), 4–6 for moderate dependence (i.e., requiring minor/non-weight-bearing physical assistance), 7–9 for severe dependence (i.e., requiring moderate/weight-bearing physical assistance), and 10 for complete dependence [28].

The Barthel Index (BI) was also utilized to assess the participants’ levels of dependence when performing BADL.

Other measurements

Severity of dementia was evaluated using the Clinical Dementia Rating, which was rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 0 (indicating no impairment) to 3 (indicating severe impairment), with intermediate scores of 0.5 (questionable impairment), 1 (mild impairment), and 2 (moderate impairment) [29].

Data collection

BADL was assessed by two trained nursing graduate students. Demographic information and clinical data were obtained from medical records. The Clinical Dementia Rating was assessed by a psychiatrist working at the nursing home.

Ethical considerations

The study obtained approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital (NFEC-2022-354). Participants and/or their proxies were provided a thorough explanation of research objectives, main procedures, potential risks, and benefits. In cases where participants lacked capacity to provide consent, written informed consent was obtained from their proxies. The surveys were administered anonymously.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were shown as standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The IRT-based approach was used to examine the correspondence between features of BADL limitations and dependence levels (for details, see Supplementary Methods). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). In the sensitivity analysis, 44 participants unable to walk were excluded before re-analysis to examine the robustness of the calibration when considering only participants with intact mobility, a subgroup that might represent a different profile of physical capacity within the dementia population. The analyses only included participants who had no missing values for the relevant variables, without any imputation for the missing data.

Descriptive analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0. Preliminary analyses were conducted using Winsteps® 4.1.0 software (Winsteps.com, Linacre) [30]. Mapping was performed using Multilog (written by David Thissen, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill) [31].

Among these, 8 (3.7%) were identified with questionable dementia, 59 (27.4%) with mild dementia, 89 (41.4%) with moderate dementia, and 59 (27.4%) with severe dementia. Regarding the type of dementia, the sample comprised 149 participants (69.3%) diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, 14 (6.5%) with vascular dementia, and 52 (24.2%) with unspecified forms. Category ordering of limitation features

Figure 1 illustrates the average measure (logit) of the response categories (limitations) for each item (BADL). As expected, these measures showed an increasing trend in line with the ascending scores for the categories: from “no limitations” (-3.6 to -1.8), to “initiation deficits” (-2.7 to -0.6), “planning deficits” (-2.2 to 0), “organizational deficits” (-1.6 to 0.4), “partial execution deficits” (-1.2 to 0.8), “inability with cooperation” (0.3 to 1.3), and finally to “complete inability” (1.4 to 2.1), indicating a satisfactory category ordering for all categories except UR (resistance to care) (0.8 to 1.9). The UR category demonstrated disordering across all items.

Correspondence between limitation features and dependence levels

As Fig. 2 illustrates, there was a clear correspondence between limitation features and dependence levels. Specifically, Category A (no limitations) was mapped to independence (average θ: -1.7 to -0.8). Categories B, C, and D, which reflect deficits in initiating, planning, and organizing, corresponded to mild dependence (average θ: -1.1 to 0): as they fell within the average θ ± 1SE range for mild dependence. Category E (partial execution deficits), F (inability with cooperation), and G (complete inability) corresponded to moderate (average θ: -0.4 to 0.5), severe (average θ: 0.3 to 0.8), and complete dependence (average θ: 0.8 to 1.3), respectively. The results of the sensitivity analysis corroborated these findings (see Supplementary Figure S1).

Because Category D approached the range for moderate dependence in the item ‘Eating’ and ‘Grooming’, a more fine-tuned analysis was conducted to determine the best-fitting dependence level. This involved first merging either Categories B, C, and D or Categories D and E, and then comparing the areas between the item characteristics curves of the merged categories and their corresponding dependence levels. For the item ‘Eating’, the combined Category BCD was more congruent with mild dependence (see Supplementary Figure S2), and the combined Category EF was well-mapped to the merged moderate-severe dependence level (Fig. 3). For the item ‘Grooming’, the combined Category DE showed a closer association with moderate dependence (see Supplementary Figure S2). Discussion

This study has demonstrated a hierarchical structure of dementia-specific BADL limitations and a clear alignment between limitation features and dependence levels using IRT-based approaches. Deficits in initiation, planning, organization, and execution of BADL exhibited a hierarchical order, ascending from the lowest to the highest levels across the latent ability continuum. Initiation, planning, and organizational deficits predominantly corresponded to mild dependence, whereas partial execution deficits and inability with cooperation corresponded to moderate and severe dependence, respectively. Our findings indicate that “resistance to care” is not suitable for gauging a person’s level of dependence. To our knowledge, this study is the first attempt to explore the correspondences between specific features of dementia-specific BADL limitations and quantitative levels of dependence.

BADL are highly routinized everyday activities inherently linked to procedural memory—a cognitive system that contributes to automatic behavior from an early age [32]. Observed declines in BADL in patients with dementia seem to follow a specific trajectory: they begin with deficits in controlled processing (e.g., deficits in initiation, planning, and organization), and progressively compromises automatic processes as the disease advances (e.g., execution deficits) [33]. Planning deficits, characterized by a dependence on others to prepare required things before an activity (e.g., “helped with running the water or adjusting its temperature”), are also prevalent in people with dementia [12, 14]. With a decline in semantic processing, individuals with dementia exhibit impairments in object recognition [34]. For example, they might select inappropriate clothing, wear mismatched garments, or confuse toothpaste with shower gel. Furthermore, impairments in attention [35], visuospatial function [36], and procedural memory [37] often lead to organizational deficits during daily tasks. These can manifest as omissions of proper steps during toothbrushing, dressing in an incorrect sequence, or difficulties in distinguishing the front from the back of clothing. As dementia progresses, deteriorating procedural memory can further complicate the completion of tasks [37]. Consequently, individuals may become unable to complete BADL or may merely attempt to move their limbs to assist [13], thus necessitating varying degrees of physical support. By employing IRT-based approaches, we observed a hierarchical structuring in initiation, planning, organizational, and execution deficits along a latent ability continuum. These insights can support healthcare professionals in more accurately charting disease progression and formulating effective interventions.

A critical issue is the heterogeneity in how limitation features are represented across existing dementia-specific scales. These instruments assess the impact of dementia on ADL by incorporating deficits in initiation, planning, and organization (reflecting the need for reminders, item preparation, and supervision, respectively) to differing extents. For instance, the BADLS omits assessment of initiation deficits, the ADLQ excludes deficits in planning and organization [15], and the ADFACS does not cover any of these deficits [18]. By contrast, scales such as the CSADL, Disability Assessment for Dementia (DAD), and Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study Activities of Daily Living scale (ADCS-ADL) provide more comprehensive evaluations of dementia-specific BADL limitations [11, 12]. Nonetheless, the CSADL and DAD set each ADL as a distinct domain, including items pertaining to initiation, planning, organization, and execution of tasks [12]. Moreover, descriptions of the same limitation features vary across the scales. A precise understanding of these limitations is essential. This study helps fill this gap by rigorously calibrating deficits in initiation, planning, and organization to mild dependence, and by linking partial execution deficits and inability with cooperation to moderate and severe dependence, respectively. Our findings could refine existing ADL scales by introducing a nuanced gradation of dependence levels for the diverse BADL limitations documented in the literature.

Although the item options in the ADCS-ADL, BADLS, ADLQ, and ADFACS are arranged hierarchically to reflect the varying levels of support required by individuals with dementia, discrepancies exist in how these levels of dependence are interpreted across the scales. For instance, the ADCS-ADL considers individuals as “independent” if they complete any ADL task unassisted or with only occasional reminders or prompts [11]. This potentially underestimates the burden imposed on caregivers by the necessity for reminders and may overlook initiation deficits in individuals with dementia. These individuals might rely on external prompts to initiate BADL, which, according to our findings, is indicative of mild dependence rather than true independence. Advancements in technology may offer alternatives to manual supervision and thus hold promise for improving autonomy in individuals with mild dementia. Inconsistencies also exist in categorizing non-contact support. The ADCS-ADL scale classifies planning deficits such as “running water or adjusting its temperature” as requiring “minor physical help” [11]. The CSADL, by contrast, provides a spectrum of four options for “prepares bath/shower” ranging from complete independence to complete dependence [12]. Our findings indicate that non-contact support typically corresponds to mild dependence. Divergences also exist in classifying levels of physical support. The ADCS-ADL and ADLQ recognize physical assistance needs as minor or complete help [11, 15], while the BADLS does not offer such distinctions [13]. Such discrepancies may lead to less individualized care planning. Our calibration shows that partial execution deficits correspond to moderate dependence, while inability with cooperation correspond to severe dependence, offering a potential solution to standardizing BADL assessments in dementia.

An exception was observed for grooming, for which this study aligned organizational deficits with moderate dependence, even after a sensitivity analysis. Grooming represents a form of social connectedness, as opposed to a fundamental living necessity such as feeding, and involves more elaborate upper limb movements. The ability to perform grooming tends to be relatively well preserved among patients with dementia [38]. Nevertheless, individuals may neglect their appearance because of neuropsychiatric symptoms such as apathy and depression. This potentially imposes a significant time burden on family caregivers owing to extensive supervision requirements [39]. Further qualitative research is needed to elucidate the underlying causes for why organizational deficits in grooming are associated with a higher level of dependence compared to other BADL tasks.

Additionally, this study found that “resistance to care” failed to align consistently with any level of dependence and demonstrated category disordering across all items. “Resistance to care”—a prevalent challenge in supporting BADL for individuals with dementia—involves active refusal or behavioral opposition to care attempts even when the individual is physically capable of cooperating. Certain tools, such as the ADFACS [18], equate “resistance to care” with the most severe level of disability. However, from a person-centered perspective, “resistance to care” is a complex phenomenon often driven by unmet needs, environmental stressors, or negative interactions between caregivers and care recipients [40], rather than merely functional incapacity. Our analysis suggests that “resistance to care” lacks validity as a marker for determining the extent of ADL dependence. However, rather than being excluded from assessment, it should be interpreted as a valuable indicator to inform person-centered care strategies.

While direct observation by trained professionals may be considered an ideal method for assessing functional abilities, this is impractical due to constraints on time, resources, and the need to respect personal privacy during basic activities in most real-world clinical and care settings. Consequently, structured reports from primary caregivers, who possess longitudinal, contextual knowledge, become an indispensable primary source of information. The fundamental challenge, therefore, lies not in choosing between direct observation and informant-based tools, but in ensuring that the standardized instruments used to codify these caregiver reports are themselves valid and precise. Existing scales, while practical, often lack a well-calibrated mapping between the specific limitations they describe and the continuum of dependence. Our findings directly address this calibration gap by employing IRT to establish a clear hierarchy and map specific limitations onto levels of dependence. This ensures that scores derived from caregiver-informed instruments accurately and meaningfully reflect underlying levels of dependence, thereby supporting consistent care planning, tracking of progression, and communication across different care providers and settings.

This study, along with our previous study involving community-dwelling older adults with dementia [41], collectively underscores that the core hierarchy of dementia-specific BADL limitations is a stable and recognizable construct. By mapping specific observed limitations—such as initiation, planning, or organizational deficits—onto a quantifiable continuum of dependence, this calibration allows for more accurate staging of functional decline, facilitates the development of tailored, person-centered care plans that target specific areas of difficulty, and establishes a reliable metric for tracking progression or response to interventions over time.

Limitations

Several limitations in the present study should be noted. First, the study was conducted at only one home for older adults, which might limit the generalizability of the findings to community-dwelling older adults and individuals with younger-onset dementia. Future multi-center studies involving diverse settings and populations are needed to enhance the external validity and developmental implications of these findings. Second, convenience sampling was used, which may have introduced selection bias. Third, ADL limitations relied on caregiver reports, which may be subject to recall bias. Moreover, formal caregivers usually work in teams to help people with dementia with their daily activities, so they might not fully identify and address the unique limitations experienced by each participant. Fourth, the study did not stratify analyses by specific dementia subtypes. The limited size of non-Alzheimer’s groups and the clinical heterogeneity of participants with an unspecified subtype precluded robust subtype-specific analysis. Future studies with larger, well-characterized cohorts are needed to examine whether the hierarchy of BADL limitations varies across etiologies.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate a hierarchical structure of dementia-specific BADL limitations and a clear alignment between these limitation features and dependence levels. More specifically, using IRT-based approaches, we clarified that deficits in initiation, planning, and organization among people with dementia corresponded to mild dependence, while partial execution deficits and inability with inability equated to moderate and severe dependence, respectively. Our findings may contribute to the development of a theoretical framework for dementia-specific BADL assessments.
