Bronchial artery embolization before endobronchial resection to avoid severe bleeding in central airway obstruction: a single-center retrospective pilot study
Authors: Guanghui He, Liping Zhang, Fumei Zhang, Zuotao Li, Chengfeng Li, Zhi Gao, Xiangxu Feng, Aiqiang Han, Wujie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchoscopic resection represents a facilitated alternative option for restoration of airway patency in selected cases. However, the procedure is susceptible to complications arising from severe hemorrhaging. This single-center retrospective pilot study aimed to evaluate the safety and feasibility of bronchial artery embolization (BAE) prior to endobronchial resection in patients with central airway obstruction (CAO).MethodPatients with CAO caused by hypervascular tumors with strong enhancement on computed tomography (CT) and/or hemoptysis were included in the study. All neoplasms were excised using argon plasma coagulation during bronchoscopic intervention. Preceding the procedure, selective bronchial artery embolization was conducted to occlude the neoplastic blood supply 48 h in advance. The analysis encompassed an evaluation of hemorrhage severity, reconstruction of airway patency, and associated complications.ResultsA total of 24 patients (15 males and 9 females) with central airway obstruction were included for analysis, with a mean age of 61 ± 10 years (42–87 years) and followed-up for a median (range) of 26 (20–54) months. The locations of tumor in the airway included lobar bronchus (17/24, 70.8%) and trachea (7/24, 29.2%). The obstruction degree was Grade III in 15 (62.5%) patients and Grade IV in 9(37.5%) patients. Embolization and bronchoscopy tumor resection were successfully performed in all the patients. Procedure-related minor bleeding occurred in 19 (79.2%) patients and moderate bleeding in 5 (20.8%) patients; No severe bleeding occurred. Reconstruction of airway patency with complete response and partial response were seen in 16 (66.7%) patients and 8 (33.3%) patients, respectively, and symptom relief was achieved in all the patients. Complications after BAE, including chest pain (11/24, 45.8%) and abdominal pain (3/24, 12.5%), were alleviated on the next day after receiving supportive therapies. Fever, shivering, and vomiting occurred in 9/24 (37.5%), 4/24 (16.7%), and 3/24 (12.5%) patients, respectively. Spinal cord injury was not observed in this study.ConclusionsBronchoscopic resection of airway tumors following selective bronchial artery embolization represents a potentially safe and viable approach for mitigating severe bleeding during the excision of hypervascular airway tumors. Further validation through a comprehensive prospective study is warranted.Keywords: Bronchial artery embolization, Endobronchial resection, Bleeding, Central airway obstruction

Implications for practice

This study highlights the potential of bronchoscopic resection as a safer alternative for treating airway tumors, particularly those with a high risk of bleeding. By performing bronchial artery embolization prior to the procedure, we found that the risk of severe hemorrhaging can be significantly reduced. This approach not only aids in restoring airway patency for patients but also enhances the overall safety of the procedure. Our findings may encourage healthcare providers to adopt this method more widely, ultimately improving outcomes for individuals with hypervascular airway tumors and advancing treatment options in clinical practice.

Introduction

Central airway obstruction (CAO) has a devastating impact on patients’ quality of life and can even pose a life-threatening risk requiring urgent action [1]. It is often associated with benign and malignant tumors, granulomatous lesions, tuberculosis, and other causative factors [2]. Approximately 40% of individuals diagnosed with lung cancer experience some degree of central airway obstruction, which has the potential to result in severe respiratory failure or fatality [3].

The core principle of CAO treatment is to relieve patients’ symptoms by reestablishing airway patency. Surgical resection is the primary treatment option [4]. However, increasing evidence supports the use of thermoablative techniques such as electrocautery, laser therapy, argon plasma coagulation, and cryotherapy for specific typical carcinoid patients and patients who are not suitable for surgery [5–7]. Furthermore, effective management is generally aimed at palliative care rather than curative intent due to its late presentation [5].

In the realm of bronchoscopy resection, challenges arise, such as incomplete tumor resection and tumor recurrence, particularly in cases of tumors with a profuse blood supply, where endobronchial therapy poses the risk of substantial bleeding, warranting urgent surgical intervention [8].

To address these constraints, a retrospective safety and feasibility pilot study was initiated. This involves the application of bronchial artery embolization (BAE) to embolize the blood supply to the tumor, followed by resection via argon plasma coagulation (APC), high-frequency electrotome, and freezing methodologies.

Materials and methods

Patients suffering from CAO scheduled for endobronchial resection of airway tumors were included retrospectively from December 2020 to November 2022. The study was approved by the institutional ethics review board (reference No. 202111).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: aged 18 years or older; CAO due to airway tumors; symptoms caused by airway obstruction and symptoms expected to be relieved by intervention; and tolerance for general anesthesia. The exclusion criteria included multiple organ failure, uncontrolled arrhythmias, and a platelet count < 50 × 109/L.

All patients were assessed with contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) prior to treatment. CAO was defined as occlusion of ⩾50% of the trachea, main-stem bronchi, bronchus intermedius, or lobar bronchus.

Cases for BAE were strictly selected based on high intraprocedural bleeding risk, with two explicit, standardized criteria: first, radiological hypervascularity—defined as tumor lesions showing ≥ 50 HU enhancement on contrast-enhanced CT relative to non-contrast CT scans; second, covering patients with moderate-to-severe hemoptysis (≥ 100 mL of blood loss within 24 h) or recurrent minor hemoptysis.

All eligible patients underwent a multidisciplinary team discussion involving interventional pulmonologists, thoracic radiologists, and surgical oncologists to confirm that the benefits of prophylactic BAE outweighed potential risks, and due to these strict high-risk criteria, cases included in the BAE cohort were non-consecutively enrolled.

Bronchial artery embolization

A 5 F arterial sheath was inserted via the femoral artery to establish vascular access. Subsequent arteriography with a 5 F catheter was performed to identify the tumor-feeding bronchial arteries. Superselective catheterization of the tumor-feeding artery was subsequently performed via a 1.98 F ASAKI soft catheter, followed by embolization. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles with a diameter of 300–500 μm were employed as embolic materials until the embolization process was complete. Confirmation of successful embolization was ascertained through angiography, indicating the disappearance of tumor staining and the nonemptiness of contrast agent within the main artery of the feeding artery for 4 cardiac cycles. A second angiography was executed after a 5-minute hiatus to verify the completeness of the embolization process. The procedure was performed by two interventional radiologists with 15 years of experience. Identification of the spinal arteries during the procedure is imperative for mitigating the risk of severe complications associated with ectopic embolism.

Bronchoscopic resection

Rigid bronchoscopy was carried out under general anesthesia 48 h after arterial embolization.

For lesions located in the lobar bronchi, we adopted a combined rigid-flexible bronchoscopy approach: after advancing the rigid bronchoscope to the ipsilateral main bronchus, a flexible bronchoscope or flexible interventional devices were inserted through the rigid bronchoscope’s large working channel. Initially, APC (ERBE, Tübingen, Germany) was administered for treatment. The APC output power ranged from 30 to 50 W, accompanied by an argon gas flow rate of 0.8 to 1.6 L/min. In instances where the lesion surface subsequently exhibited discoloration, including whitening, yellowing, or darkening, a high-frequency electrotome snare (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) or cryotherapy (Kooland, Beijing, China) was employed for tumor resection. Normal saline was periodically used to maintain a clear visual field. The removal process was conducted incrementally via a combination of rigid and flexible forceps.

Endpoint definition

The primary endpoint was intraoperative bleeding, and the secondary endpoints included symptom relief, the restoration of airway patency, overall survival and complications.

The classification of intraoperative bleeding severity is as follows: minor (requiring a single repeat application of the APC), moderate (involving prolonged mechanical compression and/or multiple repeat applications of the APC), and major (necessitating arterial embolization or emergency surgery) [9].

The classification of airway obstruction severity was conducted in accordance with the Myer‒Cotton stenosis grading system, encompassing Grade I (≤ 50% luminal stenosis), Grade II (51–70% luminal stenosis), Grade III (71–99% luminal stenosis), and Grade IV (absence of lumen) [10].

Symptom relief was defined as a reduction of ≥ 1 point on the Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) Dyspnea Scale after bronchoscopy resection.

Survival after intervention was calculated as the duration from the time of bronchoscopy resection to the time of death or data collection.

Restoration of airway patency was quantified using contrast-enhanced chest CT, with measurements conducted independently by two radiologists; the minimal luminal diameter was calculated as the average of the anteroposterior and transverse diameters at the narrowest segment of the obstructed airway, and response was categorized into three levels: Complete Response (CR), defined as the minimal luminal diameter at the obstruction site restored to ≥ 95% of the contralateral normal bronchus/trachea diameter at the corresponding anatomical level; Partial Response (PR), defined as the minimal luminal diameter restored to 50–94% of the contralateral normal airway diameter; and No Response, defined as the minimal luminal diameter restored to < 50% of the contralateral normal airway diameter.

The duration of the bronchoscopic procedure was accurately recorded.

Follow-up

The patients received follow-up assessments through chest CT scans at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months and subsequently every 6 months after the procedure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out via SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) was applied to variables with a normal distribution (patient age, luminal diameter, mMRC Dyspnea Scale score, tumor enhancement CT value). Median was used for variables with a skewed distribution (follow-up duration, maximum axial diameter of tumors, tumor-airway contact base area, minimum distance to adjacent major thoracic vessels). Categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages.

Results

During the study period, a total of 85 patients with CAO due to airway tumors presented to our center. Of these, 47 patients with CAO did not undergo BAE because they lacked objective evidence of high intraprocedural bleeding risk per protocol; Among these 47 non-BAE patients, 17 underwent endobronchial resection without prior embolization; the other 30 received alternative management (palliative stenting, radiotherapy, or best supportive care) per MDT consensus. The remaining 38 patients met initial eligibility criteria for potential BAE and airway intervention, and 24 constituted the final BAE cohort.

These 24 CAO patients (15 males and 9 females), with a mean age of 61 ± 10 years (42–87 years), were followed for a median (range) of 26 (20–54) months. Figure 1 presents the flow diagram of the enrolled patients.

Cough was observed in all 24 patients, dyspnea in 20 patients, and hemoptysis in 18 patients. All patients with hemoptysis underwent hemostasis with drugs. CT revealed a polypoid shaped tumor morphology with narrow or wide basements. Distal airway atelectasis was present in 7 patients. All the masses showed strong enhancement (106.6 ± 26.8 Hu) in contrast-enhanced CT images.

The locations of the tumors in the airway included the lobar bronchus (17/24, 70.8%) and trachea (7/24, 29.2%). The degree of obstruction was Grade III in 15 (62.5%) patients and Grade IV in 9 (37.5%) patients.

The maximum axial diameter of tumors had a median of 3.2 cm(interquartile range [IQR]: 2.5–4.0 cm) ; the minimum distance to adjacent major thoracic vessels (aorta, main pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein) was a median of 1.1 cm (IQR: 0.6–1.5 cm); 8 patients had intramural tumor invasion while 16 had transmural invasion; the tumor-airway contact base area had a median of 4.5 cm² (IQR: 3.0–6.2 cm²).

The pathological diagnoses included lung squamous cell carcinoma in 10 patients, of whom 5 were stage IIB (T3N0M0), 3 stage IIIA (T3N2M0), and 2 stage IVA (T2N0M1a); lung adenocarcinoma in 7 patients, with 2 stage IIA (T2bN0M0), 2 stage IIIB (T3N3M0), and 3 stage IVA (T2N1M1a); carcinoid tumors in 4 patients, all staged as stage 1 (3 cases, T1bN0M0) or stage 2 (1 case, T2aN0M0) ; metastatic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma in 2 patients, both stage IVB (T4N1M1b); and lipoma in 1 patient.

The mean duration of bronchoscopic therapy was 40.2 ± 10.7 min. No respiratory distress or pneumothorax was observed. The arteries that were embolized included the right bronchial arteries and the left bronchial arteries in 23 patients. In one patient, the inferior thyroid artery, which was involved in the tumor blood supply, was embolized. Complications after BAE, including chest pain (11/24, 45.8%) and abdominal pain (3/24, 12.5%), were alleviated the day after receiving supportive therapies. Fever, shivering, and vomiting occurred in 9/24 (37.5%), 4/24 (16.7%), and 3/24 (12.5%) patients, respectively. Spinal cord injury was not observed in this study.

Symptom relief was observed in all patients. The mean baseline mMRC Dyspnea Scale score was 3.2 ± 0.5, which decreased to 1.1 ± 0.4 after the procedure.

Baseline mean minimal luminal diameter at the obstruction site was 3.1 ± 1.0 mm. At 1-month follow-up, CR was observed in 16 patients (66.7%) who had a mean diameter of 12.5 ± 1.3 mm, while 6 (33.3%) PR patients had a mean diameter of 8.7 ± 1.2 mm.

Among the 17 NSCLC patients, obstruction degree was Grade III in 10 (58.8%) patients and Grade IV in 7 (41.2%) patients; tumor location included 12 (70.6%) in the lobar bronchus and 5 (29.4%) in the trachea; treatment response was complete in 11 (64.7%) patients and partial in 6 (35.3%) patients. Thirteen patients received adjuvant therapy (5 stage ⅡB and 3 stage ⅢA patients received platinum-based chemotherapy; 3 stage ⅢB patients received concurrent chemoradiotherapy; 2 stage ⅣA patients received palliative chemotherapy/targeted therapy), while 4 with ECOG PS ≥ 2 only received best supportive care (BSC). All 4 carcinoid patients received no adjuvant therapy due to early-stage and low malignant potential. Both 2 metastatic esophageal carcinoma patients received palliative chemotherapy. During the follow-up, NSCLC patients who received adjuvant therapy had a mean survival of 36.8 ± 21.5 months, significantly longer than the 12.2 ± 4.1 months in the BSC-only group (P = 0.028). Local recurrence of airway obstruction was observed in 3 (17.6%) of the 17 NSCLC patients, occurring at 12, 18, and 24 months post-intervention. All recurrent cases underwent repeat bronchoscopic resection which successfully restored airway patency.

Discussion

In this study, we present a series of patients with central airway obstruction due to airway tumors treated with bronchial artery embolization before endobronchial resection. This treatment modality was shown to be safe and feasible, which simplified the process of reestablishing airway patency by reducing procedure-related bleeding. For malignant tumors located in the central airways, this combined therapy may also reduce the recurrence rate because residual tumors, which are invisible endoluminally during endobronchial resection, are also embolized and suffer from critical ischemia. We propose an actionable clinical algorithm: consider pre-bronchoscopy BAE in patients with central airway obstruction due to hypervascular tumors characterized by intense enhancement on contrast-enhanced CT or a history of significant hemoptysis.

At present, surgical resection continues to stand as the primary treatment modality for bronchial malignancies, particularly in cases of central airway obstruction. Nevertheless, the procedure entails inherent risks of considerable surgical trauma and postoperative complications [3]. Tumors commonly proliferate along the submucosal longitudinal axis or within the bronchial lumen, posing challenges to complete resection and predisposing patients to local recurrence [4, 11]. Endobronchial treatment has emerged as a viable alternative for patients ineligible for surgical intervention, boasting reported success rates ranging between 72.5% and 95% [12]. Notably, our study yielded a remarkable 100% success rate, which was attributed to the marked reduction in intraprocedural bleeding achieved through preoperative embolization.

The occurrence of significant bleeding during bronchoscopic surgery has been a substantial concern in recent publications [13, 14]. In cases of severe airway obstruction, intraprocedural bleeding poses a heightened risk of asphyxia. For example, Diaz-Jimenez et al. reported that two out of 14 carcinoid patients experienced bleeding exceeding 250 ml during endobronchial laser treatment [15]. Notably, in a substantial study by Brokx et al., problematic bleeding necessitated emergency surgery in one of 72 patients [16]. Similarly, in a separate investigation, one in 24 patients required emergency surgery due to massive hemorrhage [17]. A comprehensive analysis encompassing 587 procedures revealed that the incidence of emergency thoracotomy for controlling substantial bleeding was 0.7% [18]. Predominantly, instances of major hemorrhage manifest in hypervascular settings or cases where resection inadvertently severs a large arterial vessel within the tumor stalk. Previous research has indicated that tumors with prominent feeding arteries visible on contrast-enhanced chest CT scans require transcatheter arterial embolization prior to endoscopic debulking [19]. Notably, the average CT value of the tumors in this study was 106.6 ± 26.8 Hu, indicating a heightened risk of bleeding during direct endoscopic treatment.

Our study cohort was predominantly composed of lung squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), with bronchial carcinoid tumors accounting for a small minority—a distribution differing from some published series focused on carcinoid tumors, which were widely recognized for their marked vascularity [20]. Although SCCs generally have lower vascularity than carcinoids, accumulating evidence confirms that BAE remains a highly effective strategy for managing this histological subtype. A recent clinical study enrolling 41 NSCLC patients, 87.7% of whom were diagnosed with SCC, reported that BAE achieved an overall disease control rate (DCR) of 80.5% [21].

BAE represents a minimally invasive and efficacious technique for managing hemoptysis across a spectrum of diseases, including lung cancer [22]. Moreover, the documented efficacy of BAE conducted prior to endobronchial intervention encompasses bronchial tumors such as carcinoid tumors and the metastasis of renal cell carcinoma [23, 24]. Notably, Haiming Yang et al. successfully employed bronchoscopy in conjunction with BAE for the treatment of hypervascular primary airway tumors in four pediatric patients, and no instances of major bleeding or asphyxia were noted [25]. Within our cohort of 24 patients, angiographic imaging revealed the effective invisibility of contrast-enhanced masses subsequent to embolization. Subsequent bronchoscopic examinations revealed the characteristic dark green coloration and transparent surface of the tumor, with only 5 patients exhibiting manifestations of moderate bleeding. These observed outcomes indicate the potential utility of BAE preceding endobronchial resection in mitigating the vascularity surrounding the tumor and reducing the substantial risk of intraoperative bleeding.

To successfully remove a tumor from the airways, it is important to be able to access the entire extent of the tumor in the central airways, with a visible tumor margin [26]. However, the presence of an underlying extraluminal tumor hidden in the tracheal wall can make complete endobronchial resection more challenging [27]. There is a possibility that BAE could cause critical ischemia to residual tumors in the bronchial wall following resection, which may increase the chance of a cure [28]. One case report involved a patient with bronchial carcinoid who received arterial embolization, endobronchial resection, surgical lobectomy, and lymph node dissection. Postoperative pathology revealed no evidence of residual tumor [23]. This combined therapy may offer a potential cure for intratracheal tumors, but further studies are needed to confirm its effectiveness.

Spinal cord injury was not observed in this study. This favorable outcome may reflect both meticulous intra-procedural identification and avoidance of spinal arteries by our experienced operators, but it is critical to interpret this finding with caution given our small sample size. Spinal cord injury remains a rare but catastrophic potential complication of BAE, and vigilant assessment of spinal arteries must be prioritized in every procedure to mitigate the risk of ectopic embolization.

There were several limitations in this study. First, the study’s sample size was small, raising the possibility of selection bias. Second, this was a single-arm retrospective pilot study, which was neither designed nor powered to conduct inferential statistical analyses. No comparative analyses were performed, and all findings should be interpreted as preliminary observations rather than definitive proof of efficacy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this exploratory single-arm retrospective study suggests that prebronchoscopic BAE represents a potentially viable and safe option for minimizing bleeding during endobronchial resection of central airway tumors. Importantly, further validation through well-powered comparative prospective studies is warranted to confirm the clinical utility of this combined approach.
