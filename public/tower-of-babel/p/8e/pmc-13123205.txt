Changes in childhood vaccine hesitancy during the COVID-19 pandemic: a retrospective study based on data from Turkey
Authors: Seda Özmen Sever, Saliha Büşra Aksu, Güzin Zeren Öztürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaccine refusal is a growing public health concern, threatening herd immunity and the control of vaccine-preventable diseases. The COVID-19 pandemic has influenced healthcare delivery and vaccine refusal behavior as a proxy for vaccine hesitancy. This study aimed to assess changes in childhood vaccine refusal behavior during the pandemic in Turkey.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study including 2,786 children born between 2017 and 2022 who reached 24 months of age and had complete vaccination records from primary healthcare. Children were categorized as pre-pandemic or post-pandemic based on birthdate. Data on vaccine refusal type, timing, and frequency were collected. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests, with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsOverall, 3.7% (n = 102) of children had at least one vaccine refusal. Refusal rates were 3.9% before and 3.4% after the pandemic onset, with no significant difference (p = 0.474). However, the median age of first refusal shifted from 12 months pre-pandemic to 1 month post-pandemic. Vaccines given after one year (e.g., MMR, varicella, Hepatitis A) were more frequently refused pre-pandemic, whereas those scheduled in infancy were more often refused post-pandemic. Live vaccines and later doses in multi-dose schedules had the highest refusal rates. Early-onset refusals were strongly associated with multiple vaccine refusals (p < 0.01).ConclusionWhile overall refusal rates remained stable, the timing of hesitancy shifted to earlier infancy during the pandemic. These findings emphasize the need for targeted parental education and early communication strategies to sustain vaccine coverage and prevent resurgence of vaccine-preventable diseases.

Introduction

Vaccine refusal, regarded as one of the major threats to global health, is a complex phenomenon characterized by the delay in acceptance or outright rejection of vaccines despite the availability of vaccination services. It varies across time, place, and specific vaccines [1]. This concept poses a significant threat by potentially reversing the progress achieved through immunization, which remains the most cost-effective approach to preventing vaccine-preventable diseases [2]. A decline in optimal vaccination coverage further reduces herd immunity rates, leading to an increase in the incidence of communicable diseases.

Vaccine refusal is of particular importance for children, who are at heightened risk for vaccine-preventable diseases and their associated complications. To ensure optimal immunization coverage in global vaccination programs, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the inclusion of vaccines such as Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), Hepatitis B (Hep B), Polio, Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Measles-Mumps-Rubella (MMR), Haemophilus influenzae type B (Hib), pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), Rotavirus, and Human Papillomavirus (HPV) [3].

In Turkey, under the framework of the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) implemented in 2008, vaccines such as BCG, Hep B, Hep A, Hib, 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13), polio, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, MMR, and varicella are administered free of charge during childhood [4]. Vaccination services are primarily carried out in primary healthcare centers, and all vaccines included in the national immunization program are provided free of charge under national health policy. Although childhood vaccinations are not mandatory, families of children who miss their scheduled vaccinations are contacted and invited for immunization. During the pandemic, routine childhood immunization services continued without interruption across all primary healthcare centers, and necessary planning was undertaken to maintain appropriate conditions for vaccination delivery [5].

The frequency of vaccine refusal varies between countries [6]. The most significant reasons for vaccine refusal include concerns regarding risk-benefit issues such as vaccine safety and potential side effects, lack of information and awareness about vaccination and its importance, and various sociocultural and socioeconomic factors [7]. The COVID-19 pandemic further influenced vaccination rates by causing disruptions in healthcare service delivery [8] and altering public attitudes towards vaccination [9]. During this period, parental vaccination decisions and refusal behaviors were affected by multiple factors, including their socioeconomic and sociocultural status, sources of information, their own vaccination status, and the country in which they resided [10].

This study aimed to evaluate changes in childhood vaccine refusal behavior during the COVID-19 pandemic for primary immunizations administered during the first two years of life among children who presented to primary healthcare centers for vaccination in our country, using documented refusal events as a proxy indicator of vaccine hesitancy.

Materials and methods

This study is observational and retrospective.

Study population and sample

The study was conducted in the Family Health Centers, which are primary healthcare institutions affiliated with the Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital.

Children born between 2017 and 2022 were included in the study. Based on 2020-the year when COVID-19 cases were first reported in Turkey-the children were divided into two groups: those who were born before the pandemic and had reached the age of two prior to 2020, and those who were born after the onset of the pandemic and had reached the age of two thereafter, ensuring that all routine vaccinations scheduled up to 24 months were completed within the same calendar period for each cohort.

Data collection

Data for a total of 2.786 children who were born between the specified years, had reached the age of two, and had complete records were obtained through file review.

Information collected included the child’s date of birth, sex, the presence or absence of vaccine refusal for the immunizations recommended within the first two years of life, and if applicable, the specific vaccines and doses that were refused. For children with vaccine refusal, the specific age (month) at which the first vaccine refusal occurred was also documented.

In this study, only the vaccines scheduled to be administered by 24 months of age were evaluated. Vaccinations due after the 24th month were not included in the analysis. According to the EPI in Turkey, by the age of two, children are expected to have received all scheduled doses of Hep B, Hep A, DTaP-IPV-Hib, PCV13, oral polio vaccine (OPV), and varicella vaccine, as well as the first dose of MMR vaccine.

As this study was based on retrospective vaccination records, refusal was defined operationally by non-receipt of scheduled vaccines recommended within the scope of the national immunization program.

Statistical analysis

The study parameters were classified as categorical and discrete variables. Descriptive statistics were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for numerical data, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and p-values < 0.05 were considered to indicate non-normal distribution. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using Pearson’s Chi-square test. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate vaccine refusal and its associated factors. The presence of vaccine refusal, multiple vaccine refusals, and the timing of the first refusal were considered as dependent variables, while sex, pre-/post-pandemic birth status, and the month in which the first refusal occurred were included as independent variables. Model fit was assessed using the Nagelkerke R² statistic, and results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In addition, time-to-event analysis was performed to evaluate the timing of the first vaccine refusal. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to compare time to first refusal between children born before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital (Approval No: 4395, dated May 14, 2024) and by the Ethics Committee of the Istanbul Provincial Directorate of Public Health (Approval date: December 25, 2024).

Results

A total of 2.786 children were included in the study. Of these, 49.6% (n = 1.382) were female and 50.4% (n = 1.404) were male. Among the participants, 1.460 children (52.4%) were born before the pandemic period, while 1.326 children (47.6%) were born after the onset of the pandemic.

Among all children, 102 (3.7%) had documented vaccine refusal. Only one child had refused all vaccines scheduled up to two years of age. When vaccine refusal was analyzed by vaccine type and dose, the most frequently refused vaccines were the first dose of the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine (n = 63, 61.8%), the booster dose of the PCV13 (n = 63, 61.8%), and the varicella vaccine (n = 60, 58.8%).

Regardless of birth period, the median age at which the first vaccine refusal occurred was 6 (IQR: 1–12) months. Among children born before the pandemic, the median month of first vaccine refusal was 12 (IQR: 2–18), whereas it was 1 (IQR: 1–6) month among those born after the onset of the pandemic.

Among children with vaccine refusal, 11 (10.8%) had not received any doses of Hep B, 26 (25.5%) had missed all doses of DTaP-IPV-Hib, 4 (3.9%) had not received any doses of PCV13, 30 (29.4%) had missed all doses of OPV, and 36 (35.3%) had not received any doses of Hep A.

Among those who refused the MMR vaccine, 92.1% (n = 58) also refused the varicella vaccine, and this association was statistically significant (p = 0.000). Additionally, all children who refused all doses of OPV also refused MMR, and 90% (n = 27) of them also refused varicella; both of these associations were statistically significant (p = 0.000, p = 0.000, respectively).

When vaccine refusal rates were evaluated in relation to the pandemic period, 57 children (3.9%) born before the pandemic had documented vaccine refusal, compared to 45 children (3.4%) born after the onset of the pandemic. There was no statistically significant difference in the frequency of vaccine refusal between children born before and after the pandemic (p = 0.474).

Among the children with vaccine refusal, 18 (17.6%) had refused only one type of vaccine, whereas the majority (n = 84, 82.4%) had refused more than one type. In both the pre-pandemic and post-pandemic groups, children who exhibited vaccine refusal at earlier months of age were more likely to refuse multiple types of vaccines (p = 0.000 and p = 0.006, respectively).

As shown in Table 2, the frequency of vaccine refusal for vaccines administered at or after one year of age-including the booster dose of DTaP-IPV-Hib, both doses of Hep A, the second dose of OPV, varicella, and MMR-was found to be lower in the post-pandemic period. In contrast, vaccine refusal rates for those administered before the age of one appeared to have increased during this period. Additionally, for most multi-dose vaccines, the frequency of refusal was higher for subsequent doses compared to the initial dose.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between the presence of vaccine refusal and sex as well as pre-/post-pandemic birth status. Male sex was not significantly associated with vaccine refusal (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.64–1.41, p = 0.786). Although the likelihood of vaccine refusal appeared lower among children born after the pandemic compared with those born before, this difference was not statistically significant (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.58–1.29, p = 0.477). The model demonstrated very low explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.001).

A separate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between the presence of one or more vaccine refusals and sex and pre-/post-pandemic birth status. Neither male sex (OR = 0.58, p = 0.311) nor being born after the pandemic (OR = 2.28, p = 0.150) was significantly associated with having one or more vaccine refusals. The model showed low explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.056), indicating that the included variables were insufficient to explain vaccine refusal.

In the logistic regression analysis evaluating the association between multiple vaccine refusals and the timing of the first refusal, the model demonstrated high explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.548). A later month of first refusal was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of multiple vaccine refusals (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.69–0.86, p < 0.001).

Among children born after the pandemic, the first vaccine refusal occurred at significantly earlier months compared with those born before the pandemic. In the logistic regression analysis, this difference was statistically significant (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.85–0.96, p = 0.001). The model showed moderate explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.169), indicating that birth period partially explains the timing of the first refusal.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a marked difference in the timing of the first vaccine refusal between children born before and after the pandemic. Among children born after the pandemic, the median time to first refusal was 1 month (95% CI: 0.34–1.65), with a mean of 4.9 months. In contrast, among children born before the pandemic, the median time to first refusal was 12 months (95% CI: 9.74–14.26), with a mean of 10.6 months. For the entire sample, the median time to first refusal was 6 months (95% CI: 3.21–8.79).

The log-rank test confirmed that the difference between groups was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This retrospective study investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on vaccine refusal for childhood immunizations administered within the first two years of life. The overall frequency of vaccine refusal among children was 3.7%, with no statistically significant difference observed between children born before and after the pandemic. However, while vaccine refusals were more frequently observed for vaccines administered at or after one year of age in the pre-pandemic period, vaccines scheduled before one year of age were more frequently refused in the post-pandemic period. Furthermore, for multi-dose vaccines, refusal rates tended to increase with subsequent doses, suggesting that refusal behavior may persist or expand across doses over time. Early-onset vaccine refusal was also found to be associated with the refusal of multiple vaccine types, indicating a broader pattern of repeated vaccine refusal behavior, rather than refusal limited to a single immunization.

The frequency of vaccine refusal varies by year and across countries. In a 2020 study conducted in 18 European countries, national vaccine refusal rates were reported to range between 14% and 41% [11]. A meta-analysis that reviewed studies from 23 different countries found a cumulative prevalence of vaccine refusal of 21.1% among children aged 0–6 years. Based on national income levels, vaccine refusal rates ranged from 3.4% to 41.6% in low-income countries, from 5% to 34.9% in middle-income countries, and from 7.7% to 22.5% in high-income countries [6]. In Turkey, one study conducted in a region with a low level of socioeconomic development reported a vaccine refusal rate of approximately 25%, whereas another study in a highly developed region found a much lower rate of 2.3% [12, 13]. In our study, which was conducted in a highly developed region, the overall vaccine refusal rate among all children was found to be 3.7%.

In our study, vaccine refusal was more frequently observed for vaccines administered at or after one year of age in the pre-pandemic period, whereas it was more common for vaccines scheduled within the first year of life in the post-pandemic period. This age-specific shift may be explained by pandemic-related factors that disproportionately affected parents of infants. During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, infants were perceived as particularly vulnerable, and parents may have been more likely to avoid healthcare settings due to fear of SARS-CoV-2 exposure. In addition, the first months of life coincided with the most uncertain period of the pandemic, characterized by mobility restrictions, inconsistent public health messaging, and heightened anxiety, which may have temporarily disrupted vaccination behaviors in this age group. Also this may indicate that the influence of vaccine refusal, which began prior to the pandemic, persisted into the post-pandemic period. It could also be interpreted as an indicator of the stability of vaccine refusal behavior. Previous studies evaluating the reasons for vaccine refusal in Turkey have reported various factors, including religious beliefs, distrust in pharmaceutical companies, doubts about vaccine efficacy, and concerns about vaccine ingredients [14–16]. These reasons often reflect personal beliefs shaped by sociocultural and socioeconomic differences, where individual perceptions may override evidence-based recommendations.

However, despite the earlier onset of vaccine refusal in the post-pandemic period in our study, the intensity of refusal appears to have diminished, as it was less sustained over time. This interpretation is supported by the finding that the median age of first vaccine refusal was 12 months in the pre-pandemic group and only 1 month in the post-pandemic group. Additionally, refusal rates for vaccines administered at or after one year of age were lower in the post-pandemic period. It is important to note that routine childhood vaccines included in the national immunization program remained available throughout the pandemic period in Türkiye, and primary healthcare vaccination services continued without interruption. Therefore, the lower refusal rates observed for vaccines administered at or after one year of age are unlikely to be attributable to vaccine unavailability and more likely reflect changes in vaccination behavior rather than supply-related barriers. These findings suggest that although vaccine refusal emerged earlier, it did not persist throughout the full vaccination schedule, possibly indicating a shift from rigid refusal to more transient hesitancy as individuals were exposed to more evidence-based information and awareness increased.

The COVID-19 pandemic has been a globally influential period affecting both vaccine refusal and acceptance. This era was marked by a surge in social media use and a corresponding rise in misinformation [17]. As a result, individuals were exposed to an overwhelming amount of vaccine-related claims—most of which were inaccurate—through social media platforms [18]. This environment contributed to increased public anxiety, the development of false beliefs, and widespread dissemination of misinformation regarding vaccines. Although some studies have shown that parental vaccine hesitancy increased during the post-pandemic period and that the frequency of routine childhood immunization decreased compared to the pre-pandemic era [19, 20], other studies have reported that parents’ perceptions of the importance of vaccination did not change and that vaccination rates remained stable [21–23]. The pandemic significantly disrupted daily life through social isolation, travel bans, and the loss of routine activities. Despite numerous non-pharmaceutical interventions—such as hand hygiene, mobility restrictions, and lockdowns—effective pandemic control was largely achieved through vaccination efforts. As demonstrated in our study, the observed decline in vaccine refusal in the post-pandemic period may reflect renewed recognition of the importance and effectiveness of vaccines. This trend may represent a transition from vaccine refusal to hesitancy or even acceptance. The role of COVID-19 vaccines in controlling the pandemic may have supported this shift in public attitude.

In our study, two notable patterns emerged regarding the vaccines that were refused: a relatively high frequency of refusal for live vaccines and a tendency for higher refusal rates in the subsequent doses of multi-dose vaccines.

In recent years, there has been a global increase in cases of measles and poliomyelitis. In 2022, the number of measles cases more than doubled compared to the previous year. During the same period, the number of children who developed paralysis due to poliovirus increased by 16% compared to the prior year. Additionally, between 2019 and 2021, the number of children who developed paralysis due to polio was reported to be eight times higher than in the previous three-year period [24]. Stanescu et al. reported that MMR vaccination coverage in their country reached its lowest level in the past decade in 2022 [25]. According to CDC data, MMR vaccination coverage decreased by 2.1% in 2022–2023 compared to 2019–2020 [26]. In 2021, only 25 of the 53 European regions had an OPV vaccination coverage rate above 95% [27]. Similarly, a study by Kara et al. reported a decline in OPV and Hep A vaccine uptake in Turkey in 2020 compared to 2019 [28].

Consistent with these findings, our study also identified MMR and OPV as the most frequently refused vaccines, both before and after the pandemic. Notably, refusal rates for the varicella vaccine were also high. Furthermore, children who refused one of these vaccines were significantly more likely to refuse the others as well. The high refusal rates for live vaccines may be influenced by parental beliefs that these diseases are no longer common or by misconceptions about serious but unfounded side effects, such as the belief that vaccines cause autism.

The increased frequency of refusal for subsequent doses in multi-dose vaccine series may be influenced by parental beliefs that fewer doses are sufficient and that completing the full vaccination schedule is unnecessary. As routine childhood vaccines in Türkiye are routinely provided free of charge and immunization services continued during the pandemic, the observed refusal patterns are unlikely to be explained by access, cost, or service disruption. In addition, adherence to the vaccination schedule requires active follow-up, and missed appointments or a lack of awareness about booster schedules may also contribute to the higher refusal rates for later doses. Implementing reminder and notification systems for parents-regardless of the child’s reason for visiting a healthcare facility-may improve compliance with vaccination schedules and help reduce refusal rates. In both scenarios, we believe that providing accurate information to all parents about the benefits, efficacy, and potential side effects of vaccines, and addressing concerns that may lead to hesitancy, can play a key role in decreasing vaccine refusal.

Limitations.

This study has several limitations. The sample was drawn from family health centers located in regions with relatively similar socioeconomic characteristics, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the national level. Future studies including data from regions with diverse socioeconomic profiles, as well as from both urban and rural settings, would yield more representative results.

In addition, due to the retrospective design of the study, the underlying reasons for parental vaccine refusal could not be directly assessed. To better understand these factors, future prospective studies incorporating questionnaires or qualitative methods are warranted. Furthermore, the available dataset included a limited number of variables, such as sex and pre-/post-pandemic birth period, and did not capture socioeconomic or cultural factors. As a result, the explanatory power of the multivariable analyses was limited. Future studies with larger samples and a broader range of variables are expected to provide more robust insights into the determinants of vaccine refusal.

Conclusion

This study, which evaluated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on childhood vaccine refusal, found that overall vaccine refusal rates were similar in the pre-pandemic and post-pandemic periods. However, in the post-pandemic period, vaccine refusal was observed to begin at earlier ages, with an increase in refusals particularly for vaccines scheduled before one year of age, while refusal rates declined for vaccines administered after one year. In both periods, higher refusal rates were observed for live virus vaccines. Additionally, in multi-dose vaccine schedules, later doses were more frequently refused than initial doses.

These findings suggest that patterns of parental vaccination behavior and refusal timing have changed during the pandemic and highlight the critical role of healthcare providers in addressing vaccine hesitancy through informative and supportive communication. Therefore, developing strategies to enhance trust in vaccines and ensuring access to accurate information will be essential in improving childhood immunization coverage.
