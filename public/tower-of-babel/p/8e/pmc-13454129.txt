Association between HALP score and in-hospital outcomes in patients with acute kidney injury: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Fulian Lin, Zhihui Chen, Tong Zhou, Jincheng Tang, Heping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a prevalent clinical syndrome among hospitalized patients, affecting 20–30% of inpatients, with mortality rates exceeding 50% in ICU settings. The HALP score (hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet), a composite biomarker reflecting inflammatory and nutritional status, has shown prognostic value across various conditions, while the application of the HALP score in AKI remains underexplored. In this study, a total of 1,005 patients with AKI were included. Fine-Gray competing risk models were employed for the primary analysis, with the robustness of the findings assessed through cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves and sensitivity analyses. After adjusting for confounders, a higher HALP score was significantly associated with partial recovery and complete recovery. The Q4 group also demonstrated the lowest cumulative risk of therapy cessation (Day 21: 11.0%), with significantly reduced risks observed in Q3 (SHR 0.66) and Q4 (SHR 0.39). Taken together, the HALP score is significantly associated with renal recovery and therapy cessation in patients with AKI and may serve as a convenient tool for risk stratification, thereby complementing personalized treatment decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55184-7.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by rapid renal function deterioration over hours to days, stemming from diverse etiologies and complex pathophysiological mechanisms, with clinical manifestations including azotemia, electrolyte imbalances, and volume disturbances1. AKI represents a significant global health burden, with approximately 13.3 million new cases annually worldwide2. Among hospitalized patients, the incidence ranges from 20 to 30%, with mortality rates averaging 15–20%3. In intensive care units (ICUs), mortality can exceed 50%4. AKI survivors face long-term risks, including a ninefold increased risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression and a threefold higher risk of end-stage renal disease (ESRD)5, along with elevated rates of adverse cardiovascular events and rehospitalization, further straining global healthcare systems6.

Given the poor prognosis of AKI, early identification of high-risk patients is essential for guiding clinical decision-making, such as intensified monitoring and early intervention, thereby potentially improving patient outcomes. While emerging biomarkers such as NGAL, KIM-1, L-FABP, and cystatin C show prognostic promise7, they require specialized detection methods, incur high costs, and involve prolonged turnaround times, limiting their clinical applicability. Therefore, scoring systems based on routine laboratory parameters offer considerable clinical utility for prognosis assessment.

The HALP score, introduced by Chen et al. in 20158, provides a comprehensive reflection of the body’s inflammatory, nutritional, and immune status. Specifically, hemoglobin indicates oxygen transport capacity and anemia, albumin represents nutritional status and hepatic function, lymphocytes reflect immune function, and platelets are involved in inflammatory responses and coagulation9. The prognostic value of the HALP score has been validated in several clinical contexts. In oncology, it predicts survival in various cancers, including gastric and lung cancers10; in respiratory medicine, it forecasts outcomes in acute COPD exacerbations11; in cardiovascular medicine, it correlates with in-hospital mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with myocardial infarction12; and in neurology, it predicts functional outcomes in patients with ischemic stroke13.

Despite its established prognostic value in multiple diseases, the application of the HALP score in AKI—a syndrome of acute organ dysfunction—remains underexplored. Following AKI onset and during recovery, patients are vulnerable to protein-energy malnutrition14. Inflammatory-nutritional biomarkers, including the lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and red cell distribution width-to-platelet ratio (RPR), have been used to assess AKI prognosis15–17. However, these markers primarily reflect single pathophysiological dimensions, lacking the multi-dimensional integrative approach offered by the HALP score. Notably, the HALP score has shown predictive potential in CKD, with Yuan et al.18 demonstrating its ability to independently predict the progression of IgA nephropathy, suggesting its broader applicability in renal disorders.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE statement guidelines. A completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1. A total of 1,005 adult patients with AKI were consecutively enrolled from the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College between February 2020 and February 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 18 years or older; (2) patients meeting the AKI diagnostic criteria defined by the 2012 KDIGO guidelines19, specifically fulfilling one of the following conditions: serum creatinine (SCr) elevation ≥ 26.5 µmol/L within 48 h; SCr elevation ≥ 1.5 times the baseline value within 7 days; urine output < 0.5 mL/(kg·h) for 6 consecutive hours. There is currently no universally accepted standard for defining baseline SCr in AKI studies20. According to literature and previous studies21,22, baseline SCr was defined as the most recent SCr value obtained within 7 days before admission, if available; otherwise, the initial SCr measurement upon admission was used as the baseline reference. (3) For patients with multiple hospital admissions, only data from the initial hospitalization were included in the analysis. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) hospital stay of less than 48 h (insufficient time for necessary examinations and observations); (2) hematological disorders (including hematological malignancies or severe blood diseases); (3) pre-existing CKD stage 3b or higher (eGFR < 45 ml/min/1.73 m2), ESRD, or history of kidney transplantation; (4) pregnancy or lactation; (5) patients with incomplete research data. The detailed screening process is shown in Fig. 1. Among them, 821 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, including 204 with a hospital stay of less than 48 h, 85 with hematological disorders, 298 with a history of CKD stage 3b or higher, 170 with ESRD, 7 with a history of kidney transplantation, and 57 who were pregnant or lactating. An additional 689 patients were excluded due to missing data, including 88 with incomplete HALP score-related parameters, 476 with > 30% missing baseline clinical and laboratory data, and 125 lacking follow-up information (defined as having only a single serum creatinine measurement during the entire hospitalization with no subsequent renal function follow-up records). Given no known biological or clinical association between these exclusion reasons and the exposure variable (HALP score), the exclusion process essentially constitutes non-informative censoring with respect to the exposure. Ultimately, 1,005 patients were included.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College (approval number: 2025ER274-1) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective design and anonymized data processing, the Medical Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College waived the requirement for informed consent.

Data collection

Research data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system (EMR) and laboratory information system (LIS) and were independently collected by two researchers, followed by cross-verification. The variables collected included: (1) Baseline characteristics: age, gender, body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kg divided by height in m2); (2) First vital signs within 24 h of admission: heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean blood pressure (MBP, calculated as DBP plus one-third of the pulse pressure).; (3) Medical history and comorbidities (according to ICD-10 codes): hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, heart failure, chronic liver disease, history of malignant neoplasm; admission diagnosis: sepsis; (4) Treatment measures (within 48 h of AKI diagnosis): Drug therapy, including vasoactive drugs (norepinephrine, dopamine, etc.) and statins; organ support, including mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy (RRT, comprising intermittent hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapy); intensive care: ICU admission; (5) AKI-related variables were defined based on KDIGO criteria19: AKI staging: Stage 1—SCr increase of 1.5–1.9 times baseline or increase ≥ 26.5 µmol/L; Stage 2—SCr increase of 2.0-2.9 times baseline; Stage 3—SCr increase of ≥ 3 times baseline or ≥ 353.6 µmol/L or initiation of RRT. Of the 1,005 patients included in the final analysis, 128 (12.7%) had baseline SCr defined using admission values, and the remainder had baseline SCr defined using the most recent SCr value obtained within 7 days before admission. AKI etiology was classified into prerenal, renal, and postrenal categories. Prerenal AKI was defined by evidence of volume depletion or decreased effective circulating volume. Renal AKI was defined by evidence of intrinsic renal parenchymal injury, including acute tubular necrosis, acute interstitial nephritis, or glomerular disease. Postrenal AKI was defined by imaging-confirmed urinary tract obstruction. Classification was performed independently by two nephrologists, with disagreements resolved by a third nephrologist; (6) Laboratory indicators: First test results at the time of AKI diagnosis (within ± 6 h), including HALP score-related indicators: Hemoglobin (reference values: male 130–175 g/L, female 115–150 g/L); albumin (reference values: 40–55 g/L); lymphocyte count (reference values: 1.1–3.2 × 109/L); platelet count (reference values: 125–350 × 109/L); other laboratory indicators: red blood cell count, neutrophils, RDW, SCr, blood urea, eGFR (MDRD formula), blood uric acid, blood triglycerides, blood high-density lipoprotein, fasting glucose. The HALP score was calculated using the formula: HALP = [Hemoglobin (g/L) × Albumin (g/L) × Lymphocytes (109/L)] / platelets (109/L). To minimize the impact of skewed distribution, a natural logarithmic transformation was applied to the HALP score during analysis.

Endpoints

The definition of renal function recovery followed the ADQI consensus and recent literature23,24. Renal recovery was classified based on SCr levels at discharge: complete recovery was defined as SCr within 105% of baseline; partial recovery was defined as SCr between 105% and 150% of baseline; non-recovery was defined as SCr exceeding 150% of baseline or the requirement for dialysis. Primary endpoints: Complete renal function recovery (at discharge); Secondary endpoints: (1) Partial renal function recovery; (2) Therapy cessation. Therapy cessation was defined as patient-initiated discharge from the hospital while renal function had not yet recovered; (3) In-hospital all-cause death.

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by a pre-specified directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify a minimally sufficient adjustment set (MSAS). The MSAS was defined a priori based on a synthesis of existing literature1,25 and expert clinical knowledge. It included the following variables: age, sex, baseline renal function (eGFR), body mass index (BMI), AKI stage, etiology of AKI, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, chronic liver disease, and malignancy), a proxy for inflammation (sepsis), and proxies for hemodynamic status (mean blood pressure, heart rate, and respiratory rate). Potential mediators, such as in-hospital interventions following admission, were intentionally excluded from the primary analysis model to focus on direct effects. The corresponding DAG is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Based on effect sizes and expected event rates from previous studies, a sample size of 1,005 cases was determined to detect an effect with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.5 (α = 0.05, β = 0.20). Patients were categorized into HALP score quartiles: Q1(< P25), Q2(P25-P50), Q3(P50-P75), Q4(> P75), with cutoff values of 12.35, 20.93, and 36.17. As visually summarized in Supplementary Fig. 3, the HALP score and its individual components—hemoglobin, serum albumin, lymphocyte count, and platelet count—were evaluated for their distributional characteristics. The raw HALP score displayed marked right-skewness (Shapiro–Wilk test, P < 0.001). To address this skewness, reduce the influence of extreme values, and enhance numerical stability, a natural logarithmic transformation was applied. The transformed ln (HALP) variable demonstrated approximately symmetric distribution and was used as a continuous variable in primary analyses.

For handling missing data, variables with missing rates exceeding 30% were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm26 via the knn Imputation function from the DMwR package in R. The distribution of missing data is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. To prevent data leakage, the imputation process was strictly conducted without incorporating any outcome information. To assess the robustness of our findings, a complete-case sensitivity analysis was also performed (Supplementary Table 1).

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as n (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test (when expected frequencies were less than 5). In this study, in-hospital death, therapy cessation, and partial recovery were considered competing risks for the outcomes of renal function recovery. To account for the competing risk nature of the data appropriately, we employed Fine-Gray proportional subdistribution hazards models for our primary analyses. For each primary outcome, the other events were treated as competing risks. This method allows for the direct estimation of the subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR). The association between HALP score quartiles (with Q1 as the reference group) and each clinical outcome was evaluated by calculating the SHRs along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cause-specific hazard models were employed in sensitivity analyses for in-hospital death to assess the potential influence of competing events. All models were adjusted for a comprehensive set of prespecified potential confounders.

To visually depict the time-dependent incidence of each outcome across HALP score quartiles under the competing risk framework, we plotted the corresponding cumulative incidence functions (CIFs). Restricted cubic splines (RCS, 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) were used to assess the nonlinear relationship between the natural logarithmic transformation HALP score and outcomes, with nonlinear testing conducted using likelihood ratio tests. The predictive performance and incremental value of the HALP score were assessed using time-specific AUCs and Brier scores evaluated at fixed time points (days 7, 14, 21 and 28). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the HALP score.

To ensure the robustness of the results, a series of sensitivity analyses was conducted. First,

Landmark analyses were performed at 7 and 14 days. Only patients who remained at risk at the respective landmark time points were included. The time origin was reset to the landmark time, and Fine-Gray models were refitted to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs), thereby assessing the influence of early clinical events (such as discharge or death) on risk estimation. Additionally, to minimize potential bias arising from variations in hospitalization duration and discharge policies, follow-up was uniformly administratively censored at 21 and 28 days. Outcomes occurring beyond these time windows were treated as censored. Using the same covariate set as in the primary analysis, Fine-Gray models were refitted on these censored datasets to further verify the consistency of the association between HALP and the clinical outcomes. Other sensitivity analyses included the exclusion of ICU patients and trimming of extreme values of the HALP score to minimize the influence of extreme physiological states. To further explore the predictive performance of the HALP score across different populations and assess potential heterogeneity, interaction analyses were conducted for age, sex, and ICU admission. All tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at 0.05. Bonferroni and Holm corrections were applied to control for multiple comparisons (corrected α = 0.05/n).

Results

Demographic and baseline characteristics

Based on HALP score quartiles, this study categorized 1,005 patients with AKI into four groups (Q1-Q4). The mean age of the included patients was 61.60 ± 16.51 years. AKI staging was as follows: stage 1 in 353 cases (35.12%), stage 2 in 196 cases (19.50%), and stage 3 in 456 cases (45.37%). Compared to the Q1 group, the Q4 group had a younger age, higher proportion of male patients, higher BMI, lower prevalence of malignant tumors, and a lower rate of statin medication use. Differences in the distribution of AKI etiology were observed (P = 0.009). The Q4 group had a lower proportion of prerenal causes compared to Q1 (59.36% vs. 68.65%), a higher proportion of renal causes (27.49% vs. 20.63%), and a similar proportion of postrenal causes (7.97% vs. 9.52%). Regarding prognosis, the Q4 group exhibited significantly higher rates of partial renal function recovery (32.67% vs. 23.81%, P < 0.001) and complete recovery (43.43% vs. 32.14%, P < 0.001) compared to the Q1 group (Table 1).

Table 2 presents the distribution of laboratory indicators across HALP score groups. As anticipated, the four HALP score components (hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocytes, and platelets) showed significant differences between groups (all P < 0.05). Compared to the Q1 group, the Q4 group had higher red blood cell count, higher eGFR, higher triglycerides, and higher uric acid levels, while RDW and blood urea were lower (all P < 0.05).

Association between HALP score and clinical outcomes

The mean follow-up duration was 12 days and the median follow-up was 10 days. During this period, complete renal function recovery occurred in 356 patients (35.4%), partial recovery was observed in 310 patients (30.9%),205 patients (20.4%) requested therapy cessation, and in-hospital death was recorded in 106 patients (10.6%). Competing risks regression analysis, using Fine-Gray models, revealed the association between the HALP score and clinical outcomes (Table 3).

For complete renal function recovery, after adjusting for multiple confounding factors, a significant association was observed only in the Q4 group (SHR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.06–1.98, P = 0.019). The outcome of partial renal recovery demonstrated a consistently positive association across all higher quartiles, with Q2 (SHR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.02-2.00, P = 0.044), Q3 (SHR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.11–2.14, P = 0.009), and Q4 (SHR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.09–2.20, P = 0.015) groups all showing significantly elevated subdistribution hazard ratios compared to Q1. Compared to the Q1 group, the Q3 group (SHR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.45–0.97, P = 0.034) and the Q4 (SHR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.25–0.61, P < 0.001) group showed lower therapy cessation. The analysis of in-hospital mortality revealed no statistically significant associations across the quartiles. However, the pattern of point estimates suggested a potential non-linear relationship, wherein the Q3 group exhibited the lowest risk (SHR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.41–1.25, P = 0.240).

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. The associations between HALP score quartiles and outcomes remained consistent after excluding ICU patients (Supplementary Table 2), and no significant interaction was observed with age. Sex interactions were isolated to Q3 for renal recovery and lacked consistency across quartiles (Supplementary Table 3). Additional analyses addressing extreme HALP values and baseline physiological instability yielded consistent results (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Landmark analyses (at 7 and 14 days) and administrative censoring analyses (at 21 and 28 days) showed hazard ratios directionally consistent with the primary analysis, although some associations lost statistical significance (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7).

Patients with therapy cessation had a more severe clinical profile, including higher rates of sepsis, ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, RRT, and AKI stage 3 (Supplementary Table 8). Among these patients, the median ratio of discharge SCr to SCr at AKI diagnosis was 1.19 (IQR, 0.73–1.72). Based on the direction of SCr change, they were stratified into deteriorated (SCr increase > 20%; n = 100, 48.8%), stable (change within ± 20%; n = 51, 24.9%), and improved (decrease > 20%; n = 54, 26.3%). Of note, the term “improved” refers only to the direction of SCr change, not to renal recovery. After excluding the improved subgroup, the HALP score showed a dose–response relationship with adverse therapy cessation (Q3: SHR, 0.52, 95% CI, 0.32–0.84, P = 0.007; Q4: SHR, 0.35, 95% CI, 0.20–0.58, P < 0.001 vs. Q1), consistent with the main analysis of all 205 events (Supplementary Table 9).

Cumulative incidence of clinical outcomes by HALP score

To visually depict the dynamic association between the HALP score and clinical outcomes in patients with AKI, we plotted cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves. Cumulative incidence rates at pre-specified time points (Days 7, 14, and 21) are reported to provide estimates of absolute risk (Fig. 2).

The CIF curves revealed significant divergence in the time-to-event processes for renal recovery and therapy cessation across HALP score quartiles. For complete renal recovery, the highest HALP quartile (Q4) consistently demonstrated a higher cumulative incidence than the lowest quartile (Q1) at all time points. For instance, by Day 21, the cumulative recovery rate in the Q4 group was 37.3%, substantially surpassing the 28.3% observed in the Q1 group (Fig. 2A). A similar trend was observed for partial renal recovery, with the Q4 group maintaining higher cumulative incidence rates than the Q1 group on Days 14 and 21(Fig. 2B). Conversely, for therapy cessation, the Q4 group exhibited a significantly lower cumulative incidence (Day 21: Q4 = 11.0% vs. Q1 = 24.5%), indicating that a higher HALP score was associated with a reduced risk of adverse renal prognosis (Fig. 2C). Regarding in-hospital mortality, the CIF curves for the HALP quartiles largely overlapped throughout the hospitalization period. However, the Q3 group consistently showed numerically lower cumulative mortality across all time points compared to the Q1 reference group (Fig. 2D).

The testing of non-linear relationships

Restricted cubic splines were employed to further investigate the dose-response relationship between HALP score and clinical outcomes. No significant non-linear relationships were observed for the endpoints of complete recovery (P for non-linearity = 0.143, Fig. 3A), partial recovery(P for non-linearity = 0.068, Fig. 3B), and therapy cessation (P for non-linearity = 0.172, Fig. 3C), implying that these outcomes may follow a linear or other association pattern.

The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between the ln-transformed HALP score and in-hospital mortality (P for non-linearity = 0.028, Fig. 3D). Given the performance of four tests within the same family, we applied Bonferroni and Holm corrections. After this adjustment, the association with in-hospital mortality was no longer statistically significant (adjusted P = 0.112). In the cause-specific hazard model, the hazard ratio for Q2 versus Q1 was 0.97 (95% CI, 0.57–1.65, P = 0.908), which decreased to 0.59 (95% CI, 0.32–1.08, P = 0.086) in Q3 and returned to 1.04 (95% CI, 0.61–1.79, P = 0.880) in Q4. The protective effect in Q3 was more pronounced than in the Fine-Gray model (Q3: SHR, 0.72; Q4: SHR, 1.06).

Prediction performance of the HALP score

To quantify the predictive performance and incremental value of the HALP score for clinical outcomes in patients with AKI, we assessed the time‑specific AUC and Brier score at four fixed time points (Days 7, 14, 21, and 28). As detailed in Supplementary Table 10, the full model incorporating the HALP score consistently demonstrated superior predictive performance over the base model across all outcomes and time points. Regarding discrimination, the full model yielded higher AUC values. The most notable improvement in discrimination was observed for predicting therapy cessation, where the ΔAUC (Full - Base) exceeded 0.015 at all time points and reached 0.025 at Day 7. Smaller yet stable and consistent improvements in ΔAUC were noted for complete renal recovery and in-hospital death. In terms of overall performance, the full model also systematically achieved lower Brier scores than the base model (Δ Brier > 0), indicating a combined improvement in calibration and accuracy of the predicted probabilities after the inclusion of the HALP score.

To further dissect the contribution of each component, SHRs comparing the highest versus lowest quartiles of the HALP score and its four constituents were examined (Supplementary Table 11). Hemoglobin and albumin were the strongest individual predictors at certain time points for complete recovery and partial recovery. Discriminative performance was then compared at fixed time points (Supplementary Table 12). Although the combined model yielded the highest AUCs, the HALP score achieved near-equivalent discrimination, particularly for therapy cessation, where its performance closely approximated that of the combined model. For in-hospital mortality, all models showed strong and broadly comparable discriminative performance, with AUCs consistently exceeding 0.80. Based on these findings, clinical utility was further assessed using decision curve analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4). For therapy cessation, the net benefit curve of the HALP score was marginally higher than those of albumin and hemoglobin across the threshold probability range of 0% to 25%, whereas for in-hospital death, the net benefit curves of HALP, albumin, and hemoglobin largely overlapped.

Discussion

Based on a large cohort of patients with acute kidney injury (AKI), this study systematically evaluated the association between the HALP score and clinical outcomes in patients with AKI. Key findings were as follows: (1) a higher HALP score (Q4 group) was significantly associated with increased probabilities of both complete and partial renal function recovery; (2) higher HALP scores (particularly in the Q3 and Q4 groups) were significantly associated with a reduced risk of adverse kidney outcomes, including therapy cessation; (3) the potential non-linear relationship was observed between the HALP score and in-hospital mortality, although it did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple testing. These findings remained consistent across a series of sensitivity analyses.

Previous studies have consistently indicated that lower HALP scores are closely linked to adverse clinical outcomes. A meta-analysis encompassing 45 cohorts and 17,049 cancer patients showed that a higher HALP score was independently associated with improved overall survival (HR = 0.60), cancer-specific survival (HR = 0.53), and progression-free survival (HR = 0.62)27. Similar patterns have been observed not only in the general population28 but also among patients with diabetes29, coronary heart disease30, and critically ill patients31, which aligns with the findings of our present study. A key finding in this study was the significantly elevated SHR for complete renal function recovery in Q4 (SHR, 1.45, 95% CI, 1.06–1.98, P = 0.019), indicating a 45% higher probability compared with Q1 after accounting for competing risks. The association with partial recovery was broader, with significantly higher SHRs in Q2 (1.43, 95% CI, 1.02–2.00, P = 0.044), Q3 (1.54, 95% CI, 1.11–2.14, P = 0.009), and Q4 (1.55, 95% CI, 1.09–2.20, P = 0.015). Renal repair after AKI is a complex process involving the resolution of inflammation and cellular regeneration and repair32. Consequently, a higher HALP score may be beneficial for initiating or sustaining processes contributing to partial renal function recovery, potentially through mechanisms such as mitigating ongoing injury, supporting cell survival, or activating early repair pathways31,33.

The four components of the HALP score reflect distinct pathophysiological dimensions of AKI, and their combined effect helps explain the complex associations observed in the study34,35. Specifically, hemoglobin levels indicate oxygen delivery capacity36, and patients with AKI often suffer from concurrent anemia, which exacerbates renal hypoxia37. Albumin serves not only as a marker of nutritional status but also has several protective functions, including antioxidation, maintenance of colloid osmotic pressure, and toxin binding. Low albumin levels worsen oxidative stress and endothelial injury38,39. Lymphocyte-mediated adaptive immunity plays a pivotal role in AKI recovery; for instance, regulatory T cells aid tissue repair and inhibit fibrosis, while reduced lymphocyte counts often signal immune dysfunction and poor prognosis40. Lastly, platelets moderate activation aids tissue repair, whereas excessive activation promotes microthrombus formation and amplifies inflammation41. The study by Marín-Corte et al.42 demonstrated that the HALP score at the time of lupus nephritis diagnosis is an independent risk factor for renal relapse (HR = 18.2, 95% CI: 5.3–30.1, p < 0.001). This association may be attributed to the interference of systemic inflammation and nutritional imbalance, as reflected by the HALP score, with intrinsic renal repair processes. Supporting this concept, a retrospective study by Liang et al. involving 895 patients with IgA nephropathy found that a lower HALP score was significantly associated with an increased risk of severe tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis43, suggesting that the HALP score may capture key pathological states following AKI.

AKI-induced cell death occurs predominantly in the proximal tubule (PT), triggering an intrinsic repair process characterized by the dedifferentiation and proliferation of proximal tubular cells (PTCs), accompanied by compensatory mechanisms including inflammation and fibrosis44. The AKI recovery process can lead to one of two potential outcomes: successful adaptive repair, involving effective PTC replication to restore renal structure and function; or maladaptive repair, characterized by persistent pro-inflammatory PTCs, fibrosis, and the initiation of chronic kidney disease45. Systemic factors reflected by the HALP score, such as ischemic hypoxia, oxidative stress, and nutritional status, can directly influence this local repair process46,47. In renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) models, renal ischemia induces significant transcriptional changes in the proximal tubule. Although most damaged cells can repair, a specific subset of PTCs enters a state of maladaptive senescence. These cells fail to complete the repair program and become a source of persistent inflammation and fibrosis, thereby mediating incomplete recovery from AKI48. In this in-hospital cohort, incomplete renal recovery primarily manifested as therapy cessation and in-hospital death.

The association between the HALP score and reduced risk of therapy cessation was particularly noteworthy. Patients in the Q3 and Q4 groups demonstrated risk reductions of 34% (SHR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.97, P = 0.034) and 61% (SHR 0.39, 95% CI 0.25–0.61, P < 0.001), respectively, compared with the Q1 group. The pattern was preserved in a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with improving renal function at discharge (Q3: SHR 0.52, P = 0.007; Q4: SHR 0.35, P < 0.001), suggesting that the observed association was not driven by patients discharged for non-medical reasons. Notably, patients with therapy cessation had a significantly more severe clinical profile, with higher rates of sepsis, ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, and AKI stage 3, and nearly half (48.8%) exhibited worsening renal function at discharge. These findings indicate that therapy cessation in this cohort predominantly reflects withdrawal of care in the context of clinical deterioration rather than discharge against medical advice for socioeconomic reasons. In China, home has consistently been the predominant place of death49,50, and many patients express a preference for dying at home at the end of life51. A large-scale prospective study of a Chinese population (n = 42,956) demonstrated that 71.5% of deaths among patients occurred at home, while only 21.6% occurred in hospital, with a substantial proportion of patients dying shortly after discharge52. Given this background, it is reasonable to infer that a considerable number of patients in the therapy cessation group would have died in hospital had they not been discharged. The observed association between the HALP score and therapy cessation, therefore, likely captures the score’s ability to reflect overall illness severity and physiological reserve.

Regarding in-hospital death, no statistically significant association was observed across HALP quartiles in the Fine-Gray model (Q3: SHR 0.72, 95% CI 0.41–1.25, P = 0.240). The cause-specific model yielded a stronger protective signal in Q3 (HR, 0.59, 95% CI, 0.32–1.08, P = 0.086), suggesting that the high incidence of competing events—particularly therapy cessation—may have attenuated the association by removing high-risk patients from the risk set before death occurred. Both models consistently revealed a U-shaped pattern, with the lowest risk in Q3 and risk in Q4 reverting to a level comparable to Q1. Restricted cubic spline analysis initially suggested a potential nonlinear association (P for nonlinearity = 0.028), which did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing (adjusted P = 0.112).Li et al.53 demonstrated a nonlinear association between HALP score and 28-day mortality in 2,968 patients with sepsis (inflection point: 24.69). Given that sepsis is a common etiology of AKI, future large-scale studies are needed to validate this potential nonlinear relationship in patients with AKI. The absence of a statistically significant association in the present study must also be interpreted in light of the limited number of deaths (n = 106) and the potential for residual confounding, including by unmeasured factors such as illness severity scores, fluid balance, and transfusion status.

Supplementary analyses confirmed the incremental predictive value of the HALP score over its individual components and the base model. Although albumin or hemoglobin showed higher SHRs and AUCs at certain time points, this is not contradictory. The value of the HALP score lies not in surpassing every component at every time point, but in identifying patients with “local normality but global imbalance”—for instance, normal albumin with markedly reduced lymphocytes—where individual markers may miss the overall risk. While statistical discrimination metrics are important, they do not directly reflect clinical utility, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) offers a more clinically meaningful assessment54. In this study, the net benefit of the HALP score was marginally higher than that of albumin and hemoglobin for therapy cessation. In terms of discrimination, the combined model yielded the highest AUCs, as statistically expected, as more parameters inevitably improve goodness-of-fit. However, the combined model requires four variables and a regression equation, whereas the HALP score is derived from a single, simple calculation. This logic parallels widely used tools such as the Child-Pugh55 and SOFA scores56. In the recent development of the SOFA-2 score, the investigators deliberately avoided weighting variables to maximize the AUROC, aiming instead to consistently describe organ dysfunction across settings57. Similarly, the HALP score is positioned not to replace albumin or maximize discrimination, but as a convenient, integrative screening tool to complement individual biomarkers.

This study has several strengths: First, the sample size of 1,005 patients is substantial, encompassing AKI cases of varying etiologies and severity, thereby ensuring robust statistical power. Second, competing risk models were employed to handle competing events, with extensive adjustment for potential confounders, and the robustness of the findings was verified through multiple sensitivity analyses. However, this study also has several limitations. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias and residual confounding. The lack of comparison of baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients may affect representativeness, and the absence of certain clinical data (e.g., fluid balance, transfusion status) represents an important source of potential confounding. Second, only baseline HALP scores were analyzed, without evaluating dynamic changes. Third, the use of KDIGO diagnostic criteria may miss subclinical AKI, and the definition of baseline creatinine may influence AKI staging. Fourth, therapy cessation—a key outcome—is not a standardized clinical endpoint; its interpretation is influenced by cultural, economic, and healthcare system factors, thereby limiting generalizability. Finally, this was a single-center study focused on in-hospital outcomes with relatively short follow-up. Given these limitations, the present findings suggest that the HALP score is significantly associated with renal recovery and therapy cessation in patients with AKI, but do not support its use as a standalone tool for clinical decision-making. Further validation through multicenter, prospective studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, more comprehensive adjustment for disease severity, and assessment of cause-specific mortality is warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that a higher baseline HALP score is significantly associated with increased probabilities of both complete and partial renal function recovery and a reduced risk of therapy cessation in patients with AKI. As a composite metric readily derived from routine blood tests, the HALP score may serve as a convenient risk stratification tool, complementing individual biomarkers. Given the retrospective, single-center design, these findings are preliminary and require validation through multicenter prospective studies.
