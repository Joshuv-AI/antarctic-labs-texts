Incision and plasty of the descending duodenal diverticulum using an ESD technique in a child with acute pancreatitis: a case report
Authors: Lei Liu, Bi-Ning Yang, Han Pan, Rui-Xue Ma, Wen-Hai Luo, Yun-Fen Tian, Zan Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 10-year-old boy presented with recurrent abdominal pain and vomiting for over a 20-month period. He had previously been diagnosed with recurrent acute pancreatitis at local hospitals and treated conservatively, but the symptoms continued to recur. Upon admission, a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation was undertaken, which ruled out the majority of established causes of pediatric pancreatitis. It was postulated that the diverticulum itself may have led to intermittent duodenal obstruction and contributed to the recurrent episodes of pancreatitis. The patient subsequently underwent incision and plasty of the descending duodenal diverticulum using an endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) technique. At one-year follow-up, the patient’s serum amylase and lipase levels remained within normal ranges. Although the absence of recurrent pancreatitis postoperatively strongly suggests that the intraluminal duodenal diverticulum (IDD) was the underlying etiology, definitive causation cannot be established based on a single case report with a follow-up period of only one year.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07293-5.Keywords: Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), Duodenal diverticulum, Pancreatitis

Case report

Over a 20-month period, a 10-year-old boy exhibited a recurrent pattern of acute pancreatitis. The clinical presentation during each attack consistently included epigastric pain and vomiting.Serum amylase (344 U/L) and lipase (634 U/L) levels were approximately three times the upper limit of normal. A comprehensive evaluation was undertaken to exclude common etiologies of pancreatitis in children. Abdominal CT suggested a possible duodenal diverticulum (Fig. 1a), and neither abdominal CT nor magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed evidence of pancreatic divisum or choledochal cyst (Fig. 1b). Gastroscopy showed a large diverticulum in the descending part of the duodenum. Serum electrolytes (calcium 2.4 mmol/L), triglycerides (0.84 mmol/L), and glucose (4.8 mmol/L) were all within normal ranges, and autoimmune markers were also unremarkable. The patient’s family denied any history of surgery or abdominal trauma. Genetic testing was discussed with the family; however, due to financial concerns, the family declined further genetic analysis. Additionally, an electroencephalogram (EEG) was performed to investigate the possibility of neurological causes for the patient’s vomiting, particularly given the episodes of projectile vomiting; the EEG findings were normal, effectively ruling out seizure-related vomiting or other central nervous system disorders. Collectively, these investigations effectively ruled out the majority of established causes of pediatric pancreatitis, with the limitation that genetic etiologies could not be completely excluded. The patient’s symptoms were relieved by conservative medical treatment, and he was subsequently discharged. After two further bouts of acute pancreatitis, on January 15, 2025, he was admitted for the fourth time, presenting with abdominal pain similar to previous episodes and projectile vomiting. Of note, while vomiting can occur in acute pancreatitis, the presence of projectile vomiting raised the suspicion of mechanical duodenal obstruction. Given the known intraluminal duodenal diverticulum (IDD), it was postulated that the diverticulum itself may have caused intermittent obstruction of the duodenal lumen, contributing to or even primarily accounting for this symptom, rather than the vomiting being attributable solely to pancreatic inflammation. This clinical distinction is important, as it suggests that the IDD may have contributed to both obstructive symptoms and recurrent episodes of pancreatitis. On January 20, 2025, incision and plasty of the descending duodenal diverticulum using an endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) technique was performed following completion of preoperative preparation and exclusion of contraindications.

a Gas–fluid level observed in the descending portion and medial wall of the duodenum, suggestive of a duodenal diverticulum. b Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed no evidence of pancreatic divisum or choledochal cyst

During surgery, a limited outward bulging was observed on the anal side of the duodenal papillary plane. The diverticulum measured approximately 3 × 5 cm, with septal ridges showing mucosal congestion and erosion, and containing food residue (Fig. 2a). proximal duodenal dilatation was noted. A submucosal injection of physiological saline mixed with methylene blue was administered at the mucosal crest of the duodenal diverticulum to create a submucosal cushion and facilitate mucosal elevation (Fig. 2b). An electrosurgical unit set at 30 W high-frequency current, in combination with a disposable electrosurgical knife (Dual Knife), was used to incise the mucosal layer along the diverticular septum and dissect the submucosal plane (Fig. 2c). Because the operative space in the descending duodenum was limited, an insulation-tipped diathermic knife (IT knife) was subsequently employed to minimize the risk of injury to surrounding tissues. The diverticular septum was fully incised until it was released without intraoperative bleeding and the intestinal lumen became unobstructed. At the end of the procedure, 27 clips were applied in succession to achieve complete closure of the mucosal defect (Fig. 2d).

a In the descending part of the duodenum, a large pouch-like diverticulum measuring approximately 3 cm × 5 cm was observed. b A submucosal injection of physiological saline mixed with methylene blue was administered at the mucosal crest of the duodenal diverticulum. c The mucosal layer was incised along the diverticular septum and the submucosal plane was dissected. d 27 clips were applied in succession to achieve complete closure of the mucosal defect. e Postoperative examination confirmed that the duodenal papilla was intact. f The 6-month postoperative follow-up was performed at a local primary healthcare facility. Endoscopy revealed satisfactory healing of the mucosal incision and a patent intestinal lumen. g The 1-year follow-up endoscopy at our hospital showed satisfactory mucosal healing, a patent intestinal lumen, and a normal-appearing papilla in the descending duodenum, with no food residue at the site of the original diverticulum (distal to the papilla). The degree of luminal dilation proximal to the diverticulum showed no significant improvement compared with the preoperative status. In addition, a single clip was noted to remain in place.(For further details, please refer to the supplementary video.2)

The necessity and feasibility of applying 27 clips will be addressed from the following three perspectives: (1)Location: The lesion was located approximately 1.0 cm distal to the level of the major duodenal papilla, away from the ampulla, ensuring that clip placement did not compromise the biliary or pancreatic orifices(Fig. 2e).(2)Defect morphology: The duodenal mucosa is thin, highly vascular, and subject to constant peristalsis, resulting in significant tension along the incision line. To achieve complete, tension-free closure and prevent exposure of the submucosal layer to bile and pancreatic juices-which could lead to delayed bleeding, perforation, or infection-dense and evenly distributed clip placement was necessary.(3)Feasibility: Dental floss traction was employed during the procedure to facilitate optimal clip positioning and ensure secure closure.(For further details, please refer to the supplementary video.1).

The child was followed up at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after discharge, with repeat tests showing normal serum amylase and lipase levels. Follow-up gastroscopy at 6 months and 1 year postoperatively demonstrated satisfactory healing of the surgical site (Fig. 2f and g), and long-term follow-up is ongoing.(For further details, please refer to the supplementary video.2).

Discussion

Intraluminal duodenal diverticulum (IDD) was first described by Boyd in 1845 as a rare congenital abnormality [1]. In 1947, Nelson further characterized its distinctive radiographic appearance, which was initially referred to as the “windsock sign” or described as a diaphragm or incomplete diaphragm. The term “IDD” was later introduced by Kinzer in 1949 [2]. Pathologically, IDD is a cystic intraluminal structure lined by duodenal mucosa on both its luminal and diverticular surfaces. It is partially or completely circumferentially attached to the duodenal wall, most frequently in the periampullary region, and usually measures from a few centimeters to approximately 10 cm in length. The diverticulum typically communicates with the duodenal lumen through an opening at either its base or apex, and its size may further increase after ingestion of food or foreign material. Proximal duodenal dilation occurs in nearly half of the reported cases [3–6].

The exact mechanism underlying the development of IDD remains unclear. Most scholars agree that, during early embryogenesis, the duodenal lumen is initially occluded by proliferating epithelium, followed by a process of recanalization. Incomplete recanalization of the duodenal lumen during early embryogenesis may result in duodenal obstruction, stenosis, or diaphragm formation. Over time, peristaltic forces can cause the affected segment to invaginate, eventually forming an IDD, which most commonly occurs in the second portion of the duodenum near the papilla of Vater [4–6].

The symptoms of patients with IDD are often nonspecific, typically including mild upper abdominal pain or a sensation of fullness; physical examination is usually unremarkable. Symptoms often persist despite years of targeted treatment, and children with IDD may be asymptomatic. Nearly one-third of patients experience nausea and vomiting, particularly after meals, with some symptoms relieved by emesis. Approximately one-fourth of patients present with gastrointestinal bleeding. Most cases are diagnosed incidentally when managing these complications, which often leads to a delayed diagnosis of IDD [2, 6, 7]. IDD may progress with age or enlarge due to food accumulation or repeated peristaltic activity. It behaves like a large intraluminal foreign body, which can lead to partial or intermittent duodenal obstruction, accounting for the early symptoms of fullness and intermittent postprandial abdominal pain and vomiting [8]. The diagnosis of IDD is typically established through barium radiography and endoscopy, with endoscopy playing an increasingly important role in both the diagnosis and management of the condition.

The main complications of IDD include duodenitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, gastrointestinal obstruction, acute perforation, pancreatic or biliary diseases, local abscess formation; acute pancreatitis has been reported to occur in approximately 20% of adults with intraluminal duodenal diverticulum (IDD) [2, 5]. However, this figure should be interpreted with caution, as it is derived from a limited number of case reports and literature reviews given the extreme rarity of this anatomical variant. Pediatric cases of IDD-associated pancreatitis are exceptionally uncommon. In the present case, the child presented with recurrent episodes of acute pancreatitis. Chronic pancreatitis was excluded based on imaging studies. Notably, following incision and plasty of the descending duodenal diverticulum using an ESD technique, no further episodes of pancreatitis occurred during the follow-up period. Although definitive causation cannot be established from a single case report, the temporal resolution of symptoms after surgical intervention strongly suggests that the IDD was the underlying etiology in this patient.

The pathogenesis of pancreatitis in patients with IDD remains unclear. Possible mechanisms include retrograde flow of retained food into the pancreatic duct via the papilla of Vater, or traction on the neck of the diverticulum at the level of the papilla during duodenal peristalsis, combined with partial duodenal obstruction and deformation of the ampulla of Vater, leading to reflux of duodenal contents into the pancreatic duct [9, 10]. IDD is a rare cause of pancreatitis in children. When the etiology is not identified by standard diagnostic tools, or common causes have been excluded in cases of recurrent acute pancreatitis, IDD should be considered [8, 10].

In cases of diverticulitis, almost all patients require inpatient management. Initial interventions include bed rest, fasting, nasogastric tube placement, intravenous fluids, and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, under the supervision of a specialist [7]. In most cases of IDD, surgical treatment is preferred. Symptomatic intraluminal diverticula can be managed endoscopically using electrocoagulation with a loop, or surgically via duodenotomy and diverticulectomy. Care must be taken to avoid injury to the ampulla of Vater during the procedure. For patients at high surgical risk or who are asymptomatic, conservative management may be considered. However, large diverticula generally require surgical intervention due to the increased risk of infection and perforation [2, 7, 9].

IDD in children is extremely rare, and reports of surgical management are particularly scarce. Literature review indicates that endoscopic electrocoagulation resection using loop electrosurgical forceps under a double-channel endoscope has been reported more frequently in adults. Notably, a Japanese report described a 33-year-old woman with IDD and refractory pancreatitis in 2013 who was successfully treated with double-channel endoscopic resection [11]. In contrast, duodenotomy with diverticulectomy remains the more common approach in pediatric patients. Several pediatric cases of IDD treated surgically have been reported: in 1971, a 16-year-old girl underwent gastric exploration and duodenotomy [6]; in 1976, an 18-year-old Mongolian male underwent duodenotomy [12]; in 1998, an 11-year-old girl with IDD and pancreatitis was treated by duodenotomy [3]; and in 2014, an 8-year-old girl with a coin retained in a duodenal diverticulum causing pancreatitis underwent duodenotomy [8]. However, duodenotomy carries risks such as a large surgical wound, delayed recovery, and hemorrhage. With the development of minimally invasive surgery, a 17-year-old girl was treated using a laparoscopic approach combined with endoscopic guidance for duodenectomy in 2018 [13]. Compared with open duodenal surgery, minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques offer smaller wounds and faster recovery; however, the duodenal wall still needs to be incised, and complications such as anastomotic leakage and intestinal adhesions remain possible.

To date, no cases in China have reported the use of ESD for treating pediatric IDD. However, ESD has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in the management of other pediatric gastrointestinal disorders, including congenital duodenal membranous stenosis, gastric duplication malformations, and ectopic pancreas [14–16]. As a novel minimally invasive technique, ESD enables precise resection of superficial gastrointestinal lesions while avoiding open surgery and preserving organ integrity.

ESD is commonly used to treat mucosal lesions larger than 2 cm, such as early gastrointestinal cancers and precancerous lesions in adults. Compared with open surgery, ESD offers several advantages, including smaller wounds, complete resection under direct visualization, faster recovery, improved safety, fewer complications, and greater cost-effectiveness [17]. In our case, during incision and plasty of the diverticulum using the ESD technique, a submucosal injection of physiological saline mixed with methylene blue was first administered at the diverticular septum of the duodenal diverticulum to create a submucosal cushion and facilitate mucosal elevation. An electrosurgical knife was then used to carefully incise the mucosa, minimizing the risk of perforation while providing hemostasis and ensuring no intraoperative bleeding, with particular attention to protecting the ampulla of Vater. The diverticular septum, composed of mucosa and submucosa, lacking a muscularis propria layer, is amenable to complete incision using the ESD technique.

Although ESD offers the aforementioned advantages in the treatment of IDD, it must be emphasized that this technique is not suitable for all cases of IDD. The most critical factor for avoiding or using ESD with caution is the risk of injury to the duodenal papilla (ampulla of Vater). When the diverticular septum is located too close to the papillary orifice, when the diverticulum directly involves the papillary structure, or when preoperative imaging or endoscopic assessment suggests an unclear anatomical relationship between the papilla and the diverticulum, performing ESD can readily cause damage to the bile or pancreatic duct orifice, leading to serious complications such as postoperative bile leakage, pancreatic leakage, stenosis, acute cholangitis, or pancreatitis. Under these circumstances, conventional surgery should be prioritized. Furthermore, the presence of signs of active perforation associated with the diverticulum, uncorrectable coagulopathy, or lack of extensive experience in duodenal ESD on the part of the operator should be regarded as relative contraindications to ESD. For cases that do not meet the safety distance criterion or in which the relationship between the papilla and the diverticulum cannot be clearly delineated, blind attempts at ESD are not recommended.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-case report, the findings cannot be generalized, and the high technical complexity of this procedure, requiring advanced endoscopic expertise, may limit its reproducibility in non-specialized centers. Second, the follow-up period of only one year is insufficient to assess the long-term durability of the repair or the risk of late recurrence. Third, although the temporal resolution of symptoms after intervention strongly suggests an association, definitive causation between the intraluminal duodenal diverticulum (IDD) and pancreatitis cannot be established based on a single case report.

Although cases using this technique in pediatric gastrointestinal diseases remain limited and clinical experience is still evolving, ESD demonstrates considerable potential and may be a valuable minimally invasive option in clinical practice.
