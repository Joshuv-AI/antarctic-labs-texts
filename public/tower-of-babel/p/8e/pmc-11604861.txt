Alopecia Areata Treatment Patterns and Satisfaction: Results of a Real-World Cross-Sectional Survey in Europe
Authors: Peter Anderson, James Piercy, Jenny Austin, Simran Marwaha, Kent A Hanson, Ernest H Law, Gregor Schaefer, Samantha K Kurosky, Sergio Vañó-Galván
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease that causes scalp, face, and/or body hair loss. Recently, oral treatments with kinases inhibition became the first approved therapies for severe AA. An understanding of the use and effectiveness of traditional therapies in real-world treatment settings is needed to guide integration of novel therapies into the treatment paradigm. This study aimed to describe traditional treatment patterns, dermatologists’ reasons for therapy choice, and dermatologists’ satisfaction with disease control among patients with AA.MethodsData were drawn from the 2021–2022 Adelphi Real World AA Disease Specific Programme™, a cross-sectional survey of dermatologists and adult patients with AA, conducted in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the UK. For each patient, using data from patient consultation and medical records, dermatologists reported % scalp hair loss (SHL), characteristics of current and prior AA therapies, and satisfaction with disease control.ResultsOverall, 239 dermatologists provided data for 1720 patients with AA. Mean (SD) patient age was 35.8 (11.6) years, and 51% were male. Based on dermatologist perception, among patients with ≤ 10% SHL, 74% were experiencing mild AA, while ≥ 95% of patients with ≥ 50% SHL were experiencing severe/very severe AA. In patients with ≥ 50% SHL, the most common therapies received included systemic immunosuppressants (31%), topical corticosteroids (24%), and oral corticosteroids (24%). Among all patients who had switched therapies, 49%, 26%, and 24% switched because of worsening AA, lack of initial efficacy with prior treatment, and loss of response over time, respectively. Among those with SHL ≥ 50%, dermatologists reported satisfaction with current therapy in < 30% of patients.ConclusionDermatologists reported low satisfaction with traditional AA therapies used in patients with extensive SHL, with some patients discontinuing treatment because of worsening disease. This suggests more effective treatments are needed for patients with severe AA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-024-01280-3.Keywords: Alopecia areata, Burden of disease, Cross-sectional study, Physicians’ practice patterns, Patient satisfaction

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Conventional treatment approaches in alopecia areata (AA) consist of off-label use of therapies; however, recently approved therapies for severe AA have the potential to change the AA treatment paradigm, and an understanding of current unmet need in the AA population can inform appropriate integration of novel treatments into clinical practice.We aimed to describe traditional AA treatment patterns, dermatologist-reported reasons for treatment choice, and satisfaction with disease control in a real-world clinical setting.What was learned from the study?Dermatologists reported that lack of treatment efficacy was a reason for low levels of satisfaction with traditional AA therapies.Lack of efficacy, patient request, and loss of response over time were cited as key reasons for patients to discontinue their prescribed therapy.These findings indicate traditional AA therapies are ineffective in meeting patient treatment goals, particularly for those with more severe AA, highlighting a critical need for patient access to newly approved, safe and effective therapies.

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune, non-scarring, dermatologic disease that results in unpredictable episodes of hair loss [1–3]. Although AA commonly presents as a sudden onset of round patches of scalp hair loss (SHL), more extensive hair loss can occur, including total hair loss on the scalp (alopecia totalis) or scalp and body (alopecia universalis) [4]. The impact of AA extends beyond hair loss. Prior research has demonstrated an association between AA and comorbid disorders, namely depression and anxiety, and considerable impacts to patients’ health-related quality of life (QoL) [5].

There are no preventive or curative treatments for AA. Therefore, goals of therapy include arresting disease progression, regrowing hair, and improving patients’ QoL [2, 6–8]. Traditionally, management of AA has included off-label treatment with topical and systemic corticosteroids, non-steroidal topical treatments, topical immunosuppressants, and contact immunotherapies [9, 10]. However, robust evidence of the clinical efficacy of these treatments is limited, and potential safety risks restrict long-term use.

Recently, ritlecitinib and baricitinib, oral treatments with Janus kinase (JAK) inhibition, became the first approved therapies for severe AA [11]. Clinical trials have demonstrated both agents are well-tolerated and promote significant hair regrowth in patients with ≥ 50% SHL. As dermatologists begin integrating these new therapies into real-world practice, it is critical to ascertain current unmet need with traditional treatment approaches, particularly among patients with severe AA, to ensure new therapies provide value to patients. The aim of this study was to describe AA treatment patterns, reasons for treatment selection, and dermatologist- and patient-reported treatment satisfaction.

Methods

Study Design

Data were drawn from the Adelphi Real World AA Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a real-world, multinational, cross-sectional survey with elements of retrospective data collection. Data were captured from dermatologists and their consulting patients, ages ≥ 18 years, with a diagnosis of AA in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the UK, between October 2021 and June 2022.

Participating dermatologists identified the next seven eligible patients attending a health care visit and completed a patient record form for each. A severity quota was applied to ensure that the identified patient population included enough severe/very severe patients for robust analyses. Among the seven dermatologist-identified patients, one had mild, three moderate, and three severe/very severe disease at the time of data collection. Disease severity was based on the dermatologist’s subjective assessment of each patient at the time of the consultation.

The patient record form captured information gathered at the time of consultation along with historical data abstracted from medical records. These data included patient demographics, clinical characteristics, estimated percentage SHL at various time points (e.g., “What percentage of this patient’s scalp is currently affected by hair loss due to their AA?"), current/previously prescribed AA therapy, reasons for current therapy selection, areas of improvement associated with current therapy, and reasons for switching from prior therapy. Dermatologists also indicated their level of satisfaction with disease control for current therapy and whether this was the best possible control that could be achieved.

The selected patients were invited to complete a patient self-completion form capturing patient-reported symptom burden, level of satisfaction with treatment, and other patient-reported outcomes at the time of the survey. Patients were also asked to provide an overall assessment of their current disease severity at the time of data collection based on their own definition of mild, moderate, severe, and very severe AA. Patients completed the form independently of their dermatologist and returned it in a sealed envelope, ensuring their responses were confidential and the dermatologist-patient relationship was not impacted. No tests, treatments, or investigations were performed as part of this survey. The DSP methodology has been published and validated [12–15].

Participants

A geographically diverse sample of dermatologists, recruited by local fieldwork agencies, contributed to the database. To be included in the study, practicing dermatologists were required to be personally responsible for treatment decisions and management of patients with AA, and treating a minimum of seven adult patients with AA per month. Dermatologists were compensated for participating in the DSP according to fair market rates consistent with the time involved. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had a dermatologist-confirmed diagnosis of AA, and were not currently involved in a clinical trial for AA at the time of data collection.

Ethics

Patients provided informed consent for the use of their anonymized and aggregated data for research and publication in scientific journals. Data collection processes ensured that patients and dermatologists could not be directly identified; all data were aggregated and de-identified before receipt. Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines [16] and did not require Institutional Review Board/Independent Ethics Committee approval. The AA Disease Specific Programme was issued an ethics exemption by the Western IRB (study protocol number: AG-8446). The DSP was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [17]. No new surveys were sent out to patients as part of this study.

Data analysis

All analyses were descriptive. Continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Results were reported for the total sample and stratified into the following groups according to dermatologist-estimated current percentage SHL: ≤ 10%, 11–20%, 21–49%, 50–74%, 75–99%, and 100%. Missing data were not imputed; therefore, the bases of patients included in analyses may have varied across variables and were reported separately for each analysis. Analyses were conducted using Stata v17 [18]. Country-specific data are available in a supplementary file.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 239 dermatologists provided data for 1720 patients with AA across France (11%), Germany (26%), Italy (18%), Spain (28%), and the UK (16%), of which 468 completed patient self-completion forms. Fifty percent of dermatologists were female, 63% identified as hair specialists, and 51% had been practicing for 14 years or more.

The mean (SD) age of patients increased with percentage SHL, from 32.5 (10.8) to 37.9 (13.7) years in patients with ≤ 10% SHL and 100% SHL, respectively (Table 1). The proportion of males was highest in the 21–49% SHL group (55%) and lowest in the 11–20% SHL group (44%). Most patients were employed full time, with the highest proportion in the 50–74% SHL group (71%) and the lowest in the 100% SHL group (51%).

Treatment Goals and Current Alopecia Areata Therapy

The main dermatologist-reported treatment goals were to reduce SHL, improve QoL, and reduce psychological impact which were selected in 92%, 49%, and 38% of patients, respectively (Fig. 1). Similarly, reducing SHL, improving scalp hair coverage, and improving QoL were primary dermatologist-reported reasons for selecting patients’ current therapy (Fig. 2).

At the time of consultation, corticosteroids were the most common therapy type; 35%, 22%, and 22% of patients were receiving topical, oral, or intralesional corticosteroids, respectively (Table 4). In patients with 0–20% SHL, topical and intralesional corticosteroids were used more frequently than in patients with more extensive SHL. Systemic immunosuppressants and JAK inhibitors were prescribed in 8% and 6% of patients with ≤ 10% SHL, respectively, compared to 29% of patients with 75–99% SHL. Among patients with 100% SHL, 29% were not receiving any AA treatment.

Alopecia Areata Therapies by Line of Therapy

Dermatologists described the proportion of patients receiving AA therapy by therapeutic line (Fig. 3). Corticosteroids were the most common first-line therapy, with 50% of patients receiving topical, 28% oral, and 18% intralesional corticosteroids. JAK inhibitors, zinc, and topical immunotherapy were used as first-line therapy in < 10% of patients. Corticosteroid prescribing declined in later therapy lines, while systemic immunosuppressants, topical immunotherapy, and JAK inhibitors were more frequently prescribed to patients beyond first line.

Dermatologist-Reported Reasons for Switch from Previous Therapy

Nearly half of patients who switched to their current therapy did so due to worsening AA. Other reasons for switching included lack of initial efficacy (26%) and loss of response over time (24%; Supplement Fig. S1). Among patients not receiving treatment at the time of data collection, reasons for discontinuation included patient request (35%), lack of initial efficacy (43%), and loss of response over time (23%).

Dermatologist- and Patient-Reported Satisfaction and Dissatisfaction with Alopecia Areata Treatments

Dermatologists reported satisfaction with current treatment in over 70% of patients with ≤ 20% SHL, but in only 7% of patients with 100% SHL (Fig. 4). Similarly, more than half of patients with ≤ 20% SHL were satisfied with their current treatment, compared to less than 20% of patients with ≥ 75% SHL.

Dermatologist- and patient-reported degree of satisfaction with current treatment

The primary reasons for dermatologists’ dissatisfaction were lack of overall efficacy (73%) and continued impact on QoL (27%; Fig. 5). Patients reported similar reasons, with 86% reporting lack of improvement in their AA, 22% noting waning effectiveness, and 22% citing side effects as their reason for dissatisfaction.

Country-Specific Findings

In the data stratified by country, the mean (SD) patient age ranged from 32.3 (11.0) years in the UK to 37.7 (11.8) years in Italy (Supplement Table S2). The proportion of males was the lowest in the UK (40%) and the highest in Germany (60%). The UK had the highest proportion of patients experiencing worsening disease (33%), followed by Germany (28%), and France (27%) (Supplement Table S3). The mean (SD) time since diagnosis ranged from 25.0 (49.6) months in the UK to 43.1 (64.9) months in Italy. Germany had the lowest proportion of patients with 100% SHL (4%) while UK had the highest (20%). The mean (SD) time on current treatment ranged from 6.5 (9.4) months in the UK to 11.0 (17.1) months in Germany. At consultation, topical corticosteroids were the most common therapy used in France (37%), Germany (39%), Italy (45%), and the UK (31%). Intralesional corticosteroids were received by 20–30% of patients in France, Italy, Spain, and the UK, but only by 6% in Germany (Supplement Table S4). Overall, 22% of dermatologists reported dissatisfaction with treatment, ranging from 16% in Italy to 30% in the UK (Supplement Table S5). Beyond reducing SHL and improving patient QoL, reducing patients’ psychological impact was a main treatment goal, ranging from 24% of patients in Germany to 53% in Spain (Supplement Table S6).

Discussion

As new treatments for AA become available in Europe, a comprehensive understanding of patient characteristics and treatment patterns in patients with AA is imperative to ensure equitable access to new treatments. This study described current treatment patterns and satisfaction among patients with AA in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the UK.

Patients in this study had a similar age, gender, and comorbidity burden as demonstrated in prior research [7, 18–24]. Although AA can present at any age, published literature suggests AA occurs by age 40 in as many as 88% of patients, with a mean age of onset occurring between 25 and 36 years [18]. Additionally, males and females appear to be equally affected by AA as measured across a range of population-based epidemiologic studies [7]. Our study findings are similar, reporting a mean age at diagnosis of 32 years and a population comprised of approximately 50% males. While we employed a soft sampling quota to ensure we captured a sufficient sample of patients with moderate and severe disease, a similar proportion of patients with at least 50% SHL was reported in a large, cross-sectional survey evaluating the point prevalence of AA in a US general population of individuals aged 11 years or older [25].

The clinical course of AA is unpredictable—hair can spontaneously grow back and resolve (an acute episode), chronically relapse and remit, or fully progress to AA totalis or AA universalis [4, 7, 26–28]. While it is difficult to predict the course of AA, several factors may indicate poor prognosis. For example, AA development at an early age, hair loss involvement extending beyond the scalp, presence of nail abnormalities, and development of other comorbid autoimmune conditions all may indicate a poorer prognosis [29]. Of the patients in our study, 88% of patients were classified by dermatologists as having moderate disease or worse. Among those with severe and very severe disease, dermatologists reported that 84% and 99% of these patients, respectively, had 50% or greater SHL, indicating these patients may potentially experience poor hair regrowth, progressive disease, and an overall poor prognosis.

Dermatologists in this study reported the main treatment goal for most patients was to preserve or reduce SHL and improve QoL. Guidelines from 2012 by the British Association of Dermatologists recommended prescribing corticosteroids to patients with mild-to-moderate disease, but evidence for the long-term efficacy of both topical and intralesional corticosteroids is limited. Guidance also recommends that topical and intralesional corticosteroids are used as first-line treatment for localized or limited AA, while systemic corticosteroids are reserved for use in patients with more extensive AA and/or actively progressing disease [9, 30, 31]. The frequent use of corticosteroids in first- and second-line settings in our study aligns with these recommendations [8, 9, 30, 31]. We also found that use of oral corticosteroids was more common among patients with ≥ 50% SHL than those with less SHL, while topical and intralesional injected corticosteroids use was more common among patients with < 50% SHL than those with ≥ 50% SHL in both the first- and second-line settings. The Alopecia Areata Consensus of Experts (ACE) consensus statement proposed the use of JAK inhibitors as an effective agent for adults with AA [30]. We found JAK inhibitor use was only 5% and 6% in the first- and second-line settings, but use increased during later lines. Similar to other literature, dermatologists in the study reported substantially greater levels of treatment dissatisfaction in patients with a greater extent of hair loss [32]. Patient-reported dissatisfaction followed a similar trend, underscoring a persistent gap between available therapies and patient/provider treatment goals.

Strengths and Limitations

This study had several limitations. The DSP recruitment methodology requires dermatologists to include consecutively consulting patients, thus findings may represent a population of patients who visit their treating dermatologist more frequently. This study used soft sampling quotas of patients by dermatologist-assessed disease severity; therefore, the distribution of mild, moderate, severe, and very severe AA across the cohort may not reflect the general AA population distribution. Given that dermatologists often consider multiple factors beyond hair loss (e.g., psychosocial functioning, eyebrow and eyelash involvement, and inadequate treatment response) when determining disease severity, some variation in the distribution of disease severity by percentage SHL may exist. The cross-sectional design of the survey results in data points such as “current scalp hair loss”, treatment satisfaction, and reasons for switching treatment to be reported from different points in the patient journey. This prevents any conclusions about causal relationships; however, identification of significant associations is possible. Although this study provides a snapshot of current treatment satisfaction and unmet need in the AA population, such findings do not provide insight on satisfaction with specific AA treatments but rather the treatment regimen as a whole.

Conclusion

Treatments for AA traditionally consisted of topical, injectable, or oral corticosteroids, broad immunosuppressants, and contact immunotherapies. This study showed that such therapies were insufficient in meeting AA treatment goals, as evidenced by patients switching therapies due to lack of initial effectiveness and insufficient durability. Moreover, many patients and their dermatologists were dissatisfied with outcomes on current therapeutic options, leaving a persistent gap for effective treatments, particularly among those with more extensive SHL. With recent European Medicines Agency approval of ritlecitinib and baricitinib for severe AA, additional research is needed to understand how they can be incorporated into practice and whether they offer patients new opportunities to achieve their treatment goals.
