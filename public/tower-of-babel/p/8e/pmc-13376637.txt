In situ electrochemiluminescence microneedle device for real-time biomarker monitoring in vivo
Authors: Huiwen Xiong, Chenxin Zhu, Ghazala Ashraf, Xiaopeng Guo, Lin Liu, Haonan Wang, Hui Chen, Wenhao Weng, Huali Shen, Jilie Kong, Xueen Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContinuous in vivo monitoring of biomarkers remains challenging due to limited sensitivity, integration, and biocompatibility. Here, we report an integrated microneedle-based electrochemiluminescence device (MN-ECLD) for real-time detection of protein biomarkers in interstitial fluid. Leveraging hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks with ultrabright, biocompatible electrochemiluminescence, the emitters were incorporated into porous gold-coated microneedle arrays and regulated via interface-specific Y-shaped probes, enabling efficient coreactant-free signal generation. The device achieved ultrasensitive protein detection in vitro with a linear range of 100 fg/mL to 10 ng/mL, a detection limit of 21.3 fg/mL, and stability over 12 days, delivering an 87-fold sensitivity enhancement over conventional emitters. In vivo, MN-ECLD enabled real-time monitoring of cardiac biomarkers, achieving early warning of acute myocardial infarction in rats and pigs, with biomarker trends consistent with serum ELISA. This work establishes a versatile platform for continuous in vivo diagnostics of acute cardiovascular and metabolic disorders.Subject terms: Sensors, Electronic devices

Introduction

Wearable diagnostic technologies have emerged as a transformative alternative to traditional serological testing, offering real-time, non-invasive monitoring of physiological signals and biomarkers1–4. Among these, interstitial fluid (ISF) presents a promising surrogate to blood due to its rich biomolecular content and strong correlation with systemic biomarker levels5,6. Extracted non-invasively through the skin, ISF enables dynamic monitoring of disease-related analytes with minimal discomfort, thereby facilitating decentralized and continuous health tracking7.

Microneedles (MNs), particularly those with porous architectures, have shown great potential for ISF extraction via capillary-driven uptake without the need for external force or reverse iontophoresis8–11. Pang et al. presented a low-cost porous microneedle (MN) patch that creates interconnected porous microstructures via phase separation in deionized water. This patch facilitates the quantitative analysis of interstitial fluid (ISF) biomarkers (glucose, Ca²⁺, and Cl⁻) with the limits of detection (LOD) of 0.05 mM, 0.13 mM, and 2.22 mM, respectively, and completes the assay within 30 min12. Additionally, Bakhshandeh et al. reported an Aptalyzer device based on microneedles by constructing an aptamer-based electrochemical sensing system13. In this work, a transparent hyaluronic acid hydrogel microneedle electrochemical sensor enabled real-time, continuous, and multiplex monitoring of glucose and lactate, achieving limits of detection of 7.44 mM and 4.43 mM, respectively. Notably, the hydrogel microneedle architecture physically separates the aptamer probes from direct exposure to interstitial fluid, thereby mitigating probe degradation and improving signal stability—effectively addressing key limitations associated with solid microneedle platforms. These patches can be easily integrated into wearable platforms for real-time analysis. While MNs have been combined with various detection modalities including fluorescence1,14, electrochemistry13, and field-effect transistors15, each of these approaches faces distinct limitations. Electrochemical methods, though compact, suffer from interference and reproducibility issues, whereas fluorescence-based sensors often require bulky optics and complex assay formats.

Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) offers a compelling alternative by combining the sensitivity of chemiluminescence with the spatial and temporal control of electrochemical systems16–19. ECL-based devices (ECLDs) are particularly well-suited for on-skin applications due to their high luminescent efficiency, minimal background signal, and compatibility with transparent electrodes20–22. Chen et al. designed a fully-stretchable ECLDs with excellent conductivity (10 Ω sq−1), and transparency (above 60%)23. However, the development of in vivo MN-based ECLDs remains limited by three critical challenges: (i) poor light transmittance and electrical conductivity of traditional electrodes17, (ii) toxicity of commonly used coreactants such as tripropylamine (TPrA) or persulfates24, and (iii) insufficient brightness and biocompatibility of conventional ECL emitters such as Ru(bpy)₃²⁺25,26.

Hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) have recently emerged as promising ECL materials27,28, owing to their porous crystalline structure, high luminescent efficiency, low toxicity, and excellent biocompatibility29–32. Li et al. constructed a dual-mode biosensor based on HOF-101 for photoelectrochemistry (PEC) and ECL sensing, using polydopamine nanoparticles (PDAs) as a quencher for the ultrasensitive detection and imaging of oxytetracycline (OXY)33. The presented dual-mode detection method enhances the accuracy of the assay. Despite their potential, the use of HOFs in in situ prototype ECL platforms remains rare, and most reported systems still rely on toxic coreactants incompatible with in vivo deployment. A key step toward advancing ECL-based wearable diagnostics lies in the development of integrated platforms that combine biocompatible emitters, porous MNs, and efficient signal capture systems for continuous in situ biomarker detection.

Cardiac troponins (cTn), as prominent biomarkers for myocardial injury, regulate calcium-mediated cardiac muscle contraction34. The concentration of cTn specifically indicates the high risk of heart-related disease such as acute myocardial infarction (AMI). For instance, Li et al. encapsulated luminophores within a metal–organic framework (MOF) and leveraged the catalytic active sites of the framework’s Fe atoms to generate reactive radicals in situ from a coreactant at the nanoscale. By drastically shortening the diffusion distance between the active species and the MOF, they achieved significant amplification of the ECL signal from tetraphenylethylene luminophores35. In our previous work, an alternating current electroosmotic flow-mediated ECL microfluidic system for in vitro detection of cTnI from serum, ISF, and urine samples was achieved with a response time of 5 min24. However, these cTnI assays remain off-line ECL devices. For ICU patients with severe heart-related disease, continuous monitoring of cardiac biomarkers is still required.

In this study, we report the design and validation of an ultrasensitive MN-based ECLD for real-time monitoring of multiple cardiac biomarkers in ISF. The system features a disposable 3D-printed porous MN array and a reusable flexible printed circuit board (PCB), forming a bracelet-style wired platform. To enable ECL readout, a transparent gold coating was applied to the MNs, and luminescence signals were captured using a photomultiplier tube (PMT) positioned beneath the patch. Importantly, we employed an ultrabright and non-toxic HOF-101 emitter, enabling coreactant-free, high-sensitivity detection of AMI-related biomarkers such as cardiac troponin I (cTnI), cTnT, NT-proBNP, D-dimer, myoglobin (myo), and human serum albumin (HSA). Using ferrocene (Fc)-quenched Y-shaped dual-aptamer probes, the system achieved an 87-fold improvement in sensitivity over conventional ECL systems. This platform represents a major step toward clinically relevant in vivo diagnostics for acute cardiovascular conditions, with potential applications in pre-hospital emergency settings and post-discharge monitoring.

Results

System architecture, operating principle, and functional components of the HOF-101-based ECLD

The structural design and operating workflow of the HOF-101-based ECLD are illustrated in Fig. 1a. The system comprises two main components: a MN detection module and an integrated control and display unit. The two components are connected using wires. The detection module features a porous MN patch mounted on a custom-fabricated PCB, coupled with PMT, a micro-electrochemical workstation, and a photon-transparent outer housing. These components are assembled into a compact, wrist-worn format using a watch band and a square flexible rubber ring to ensure ergonomic wearability and secure skin contact.

The control and display unit consists of a photon counter, a microcontroller-based control circuit, and a real-time display interface (Supplementary Fig. 1). These subsystems are interconnected via a flexible communication cable, enabling simultaneous transmission of control signals and real-time ECL data for in situ biomarker monitoring.

Figure 1b presents the exploded view of the ECLD system. The wearable MN detection module incorporates a PMT and a miniaturized electrochemical workstation (EWS), both housed within a custom-designed outer casing. The micro-EWS generates the electrical signals required to initiate the ECL reaction at the MN interface, while the PMT captures the resulting luminescent emissions and converts them into electronic pulse signals. The intensity of the emitted light is quantitatively reflected in the number of pulse signals recorded.

A flexible PCB is affixed to the light detection port of the housing and electrically connected to the mini-EWS. To minimize background light interference during on-skin operation, a square flexible rubber ring is mounted around the light detection port, forming an enclosed dark chamber when in contact with the skin. The entire MN detection module is secured to the body using an adjustable watch band, ensuring stable skin contact and mechanical coupling.

Once the MN patch is installed within the light detection chamber, its conductive edge interfaces with the PCB, allowing electrical signals from the μ-EWS to be distributed across the three active zones of the MN array. Upon skin insertion, the MNs penetrate the stratum corneum, ensuring that the microneedle tips fully access the ISF for in situ biomarker interaction.

The control and display module comprises a photon counter, a processing unit, and a visualization interface. The photon counter quantifies the pulse signals emitted by the PMT during each ECL event, providing a real-time measure of ECL intensity. The control unit orchestrates the operation of all subsystems and processes the acquired data into clinically interpretable output. The display unit presents the dynamic trend of cTnI levels over time. Additionally, the ECLD supports both wired connection to external monitors and wireless data transmission via Bluetooth, enabling remote monitoring and timely alerts for early-stage myocardial infarction diagnosis.

As shown in Fig. 1c, the MN array patch is primarily undergoing 3D-printing process to form the porous morphologies, then it is divided into three distinct regions by spluttering different metal targets: the ultrathin gold working electrode (WE), the silver reference electrode (RE), and the gold counter electrode (CE). Each MN measures 1000 μm in length, sufficient to penetrate the epidermis and establish contact with ISF for biomarker sensing. The MNs are modified with functional molecules for further monitoring of AMI-related biomarkers in vivo. To construct the sensing interface, Y-shaped dual-aptamer probes were assembled by hybridizing two long strands (CP1 and CP2), each containing aptamer sequences, with an auxiliary probe (AP) and a (Fc)-labeled quencher strand (Fig. 1d). The hybrid structure was stabilized by high-temperature denaturation followed by rapid cooling.

The luminophore HOF-101, combined with AuNPs and chitosan (CS), was drop-cast onto the WE region. Y-shaped probes were immobilized via Au–S bonding. Initially, the Fc quencher suppresses the ECL signal of HOF-101, generating a low baseline output. Upon insertion into the skin, the MN tips access ISF, and the dual aptamers specifically bind to target proteins at distinct sites. This dual recognition causes the Fc-labeled strand to dissociate, triggering a significant increase in ECL intensity—a turn-on response that enables real-time in vivo monitoring. This integrated sensing strategy—combining porous MNs, HOF-based luminophores, and dual-aptamer probes—demonstrates the first successful implementation of in vivo ECL detection, with strong potential for early, pre-symptomatic diagnosis of acute diseases.

Characterization of the MN Patch

The SEM images obtained were consistent with the original 3D printing design, confirming the structural fidelity of the fabricated microneedles. Figure 2a shows the image of the MN patch, with the WE and CE sputtered with gold and the RE with silver. As shown in Fig. 2b, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to characterize the morphology of the porous microneedles (MNs). The MNs exhibited an average height of approximately 1000 μm and featured symmetric inward-facing pores on both sides, each with a diameter of ~100 μm, confirming the designed geometry for efficient ISF access and reagent loading. The porous MN arrays from different views were further examined under an optical microscope. The porous MNs featured well-defined bilateral openings, consistent with the SEM characterization (Fig. 2c). The wristwatch-style ECLD (Fig. 2d) is compact, lightweight, and wearable on the arm, enabling the first demonstration of direct in vivo ECL monitoring under ambient light conditions. To evaluate the conductivity of the fabricated MNs, an oscilloscope was employed to monitor the input and output electrical signals. A 10 V sinusoidal voltage was applied to the working electrode area, and the oscilloscope displayed an output in the form of a sine wave with a peak value of 10 V (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The silver-coated MN region showed the same waveform and amplitude, indicating that the voltage output efficiency applied to the conductive MNs was 100%, demonstrating excellent conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

To assess the mechanical robustness of the ECL MNs, compression tests were conducted using an electronic universal testing machine on both porous and non-porous 3D-printed MN arrays, as well as individual MNs. Under a constant loading speed of 10 μm/s and a maximum applied force of 800 N, the non-porous MN array exhibited a deformation of 587 μm, while the porous MN array deformed by 632 μm (Fig. 2e), indicating comparable structural integrity suitable for skin penetration. The elastic modulus of the porous MN array was measured to be 5.8 MPa, and that of the non-porous MN array was 15.67 MPa, far exceeding the minimum force of 0.045 N required to penetrate the stratum corneum36. Both porous and non-porous MNs showed almost no deformation at around 0.045 N. A single porous MN required at least 45.73 N to fully deform, with an elastic modulus of 34.9 MPa, while the elastic modulus of a non-porous MN was 39.64 MPa (Fig. 2f). Given that the modulus of skin reported in literature is 25–200 kPa, much lower than the experimental test results, the 3D-printed MN arrays designed are capable of penetrating human skin.

The swelling performance of wearable MNs can easily lead to deformation of the needle tips, which can become a factor of signal instability in in situ monitoring. Therefore, the swelling performance of 3D-printed porous MNs was investigated. As shown in Fig. 2g, the average mass of the original MNs was 0.41 g. When the MNs were immersed in PBS for 15 min and then blown dry, the measured average mass was 0.43 g. After placing them in a 37 °C oven for 15 min, the average mass obtained was 0.41 g, indicating that the mass of 3D-printed porous MNs remains essentially unchanged after dehydration, demonstrating high stability.

The light transmission efficiency of 3D printed porous MNs was investigated (Fig. 2h). The PMT was fixed on the front side of the MN array, and the ECL signal of HOF-101/AuNPs/CS in 100 μL of 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) was tested, with an average luminous intensity of 1.51 × 106 a. u. Subsequently, the PMT was fixed on the back side of the MN array, with the detection port at the same distance from the MN tips as in the previous test. The ECL signal of HOF-101/AuNPs/CS in 100 μL of 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) was tested again, with the MN array immersed to the same depth in PBS. The average luminous intensity measured was 1.23 × 106 a. u., and the light transmission efficiency was calculated to be 81.8%.

To explore the penetration of 3D printed MN arrays, the ECL MN patch was compared before and after piercing piglet skin through tissue sections, trypan blue staining, and SEM characterization methods (Fig. 2i). From the tissue sections, it was observed that the surface of the pig skin had obvious puncture marks, with a puncture depth of 325 μm, which was sufficient to capture infarction-related markers in the ISF since the average thickness of the epidermis was 100 μm. Trypan blue staining revealed that after puncture, distinct blue holes appeared on the piglet’s skin, indicating that the cell membranes of some skin cells had been disrupted by the MNs. SEM images showed that the surface of the piglet’s skin, which had not been punctured, was relatively smooth. After punctured by the MNs, clear needle holes were left on the surface of the piglet’s skin, demonstrating that the mechanical strength of the MNs was sufficient to penetrate the piglet’s skin.

Characterization of the functionalized interfaces on MNs

HOF-101 was synthesized under a long-term crystal growth process using TCPPY monomers (Fig. 3a). The morphology of the synthesized HOF-101 was examined via SEM and is shown in Fig. 3b. The material exhibited a well-ordered, flake-like structure with uniformly arranged edges, indicative of high crystallinity. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was conducted to further characterize HOF-101 and AuNPs. As shown in Fig. 3c, the cross-sectional width of the HOF-101 nanosheets was approximately 3 μm. AuNPs exhibited a regular spherical shape with a particle diameter of about 24 nm (Supplementary Fig. 3).

EDS mapping confirmed the abundant presence of C and O elements (Fig. 3d, e). Compared with the monomer structure of TCPPY (Supplementary Fig. 4), no elemental changes were observed in the EDS analysis, suggesting that the formation of the porous crystalline structure was driven by non-covalent hydrogen bonding between the organic monomers. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the chemical composition of HOF-101 (Fig. 3f). The XPS survey spectrum of HOF-101 confirmed the presence of C and O elements in the material. The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum of HOF-101 exhibited peaks at 288.9 eV, 285.1 eV, 284.5 eV, and 284.4 eV, corresponding to C=O, C-O, C=C, and C-C bonds, respectively (Fig. 3g)37. The core scan spectrum of O 1 s was deconvoluted into two distinct peaks at 533.3 eV and 531.8 eV, corresponding to O–H and C–O bonding environments, respectively. These findings, along with the absence of additional elemental signals in the XPS survey (Fig. 3h), confirm that HOF-101 is composed exclusively of carbon and oxygen atoms, consistent with its hydrogen-bonded organic framework structure. The molecular structure of HOF-101 was investigated using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) technique (Supplementary Fig. 5). A series of diffraction peaks appeared at 8.7°, 12.7°, 23.2°, and 27.1°, confirming the good crystallinity of the synthesized HOF-101 in consistency with the previous literatures32,38. The results of thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicated that HOF-101 exhibited significant thermal stability (Supplementary Fig. 6). Starting from room temperature, the weight loss of HOF-101 was minimal below 100 °C, and it only experienced a 3.53% weight loss below 200 °C. In contrast, the monomer TCPPY of HOF-101 showed continuous weight loss with increasing temperature, reaching a 6.19% weight loss at 200 °C. This demonstrated that HOF-101 had excellent thermal stability, which was mainly attributed to the stable supramolecular framework composed of hydrogen bonds. Additionally, sensing platforms are designed to operate under physiological conditions, typically around 37 °C. Thermal stability analysis confirmed that HOF-101 remains structurally stable at this temperature, supporting its suitability for in situ applications in the human body.

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was conducted to verify the successful assembly of the Y-shaped DNA probe. Individual single-stranded components—CP1, CP2, AP, and L-Fc—along with the assembled Y-probe, were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 3i, CP1 exhibited two distinct bands, likely due to partial self-dimerization. The migration patterns of the remaining single strands were consistent with their expected molecular weights. The Y-shaped probe displayed a distinct band with lower mobility compared to its individual components, confirming successful hybridization and formation of the higher-molecular-weight Y-structure. The other three single strands each show a clear band with a small molecular weight. After high-temperature treatment and rapid cooling process, multiple clear bands greater than 500 bp were formed, along with an excess unpaired CP1 band, proving the successful synthesis of the Y-shaped probe. AFM analysis was conducted to verify the linking condition of Y-shaped probes. A 0.25 μM Y-shaped probe was incubated onto a silicon wafer with gold nanoparticles sputtered by magnetron at 4 °C for 8 h. As depicted in Fig. 3j, the 3D images of AFM indicated that the sputtered AuNPs were evenly spread on the surface of the silicon wafer, with an average height of approximately 5 nm. Upon modification with the Y-shaped probe. The regions modified with the Y-shaped probe exhibit minimal height variation, with an average height of about 25 nm (Fig. 3k). The regions without probe modification have an average height of approximately 5 nm, signifying no collapsed probes were observed in these areas. The height of Y-shaped probes was calculated as 22–28 nm based on the number of bases, aligns well with the height observed in the AFM images, suggesting that the probes were bound through Au-S bonds and were in an upright orientation on AuNPs surface, thereby facilitating maximum interaction with antigen molecules.

As shown in Fig. 3l, the docking model revealed multiple non-covalent interactions, including salt bridges, electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonds, carbon–hydrogen interactions, and Sulfur–X contacts. The calculated docking score was −159.7, and the confidence score was 0.5483 (Supplementary Table 7), indicating a favorable binding orientation. These results suggest that the two aptamer sequences within the Y-shaped probe—designed to be partially complementary to the L-Fc quencher strand—possess sufficient structural compatibility to specifically recognize and bind to cTnI. The exploration of the binding affinity between the Y-shaped probe as a ligand and cTnI as an analyte was conducted using the Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) method. As illustrated in Fig. 3m, n, within the concentration range of 3.125 nM to 100 nM of cTnI, there existed a nonlinear regression relationship between the relative response and the concentration of cTnI, which could be fitted into a four-parameter curve with the equation Rmax (a. u.) = 97.99–5010/(C (nM) + 51.21) (R2 = 0.9999). As shown in Fig. 3o, the association rate constant (Ka) was calculated as 1.55 × 105, and the dissociation rate constant (Kd) was 8.19 × 10-3. According to the previous literature, the affinity constant, within the range of 1 × 10−6 to 1 × 10−9, indicates a strong binding capacity between the ligand and the analyte. According to the formula for the affinity constant (KD) = Kd/Ka, the KD value for the Y-shaped probe and cTnI was calculated to be 5.27 × 10−8, suggesting that the Y-shaped probe, due to the two aptamers targeting cTnI, exhibited a strong binding affinity with the cTnI molecule. Molecular docking analysis was performed to evaluate the binding affinity and interaction modes between the Y-shaped probe and the target biomarker cTnI.

To verify the stepwise construction of the ECL sensing interface, standard glassy carbon electrodes were employed using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) methods to assess the surface modification (Fig. 3p, q). The electron-transfer resistance (Ret) was acquired from the semicircle diameter of the EIS curve. The bare electrode showed maximum current while the Ret is minimum compared to those of other steps due to the good conductivity of the electrode, which had almost no resistance to hinder the electron transfer process. After coating the luminescent complex HOF 101/AuNPs/CS, the peak current decreased, and the Ret increased significantly because of the insulated CS layer39. After incubated with the Y-probes, the CV response continued to decrease while the Ret kept increasing, probably ascribed to the electronegative phosphate backbone of the probes repelled the [Fe(CN)6]3-/4- probes from reaching the electrode surface40. Meanwhile, the low conductivity of MCH layer led to the decrease of CV response and increase of the Ret further. The results suggested that the Y-probes were successfully immobilized to the electrode surface through Au-S bond. As cTnI was recognized by the Y-probes, the CV peak current further decreases while the Ret value continues to increase, demonstrating that cTnI was successfully captured onto the electrode surface through the bivalent recognition of the probes41. The above experimental results demonstrated that the layer-by-layer modification process was successfully carried out.

To verify the binding efficiency of AuNPs with Y-shaped probes, we performed fluorescence characterization. The L-Fc single strand DNA was replaced with a L-FAM single strand DNA. CP1, CP2, AP, and L-FAM were hybridized using the same protocol to form Y-shaped probes. The fluorescence values of the Y-shaped probes at different concentrations were tested and subjected to linear fitting. The measured fluorescence intensity gradually increased with the increase in concentration of the FAM modified Y-shaped probes (Supplementary Fig. 7). Within the concentration range of 0.03125 μM to 1 μM, a linear relationship was observed between the fluorescence intensity and the concentration of the Y-shaped fluorescent probes (Supplementary Fig. 8). The equation for this relationship is: Fluorescence Intensity (a. u.) = 1204482 × C (μM) - 17119 (R² = 0.9914). 90 μL of 0.2 nM AuNPs were mixed with 50 μL of 0.25 μM Y-shaped probes and incubated at 4 °C for 8 h42,43. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant, which contained the Y-shaped probes that didn’t bind to AuNPs, was collected and tested for fluorescence intensity. The fluorescence intensity of the supernatant was 12,000 a. u., corresponding to a concentration of approximately 0.02 μM (Supplementary Fig. 9). This indicates that about 8% of the Y-shaped probes didn’t bind to AuNPs. Therefore, the binding efficiency of AuNPs with the Y-type probes was approximately 92%.

Establishment of ECL in wearable MN-based sensing

A fixture is used to tightly contact the side of the MNs with the spring contacts on the flexible PCB board to apply real-time electrochemical signals to the MNs (Fig. 4a). Meanwhile, the changes in ECL signals in the WE region of the MNs are transmitted through the transparent porous MNs and sensitively captured by the PMT. The artificial ISF containing a certain concentration of biomarkers is placed in a petri dish, thereby achieving an in vitro simulation operation (Fig. 4b).

To evaluate ECL signal interference from individual components, ECL responses were recorded for bare MNs, CS-modified MNs, AuNPs/MNs, and fully modified HOF-101/AuNPs/CS/MNs. Meanwhile, ECL signals were also detected for 1 mg/mL of Ru(bpy)32+/MNs compared with HOF-101/AuNPs/CS/MNs. Materials adopted in the reaction system, such as CS and AuNPs, showed almost no ECL signal (Fig. 4c), indicating that the ECL signal in this system originates from HOF-101. Moreover, compared with the ECL of Ru(bpy) 32+/MNs, the ECL signal of HOF-101 is 87 times that of Ru(bpy)32+. Therefore, HOF-101 was more suitable as an ECL material for in vivo in situ monitoring. The ECL signal of HOF-101 could be quenched by the Fc of the Y-shaped probe immobilized on the MNs, with the intensity of 2.88 × 105 a. u. (curve a and b, Fig. 4d). However, the L-Fc chain was released when a certain concentration of cTnI was specifically recognized by the two aptamers, leading to the recovery of the HOF-101 signal of 9.28 × 105 a. u. (curve c). It could also be seen under the confocal fluorescence microscope that the fluorescence of HOF-101 on the MN array was quenched by the Y-shaped probes. After incubation with a certain concentration of cTnI, the fluorescence signal of HOF-101 was recovered (Supplementary Fig. 10).

To ensure optimal in vivo sensing performance, the applied potential range for the HOF-101/AuNPs/CS-modified ECL MN array was optimized to maximize ECL response (Fig. 4e). As the potential range increased, a modest enhancement in ECL intensity was observed. Notably, at a range of −1 V to +1 V, the ECL signal was nearly undetectable, indicating insufficient excitation under these conditions. However, when the applied potential range was −8 V to +8 V, a significant ECL signal was observed, with the luminescence signal value reaching 1.23 × 106 a. u. The range of −8 V to +8 V is the limit potential range that the electrochemical workstation can provide, and also the starting potential range for generating ultra-strong ECL. Since the safe voltage for humans is 36 V, the range of −8 V to +8 V is within the safety limit and can be used for in situ ultra-bright ECL monitoring in rats to further discuss its safety44.

The concentration of the Y-shaped probe was also systematically optimized (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Y-shaped probes with different concentrations of 0.05 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.5 μM, 0.75 μM, and 1 μM were immobilized on the AuNPs-modified MNs. The ECL signal was obtained after quenching. It was observed that the ECL signal showed slight changes with the increase of the Y-shaped probe concentration less than 0.25 μM, indicating increasing cTnI could bind to the Y-shaped probe. When the concentration exceeded 0.25 μM, the quenching ECL value increased rapidly with the increase in the concentration of the Y-shaped probe. This might be attributed to the fact that at concentrations higher than this value, a large number of Y-shaped probe molecules existed, causing significant steric hindrance that hinders electron transfer at the electrode interface, thereby reducing the quenching effect. Therefore, the optimal concentration of the Y-shaped probe was determined to be 0.25 μM.

The incubation time of cTnI was also optimized (Supplementary Fig. 11b). The incubation time started from 10 min, with a gradient of 10 min each. When the MN electrode was incubated in artificial ISF containing 10 ng/mL cTnI for 20 min, the ECL signal reached its peak. When the incubation time was less than 20 min, the incubation became more sufficient as time extended. However, when the incubation time exceeded 20 min, prolonged soaking in a large amount of liquid could easily lead to the detachment of the thin conductive gold film, thereby affecting electron transfer in the ECL reaction. In the in situ monitoring, the limited volume of interstitial fluid minimizes potential impact on the gold film coating. To ensure biosafety during dynamic in vivo operation, the ECL reaction was conducted in pure phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) without the use of toxic co-reactants.

Analytical performance of MNs sensing platform

The detection sensitivity of the MNs sensing platform under optimal testing conditions is crucial for sensitively reflecting the swift changes in the concentration of protein biomarkers at the beginning of low levels in ISF. Figure 4f exhibited the relationship between the ECL recovery value and cTnI solutions with concentrations of 10 ng/mL, 1 ng/mL, 100 pg/mL, 10 pg/mL, 1 pg/mL, 100 fg/mL, 10 fg/mL, and the blank. Increasing concentrations of cTnI led to enhanced bivalent binding by the dual-aptamer Y-shaped probes on the MN surface, resulting in greater release of L-Fc strands and correspondingly stronger recovery of the ECL signal. As shown in Fig. 4g, the change in the ECL signal value was linearly related to the concentration of cTnI within a certain range. The linear equation is ΔECL (a. u.) = 98197.00 lg C (pg/mL) + 229465.24 (R² = 0.9953). According to the detection limit (LOD) calculation formula (3σ-b)/k (where σ refers the standard deviation of the blank, b refers to the intercept, and k refers to the slope), the LOD was calculated to be 21.3 fg/mL. Notably, the proposed ECLD system enabled highly sensitive detection without relying on reverse iontophoresis.

To verify the specificity of the MNs, the ECL signal recovery values of four AMI related biomarkers containing blank artificial ISF, 1 μg/mL of D-dimer, myo, HSA, cTnT, 10 ng/mL of cTnI, and a mixed solution were tested (Fig. 4h). The concentration of interfering substances was 100 times that of cTnI, while the corresponding ECL signal recovery values were close to that of blank. The difference between the mixed solution and the independent detection results of cTnI was slight, indicating the high concentration of interfering substances had almost no effect on the detection of cTnI. Therefore, the ECLD exhibited excellent specificity.

To validate the repeatability of the ECLD MNs, three modified MNs were stored in a 37 °C oven and subjected to continuous ECL signal detection for 15 days (Fig. 4i). The repeatability was excellent for the first 12 days, with a CV value of 3.16%. On the thirteenth day, the ECL signal difference was significant, indicating a decline in the performance of MNs. Therefore, the ECLD MNs could be repeatedly tested within 12 days.

To verify the stability of the ECLD MNs, continuous 25 cycles of ECL signal scanning on the MNs were performed (Fig. 4j). The MNs maintained relative stability within 25 cycles, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 5.25%, indicating that the ECLD MNs had good stability.

To verify the versatility of the ECLD MNs platform, Y-shaped probes targeting AMI-related protein biomarkers such as NT-proBNP, D-Dimer, myo, HSA, and cTnT were designed. The sequences of the four primers corresponding to each Y-shaped probe are all shown in Supplementary Table 2–6. PAGE was used to indicate the successful synthesis of each Y-shaped probe (Supplementary Fig. 12). New heavy bands appeared in the lanes of each Y-shaped probe, indicating that the five Y-shaped probes targeting different AMI protein biomarkers were successfully synthesized. The quenching and recovery of ECL signals on the flexible ECLD MNs platforms were investigated (Fig. 4k). In the in vitro detection, each ECL platform exhibited different degrees of quenching and recovery, among which platform targeting cTnT showed the best quenching effect and the greatest recovery degree. Therefore, the versatile platform has the potential to detect multiple protein biomarkers.

The ECL mechanism of HOF-101 was also considered as annihilation pathway. Within the applied potential window, HOF-101 underwent an oxidation reaction at the anode to form the HOF-101 cation radical (HOF•+) during the positive scan (Fig. 4l). During the cathodic reduction reaction, the HOF-101 anion radical (HOF•-) was generated. The generated cationic and anionic radicals underwent annihilation at the MN electrode surface, forming the excited-state species HOF-101*, subsequent relaxation to the ground state resulted in the emission of ECL signals.

The biocompatibility of the MNs sensing sensor was also an important indicator for further in vivo analysis. The morphology of rat cardiomyocytes (H9c2) cells was observed under an optical microscope, appearing spindle-shaped with distinct nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 13). The nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Under a fluorescence microscope, distinct blue fluorescent nuclei were observed (Supplementary Fig. 14)45. Mycoplasma detection was performed on H9C2 cells using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (D101) from Nanjing Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Supplementary Fig. 15). The color of the culture medium of the H9c2 cells was consistent with that of the negative standard, indicating that there was no mycoplasma contamination during the cell culture process. The cytotoxicity of Y-shaped probes and HOF-101 material on cells was verified using the CCK8 assay kit. H9c2 cells were seeded at a density of 1×10³ cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium and cultured overnight. Subsequently, 2 μL of Y-shaped probe solution (at concentrations of 1 μM, 0.5 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.125 μM, 0.0625 μM, and blank) and 2 μL of HOF-101 solution (at concentrations of 0.4 mg/mL, 0.2 mg/mL, 0.1 mg/mL, 0.05 mg/mL, 0.025 mg/mL, and blank) were added to different wells, respectively. The cells were then cultured for 0 h, 16 h, 24 h, 32 h, and 48 h (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Afterward, 10 μL of CCK8 reagent was added to the mixture and further incubated for 1 h under the same conditions. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used as the blank control, and the absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm. Each experiment was repeated three times. The cell viability was calculated using the following formula: Cell viability (%) = [(Experimental group means OD value − Blank mean OD value)/(Control group means OD value − Blank mean OD value)] × 100%. Neither the Y-shaped probes nor HOF-101 significantly inhibited the viability of H9c2 cells (Fig. 4m, n), serving as promising materials with low toxicity adopted in live animals. The apoptosis of mouse H9c2 cells treated with 1 μM Y-shaped probes and 0.4 mg/mL HOF-101 for 48 h was analyzed using a flow cytometer. Compared with the negative control group (Supplementary Fig. 16a), no significant differences were observed in the apoptosis results of the cells treated with the materials (Supplementary Fig. 16b). The cells maintained their normal morphology and structure, indicating good biocompatibility. These data, collected by the ECLD, provided sufficient possibilities of the in vivo real-time monitoring of protein biomarkers.

In situ dynamic ECL monitoring of AMI related biomarkers in live animals

Therefore, we further assessed the in vivo diagnostic potential of the ECLD, SD rat models of AMI, sham surgery, and healthy groups were constructed to verify the feasibility of in situ real-time monitoring (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Additionally, Y-shaped probes targeting cTnI and cTnT were used to construct different response platforms to verify the universality of ECLD. After being raised under the same conditions for four weeks, the rats in the AMI group, sham surgery group, and healthy control group underwent their respective procedures. Following insertion of the ECLD into the skin via the MN patch, the device was allowed to stabilize for a period before initiating the monitoring phase. Meanwhile, serum from the rats during the same time period was collected for ELISA signal comparison. According to the classical ELISA procedure as described in the instructions, the equilibrium incubation time was 5 h, and the detection time was 5 min (Fig. 5a). AMI is an acute disease; the early warning of AMI and the therapeutic intervention by doctors are crucial for improving the survival rate of patients. Therefore, the in vivo concentration changes of AMI-related biomarkers, including cTnI and cTnT, were monitored by ECL MN patches within 6 h after the establishment of the tested model. After depilating the abdomen of the rats, the ECLD was continuously fixed on the rats’ lumbar abdomen, with MNs inserted into the abdominal skin to contact the ISF (Fig. 5b). After equilibration, the ECLD test was initiated every 30 min, and blood was collected from the eye of the rats at the same time. The ECL test signal obtained at the moment of the first insertion into the skin was used as the baseline for subtraction. The puncture marks left after the insertion of the MNs indicated that the MNs had successfully penetrated the epidermis.

Discussion

In summary, a highly sensitive, selective, and versatile in-situ ECLD system toward future wearable integration for real-time biomarker monitoring is proposed. By seamlessly integrating microneedles (MNs) with miniaturized ECL components, the platform enables ultrasensitive in vivo detection of protein biomarkers using an electrochemiluminescence (ECL)-based strategy.

Selective and continuous in vitro monitoring was achieved through the adoption of Y-shaped dual-aptamer probes, offering high specificity and a wide linear detection range from 100 fg/mL to 10 ng/mL, with a limit of detection (LOD) as low as 21.3 fg/mL. The system leveraged an ultrabright and biocompatible luminophore, HOF-101, which produced ECL intensities 87-fold higher than traditional emitters. This enhancement effectively addressed the longstanding challenge of insufficient light penetration through both MN structures and skin during in vivo sensing.

The ECLD demonstrated operational stability over 12 days and successfully enabled the development of flexible sensing platforms for multiple AMI-associated protein biomarkers, including cTnI, NT-proBNP, D-dimer, myoglobin (myo), human serum albumin (HSA), and cTnT. Together, these results highlight the system’s robustness, sensitivity, and the proposed ECLD system prohibited the injection in skin and irritation issues associated with ECL coreactants, thus transforming traditional ECLD from in vitro detection to in vivo monitoring. The monitoring performance of the designed ECLD system was evaluated in vivo using both SD rats and Bama pigs. The ECLD-based early warning system demonstrated a clear distinction in signal output between AMI and non-AMI groups within 2 h of onset. Notably, in cases of more severe AMI, the signal differentiation from healthy controls occurred even more rapidly, underscoring the system’s potential for prompt detection and early-stage intervention. Compared to previously reported systems (Table 1), the proposed ECLD has shown remarkable sensitivity, wide linear dynamic range, and online detection capability. A key advantage of the ECLD is its online detection capability, enabling real-time monitoring. In contrast, methods like fluorescence microscopy for PSA or interleukin-6 require offline processing, limiting their use in point-of-care settings. In terms of sensitivity, our ECLD achieves an ultralow detection limit of 21.3 fg/mL, significantly surpassing colorimetric (TNF-α: 4.5 pg/mL) and fluorescence-based assays (IL-6: 0.33 pg/mL)1,46. Regarding the linear dynamic range, the platform covers 100 fg/mL–10 ng/mL (five orders of magnitude), broader than cytokine-active reset sensors (10–500 pg/mL)47. Unlike fluorescence microscopy or offline assays1,48, our ECLD provides continuous, online monitoring in vivo, a critical advantage for early detection and intervention in acute conditions.

The proposed ECLD offers several notable advantages. First, the ultrabright HOF-101 emitter achieved a high ECL intensity of 1,231,942 a.u. in ISF without the need for external coreactants, enabling reliable in vivo signal acquisition via the PMT. The transparent MNs, with a light transmittance of 81.8%, further facilitated effective signal transmission, supporting robust in situ ECL monitoring.

Second, the integration of the ECL system with porous MNs was accomplished without reliance on reverse iontophoresis, significantly simplifying the sampling and detection process.

Third, in comparison to other real-time monitoring platforms for protein biomarker detection, the ECLD demonstrated superior analytical performance, offering a lower limit of detection and broader linear range, thus enhancing its utility for early-stage, real-time clinical monitoring.

The primary application scenario of the proposed ECLD early warning system is critical-care environments, such as intensive care units (ICUs), emergency rooms, and ambulances, where patients are typically bedridden and continuous monitoring is required. In such settings, wired connections remain clinically practical and widely adopted, offering superior signal stability, high signal-to-noise ratio, and reliable data acquisition while avoiding potential issues associated with wireless transmission, such as latency, signal loss, or electromagnetic interference. By providing real-time biomarker feedback, the system can assist healthcare professionals in making prompt and informed interventions, thereby reducing the risk of mortality in patients with acute diseases. For patients at high risk of acute myocardial infarction, ICU-deployed ECLDs enable real-time tracking of cardiac biomarker dynamics, supporting timely and data-driven clinical decision-making. When integrated with telemedicine platforms, these continuous data streams can extend outcome-oriented monitoring beyond the hospital setting, facilitating early intervention during critical deterioration. Such early-warning capability—already transformative in other acute-risk clinical scenarios—highlights the translational potential of ECLDs as an enabling technology for next-generation emergency and remote cardiac care6.

Compared with a wearable surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) microneedle system with flexible electronics49, achieving wireless and highly miniaturized integration of electrochemiluminescence devices (ECLDs) presents two primary technical challenges related to system-in-package integration and power management. First, further miniaturization of photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) is required, as the size of the currently employed PMTs limits the integration of additional functional modules within the ECL microneedle unit. Second, ECL detection—particularly under high-frequency or continuous monitoring conditions—is intrinsically energy-intensive, placing stringent demands on low-power circuit design and energy-management strategies. Future development will therefore focus on modularizing device components and implementing wireless data transmission to decouple the sensing and control/display modules. In parallel, the adoption of high-energy-density miniature batteries, together with low-power microprocessors operating in intelligent intermittent modes, is expected to reduce power consumption, improve user comfort, and enhance freedom of movement during long-term monitoring.

Also, the proposed ECLD faces limitations. (Ⅰ) Due to interfacial effects of wearable sensors caused by dynamic deformation, the detection accuracy of the device is influenced by the environment. Coating a hydrogel or chitosan layer may well protect the sensing interface. (Ⅱ) The small sample size limits the generalizability of the results. Improvements will concentrate on increasing the number of animal subjects to further enhance the reliability of the sensing data. (Ⅲ) Ethical and regulatory barriers have delayed validation of the ECLD in real human settings. The ECLD will be miniaturized and integrated to enable robust, low-power operation under the dynamic and complex conditions of human physiology. (Ⅳ) The use of solid microneedles may mechanically disrupt ECL probes upon repeated contact with dermal interstitial fluid. In future designs, flexible microneedles based on hydrogel or chitosan substrates will be explored to fabricate soft MN architectures that improve mechanical compliance and interfacial stability. (Ⅴ) The operational lifetime of continuous in vivo monitoring is currently limited by the stability of the modified ECL emitters. To address this, organic linkers within HOF structures may be further coupled with electroactive materials or metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) to optimize optical performance, yielding highly stable and biocompatible HOF-based composites for incorporation into ECLDs and thereby enhancing long-term monitoring stability. (Ⅵ) Given that the current ECLD relies on wired signal transmission, the device is designed to detect a single target per measurement. Multi-biomarker monitoring would require sequential replacement of aptamer sequences and microneedle arrays. In future developments, multi-channel electrochemical addressing combined with wireless data transmission and ECL luminophores resolved by distinct excitation potentials may enable simultaneous detection of multiple biomarkers.

Looking forward, the responsiveness of the ECLD system could be further enhanced through the integration of machine learning algorithms50–52. Deep learning architectures applied to biomarker time-series data may enable the system to recognize subtle temporal trends in ECL signal intensities, allowing accurate prediction of pathological changes within 1–2 min. This advancement would not only reduce the required monitoring duration but also improve patient comfort and compliance, ultimately enhancing the overall user experience and clinical efficacy53.

In addition, future efforts will focus on embedding a therapeutic module within the ECLD platform. By functionalizing the MN array with both recognition probes and corresponding therapeutic agents, and by engineering a stimulus-responsive release mechanism, the system can evolve into a closed-loop diagnostic–therapeutic platform54,55. This would pave the way for personalized, on-demand treatment strategies tailored to chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and asthma, thereby extending the utility of the ECLD from early diagnosis to integrated disease management56,57.

Despite the promising performance of the ECLD system, several challenges remain concerning its wearable integration. The fabrication of conductive MNs via noble metal sputtering is highly sensitive to parameters such as sputtering height, current, and duration. Consequently, both the intrinsic stability of the ion sputtering instrument and the operator’s precision in controlling deposition conditions are critical for achieving uniform and reproducible conductivity. Our current validation indicates the in vivo stability of long-term monitoring to clinically relevant use cases for acute patients. Though the ECL MN module is connected to the control/display unit via wires. With future miniaturization and Bluetooth connectivity, the platform could evolve into a wireless system for free-moving animals and portable patient monitoring.

Furthermore, the optical transparency of the MNs directly influences the efficiency of luminescence transmission. Surface contamination or imperfections can significantly attenuate the ECL signal, necessitating stringent surface cleaning protocols. To overcome these limitations, the adoption of high-performance ion sputtering equipment, implementation of automated fabrication workflows, and application of high-standard surface cleaning treatments are essential for ensuring device reliability and scalability.

Synthesis of HOF-101

A vapor diffusion process was carried out to form HOF-101 according to the literature58. Firstly, 5 mg of TCPPY was dispersed homogeneously in 1.0 mL of DMF under ultrasonic for 5 min in a 5 mL beaker. The beaker was then placed into a sealed 100 mL beaker containing 10 mL of acetone in dark conditions. To maintain solvent levels, acetone was periodically replenished to 10 mL over the course of one month, during which yellow, needle-like crystals gradually formed.

Synthesis of AuNPs

Before the synthesis24, all reaction vessels were immersed in aqua regia to ensure cleanliness. The synthesis underwent by mixing 100 mL of distilled water with 0.5 mL of a 2% HAuCl4 aqueous solution and boiled in an oil bath. 0.5 mL of a 1% sodium citrate aqueous solution was added dropwise under continuous stirring for a duration of 20 min59. Once the reaction solution turned to wine-red, the solution was cooled to room temperature before being stored at 4 °C for future applications.

Preparation of chitosan

1 g of chitosan powder was dissolved in 50 mL of 2% acetic acid and stirred for 2 h36. The solution was heated at 80 °C until completely dissolved. Then the solution was centrifuged at 1698 × g for 0.5 h to diminish bubbles.

Synthesis of Y-probes

100 μL each of 4 μM CP1, CP2, AP, and L-Fc chains were mixed in equal volumes in an Eppendorf tube, and then heated in a metal bath at 95 °C for 10 min41. The resulting solution was immediately transferred to an ice bath to facilitate rapid cooling. For short-term storage, the mixture can be preserved at 4 °C under refrigeration.

Design of 3D printing MNs

Digital light processing printing method was considered to construct a batch of hollow MN arrays using biocompatible ultraviolet (UV) curable resin. The integrated ECL MNs comprised three parts, including working electrode with array size of 11 × 15, a reference electrode, and counter electrode with array size of 3 × 15, respectively. MNs were patterned with a length of 1000 μm, a diameter of MN base of 250 μm, a diameter of MN tip of 80 μm, and an interspacing of 750 μm60.

Following 3D printing of the integrated MNs based on the designed schematics, residual uncured resin was removed by ultrasonic cleaning in ethanol. The fabricated MNs were then carefully detached from the 3D-printed holder by precision cutting and subsequently cured under UV light for 5 minutes using a UV curing system.

Construction of ECL MN Sensor

For conducive ECL sensing capabilities, preparation step was referred to a previous literature with slight modifications. To increase the adhesion of Au on MNs, the MN was treated with TMCS steam for 3 min to form the hydrophobic surface. Then, a thin film of Au was deposited using Au spurring (10 mA, 40 s)61. For construction of the reference electrode, thin silver film was deposited on the MN specific area (10 mA, 100 s) and placed at room temperature. Next, 60 μL of 1 mg/mL HOF-101, 30 μL of 0.1 μM AuNPs, along with 3 μL of 2 wt% CS, were mixed together, followed by 80 μL of the mixture coated on the working MNs. After dried at ambient temperature, the modified MN was carefully rinsed with ultrapure water, and 50 μL of 0.25 μM Y-probes were incubated on the MNs and incubated at 4 °C for 8 h. 30 μL of 1.0 mM 6-Hydroxy-1-hexanethiol (MCH) was utilized to block the nonspecific binding site and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. For the ECL measurements, the scanning potential range of cyclic voltammetry (CV) was set from −8 V to +8 V at a scan rate of 1 V/s in a 0.1 M PBS solution (pH 7.4). For electrochemical characterization, CV was adopted from −0.2 V to 0.6 V at a scan rate of 0.1 V/s in 5 mM K3[Fe(CN)6]/K4[Fe(CN)6] solution containing 0.1 M KCl. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was also conducted in the same electrolyte with CV characterization in the frequency range of 1 Hz to 100,000 Hz and at an alternating voltage of 5 mV61.

PAGE gel electrophoresis

The as-prepared polyacrylamide gel (8%) was adopted to load the 10 μL of mixture solution containing 8 μL of 4 μM DNA strands and 2 μL of loading buffer Ⅲ. The gel was immersed in 1×TAE standard buffer, and the electrophoresis was conducted at 120 V for 120 min. Then the gel was stained in Yea Red and imaged by 4100 digital gel image processing system.

Cell culture and apoptosis assays

H9c2 cells were cultured with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS in 6-well culture plates (1 × 105 cells/well) overnight, then treated with 10 μL Y-probes and 10 μL HOF-101 independently for 24 h45. The cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with 50 μL 1 × binding buffer, 2.5 μL Annexin V-FITC, and 5 μL PI staining solution for 10 min in the dark environment. After adding 200 μL 1 × binding buffer, the apoptotic cells were detected using flow cytometry within 1 h.

Toxicity analysis

H9c2 cells, at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well, were cultured in 100 μL of the aforementioned medium for a period of 12 h. Subsequently, 2 μL of a 1 μM Y-shaped probe solution and 2 μL of a 1 mg/mL HOF-101 solution were introduced into different wells, respectively. After incubation periods of 0 h, 16 h, 24 h, 32 h, and 48 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The plate was then further incubated in the cell culture incubator for an additional hour at a temperature of 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Absorbance measurements at 450 nm were conducted to assess cellular viability. The percentage of cell survival was calculated using the formula: (OD value of experimental group − OD value of blank solution)/(OD value of control group − OD value of blank solution) × 100%.

Histology

Masson’s trichrome staining was conducted to identify the content of collagen fibers in the tissue and calculate their distribution following the guidance of the manufacturer. The infarct size was calculated via Fiji software through calculating the quantification percentage of the area of blue fibrosis in the total area of the heart.

Animal experiments

Six-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were housed under conditions of 65–70% relative humidity, 22 °C ± 1 °C, and a 12-h dark/12-h light cycle. Fifteen rats were equally divided into three groups, including an AMI model group, a sham operation group, and a healthy group, for detection of both cTnI and cTnT. The AMI model in mice was constructed using the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation technique. Initially, three mice were anesthetized, and their limbs were secured to ensure stability during surgery. After shaving the fur on the left thoracic region, the surgical site was disinfected. Blunt dissection was performed to separate the muscle layers and expose the rib cage. The third and fourth ribs were carefully fractured to access the thoracic cavity, followed by incision of the pericardium. The LAD coronary artery was ligated to induce myocardial infarction. Subsequently, the muscle and skin layers were sutured, and air was aspirated from the pleural cavity to restore negative intrathoracic pressure.

Given the anatomical and physiological similarities between porcine and human hearts, a 30 kg Bama pig was selected to establish an acute myocardial infarction (AMI) model for further validation of the ECLD’s universality in in situ real-time biomarker monitoring62. A 30 kg Bama pig was anesthetized via intramuscular injection of 1.2 mL of Zoletil 50, a combination of tiletamine and zolazepam, to ensure deep sedation prior to surgical intervention. After the pig was placed in a supine position, a tracheal tube was inserted and connected to a ventilator. A thoracotomy was performed along the 3rd and 4th intercostal spaces of the pig to expose the heart. The pericardium was lifted and longitudinally incised. A silk suture was pre-threaded below the middle-lower one-third junction of the main trunk of the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) for subsequent ligation. Subsequently, four pieces of multi-point fixed epicardial electrode patches were sutured onto the heart surface, and the epicardial electrocardiogram was recorded before and after ligation to observe for ST-segment elevation. Finally, the thoracic cavity was closed, and the ventilator was removed.

In situ ECL monitoring with the MN patch

Animal experiments were carried out under controlled conditions of 22–25 °C and 40–60 % relative humidity. After anesthesia by isoflurane and depilation by a shaver, the skin was gently wiped with sterile saline and kept at 37 °C with a thermostatic heating pad. Throughout the experiment, the MN array was firmly secured to the designated site with medical tape and enclosed in a protective cone to prevent the animal from chewing the electrodes. The ECL-MN patch was attached tightly to the abdomen of immobilized rat models or the ear of Bama pigs. Animals were immobilized for exactly 2 min during each recording session and remained tethered for a total duration of 10 days for rats and 16 h for Bama pigs per experiment. The ECL mode contained the scanning potential range of CV from −8 V to +8 V at a scan rate of 1 V/s in ISF.

Statistical analysis

All the analytical performance were carried out with three replicates. The error bars refer to the standard deviation (SD). The animal sample sizes are randomly assigned (five rats per group) without blinding. All statistical results were analyzed by two-way ANOVA using Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test by GraphPad Prism 9 and linear regression analyzed by using origin 2022 software. Significance level was implied by p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****), and ns (no significance), respectively. The LOD for detection of cardiac biomarkers using ECLD MN sensors in vitro was calculated using the formula: LOD = (3σ - b)/k, where σ refers to the standard deviation, b refers to the intercept and k refers to the slope of the fitted line.
