Diversity of culturable gut bacteria associated with brown planthopper, Nilaparvata lugens (Stål) and their role in imidacloprid degradation
Authors: D Dhyan Chowdary, Y Sridhar, G Ramachandra Rao, M Sheshu Madhav, P C Latha, V M Malathi, A P Padmakumari, R M Sundaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe brown planthopper, Nilaparvata lugens (Stål), is a major pest of rice across Asia and has developed significant metabolic resistance to multiple insecticides. Microbiome-mediated insecticide resistance is of emerging interest in various insect pests, including N. lugens. In this study, we assessed the diversity of culturable gut bacteria in imidacloprid-susceptible (IMI-S) and imidacloprid-resistant (IMI-R) populations of N. lugens and their potential to degrade imidacloprid. Molecular characterization and phylogenetic analysis identified 13 bacterial isolates, representing ten families from three phyla: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Firmicutes. The IMI-R population has contributed to nine of the 13 isolates. Screening for imidacloprid degradation identified four bacterial strains viz., Paenibacillus amylolyticus, Serratia marcescens, Acinetobacter soli and Brucella sp. associated with the IMI-R population were capable of growing in minimal salt medium supplemented with imidacloprid. Notably, S. marcescens and P. amylolyticus could utilize imidacloprid as a sole source of carbon and nitrogen, respectively. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that P. amylolyticus and S. marcescens after 14 days of inoculation degraded 73.07 and 66.92% of imidacloprid (100 mg/L), respectively. These findings underscore a strong association between gut-microbial composition and insecticide resistance in N. lugens and highlight the potential of specific bacterial strains for microbiome-based pest and resistance management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41348-y.Keywords: Gut-bacterial diversity, Paenibacillus amylolyticus, Serratia marcescens, Degradation, Insecticide resistance, Nilaparvata lugensSubject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The brown planthopper, Nilaparvata lugens (Stål) (Hemiptera: Delphacidae), remains one of the most destructive pests of rice, causing yield losses of 20–80%1 and annual economic damage of approximately $300 million in Asia2. N. lugens inflicts damage primarily through phloem feeding, leading to “hopper burn” symptoms and also transmits viral diseases such as ragged stunt, grassy stunt and wilted stunt viruses3. For the management of N. lugens chemical insecticides are being used extensively and the ability of N. lugens to metabolize these insecticides has resulted in development of resistance to nearly all major insecticide classes. Globally, N. lugens has been reported to have developed resistance against 35 insecticide active ingredients, with a total of 457 documented cases of field-evolved resistance4. Imidacloprid, the first commercial neonicotinoid introduced in the early 1990 s for planthopper control, acts systemically and exerts its toxicity by competitively modulating nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), disrupting the insect nervous system5. Despite its long-term use, imidacloprid remains a key component in the management of N. lugens within the Indian rice ecosystem6–10. Nevertheless, extensive and prolonged usage has led to the development of resistance in multiple field populations of N. lugens across India11–14.

In recent years, the role of insect-associated microbiota in influencing host physiology and adaptation has received considerable attention. Insects harbor diverse microbial communities, predominantly within their gut, which contribute to a wide array of functions beyond digestion, including nutrient provisioning, development, reproduction, immunity, behavioral modulation, degradation of recalcitrant substrates and detoxification of xenobiotics15,16. Symbiotic bacteria and fungi in N. lugens have been linked to host development, survival and fitness17. Notably, several studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota play a role in insecticide resistance, either through the direct metabolic degradation or through the synergistic interactions with the host’s detoxification pathways12,18–21. Therefore, deciphering the composition and functional roles of insect gut microbiota is of great importance in decoding the insecticide resistance mechanisms. Malathi et al.22 investigated the bacterial diversity associated with laboratory-reared (insecticide-susceptible) and four field-collected (insecticide-resistant) populations of N. lugens through 16 S rRNA gene-based molecular characterization and identified 17 bacterial isolates belonging to nine genera across seven families, primarily within the phyla Firmicutes and Proteobacteria. Similarly, Wang et al.23 analyzed the symbiotic bacterial communities in various N. lugens populations, identifying 18 operational taxonomic units (OTUs), with the majority of taxa classified within the classes Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidetes. These findings underscore the growing interest in understanding the role of microbiota in insecticide resistance and its implications for pest management. Comparative analyses of bacterial communities between insecticide-susceptible and resistant N. lugens populations may provide valuable insights into the microbiome’s contribution to resistance mechanisms, offering potential avenues for the development of innovative, microbiome-targeted pest management strategies.

Chemical insecticides are among the most pervasive classes of xenobiotics released into the environment, contributing to widespread contamination of soil, water and food resources. Among the various degradation mechanisms, microbial degradation has emerged as an effective and environmentally sustainable approach for the in-situ remediation of neonicotinoid residues24. Several studies have highlighted the role of insect-associated microbiota in pesticide degradation. For instance, Madhusudhan et al.25 demonstrated that gut bacteria from Helicoverpa armigera contribute to insecticide resistance, with isolates CL4 (Rhodococcus sp.) capable of utilizing chlorpyrifos and CL2 (Enterococcus casseliflavus) able to degrade both chlorpyrifos and malathion in minimal salt media (MSM). Whereas, Leifsonia sp. a soil bacteria isolated from the contaminated sites degraded 70% of imidacloprid within 14 days, producing desnitro and urea metabolites26. Despite these findings, there are relatively few works investigating the role of insect gut microbiota in insecticide detoxification specific to N. lugens. Identifying and characterizing gut microbiota involved in the insecticide metabolism could provide critical insights into microbially assisted insecticide detoxification pathways and their potential application in pest management strategies. Therefore, the present study was undertaken for a comparative analysis of the symbiotic gut bacterial communities associated with imidacloprid-susceptible (IMI-S) and imidacloprid-resistant (IMI-R) populations of N. lugens, with a focus on their diversity and potential role in imidacloprid metabolism.

Diversity of culturable gut bacteria

In the present study a total of 13 bacterial isolates were identified and characterized on molecular basis (Table 3 & Supplementary Fig. 1,2). Taxonomic analysis revealed that these isolates belonged to ten families distributed across three bacterial phyla: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Firmicutes. Proteobacteria was the most abundant phylum accounting for seven of the total isolates, followed by Actinobacteria (4 isolates) and Firmicutes (2 isolates). The diversity of culturable gut bacteria in IMI-R population was higher with nine isolates representing all three phyla. In contrast, only four isolates were obtained from the IMI-S population, which were restricted to two phyla, Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria. Acinetobacter was the only genus found to be associated with both the IMI-S and IMI-R populations, indicating its broader ecological association.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic relationships of the gut bacterial isolates from IMI-S and IMI-R populations of N. lugens were inferred on the basis of 16 S rRNA gene sequences. The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) constructed by the maximum likelihood method resolved the 13 bacterial isolates into two major clades. The clade-1 comprised of the genera Rothia, Kocuria, Microbacterium, Paenibacillus, Rossellomorea and Brucella. Within this clade, Brucella formed a distinct branch, separated from the other genera with a bootstrap support value of 62, indicating a moderate phylogenetic divergence. Clade-2 comprised of the genera Serratia, Pantoea, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas and Achromobacter. Notably, within the clade-2, the genera Achromobacter and Pseudomonas formed independent branches separated from the remaining genera with bootstrap support values of 46 and 56%, respectively suggesting a clear phylogenetic separation. The phylogenetic tree was rooted using Thermoproteota as the outgroup, providing a reference point for evolutionary divergence among the studied isolates.

Screening of bacterial isolates for imidacloprid degradation

The nine bacterial isolates obtained from the IMI-R population were screened for their ability to grow in different MSM supplemented with imidacloprid at varying concentrations. Among these, only four isolates Serratia marcescens strain RP1, Paenibacillus amylolyticus RP4, Acinetobacter soli RP2 and Brucella sp. RP3 grew on full-strength MSM containing imidacloprid up to a concentration of 100 mg/L. Further screening revealed that S. marcescens strain RP1 was capable of growing on the C-L-MSM and P. amylolyticus strain RP4 on the N-L-MSM. The isolates S. marcescens and P. amylolyticus that were able to utilize imidacloprid as a sole source of carbon and nitrogen, respectively in MSM plate assays were further screened in MSM broth. Both the S. marcescens and P. amylolyticus exhibited an enhanced growth in the full-strength MSM broth supplemented with imidacloprid @ 100 mg/L compared to the lower concentrations of 25 and 50 mg/L, indicating a dose-dependent response (Fig. 2). Additionally, P. amylolyticus showed increased growth in N-L-MSM, while S. marcescens failed to grow in C-L-MSM under the same conditions.

LC-MS/MS analysis for estimation of imidacloprid degradation

The LC-MS/MS analysis of the broth samples (supplemented with imidacloprid @ 100 mg/L) 14 days after incubation with the bacteria showed a single peak at a retention time of 3.88 min (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3). The quantities of the imidacloprid remaining in the broth that was inoculated with P. amylolyticus strain RP4 and S. marcescens RP1 were 21.47 and 26.38 mg/L respectively, as compared to 79.75 mg/L in the uninoculated control (Table 3). The decrease in the concentration of imidacloprid in the control treatment was attributed to the abiotic degradation and was accounted for in calculating the corrected %degradation by the bacterial activity. The degradation of imidacloprid (100 mg/L) 14 days after inoculation by P. amylolyticus strain RP4 and S. marcescens strain RP1 were 73.08 and 66.92%, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

Discussion

The gut microbiota of insects plays a crucial role in several functions such as host nutrition, development, immune response, detoxification of xenobionts including insecticides, synthesis of essential metabolites, resistance to pathogens and overall physiology18,27–29. However, in N. lugens, the gut bacterial community is reported to be relatively less diverse30,31. In the present study, 13 bacterial isolates representing 11 genera were identified from two distinct populations of N. lugens. Among these, the genera Acinetobacter, Serratia, Microbacterium, Pantoea, Pseudomonas, Paenibacillus and Brucella have been previously reported from various N. lugens populations22,32–35. Whereas, Kocuria, Rothia and Achromobacter genera were frequently reported from other insect species36–38. Importantly, this study reports the detection of the genus Rossellomorea of the family Bacillaceae in insects for the first time. However, it was earlier reported from diverse marine ecosystems, plant roots and rice husk39–41.

We found the diversity of gut bacterial communities to be less in the laboratory-reared imidacloprid susceptible N. lugens population compared to the resistant population. Similar findings were reported in Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner)42 and Plutellaxylostella L43.. The insects of resistant population were exposed to continuous insecticide pressure, that can favour the proliferation of specific microbial taxa capable of degrading or detoxifying xenobiotics44. Consequently, the gut microbiota of resistant populations tends to be more diverse and functionally specialized compared to laboratory-reared susceptible populations. Several bacterial isolates identified in the present study have been previously reported for their ability to degrade various pesticides. For instance, Xia et al.45 isolated a strain of S. marcescens from the midgut of Riptortus pedestris(Fabricius), demonstrating its capacity to solubilize phosphorus and degrade the organophosphorus insecticide dimethoate. Zeng et al.34. isolated S. marcescens from buprofezin-resistant N. lugens and also demonstrated the upregulation of several candidate genes involved in buprofezin degradation upon exposure to buprofezin. Similarly, Paenibacillus sp. was found effective in biodegradation of organochlorine pesticides (17 compounds), ranging between 24 to 98%46. Furthermore, strains of Pseudomonas have demonstrated a significant potential in degrading modern insecticides. Gao et al.47 reported that the Pseudomonas strain GW13 could co-metabolize chlorantraniliprole, achieving optimal degradation efficiency at a concentration of 200 mg/L. Likewise, the Pseudomonas strain BYT-1 was capable of utilizing pymetrozine as the sole carbon source, degrading 2.30 mM of pymetrozine within 20 hours48.

In the present study, P. amylolyticus strain RP4 and S. marcescens strain RP1, the two gut bacterial isolates from the N. lugens, degraded imidacloprid by 73.08 and 66.92%, respectively within a 14-day period. These results clearly establish the role of these two gut bacteria in the detoxification of imidacloprid in N. lugens thereby contributing to development imidacloprid resistance in N. lugens populations. In a similar study, the silk worm the gut bacteria, Klebsiella pneumoniae strain BCH1, was found to degrade imidacloprid (50 mg/L) to an extent of 78% within seven days49. Madhuban et al.50 reported that an aerobic bacterium Burkholderia cepacia strain CH9 was able to degrade imidacloprid (50 mg/L) in a mineral-salt medium and resulted in 69% degradation of imidacloprid within 20 days. Co-metabolism is a frequently observed mechanism during the microbial degradation of xenobiotics26,51,52. In the present study also, S. marcescens and P. amylolyticus were found to degrade imidacloprid by utilizing D-glucose and NH4Cl from the culture medium as the alternate sources of carbon and nitrogen, respectively along with those from the imidacloprid.

The ability of the bacterial strains P. amylolyticus RP4 and S. marcescens RP1 to degrade imidacloprid could serve as a foundation for understanding the microbial contributions to insecticide resistance mechanisms in the N. lugens, complementing the metabolic and genetic bases of resistance. Throughout the co-evolution, indigenous gut microbiota has adapted to the specific ecological niche of the insect gut, offering diverse metabolic benefits to their hosts. Symbiotic microbial communities enable insects to tolerate or detoxify xenobiotics through specialized metabolic pathways. One well-characterized mechanism involves the production of detoxification enzymes by symbionts upon exposure to insecticides. These enzymes facilitate the breakdown of insecticides via co-metabolism or complete mineralization, thereby reducing their toxicity53. In addition to enzymatic degradation, interactions between the host immune system and gut symbionts contribute to cooperative resistance mechanisms54. Another important defense strategy is the formation of a peritrophic matrix, composed of chitin microfibrils embedded in a protein-carbohydrate complex55,56. This semi-permeable barrier not only facilitates nutrient absorption and digestive enzyme activity but also protects gut epithelial cells from pathogenic microbes and toxins57,58. Such physiological barriers effectively reduce pesticide penetration and activation within the host, thereby contributing to resistance mechanisms without necessarily altering the gut microbial load59. In Lepidoptera, for instance, gut symbionts like Enterococcus species form biofilm-like structures attached to the mucus layer of the gut epithelium, providing additional protection against insecticides and pathogenic invasions60–62. The present findings, along with previous studies, not only deepen our understanding of host- microbe interactions in the context of insecticide metabolism but also highlight the importance of exploring gut microbial diversity and functionality, thereby paving the way for future research aimed at harnessing microbial biodegradation capacities for pest management strategies.

The present study documented the gut bacterial diversity in IMI-R and IMI-S populations of N. lugens and demonstrated the imidacloprid-degrading potential of two bacterial strains, P. amylolyticus RP4 and S. marcescens RP1. These findings provide foundational evidence for the role of gut microbiota in mediating insecticide resistance in N. lugens. However, in this study the pathways of imidacloprid degradation which may be hydroxylation and nitro-reduction pathways63 and the resultant metabolites were not assessed. Overexpression of host detoxification genes64,65 due these two bacterial isolates if any, needs to be investigated. Further, it will be interesting to screen these two bacterial strains for their ability to degrade other commonly used insecticides in N. lugens management. Interactions between the different insect gut bacterial communities are reported to influence their functional roles, adding another dimension to the subject. Thus, future studies on the factors altering the abundance of gut bacteria, their interactions and role in the expression of host detoxication genes will enrich the understanding of insect gut microbe mediated resistance to insecticides, that might aid in the development of novel, microbiome-based strategies for sustainable insect pest control.

Materials and methods

Insect populations

A nucleus culture of N. lugens was obtained from Bayer Bio-Sciences, Hyderabad, India, which had been maintained in a glasshouse for five years without exposure to insecticides. To ensure genetic uniformity and to maintain insecticide susceptibility, the population was further reared for 10 consecutive generations without any insecticide exposure under glasshouse conditions at the ICAR-Indian Institute of Rice Research, Hyderabad, and was designated as the imidacloprid-susceptible strain (IMI-S). For developing the imidacloprid resistant population (IMI-R), a subset of the nucleus culture was subjected to a continuous selection pressure for 10 successive generations by exposing to the sub-lethal doses of imidacloprid. Selection was conducted at each generation to enhance resistance development. Both IMI-S and IMI-R populations were maintained in insect-proof cages on 45-day-old potted rice plants (TN-1) under controlled environmental conditions: temperature 27 ± 1 °C, relative humidity 70–80% and a 16-hour light: 8-hour dark photoperiod. A commercial formulation of imidacloprid 17.8 SL (Confidor®, Bayer Crop Sciences) was used for selection and all bioassays.

Insecticide bioassay

The rice seedling dip method, as standardized by the Insecticide Resistance Action Committee, with suitable modifications was employed for the bioassay66,67. The experimental procedure for bioassay was followed as per the methodology of Dhyan et al.68, wherein 45-day-old TN- 1 rice plant stems were used. Preliminary trials were conducted to establish an appropriate concentration range of imidacloprid. Based on these exploratory assays, eight final concentrations were selected, along with an untreated control. For each of the eight concentrations three replications were maintained. Fifteen third-instar nymphs of N. lugens were released onto the treated rice stems in each replication and the mortality of the nymphs was assessed 72 h post-exposure. The mortality data was subjected to probit analysis69 using the Polo Plus software70. The Resistance Ratio (RR) was calculated as the ratio of the LC50 of the resistant population to that of the susceptible population71. Based on the RR, the insecticide resistance was categorised into four groups; low resistance: RR < 10-fold, moderate resistance: RR = 10 to 40-fold, high resistance: RR = 40 to 160-fold and extremely high resistance: RR > 160-fold72. Based on this classification, the imidacloprid resistant (IMI-R) N. lugens population that was developed in this study was moderately resistant.

Isolation of culturable gut bacteria

Culturable gut bacteria were isolated from both the IMI-S and IMI-R populations of N. lugens following the standard protocol73. A total of 20 fifth-instar nymphs from each population were collected and starved overnight to the clear gut contents. The nymphs were immobilized by chilling at − 4 °C for 5 min, followed by surface sterilization with 70% ethanol for 1 min and 0.1% sodium hypochlorite for 1 min. Subsequently, rinsed thoroughly with autoclaved distilled water. All the procedures were performed under aseptic conditions inside a laminar airflow chamber. The antennae and legs were carefully removed using sterile forceps and micro-scissors. The head also was removed, abdomen was excised and the gut contents were transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing 1 mL of 0.2% sodium chloride solution. The gut contents were homogenized using a sterile micro pestle and were serially diluted in sodium chloride solution up to 10− 6 concentration. From each dilution (10− 3 to 10− 6), 100 µL aliquots were plated onto the Nutrient Agar (NA) and Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates, with three replicates. Inoculated plates were incubated at 34 °C in a BOD incubator for 72 h. Colony growth was monitored at 24-hour intervals. Emerging colonies were differentiated based on morphological characteristics, including the colony shape, size and colour. Distinct morphotypes were selected and sub- cultured onto fresh plates by streaking (five to six passes) to obtain pure cultures. Pure bacterial cultures were preserved in 20% glycerol stocks at − 80 °C for long-term storage until further use.

Molecular characterization and phylogenetic analysis of gut bacteria

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted using GSURE® bacterial genomic DNA isolation kit (GCC BIOTECH, India). Isolated DNA was amplified with 16 S rRNA gene target primers 341 F (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 785R (GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC)74. PCR amplification products were sequenced using the Sanger dideoxy chain termination method with the BigDye™ Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit on an ABI 3730xL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, UK). The sequencing of the bacterial isolates was performed by Barcode BioSciences Pvt.Ltd (Bangalore, India). The obtained sequences were quality-checked, aligned and assembled into contigs using Cap3 sequence assembly within the BioEdit software. Homology search of the sequences was done in the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank nucleotide database employing BLASTn. Sequence identity was determined based on the percentage similarity to the sequences in the NCBI public database. The obtained sequences were submitted to the NCBI database. For phylogenetic analysis, partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences of the isolates and closely related reference sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the CLUSTALW program. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA version 12.0 with 1000 bootstrap replications to assess branch support.

Screening of bacterial isolates for imidacloprid degradation

Degradation assays were conducted using the technical-grade analytical standard of imidacloprid (≥ 99.90% purity) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). All the bacterial isolates were screened for their ability to degrade imidacloprid by agar plate assay method supplemented with imidacloprid @ 25, 50 and 100 mg/L, along with the control treatment (without imidacloprid). Four versions of minimal salt medium (MSM) were used: full-strength MSM, nitrogen-limiting MSM (N-L MSM), carbon-limiting MSM (C-L MSM) and carbon- and nitrogen-limiting MSM (C-N-L MSM), each supplemented with the aforementioned concentrations of imidacloprid. The full-strength MSM was composed of Na2HPO4 (6.0 g/L), K2HPO4 (3.0 g/L), NaCl (0.5 g/L), MgSO4 (0.25 g/L), NH4Cl (1.0 g/L) and glucose (1.0 g/L). In N-L MSM, the NH4Cl was omitted; in C-L MSM, the glucose was omitted and in C-N-L MSM, both the NH4Cl and glucose were omitted. The bacteriological agar was used as the solidifying medium.

Bacterial isolates capable of utilizing imidacloprid as the sole carbon and/or nitrogen source were selected for quantitative degradation assays in liquid MSM broth. Cells of the selected bacterial isolates were harvested from the LB broth precultured at 28 °C and 120 rpm until late-exponential phase. The LB broth was centrifuged at 5000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellets were washed twice with sterile distilled water and then resuspended in the same. These suspensions were referred to as LB-precultured resting cells, in which the optical density (OD660) was adjusted to 2.0. For each of the above- mentioned concentration 30 mL broth was transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes and inoculated with 50 µL of precultured resting cells of the selected bacterial isolates. The MSM broth without bacterial inoculum were maintained for each concentration was the control treatment to monitor the bacterial growth and the abiotic degradation of imidacloprid. These centrifuge tubes were kept in an incubator shaker at 28 °C and constant shaking at 120 rpm. The OD readings at 660 nm were recorded at 0, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h. Growth curves were plotted for the bacterial growth and the OD values across the tested imidacloprid concentrations.

LC-MS/MS analysis for estimation of imidacloprid degradation

The selected bacterial isolates were inoculated into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL of the full-strength MSM supplemented with 100 mg/L of imidacloprid. Uninoculated flasks containing the same concentration of imidacloprid served as abiotic controls to estimate the natural degradation. All flasks were incubated at 28 °C with constant shaking at 120 rpm for 14 days. After incubation, broth samples were collected, centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and the resulting supernatants were stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. For extraction, 10 mL of culture supernatant was subjected to liquid–liquid extraction twice using an equal volume of dichloromethane (DCM). The combined organic layers were passed through anhydrous sodium sulphate and concentrated using a rotary evaporator. The residue was reconstituted in 10 mL HPLC-grade acetonitrile. Extracts were filtered through 0.2 μm PTFE membrane filters and 2 mL aliquots were analyzed using a Shimadzu LC-MS/MS 8045 system at the National Institute of Plant Health Management (NIPHM), Hyderabad. Analytical parameters were optimized for imidacloprid detection and quantification.
