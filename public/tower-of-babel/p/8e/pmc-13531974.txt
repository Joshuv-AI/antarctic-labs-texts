Adaptation and exploratory factor analysis of a PCOS-specific HRQoL questionnaire in a South Indian population
Authors: Ashitha Washington, Heera T Shenoy, Prejisha B, Ravindra Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPolycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a multifaceted disorder with physical, emotional, and social implications. Culturally adapted tools for assessing Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) in Indian women, particularly those under 18 and over 45 years of age, need to be developed. This study aimed to culturally adapt the Nasiri-Amiri PCOS-specific HRQoL questionnaire for Malayalam-speaking women, evaluate its reliability and factor structure using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and identify key HRQoL domains in this population.MethodsBetween July 2024 and December 2025, a 52-item Malayalam-adapted questionnaire was distributed to women aged 13–52 years, of whom 201 met the inclusion criteria. Reliability assessments were performed using Cronbach’s alpha. The ability of the data to be factored was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. Maximum likelihood extraction with Equamax rotation was used for exploratory factor analysis, with factor retention guided by the root-mean-square residual.ResultsStrong internal consistency for the instrument (Cronbach’s α = 0.818) and adequate sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.794; Bartlett’s p < 0.001) were assessed for exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which provided eight clinically relevant HRQoL domains (29 items total). The domains identified in this study included hirsutism, skin disease, menstrual irregularities, physical/emotional burden, emotional inconsistency, body image, problems with self-care, and social support. The major contributors to impaired HRQoL were menstrual dysfunction and other visible symptoms. Within the study sample, women reported greater distress associated with their weight than their appearance. Paracetamol use was comparatively higher for self-medicated pain relief during menstruation.ConclusionExtending the assessment beyond the conventional 18–45 year reproductive window reveals that the PCOS-related burden persists into adolescence and adulthood. The adapted Malayalam PCOS-HRQoL tool showed acceptable reliability, making it a useful measure of quality of life in women with PCOS in this population. Further confirmatory factor analysis and longitudinal studies are required to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04609-6.

Introduction

Rapid changes in lifestyle and food habits over the last few decades have caused a huge increase in metabolic disorders, particularly in women, as evidenced by the global prevalence of approximately 9.5% of polycystic ovary syndrome [1]. The prevalence varies across population samples, as age, ethnicity, race, and diagnostic criteria influence the statistics [2]. In polycystic ovary syndrome, the genetic disposition of affected individuals, along with lifestyle and epigenetic changes, determines the severity and manifestation of the disease [1]. In a vast country like India, the prevalence ranges from 3.7% to 22.5% [3]. According to Rotterdam standards, the prevalence of PCOS in India is up to 35.3% and is expected to increase over the next few decades [4]. From 1990 to 2021, both the global prevalence and disability burden of PCOS rose by over 30%, with low- and middle-income regions showing the fastest growth [5]. This growth is expected to continue, with an anticipated global increase of over 5.5% compared to current trends [5]. Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) was first described in 1935 by Stein and Leventhal as a reproductive disorder characterized by the presence of immature follicles within the ovaries [6]. The understanding of PCOS has evolved, leading to the introduction of the Rotterdam criteria in 2003. These criteria expanded the diagnostic approach for PCOS phenotypes, requiring the presence of at least two of the following three features: (1) oligo- or anovulation, (2) clinical or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism, and (3) polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) [7, 8]. This revised definition allowed for a more comprehensive identification of PCOS, acknowledging the syndrome’s diverse manifestations [7, 8]. In India, the most prevalent PCOS phenotypes are the C (ovulatory PCOS: Hyperandrogenism + Polycystic Ovary Morphology) and D phenotypes (non-Hyperandrogenic PCOS: Irregular Periods + Polycystic Ovary Morphology) [9]. Certain lifestyle choices, such as exposure to BPA through plastic use, detergents, emissions from fuel combustion, and pesticide exposure through fruits and vegetables, can be risk factors for developing polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) [10]. The alterations in the hypothalamus, pituitary, and ovarian axis, elevated C-reactive protein levels, androgen excess, and metabolic disturbances remain the prominent causal factors for PCOS [11]. There are also genetic causal factors, such as FTH1, GPX4, HAMP, and A2M gene dysregulation, that have been shown to negatively impact PCOS in recent studies [12]. The genetic causal factors also vary, with Indian women who report the homozygous deletion type of the XRCC4 gene showing significantly higher susceptibility to developing PCOS, up to 25 times higher than those with the wild-type insert [13].

Evidence indicates that most women in their process of seeking treatment for infertility are diagnosed with PCOS [14–16]. Although the golden criteria for polycystic ovary syndrome exist, and there has been more public awareness about this condition, there is still a lacuna in arriving at a proper diagnosis [17]. This is due to the existence of multiple diagnostic criteria (Rotterdam, NIH, and AES), the time at which a proper diagnosis is made, requiring multiple consultations with different health practitioners [17], disease labelling of women with mild symptoms being seen as unnecessary [18], and, in some cases, lack of awareness [19].

PCOS is associated with comorbidities such as type 1 diabetes, insulin resistance, cardiovascular diseases, endometrial cancer, binge eating, bulimia, impaired psychosexual function, obesity, sleep apnea, and infertility. PCOS is more common in women with type 1 diabetes(T1DM), with a recent meta-analysis reporting a pooled prevalence of 24% using the Rotterdam criteria [20]. Hyperglycemia, autoimmune effects on the ovaries, and depletion of the ovarian reserve depletion put T1DM women at risk of infertility, shortened fertile window, and early menopause [21, 22]. PCOS is caused by hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance, which are worsened by obesity [23]. Hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance directly increase androgen synthesis and limit hepatic SHBG formation, thereby increasing free testosterone levels [24]. Insulin resistance occurs when adipose tissue becomes saturated with lipid storage, leading to excess fat retention in visceral tissues (liver, heart, and pancreas) and damage to these tissues [23–25]. The 2023 International Evidence-Based PCOS Guideline recommends recognizing cardiovascular disease as a risk factor [26]. This conclusion was based on a comprehensive and rigorous analysis of the evidence, including an updated meta-analysis [27]. Endometrial cancer with a causal association with polycystic ovary syndrome has been estimated to be an economic burden of more than 400 million dollars per year [28]. Women with PCOS exhibited a 1.5-fold increased risk of any eating disorder, notably bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, and disordered eating (excluding anorexia nervosa), with more pronounced relationships in studies employing the Rotterdam criteria and elevated disordered eating scores, irrespective of BMI [29]. Recent research suggests high sexual distress in women with PCOS due to body image issues, regardless of weight [30–32], with insulin resistance impacting hyperandrogenism; however, its impact on female sexual function remains unclear [33–36]. A recent study conducted on data from 24 countries revealed that for a 1 per cent increase in obesity, there was an increase of approximately 0.4 per cent in polycystic ovary syndrome, showing a moderate association [37]. Another recent study revealed that one in every 5 PCOS affected women with PCOS was affected by comorbid obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome [38]. Approximately 13 per cent of women with PCOS are infertile, with the odds increasing with insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia [39]. Most women seeking treatment at infertility clinics have PCOS due to anovulation and irregular periods [39–41].

The burden of poor lifestyle choices conducive for polycystic ovary syndrome to the prevalence of PCOS is a concern that needs to be studied from time to time, especially with an increase in non-communicable diseases such as stress, depression, and weight gain during the challenging lockdown of Covid-19, as observed in a study involving women from Kozhikode district in Kerala [42]. Mental health conditions and quality of life have been found to be highly associated with women with polycystic ovarian syndrome. PCOS also has detrimental effects on pregnancy, with 1 in every 2 births having a chance of being stillborn, 1 in 3 chances of pre-eclampsia, and approximately 16 per cent chance of early pregnancy loss [43–45]. PCOS is also associated with significant psychological morbidity, with higher rates of depression and anxiety reported among adolescents and young women, as highlighted in recent literature [46].

Health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) questionnaires assess the impact of the physical, psychological, and social domains of health on physical, emotional, and social well-being [47]. Evaluation can also be conducted for specific diseases, as the domains are influenced by individual experiences, expectations, perceptions, and beliefs [48, 49]. One of the initial HRQoL questionnaires developed for patients with PCOS dates back to 1988 when it was developed by Cronin [50], after which it was modified by Nasiri-Amiri [51, 52]. While studies from an Indian perspective have shown that concerns related to infertility and social stigmatization are some of the key priorities [53], current trends show that issues such as menstrual bleeding are becoming increasingly important, especially among young girls [54]. Now, majority of the existing questionnaires only cover the ages between 18 and 45 years, thus failing to take care of the interests of adolescent patients and those above 45 years of age. Polycystic ovary syndrome can occur as early as pre-adolescence [55] and can extend to late fertile age [56]. Considering the following gaps in the literature review, including the lack of emphasis on diverse ages and the requirement for culturally appropriate instruments for assessing HRQoL, the objectives of the current study are as follows: (1) Adaptation of the Nasiri-Amiri HRQoL instrument for Malayalam-speaking females in Kerala; (2) Evaluation of the psychometric properties and factor structure of the instrument using EFA; and (3) Determining the HRQoL domains for the particular population. We hypothesized that the adapted Malayalam version would demonstrate acceptable internal consistency and a stable factor structure in this population.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, survey-based study was conducted at the Malabar Medical College Hospital, Kozhikode, Kerala. The questionnaire was shared in two ways: hard copy and Google Form, to facilitate ease of access. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female menstruating participants were either patients or bystanders visiting the gynecological outpatient department/staff or students of the hospital; (2) had menstrual issues such as irregular periods; (3) consented to be interviewed; (4) Age range 13–52 (5) minors whose parents consented to the questionnaire; and (6) patients who had visible signs of hirsutism [52]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnant patients, (2) patients who visited for a mammogram checkup, (3) patients who had/were recommended a hysterectomy, (4) patients who had gynecological issues such as prolapsed uterus, cysts on the uterus, and menopause, and (5) men. A few women with regular periods who were willing to participate were also included to minimize bias. The questionnaire followed a non-probability convenience sampling method, with exclusion and inclusion criteria [52]. In relation to the inclusion criteria, visible symptoms of hirsutism were clinically confirmed by a gynecologist. The total number of collected responses was compared to the total number of patients visiting the tertiary care center on Wednesdays and Saturdays from October 1st, 2024, to December 14th, 2024, between 10AM-2PM (OPD time), giving 18 days of data (as the gynecologist had OPD only on those two days) (Supplementary Tables 1a and 1b).

Study population and ethics committee

The study primarily focused on women who visited the outpatient department and students of Malabar Medical College. The hospital caters to patients from the nearby hilly regions of Koorachundu and Talayad, making it a major tertiary care center for rural areas. The hospital campus also has hostels for medical, nursing and dental students who study at the hospital. The sample size was calculated based on Cochrane’s formula for an infinite population, wherein the percentage was taken as 0.095, giving a minimum of 132 responses needed for this survey [57]. Outpatients and medical students were considered for the questionnaire survey. Ethics approval to conduct the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Malabar Medical College, Ulliyeri, Kozhikode on 14th May 2024(MMCH&RC/IEC/2024/158).

Contextualization of the questionnaire for use in Kerala and data collection

This study employed an adapted version of the 43-item questionnaire developed by Nasiri-Amiri. The process of adaptation included translating the questionnaire into Malayalam. This is done to guarantee that the questionnaire is culturally relevant to the people from the Kozhikode district. A forward translation of the questionnaire by Nasiri-Amiri was carried out from English to Malayalam by the authors and verified by local language experts [58]. Additional questions, such as being biologically female, sexual activity and employment, were given with options “Prefer not to say”/ “Don’t know”, as the questions were delicate in nature. For questions regarding seeking help from support groups, the groups were identified as social media groups and local health workers from ASHA, Anganwadi and primary health centers. After assessing the technical correctness of the questionnaire, the final questionnaire form had 38 items and 14 additional questions related to age of menarche, socio-demographic details and medication, making a total of 52 questions in total, with Likert scale of “Never”, “Rarely”, “Sometimes”, “Often” and “Always” [51]. Due to the sensitive nature of the question, questions related to sex and intimate questions were omitted and were converted to a yes or no or chose not to answer question for this survey. The survey was designed to be attended by women from age group 13 above and was inclusive of women who had no prior diagnosis of PCOS [51]. Duplicate or conceptually overlapping items were retained during data collection but were identified and excluded during exploratory factor analysis. This was true for the question where ‘unattractiveness due to weight gain’ had the coincidence with question ‘unattractiveness due to PCOS weight gain’, as respondents with suggestive PCOS symptoms as well as PCOS symptoms would give the same answer for the question. Therefore, the final list contained 37 questions from the original Nasiri-Amiri questionnaire. Codes were assigned to the questions for ease during exploratory factor analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Individuals eligible for the study were first identified by the attending gynecologist in their clinic, and those who were suitable for the study were referred to the researcher by the gynecologist. In turn, the researcher explained the purpose of the study and sought permission from the individual before giving them the questionnaire. The interview was done face-to-face, and the average time taken to complete the questionnaire was 15–20 min. Out of 110 individuals referred to the study, 104 responded, indicating a response rate of 94.55% (Supplementary table 1a). In case of prior PCOS diagnosis, details about the ultrasound and tests were enquired [17]. Financial or material rewards were not offered to the participants. In case of the questionnaire link being shared, the consent was recorded in the Google sheet, and the description box gave details about the survey. Through this, 97 responses were collected. The respondents belonged to a variety of backgrounds, ranging from self-employed to seasonal workers (Table 1), as well as students from both the medical college and other institutions. A total of 201 responses were collected; from 30/7/2024 to 31/12/2025, with the data collected by link sharing extended even after hard copy collection.

Descriptive-inferential statistics and factor analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.5) and R (version 4.4.2) in Jupyter Notebook [59] and R Studio (version 2024.12.1 + 563) [60], respectively.

The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to evaluate whether questionnaire scores differed significantly across groups (participants with prior PCOS diagnosis (PCOS diagnosed) and without a prior diagnosis of PCOS (first-time consultation)) [61–64]. This was done to compare BMI, Age and Menarche age between the two groups. Matplotlib package (version 3.10.1) in Python was used for inferential statistics plots [65]. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Effect size (r) and Z-scores were calculated using the SciPy (version 1.15.2) library in Python.

Factor Analysis, a multivariate statistical technique, was used to combine related survey questions to reduce redundancy and remove variables that did not contribute to the final outcome of the questionnaire [66]. The suitability of the newly collected information for factor analysis was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity [66–69], in Python using the factor_ analyzer package [70]. The aggregate Cronbach’s alpha was estimated for each item, and the variation resulting from the exclusion of each item was assessed [71]. The core concept of Cronbach’s alpha is that the questionnaire items should be internally consistent through pairwise correlation within an acceptable range (above 0.70) [72].

Exploratory factor analysis

The core of exploratory factor analysis is to estimate the latent factors or internal attributes that cause the fundamental differences between the people observed in the data [73–75]. It is a group of items combined under a specific factor (the group of items would be highly correlated to each other) [73–75]. Since there is no idea about how the observed data would perform, given the inclusion of a wider age group than Nasiri-Amiri and a sample set from different ethnicities, exploratory factor analysis was needed [76]. The steps conducted in exploratory factor analysis are factor retention, factor extraction, factor rotation, and factor interpretation [77]. For factor retention, two methods were used: the Kaiser-Guttman criterion plotted on a scree plot and Comparison Data Forest using the R package EFA factors. Model fit was determined by the Root Mean Square Residual (RMSR; cut-off ≤ 0.08), which was also used as a selection criterion to choose the factor retention technique [78–82]. Maximum Likelihood was used to extract the factors and Equamax was used to rotate the factors, followed by factor interpretation, all of which was done using Python factor_analyzer package. Equamax rotation was used because it simplified the items and factor structure, considering that the HRQoL concepts were multidimensional in nature [49]. It also forces the factors to be independent, and equamax, being a mix of varimax and quartimax orthogonal rotation, simplifies both the factors and the variables [49]. Items with more than 5% missing responses were excluded before the analysis. This mainly pertained to the question related to spousal support, as unmarried women did not qualify for this. The question was removed from the factor analysis, resulting in 36 items [83]. In factor analysis, communality, which ranges from 0 to 1, is the percentage of a variable’s variation explained by common factors [84]. Higher values indicate a stronger explanation by the factors [84]. Consistent across unrotated and rotated loadings, it helps decide variable retention. Communality measurement will also be performed using the Python package factor analyzer [70].

Results

Statistical inference from data collected

Although there were more than 1000 OPD registrations, most patients had come for various ailments such as uterine prolapse, fungal vaginal infection, tumor in the uterus, urinary tract infections, stomach pain, fibroids, post-menopause discomfort, breast tumors, hysterectomy or post-hysterectomy visits, pregnancy cases, regular checkups, miscarriages, and post-delivery visits. OPD cases also included men who came for infertility treatment and individuals who came for premarital tests. Responses were collected according to the inclusion criteria and consent. Of the responses received, 22.39% of the respondents had a prior clinical diagnosis (Table 1).

No significant differences were observed between first-time consultation participants and previously diagnosed PCOS participants with respect to BMI (Mann–Whitney U = 3135.50, Z = − 1.09, p = 0.276, r = 0.08), age (Mann–Whitney U = 3386.00, Z = − 0.36, p = 0.718, r = 0.03), or age at menarche (Mann–Whitney U = 4088.50, Z = 1.68, p = 0.081, r = 0.12) (Fig. 1). Small effect sizes indicate minimal differences between the two groups across these demographic variables (Fig. 1). These findings suggest that the demographic characteristics were broadly comparable between participants with and without a prior PCOS diagnosis (Fig. 1). The demographic results show that most of the participants were aged 13 at menarche (Table 1) and the majority of participants were in the age group 19–24 (~ 49%).

Factor analysis

Suitability tests and preparing data for exploratory factor analysis

Suitability tests determined that the data collected were suitable for factor analysis, with a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of 0.794, which is in the good range, and Bartlett’s test chi-square measure of 3178.234, with a p-value < 0.001, which means there is a considerable difference between expected and observed outcome values [66]. Due to missing values, the item “Support spouse” was excluded from the analysis.

Cronbach’s alpha determined the data reliability and internal consistency, with an overall value of 0.818 (item exclusion Cronbach’s alpha range:0.806–0.825) (Table 2).

Deciding number of initial factors and rotation method

The exploratory factor analysis followed an iterative loop to find the factor loadings and the domain structure (Fig. 4). The factor analysis was conducted iteratively, whereby different factor-retention solutions and rotation methods were assessed based on RMSR, factor loadings, and interpretability. Unsatisfactory solutions were refined through repeated analyses until the final factor structure was identified (Fig. 4).

An overview of the study showing the flow of exploratory factor analysis done for the specific population

The scree plot suggested ten factors according to the Kaiser-Guttman criterion (Fig. 5a). However, 11 and 12 were close to the cut-off. Therefore, an exploratory factor analysis with 11- and 12-factor cut-offs was also planned to examine the factor structure, as they were close to the cut-off line (eigenvalue cut-off = 1). However, the Kaiser-Guttman criterion is also known to overfit factors [85]; an additional factorization was to be performed to determine whether the number of factors could be lower and, if so, by how much (Fig. 4). The Comparison Data Forest Approach (CDF) is a machine learning application that uses factor analysis to determine the number of factors to be retained [81]. The CDF uses the principles of the factor forest and comparison data approaches to accurately predict the factor retention [81]. The CDF predicted eight factors, which was much lower than the Kaiser-Guttman criterion suggested.

However, after exploratory factor analysis, the fit of the structure can be assessed using the Root Mean Square of Residuals (RMSR) (Fig. 4). This measure was used to determine whether the eight factors truly performed better than 10, 11, or 12. This process was performed iteratively to ensure the best results at every stage. The iterative pipeline provided clarity, as 12 was determined as the number of factors to retain initially (Supplementary Table 3), since the RMSR value obtained after the Equamax rotation and factor analysis was less than 0.08 [86] (Supplementary Table 4a). Based on the lower Root Mean Square Residual, there were 12 factors as defined by the Kaiser-Guttman criterion, with an RMSR value of 0.079, which was selected as the optimum number of factors. The maximum frequency of responses was 158 across all questions/items (Fig. 5b). This complete pre- and post-exploratory factor analysis confirmed the optimal factor rotation and the number of factors initially needed. With 12 factors to retain and Equamax rotation, the factor loadings obtained at this iteration were examined.

Factor loadings and domains after vertical and horizontal elimination

The factors were rotated using Equamax rotation and extracted using the maximum likelihood method to obtain factor loadings and communalities. The communality values ranged from 0.19 to 0.99 (Table 3). Communalities indicate how well the latent-variable structure explains each item, whereas factor loadings indicate the latent variable to which each item belongs. There were initially 38 items in the questionnaire, of which ‘weight gain due to PCOS unattractiveness’ was removed due to duplicate answers, and ‘spouse support’ was removed due to unmarried candidates not fitting the criteria. This reduced the number of items to 36. Horizontal and vertical eliminations were applied. First, horizontal elimination was performed, removing any items with loadings below 0.35 (the cut-off value for factor loadings) [49]. ‘Consequences PCOS,’ ‘Exercise,’ ‘Hydration,’ and ‘PCOS support group were removed from the factor structure (Supplementary Table 3). This reduced the number of items from 36 to 32. Before the next elimination, items with loadings greater than 0.35 on more than one latent variable were marked, and each item was assigned to the latent variable with the highest loading. Each column corresponds to a latent variable (Supplementary Table 3). ‘Weight gain unattractiveness’ had loadings in the 2nd and 6th columns; the higher loading was in the 6th column, so the item loaded on the 6th latent variable (Supplementary Table 3). ‘Poor sleep quality’ had loadings in the 3rd and 8th columns; the higher loading was in the 3rd column, so the item loaded on the 3rd latent variable (Supplementary Table 3). ‘Mood swings’ had loadings in the 3rd and 10th columns; the higher loading was in the 10th column, so the item loaded on the 10th latent variable (Supplementary Table 3). ‘Not being able to be active’ had loadings in the 3rd and 12th columns; the higher loading was in the 3rd column, so the item loaded on the 3rd latent variable (Supplementary Table 3). Since latent variables call for more than one item to be correlated, any latent variable with just one item was removed from the factor structure using vertical elimination (column-wise elimination). ‘Healthy diet’,’ ‘Inconvenience faced, and ‘Acne products were vertically eliminated (Supplementary Table 3). This further reduced the number of items to 29. The 12th column had no loadings assigned as ‘not being able to be active’ loaded under the 3rd column (Supplementary Table 3). The 7th, 8th, 11th, and 12th columns were eliminated from the factor loadings structure at this point, leaving the remaining eight columns as eight domains (to be considered as latent variables) (Supplementary Table 3).

Exploratory factor analysis results

From the exploratory factor analysis, eight domains and 29 items were finalized (Table 4). Exploratory factor analysis identified multiple clinically meaningful domains reflecting the health-related quality of life in women with PCOS. ‘Hirsutism’ emerged as a strong and coherent factor, with high loadings for ‘difficulty getting rid of facial hair’ (0.877), ‘grooming difficulty’ (0.883), ‘excess hair–related underconfidence’ (0.859), and ‘fast hair growth’ (0.620), indicating that visible hair growth and its management substantially impair self-perception and daily functioning (Table 4). Skin problems also formed a distinct domain, characterized by difficulty in hiding darker skin and its effect on clothing choice (0.747 and 0.701), ‘unattractiveness of darker skin patches’ (0.827), ‘difficulty finding suitable skincare products’ (0.627), and ‘PCOS-related skin unattractiveness’ (0.522), highlighting the cosmetic and psychosocial burden of pigmentation and skin changes (Table 4). A broad physical and emotional burden factor was identified, dominated by ‘fatigue’ (0.797), ‘depressed or sad mood’ (0.725), ‘brain fog’ (0.593), and ‘reduced ability to be active’ (0.457), along with ‘heavy menstrual flow’ (0.457), ‘concentration loss’ (0.451), and ‘poor sleep quality’ (0.362), indicating that PCOS exerts a combined somatic and psychological toll (Table 4). The support system formed a strong protective domain, with high loadings for ‘family support’ (0.840), ‘friends’ support’ (0.825), and ‘support from male family members’ (0.771), while ‘societal awareness of PCOS’ loaded more weakly (0.475), suggesting that interpersonal support is more influential than community-level awareness (Table 4). Irregular menstruation was one of the most robust clinical domains, with very high loadings for ‘irregular periods’ (0.924) and ‘delayed menstrual cycles’ (0.854), confirming that menstrual dysfunction is a core feature affecting QoL (Table 4). Body image disturbance emerged primarily through weight-related distress, driven by ‘feeling misunderstood about weight gain’ (0.849) and ‘weight-related unattractiveness’ (0.563), whereas ‘feeling different’ showed a weaker contribution (0.364) (Table 4). Difficulties in personal care clustered around challenges in ‘maintaining skin care’ (0.786), with ‘fitness maintenance’ contributing less strongly (0.368), reflecting the daily self-management burden of PCOS (Table 4).

Finally, emotional dysregulation was represented by ‘impatient’ (0.738) and ‘mood swings’ (0.476), indicating that emotional instability constitutes a meaningful but narrower psychosocial domain. Collectively, these factors demonstrate that PCOS-related HRQoL impairment in this population is multidimensional, encompassing visible symptoms (hirsutism and skin changes), menstrual dysfunction, emotional and physical exhaustion, body image distress, reliance on social support, and challenges in self-care (Supplementary Table 3) (Table 4). The most common complaint among a few respondents was the lack of excessive menstrual flow, suggesting that there is an abundance of scant menstrual cycles. This finding corresponds with the clinical characteristics of patients with PCOS, since oligomenorrhea and hypomenorrhea are usually found among these patients but remain less recognized than symptoms such as menorrhagia.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the underlying factors associated with HRQoL in women with PCOS in a South Indian population using exploratory factor analysis. The main strength of this study is that the questionnaire was made inclusive for a broader age group, from 13 to 52, open to symptoms faced by women with PCOS beyond the reproductive age group. The results revealed the existence of multiple factors associated with HRQoL in women with PCOS based on the modified questionnaire, including psychological distress, body image problems, and social and emotional well-being. These results imply that HRQoL is influenced not only by clinical factors but also by sociocultural aspects of life. This study used an iterative loop to connect the pre-EFA and post-EFA analyses, linking factor retention with the RMSR value to improve the exploratory nature of the EFA (Fig. 4).

Contextual observations supporting interpretation of latent constructs

In this survey, 22.38% of the respondents had recurring PCOS, while 33.83% of the respondents took medications, mostly for pain management purposes. The rural population constituted the majority of the population facing heavy periods (12.44%), irregular periods (13.43%), and late menstrual cycle (11.94%) in the subgroup who had come for their first consultation. Recent research shows that the incidence of PCOS is more than 10 per cent, with most women facing irregular periods, and psychosocial problems are often poorly addressed [15, 87, 88]. The present study showed results consistent with previous observations, supporting an increasing trend among polycystic ovary syndrome patients with PCOS in rural areas [88]. PCOS-related concerns should also be assessed in older women, as evidence indicates that women with PCOS may experience menopause later than normo-ovulatory women, resulting in an approximately two-year longer reproductive lifespan [89].

During the questionnaire data collection, the respondents were asked whether they had any additional comments regarding polycystic ovary syndrome. More than 70% of the individuals surveyed felt that thin facial hair had a lesser impact on their body image than obesity. Comments were primarily negative when facial hair or acne was present. Participants who experienced body shaming recognized that obesity is mistakenly perceived to result only from overindulgence. The present results correspond to the latest research showing that body image issues are less prevalent in non-white ethnicities [90]. The Likert scale also recorded a reluctance in approaching support groups for PCOS, whereas in Kerala, this is mostly possible through social media, ASHA workers, primary health centers, or local women’s societies. However, this reluctance stems from the fact that women proactively visit hospitals for any menstrual irregularities. Although not included in the questionnaire, informal conversations revealed limited awareness of polycystic ovary syndrome, especially among older individuals. In contrast, younger respondents were more aware of the condition through social media and the experiences of friends and family members facing symptoms such as mood swings, severe stomach pain, heavy menstrual bleeding, obesity, and depression. Similar research on awareness of polycystic ovary syndrome among undergraduate students revealed that proper information dissemination significantly enhanced awareness of PCOS among undergraduates [91]. These responses reflect the need to improve questionnaire assessments for women with PCOS from different cultural backgrounds. Apart from considering the superficial adolescent and young women’s fertility concerns, PCOS should be taken seriously as it could have unhealthy consequences in the perimenopausal and postmenopausal age. South Asian women with PCOS often face insulin resistance that can impact their metabolic health, with a tendency of more than 80% [92, 93]. The findings suggest that PCOS-related HRQoL impairment may persist beyond the reproductive age group, although this requires confirmation through age-stratified longitudinal studies. These observations also explain why the factor structure is heavily influenced by the cultural context and population studied.

Factor analysis-based results

In the current analysis, an 8-factor structure with 29 items was found, unlike the 6-factor structures described in both the PCOSQ-50 and the updated version of PCOSQ-43 designed by Nasiri-Amiri et al., where domains of psychosocial and emotional well-being, fertility, sexual function, obesity and menstrual irregularity, hirsutism, and coping/body image were included [51, 52]. Although critical aspects, such as hirsutism and menstrual irregularities, were still part of the new structure, more concrete dimensions emerged in relation to wider categories, such as psychosocial functioning, for instance, in the case of emotional dysregulation and physical-emotional burden [51, 52]. Brain fog and mood swings have been attributed to young, pre-menopausal, and peri-menopausal women [94, 95]. Diet, exercise, and stress reduction are lifestyle modifications that may enhance long-term health outcomes [96].

Moreover, there are other emerging domains, such as dermatological issues, support structures, and personal care, which were not covered by the initial tool [51, 52]. This indicates the significance of visible signs, management issues, and interpersonal support in determining QoL among the participants [52, 53]. However, some other domains, such as fertility and sexual performance, were not identified as distinct dimensions, which may be attributed to the different demographics of the study sample, including adolescents and unmarried people [51, 52].

Methodological disparities, such as the application of maximum likelihood extraction and equamax rotation, could account for the observed diversity in the factor structure. In general, the increase from six to eight factors underscores the level of specificity and adaptability rather than conceptualization inconsistency, highlighting the significance of culturally adapting HRQoL measurement tools across different populations.

Additionally, Equamax was used for rotation because of its performance during the initial iterative process of determining the number of factors and its effect on the RMSR value. Promax and Oblimin are popular rotation methods, and Equamax is a mix of Varimax and Quartimax, making it relatively new in EFA rotations [97, 98]. The iterative exploratory analysis showed better performance for the Equamax rotation, with the RMSR dropping below 0.08 at the Equamax iteration with 12 factors, which is the standard cutoff for RMSR (Supplementary Table 4a). This approach combined pre- and post-EFA analyses to determine the optimal factor retention and rotation parameters. The rationale for this iterative process was the uncertainty regarding how the items would be arranged in the factor structure, given the diverse range of respondents and a broader age range. However, for 12 factors, both promax and oblimin showed different factor structures, with promax leaning towards 10 latent variables and oblimin leaning towards nine latent variables (Supplementary Table 4b). Promax yielded 31 items distributed across ten latent variables (Supplementary Table 5). Oblimin had a total of 27 items distributed under nine latent variables (Supplementary Table 6). The results suggest that the data had a more orthogonal structure than an oblique structure. Equamax brings both the essence of varimax and quartimax, which gives a balanced factor structure with an orthogonal structure [98]. Even though there might be some connections between certain psychosocial HRQoL constructs, the main goal of this study was to determine unique factors of PCOS-specific HRQoL from a cultural perspective. Oblique rotations (Promax and Oblimin), despite having higher RMSR values than Equamax, were also tested; however, they did not yield a clearer pattern of factor loadings. Equamax has an added advantage that it redistributes loadings in such a way that each item has a dominant loading, while minimizing cross loadings [98]. Consequently, an orthogonal Equamax rotation became the preferred rotation method in this case. Another observation is how well the comparison data forest predicted 8, and the latent variables turned out to be eight as well. This observation highlights the need to emphasise that reliance on multiple methods for factor retention can enhance iterative EFA [99]. Connecting pre-EFA and post-EFA analyses is also a good way to conduct a comprehensive analysis from start to finish, ensuring that no technical steps in the EFA process are overlooked.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting its results. First, the choice of a non-random convenient sample design in combination with a referral strategy among females experiencing menstrual problems or suffering from PCOS led to serious selection bias. In other words, this study does not allow for conclusions regarding the prevalence of PCOS among women with regular periods. Very few cases of regular periods have been included to introduce robustness through random selection. The estimated sample size was 132; however, the final sample size was 201 to improve data robustness. The presence of medical students among outpatients will affect the findings, as the former group has a higher socioeconomic background than the general public in Kerala. They also have greater health literacy, awareness of menstrual and hormonal disorders, and access to healthcare resources. The consideration to include them arose from the fact that most medical students had already spent more than 6 months in the area, having adapted to the local food and culture. However, the differences between them and the public might affect symptom perception, understanding of HRQoL experiences, health service utilization behavior, and perception related to psychological well-being, body image, and self-care activities. Thus, these respondents’ responses may have contributed to the observed variability in the latent variables and factor loadings, thereby affecting the overall factor structure.

Some measures, such as ‘Exercise’ (0.193) and ‘Hydration’ (0.233), were minor contributors to the HRQoL latent factors, as indicated by their low communalities in the factor analysis, and therefore warrant improvement in subsequent versions of the questionnaire. Finally, although the sample size was large enough to meet the minimum required by Cochrane’s formula, the targeted sampling approach warrants caution when drawing conclusions about a broader population. The survey included women aged 13–52 years from the Kozhikode district of Kerala, India, which strengthened the identified distinct factor structure. However, larger multicenter studies are needed to better correlate the wide range of PCOS symptoms. Similar studies across diverse populations in India could collectively help develop a comprehensive profile of Indian women with PCOS.

Conclusion

The present study offers initial findings on the existence of a multidimensional construct of HRQoL in patients with PCOS from South India, based on exploratory factor analysis. The modified scale has been shown to have acceptable reliability and can identify dimensions such as menstrual disorders, physical problems, psychological stressors and social relationships. Nevertheless, owing to the use of a single-center sample without full psychometric testing, the current results should be considered exploratory.
