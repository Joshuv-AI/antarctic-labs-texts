Conserved mRNA expression patterns of the Klotho family in canine mammary tumors and human breast cancer
Authors: Luciana Kluppel, Rebecca R Florke Gee, Tanja Plavec, Tanja Švara, Juan Sebastian Solano Gutierrez, Maria Camila Hoyos Sanchez, Vladimira Erjavec, Joseph Emmons, Heather Sheppard, Matej Tacer, Denis Štepihar, Alicia K Olivier, Mitja Gombač, Fernanda Rosa, Klementina Fon Tacer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanine mammary tumors (CMTs) are a naturally occurring comparative model for human breast cancer (HBC) due to their genetic, molecular, and epidemiological similarities. Our previous study in HBC suggested that αKlotho and βKlotho function as tumor suppressors, but γKlotho has an oncogenic effect. Strikingly, we found γKlotho to be overexpressed in more than 65% of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cases. However, the expression and role of Klothos in CMTs have not been investigated. In this study, we analyzed the mRNA expression of the Klotho family, hormone receptors, and FGFRs in 67 fresh-frozen and archived formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) CMTs stratified by histologic subtype. We validated our findings with a publicly available dataset comprising 157 CMTs. Compared with adjacent normal tissue, we found that αKlotho and βKlotho mRNA levels were reduced in CMTs, but γKlotho mRNA expression was increased, with higher levels observed in more aggressive tumors. Differential expression analysis comparing γKlotho-high and -low tumors identified 52 genes that were similarly dysregulated in human and canine datasets, with enrichment in androgen receptor (AR), VEGF, and ERK signaling pathways. The similar expression patterns of Klothos in HBC and CMTs suggest Klothos may have conserved roles in CMT pathogenesis. Further investigation is warranted to determine γKlotho’s potential as a prognostic biomarker or therapeutic target in humans and dogs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54898-y.Keywords: Comparative oncology, Lctl, Klotho, Canine mammary tumors, Human breast cancer, Triple-negative breast cancerSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Molecular biology, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women1. Despite significant advances in basic and clinical research, as well as improved diagnostic approaches that have enhanced survival and prognosis of patients with metastatic disease, it remains the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women1–5. Human breast cancer (HBC) is a highly heterogeneous disease, and its classification has evolved over the years6–8. The most common method of classifying HBC is based on the presence or absence of hormone receptors, specifically estrogen and progesterone, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)8–10. At the molecular level, breast cancer can be classified into several subtypes: luminal A, luminal B, normal-like, HER2-enriched, or triple-negative breast cancer11. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a highly aggressive and metastatic basal-like subtype characterized by the absence of hormone receptors and HER2 expression, accounts for 15–20% of all invasive breast cancer cases, often affecting younger women, and is associated with poor prognosis2,12–14.

As in humans, cancer is one of the leading causes of death among dogs in the developed world15. Similar to breast cancer in women, canine mammary tumors (CMTs) in intact female dogs are the most common malignant tumor, accounting for nearly half of all tumors diagnosed in this canine population worldwide16–21. CMTs are a promising model for comparative oncology due to their clinical, molecular, and epidemiological resemblance to HBC22,23. Similar to HBC, CMTs are highly heterogeneous with many histological subtypes, and about half of them are classified as malignant24. Molecular markers, such as receptors and gene expression signatures that are important predictors of tumor progression and outcome in HBC, are not routinely used in clinical decision-making for dogs. Surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment for CMTs, but their unpredictable recurrence and metastatic potential often lead to poor clinical outcomes25. Unlike the predominantly epithelial cell origin of HBC, CMTs display greater cellular diversity, arising as simple (epithelial), complex (epithelial and myoepithelial), or mixed (epithelial and mesenchymal) tumors26,27. Substantial heterogeneity among CMTs has led to the development of multiple classification approaches; however, standardized classification systems and consensus guidelines are needed to improve agreement in distinguishing benign from malignant lesions and in histological grading28. Furthermore, molecular subtypes of CMTs are not well defined, although attempts have been made to apply HBC classification schemes to CMTs. For example, CMTs may be classified based on molecular expression patterns into the same four subtypes as HBC: luminal A, luminal B, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+), and basal-like29,30. The basal-like subtype exhibits specific gene expression patterns that are similar to normal breast basal cells31.

CMTs and HBC, particularly TNBC, share features of invasiveness and poor prognosis32–35. Dogs are naturally exposed to environmental carcinogens similar to those affecting humans, and unspayed female dogs frequently develop spontaneous mammary tumors. These shared features position CMTs as a valuable comparative model for HBC. Thus, a better understanding of the molecular characteristics of CMTs, including mutational burden, transcriptomic profiles, and conserved signaling pathways, is important to support preclinical research36–43. Yet, the molecular basis of CMTs still remains poorly defined, underscoring the urgent need to clarify similarities and differences with HBC on a molecular level and to identify shared biomarkers for patient stratification and targeted therapies44,45. Within potential biomarkers, the Klotho protein family αKlotho (KL), βKlotho (KLB), and γKlotho (LCTL), initially characterized for its role in aging suppression, has emerged as a key player and potential regulator in oncogenesis46–49.

The α- and βKlotho proteins bind to and act with fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs), enabling biological functions of endocrine fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) to regulate metabolism and other physiological functions50–54. Specifically, FGF23 requires αKlotho to regulate phosphate and vitamin D homeostasis through the kidney-bone endocrine axis51,55–57. βKlotho acts as a co-receptor for FGF19 and FGF21, which control lipid, bile acid, and glucose metabolism56,58,59. γKlotho, which was discovered by homology with αKlotho and can also bind several FGFRs, is selectively expressed in the lens epithelium of the eye, brown adipose tissue, and kidneys56,58,60,61. α- and βKlotho are downregulated in HBC and function as tumor suppressors62,63. In contrast, γKlotho is upregulated in HBC, particularly in TNBC, as well as in gliomas, prostate cancer, and bladder cancer48,49,64,65. We previously showed that γKlotho is essential for cell survival, and its depletion leads to constitutive ERK activation, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis, potentially due to increased oxidative stress48. Previous studies have shown that lower αKlotho expression in canine tumors (i.e., liver and mammary) compared to normal tissues is associated with poor prognosis66,67. However, the roles of βKlotho and γKlotho have not yet been investigated in CMTs, highlighting a significant gap in current research. Given that γKlotho may act as an oncogene and diagnostic marker in HBC48,49,64,65, studying γKlotho in CMTs has translational potential for understanding the biology of aggressive mammary tumors in humans43,68. Furthermore, the functional heterogeneity and molecular signature of the Klotho family in HBC (i.e., α/βKlotho as tumor suppressors and γKlotho as an oncogene)48 can potentially be used as diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic markers.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether Klotho family members in CMTs exhibit expression profiles similar to those in HBC. In both fresh-frozen and archived CMT samples, we used RT-qPCR and RNA sequencing to assess the expression of Klothos, FGFRs, and hormone receptors relevant to HBC (Fig. 1). Then, we analyzed previously published datasets to validate our findings and compare γKlotho-associated transcripts in HBC and CMTs (Fig. 1). Our findings provide new insights into shared biomarkers between humans and canines, which is particularly important for enabling comparative clinical trials and advancing translational oncology48.

Methods

OncoPrint generation

To visualize gene expression across a set of human patient samples, we used the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (http://www.cbioportal.org/) and generated an OncoPrint (Fig. 2) using the Breast Invasive Carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) dataset (n = 1084, accessed on September 13, 2025). We queried a gene list encoding αKlotho (Kl), βKlotho (Klb), γKlotho (LCTL), estrogen receptor α (ERα, ESR1), progesterone receptor (PGR), androgen receptor (AR), HER2 (ERBB2), and fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, and FGFR4). Data are presented as z-scores, which represent mRNA levels in tumor samples relative to the log-transformed expression distribution of adjacent normal tissues within the cohort. Data are organized by tumor subtype: normal-like (N), luminal A, luminal B (these 2 marked by the expression of ERα), HER2-expressing, and basal-like, which includes the TNBC subtype (i.e., ER-, PR-, and HER2-).

Differential mRNA expression of Klothos, hormone receptors, and FGFRs in HBC samples. In this OncoPrint, expression z-scores represent mRNA levels in tumor samples relative to the log-transformed expression distribution of adjacent normal tissues within the cohort. OncoPrint was generated with the TCGA-BRCA dataset (n = 1084, accessed on September 13, 2025) using the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (http://www.cbioportal.org/).

Sample collection

As a part of routine interventions and surgical procedures at the Small Animal Clinic, University of Ljubljana, Slovenia, veterinarians—with informed consent from their owners—collected opportunistic samples of spontaneously occurring mammary tumors > 2 cm3 (n = 19) and normal mammary tissues (n = 8) from intact and spayed female dogs from the period between 2016 and 2023. The samples were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C. Additional information on the characteristics of these frozen samples is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples of CMTs (n = 31) and normal mammary tissues (n = 10) were retrospectively obtained from the archive of the Institute of Pathology, Wild Animals, Fish, and Bees at the University of Ljubljana, from the period between 2020 and 202321. Additional information on the characteristics of these FFPE samples is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Histopathological evaluation of tumor samples and archived FPPE samples

For histopathological evaluations, CMT sections were prepared from FFPE blocks at 4-µm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard laboratory protocols. Histopathologic examinations were performed independently by two experienced board-certified veterinary pathologists from the University of Ljubljana. H&E-stained slides were scanned in brightfield at 40× magnification using the Echo Revolution slide scanner (Discover Echo Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Digital images were captured and analyzed using the accompanying Echo software (Discover Echo Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and representative images were exported as TIFF files for figure preparation.

For the retrospective analysis from archived FFPE CMTs21, scrolls were prepared for RNA extraction and gene expression analysis. Samples selected for this study were diagnosed as mammary hyperplasia (n = 10), simple adenoma (n = 10), simple carcinoma grade I (low grade of malignancy; n = 10), and simple carcinoma grade III (high grade of malignancy; n = 11), according to the World Health Organization (WHO) histological classification for canine and feline mammary carcinomas17.

Cell culture

CMT-U27 (Cat #CRL-3456) and CF41.Mg (Cat #CRL-6232) CMT cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). CMT-U27 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 medium and CF41.Mg cells in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium, both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell counts were obtained with a hemocytometer or with a Countess II Automated Cell Counter using trypan blue staining. Cells were plated in 10 cm plates and, when ~ 80% confluent, harvested for RNA isolation.

Total RNA isolation from frozen and FFPE samples

Frozen normal and tumor tissues were immersed in liquid nitrogen and pulverized using a tissue pulverizer (Repligen, Cat #189470). Then, total RNA was extracted from these pulverized tissues and CMT cells (CMT-U27 and CF41.Mg) using Quick-RNA Miniprep Plus Kit (ZymoResearch, Cat #R1057) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. PureLink FFPE Total RNA isolation kit (Invitrogen, Cat #457015) was used to extract RNA from FFPE samples following the manufacturer’s instructions with some adaptations. 5–10 μm scrolls (3 per sample) were transferred into RNase-free microcentrifuge tubes and deparaffinized with xylene and ethanol washes, followed by Proteinase K digestion and heat treatment to reverse formaldehyde crosslinks. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). RNA integrity and quality were checked using the 4200 TapeStation with RNA ScreenTape Assay (Agilent Technologies).

cDNA synthesis and reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR were performed as previously described56,69. Briefly, RNA was treated with DNase I (Roche) in 4.5 mM MgCl2. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 3 µg of DNase-treated RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Life Technologies, Cat #4374967) in 20 µL final volume. The cDNA synthesis reaction was performed as follows: 25 °C for 10 min, 37 °C for 120 min, 85 °C for 5 min, and hold at 4 °C. The synthesized cDNA was then diluted in 120 µL RNase-free water before the qPCR analysis. qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 7 Pro Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher) in triplicate wells of MicroAmp Optical 384-well reaction plates with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix for qPCR (Applied Biosystems, Cat # A25742). Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0 (RPLP0) and 18 S ribosomal RNA genes were used as reference genes to normalize the mRNA expression of target genes. Normalized gene expression levels were obtained with the modified ΔΔCt method70 by dividing the averaged, efficiency-corrected values (efficiency was set to 100%) for mRNA expression by that for RPLP0 or 18 S and multiplying the quotient by 1 × 102 (when normalized to RPLP0) or 1 × 105 (when normalized to 18 S) for scaling purposes. Target gene sequences were obtained from the NCBI database, and primers 15–25 nucleotides in length were designed using Primer-BLAST with < 50% GC content and no more than four consecutive G or C bases, while avoiding primer-dimer formation and secondary structures. Primers were validated by examining the melting temperature curve and efficiency70. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and data analysis

All RNA-seq procedures, including the library preparation and data analysis, were conducted by Novogene Corporation Inc. (Sacramento, CA). Total RNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs). Messenger RNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. After fragmentation, the first strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by the second strand cDNA synthesis using either dUTP for directional library or dTTP for non-directional library71. The directional library was ready after end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, USER enzyme (New England Biolabs) digestion, amplification, and purification. The same steps, except without USER enzyme digestion, were followed to prepare the non-directional library. The library was checked with Qubit and real-time PCR for quantification and a bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. Quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced with a paired-end 150 bp read length on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw data were filtered with fastp software to remove reads with adapters and low-quality reads. The clean reads were then aligned to the canine reference genome (Ensembl, CanFam3.1) using HISAT2 (v2.0.5)72. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled by StringTie (v1.3.3b)73 in a reference-based approach. FeatureCounts (v1.5.0-p3)74 was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene. For graphing RNA-seq data, Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) of each gene was calculated based on the length of the gene and the read count mapped to it.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using RNA-seq data from our frozen CMT samples described in Supplementary Table S1 and from the TNBC samples in the TCGA-BRCA dataset, obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-BRCA, downloaded on September 20, 2023). First, transcripts per Million (TPM) values were calculated by (1) dividing the read counts by the length of each gene in kilobases to get reads per kilobase (RPK), (2) counting all the RPK values in a sample and dividing this number by 1 million to get the “per million” scaling factor, and (3) dividing the RPK values by the “per million” scaling factor to get TPM. Next, human and dog tumor samples were classified into high or low expression groups by fitting an exponential distribution to the TPM values of LCTL75. Based on this fitted distribution, 5,000 random samples were generated and clustered using the DBSCAN algorithm, with an epsilon of 0.1 and a minimum sample size of 5. The difference between the means of the two largest resulting clusters was used to determine a cutoff value of 0.9397 for the human dataset and 0.2676 for the canine dataset. Samples with an LCTL TPM value greater than or equal to the cutoff were assigned to the high expression group (n = 31 for human, n = 6 for dog); otherwise, they were classified as low expressers (n = 91 for human, n = 3 for dog).

Then, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2R package (v1.20.0) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high and low LCTL-expression groups76. This analysis was run separately for the HBC and CMT datasets. The resulting p-values were adjusted (padj) using the Benjamini-Hochberg approach for controlling the false discovery rate. The differential gene screening threshold was set as |log2(FoldChange)| ≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05, where a log2 fold change (FC) of 1 indicated a two-fold difference in expression between conditions, and a padj value of 0.05 represented a significance threshold after multiple testing correction. Using this threshold, the human TNBC dataset yielded 1461 DEGs, and the CMT dataset yielded 78 DEGs. Since the intersection of these two DEG lists initially yielded 3 genes, we set the threshold as |log2(FC)| ≥ 1 and p-value ≤ 0.05 to yield 52 genes that are differentially expressed in both humans and dogs. These 52 shared DEGs were then analyzed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen Inc., Redwood City, CA, USA). Human gene identifiers (ENSEMBL), along with their log2FC and adjusted p-value, were used as input for IPA. Gene identifiers were mapped to the Ingenuity Knowledge Base (Genes Only), and a Core Expression Analysis was performed using the log2FC values to calculate the directionality in the networks. Four significantly enriched molecular networks were identified and retained for downstream interpretation.

Plotting and statistical analyses

Unless indicated otherwise, GraphPad Prism Software (v10.4.0) was used for all plotting and statistical analyses. Since the RT-qPCR data were continuous, unpaired, and failed to meet the assumption of normality, statistical significance was determined by nonparametric methods, either Mann-Whitney test or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical comparisons for paired samples used either one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test or two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons test. For all analyses, p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant [P ≤ 0.05 (∗), P ≤ 0.01 (∗∗), P ≤ 0.001 (∗∗∗), P ≥ 0.05 (non-significant, ns)].

Results

mRNA expression of the Klotho family, hormone receptors, and FGFRs in HBC

To confirm our previous finding of distinct expression profiles of Klothos in human cancer48, we analyzed the TCGA Breast Invasive Carcinoma dataset and visualized the gene expression as an OncoPrint (Fig. 2). OncoPrint illustrates the mRNA expression changes across the patient cohort, organized by tumor subtype: normal-like (N), luminal A, luminal B, HER2-expressing, and basal-like, which includes TNBC (i.e., ER-, PR-, and HER2-). As seen in other studies48,49,64,65 and in Fig. 2, αKlotho and βKlotho are downregulated in cancer compared to normal samples, whereas γKlotho is upregulated, predominantly in the basal-TNBC group. More than half of the patients in the basal-TNBC group show increased expression of γKlotho, which is consistent with our original findings48. Furthermore, the pan-cancer box plots we generated on TNMplot.com (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/)77 show a similar expression pattern of Klothos in other cancers (Supplementary Figure S1). For example, renal cancers exhibit decreased αKlotho expression, pancreatic tumors have lower βKlotho expression, and higher γKlotho expression is evident in AML and ovarian and skin tumors (Supplementary Figure S1). As Klothos are involved in FGFR signaling, we also examined FGFR mRNA expression and found that FGFR3 and FGFR4 are upregulated in HBC, predominantly in hormone-dependent and HER2-dependent subtypes, respectively (Fig. 2).

mRNA expression of the Klotho family, hormone receptors, and FGFRs in frozen mammary tissue and canine mammary tumor samples

To determine whether the gene expression patterns of Klothos in CMTs are like those in HBC, we first performed a pilot experiment and analyzed mRNA expression of γKlotho in 4 normal and 10 tumor samples collected during routine surgeries at the Small Animal Clinic, University of Ljubljana (Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 3a). As determined by RT-qPCR, the expression of γKlotho was very low in normal mammary tissues but increased in several tumor samples (Fig. 3b). Although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.0699, unpaired Mann–Whitney test), the trend was convincing. Therefore, we expanded our cohort to 8 normal and 19 CMT samples and analyzed gene expression by RNA-seq (Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 3a). Given the heterogeneity of mammary tumors within the canine population, tumor histopathological diagnoses ranged from hyperplasia and adenomas to simple and complex carcinomas. The average age of the dogs was 9.8 years, consistent with the occurrence of these tumors in older animals. Dogs were of various breeds, and 80% were unspayed. Besides veterinary patient samples, we included two CMT cell lines to compare gene expression profiles with primary tumor samples. CF41.Mg and CMT-U27 are established CMT cell lines that differ in tumor origin, growth characteristics, and hormone receptor profiles. CF41.Mg is derived from a mixed/complex carcinoma and exhibits moderate proliferation, whereas CMT-U27 originates from a simple carcinoma and is generally more aggressive. Both CMT-U27 and CF41.Mg cell lines are generally reported to lack estrogen receptor (ER) expression and were therefore treated as ER-negative models in this study, consistent with their established use as basal-like canine mammary carcinoma models78. Based on our RNA-seq data, CMT-U27 is ESR1-negative, PGR-negative, ERBB2-positive, ERBB3-positive, and AR-negative, whereas CF41.Mg is ESR1-negative, PGR-positive, ERBB2-positive, ERBB3-negative, and AR-negative.

Consistent with the expression patterns observed in HBC, αKlotho and βKlotho mRNA were downregulated in tumor samples, and γKlotho was significantly upregulated in tumor samples and CMT cell lines (Figs. 3c–e). This expression pattern of decreased αKlotho and βKlotho with increased γKlotho in CMTs was also evident when we graphed tumor samples together with their matched normal samples (Supplementary Figure S2). Though both CMT cell lines expressed γKlotho, only CMT-U27 cells expressed αKlotho and βKlotho (Figs. 3c-e). The mRNA expression of breast cancer–associated hormone receptors [i.e., ERα, PR, HER2, and AR] did not significantly differ between normal and tumor tissues in this cohort, although there was a trend toward lower ERα and higher PR mRNA levels in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 3f). Reduced ERα protein levels were further confirmed by western blot analysis (Supplementary Figure S3). HER3 (ERBB3) was significantly downregulated in CMTs. Unlike other ErbB receptors, HER3 has impaired intrinsic kinase activity and depends on forming heterodimers, particularly with HER2, to activate downstream signaling. Contrary to the downregulation observed in CMTs, HER3 mRNA is overexpressed in 50–70% of breast cancers79. These CMT samples did not show any statistically significant differences in the expression of FGFRs (Fig. 3g). Though most FGFs were not significantly different, FGF10 and FGF16 were downregulated in tumors, and FGF11 was upregulated (Supplementary Figure S4).

mRNA expression of the Klotho family, hormone receptors, and FGFRs in FFPE canine samples stratified by histological subtype

To investigate the mRNA expression of the Klotho family during the progression of CMT, we retrospectively obtained FFPE samples of normal mammary tissues (n = 10) and CMTs (n = 31) stratified into the four histological subtypes according to WHO criteria (Supplementary Table S2). Representative images of FFPE mammary tissue sections from female dogs are shown in Fig. 4, including mammary hyperplasia (MH), simple adenoma (SMA), simple carcinoma grade I (SMC-lg), and simple carcinoma grade III (SMC-hg). After isolating RNA from FFPE scrolls, we performed RT-qPCR to measure the mRNA expression levels of the Klotho family members, hormone receptors, and FGFRs. Though the mRNA level of αKlotho and βKlotho did not differ significantly among the different histological subtypes, the expression of γKlotho was significantly higher in high-grade carcinoma compared to hyperplasia (Fig. 5a). This finding further suggests a potential role of γKlotho in more aggressive disease in dogs and its relevance to TNBC in humans48. Among the hormone receptors, only ERα expression differed among the tumor histological subtypes, with significantly lower expression in high-grade carcinoma (Fig. 5b). In line with this phenotype, AR expression trended up in higher-grade tumors, though not statistically significant (Fig. 5b). ERβ and PR expression did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 5b). Among FGFRs, FGFR2 expression trended up in high-grade carcinoma compared to hyperplasia, whereas FGFR1 expression did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 5b).

Validation of the Klotho mrna expression patterns using a public dataset of spontaneous CMTs

To validate the expression patterns observed in our CMT samples, we analyzed a publicly available RNA-seq dataset of spontaneous CMTs, which included 157 tumor samples and 64 matched, normal adjacent tissues (GSE119810)80. As in our CMT data, αKlotho and βKlotho mRNA levels were significantly lower in tumors compared to normal tissues, whereas γKlotho mRNA level was significantly higher in tumors (Fig. 6a). When the paired samples were grouped by histologic grade, the decrease in αKlotho and βKlotho expression in grade 2 tumors and the increase in γKlotho expression in grade 1 and grade 2 tumors were statistically significant (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Figure S5a). We also noted a statistically significant Spearman correlation for γKlotho with histologic grade (rho = 0.215, P = 0.00688). When the paired samples were grouped by histological diagnosis, the decrease in βKlotho expression and the increase in γKlotho expression were statistically significant in complex carcinomas (Figures S6A and S7A).

Besides the Klothos, we also analyzed mRNA expression of hormone receptors and FGFRs from the same CMT RNA-seq dataset. In tumors, ERα was downregulated, PR and HER2 were upregulated, and AR was not significantly different (Fig. 7a). None of the hormone receptors showed statistically significant differences in expression in the matched samples grouped by histological diagnosis (Supplementary Figures S6b and S7b), and only ERα and HER2 showed significant changes in expression when grouped by tumor grade (Supplementary Figures S5b and S8a). Among the FGFRs, FGFR1 and FGFR4 expression was significantly higher in tumors, with an increase in FGFR1 seen specifically in benign tumors when looking at the paired samples by grade (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Figure S5c and S8b). Stratifying the paired samples by histology did not reveal any significant changes in FGFR expression, except for a significant increase in FGFR3 expression in osteosarcoma (Supplementary Figures S6c and S7c). Pairwise gene correlations determined by Pearson coefficients were weak between most genes, except for moderate positive correlations among the hormone receptors and FGFR1 (ERα and PR: 0.60, PR and HER2: 0.52, ERα and HER2: 0.41, ERα and AR: 0.39, FGFR1 and PR: 0.39, FGFR1 and ERα: 0.38) (Fig. 7c).

Intersection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CMTs and HBC associated with high γKlotho mRNA expression

After establishing increased γKlotho mRNA expression in HBC and CMTs, we sought insight into genes and pathways potentially associated with high γKlotho mRNA expression in tumors in both species. Thus, we performed differential expression analyses of the RNA-seq datasets from our CMT cohort and TNBC samples from TCGA. First, the tumor samples were divided into high or low γKlotho expression groups by fitting an exponential distribution to the TPM values of LCTL (the gene symbol for γKlotho). Then, random samples were generated from the fitted distribution and grouped using DBSCAN clustering. The cutoff value separating high and low expression was determined as the difference between the average values of the two largest clusters (cutoff value = 0.9397 for the TNBC dataset and 0.2676 for the CMT dataset). After performing the differential expression analyses, the initial comparison of the DEGs from the human and canine datasets identified only 3 shared genes (Fig. 8a). Given the exploratory nature of this analysis, we used p-value ≤ 0.05 rather than padj ≤ 0.05, and the subsequent comparison of this deeper pool of DEGs led to the identification of 52 genes that were differentially expressed in both humans and dogs (Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table S4). Of these shared DEGs, 39 genes were upregulated or downregulated in both humans and dogs, while 13 were changed in opposite directions in humans and dogs (Fig. 8b).

To validate these DEGs in a dataset with a larger cohort of CMTs, we divided the publicly available dataset of CMTs (i.e., GSE11981080) into low or high γKlotho-expressing tumors (cutoff value = 0.2887) using the same approach and compared the expression of the 52 DEGs between these groups. Notably, 16 of the DEGs showed statistically significant differences in expression between the low and high γKlotho-expressing CMTs (Supplementary Figure S9). Several of the DEGs also showed statistically significant differences in expression between CMTs and their matched normal samples (Supplementary Figures S9 and S10). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) of these 52 shared DEGs yielded four predicted networks, suggesting deregulated pathways with AR, VEGF, and ERK signaling (Fig. 8c and Supplementary Table S5). However, further investigation into these pathways (Supplementary Figure S11) is needed to better understand the oncogenic function of γKlotho, elucidate its molecular role, characterize tumor heterogeneity, and assess the conservation of tumor-associated molecular mechanisms between CMTs and HBC.

Discussion

To determine the relevance of Klotho proteins in comparative oncology, this study aimed to determine whether their expression patterns are conserved between HBC and CMTs. Current research is increasingly focused on identifying reliable, conserved biomarkers to improve early detection, enable patient stratification, and support the development of targeted therapies in humans and animals. Our analysis of frozen and FFPE CMT samples showed that the Klotho family displays a similar mRNA expression pattern in humans and dogs, characterized by downregulation of αKlotho and βKlotho and overexpression of γKlotho in tumors compared to normal tissue (Figs. 1, 2 and 3)48. We further validated these findings in a publicly available dataset of CMT samples with matched normal tissues (Figs. 6 and 7, and Supplementary Figures S5-S7). This γKlotho expression pattern was originally observed in HBC, where it appears to mark the most aggressive TNBCs and confer oxidative stress and therapy resistance to cancer cells48. As in humans48, γKlotho was highly expressed in more aggressive subtypes of CMTs in dogs, specifically high-grade carcinomas that resemble TNBC (Fig. 5). High-grade mammary carcinomas also showed decreased expression of ERα (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figure S3), which is consistent with decreased ERα expression in HBC, a hallmark of TNBC12,48. In contrast to HBC, CMTs seem to lack a distinct HER2 subtype, suggesting species-specific molecular mechanisms80. Our results provide a basis for further studies to establish whether the conserved mRNA expression patterns of the Klothos have functional consequences and may serve as reliable biomarkers for patient stratification in both human and veterinary oncology.

Due to the heterogeneity of CMTs16,25,27,41,42,81–83 and the relatively small number of samples in our datasets, some differences did not reach statistical significance in the initial analyses (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Additional analyses of a larger CMT sample set revealed specific patterns in Klotho mRNA expression in benign and malignant mammary tumors (Figs. 6 and 7, and S5-S7). The variability in γKlotho mRNA expression levels detected in individual CMT samples (Figs. 2, S5, and S6) suggests a heterogeneous cellular population that reflects the complexity and cellular diversity of these tumors and parallels the variability seen in HBC23. The heterogeneity of γKlotho expression highlights the importance of considering sample diversity, as more aggressive histological subtypes consistently exhibited higher γKlotho mRNA levels. This heterogeneity also suggests that γKlotho may contribute to variability in tumor response to therapy and recurrence after surgery; however, additional studies are needed to assess its protein level and the functional consequences of gene expression activation. Furthermore, our data indicate that Klotho mRNA expression varies across tumor types (Figs. 3, S5, and S6), suggesting that the tumor microenvironment may modulate its functional role. For instance, VEGFR2, a key receptor driving angiogenesis, is expressed in a subset of γKlotho-positive tumors (Supplementary Figure S11), which may influence γKlotho function and contribute to context-dependent roles in tumor biology; however, further studies are needed to fully understand these interactions.

The increased expression of γKlotho mRNA in CMTs and HBC suggests a conserved oncogenic role. We previously reported that TNBC cells expressing γKlotho become dependent on it for survival and that γKlotho confers resistance to oxidative stress48,64. Given that γKlotho is expressed in other human cancers, including glioblastoma and liver cancer, its role may also extend beyond breast cancer in other species (Supplementary Figure S1)48,49,61,65. Indeed, suppression of γKlotho expression in a xenograft model of human castration-resistant prostate cancer led to smaller tumors and greater sensitivity to docetaxel; these findings provide support for γKlotho as a potential therapeutic target64. However, nothing is yet known about γKlotho expression in other canine cancers. Further studies are warranted to investigate the relationship between Klotho expression and treatment response, as well as to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of Klotho function in human and canine tumors.

Since Klothos were originally found to function as coreceptors for FGF signaling54, we included FGFRs in our expression analyses. Though our cohort did not show significant differences in expression of any FGFRs, FGFR1 was significantly upregulated in the larger set of CMT samples (Fig. 7)80. Amplification of FGFR1 is frequently observed in human TNBC, contributing to tumor progression by promoting key pathways involved in cell survival, proliferation, and migration84,85. This suggests a potentially conserved role of FGFR1 in both HBC and CMTs, warranting further investigation, particularly as FGFR1 inhibitors are currently in clinical trials for HBC. Moreover, in patients with ER-positive breast cancer who have progressed on aromatase inhibitors, the addition of an FGFR inhibitor provides clinical benefit irrespective of FGFR amplification, with the greatest benefit observed in tumors displaying gene expression patterns linked to FGF signaling86. Understanding the relationship between FGFR1 and γKlotho could provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms driving the progression of TNBC and other aggressive cancers. If this pathway proves to be significant, it may open new therapeutic avenues, including strategies aimed at inhibiting FGFR1 or modulating γKlotho expression. In addition, targeting the FGF19–FGFR1 or FGFR4–γKlotho axis could represent another promising therapeutic approach with comparable potential87.

To investigate conserved genes and pathways associated with high γ Klotho expression in human and canine tumors, we performed differential expression analyses on TNBC and CMT RNA-seq datasets stratified into high- and low-γKlotho groups (Fig. 8a; Supplementary Table S4). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis of the 52 shared DEGs identified three potentially deregulated pathways: AR, VEGF, and ERK signaling (Fig. 8c; Supplementary Table S5). These findings suggest further evaluation of γKlotho’s oncogenic role to determine the extent to which molecular mechanisms are conserved between CMTs and HBC. AR activation is well defined in prostate cancer and is increasingly implicated in breast cancer, where it promotes tumor growth in both estrogen-dependent and independent contexts. AR, like ER, is a steroid nuclear receptor widely expressed in breast cancer and a potential therapeutic target, although its prognostic relevance appears ER-dependent. In ER⁺ tumors, AR generally opposes ER signaling and may represent a therapy option in endocrine-resistant disease, whereas in ER⁻ tumors, AR can act as an oncogenic driver in a subset of TNBCs characterized by high AR expression, luminal-like features, and sensitivity to AR antagonists88,89. Our data raise the possibility that γKlotho-positive TNBC represents an AR-negative subtype, whereas the apparent upregulation of AR in γKlotho-positive CMTs may reflect species-specific differences or the need for refined molecular stratification within canine tumors (Fig. 8). Importantly, analysis of a publicly available CMT dataset (GSE119810) also revealed AR downregulation in γKlotho-high tumors, further supporting a more complex and context-dependent relationship between γKlotho and AR signaling (Figure S9)90.

In parallel, we suggest VEGF signaling as another potentially deregulated pathway. Given the central role of VEGF in TNBC-driven angiogenesis, our transcriptomic analyses suggest that VEGF pathway activity is reduced in γKlotho-positive tumors, which may imply altered angiogenic dependence and potential differences in responsiveness to anti-angiogenic therapies. However, heterogeneity in VEGFR2 protein expression among γKlotho-high CMT samples highlights substantial intra-tumoral variability and suggests that angiogenic signaling may be differentially engaged across tumor subsets (Supplementary Figure S11)91,92.

Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis indicated deregulation of the ERK pathway in γKlotho-positive tumors. While ERK signaling is frequently altered across cancers, TNBC typically lacks the sustained ERK activation required for sensitivity to RAS/RAF/ERK-targeted therapies93. In line with this, our previous work in TNBC cells demonstrated that γKlotho knockdown leads to increased ERK activation even in the absence of exogenous FGF ligand, suggesting that γKlotho may act as a modulator of ERK signaling upstream of FGF-dependent activation48. However, whether a similar regulatory mechanism operates in CMTs remains unclear. Our initial Western blot analyses did not reveal a consistent association between ERK activation and γKlotho expression in CMT samples, further underscoring tumor heterogeneity and the need for additional mechanistic studies (Supplementary Figure S11). Collectively, our findings suggest that γKlotho-positive tumors are characterized by a distinct and context-dependent signaling landscape across human and canine mammary tumors. This highlights γKlotho as a candidate regulator of tumor signaling plasticity and underscores the importance of further functional studies to define its mechanistic role in both human breast cancers and canine mammary tumors. The data presented in this manuscript provide an excellent resource for further analysis.

Study limitations

We acknowledge that our results are based on gene expression data; therefore, functional analyses to reveal the underlying mechanisms of Klothos in CMTs and HBC were not performed in the present study. Additional IHC validation for subtyping and treatment-relevant classification is warranted to enable more accurate comparisons with HBC subtypes. A limitation of this study is that clinical stage information was unavailable for the CMT samples in our cohort, which restricted our ability to assess the prognostic value of γKlotho beyond histologic grade. In addition, due to the nature of veterinary patient cohorts, survival data are limited, highlighting the need for more comprehensive comparative clinical repositories to enable robust translational analyses. The findings reported here may inform future studies aimed at validating potential prognostic markers and molecular therapeutic targets for both humans and canines, but functional studies are still needed. Our differential expression and downstream pathway analyses are more exploratory than definitive, as a more permissive statistical threshold was used to identify the 52 DEGs shared between TNBC and CMTs. Alongside the mRNA expression profiles of Klothos, hormone receptors, and FGFRs presented here, our data may provide a resource for further investigating the functions of the Klotho family in human and canine mammary tumors, ultimately helping to determine if and how they contribute to both universal and context-dependent mechanisms of breast cancer development.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated for the first time that the mRNA expression patterns of Klothos are conserved in human and canine breast tumors, suggesting their potential to serve as unique markers for stratifying human and canine patients with respect to prognosis and treatment. Our findings, together with the established roles of these genes in HBC, support further investigation of Klotho gene function in HBC and CMT. The variability observed in Klothos mRNA levels across different samples highlights the critical importance of accurately classifying cancer type, grade, and receptor expression profile in each patient, including dogs. We acknowledge that this study provides an exploratory transcriptomic analysis of Klotho family members in CMT and HBC. However, the absence of functional validation, limited clinical staging and survival data, and reliance on permissive statistical thresholds constrain definitive interpretation. These findings highlight the need for immunohistochemical validation and expanded comparative clinical datasets, while offering a foundation for future studies aimed at elucidating the mechanistic and prognostic roles of Klotho proteins in mammary tumor biology across species. Further studies are needed to correlate the presence of Klothos with their functional roles in tumor microenvironments, and longitudinal clinical outcomes are warranted to confirm the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of these candidate biomarkers in both veterinary and human oncology.
