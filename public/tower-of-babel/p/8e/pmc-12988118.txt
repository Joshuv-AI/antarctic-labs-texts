Multifunctionality of TIM-3: from immunological aging to pathological progression
Authors: Berta Segura-Collar, Lucía Mondejar-Ruescas, Carmen Jareño-Bonilla, Ricardo Gargini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe connection between aging and immune dysfunction is uncovering how immunoaging processes contribute to disease. Recent data from Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and adult and pediatric glioblastomas reveal a novel role for TIM3 in brain immune alteration. These findings highlight the role of TIM3 in promoting myeloid cell dysfunction toward an immunosuppressive profile. TIM3-blocking antibody treatments for central nervous system pathologies could be a new therapeutic window for healthcare systems.Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

The aging brain experiences profound immunological and vascular changes that increase its vulnerability to both neurodegenerative pathologies and neoplastic processes. One of the most distinctive features of this deterioration is the phenomenon known as inflammaging, a state of chronic low-level inflammation that contributes to synaptic loss, sustained glial activation and immune system dysfunction1. A central axis in these processes is the progressive disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a key event that allows the entry of peripheral immune cells into the central nervous system (CNS). Once inside, these cells can adopt maladaptive phenotypes that directly impact the onset and progression of multiple diseases. This phenomenon particularly affects the brain’s resident myeloid cells, which perform fundamental homeostatic functions. When their balance is disrupted, a generalized disorder characterized by an imbalance between proinflammatory and immunosuppressive mechanisms is generated.

In this context, the role of immunosuppressive pathways, such as those mediated by TIM-3, is becoming increasingly relevant. TIM-3, which is encoded by the HAVCR2 gene, is a co-inhibitory receptor expressed on multiple immune cell population, including T cells and myeloid cells. Its presence on IFN-γ–producing effector T cells, FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells, exhausted T cells, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes underscores its role in limiting T-cell activity. In parallel, its expression on myeloid population, including microglia, dendritic cells and macrophages derived from monocytes, reflects its functional relevance both peripheral tissues and the central nervous system. The interaction of TIM-3 with its known ligands such as galectin-9, CEACAM1, HMGB1 and phosphatidylserine, contributes to immunosuppression by promoting T-cell exhaustion and modulating the suppressive activity of myeloid cells (Fig. 1)2. Notably, its expression has been detected not only in aggressive brain tumors such as glioblastoma, but also in neurodegenerative diseases associated with aging, such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s, has gained relevance. This pathological convergence in immune modulation of the aging brain points to a common mechanism that transcends traditional clinical categories.

Understanding the mechanisms of immunosuppression in the CNS, both under physiological and pathological conditions, is increasingly recognized as a critical scientific and clinical need. Advancing the functional and molecular characterization of these immune circuits is essential—not only to explain treatment resistance and disease progression in neurological disorders, but also to inform the development of more precise and effective therapeutic strategies. This research will deepen our knowledge of brain immunobiology across the lifespan and pave the way for interventions tailored to the unique immunological landscape of the CNS.

Immunoregulatory role of TIM-3 in brain aging and neurodegeneration

In our recent study we characterized the transcriptional landscape of myeloid cells in aged human and murine brains, identifying an infiltrating population of TREM2⁺/TIM-3⁺ macrophages that begins to expand at age 57 years and older3. The presence of these cells correlates closely with the disruption of BBB, an event that allows the entry of circulating monocytes into CNS and once inside they adopt an immunosuppressive phenotype. The role of TIM-3 in myeloid cell dysfunction in antigen-specific immunity has been established. Thus, TIM-3 could control one of the primary processes in pathogenic antigen recognition by impairing STING pathway activation, which has a significant impact on pathological and therapeutic development4,5. Their infiltration in gliomas and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) suggests a common immunopathological axis in which age-related vascular dysfunction facilitates immune escape mechanisms.

Furthermore, a comparative analysis of the transcriptional profile between brains of healthy young and old humans revealed a significant set of differentially expressed genes, not only associated with classical aging processes, but also related to an immunological signature characteristic of TIM-3⁺ immunosuppressive myeloid cells (Fig. 2). This result suggests that the progressive acquisition of an immunosuppressive state regulated by TIM-3 could be part of a biological program characteristic of healthy brain aging, showing a reduction in the functional flexibility of the brain immune system to injurious or pathological stimulation.

Complementary data from the study by Kimura et al. 6 delve deeper into the role of TIM-3 in resident immune cells of the central nervous system, particularly within microglia. The authors demonstrated that in murine models of AD, targeted deletion of the Havcr2 gene-encoding TIM-3-in microglia leads to the development of an activated microglial phenotype, with a significant reduction in amyloid burden and improved cognitive performance. These results suggest that, far from being simply a homeostatic regulator, TIM-3 plays a functionally detrimental role in the context of AD by maintaining a state of immune quiescence that limits the ability of microglia to clear toxic deposits.

Although the role of TIM-3 in Parkinson’s disease (PD) is less studied than in Alzheimer’s disease, increased TIM-3 expression has been observed in peripheral immune cells and CNS lymphocytes in PD patients, linking to chronic neuroinflammation. The Galectin-9 (Gal-9)/TIM-3 pathway enhances microglial activation and dopaminergic neurodegeneration, whereas blocking TIM-3 improves neuronal protection and motor performance in animal models7. TIM-3 could also limit the clearance of neurotoxic aggregates such as α-synuclein, positioning it as a negative regulator of neuroprotective immunity and a potential therapeutic target in neurodegenerative disorders.

The role of TIM-3 in brain tumors: immunoevasion and tumor progression

TIM-3 has emerged as a key regulator of immunosuppression in the central nervous system tumor microenvironment. In contrast to other types of cancer, where TIM-3 is mainly found in T cells, in gliomas its expression is mainly concentrated in myeloid cells, especially in tumor-infiltrating macrophages. In adult gliomas patients with high TIM-3 expression in these myeloid cells had significantly lower survival, positioning it as a poor prognostic marker8.

However, its pathological relevance is even more notable in pediatric settings. A study by Ausejo-Mauleon et al. investigated its role in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG), a highly lethal childhood brain tumor with very little immune infiltration. The work demonstrated that TIM-3 is strongly expressed in both tumor cells and tumor-associated myeloid cells9. Remarkably, the blockade of TIM-3 with monoclonal antibodies reprogrammed the myeloid cell phenotype toward a proinflammatory profile, increasing CD8⁺ T-lymphocyte infiltration and activation. This therapeutic approach not only achieved sustained tumor control in murine models, but also induced long-lasting immunological memory, suggesting that even immunologically cold tumors could benefit from blocking this pathway. This finding of similar patterns of aging immunosuppression in pediatric brain tumors suggests that this phenomenon is not exclusively linked to aging but could represent a shared vulnerability of the brain microenvironment to oncological or degenerative processes.

Due to de antitumor effects observed with TIM-3 blocking antibodies in multiple preclinical tumor models, clinical trials have quickly been initiated, with initial results now emerging. TIM-3 has become a promising immunotherapy target due to its central role in the tumor microenvironment. Preclinical studies show that TIM-3 blockade promotes macrophage polarization toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype and enhances the function of dendritic and NK cells, improving antigen presentation2. Reactivation of exhausted T cells occurs primarily through combined TIM-3 and PD1-blockade, restoring proliferation and cytokine production in T cells10. Clinically, trial such as NCT03961971 (recurrent glioblastoma), NCT03489343 (lymphomas), NCT03680508 (liver cancer) and NCT06125652 (acute myeloid leukemia) are evaluating anti TIM-3 alone or in combination with anti-PD1. Despite its promise, challenges remain, including tumor heterogeneity and lack of predictive biomarkers, highlighting the importance of understanding the role of TIM-3 to optimize its clinical application.

Intrinsic expression of TIM-3 in tumor cells: functional and prognostic implications

There is increasing evidence that TIM-3 is not only expressed in immune cells, but also in the tumor cells themselves in different types of cancer, such as GBM and metastatic breast cancer, where it plays active roles in tumor progression. In GBM, TIM-3 regulates malignant behaviors of tumor cells and promotes an immunosuppressive microenvironment by activating TIM-3/IL-6 signaling, which favors macrophage polarization towards a pro-tumor phenotype11. Recently, a published study in breast cancer metastasis models has revealed that TIM-3 is highly expressed in early stages and facilitates tumor cell survival and immune escape through the β-catenin/IL-1β pathway, being associated with poor prognosis12.

The integration of functional studies with transcriptomic analyses from large clinical cohorts allows us to consolidate the clinical value of TIM-3 as a marker in oncology. From the public database including advanced and metastatic tumors13, it is observed that a high expression of TIM3 correlates consistently with a lower overall survival (Fig. 3a), positioning it as a negative prognostic marker in different types of cancer. In addition, the study of genes co-expressed with TIM-3 in this set of tumors reveals a significant enrichment in key pathways for several immune response process, such as antigenic presentation, cytokines and chemokines, inflammatory response and negative regulation of lymphocyte activation (Fig. 3b). Nevertheless, prolonged activation of TIM-3 is associated with chronic immune dysfunction, marked by T-cell exhaustion, which promotes anti-tumor immune evasion and, consequently, a worse clinical prognosis.

Conclusions

Taken together, the accumulating evidence highlights the central role of TIM-3 as a key regulator of tumor immune evasion, regardless of whether its expression occurs in tumor cells or in components of the immune microenvironment, such as macrophages or T lymphocytes. Its involvement in the suppression of effective immune responses positions it as a therapeutic target of great interest in multiple types of cancer, with the potential to improve the efficacy of current immunotherapies.

However, the question remains as to whether TIM-3 expression in tissues outside the brain context is also directly driven by myeloid cell aging. If this link is confirmed, TIM-3 could represent not only a marker of tumor immunosuppression, but also a common axis between immune aging and immunoevasion in multiple organs. This possibility opens new lines of research on the systemic impact of immunoaging on tumor progression and reinforces the interest in targeting therapies against TIM-3 as a transversal strategy in oncology and aging.
