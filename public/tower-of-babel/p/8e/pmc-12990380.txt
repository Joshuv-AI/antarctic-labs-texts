Enzyme-specific modulation of rice peptide structure and alginate hydrogel functionality for food applications
Authors: Xishuang Ren, Ning Ma, Huaqing Du, Siyu Li, Heng Wang, Hao Long, Jie Zhou, Xianghao Zhu, Guangjie Xie, Xiaosong Ye, Yixuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated how protease-specific hydrolysis modulates rice protein hydrolysate (RPH) structure and properties to tailor rice peptide‑sodium alginate composite hydrogels. Using Alcalase, Trypsin, and Flavourzyme (E/S: 3000–10,000 U/g), Flavourzyme yielded the highest hydrolysis (43.87%) and antioxidant activity (82.1%), with minimal particle size (352.0 nm). Structurally, Alcalase most reduced ordered conformations, while Flavourzyme induced minimal changes. Composite gels prepared via glucono-delta-lactone (GDL)-induced gelation exhibited enzyme-dependent characteristics: Flavourzyme-RPH enhanced water holding capacity (97.2%) and storage modulus; Trypsin-RPH improved hardness and strength (1.8–2.3× control) via Ca2+ crosslinking; Alcalase-RPH showed highest resilience (0.47) but reduced strength, likely due to low-MW peptides. Microstructure confirmed RPH incorporation promoted denser alginate networks. Protease selection critically determines hydrogel functionality, enabling tailored plant protein-polysaccharide composites for food and delivery applications.

Introduction

Gelation plays a crucial role in food science due to its critical influence on the textural, sensory, and functional attributes of food products. Plant-derived protein gels, in particular, significantly affect food matrix stability, nutrient delivery, and organoleptic properties (Ye et al., 2023). However, these systems exhibit inherent limitations including susceptibility to environmental stressors (e.g., ionic strength, pH fluctuations, thermal processing, and antinutritional factors) as well as suboptimal solubility, gelation capacity, and biocompatibility. In contrast, polysaccharide-based gels present distinct advantages as natural edible matrices, offering enhanced water retention, viscoelasticity, biodegradability, and cost-effectiveness while maintaining high biocompatibility (Guo et al., 2024). Their unique physicochemical characteristics, including non-antigenicity and metal-chelating capacity, further expand their utility in biomedical applications (M. Khalesi et al., 2024).

The synergistic combination of proteins and polysaccharides has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome individual component limitations. Binary polymer systems exhibit distinct phase behaviors compared to single-component solutions, enabling the creation of hybrid networks with tailored functional properties (Singh, 2025). Such composites not only improve mechanical stability and processing efficiency but also enhance nutritional profiles, serving as effective platforms for dietary protein supplementation (van den Berg et al., 2015).

Among polysaccharides, sodium alginate—a polyanionic biopolymer—has gained particular attention for its rapid gelation under mild conditions, a critical advantage for preserving thermolabile proteins and bioactive compounds (Gombotz & Wee, 2012; Pawar & Edgar, 2012). Its gelation mechanism involves Ca2+-mediated ionotropic crosslinking via G-block (α-L-guluronic acid) dimerization, forming thermally stable “egg-box” structures with notable elasticity (Hong & Chin, 2010). Alternative acid-induced gelation using glucono-delta-lactone (GDL) enables controlled pH reduction and Ca2+ release from insoluble salts like calcium carbonate, yielding homogeneous networks with enhanced micromechanical properties (H.-B. Chen et al., 2012).

Despite these advances, fundamental gaps persist in understanding peptide-polysaccharide composite systems, particularly regarding structure-function relationships influenced by enzymatic preparation parameters. Rice peptides, derived through controlled enzymatic hydrolysis, represent underexplored building blocks for such hybrid gels. Current research has not systematically addressed how enzymatic conditions (e.g., protease specificity, hydrolysis duration, and degree of cleavage) modulate peptide conformation and subsequent interactions in composite matrices. This study therefore investigates the structural and functional evolution of rice peptides under varied enzymatic protocols, and their synergistic effects on sodium alginate-based composite gel properties. In this study, alcalase, trypsin, and flavourzyme were selected for their well-documented efficacy in hydrolyzing rice protein and their complementary cleavage specificities, a combination that generates hydrolysates with a diverse peptide profile. Based on the above, we aim to establish a theoretical framework for designing functional peptide-polysaccharide hybrids with applications in nutraceutical delivery and novel food development in the future.

Materials and methods

Materials

Rice protein isolate (≥85% protein content) was purchased from Jnpro Co., Ltd. (Wuxi, China). Alcalase® (200,000 U/g, EC 3.4.21.62) and trypsin (250,000 U/g, EC 3.4.21.4) were sourced from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Flavourzyme® (100,000 U/g, EC 3.4.11.1), sodium alginate and nano-sized calcium carbonate were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Glucono-δ-lactone (GDL, ≥99%) was supplied by Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Preparation of enzymatic hydrolysate of rice protein

A rice protein isolate was reconstituted in deionized water to prepare ten protein solutions (10% w/w, 100 mL each; designated as Groups 1–10). All solutions were homogenized using a SCIENTZ-IID ultrasonic homogenizer (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., China) under controlled thermal conditions. Specifically, samples in 250 mL beakers were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C via an ice-water bath during ultrasonication (400 W amplitude, 20 kHz frequency) for 15 min.

Three enzyme-to-substrate (E/S) ratios (10,000, 6000, and 3000 U/g protein) were evaluated per enzyme group. Reactions proceeded for 4 h with continuous agitation (150 rpm), while the control group (Group 10) remained untreated as the native protein control. To ensure consistency, the control group was incubated under identical conditions (temperature, agitation, and time) as enzyme-treated samples. Enzymatic activity was terminated by thermal inactivation under 100 °C for 10 min in water bath.

Post-hydrolysis samples were immediately cooled to 4 °C, centrifuged (10,000g, 20 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were collected followed by lyophilization for 48 h. The freeze-dried samples were stored desiccated at −20 °C for further analysis.

Hydrogel fabrication

Composite hydrogels were synthesized using a pH-triggered ionotropic gelation method. Briefly, a sodium alginate solution (1.5% w/v, 15 mL) was formulated and adjusted to pH 11.0 using 0.5 M NaOH. Nano-sized calcium carbonate (0.03 g; 0.2% w/v) and lyophilized protein hydrolysates were uniformly dispersed into the solution by vortex mixing (600 rpm, 5 min) successively. Gelation was initiated by adding glucono-δ-lactone (GDL, 0.8% w/v) to the homogeneous mixture, followed by stirring (400 rpm, 1.5 min) to ensure even acid distribution. The system was allowed to undergo controlled acidification at 25 °C for 20 min, during which pH was monitored at 1-min intervals (Final pH: 7.0–7.4). A blank hydrogel was prepared identically by removing peptides. All hydrogel matrices were equilibrated at 4 °C for 2 h prior to further analysis.

SDS-PAGE analysis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed to characterize the molecular weight distribution of hydrolysates following a previous method (D. Chen & Campanella, 2022). Samples were diluted to 20 mg/mL protein with 50 mM Tris-Gly buffer, mixed with 4× Laemmli loading buffer containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol, heated in a boiling water bath for 5 min, and cooled to room temperature. Then, 7.5 μL of each sample was loaded onto 12% polyacrylamide gels. Electrophoresis was run at 100 V for 70 min. Gels were fixed (methanol:acetic acid:H₂O, 5:1:4), washed with water, stained with Bio-safe Coomassie Blue G250 (30 min), destained with 10% acetic acid and 10% methanol (1:1, v/v), and imaged using a GelDoc Go Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

Particle size analysis

The particle size was determined according to previous research with slight modifications (Y.-Y. Wang et al., 2021). Protein particle size distribution was characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 system (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Samples were prepared by dissolving lyophilized hydrolysates in 0.2 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) to a final concentration of 15 mg/mL. Triplicate measurements were performed for each sample at ambient temperature.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

Secondary structure analysis was performed using far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy adapted from the previous study (Cheng et al., 2024)Lyophilized samples (Groups 1–10) were reconstituted in 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL(w/v). Measurements were conducted in a quartz cuvette with 1 mm path length by a spectropolarimeter (Jasco Corporation, Japan). Spectra were recorded under the following conditions: scanning range from 190 nm to 260 nm, scanning speed of 200 nm/min, bandwidth of 1.0 nm, sensitivity of 20 mdeg, and temperature maintained at 25 °C. A blank control (10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0) was subtracted from all measurements. Results represent mean ± SD from three independent sample preparations.

Hydrogel properties

Rheological properties

The gelation properties of peptide-based hydrogels was determined using a rheometer (AR-3000, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) according to the previous method (Du et al., 2023) with modifications. The hydrogel precursor solution was homogeneously mixed with glucono-δ-lactone (GDL) and stirred for 90 s. The reacting mixture was promptly loaded onto the rheometer plate maintained at 25 °C, with a constant strain of 0.01 and a frequency of 1 Hz, using a parallel plate (40 mm) geometry. These parameters allowed measurements within the linear viscoelastic frequency region. Mineral oil was used to prevent the evaporation of the samples. The gelation point was defined as the point when the elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G′′) cross over (tan δ, where δ is the phase angle =1).

A frequency-dependent characterization of gels was performed in the frequency range of 0.01–100 Hz at a constant strain of 1% and temperature of 25 °C. The changes of G′ and G′′ during gelation were determined.

Gel morphologies

Gels prepared with different enzymatic hydrolysates were observed for their morphology. The freeze-dried hydrogel sample was pasted on an aluminum plate with a double-sided carbon tape and coated with gold. Then the samples were observed using a SU8600 scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (S. Zhang et al., 2024).

Texture analysis

Cylindrical hydrogel samples (20 mm diameter × 15 mm height) were prepared [using PTFE molds and equilibrated at ambient temperature for 1 h prior to testing. The textural properties of hydrogels were characterized using a TA.XTC-18 texture analyzer (BosinTech Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) equipped with a TA/0.5 probe at a test speed of 5 mm s−1 and compressed twice up to 50% of the original height. The values of hardness, resilience, gel strength, and breaking force were calculated as described in the research of Ding et al. (2019).

Water holding capacity

The water holding capacity (WHC) was evaluated according to the centrifugal drainage method. A 10 g aliquot of hydrogel was weighed into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. at 25 °C.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in three independent experimental replicates. The results were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's test was employed to evaluate significant differences between groups, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Data visualization was generated using OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Hydrolysis efficiency of ultrasonicated rice protein by three enzymatic preparations

The degree of hydrolysis (DH) of ultrasonicated rice protein varied significantly among the three enzymatic preparations (flavourzyme, alcalase, and trypsin) at different enzyme-to-substrate (E/S) ratios (Fig. 1). Notably, flavourzyme achieved the highest DH value of 43.87% at an E/S ratio of 10,000 U/g, followed by alcalase (28.00%) and trypsin (4.25%). These differences in hydrolysis efficiency may be attributed to the distinct cleavage specificities of the enzymes. Trypsin, a highly specific serine protease, exclusively targets peptide bonds at the C-terminal of lysine (Lys) and arginine (Arg) residues, which likely limited its accessibility to cleavage sites in the rice protein substrate. In contrast, both flavourzyme and alcalase exhibited broader substrate specificity (Y. The superior performance of flavourzyme could be explained by two synergistic mechanisms. First, its exopeptidase activity facilitates the cleavage of terminal peptide bonds, effectively reducing steric hindrance and enhancing subsequent proteolysis when used in combination with other enzymes (Knezevic-Jugovic et al., 2023). Second, flavourzyme demonstrates inherently higher proteolytic activity compared to alcalase and trypsin under the tested conditions, as evidenced by its significantly elevated DH values across all E/S ratios. This observation aligns with previous reports on the synergistic effects of flavourzyme in multi-enzyme hydrolysis systems (Knezevic-Jugovic et al., 2023), where its terminal cleavage capability promotes deeper substrate degradation.

Molecular weight distribution of enzymatic hydrolysates

SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 2) revealed distinct protein bands for untreated rice protein, confirming an intact molecular weight (MW) distribution. Notably, the RPI sample exhibited intense staining at the stacking/resolving gel interface (Fig. 2), indicative of high-molecular-weight aggregates. This retention was a characteristic feature of native rice proteins, particularly the glutelin fraction, which formed large, insoluble supramolecular complexes stabilized by intermolecular disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions (L. Wang et al., 2023). These aggregates exceeded the pore size of the polyacrylamide matrix, preventing migration into the resolving gel. In contrast, these aggregate bands were absent in all hydrolysates, indicating that enzymatic hydrolysis effectively disrupted the disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions maintaining the aggregates, yielding smaller, soluble peptides.Fig. 2SDS-PAGE pattern of RPI and its hydrolysates. Enzymatic hydrolysis altered the banding patterns, with the extent of modification varying among treatments. Hydrolysates generated by alcalase and flavourzyme showed predominant molecular weight fragments below 17 kDa, consistent with proteolytic cleavage patterns reported for plant proteins in prior studies (Zaky et al., 2019). In contrast, trypsin-treated hydrolysates retained higher MW fragments (17–25 kDa), indicative of comparatively limited proteolytic activity. This observation aligns with previous findings that highlight trypsin's reduced efficacy in hydrolyzing rice protein due to its strict substrate specificity for lysine and arginine residues (Ngamsuk et al., 2020). Notably, the absence of a distinct molecular weight reduction trend across the nine hydrolysis conditions may be attributed to the formation of short peptides (<10 kDa) during prolonged hydrolysis, which fall below the resolution threshold of conventional SDS-PAGE (Muley et al., 2021). This phenomenon has been documented in extensively hydrolyzed plant protein systems, where small peptide fragments fail to resolve as discrete bands. The disparity in MW profiles further corroborates the DH data (Fig. 1), collectively underscoring flavourzyme and alcalase as more effective candidates for rice protein modification under the tested conditions.

Particle size reduction of enzymatically hydrolyzed rice protein

The hydrodynamic diameter of ultrasonicated rice protein decreased significantly upon enzymatic hydrolysis, with distinct variations observed among treatments (Fig. 3). Untreated protein exhibited an average particle size of 785.0 ± 10.0 nm, whereas hydrolysates generated at an E/S ratio of 10,000 U/g demonstrated marked reductions: 382.1 ± 9.9 nm (alcalase), 466.8 ± 7.6 nm (trypsin), and 352.0 ± 4.3 nm (flavourzyme). Notably, flavourzyme produced the smallest particle size, which correlated directly with its superior degree of hydrolysis (DH, 43.87%, Fig. 1). The limited hydrolysis efficiency of trypsin, as evidenced by its low DH (4.25%) and higher residual molecular weight fragments (Fig. 2), corresponded to its reduced capacity to decrease particle size. This mechanistic consistency aligns with prior studies demonstrating that proteases with restricted substrate specificity (e.g., trypsin) yield hydrolysates with broader particle distributions due to incomplete disaggregation of protein networks (Y. Wang et al., 2025). Conversely, flavourzyme's exopeptidase activity and broad cleavage specificity likely promoted the generation of low-molecular-weight peptides, thereby enhancing colloidal destabilization and minimizing particle size. These findings collectively highlight enzymatic specificity as a critical determinant of both hydrolysis efficiency and nanostructural modulation in rice protein systems.

Antioxidant capacity of enzymatic hydrolysates

The DPPH radical scavenging capacity, a key indicator of antioxidant activity through hydrogen atom donation mechanisms, exhibited significant variations among enzymatic hydrolysates (Fig. 4). Flavourzyme-treated hydrolysates demonstrated superior antioxidant performance, achieving 69.24% scavenging activity at E/S = 3000 U/g, which exceeded values for alcalase (31.48%) and trypsin (21.77%) by 2.2- and 3.2-fold, respectively. The exceptional antioxidant capacity of flavourzyme hydrolysates may be attributed to its dual proteolytic characteristics: Exopeptidase-driven peptide liberation: Sequential cleavage of terminal residues generates short-chain peptides (<3 kDa) rich in exposed aromatic (Tyr, Trp) and sulfur-containing (Cys, Met) amino acids, which exhibit enhanced radical quenching via electron transfer pathway(Chawner et al., 2012). Synergistic hydrolysis patterns: Combined endo- and exo-peptidase activities in flavourzyme promote the release of bioactive sequences with clustered antioxidant motifs (e.g., His-Pro-His, Glu-Cys-Gly), as identified in rice protein hydrolysates by Duan et al. (2024).

Notably, the concentration-dependent enhancement of DPPH scavenging (R2 = 0.93, Fig. 3) correlates strongly with both degree of hydrolysis (DH, Fig. 1) and abundance of low-MW peptides (<10 kDa, Fig. 2), suggesting that proteolytic generation of bioactive fragments governs antioxidant efficacy. This aligns with established quantitative structure-activity relationships (QSAR) in plant protein hydrolysates, where peptide chain length and residue positioning critically determine redox potential (Nongonierma & FitzGerald, 2016).

Rheological evolution of enzymatic hydrolysate-alginate composite gels

The elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G′′) are key parameters for evaluating viscoelasticity and stability. As shown in Fig. 6, all samples exhibited a notable increase in G′, which then remained relatively stable. Throughout the frequency sweep, G′ was consistently dominant over G′′ (Fig. 6a, e, i), confirming an elastically dominant, stable three-dimensional network. The greater frequency dependence of G′′, however, indicates an increased contribution of viscous dissipation at higher deformation rates. The viscoelastic properties of composite gels, characterized by time-dependent storage modulus (G′) evolution (Fig. 6 c, g, k), revealed distinct network maturation kinetics across enzymatic treatments. All formulations exhibited progressive G′ enhancement, indicative of continuous cross-linking and structural consolidation. Notably, hydrogels incorporating flavourzyme hydrolysates (E/S = 10,000 U/g) achieved the highest terminal G′, suggesting the formation of a densely interconnected network through synergistic peptide-alginate interactions. Comparatively, lower enzyme loadings (E/S = 3000 U/g) generated weaker gels with delayed G′ growth kinetics, likely due to insufficient bioactive peptides for effective matrix reinforcement. Frequency sweep analysis (Fig. 6 a, e, i) further corroborated the structural hierarchy, demonstrating superior mechanical stability (lower frequency dependence of G′) in high E/S ratio (10,000 U/g) systems. Contrasting trends emerged in elasticity loss factor (tan δ) dynamics (Fig. 6 d, h, l). While all systems showed declining tan δ values over time—reflecting a transition from viscous to elastic dominance—alcalase-derived hydrolysates (E/S = 10,000 U/g) achieved the lowest final tan δ, indicative of superior elastic network reorganization. Paradoxically, flavourzyme formulations exhibited higher tan δ (0.18 ± 0.02) despite their elevated G′, suggesting that its tightly cross-linked architecture may incorporate topological defects (e.g., heterogeneous chain packing) that enhance energy dissipation (Masia et al., 2023). Its distinct rheological behavior—high storage modulus (G′) but elevated tan δ (Fig. 6) probably correlates with its limited structural perturbation (retained α-helix/β-sheet content). This indicates that partial retention of native secondary structures may restrict molecular flexibility, yielding rigid yet heterogeneous networks prone to localized stress concentration. The structural-function dichotomy observed across enzymatic treatments underscores that optimal gel performance requires balancing peptide disorder (to enable chain mobility) and residual ordered motifs (to maintain mechanical coherence), a design principle previously demonstrated in hybrid protein-polysaccharide gels (H. Microstructure of rice protein-alginate hydrogels

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to characterize the microstructure of the composite gels. The SEM images in Fig. 7 provided direct visual evidence of microstructural changes induced by the hydrolysates. A distinct contrast could be observed between the control alginate gel (without hydrolysates), which displayed a loose and fragile architecture, and the composite gels containing hydrolysates (Fig. 7A–C), which all exhibited a markedly denser network. This microstructural densification strongly indicated the formation of additional cross-links. Moreover, the degree of structural density was influenced by both the enzyme type and dosage. For example, among the hydrolysate-modified gels, sample B showed a looser network compared to samples A and C. More notably, within the Flavourzyme group, increasing the E/S ratio from 3000 U/g to 10,000 U/g (Fig. 7C, E, F) resulted in a progressively more compact and cross-linked gel morphology. The interpretation of enhanced cross-linking was further supported by rheological analyses, which independently demonstrated improved gel strength and stability in hydrolysate-containing samples (see Section 3.6). Together, the SEM and rheology data provide multi-faceted evidence that the incorporation of hydrolysates promotes the formation of a more robust and cross-linked network. Enzymatic regulation of alginate gel textural properties

The textural parameters of calcium alginate gels, including hardness, resilience, gel strength, and breaking force, were systematically modulated by enzymatic hydrolysis of rice protein (Table 2). Notably, hydrolysates generated by trypsin and flavourzyme at E/S ratios of 3000–10,000 U/g produced gels with significantly elevated hardness (1.8–2.3-fold increase versus control), whereas alcalase-treated counterparts showed negligible improvement. This divergence in mechanical reinforcement was further corroborated by parallel enhancements in gel strength and breaking force for trypsin and flavourzyme hydrolysates, contrasting with alcalase's limited performance (<5% increase).

The superior textural reinforcement by trypsin and flavourzyme-derived hydrolysates may originate from their distinct peptide profiles. Relatively larger peptide fragments (17–25 kDa, Fig. 2) in hydrolytes generated through partial hydrolysis likely served as structural reinforcers by intercalating within the alginate network, forming hybrid cross-linking nodes through Ca2+-mediated bridging and electrostatic interactions (Q. Chen et al., 2020). This hypothesis aligns with previous studies demonstrating that moderately hydrolyzed plant proteins enhance composite gel rigidity by optimizing filler-matrix interfacial adhesion (Lafarga et al., 2020). Conversely, alcalase's extensive proteolysis (DH 28.00%, Fig. 1) potentially generated excessive low-molecular-weight peptides (<10 kDa), which may disrupt alginate chain entanglement through steric interference (H. Zhang et al., 2021).

Resilience exhibited a concentration-dependent enhancement across all treatments, reaching maximum values of 0.47 for alcalase at E/S = 10,000 U/g. This phenomenon may be attributed to enzymatic exposure of hydrophobic domains and polar side chains (-OH, -NH2), which amplify non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic forces) within the gel network. The apparent contradiction between alcalase's high resilience and low mechanical strength suggests a decoupling of energy dissipation mechanisms (governed by non-covalent bonds) from structural rigidity (dependent on covalent cross-links) in these composite systems (H. Khalesi et al., 2020).

Enzymatic modulation of water-holding capacity in alginate gels

The water-holding capacity (WHC) of calcium alginate gels, reflecting the structural integrity of their three-dimensional network to entrap water molecules, exhibited a concentration-dependent improvement with increasing enzyme-to-substrate (E/S) ratios (Fig. 5). At the highest E/S ratio tested (10,000 U/g), WHC values reached 97.0% (alcalase), 96.93% (trypsin), and 97.2% (flavourzyme), corresponding to incremental enhancements of 1.03%, 0.96%, and 1.23%, respectively, compared to the untreated control (95.97%). While the absolute differences among enzymatic treatments were marginal, flavourzyme demonstrated a consistent superiority across E/S gradients, paralleling its previously observed higher hydrolysis efficiency (Fig. 1) and finer particle size distribution (Fig. 4).

This enhancement in WHC may be attributed to the incorporation of low-molecular-weight (LMW) peptides generated during proteolysis into the alginate gel matrix. Specifically, LMW peptides (<20 kDa, as evidenced by SDS-PAGE in Fig. 2) can act as interfacial modifiers, forming supplementary cross-linking junctions through electrostatic interactions with calcium ions and hydrogen bonding with alginate chains (Rosiak et al., 2021). Concurrently, the exposed hydrophilic amino acid residues (e.g., -COOH, -NH2) on these peptides enhance water-binding via polar interactions, thereby stabilizing the gel network against syneresis (Panja et al., 2022). The marginally higher WHC of flavourzyme-derived hydrolysates likely stems from their superior abundance of LMW peptides, consistent with their elevated DH values and reduced particle sizes. These findings aligned with established structure-function relationships in protein-polysaccharide composite gels, where enzymatic generation of bioactive peptides amplifies both textural and hydration properties (Y. Xu et al., 2023).

Conclusion

This study systematically established enzyme-specific design principles for rice protein-alginate hydrogels via controlled enzymatic hydrolysis. While flavourzyme at 10,000 U/g produced hydrogels with maximal water retention and elastic modulus via reinforcing nanopeptides (352.0 nm) enabling synergistic electrostatic/hydrogen bonding, alcalase generated superior elasticity but compromised strength due to disruptive low-MW peptides from excessive proteolysis. This contrast reveals a critical enzyme specificity–peptide size trade-off governing hydrogel functionality. Elevated E/S ratios concurrently enhanced mechanical properties and antioxidant activity, demonstrating hydrolysis' dual structural and bioactive enhancement. These findings offer a protease-oriented strategy for customizing plant protein-polysaccharide hydrogels in functional foods and delivery systems. Specifically, flavourzyme-based gels are ideal for nutrient delivery applications owing to their high water-holding capacity, trypsin-reinforced gels serve as texture modifiers in structured foods, and alcalase-derived gels suit soft formulations such as sauces or fillings due to their high resilience. Such functional versatility underscores the practical relevance of enzyme-specific hydrogel design in advancing food innovation. Future investigations should prioritize the integration of multi-enzyme systems with advanced analytical techniques, such as in situ rheo-microscopy, to clarify the multiscale gelation behavior and structural characteristics of rice peptide-based biomaterials. Such insights will help establish structure–function relationships and broaden the scope of their applications.
