Fresh Talus Allograft Preparation for the Treatment of a Large Off‐Track Hill‐Sachs Lesion
Authors: Michael J Nocek, Mikalyn T DeFoor, Taylor Calibo, Nate J Dickinson, Madeleine Blankenship, Matthew T Provencher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesions present a significant challenge in the management of recurrent anterior shoulder instability, particularly in young, active patients. Traditional Hill‐Sachs management procedures include remplissage, coracoid transfer and free bone block reconstruction, which all carry associated limitations, including non‐anatomic reconstruction, donor‐site morbidity, risk of resorption, and/or glenohumeral contour mismatch, in addition to the risk of insufficient restoration of the glenoid track in the setting of a large off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesion. The fresh talus allograft free bone block has recently emerged as an excellent alternative source for a Hill‐Sachs lesion, given its close anthropometric match to the humeral head, robust articular cartilage, and variable graft sizing options. The purpose of this Technical Note is to describe a detailed, reproducible technique for the preparation of a fresh talus osteochondral allograft for reconstruction of a large off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesion.

Hill‐Sachs lesions frequently occur in recurrent anterior shoulder instability, with prevalence increasing from 66% to 71% after a first‐time dislocation up to 80%‐88% after recurrent instability events. 1 These lesions are particularly problematic when “off‐track,” as they compromise the glenoid track and can predispose patients to recurrent engagement and instability. 2 Standard soft‐tissue procedures such as Bankart repair and remplissage alone are insufficient in the treatment of large off‐track lesions, typically defined as >20%‐40% of the humeral head, and adjunctive bone reconstruction procedures are often required. 3

Reconstruction options for large, off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesions include coracoid transfer (Latarjet) and free bone block autograft or allograft reconstruction. 4 The Latarjet procedure remains an excellent option in the setting of significant glenoid deficiency but is insufficient in restoring the glenoid track when a large off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesion is present. 5 , 6 , 7 Autograft solutions, such as rotational humeral head osteotomy or iliac crest bone grafting, are limited by donor‐site morbidity and the challenges of restoring a congruent articular surface. 8 Structural allografts from femoral or humeral head sources have been described yet carry risks of graft resorption, collapse, and contour mismatch. 9 These limitations highlight the importance of an anatomic graft option that restores humeral head sphericity and maintains glenohumeral kinematics without significant donor‐site morbidity. As a result, the talus allograft has been described as an excellent alternative for humeral head reconstruction due to the radius of curvature of the talar dome closely approximating that of the humeral head, as well as its dense, weight‐bearing cartilage providing a congruent and durable articular surface. 10 , 11 In this Technical Note, we aim to describe a reproducible, detailed technique for preparation of a talus allograft to address a large off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesion (Video 1).

This technical note highlights a step‐by‐step guide and preparation of a talus allograft for the treatment of a large off track Hill‐Sachs lesion. This case was performed at the Steadman clinic in Vail, Colorado. This patient is a 29‐year‐old right‐hand‐dominant male with complaints of recurrent anterior instability of the right shoulder with multiple low energy dislocation events with day‐to‐day use. His treatment plan consisted of a right open Bankart repair and humeral head bone grafting with a fresh osteochondral talus allograft. On plain films, there is a moderate‐sized posterosuperior Hill‐Sachs lesion and no evidence of early glenohumeral osteoarthritis. Advanced imaging shows no glenoid bone loss with the best fit circle method, as well as a large, off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesion measuring 25% of the humeral head diameter. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed an associated anteroinferior Bankart injury. Through a standard deltopectoral approach in the beach chair position, the posterosuperior impaction defect of the humeral head is fully exposed. Once fully exposed, the defect is prepared for bony incorporation with a micro‐sagittal saw and power rasp to abrade the impacted and sclerotic edges of the lesion back to a smooth, bleeding surface of cancellous bone. The size of the defect is measured using a calibrated probe or ruler. Both the medial and lateral aspects of the talar dome are inspected for the optimal radius of curvature. A sharp towel clamp is used for graft control. The 4 major cuts are marked, prior to being cut with a micro‐sagittal saw. Typically, the lateral articular surface and lateral gutter are selected as the most favorable match for radius of curvature. Cut 1 consists of a longitudinal cut along the lateral edge of the talar dome perpendicular to the articular surface to remove the cartilage from the lateral fibular gutter. Cut 2 is made parallel to the articular surface of the talar dome, and perpendicular to Cut 1, to help set the depth of the osteochondral allograft, which is typically 16‐20 mm from the apex of the talar dome. Anatomic landmarks to assist with the depth include the junction of the talar articular surface, talar neck anteriorly, and the posterior margin of the talar cartilage posteriorly. Cut 3 is critical in order to appropriately match the sphericity of the humeral head. This cut is made at an oblique angle relative to the axis of the articular surface of the lateral talar dome, not perpendicular to the floor. It is important to understand the orientation and importance of the obliquely oriented Cut 3 to match the sphericity of the native humeral head. Prior to Cut 3, the osteochondral allograft has a flat contour. Cut 4 is the final cut to help establish the articular surface which creates the lemon wedge shape, following the full length of the articular surface of the allograft to the apex of its concavity. It is recommended that the surface width of the allograft be measured approximately 4‐5 mm wider than expected and then sequentially contoured down to size as appropriate, typically 8‐12 mm. Care should be taken to remain medial to the lateral downslope of the talar dome cartilage, as inclusion of this region may compromise articular conformity when the graft is seated into the humeral head defect. The graft is then remeasured and incrementally contoured to match the prepared defect size. The graft is pulse lavage for approximately 10 min and then allowed to soak in biological augmentation. Once the graft is appropriately contoured, provisional fixation is secured with two or three 2.4 mm Kirschner wires placed through the graft in parallel orientation to facilitate handling of the allograft and later screw fixation. SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Evaluation

A detailed history and physical examination is required preoperatively to determine the severity of patient symptoms, which should be correlated with complete imaging workup to include a full series of shoulder plain radiographs (Figure 1) and advanced imaging with magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography (CT) (Figure 2). In general, large humeral head defects >20%‐40% typically require surgical treatment in the young, active patient with symptomatic recurrent shoulder instability. 3 , 12 Magnetic resonance imaging is used to evaluate associated pathology such as labral or capsular injury, whereas CT with 3‐dimensional reconstructions allows for accurate assessment of humeral and glenoid bone loss, as well as medial extension and determination of on‐ versus off‐track status. 13

Graft Selection

A fresh osteochondral talus allograft (JRF Ortho, Centennial, CO) is selected by gender‐matching, obtained, and prepared on the back table (Figure 3). Proper preparation of the allograft is critical to ensure congruency with the humeral head defect articular surface and to optimize biologic incorporation.

Clinical image of a fresh talus osteochondral allograft for preparation with identifiable landmarks.

Humeral Head Defect Identification and Templating

The patient is positioned in the beach chair position and a standard deltopectoral approach is made to access the glenohumeral joint. Even in fairly posterior Hill‐Sachs lesions, access from the anterior deltopectoral approach is achievable without takedown of the subscapularis with external rotation and adduction of the operative extremity and two sharp Hohmann retractors positioned over the defect to retract soft tissue.

Once the defect is fully exposed, it is prepared for bony incorporation with a microsagittal saw and power rasp to abrade the impacted and sclerotic edges of the lesion back to a smooth, bleeding surface of cancellous bone (Figure 4). The size of the defect is then measured including the length, width and depth using a calibrated probe or ruler. Typically, the size dimensions of the final osteochondral graft to match the associated defect are 36‐38 mm in length (superior to inferior), 8‐12 mm in width (anterior to posterior) and 16‐20 mm in depth (measured from the apex). These dimensions are then transferred to the talar allograft, with markings placed on the articular surface to outline the desired graft dimensions. It is recommended to oversize the graft outline by 2‐5 mm to account for kerf of the saw blade, measurement error, and to allow for subsequent fine contouring.

Graft Preparation: 4 Major Cuts

The talar allograft is secured with a sharp towel clamp for graft control. The 4 major cuts are marked and then cut with a microsagittal saw (Figure 5). Continuous irrigation is used throughout to keep the graft cool and to minimize the risk of thermal necrosis to the articular cartilage and underlying subchondral bone.

Cut 4, cut along the full length of the articular surface from the apex of the concavity of the talar dome with rounded edges to establish the lemon‐wedge width, which ultimately will be contoured down to a size of about 16 mm.

Graft Preparation: Final Contouring and Trial

After the initial cuts are made, the graft is remeasured and incrementally contoured to match the prepared Hill‐Sachs defect. This requires constant transfer of the graft between the back table and surgical field at the site of the defect. The graft is sequentially contoured with a rasp, rongeur, or microsagittal saw until it seats flush within the humeral head defect without overhang or step‐off at the articular surface. Ideally, a snug press‐fit configuration of the graft is observed within the humeral head defect. Again, frequent trial placement is recommended to refine the contour and avoid over‐resection of the graft.

Graft Preparation: Conditioning and Biologic Augmentation

Prior to implantation, the allograft undergoes pulse lavage for at least 10 min to remove marrow elements and reduce immunogenicity. The graft is then soaked in autologous conditioned plasma and platelet‐rich plasma using a double‐syringe system to enhance biologic incorporation.

Graft Fixation

Once the graft is appropriately contoured, provisional fixation is secured with two or three 2.4 mm Kirschner wires placed through the graft in parallel orientation to facilitate handling of the allograft and later screw fixation (Figure 11). Screw trajectory should avoid penetration of the articular surface and maximize compression at the graft–host interface. The allograft is fixed by placing two or three 3.5 mm cannulated, headless compression screws (Arthrex, Naples, FL) over the Kirschner wires, and burying them at least 2‐3 mm below the chondral surface (Figure 12). Immediate postoperative plain films are obtained to ensure appropriate fixation, graft contour, and screw trajectory (Figure 13).

DISCUSSION

Management of large off‐track Hill‐Sachs lesions remains a clinical challenge. As these defects compromise the glenoid track and predispose to recurrent instability even after labral repair or glenoid reconstruction, several strategies have been developed to address the humeral‐sided bone loss. Due to the radius of curvature match to the native humeral head, robust articular cartilage, and graft availability, the talus allograft has emerged as an attractive option for humeral head reconstruction of Hill‐Sachs lesions >20%‐40% of the humeral head (Table 1). Successful application of this technique relies heavily on meticulous graft preparation (Table 2). Key considerations include accurate intraoperative measurement of the defect, harvesting the graft in line with the downslope of the talar dome, oversizing to accommodate the kerf of the saw blade, and creating a wedge‐shaped configuration after fine contouring to maximize host–graft contact (Table 3).

Although there is a paucity of clinical outcome studies evaluating talus allograft reconstruction for recurrent shoulder instability, biomechanical and anthropometric analyses are promising. 11 , 14 These studies have shown that the talus closely replicates the radius of curvature of the humeral head and provides a dense, weight‐bearing cartilage surface well suited for glenohumeral articulation. 10 , 11 Historical series using femoral or humeral head allografts have reported variable results, including concerns over graft resorption and revision surgery. 15 By contrast, the morphology and conformity of the talus suggest that it may offer more reliable restoration of shoulder stability and native motion, though outcome studies are needed to validate its clinical effectiveness.
