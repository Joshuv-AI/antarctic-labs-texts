Research on the evolution law and control technology of deviatoric stress in the surrounding rock of gob-side entry retaining
Authors: Dingchao Chen, Xiangyu Wang, Jianbiao Bai, Jiaxin Zhao, Qingcong Zhou, Xiang Ji
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGob-side entry retaining (GER) play a crucial role in enhancing coal recovery, improving roadway stress conditions, and ensuring safe and efficient mining operations. This study focuses on investigating the stability characteristics of surrounding rock using flexible formwork concrete (FFC) along the headgate of 3405 panel. According to the fracture pattern of roof, a mechanical model of GER was developed. This study derived expressions for the support resistance of the filling body and determined its optimal bearing capacity. A FLAC3D numerical model was employed to simulate the GER process, segmented into early, middle, and late stages. Simulation results demonstrate a progression in peak deviatoric stress and extent of plastic zones in the surrounding rock. This evolution includes an initial minor increase (early stage), followed by rapid escalation (middle stage), and eventual stabilization (late stage). The middle stage emerges as critical, characterized by pronounced mining-induced stress effects on the roof. During this phase, significant changes are observed in deviatoric stress curves, with plastic zones rapidly migrating deeper into the rock mass. Practical implementation of the findings revealed a maximum deformation of the filling body of only 198 mm, affirming the feasibility of employing FFC in GER. This research provides valuable insights into managing stability concerns in GER thereby enhancing safety and operational efficiency in coal mining under similar conditions.Keywords: GER, FFC, Deviatoric stress, Plastic zone, Mechanical model

Introduction

As the intensity of coal mining in China continues to increase, the situation regarding coal resources has become increasingly strained, and the drawbacks of the traditional coal pillar extraction method have become more pronounced [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. To improve coal recovery rates, reduce resource wastage, and alleviate the pressures of mining, more and more mines are adopting and promoting the coal mining without pillars technique [[6], [7], [8]]. This approach not only significantly boosts coal production but also minimizes resource wastage to the greatest extent possible [[9], [10], [11]].

GER, as a coal mining without pillars technique, involves constructing a backfill body along the edge of the gob to retain the current panel's headgate for use as the tailgate in the next panel [[12], [13], [14]]. The roadside backfill body is crucial for GER [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. In response, scholars have developed various roadside support methods. These methods include high water filling material (HWFM) [19] (as shown in Fig. 1(a)), concrete-filled steel tubular columns (CSTC) [20] (as shown in Fig. 1 (b)), waste rock filling (WRF) [21] (as shown in Fig. 1 (c)), flexible formwork concrete wall (FFC) [22] (as shown in Fig. 1 (d)), mortise-and-tenon structure hollow wall (MSHW) [23] (as shown in Fig. 1 (e)), block wall structure (BWS) [24] (as shown in Fig. 1 (f)), and others. These innovations are advancements in GER technology. Among them, FFC has gained increasing application in deep mines due to its high support strength and excellent load-bearing performance [25].

Types of roadside backfill body in GER.

A substantial amount of research has been conducted by scholars both domestically and internationally on the stability mechanisms and control techniques for GER [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Chen et al. [30] analyzed the full-cycle vertical stress evolution of FFC walls in GER using FLAC3D. They found that the significantly affected areas ahead of the working face included roof, floor, solid coal rib, and gob side, with respective ranges of 28 m, 15 m, 24 m, and 24 m. In the lagging working face section, the roof and floor were influenced by mining to distances of approximately 35 m and 18 m, respectively. Wang et al. [31] simulated the process of GER, accurately reproducing the stress states during different stages, and revealing the overall roadway deformation characteristics through the observation and analysis of the plastic zone. Meng et al. [32] used UDEC to analyze the internal failure modes and crack quantities within the backfill body under static and dynamic loads during GER. Sun et al. [33] employed a combination of similar modeling and discrete element simulation to reveal the progressive failure of the overburden, roadway deformation, and the stress environment of GER. Li et al. [34] investigated the dynamic impact mechanism of different fracture locations of the key blocks on the crack evolution within the backfill body in GER. Skrzypkowski et al. [35,36] analyzed the bearing characteristics of backfill structures with varying numbers of connection points. Sakhno et al. [37] analyzed the distribution of maximum principal stresses around GER.

To date, limited research has thoroughly examined the deviatoric stress of GER. By considering the simultaneous interaction of the maximum, intermediate, and minimum principal stresses, deviatoric stress offers a more precise understanding of the relationship between stress evolution and failure processes. Consequently, studying the evolution patterns and control techniques of deviatoric stress in GER is crucial for advancing stability control in these environments.

In this study, an in-depth investigation of the on-site conditions of Dayang Coal Mine was conducted. Combining theoretical calculations and numerical simulations, a mechanical model of GER was established. This study examined the evolution of deviatoric stress and the development of the plastic zone, shedding light on the mechanisms driving progressive instability and failure in the rock mass of GER. Building on these insights, improved support strategies for the roadside backfill were developed and effectively applied in industrial trials.

Roof structure characteristics and mechanical properties of GER

Roof structure characteristics of GER

As coal mining continues, the roof above the gob reaches its maximum deflection and eventually breaks. This breaking phenomenon typically starts at the center of the roof and extends from the long edge to the short edge, while also expanding towards the four corners. When the roof initially breaks completely, it forms a distinct "O-X″ fracture pattern, characterized by an "O" shape around the perimeter and an "X" shape in the center. This pattern typically arises during the first weighting of main roof. As the gob expands, periodic weighting continues to affect the roof, leading to recurring "O-X″ fracture patterns, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a).

Model establishment

As illustrated in Fig. 10, the model dimensions are 224 m (length) × 150 m (width) × 58.8 m (height), consisting of 479,640 elements and 502,192 nodes. To accurately represent the gradual compaction characteristics of the rock mass in gob and the dynamic bearing characteristics of FFC backfill body, the Double-yield model and the Strain-softening model were used to simulate gob and backfill body, respectively. Other rock layers were simulated using the Mohr-Coulomb model.

To accurately simulate the actual conditions of GER, the working face advances by 2 m each time, and simultaneously, the FFC backfill advances by 2 m, lagging 2 m behind the 3405 working face. This process involves a total of 80 cycles (each cycle advancing 2 m), until the 3405 panel completes its extraction (a total advance of 160 m). Along the headgate of the 3405 panel, monitoring points were set up every 16 m in the advancing direction, totaling 8 stations, as shown in Fig. 11.

Layout of monitoring points.

Station 1 is located 32 m from the open-off cut, and station 8 is 16 m from the stopping line. These stations will experience the full-cycle disturbance effects of the early, middle, and late stages of GER. Therefore, deviatoric stress data from each station were collected and recorded in real-time to obtain the evolution patterns.

Evolution law of deviatoric stress

To monitor the deviatoric stress, control points were arranged inside the rock mass and the backfill material, as shown in Fig. 16.

Layout of control points.

Filling body

Using monitoring point 4 as an example, points are arranged inside the filling body, and the results are shown in Fig. 17.

Distribution of deviatoric stress inside the filling body at different stages.

The overall deviatoric stress inside the filling body at monitoring point 4 shows a trend of first increasing and then slightly decreasing. When the working face advances to 88 m, monitoring point 4 lags behind the working face by only 8 m, and the overall deviatoric stress inside the filling body is relatively low, with a maximum value of only 3.1 MPa. However, as the working face advances to 96 m, 112 m, and 128 m, the deviatoric stress inside the filling body gradually increases, reaching a maximum value of 7.5 MPa. When the working face advances to 144 m and 160 m, the deviatoric stress inside the filling body shows a slight decreasing trend, but the reduction is not significant.

Roof

Using monitoring point 4 as an example, points are positioned within a 10-m range above the roadway roof to monitor the distribution of deviatoric stress. The results are illustrated in Fig. 18.

Distribution of deviatoric stress in the roof at different stages.

In the early stage, influenced by initial excavation disturbances in the roadway, the deviatoric stress distribution in the roof exhibits a single-peak pattern from shallow to deep. At a distance of 3 m from the roof, the stress peaks at 8.3 MPa.

In the middle stage, the roof is affected by the mining activity, resulting in the redistribution of deviatoric stress. The deviatoric stress transforms from a single-peak to a stepped pattern, and the deep deviatoric stress values increase.

In the late stage, as the filling material gradually stabilizes, the surrounding rock of the roof tends towards stability, maintaining a stepped deviatoric stress pattern with a slight overall decrease in stress levels.

Solid coal side

Using monitoring point 4 as an example, points are placed within a 10-m range along the solid coal. The results are depicted in Fig. 19.

Distribution of deviatoric stress in the solid coal rib at different stages.

Before the working face reaches monitoring point 4: The deviatoric stress exhibits a single-peak distribution, with stress peaks occurring within 2.0–3.0 m from the rib surface. Stress peaks range between 6.3 and 7.4 MPa.

After the working face reaches monitoring point 4: The deviatoric stress peak deepens and the stress magnitude increases. This indicates that the mining activity induces deformation deeper within the coal, resulting in increased deviatoric stress.

Industrial experimentation

Sidewall reinforcement technology

The width of the access drift is set at 4400 mm, and a sidewall reinforcement width of 1400 mm. To meet structural strength and stability requirements, C30 grade concrete is chosen for the sidewall reinforcement. Additionally, 0.21 % early strength agent and 0.04 % pumping agent were added to enhance the early strength and pumpability.

On the third day after forming the wall, pre-tensioning forces are applied to the tension anchor rods, with a required torque of 150 N m or higher to ensure effective anchoring. Simultaneously, the tension anchor rods are spaced at intervals of 800 × 750 mm for uniform distribution and effective support. Conclusions

(1)A mechanical model of GER was established based on the "O-X″ fracture characteristics of the roof and the critical block fracture position. Through analysis, the minimum support resistance of the filling body was determined to be 24.6 MN, corresponding to a minimum bearing capacity of 19.7 MPa for a 1.4 m filling body.

A mechanical model of GER was established based on the "O-X″ fracture characteristics of the roof and the critical block fracture position. Through analysis, the minimum support resistance of the filling body was determined to be 24.6 MN, corresponding to a minimum bearing capacity of 19.7 MPa for a 1.4 m filling body.

(2)The study revealed the evolutionary laws of deviatoric stress and plastic zones along the entire cycle of the GER using FFC. The GER process was divided into early, middle, and late stages. During the early stage, the surrounding rock experienced minimal mining-induced stress, resulting in slight increases in deviatoric stress and plastic zones. In the middle stage, peak stress deviation significantly increased, and plastic zones migrated deeper. In the late stage, a stable structure was formed with the roof and filling body, stabilizing peak stress deviation at 10–11 MPa, and the width of plastic zones in the coal seam remained stable at 5.8 m.
