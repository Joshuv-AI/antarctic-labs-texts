Effect of mixed fermentation with Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK on the flavor precursor and flavor formation of Kazak cheese
Authors: Yuechenfei Shen, Jie Shen, Ning Kang, Yu Gao, Zhexin Fan, Zhifeng Fang, Jiancheng Wang, Baokun Li, Bo Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous research has demonstrated that Lacticaseibacillus paracasei notably enhances flavor stability with the cost of diminishing the inherent milk flavor in cheese, which may be rescued by the incorporation of Limosilactobacillus reuteri. To investigate the effects of mixed fermentation with Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK on the formation of taste during the ripening period of kazak cheese, this study determined its sensory attributes, flavor precursors, volatile flavor components (VFCs), and Odorant Activity Value (OAV). The mixed fermentation increased the levels of organic acids, aromatic and umami amino acids, saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), ultimately intensifying the concentration of aldehydes and ketones. Importantly, 2,3-butanadione, 1-octane-3 ketone, 2-undecone and 1-octane-3 alcohol were prominently enriched in the mixed fermented cheese, which were contributing to the distinctive milk aroma. This study provided insights into the mechanism of flavor formation during mixed fermentation of Kazak cheese.

Introduction

Kazak cheese, a renowned traditional handmade fermented cheese in China, is crafted with care from fresh milk through fermentation, encompassing fermentation, whey drainage, dehydration, shaping, and ripening (Zheng et al., 2018). Kazak cheese is celebrated for its extraordinary taste, captivating aroma, and unparalleled nutritional advantages, where flavor stands as the cornerstone in defining its premium quality (Xu et al., 2020). Starter cultures play a pivotal role in determining the quality characteristics of Kazakh cheese, initiating a diverse range of microorganisms to convert lactose and galactose into an array of intricate volatile and non-volatile flavor compounds. The traditional Kazakh cheese undergoes fermentation by employing aged yogurt, but varying microorganisms present in the yogurt ultimately result in inconsistent product quality (Zheng et al., 2018). Ensuring the consistency of cheese flavor is a crucial area of research.

The distinctive flavor of the cheese primarily stems from its ripening process. Cheese ripening is a sophisticated biochemical metabolic process that mainly consists of three key pathways: glycolysis, fat oxidation and decomposition, and proteolysis (Y. Chen, MacNaughtan, Jones, Yang, & Foster, 2020). Volatile and non-volatile compounds jointly contribute to the distinctive flavor of cheese, primarily stemming from the flavor developed throughout the the ripening of cheese process and the inherent flavor components naturally present in milk. Lactic acid converted by glycolysis could contribute to acid flavor, and also could be metabolized into flavor substances ketones (Y. Xia et al., 2020). The free amino acid (FAA) and free fatty acid (FFA) serves as pivotal constituents that contribute significantly to the overall flavor of the cheese, but also could potentially transform into a diverse array of flavor compounds, including benzoic acid, benzaldehyde, ethyl acetate, 1-octen-3-ol,2,3-butanedione and 1-octen-3-ketones (Tekin & Güler, 2019).

Microbial activity holds a pivotal role in the development of its flavor compounds during the maturation process of cheese, thereby contributing significantly to the overall aroma of the cheese (Song et al., 2023). These microorganisms collectively contribute to the distinctive flavor of cheese through their specialized metabolic processes and their collaborative interactions with other microbial species. Non-starter lactic acid bacteria which stands for NSLAB, effectively enhances the taste quality of fermented foods by optimizing the fermentation process and successfully limiting the growth potential of pathogenic microorganisms (Bancalari et al., 2020). Studies have demonstrated that incorporating NSLAB into Kazakh cheese notably enhances the flavor quality during the ripening process, which primarily exerts a positive influence on the synthesis of crucial volatile compounds, including ethyl caprylate, ethyl caprate, capric acid, caprylic acid, nonanal and benzyl alcohol, among others (S. Li et al., 2021). Furthermore, the blended fermentation process of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Kluyveromyces marxianus SMN-S7-LBK facilitated the production of acids, alcohols and esters, thereby intensifying the comprehensive flavor profile of Kazak cheese (Yandie Li et al., 2022). Therefore, the deliberate manipulation of the microbial communities present in Kazak cheese serves as an efficacious means to enhance its overall flavor quality.

Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK has conclusively demonstrated its ability to significantly enhance the taste profile of fermented products, particularly cheese, albeit with a marginal reduction in the natural milk flavor (S. Li et al., 2021; Yandie Li et al., 2022). Limosilactobacillus reuteri has the remarkable ability to substantially elevate the concentration of ketones in fermented products (Jia et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2025). Utilizing a targeted, multi-strain blend fermentation approach could potentially serve as an innovative and effective means of enhancing the unique flavor profiles of cheese. To the best of our knowledge, although mixed fermentation has been successfully applied in cheese, there have been few studies on the effect of mixed fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Limosilactobacillus reuteri on cheese flavor. In this study, HS-SPME-GC–MS and LC-MS were employed to identify the flavor precursor substances, flavor characteristics, and core flavor substances of Kazakh cheese inoculated with a mixed fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK. This comprehensive study delves deeply into the crucial role of mixed fermentation in the intricate process of flavor substance formation and the overall flavor profiling of Kazakh cheese. To lay a solid theoretical foundation for the study of microbial influence on Kazakh cheese flavor and the development of industrial starter cultures that can enhance aroma and flavor substances.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Kazak cheese was prepared according to previous methods (S. Li et al., 2021). Through response surface methodology analysis, the optimal fermentation process conditions were determined: the strain inoculation ratio was 1:1, the inoculum size was 4 %, the fermentation temperature was 37.5 °C, and the fermentation time was 16 h. Each group was sampled at three stages: 1 day (beginning maturation), 20 days (mid-maturation), and 40 days (late maturation). The expression of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK at 1 day of maturation: F1d; the expression of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK at 20 days of maturation: F20d; the expression of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK at 40 days of maturation: F40d. Correspondingly, Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK:L; Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK:M.

The degree of protein degradation can be determined using the Kjeldahl method and a Kjeldahl nitrogen determination apparatus. Measure the pH value using a calibrated digital pH meter (PHS-3C; Liuyi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The viable count of lactic acid bacteria in cheese was determined using the plate count method, conducted in triplicate.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory assessment of the Kazak mature cheese samples (days 1,20 and 40 of maturity) was performed by a panel of 10 judges (5 men and 5 women) who were trained in descriptive analysis. All tasters voluntarily participate in the cheese flavor test after being informed of the risks and granting their informed consent, and their privacy is fully protected. And ethical permission was not required. Each cheese sample was cut into 2 cm or 3 cm cubes and presented to the panel in a random order (Y. Xia et al., 2020). Seven descriptors were selected, including milk, fruit, acerbity, alcohol, stale, nutty, and broth flavor. Choose a standard product for each descriptive flavor, milk flavor: commercial cream, fruit flavor: 20 mg/kg ethyl hexanic acid solution, acerbity flavor; 0.3 g/Lcitric acid aqueous solution, alcohol flavor: 20 % ethanol aqueous solution, stale flavor: 10 % butyric acid solution, nutty flavor: salt-free nuts and broth flavor: beef soup. Intensity ratings were scored on a scale from 0 to 9 (1–2: very weak; 3–4: ordinary; 5–6: moderate; 7–8: strong; 9: very strong)(Y. Xia et al., 2020).

Determination of E-nose

The electronic nose (PEN 3, AIRSENSE, Germany) was used to detect odors. Accurately weigh 10 g cheese samples into a 40 mL sample vial and leave the sample at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate the emission of volatile compounds before further processing using an E-nose device (Y. Liu et al., 2024).

Organic acids analysis

Weighed 4 g cheese, mixed with 20 mL sulfuric acid (0.01 N, incubated at 65 °C for 30 min, and homogenized. The mixture was centrifuged (10,000 ×g for 25 min，4 °C) and the upper layer was filtered, then the filtrate was filtered again through a 0.45 μm extraction filter, and the final fixed volume in a 20-ml volumetric flask (Liu, Al-Dalali, et al., 2024). Reversed phase HPLC was used to determine the organic acids in the cheese (Bancalari et al., 2020).

Free amino acids (FAA) analysis

Kazak cheese FAA content was measured using reversed-phase HPLC with OPA column UV detection. Add 2 g of sample to 20 mL of 10 % TCA, homogenize for 2 min, sonicate at 25 °C for 20 min, then centrifuge at 10000 rpm for 20 min. Filter the solution, then transfer it to a 20 mL volumetric flask. Finally, 400 μL sample and 10 μL deuterium internal standard were transferred to the autosampler for testing (Luo et al., 2023).

Free fatty acids (FFA) analysis

The FFA of Kazakh cheese was determined according to the method of Chinese national standard GB 5009.168–2016 with minor modifications. Added 5 g cheese and 0.05 % BHT to 20 ml chloroform-methanol (2:1, v/v), vortexed for 3 × 10 s. The mixture was centrifuged three times (10,000 ×g for 25 min, 4 °C) after which the supernatant was dried and weighed. Then weighed the oil sample (20 mg) for saponification and methyl esterification, and finally fixed to 10 ml. A gas chromatography instrument was then used to determine the free fatty acids (Gao et al., 2024).

HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis

HS-SPME operations

The fermented cheese was incubated at about 20 °C for 30 min. At the end of the waiting time, the cheese was chopped into small pieces and put in 3 g into a 20 mL headspace culture bottle. Sterile water and cyclohexanone were added and mixed until dissolved. Thereafter, 3 g sodium chloride was added to a 20 mL bottle, shaken, and volatiles were evaporated with a rotor. The headspace bottle was placed under magnetic stirring at 60 °C for 20 min, followed by extending the fiber head probe for 60 min to extract volatile compounds.

GC–MS analysis

VOCs determination was performed as reported by Wu et al. (Wu et al., 2021) with slight modifications. High-purity helium gas, with a purity of 99.99 %, was used as the carrier gas, with the flow rate set at 1.2 mL/min. The GC temperature program was specifically as follows: the initial temperature was set at 35 °C and maintained for 6 min; then it was raised to 160 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min and held for 3 min; finally, the temperature was further increased to 230 °C at a rate of 12 °C/min and maintained for 6 min. Mass spectrometry (MS) was conducted in electron ionization (EI) mode (at 70 eV, with the temperature set to 230 °C. In scan mode, precise detection was conducted on the total ion chromatogram within the m/z range of 35 to 350.

The identified volatile compounds were compared against the NIST MS spectral library, and qualitative analysis was conducted based on retention time (RT). A semi-quantitative analysis of aroma compounds was performed using the internal standard method, with cyclohexanone serving as the internal standard. Relative quantitative analysis was conducted using the area normalization method, with the concentration unit expressed in μg/L. The calculation formula is as follows: C = A × Cj / Aj, where C represents the concentration of the volatile substance, A denotes the chromatographic peak area of the volatile substance, Cj is the concentration of the internal standard cyclohexanone, and Aj is the chromatographic peak area of cyclohexanone. Quantification was done using an internal standard, with all cheese samples measured three times.

OAV analysis

The Odorant Activity Value (OAV) for each volatile compound was calculated by using the ratio of the concentration (VOC) of each volatile compound to the corresponding odor threshold (OT) in the water. The odor threshold reference standard for volatile compounds in water was derived from websites and other literature (https://www.femaflavor.org/flavor-library, http://www.odour.org.uk).

Statistical analysis

Data were imported into IBM SPSS Statistical software version 19.0 to perform one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan-test (p < 0.05). Principal component analysis (PCA), Pleast discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), P values and FC values were calculated using the https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool website. Column diagram and heatmaps during the fermentation of kazak cheese were drawn using Prism10 software and the https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool website to explain the dynamics of flavor metabolite content. All experiments were performed at least three times and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Sensory evaluation, electronic nose analysis, and basic physicochemical indicators of Kazak cheese

Sensory evaluation stands as one of the most established and direct approaches to food detection methods (Y. Xia et al., 2020). Regarding olfactory perception, the cheese samples in mixed fermentation exhibited a more intense milk and broth aroma, whereas the fruit and stale flavors were diminished in comparison to those samples in single bacteria fermentation group (Fig. 1A). A portion of the stale flavor in cheese stems from FFA, and ester compounds represent the pivotal elements contributing to its fruity aroma (Zheng, Liu, Li, et al., 2018). The potential decline might stem from the mixed fermentation fostering the breakdown of certain free fatty acids and esters. While mixed fermentation exhibited a substantial alcohol content in the latter stages (Fig. 5A), its influence on the aroma of cheese wine failed to achieve the commensurate level of significance. This could be attributed to the substantial influence of higher alcohols on the alcohol flavor profile of cheese, rather than the total alcohol content alone (Li, Cao, Yu, Zhu, & Zhao, 2023). The electronic nose is an exceptionally efficient and swift electronic chemical sensing system that mimics the human sense of smell to accurately and objectively detect food flavor attributes (Li et al., 2023). The alterations in the response values unveiled the most pronounced response at sensors W1S and W2S in three types cheese, subsequently followed by W1W and W2W, suggesting that short-chain alkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones constitute the primary volatile compounds in cheese (Fig. 1B). The W1S and W2W levels of mixed-fermentation were notably elevated compared to those resulting from single bacterial fermentation, which aligns with distinctions discerned in their sensory evaluations. It could be speculated that the unique flavor of cheese may come from aromatic chemicals in W1S and W2S.

The pH values of the three groups of fermented cheese initially increased and then decreased, a phenomenon resulting from the fermentation of residual lactose (Fig. S1E). The pH value of the mixed fermentation group was lower than that of the Limosilactobacillus reuteri fermentation group after 1 day of ripening, indicating that mixed fermentation is more conducive to microbial growth and acid production (Bancalari et al., 2020). pH 4.6-SN denotes the ratio of water-soluble nitrogen to total nitrogen during the initial hydrolysis of cheese proteins (Xu et al., 2020). In the early stages of ripening, the content of water-soluble nitrogen in cheese remains relatively low. As the ripening period extended, the pH 4.6-SN content in the three groups of fermented cheeses exhibited a progressive upward trend, peaking at 40 days of ripening (Fig. S1F). Except during the early fermentation stage, the pH 4.6-SN content in both the mixed fermentation group and the Lacticaseibacillus paracasei group was significantly higher than that in the Limosilactobacillus reuteri group (P < 0.05). This may be attributed to the relatively higher proteolytic activity of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, which promotes the primary hydrolysis of proteins to some extent. The variation trend of 12 % TCA-SN content is similar to that of pH 4.6-SN, indicating that during the primary hydrolysis of proteins by rennet and the formation of soluble peptides, the proteases of the strain can further hydrolyze these peptides at a relatively rapid rate (Fig. S1G). Organic acid content analysis

Organic acids, important flavor compounds, are precursors of odor active compounds as well as interact with aroma. The amount of total organic acids and lactic acid in mix-fermented cheese was notably greater than that found in cheese fermented by a single bacterial strain (Fig. 2E). The total organic acids in the mix-fermented group increased with ripening, whereas in the Lacticaseibacillus paracasei-fermented group, the opposite occurred. This could potentially stem from the fact that mixed fermentation enhances the ripening process of the cheese, thereby fostering the generation of a greater quantity of total organic acids. The trend of lactate in the three types cheese mirrored the trend observed in total organic acids, primarily attributed to the notably high percentage of lactic acid within the total organic acid content. The heightened presence of lactic acid in the mixed fermentation group could potentially stem from the collaborative effect of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Limosilactobacillus reuteri, which facilitate the breakdown of lactose into lactic acid while simultaneously inhibiting its conversion into flavor compounds (Shen et al., 2025). Lactic acid provide soft and lasting acerbity and reduce the production of uncomfortable flavor through inhibiting the growth of miscellaneous bacteria during the fermentation process (Bancalari et al., 2020).

Citric acid and succinic acid are significant components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which could provide acerbity flavor, as well as serve as an antioxidant to prevent the decay of color and aroma (Liu, Al-Dalali, et al., 2024). The quantity of citric acid and succinic acid in mix-fermented cheese markedly surpassed that in single bacterial fermented cheese throughout the ripening process. Furthermore, the concentration of citric acid in the mix-fermented diminished as the ripening period progressed, whereas the level of succinic acid remained largely unaltered. This could be attributed to the fact that citric acid might be consumed through the TCA and lactic acid fermentation pathways, leading to the production of acetyl acid, succinyl acid, alcohol, and aldehydes (Zhang et al., 2023).

Overall, the results demonstrated that mixed fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK significantly augmented the levels of organic acids, thereby enhancing the production of flavor compounds, which aligns with the conclusions drawn by Na Liu et al. (Na Liu, Pan, Miao, & Qin, 2020).

Free amino acids (FAA)

FAAs, essential precursors of flavor compounds, could be converted into diverse volatile compounds various volatile compounds, encompassing aldehydes, ketones, esters, and pyrazines (Liu et al., 2024). 15 FAA were identified and exhibited significant variations among the three types of cheese. Additionally, they displayed a notable upward trend during the ripening process, with leucine (Leu), lysine (Lys), glutamate (Glu), and phenylalanine (Phe) emerging as the predominant FAAs (P < 0.05, Fig. 3). The elevation of FAA could be attributed to the continuous breakdown by proteolytic enzymes secreted by microorganisms: proteins initially breakdown into peptides, followed by the breakdown into FAA by enzyme during the secondary proteolytic stage (Song et al., 2023). Based on taste characteristics, FAAs were classified as umami, sweet and bitter amino acids. Notably, the majority of FAAs belong to the bitter amino acid, followed by sweet and umami amino acids, which aligns with the conclusions drawn by Xiong et al. (Xiong et al., 2024). Phenylalanine is an aromatic amino acid. The concentration of phenylalanine in the the mixed fermented cheese was notably higher (p < 0.05) compared to the single bacterial fermented cheese (p < 0.05) during the late maturation, whereas minimal variation was observed during the pre-and mid-maturation stages. This could potentially be attributed to the substantial rise in acid concentration during the mixed fermentation process (Fig. 2F), leading to a heightened activation of endogenous proteases and peptidases, thereby facilitating the seamless progression of protein secondary metabolism (Q. Chen, Kong, Han, Xia, & Xu, 2017). Phenylalanine has the potential to be transformed into a range of flavor compounds, including phenylacetaldehyde, phenylethanol, and benzaldehyde. Notably, benzaldehyde often exhibits an almond-like aroma, whereas phenylacetaldehyde possesses a distinct, intense rose-green fragrance flavor in cheese (Xiong et al., 2024).

Glutamate (Glu) and aspartate (Asp) are typical umami amino acids in cheese. The content in mixed fermentation underwent a notable increase in comparison to Lacticaseibacillus paracasei-fermented group, yet it failed to exhibit a marked distinction from the Limosilactobacillus reuteri-fermented group. This could potentially stem from the fact that Limosilactobacillus reuteri markedly increases glutamate levels by bolstering glutamine deamination at the mature stage (Yu & Sun, 2021). Glutamate (Glu) and aspartate (Asp), in conjunction with kokumi flavor elements, elevate the comprehensive taste sensation by modulating sweetness, intensifying saltiness, and suppressing sourness and bitterness (Bu et al., 2023). Furthermore, the metabolism of glutamate and aspartate results in the generation of ethylene coupling and diacetyl, both of which impart a creamy aroma and umami flavor to the fermented cheese (Song et al., 2023).

Methionine and leucine are typical bitter amino acids after cheese fermentation. The levels of methionine and leucine in the mixed fermented cheese were notably reduced in comparison to those in the single bacterial fermented cheese (p < 0.05) during the latter stages of ripening, whereas no significant differences were discerned during the initial ripening period. This could potentially arise from the mixed fermentation process's capability to substantially elevate the production of α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, which subsequently facilitates the efficient breakdown of certain bitter amino acids into α-ketoacid (Hua, Sun, Xu, Gao, & Xia, 2022). Sulfur-containing methionine undergoes gradual degradation into dimethyl sulfide and dimethyl trifluoride, releasing a pungent, smoky, fishy, and sulfurous aroma that potentially that may negatively influence the flavor of cheese (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2024). Leucine undergoes a sequence of decarboxylation and deamination reactions, resulting in the formation of alcohols and aldehydes, including 3-methyl butanol and 3-methylbutanaldehyde, thereby imparting a unique grassy, fruity, and sweet aroma to cheese.

The findings demonstrated that the combined fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK significantly enhances the presence of aromatic and umami amino acids, while effectively decreasing the concentration of bitter amino acids. Mixed bacteria fermentation possesses the capability to enhance the enzymatic activity of specific enzymes, notably aminopeptidases, ultimately leading to an increased liberation of free amino acids and stimulating the generation of flavor compounds, which is in line with the findings of Luo et al. (Luo et al., 2023).

Free fatty acids (FFAs)

FFA, derived from fat degradation and oxidation, not merely directly contributes to the formation of cheese flavor, as well as serves as a precursor of odor-active compounds (Gao et al., 2024). The primary volatiles resulting from FFA are aldehydes and ketones. 37 FFA (C4:0-C24:0) were detected including 17 saturated fatty acids (SFA), 9 monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) and 11 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA). Among all the fatty acids, SFA emerges as the most crucial, exerting the most pronounced influence on the flavor profile during the cheese ripening process.

The content of SFAs in the three type fermented cheeses increased first and then decreased with ripening time. The mixed fermented cheese exhibited a notably higher content of SFAs in contrast to single bacterial fermented cheeses, aside from the intermediary ripening phase. The potential cause of the increase in SFAs may lie in the fact that, lactic acid bacteria facilitate the lipid oxidation process, leading to their decomposition into MUFA or PUFA during the initial stages of maturation, which then oxidize to form SFA (Gao et al., 2024). The decline in SFAs during the later stages of ripening may be attributed to SFA into coenzymes and β -ketoacids via glucose metabolism and lipid metabolism, ultimately leading to the formation of hydrocarbons, ketones, aldehydes, and other aromatic compounds (L. Xia et al., 2023).

Palmitic acid (C16:0), myristic acid (C14:0), stearic acid (C18:0), butyric acid (C4:0) and lauric acid (C12:0) were the main SFAs in the three type cheeses, followed by caproic acid (C6:0), decanoic acid (C10:0), octanoic acid (C8:0) and pentadecanoic acid (C15:0) during the stages of ripening. The concentrations of palmitic acid (C16:0), myristic acid (C14:0) and stearic acid (C18:0) in mixed fermented cheese were markedly elevated compared to those observed in cheeses fermented by a single bacterial (P < 0.05). The diversity among the three fermented cheeses could potentially arise from the mixed fermentation process, which produces more extracellular lipase through synergy, as well as bolsters the lipase activity, thereby partly facilitating the production of related fatty acids (Xiao, Liu, Chen, Xie, & Li, 2020). Nevertheless, the butyric acid (C4:0) content in the mixed fermented cheese was notably lower than that observed in the single bacterial fermented cheese during the initial stages of maturation (P < 0.05). This might be attributed to the ability of mixed fermentation to hinder butyric acid kinase activity by establishing a high-concentration acidic milieu, aligning with the notion that mixed fermentation enhances the presence of organic acids (Fig. 2F)(Tian et al., 2023). Butyric acid (C4:0), renowned for its unpleasant combination of sweet and sour cream aroma. Lauric acid (C12:0) possesses odors of laurel oil and rancid, while concurrently imparting a favorable metabolic effect on cardiovascular health. Similarly, caproic acid (C6:0) and decanoic acid (C10:0) exhibit a distinctive fruity aroma, enhancing the rich, fatty flavor profile inherent in cheese (Hu, Li, Zhang, Chen, & Kong, 2022).

The concentration of MUFAs including C14:1, C16:1,and C18:1 in mixed fermented cheese was notably greater than those in single bacterial fermented cheeses along with the fermentation process. It was hypothesized that the mixed fermentation strains could promote the production or accumulation of these MUFAs. The liberation of MUFA primarily stems from triglycerides, likely attributable to the intensified fat hydrolysis process in cheese resulting from mixed fermentation, facilitating the effortless release of unsaturated fatty acids (Q. Chen et al., 2017). The content of PUFA initially underwent an increase, subsequently followed by a decrease, and mixed fermentation did not demonstrate any notable impact.

Overall, the outcomes demonstrated that the combined fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK had the potential to elevate the levels of MUFAs and SFAs to a significant degree. In other words, mixed bacteria fermentation could enhance the lipolytic capability of the bacteria, subsequently boosting the liberation of fatty acids and stimulating the generation of flavor compounds, which is in line with the findings of Xiang et al. (Q. Xiang et al., 2023).

Qualitative analysis of volatile flavor compounds during cheese ripening

HS-SPME-GC–MS was used to determine the volatile substances in cheese. As results, a total of 73 volatile flavor compounds were detected including 18 alcohols, 8 aldehydes, 17 acids, 9 ketones, 12 esters and 9 other compounds.

Acid compounds represent the most volatile flavor components in cheese, primarily resulting from glycolysis, amino acid breakdown, and the oxidative degradation of fats (Xu et al., 2020). Acetic acid and propionate, derived from glycolysis, progressively escalates as the ripening process advances in three types cheese. Upon completion of the maturation process, the mixed fermentation group notably surpasses the single bacteria fermentation group, potentially attributable to the facilitation of organic acid production by the mixed fermentation process. Acetic and propionic acids are characterized by their rancid flavor, which are essential for imparting a vinegary flavor to cheese and also play a role in curbing the growth of harmful microorganisms to a certain extent (Wang et al., 2022). However, butyric and valeric acids, resulting from fat oxidation, initially increase and then decrease throughout the maturation process. These acids could cause unpalatable, unpopular flavors when present in high concentrations, yet they contribute a creamy flavor when present in smaller amounts (Zeng, Wang, Han, Cao, & Wang, 2022). These oxidized fatty acids align seamlessly with the evolving trends of FFA, further strengthening the assertion that the mixed fermentation technology can significantly augment the generation of flavor compounds.

Ketone compounds are mainly produced through the degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids and the metabolism of free amino acids (Bancalari et al., 2020). Ketone compounds occupy a pivotal role in the development of the distinctive flavor of cheese, owing to their distinctive attributes of a low odor threshold and pronounced flavor traits, efficiently harmonizing and augmenting the overall flavor profile of cheese (Bu et al., 2023). 9 ketones were continuously observed during the entire process of maturation, with their concentrations occupying the second position among all the analyzed components. The compounds, consisting of 3-hydroxy-2-butyrione, 2,3-butyradione, 2-undecone, 2-heptanone, and 2-nonone, each demonstrating an OAV >1 and contributing substantially to the overall flavor profile, peaked in concentration towards the culmination of the ripening process. The notably elevated content in mixed fermentation, in comparison to single bacterial fermentation, can be attributed to the substantial rise in alcohols and SFAs present in cheese produced through mixed fermentation. 3-Hydroxy-2-butanone, a distinct volatile compound, arises from the catabolism of citrate, lactose, and amino acids, and serves as a pivotal contributor to the creation of delightful cream and fruit flavors (Song et al., 2023). 2-Heptanone and 2-nonanone, being the paramount ketone compounds in the fermentation process, are renowned for their distinctive, rich fruity aroma and herbal essence.

Alcohols typically exhibit delightful fruity and floral aromas, primarily generated via the degradation and oxidative breakdown of lipids, coupled with the reduction of base compounds during the ripening process (Xu et al., 2020). Alcoholic compounds rank third in the mature stage. Notably, the overall alcohol content of mixed fermented cheese in the latter stages of ripening exhibits a marked enhancement in comparison to that of cheese fermented solely by single bacteria. This phenomenon exhibits a notable consistency with the variations observed in the flavor precursors. This could potentially represent the beneficial impact of mixed fermentation on enhancing lactose metabolism, methyl generation, and the oxidative breakdown of free fatty acids. As some alcohols possess a heightened odor threshold, for instance, n-amyl alcohol, their concentrations may diminish over the course of maturation. This may be due to fermentation accelerating alcohol reduction, forming aldehydes or ketones, and may involve esterification (Y. Chen et al., 2020). Alcohols typically possess elevated odor thresholds, minimally impacting the overall flavor profile of food products. Nevertheless, they can indirectly modulate the flavor of cheese during fermentation, serving as participants in the response process (Gao et al., 2024). For instance, n-octanol, a product of fatty acid oxidation, effectively masking stale flavors at elevated concentrations.

Aldehydes flavor substances mainly come from the lipoxygenase-mediated unsaturated fatty acid oxidation process and carbon chain lysis catalyzed by lipid hydroperoxide lyase (Nanfeng Liu et al., 2023). The overall content of aldehyde compounds in the mixed fermented cheese exhibited a pronounced upward trajectory, significantly exceeding the levels in the Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK single-fermented cheese. This is precisely attributed to the fact that mixed fermentation facilitates the breakdown of fats, making it more favorable for the generation of aldehydes. Aldehydes exhibit an exceptionally low odor threshold and fall within a moderate concentration range, endowing cheese with its unique and appealing flavor (Yuxin Li, Cao, et al., 2023). Benzaldehyde and n-nonaldehyde emerged as the aldehydes exhibiting the highest concentrations throughout the cheese ripening process in the present study. Notably, benzaldehyde imparted an exquisite almond, floral, and nutty aroma to the cheese, whereas n-nonaldehyde endowed it with a delightful blend of citrus and beeswax floral fragrance (Merlo et al., 2021).

Ester flavor substances are mainly formed by the esterification of alcohols and acids and the oxidation and decomposition of lipids (L. Xiang et al., 2023). At the conclusion of the maturity phase, the maximum concentrations of ethyl acetate and butyl acetate were attained, with the mixed fermented group demonstrating a significantly elevated level compared to the Limosilactobacillus reuteri fermented group. This is attributed to the peak concentrations of acetate and alcohol achieved during the mixed fermentation process, thereby enabling the potential production of a substantial quantity of esters. Esters primarily impart a robust fruity aroma during the cheese fermentation process. For instance, butyl acetate, with its delightful pineapple and banana-like fragrance, could enhance the fruitiness of cheese at minimal concentrations, thereby contributing to the harmonious flavor profile of the cheese. The low odor threshold of the ester possesses the capability to effectively mask the unpleasant rancid odor of the sample (L. Xia et al., 2023). However, if the content of esters surpasses or significantly exceeds that of other flavors, it could markedly alter the taste of the cheese, imparting an unpleasant aroma.

Qualitative analysis of distinct volatile flavor compounds during cheese ripening

Identification of distinct volatile flavor compounds

Using the method optimized by Nanfeng Liu et al. (Nanfeng Liu et al., 2023), pinpointed 29 distinct flavor compounds in three types of cheese, comprising 9 alcohols, 5 ketones, 5 esters, 4 acids, 3 aldehydes, and 3 other volatile compounds. HCA, a prevalent clustering method, typically quantifies the similarity or dissimilarity among distinct objects by harnessing the distance metrics between groups within the dendrogram framework (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2024). In this research, it was observed that the 29 volatile flavors could be notably categorized into three distinct primary regions through the application of HCA.

The various flavor compounds generated during the maturation process exhibit unique characteristics. As depicted in Fig. 5B, it becomes evident that the A region of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei-fermented cheeses boasts an abundance of volatile compounds, encompassing isobutyric acid, isopentanoic acid, isoamyl alcohol and ethyl acetate. It can be observed that the principal volatile compounds in A region are predominantly concentrated in acids. Specifically focusing on the two branched short-chain acids, isobutyrate and isovalerate, their concentrations undergo significant changes throughout the entire ripening process. The isobutyric acid and isovaleric acid present in the Lacticaseibacillus paracasei fermentation group exhibited a negative correlation with leucine, stemming from their degradation pathway that is primarily sourced from valine and leucine (Bu et al., 2023). Region B boasts the highest concentration of volatile compounds within the mixed fermentation group of mixed fermented cheese, notably including dimethyl trisulfide, n-pentyl alcohol, 2,3-butanedione, and 1-octen-3-one. Remarkably, the presence within these components holds a crucial role in forging the unique aroma profile of the cheese. In the volatile compounds present in B, their content gradually augmented as the maturation process progressed, especially the ketones within. This clearly demonstrates that the mixed fermentation group is capable of progressively enhancing the biochemical processes pertaining to flavor compounds, exhibiting a remarkable consistency with its promotional influence on the production of flavor precursor substances. The presence of dimethyl trisulfur in the mixed fermentation group demonstrated a negative correlation with methionine, arising from its degradation pathway that is primarily derived from methionine. The volatile constituents present in Region C of the Limosilactobacillus reuteri fermented cheese include linalool, 2-heptanol, n-heptanol, methyl heptenone, isovaleraldehyde, butyl octanolactone, among others. It is evident that the concentration of differential volatile compounds with a fruity aroma in Zone C progressively diminishes as the ripening process advances, suggesting that the biochemical mechanisms impacting the flavor Lactobacillus reuteri fermented group predominantly occur in the early stage. Screening of pivotal differential volatile flavor compounds in the ripening process of cheese

The influence of volatile compounds on the overall flavor of cheese and the formation of characteristic flavor are not solely related to their concentration, but more importantly, on their specific odor thresholds (Xiang, Xia, et al., 2023). Volatile compounds with OAV ≥ 1 are the key flavor compounds, while those with 0.1 ≤ OAV < 1 contribute to modifying the overall flavor profile (Wu et al., 2021).

With heptanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, n-hexanol, 1-octene-3-alcohol, linalool, furfural, isovaleraldehyde, 2,3-butadione, 1-octene-3-ketone, 2-undecone, ethyl acetate and butyl lactate are pivotal differential flavor compounds based on VIP ≥ 1 and OAV ≥ 1. The concentrations of 2,3-butadione, 1-octane-3-ketone, 2-undecketo and 1-octane-3-alcohol in the mix fermented cheese were notably elevated compared to those in the single bacterial fermentation. 1-ocen-3-alcohol and 1-octym-3-ketone exhibited a discernible upward pattern throughout the ripening process, which to some extent reflected the change of the fat oxidation level of cheese. This observation aligns with the concept that the combined fermentation process could accelerate the release of free fatty acids. The OAV>20 values of 1-octene-3-alcohol and 1-octene-3-ketone, which are renowned for their unique, mushroom-like, licorice, and chicken-reminiscent aromas, play a pivotal role in shaping the distinctive flavor profile of cheese broth (Yao et al., 2022). The concentrations of 2,3-butadione and 2-undecenone peaked during the final stage of ripening, primarily resulting from the β-oxidation pathway of fatty acids and their subsequent β-keto decarboxylation reactions (Xiao et al., 2020). This may be attributed to the alignment with the prevailing trend of notably elevated MUFA content at the conclusion of the mixed fermentation process. They all emanate a distinctive citrus oil aroma and sweetness, imbuing the cheese with an exceptional creamy fragrance (Li, Yuan, et al., 2023).

The characteristic flavor substances of the Lacticaseibacillus paracasei fermented group include 3-methyl-1-butanol, n-hexyl alcohol, 2-furaldehyde and ethyl acetate. The variations in the concentrations of ethyl acetate and 3-methyl-1-butanol during ripening exhibited an initial decline, followed by a subsequent increase, with the Lacticaseibacillus paracasei fermented group notably surpassing the Limosilactobacillus reuteri fermented group in terms of these changes. This might be attributed to the heightened leucine metabolism facilitated by Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, resulting in an elevated production of isoamyl alcohol. Ethyl acetate and isoamyl alcohol, both possessing distinctive fruity and aromatic notes, play a crucial role in shaping the characteristic fruit flavor profile of cheese (Song et al., 2023). However, once its content surpasses the suitable category, it prompts the emission of a pungent aroma, thereby disrupting the harmony and equilibrium of the cheese's overall flavor profile. 1-Heptanol, linalool, 3-methylbutanal and butyl lactate together constitute their unique flavor characteristics in the Limosilactobacillus reuteri fermented group.

Therefore, the findings suggest that the combined fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK may lead to an increase in the concentration of aldehydes and ketones to a noticeable extent, while simultaneously decreasing the levels of esters with a low odor threshold. Mixed fermentation enhances the production of specific flavor compounds, namely isovaleraldehyde, 1-octane-3-ketone, 2-undeckeone, and 1-octane-3-alcohol, thereby fostering the development of cheese's distinctive aroma. Additionally, it minimizes the generation of undesirable flavors, contributing to the overall flavor balance of the cheese.

Correlation analysis and prediction of flavor metabolic networks

The variation in flavor precursor substances and volatile compounds exhibits a correlationwith the flavor formation of cheese. To achieve a profounder understanding of the intricate interplay among volatile compounds, flavor precursors, and sensory evaluation throughout the cheese maturation process (Zhang et al., 2023). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the relationship between selected differential flavor precursors (including organic acids, FAA, and FFA) and 12 key differential volatile compounds (OAV >1;VIP>1). Furthermore, the Mantel test was utilized to correlate 7 distinct flavors with 27 chemical components (Liu, Al-Dalali, et al., 2024). As evident in Fig. 7, there exists a robust correlation between key differential volatiles and flavor sensory evaluation. Additionally, a strong connection can be observed between flavor precursors and certain key differential volatiles. 2,3-Butadione, 1-octene-3-ketone, 2-undecanone, and 1-octene-3-alcohol, which are the hallmark flavor compounds in mixed fermentation processes, were positively correlated with an array of flavor precursor substances, notably lactic acid and specific FAA and specific FFA, including C4:0, C8:0, C18:0, and C14:1. This could potentially be attributed to the fact that 1-octene-3-ketone and 1-octene-3-alcohol are primarily derived from lipid degradation and oxidation processes, particularly those involving polyunsaturated fatty acids. Conversely, 2,3-butadione originates mainly from lipid metabolism and citrate metabolism pathways, further reinforcing the notion that mixed fermentation has the capacity to enhance the production within the lipid metabolism pathway (Wu et al., 2021). Ethyl acetate, isoamenthol, and n-hexanol, which are distinctive flavors in individually fermented cheese produced by L. paracasei, exhibited a positive correlation with C4:0 and C8:0 fatty acid derivatives of differential FAA, as well as a negative correlation with succinate and citrate among organic acids. However, heptane, linalool, and isovaleraldehyde, which individually constituted the distinctive flavors in L. reuteri fermented cheese, displayed a negative correlation with succinate among organic acids and C18:0 and C14:1 among differential FFA. This discrepancy may stem from the varying capabilities of individual bacterial strains in fermenting to yield phospholipases, which subsequently expedite the hydrolysis of glycerol phospholipids, resulting in diverse lipolytic potentials. According to the Mantel tests could be found that 2-eleven ketone was significantly associated with milky, acerbity and stale flavor (r ≥ 0.4 for Mantel, p < 0.05). On the positive side, the unique flavor of 2-eleven ketones, generated via a specific metabolic pathway, has the potential to enrich the creamy taste of the cheese. Conversely, the production of these ketones may lead to the consumption of flavor precursor substances, thereby diminishing the acerbity flavor. 1-ocen-3-ketone and 2,3-butanedione both exhibit a pivotal role in imparting the creamy and broth-like aroma to cheese, with 1-ocen-3-ketone notably standing out as a more prominent contributor to the broth flavor characteristic of cheese (r ≥ 0.6 for Mantel, p < 0.01). Additionally, a highly significant positive correlation was observed between furfural and the nutty flavor of cheese (r ≥ 0.6 for Mantel, p < 0.01), indicating that crucial flavor compounds exert a notable influence on the cheese's overall aroma profile. Observation of the fruit flavor in cheese revealed a significant positive correlation with heptanol, linalool, isovaleric aldehyde, and succinic acid, notably heptanol and linalool (r ≥ 0.8 for Mantel, p < 0.01). Although succinate lacks the distinct fruity aroma of linalool and isovaleric aldehyde, it nevertheless has the potential to influence the fruity taste profile of cheese. Therefore, it is speculated that the flavor during cheese fermentation is not solely attributed to individual volatile compounds but potentially influenced by the intricate interactions between these compounds.

To thoroughly investigate the disparities in metabolic pathways and alterations in flavor metabolites among three varieties of fermented cheese, this study constructed a Predicted Metabolic Network for Flavor Formation (Fig. 7B). Lactose undergoes glycolysis to convert into glucose, which can then be further synthesized into pyruvate, pentose, L-leucine, and L-phenylalanine by the action of enzymes. Pyruvate can be further converted into lactic acid, which enters downstream metabolic pathways to generate various acid flavor compounds, including acetic acid and propionic acid. Alternatively, it can be transformed into acetyl-CoA, thus entering the TCA cycle (Xiong et al., 2024). The TCA cycle serves as the pivotal hub that links the metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, and lipids. Upon conversion of acetyl-CoA into citrate and its subsequent entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, a fraction is channeled into precursors that impart a subtle acid flavor, whereas another fraction is metabolized into pivotal intermediates, including oxaloacetic acid and 2-oxoglutarate. Aspartate and glutamate, which possess the metabolic capacity to produce branched-chain alcohols, specifically 3-methyl-2-butanol and 2,3-butanediol, are unequivocally derived from oxaloacetic acid and 2-oxoglutarate, respectively (Zhao et al., 2024). L-leucine, a product of protein and lactose metabolism, undergoes a gradual transformation into isovaleric acid. This intermediate subsequently metabolizes into α-keto acids, which are essential precursors for the formation of volatile flavor compounds (Luo et al., 2023). Primary alcohol compounds in cheese, including 1-octen-3-ol and linalool, primarily arise from the degradation of unsaturated fatty acids, notably linoleic acid (Wu et al., 2021). Furthermore, their formation not only stimulates the production of the downstream metabolite 1-Octen-3-one, but may also be implicated in the oxidation process of certain straight-chain aldehydes, such as hexanal. Research observations indicate that key metabolites responsible for flavor differentiation exert significant influence on various metabolic pathways, and alterations in the levels of their precursor and subsequent metabolites exhibit a fundamental consistency.

Conclusion

This comprehensive study delved deeply into the pivotal role of mixed fermentation in modulating flavor precursors and flavor substances. The mixed fermentation with Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SMN-LBK and Limosilactobacillus reuteri LBK showed a more pronounced milk flavor and lower acerbity flavor than the single bacterial fermentation. The mixed fermentation enhanced the production of flavor precursors including organic acids, aromatic amino acids, and umami amino acids to a certain extent. Mixed fermentation also facilitated the generation of SFAs and MUFAs while inhibiting the production of certain bitter amino acids. These flavor precursor substances, in turn, can stimulate the production of flavor compounds via oxidation and decomposition processes. HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis identified 73 volatile flavor compounds in all samples, including 18 alcohols, 17 acids, 12 esters, 9 ketones, 8 aldehydes and 9 other compounds. Mixed fermentation increased the content of aldehydes and ketones and reduces the content of certain esters with low odor thresholds. Subsequently, the 12 pivotal differential flavor compounds were discerned utilizing the criteria of VIP ≥ 1 and OAV ≥ 1. Additionally, 2,3-butanedione, 1-octen-3-one, 2-undecanone, and 1-octen-3-ol may contribute to the formation of the distinctive milk flavor in mixed-culture fermented cheeses. The results of this study could provide insights into the mechanism of flavor formation during mixed fermentation of Kazak cheese and also provide a rationale for the targeted production of Kazak cheese with different flavor characteristics.
