Systematic Review of Kidney Transplant Outcomes in Monoclonal Ig Deposition Disease
Authors: Cihan Heybeli, Nelson Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHistorical case series of monoclonal Ig deposition disease (MIDD) after kidney transplantation (KTx) reported unfavorable outcomes. Recent case series support more acceptable outcomes in selected patients.MethodsWe performed a pooled analysis of case series and case reports using available individual participant data (IPD). We searched MEDLINE, Web of Science, SCOPUS, Cochrane Library, and gray literature. Recurrence-free survival, graft survival, and overall survival from the time of KTx were analyzed. Sensitivity analyses were performed according to publication type, hematologic response before KTx, exposure to novel agents, and era.ResultsEighty-seven kidney allografts were included from case series and case reports between 1986 and 2023. During the median follow-up of 46 months, recurrence occurred in 47 allografts (54%), at a median of 30 months after KTx. The estimated median recurrence-free survival was significantly longer in patients who achieved a very good partial response (VGPR) hematologic response or better. Graft survival at 5- and 10-years was 67.1% and 45.9% respectively. Recurrence was the main cause of the graft loss in the majority. Among the patients who faced recurrence, the median time from recurrence to graft failure was longer in those who received clone-directed therapies (62 vs. 11 months, P = 0.02). Overall survival rates at 5 and 10 years were 83.8% and 47.4%, respectively.ConclusionMore recent outcomes of patients with MIDD after KTx are favorable. Clone-directed therapies in recurrent disease appear to prolong graft survival.Keywords: kidney transplantation, paraproteinemias, recurrence

MIDD is one of the monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS) lesions in which light chains (light-chain deposition disease), heavy chains (heavy-chain deposition disease[HCDD]), or light and heavy chains (light and heavy chain deposition disease) deposit in the kidney in a nonorganized (Randall) manner.1 MIDD most commonly presents as a lesion within the spectrum of MGRS, in which the criteria for multiple myeloma are not met because of a low clonal plasma cell burden; however, MIDD can also occur in association with symptomatic multiple myeloma.2, 3, 4, 5 Progression to kidney failure is common and transformation into myeloma is a concerning possibility in the absence of clone-directed therapy. Evolution to end-stage renal disease is common. In a report from the Mayo Clinic3, the development of end-stage renal disease occurred in 38% of 64 patients within a median follow-up of 25 months. In another study from France6, approximately 20% (10/49) patients developed end-stage renal disease during a follow-up of 54 months, whereas 5 patients died. Some patients in older reports did not receive clone-directed therapy, negatively impacting their outcomes. Even with a deep hematologic response, renal recovery may not occur.7,8 Lower baseline glomerular filtration rate and difference in involved to uninvolved free light chain to < 40 mg/L were found as predictors of renal improvement.6 Similar to Ig light chain (AL) amyloidosis9, an early diagnosis and response appear to be crucial.10,11 On the other hand, some studies suggested that patients with MIDD with severe renal failure (i.e., stage 4–5) are more likely to have renal improvement than those with AL amyloidosis and severe renal failure.12 The former are less likely to experience acute kidney injury following an autologous stem-cell transplant as compared to the latter group. Another French study by Joly et al.,5 which included 255 patients, found that achieving a VGPR hematologic response or better along with the absence of severe interstitial fibrosis in the kidney biopsy predicted renal recovery. Even though a deep hematologic response does not always guarantee subsequent recovery of renal functions, it makes kidney transplant an option for further management.13

Earlier reports on KTx outcomes in MIDD were not favorable.14,15 Therefore, an effective reduction in light chain burden was proposed as a prerequisite before proceeding with a kidney transplant.14 Advances in multiple myeloma therapy have led to improved hematological response rates across plasma cell disorders, including the MGRS spectrum. Consequently, KTx has become a viable option in selected patients with MGRS. Many centers reported excellent KTx outcomes in selected cases with Ig AL amyloidosis.16, 17, 18 For MIDD, outcomes in more recent case series were much more acceptable than those in historical reports,5,19, 20, 21 especially in cases that achieved hematologic response before kidney transplant. This is attributed to better recognition of the “MGRS concept’’ and the numerous antiplasma cell treatment options, which have made complete hematologic response and KTx realistic targets.

Reporting the kidney transplant outcomes of every single case of MIDD is valuable because of the rarity of the disease and the lack of recommendations for patients with plasma cell disorders who undergo a kidney transplant, which has been published only recently.22 Using IPD, we aimed to provide a pooled analysis of kidney transplant outcomes of patients with MIDD who underwent KTx.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review using IPD following the Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of IPD statement.23

Eligibility Criteria –

We aimed to evaluate patients who developed end-stage kidney disease because of MIDD and underwent a kidney transplant. The literature on this topic included only case series and case reports. The primary aim was to provide a pooled analysis of estimated median overall survival (from kidney transplant), kidney allograft survival, and recurrence-free survival. IPD were extracted from published reports. Only full texts with available IPD data were included. There were no time limits, and publications through November 2025 were evaluated. The following patients were excluded:

Identifying Information

Databases were searched using related search codes (Supplementary Material). We searched MEDLINE, Web of Science, SCOPUS, Cochrane library (Central) and grey literature (Google Scholar with the first 200 results, conference papers, and captures from references of other included publications). All types of studies, including case series and case reports, were also included. One of the authors performed the search based on predefined criteria, extracted the data, and the other checked the data and reviewed the results independently. All publications were reviewed in accordance with the established inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Age, sex, MIDD type (light-chain deposition disease, HCDD, light and heavy chain deposition disease), clone-directed therapies before KTx, hematologic response category before KTx, recurrence data after the KTx, graft survival and overall survival rates, history of acute rejection, clone-directed therapies following KTx, and causes for death and graft failure were recorded. Other variables included donor type, induction, and maintenance immunosuppression. Follow-up time, graft survival, overall survival, and recurrence-free survival were available for all patients, whereas other variables were unavailable for some. Reports of 1 or 2 cases were accepted as case reports, whereas reports of 3 or more cases were considered case series. Some case series consisted of single or 2 cases that met our inclusion criteria, and these cases were included in the series.

Statistics

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Qualitative variables are presented as medians with ranges (minimum to maximum). Overall survival, graft survival (death-censored), and recurrence-free survival rates were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Comparisons between hematologic remission groups were made using the log-rank test. The time to each outcome was calculated from the date of KTx. People lost to follow-up were censored at the last visit. Pooled Kaplan-Meier estimates and median recurrence-free survival were calculated using IPD. Analyses were conducted using complete-case data for each outcome, without imputation. We used the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 28.0, IBM Corp.), and a P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Risk of Bias Assessment

For each case report, the risk of bias was evaluated using the case report guidelines.24 For each study, risk of bias was assessed across 13 domains (topic-related title, keywords, abstract, introduction, patient information, clinical findings, diagnostic assessment, therapeutic intervention, follow-up and outcomes, discussion, patient perspective, and informed consent). Each domain was independently rated by the authors as low, moderate, or high risk. For case series, we used the Joanna Briggs Institute tool.25 This included 10 domains (inclusion criteria, measurement standards, methods used for identification of the condition, consecutive inclusion, complete inclusion, reporting demographics, reporting clinical information, follow-up and outcomes, presenting site information, and statistics). Disagreements were resolved through a consensus process.

Results

Study Selection

Reporting Bias

None of the case reports satisfied all 13 criteria (Supplementary Figure S1). None of the reports included patient perspectives, and most lacked information on informed consent. Some of the publications did not have a title that included the words “case report’’, and keywords were also lacking in some. Eight of the 14 case series met the full criteria (Supplementary Figure S2). The following criteria were not met or were unclear: methods used to identify the condition, consecutive inclusion, complete inclusion, reporting of demographics, reporting of clinical characteristics, and reporting of follow-up or outcomes.

Patient Characteristics

Table 1 shows general characteristics of the patients. Four patients had LCHDD, 2 had HCDD, and the remaining 78 had light-chain deposition disease. The median age was 50 (interquartile range, 19–72), and 61.3% of the participants were male. Kappa was the involved light chain in 82% of the patients. Thirty (34.5%) of the kidney transplants were performed on patients who did not receive any clone-directed therapy before the kidney transplant (the treatment-naïve group). The majority of these individuals received a diagnosis of MIDD during the recurrence of the disease in their kidney allograft. Among the remaining 57 transplants, a VGPR/ complete response (CR) hematologic remission was achieved in 42 (48.3%) before the kidney transplant. The remaining 15 (17.2%) had no response (NR)/ partial response (PR). Hematologic response criteria were not specified in the majority of the reports. Regarding hematologic therapy before KTx, 22 of 81 patients (27.2%) received novel agents, and 35 of 82 patients (42.7%) underwent autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Donor type was deceased in 29 (33.3%), living in 47 (54%), and unknown in 11 (12.6%). Ten of 63 allografts (15.9%) were pre-emptively implanted. A standard 3-drug maintenance immunosuppression regimen (glucocorticoid, mycophenolate mofetil / mycophenolic acid, and calcineurin inhibitor) was administered to 89% of patients.

Recurrence of MIDD

During a median follow-up at 46 (3–302) months, recurrence occurred in 47 (54%) of the allografts at a median of 30 (0.5–302) months after the Ktx. The recurrence rate in treatment-naïve cases was 86.7% (26 of 30 cases), compared with 36.8% in treated patients (P < 0.001). The estimated median recurrence-free survival for the overall cohort was 46 months (95% confidence interval, 36–56). This was 30 (21–39) months for patients who were treatment-naïve before kidney transplant, and 84 (71–97) months for those who had been treated before kidney transplant (P < 0.001, Figure 2). When patients were grouped based on hematologic responses at the time of kidney transplant (treatment-naïve/NR/PR vs. VGPR/CR), the median time for recurrence-free survival was 30 (95% confidence interval, 10–-50) months and 96 (72–120) months, respectively (P < 0.001, Figure 3). The treatment-naïve and NR/PR groups had median recurrence-free survival of 30 and 37 months respectively (P = 0.433).

Graft Failure

Graft failure was reported in 30 of the allografts (34.5%), after a median follow-up of 29 (3–162) months. Only 3 of these cases did not recur, whereas rejection and acute tubular necrosis contributed to graft loss in the remaining cases. In all 27 remaining patients, histologic recurrence of MIDD was deemed to have had a significant impact on graft survival. The estimated median graft survival for the entire cohort was 107 months (95% confidence interval: 40–174). This was 50 (95% confidence interval: 25–75) months for those who had a recurrence, whereas the median was not reached for those who remained free of recurrence (P < 0.001).

Treatment of Recurrence

Among patients who had a recurrence, the estimated median time from recurrence to graft failure was 12 months (range, 9–15 months). Of the 47 patients who recurred, 4 (8.5%) did not have further follow-up, 27 (57.4%) received clone-directed therapy, and 16 (34%) did not receive anticlonal treatment. For patients who received clone-directed therapies for recurrence, the estimated median graft survival from the time of recurrence was 62 (0–160) months, compared with 11 (7–15) months in those who did not receive therapy (P < 0.02, Figure 4). Treatments of recurrence included bortezomib-based (n = 15, including lenalidomide in 3 and daratumumab in 2), lenalidomide-based (n = 2), autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplant (n = 2), thalidomide (n = 1), rituximab (n = 2), and conventional chemotherapy with melphalan and/or cyclophosphamide and dexamethasone (n = 5). Ten patients received clone-directed therapy after the KTx for a hematologic relapse or progression without histologic recurrence.

Time from recurrence to graft failure in those who recurred, comparing those who received clone-directed therapies for recurrence (red line) versus those who did not (blue line).

Overall Survival

Twenty-three (26.3%) of the patients died. Causes of death were hematologic progression in 10, secondary myelodysplastic syndrome or malignancy in 6, infection in 4, bleeding in 1, liver failure in 1, and unknown in 1. The estimated median overall survival of the whole cohort from the time of KTx was 117 (48–186) months. Median overall survival was comparable between treatment-naïve patients before kidney transplant and those who received clone-directed therapies (117 months vs. 156 months, P = 0.808). The median overall survival of the VGPR/CR group was not reached, whereas those with NR/PR had a median overall survival of 92 months (P = 0.108). The 5-year overall survival rates in NR/PR, VGPR/CR, and treatment-naïve groups were 70%, 90%, and 73% respectively. Details about rejection were available in 45 transplants, and 8 (17.8%) of these had acute rejection.

Discussion

This is the first systematic review of patients who developed end-stage kidney disease because of an MGRS lesion and underwent a kidney transplant. After the recent excellent outcomes of selected patients with Ig AL amyloidosis, the current review suggests that a selected group of patients with MIDD may also benefit from proceeding with a kidney transplant. Similarly, a deep hematologic response is a prerequisite before a kidney transplant. Patients who achieve a VGPR/CR before a kidney transplant had longer recurrence-free survival than treatment-naïve or NR/PR cases. A critical finding of this review is that patients who did not receive clone-directed therapy for recurrence had a graft survival of 11 months, whereas those who received therapy had a graft survival of 62 months after recurrence. The median time to recurrence, even in those who did not receive clone-directed therapy before a kidney transplant, was 30 months. At present, this may occur because MIDD is overlooked, leading to a delayed diagnosis that is often established only at the time of recurrence. Even in this aspect, patients appear to have a graft survival of approximately 4 years after the recurrence if they receive appropriate therapy. This meets the shortest acceptable life expectancy of an individual to get offered a kidney transplant.45 Although some centers may ask for a life expectancy of at least 5 to 7 years, some others may proceed with a kidney transplant for those who have a shorter expected survival according to the type of chronic disease.46 Despite significant differences in graft survival, clone-directed therapies did not provide a statistically significant survival advantage compared with the treatment-naïve group. This may be in part related to the exclusion of patients with symptomatic myeloma. In general, the MGRS spectrum does not appear to have a significant impact on patient survival, independent of severe kidney failure and drug toxicities, except in Ig AL amyloidosis, where cardiac involvement is associated with unfavorable survival. In the study by Joly and colleagues,5 the median overall survival was shorter in patients with pure light-chain deposition disease than in those with cast nephropathy plus MIDD (28 vs. 140 months, P = 0.006).

Because of the efficiency of modern antiplasma cell therapies, proceeding with a kidney transplant may be an alternative even in treatment-naïve cases in whom the diagnosis of MIDD is made later in the allograft. Significant differences in outcomes among patients from different eras (< 2010 vs. ≥ 2010) clearly indicate that more recent publications have reported outcomes that are significantly more favorable. This is likely because of the lower number of treatment-naïve cases, the availability of more effective treatment options (both before kidney transplant and for recurrences), and possibly better management of transplant patients.

In most case series, the median time from hematologic response to kidney transplant was unavailable. In a recent study by Garderet et al.,13 renal transplantation was performed at a median of 25 months with a range of 6 to 53 months, for patients with MIDD who achieve a VGPR/CR. We believe that proceeding with a kidney transplant without a waiting period is reasonable in such cases.

Limitations of our review include, but are not limited to, the inclusion of case series, the lack of detailed data on donor and recipient characteristics, and variations in the management of MIDD over the decades. The lack of detailed information on induction antirejection therapy precludes assessment of the potential impact of induction type and dosing on graft survival and disease recurrence. Histological characteristics during the course of recurrence would provide more information as more chronicity (higher interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy grades) is associated with worse graft survival in MIDD.5 Substantial clinical, therapeutic, and temporal heterogeneity across reports limit comparability and may influence survival estimates. However, there are only a few case series in this topic, and including them helped us provide a pooled analysis of significant outcomes. Although some details were not available for each case separately, the data and definitions for the primary outcomes were homogeneous and available across all reports. Despite the inherent limitations of case studies, this was a single-arm review of patients with a rare disease. Outcomes were binary, and time-to-event data were available for all participants.

Although randomized controlled trials are unlikely to be feasible in this setting, retrospective studies encompassing large cohorts of kidney transplant recipients treated with modern clone-directed therapies would substantially advance the field. Such studies should include detailed characterization of transplant-related variables, including induction and maintenance immunosuppressive regimens, as well as systematic assessment of the safety and tolerability of novel agents in the solid organ transplant population. In addition, comparative analyses of treatment versus observation strategies in cases of subclinical recurrence (which could be captured by protocol biopsies) could provide critical insights into optimal management approaches. Future studies will probably provide longer-term results.

The results of this systematic IPD pooled analysis support considering KTx for MIDD patients who achieve VGPR or better. The potential advantages of attaining a complete response over a VGPR are not well understood. Even in cases of late diagnosis and recurrence, available treatment options appear to be effective, thereby prolonging kidney allograft survival.
