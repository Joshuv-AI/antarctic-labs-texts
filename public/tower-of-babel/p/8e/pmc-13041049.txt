Hospital functionality in the Gaza Strip: analysis of the World Health Organization health resources and services availability monitoring system database, 7 Oct 2023 to 31 Oct 2024
Authors: David Mills, Yara Asi, Dennis Kunichoff, Erica Nelson, Juliana Nasser, Shannon Barkley, Ryan Arias Delafosse, Corey Scher, Osama Tanous, Weeam Hammoudeh, Bram Wispelwey, Nadine Bahour, Richard Peeperkorn, Egmond Evers, Lianne Gonsalves, Jamon Van Den Hoek, P. Gregg Greenough

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince October 2023, the destruction of Gaza’s health care system and the barriers to providing critical hospital-based health care services to the Palestinian population have been extensively documented.AimsThe objective of this study was to provide an aggregate and by-governornate descriptive analysis of individual hospital functionality and hospital system capacity over the first 13 months of conflict.MethodsThirty-five public, private, and non-governmental hospitals were analyzed from 7 October 2023 to 31 October 2024. Data sources included WHO ‘rapid response’ database and the WHO Health Resources and Services Availability Monitoring System. Data were combined to create a longitudinal and spatial representation of individual hospital capacity status (fully, partially, minimally, and nonfunctional). Background

Following the events of 7 October 2023, Israeli armed forces launched a military campaign in the occupied Gaza Strip [1]. The destruction has included over 300,000 munitions being fired into Gaza [2]. Repeated attacks and displacement orders have resulted in more than 1.7 million forcibly displaced Palestinians [3] with at least 65,000 people killed as of 17 September 2025 [4]. Hospitals have also served as points of shelter for internally displaced Palestinians given the frequent evacuation orders across the Gaza Strip [5]. As of February 2025, a report from the United Nations, the World Bank, and the European Union estimated USD 30 billion dollars in infrastructure damage [6, 7]. An interim damage report in April 2024 estimated 84% of all health care facilities were either damaged or destroyed [8]. There was an additional estimated USD 19 billion dollars in economic and social losses, with the health sector most severely affected (USD 6.5 billion).

The devastating effects on the health care system and the subsequent challenges to providing critical health care services to the Palestinian population have been extensively documented since October 2023 [9]. An initial blockade was imposed on the Gaza Strip to allow very little entry of goods into Gaza, with humanitarian aid flows being inconsistent due to restrictions [10]. Critical civilian infrastructure, including hospitals and clinics, have been extensively damaged, destroyed, rendered nonfunctional or partially functional, and regularly subject to attacks [11, 12].

To contribute to the understanding and documentation of hospital and health system functionality during this time, we undertook a longitudinal analysis of hospital functionality during the first 13 months of the conflict based on data reported to the World Health Organization (WHO) [13]. The objective of this study was to provide an aggregate and governorate level descriptive analysis of individual hospital functionality and hospital system capacity over time.

Methods

This study presents changes in the functionality of the existing 35 public, private, and non-governmental hospitals in the Gaza Strip following the start of the conflict on 7 October 2023 through 31 October 2024. Of note, in November 2023, Al Ranteesi and Al Nasser Hospitals were combined and reported as a singular hospital throughout the study period. Field hospitals opened post-October 2023 are not included in the study. Data presented comes from two sources: [1] a ‘rapid response database’ developed by WHO focal points to respond to quick, repeat changes in hospitals’ functionality in the initial months of the conflict; and [2] the Health Resources and Services Availability Monitoring System (HeRAMS). Each of these are described below.

Rapid response database: October 2023-April 2024

WHO’s focal persons liaised with the the Ministry of Health’s (MoH) Emergency Operations Center (EOC) in Gaza to obtain additional information, including governorate and facility-specific details, supplementing this with information obtained directly from facility focal points.

WHO focal persons developed a database to track changes in hospital functionality. Essential information on each hospital, including English and Arabic names, administrative governorate, location by GIS coordinates, bed capacity, present functionality status and date of the most recent change in functionality status were captured, while an additional text field allowed focal points to add details (when available) on what caused the functionality change on a given date.

WHO and hospital facility focal persons engaged in bidirectional communications to report updates affecting hospital functionality. Communication with hospital facility focal points was maintained via phone and messaging. WHO’s staff in Gaza and other health partners also provided updates when visiting hospitals in person. In these open-ended assessments, WHO focal persons determined hospital functionality by querying whether a facility was receiving patients, conducting surgeries, delivering other health services, verifying reported hospital attacks and the effect on health infrastructure and workforce. This database represented a pragmatic approach to reporting on hospital functionality. Systematic, more detailed reporting (including implementation of HeRAMS) was not possible in the early stage of military operations given frequent communication blackouts, rapidly changing hospital statuses due to intensifying conflict, and the displacement or self-evacuation of partners, including the MoH, local WHO staff, and hospital personnel who would otherwise support reporting.

Transition to HeRAMS: adapted instrument (April 2024 – June 2024); full instrument (June 2024 – Present)

HeRAMS is a standardized health system monitoring system, developed and supported by WHO, which has been deployed in various emergencies around the world, including armed conflicts [13]. The database provides timely and essential monitoring of hospital and health system functionality to guide the rapid allocation of resources and support. The HeRAMS reporting template may be tailored or scaled depending on a variety of factors that may affect the ability to report data.

In February 2024, WHO in collaboration with local health authorities implemented a light configuration of HeRAMS adapted for the Gaza context, with all hospitals reporting through this system by April 2024. With this adapted HeRAMS instrument, more detailed data was collected on factors influencing hospital functionality, including accessibility of the facility, availability of surgical services, and inpatient and ICU capacity.

By the end of May 2024, all hospitals had transitioned to using the HeRAMS instrument in its entirety. The full HeRAMS instrument allowed for additional detail on specific services available in each facility (e.g. noncommunicable disease, child health and nutrition, sexual and reproductive health). Consistent monthly reporting was also implemented across facilities, with hospitals generally reporting in the first week of the month on their status at the end of the previous month. In addition, facilities and WHO focal points continued with ad hoc reporting, where changes in functionality could be reported as and when they occurred, or confirmed via field verification. This version of HeRAMS continues to be implemented as of this manuscript submission.

Measuring functionality

HeRAMS has categorical definitions for what determines ‘functionality’ of a health facility, allowing for standardized data collection, consistent assessments of health facilities and their capacity, and evidence-informed response by health partners. Functionality itself is defined by the ‘absence of major or systemic issues impeding the ability to deliver the full range of expected services’ [14], and facilities can be either ‘fully functional’, ‘partially functional’ and/or ‘nonfunctional’.

In the rapid response database, functionality determination was not made based on the standardized HeRAMS framework. However, WHO focal points used the same functionality categories and definitions to guide their assessment. Additionally, for the rapid response database, WHO focal persons developed a novel category, ‘minimally functional’, in an attempt to account for specific nuance in functionality in the Gazan context. ‘Minimally functional’ was defined as “hospital under siege, inpatients remain, and health staff remain to care for [patients].” ‘Under siege’ implies no access in and out of the hospital, existing patients and health workers could not safely leave, new health workers could not enter, and new patients were unable to be accepted. Data analysis

This analysis combines the rapid response database with the later-introduced adapted and full HeRAMS instruments to create one timeline of individual hospital functionality and overall hospital system capacity between 7 October 2023 and 31 October 2024. For this analysis, we used the code of 1 for a fully functional hospital and 0 for a nonfunctional hospital. Given that partial functionality, a qualitative variable, could range in degree between 0 and 1 for any hospital at any given time over the study period, we assigned a value of 0.5 in the ordinal hierarchy between 1.0 (fully functional) and 0 (nonfunctional). For this analysis, ‘minimally functional’ ratings used in the rapid response system during the first months of the conflict were also folded into the 0.5 of the partially functional category, given the HeRAMS definition of ‘partially functional’ would include hospitals deemed ‘minimally functional’. Thus, our coding (Table 2) defined partially and minimally functional hospitals as operating at half the level of its full functionality.

In order to provide a proxy estimate of the contribution of any specific hospital to the overall hospital system capacity, we used the number of inpatient beds in each hospital prior to October 2023 as a proportion of the total number of inpatient beds within the Gaza hospital system. The total number of inpatient beds prior to 7 October 2023 was extracted from existing MoH data. The weighted contribution of an individual hospital to the overall hospital system after a given functionality change during the study period could be calculated as:Individual hospital’s present percent contribution to total hospital system capacity = Numerical functionality code * percentage of total pre-conflict inpatient beds.

Individual hospital’s present percent contribution to total hospital system capacity = Numerical functionality code * percentage of total pre-conflict inpatient beds.

Hospitals’ contributions were also calculated at the governorate level. For this analysis, total beds for each hospital within each of the five governorates were aggregated to create the respective governorate measure of contribution to the total hospital system capacity across the Gaza Strip.

Functionality changes were event-based before monthly reporting was alsointroduced in June 2024. In the dataset, functionality changes for a hospital were documented on the day of a given change, which resulted in a dataset of discrete events with dates grouped by hospital. For this analysis, we created a daily timeline from the start of the conflict, 7 October 2023, to 31 October 2024 and marked the functionality change dates from the dataset onto it.

To create spatial visualizations, tabular data were transformed into shapefiles in the World Geodetic System 1984 datum. All spatio-temporal data were managed within a geodatabase in ArcGIS Pro 3.2 (ESRI, Redlands, CA). All statistical summaries and data presented in figures and tables were generated in R [15].

Results

The 35 hospitals reported in the HeRAMS data contributed a total of 3,907 inpatient beds prior to 7 October 2023. The largest contribution to pre-October 2023 hospital health care capacity was Al Shifa Medical Hospital in Gaza City (n = 744, 19.0%). The total number of pre-October 2023 inpatient beds by governorate and their percentage of beds in relation to overall inpatient beds in the Gaza Strip are as follows: North Gaza, (n = 424, 10.9%); Gaza, (n = 1976, 50.6%); Deir al-Balah (n = 394, 10.1%); Khan Younis, (n = 920, 23.5%); and Rafah, (n = 193, 4.9%). Discussion

While several studies have reported hospital damage from attacks on and near hospitals across Gaza [17–19], the rapid response database and the HeRAMS initiative together provide a detailed understanding of hospital functionality and changes in health system capacity throughout the first 13 months of the conflict in the Gaza Strip. The functionality of individual hospitals—and in the aggregate, the entire hospital system capacity—decreased sharply after 7 October 2023. Hospital functionality continued to deteriorate, with some hospitals fluctuating between periods of nonfunctional and partially functional status, while no hospitals had full functionality after 18 October 2023. By early December 2023, more than half of all hospitals in Gaza were nonfunctional, which persisted throughout the period under study. The sustained and direct attacks on hospital systems, fuel shortages, lack of medical supplies, and access constraints due to siege and blockade have been well documented during the course of the conflict [20–23]. Hospitals are specifically protected under international law, and the findings of this study suggest a lack of protection of the hospital system as a whole during the first year [18, 21].

Key events which took place in the study period can be overlayed on to the observed changes in hospital system capacity. For example, the effect of a “complete siege” ordered on the Gaza Strip by the Israeli Defense Minister on 9 October 2023 had devastating consequences for the health system [24]. As early as 24 October 2023, WHO reports indicated that aside from the hospitals shutting down due to attacks, six additional hospitals were shuttered due to lack of fuel needed to keep the hospital generators running, following cuts of electricity to Gaza [25]. Affected hospitals included the Turkish Hospital, which served as the only oncology hospital in the Gaza Strip. The resulting functionality changes seen in this study demonstrate the effect of both miltary action and cuts to humanitarian aid on the hospital health system capacity, as local health care providers and responding humanitarian agencies (including Health Cluster partners) sought to prioritize which health facilities should remain open when utilities, medical equipment, medical supplies and consumables were limited or inaccessible.

The results of this study also demonstrate the sustained reduction in functionality experienced by Gaza’s hospitals. Our analysis showed that after October 2023, hospital system functionality for the entire Gaza Strip dropped below 40% and then fell below 33% by December 2023 without substantial recovery. At one point, close to 75% of the hospital system was confirmed nonfunctional. We found that 33 of the 35 (94.3%) hospitals reported being non-functional at least once during the study period. Fluctuations in humanitarian access, specifically the denial of personnel movement, fuel deliveries, supply deliveries, or entire humanitarian organizational missions, likely affected these variations and delays in a return to functionality [22, 23]. These same access constraints limited the ability to assess for safety due to damage and unexploded ordnance [22].

External reporting also provides additional context as to what increases/decreases in individual hospital functionality during the study period looked like in practice. In the case study of Al Shifa hospital, the largest tertiary care hospital in the Gaza Strip, an extensive military operation was initiated on 11 November 2023, lasting for over two weeks, and contributed to a fuel shortage, power outage, direct hospital attacks, and subsequent nonfunctional status of the hospital [26]. The hospital was able to regain partially functional status until a second military raid on 18 March 2024 rendered the hospital nonfunctional due to widespread infrastructure and equipment damage, patient deaths, and detainment of healthcare workers [27].

None of the hospitals were able to regain pre-7 October 2023 capacity by the end of the study period. Some hospitals, like Kamal Adwan Hospital in the North Gaza governorate, were rendered nonfunctional after military raids forced patients and staff to flee or led to their detainment [28, 29]. These raids also resulted in widescale damage to hospital infrastructure and destruction of medical equipment, as documented by the WHO and other organizations [17, 30]. Aggregated hospital functionality throughout the first 13 months of the military campaign provides an illustrative measure of the overall hospital system’s ability to maintain basic hospital services, and in many cases, provide civilian shelter and community support.

There are several limitations to this study. First, confirming hospital functionality was limited by the inability for on-site verification to understand specific hospital services available throughout the study period. Thus, specific clinical capacity measures—such as type of services or beds available (e.g. intensive care, neonatal intensive care, maternity, surgery) or non-hospital based care—were not consistently documented. Displacement of WHO and MoH staff due to evacuation orders or other safety related concerns also likely contributed to, at times, sporadic reporting. All of the above factors, combined with the intensity and variable nature of the military activity in Gaza, means there is a reasonable chance that that some functionality changes (both loss of service and attempts to reestablish services) are not captured in this analysis. Second, the implementation of first an adapted and then full version of HeRAMS in 2024 from the initial, rapid response database introduced a disruption in longitudinal tracking but steadily improved the consistency and detail of core hospital system service indicators, including many that are not presented as part of this analysis. We addressed this by reporting on only hospital functionality and our developed ‘hospital system capacity’ measure, limiting our analysis to the minimum data captured throughout the study period. Similarly, assigning ‘partially functional’ to the value of 0.5 limits the ability to capture the true spectrum of functionality. This is the particularly the case for the ‘minimally functional’ status which was used in the rapid response database because ‘partially functional’ was felt to be an overestimate of hospitals’ capacities. Al Awda hospital, for example, had a minimally functional status on 6 December 2023 due to having ‘38 patients inside with 95 health professionals, surrounded by tanks [with] no exit or entry to the hospital’ [report to WHO focal point]. As such, our calculations of hospital system capacity are very likely a conservative analysis of true, on-the-ground functionality.

With more than 24 months since October 2023, useful insight would be gained from a retrospective review of more recent, detailed HeRAMS data. This could be complemented by detailed qualitative case studies of individual facilities, tracking functionality changes, their causes (including the effect of evacuation orders, military raids, and sieges), and how facilities, staff, and patients adapted. The addition of humanitarian field hospitals to a more recent functionality analysis may also provide a more comprehensive insight into hospital system capacity during this period. HeRAMS was explicitly designed to be able to be rapidly deployable in a light configuration (collecting fewer iterative data points), with phased scalability ensuring more detailed data can be collected without compromising standardization or comparability. This scalability demonstrated in Gaza with the introduction of first the light HeRAMS adaptation in early 2024, followed by the full system later that year. Prior to HeRAMS, the use of an ad hoc system consisting of simple queries as to functionality and accessibility of a given facility, helped WHO and partners obtain the absolute minimum, necessary information to publicly report on the evolving situation and support the humanitarian health response, without being overly burdensome for facility focal persons under duress. Our analysis speaks to the ability to connect pragmatic reporting in the early days of a crises to full HeRAMS reporting, and to the utility of even lighter streamlined HeRAMS deployments for the future.

Nearly half of all hospitals (17/35) experienced some increase in functionality through the study period, with Fig. 1b showing some facilities which had only temporary loss of functionality, while others ‘flickered’ back to a higher functionality status multiple times within the same study period, or following long periods of being offline. Al Amal Hospital in Khan Younis, for example, went from minimally functional, to nonfunctional, to partially functional in under two months in early 2024: ‘24 Mar - hospital under siege and men ordered to exit hospital. [minimally functional] 26 Mar - hospital fully evacuated [nonfunctional]. 29 Apr - Emergency fully available, obstetrics and delivery fully available, laboratory partially, radiology partially.’ [partially functional] (Ad-hoc database, note input by WHO focal person following communication with hospital focal person). These rapid improvements resulted from efforts from hospital teams and surrounding communities to partially reinstate services under difficult conditions. Indeed, the ‘partial functionality’ under which the hospital system operated throughout the study period reflects facilities remaining open and health workers continuing to offer services. Cited adaptations included flexibility in space utilization and volunteering and task shifting of health service providers [31–33]. These factors, likely among others not captured in this analysis, contributed to the diversity of conditions, a wide variation of what ‘partially functional’ meant in practice, and the need for nuance when reporting using these categories.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the Gaza Strip’s hospital network was directly affected by the first year of the conflict. Protection of health services is mandated under international law.
