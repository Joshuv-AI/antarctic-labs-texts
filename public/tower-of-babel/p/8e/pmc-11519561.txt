Supercooled condensation droplets sliding on frost: Origin and anti-frosting effect observed on Cotinus coggygria leaf
Authors: Nicolò Giuseppe Di Novo, Alvise Bagolini, Nicola Maria Pugno
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryFascinated by the purple color, water-repellent, and self-cleaning properties of Cotinus coggygria Scop. leaves, we studied their morphology, wetting, and condensation frosting. Wax nanotubules confer high contact angles, enabling coalescence-induced condensation droplet (out-of-plane) jumping, which, as known, contributes to slowing down frost. Another type of movement—this time in-plane—becomes predominant in reducing the frosting velocity (vfrost) within a sub-cooling temperature range. Specifically, supercooled droplets slide toward the frost bridges upon contact, moving in the opposite direction to frost propagation. Between −11 and −2°C, Sliding on Frost (SoF) shifts from being rare to very frequent, reducing vfrost/vbridge from approximately 4 to 1, respectively. Using high-speed microscopy, we observed that the advancing contact angle of supercooled water on ice decreases with temperature. We describe the primary role of this behavior in SoF with a model that accounts for the forces involved and explains the observed transition.Subject areas: Fluid dynamics, Interface Science, Phase transitions, Physics

Introduction

Plants never cease to inspire researchers, e.g., self-cleaning and bactericidal surfaces or ones that collect dew and direct it.1,2,3,4 The well-known highly hydrophobic properties of some leaves are conferred by the presence of epicuticular wax that self-assembles into the most varied nanostructures.5,6,7 For example, the lotus (Nelumbo nucifera gaertn) leaf has been deeply studied for its water repellent properties. Here we study the Cotinus coggygria Scop., i.e., the so-called “Smoke tree,” leaf which evolved with very similar nanostructures, with respect to the lotus leaf, but in different environments, usually rocky and dusty. S. Herminghaus8 previously reported its superhydrophobicity, which is also found on a dried leaf, and highlighted its dependence on environmental pressure. Here we study its surface nanostructures, and the consequent wetting and report the property of coalescence-induced condensation droplets jumping (CICDJ), which can further clean the leaf from dirt and pathogens during dew formation3,9 and is also known to further delay frosting.

Regarding frost, it generally propagates in the population of supercooled droplets by percolation starting from a few droplets that freeze by homogeneous or heterogeneous nucleation.10,11 The mechanism is called ice-bridging because ice bridges grow from frozen droplets to liquid droplets, touching and freezing the last ones. At temperatures in a range below 0°C, water has a higher vapor pressure than ice and therefore a liquid droplet evaporates, the vapor mainly diffuses toward the nearest frozen droplet and desublimates, forming the bridge.12 What regulates the average speed of frost propagation is the diameter (d) and distance (L) distributions of the condensation droplets, while the effect of the substrate thermal conductivity seems to be negligible in comparison.13 The ice-bridging parameter S≡L/d determines the faith of an ice-bridge. A droplet that is sufficiently close to a frozen droplet and/or large enough is reached by the bridge and freezes (S≤ 1). If all the droplets satisfy this condition, frost propagates at ten to hundreds of μm/s. If, on the other hand, a droplet is sufficiently distant and/or small (S> 1), it evaporates completely before contacting the ice bridge, nullifying the rapid advance of frost by freezing.14 The latter case is frequent on surfaces where the rate of CICDJ is high,15,16,17 as space is freed up for the re-nucleation of small droplets, causing a percentage of ice bridges to fail and the frost propagation to slow down (some um/s).18,19,20,21,22,23,24,25 Instead, on surfaces exhibiting single droplet self-ejection,26 100% frustration of ice bridges can be achieved (S> 1 for all drops) and the average frost velocity is less than 1 μm/s.27

We here focus on the condensation frosting process on the Cotinus c. leaf at various temperatures, as frosts, in general detrimental for plants, are likely to occur in its vegetative phase. The dew jumping provides an anti-frosting effect, as known, but we have noticed an effect that further delays frosting, overlooked in previous literature. The supercooled droplets, when touched by an ice bridge, slide over it before freezing, for temperatures between 0 and about −8°C. This Sliding on Frost (SoF) contributes to increasing the area of the leaf which remains free of droplets. We have investigated this phenomenon, which is of general interest as it concerns the wetting of supercooled water on ice, difficult to measure in an ad hoc setup due to the instability of supercooled water and thus not reported for a temperature range down to −11°C. The condensation frosting process itself offers a good setup to study the SoF of a sessile supercooled droplet, by observing it with a high-speed camera coupled with microscopy objectives. In the following, the surface characterization of the leaf, the condensation frosting experiments, and of SoF, supported by a model of the forces at play, are presented.

Coalescence-induced condensation droplet jumping

In condensation experiments, frequent jumps induced by coalescence are observed even for drops with a diameter < 9 μm (Figure 2) which indicates minimal droplet adhesion. This peculiarity had never been reported so far for the Cotinus c. leaf, although it is a property one can expect from a superhydrophobic surface. As mentioned in STAR Methods, this small tree is frequently found on the hills and at the foot of the mountains in rocky and dusty areas. Despite this, especially in the first months of its life, the leaves appear very clean, free from dust. This cleaning could be activated by the so-called self-cleaning (lotus-) effect (deriving from the rolling of the rain droplets accumulating and removing the dust particles from the surface) but can also be mediated by CICDJ, as reported. Indeed, dew forms on a daily basis in the spring months. The impurities are incorporated in the drops, which then touch each other and jump off the surface, carrying the particles with them. The jump-mediated cleaning effect can in principle remove both artificial and natural particles such as silica particles3 and pathogens9

Condensation frosting experiments

We studied the leaves in the vegetative phase, which is most critical in nature due to common frosts taking place during the corresponding spring period of the year. The first experiments were carried out at −3°C, a likely nocturnal temperature for our location in spring. We observed a frost delay due to the jumps, as already documented for artificial surfaces12,18: the empty areas left by the jumping drops cause several ice bridge failures.

Another phenomenon caught our attention (Figures 3A–3C and Video S1): when a supercooled water drop is touched by an ice bridge it may not freeze on the spot but instead slide toward the frost. This sliding, arising along a direction opposite to the frost propagation, leaves an empty space like an “ice trail”, similar to the empty space left by the out-of-plane jumps, and further reduces frost velocity. This phenomenon was captured at −6°C in a frosting study with Environmental SEM28 but has not yet been thoroughly studied. It could have been overlooked by the large literature on frosting because surfaces are often tested at temperatures below −10 °C at which SoF is rare, as we shall show.

We performed other condensation frosting experiments in a temperature range from −2 to −11°C to find out until which temperature SoF phenomenon takes place (Figures 3D–3J) and to measure the mean velocity of both the faster frost branches (vfrost, see Methods) and the single ice bridges (vbridge) (Figure 3H). vfrost represents the paths with a high frequency of successful ice bridges and is higher for lower temperatures. However, the known vbridge increase with the degree of subcooling12 is not sufficient to explain an order of magnitude difference between vfrost in the range of −2 to −5 and that at −11°C. Videos show that SoF remains frequent (observed nearly in all the monitored droplets) for T > −5°C, associated with the droplet’s complete or partial spreading on the neighboring ice (Video S2); the free area left introduces a considerable delay in frosting, with vfrost lower than 1 μm/s. In the temeprature range of −7 to -8°C, SoF occurs only when the ice bridge size is similar to or greater than the liquid droplet size, and in any case, the shedding is reduced (Video S3); we observed a final (post-sliding) droplet contact angle on the ice bridge around 50°. At −11°C, the SoF becomes very rare (almost no drop shows it) and when liquid drop displacement occurs it is a small fraction of its diameter without complete sliding (Video S4). In order to identify a successful SoF, we have chosen as a condition that every point on the droplet contact line moves. At this low T, either no points or eventually only those at the front move.

The main role played by SoF is supported by the increase in the ratio vfrost/vbridge with the subcooling (Figure 3K). In fact, at −11°C the majority of the droplets reached by the ice bridges fail to slide on frost and freeze in place. The ice rapidly advances by a discrete length and globally vfrost ∼ 4 vbridge. At −2°C, instead, the bridges that successfully touch the liquid drops do not cause any net frost advancement since the totality of droplets slide on frost and therefore vfrost ∼ vbridge. Propagation by contact-freezing in place is less and less influential as the temperature increases.

It is always reported that the droplet freezes in place when touched by an ice bridge. In contrast, we observed SoF under certain conditions rather than the classical assumption of in place solidification and a significant role of it in the anti-frosting ability of a surface. In particular, SoF is more frequent for T closer to 0°C and when the bridge is wider than the drop. The mechanism that determines whether or not a drop slides on the frost is worth investigating and a criterion of successful or unsuccessful SoF would be useful to include in frosting simulations.

In a preliminary analysis, given its dependence on T, one can expect that SoF be hindered by solidification. It is known from the literature that the droplet freezing touched by the bridge occurs in two phases:

Recalescence

The liquid is supercooled and therefore unstable. For instance, either mechanical vibration or the presence of nucleation sites can activate ice nucleation. The ice bridge touching the droplet is an ideal nucleation site. It grows inside the drop in the shape of a pointed dendrite at the expense of the solidifying supercooled water. Its speed is about 0.15 mm/s at −1°C and 50 mm/s at −10°C, as reported in Kenneth’s Snow Crystals.29 It crosses a 100 μm diameter drop in 2 ms at −10°C. A universal propagation mechanism expanding the theory of Ivantsov was first proposed by J. Langer and H. Muller-Krumbhaar30 and verified experimentally.31 It is a heat conduction problem stabilized by the surface energy of the crystal–melt interface. For high supercooling, surface kinetics also play an important role.32

Freezing

Only a fraction of liquid solidifies during recalescence, depending on T. Given its high speed, recalescence can be considered adiabatic; the latent heat of solidification raises the temperature of the drop (solid dendrites + liquid) up to 0°C which then solidifies entirely starting from the substrate with which it exchanges heat.33 Complete freezing occurs in hundreds of ms in a 100 μm droplet at −T = 9 °C–11°C, two orders of magnitude slower than recalescence.

Both the recalescence and freezing phase could immobilize the droplet in place, thwarting SoF. At the same time, however, the phenomenon is influenced by capillary forces, as supercooled water tends to wet the ice well. It is therefore necessary to determine the role of solidification and capillary forces in the SoF phenomenon. The microscopic origin of SoF is investigated in the next section, suggesting a predominant role of the dynamic contact angles involved in capillary forces.

Droplet sliding on frost

To investigate SoF on smaller time and spatial scales we used a high-speed camera (frame rates up to 50 000) coupled with microscopy long working distance objectives. With this setup, it is possible to observe the supercooled droplet as it wets the ice with a certain advancing contact angle (ϑa,ice), it deforms and then slides (or not) on frost, as well as the growth of ice dendrites. We observed that ϑa,ice and the final contact angle the drop has when it stops and freezes (ϑfin) decrease considerably as T increases. These observations are of general interest, not limited to the current study, since the contact angle of supercooled water on ice affects various ice-related phenomena (supercooled water impact on pre-existing ice, objects sliding on ice, and so forth). Only a few articles report ice contact angles because of experimental difficulties: Knight (1966) first reported a not null receding contact angle of water on the ice of ϑr,wi = 12° at a T slightly below 0°C34; and an estimate of the equilibrium contact angle of water on ice (ϑwi) obtained by applying the a-polar and acid-base interfacial interactions theory is 24.2° at 0°C,35 in good agreement with the experiments of Ketcham and Hobbs (20 ± 2°).36

In Figures 5A–5Q three SoF events (correspondent to Videos S5, S6, and S7, respectively) are shown. For T closer to zero the dendrites slowly grow at the end of successful SoF, with the droplet already spread on ice (Figures 5A–5H); this event can be traced back to the case of Figures 3A–3C. At lower T, when SoF fails, dendrites grow on the spot. However, it is not the recalescence that blocks the droplet in place and causes the SoF to fail, according to several videos recorded in the range of −7 to -11°C (two examples in Figures 5I–5K, 5L–5Q and Videos S6, and S7). In general, the drop is touched and wets the ice with a high ϑa,ice and the front contact line advances a fraction of its diameter while the rear one does not move as ϑr is not achieved; the front contact line stops and the droplet oscillates in place before recalescence ends. The droplet oscillation occurs as the dendrite grows into the bulk (not toward its interfaces). This indicates that the droplet surface and contact area are liquid and thus the air-liquid surface tension is still present. Thus, the frustration of the SoF is due to a pulling force exerted on the drop by ice that is small compared to the retentive forces (mechanism 1). This can be due to either small surface tension forces (high ϑa,ice) or to a small ice bridge compared to the drop size and thus a short water-ice contact line compared to the drop-substrate contact line. Both factors can be combined. We define the adimensional k to account for the relative size. Assuming an ice bridge with a characteristic width λ and a droplet of diameter D, k≡λ/D. The event of Figures 5L–5Q can be traced back to the case of Figures 3H–3J both because of a large ϑa,ice and small k. The event of Figures 5I–5K, instead, is analogous to Figures 3D–3G and SoF occurs even if θ(a, ice) is large because of a large k. We also report a video (Video S8) of the subsequent freezing of a droplet with a diameter of 90 μm at −9°C; it occurs in ∼ 0.2 s, much slower than recalescence and SoF. Thus, also complete freezing does not play a role in SoF frustration or success. We measured the dendrites' mean velocity (vd) ± SD at various T (Figure 5R) (sample size is 12–55). The values agree well with the literature.29 The highest contact line velocities (vcl) measured, corresponding to the beginning of SoF transient, are much higher than vd. vcl provides the order of magnitude of the droplet translation velocity. Therefore, for further confirmation, even if the dendrites propagate toward the water-ice and/or water-substrate contact lines (although not visible in the captured videos), the drop has already had time to translate and wet ice. The capillary forces have the main role in SoF. Video S9 further shows that the dendrites grow toward the droplet bulk during sliding and do not obstacle the contact line motion or alter the liquid behavior.

In reference to Figures 6A–6E, let us illustrate a simple model we conceived to interpret the SoF dependence on T and k. The ice-bridge position in Figure 6A is not casual; it is often represented as a bridge with the bottom face touching the substrate, this being certainly true for hydrophilic and slightly hydrophobic surfaces. However, on the Cotinus c. hydrophobic leaf we repeatedly observed ice crystals departing from the frozen droplet's middle height and growing toward the center of the liquid droplet. This is actually in agreement with the ice bridge models12: its velocity vb∝1/δ where δ is the distance between ice and water, thus the actual bridge that forms is the fastest potential one, i.e., the shortest traceable bridge between two objects. When a drop is touched by an ice bridge, it begins to wet it, a supercooled water-ice-vapor contact line forms and advances on ice very quickly (hundreds of mm/s). The capillary force the bridge exerts on the drop (first term in Equation 1) is aligned along the bridge axis, directed toward the ice, and proportional to the surface tension force σlvλn projected along the axis with cosϑa,ice, where λn is the water-ice contact line perimeter, and n is an adimensional parameter accounting for the bridge cross-section geometry (Figures 6B and 6C). At the same time, the leaf surface exerts retentive forces on the drop (second term in Equation 2).39,40 The limit configuration in which retentive forces are maximum is the one in which enough water has been transferred onto the bridge to reach the receding angle at the rear part of the droplet (Figure 6C). The parameters b and n present various modeling uncertainties due to the non-ideality of the sliding configuration and of the ice crystals shape. For a droplet on a tilted plane, based on the deformation assumed before sliding, the parameter b is usually set in the range 1/2÷ π/2.40 Since in the present case the droplet shape and thus the surface tension directions along the contact line are not trivial and cannot be determined analytically, we assumed the entire range as likely. The n parameter multiplying the width λ, instead, depends both on the shape of the crystal (m) and on the bridge wetting extent (l); thus, n=m·l. The crystal shape depends on the T29; for plates m∼ 2, for needles m=π. While for frozen and liquid droplets of similar diameter, we observed the ice bridge to grow between the equators and to be fully wetted (l = 1), for large mismatches the bridge is expected to touch the substrate (Figure 6C) and to be wetted on top and lateral parts (l≳ 1/2) (Figure 6E). Therefore, we expect n in the range 1÷ π. Let us define α≡b/n which, by combining the b and n ranges, exists in the range ∼0.16–1.57 and accounts for both droplet and bridge shape. The data support the transition between a SoF and a pinning zone and we can infer that α = 0.7-1 is a candidate value to represent the phenomenon. From Equation 2, it is interesting to note that at a certain T, if surfaces with the same ϑa but different ϑr were considered, kc decreases as ϑr increases. In other words, the lower the contact angle hysteresis, the higher the SoF frequency and, deductively, the better the anti-frosting performance.

Further experimental investigations and fluid dynamics simulations may elucidate how the bridge and droplet shapes and substrate contact angles influence SoF (α). Note that n is presumably T-dependent. Regarding the origin of the ϑa,ice dependence on T, a possible explanation lies in the presence of the Quasi Liquid Layer (QLL) on ice41 whose thickness reduces from 15 nm at 0.03°C to 0.3 nm at −10°C.42

As said, SoF was previously recorded with an ESEM,28 although the authors did not argue on it. Their Video S2 shows frequent SoF events on a hydrophobic nanostructured surface cooled at −6°C, which aligns with our experiments and model. SoF is a general phenomenon that occurs on surfaces with sufficiently small hysteresis and at moderate subcooling. It is appropriate to consider it for future models and simulations of frost propagation, as the remaining free area and the increase of L (and thus S) induced by SoF has a significant effect on ice-bridging. The model highlights that for a given temperature there is a critical ratio, kc, above which SoF occurs and which ultimately depends on the microdroplets' dynamic contact angles on the substrate. The function sinϑa[cos(ϑa−Δϑ)−cosϑa], where Δϑ is the contact angle hysteresis, is peaked (Figure S3). Based solely on the angle function, a guideline to enable frequent SoF and consequently its anti-frosting effect is to use a surface with low hysteresis, regardless of hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity. For highly hydrophobic surfaces such as the Cotinus c. leaf, the indication aligns with the definition of a superhydrophobic surface (large contact angles and small hysteresis). For these, CICDJ is also possible due to low adhesion. Conversely, for a hydrophilic surface with small hysteresis, SoF would occur but not CICDJ. To delay frost at low subcooling with a hydrophilic surface would be interesting for durability aspects. However, this latter observation is dubious and requires experimental verification. The α factor could indeed increase as ice bridges do not grow in mid-air, potentially invalidating SoF for most droplets.

The other interesting aspect of the study is the trend of ϑa,ice as the T varies. It adds a piece to the water puzzle and helps explain other phenomena as well. In the study of H. Park et al.,43 it is reported that impacting droplets either penetrate or not into a frost layer, depending on whether the droplet is non-supercooled or supercooled, respectively. They propose that non-penetration occurs because the lateral wicking time of a droplet into frost is shorter than that of impalement and freezing. However, this might not be the sole consideration and a null contact angle of water on ice is a general assumption not supported by data. Our study suggests that a −20°C supercooled droplet should have ϑa,ice of at least ∼70°. Thus, an impacting supercooled droplet could achieve a suspended equilibrium configuration between the ice crystals. A warmer droplet, in contrast, would penetrate the frost as it further wets the ice. The sliding of millimetric saline droplets on frost layers grown on superhydrophobic surfaces has also been observed.44 In describing the driving capillary force, a zero contact angle between the saline solution and ice was assumed. Also, for this phenomenon, our study highlights the necessity of characterizing such contact angles with temperature, as capillary forces are quantitatively influenced by them.

The damage caused by spring frost on certain plant species and the associated ecosystem is a well-known phenomenon,45 and is growing due to climate change. In general, as described in the literature,18,19,20,21,22,23,24,27 a frost velocity in the order of a few μm/s is a key major indicator of the anti-frosting properties of a surface. In addition to SoF, of general validity, our study thus reveals excellent anti-frosting properties of the Cotinus c. leaf for typical frost temperatures in the vegetative phase due to the combined effect of CICDJ and SoF, enabled by the microdroplets' dynamic contact angles. Note that these two phenomena may also play a role in the removal of bacteria that facilitate frost nucleation.46 Illustrated in Video 10, de-frosting involves the re-establishment of the highly hydrophobic properties of the Cotinus c. leaf, causing the frost on the surface to shift and reorganize. Ultimately, with the leaf positioned horizontally, the frost partially peels off, leaving spherical droplets behind. A tilted leaf would result in the sliding of the partially frozen droplet.47 Moreover, Figure S4 shows a leaf under condensation frosting at −11 °C before and after a room temperature droplet impact. It rebounds and swaps the supercooled droplets, leaving a droplet-free area. All these observations acknowledge the superhydrophobic and anti-icing properties of the leaf. Together with the documented resistance to the freezing of its buds and bark,48 the Cotinus c. plant exhibits anti-icing properties that may have provided a significant evolutionary advantage. Further studies could identify links with the history of its native habitat.

Video S10. Sliding on frost atT= -4.2°C, 1600 fps

The present coalescence jumping and anti-frosting property study of Cotinus Coggygria Scop. leaf has shed light on the wettability of ice by supercooled water. We have observed that the frost propagates in an unusual way for temperatures between about 0 and -11°C. The drops sliding on the frost crystals leave more free space, added to that created by out-of-plane jumps, and further delay frost formation. We investigated the sliding on frost (SoF) phenomenon at different temperatures and with high spatial and temporal resolution recordings. Responsible for the transition between frequent and rare SoF are the supercooled water advancing contact angle on ice, the surface dynamic contact angles and the size ratio of the ice bridge and liquid droplet, as experimentally and theoretically observed. In addition, these results provide new water-ice contact angle data of general importance in several ice-related phenomena.

Limitations of the study

The ϑa,ice values were measured by analyzing high-speed videos where water wets ice bridges and curved frozen drops, which are not planar, smooth surfaces. Therefore, they are not the intrinsic values for supercooled water on an ideal ice surface, i.e., planar and smooth. As far as curved ice is concerned, we have considered ϑa,ice with respect to the line tangent to the ice. The effect of the roughness of the ice, on the other hand, cannot be eliminated. On the other hand, it can be said that since the ice is hydrophilic, roughness can only decrease the contact angle with respect to ideal ice due to the Wenzel effect. We therefore expect slightly larger angles on ideal ice. In future studies, a setup for systematically testing ϑa,ice on ideal ice may be devised.

Experimental model and study participant details

The Cotinus coggygria Scop. (1771) ‘Royal Purple’ (Figure 1A), native to the Mediterranean region up to central-west Asia, is a small tree that prefers dry environments such as bushes, hedges, calcareous rocks, arid coasts, thickets, stony places, but it can be found also in the woods up to 800 m altitude.49 It is very present in the forests of northern Italy where we conducted the study. The leaves color of Cotinus may be purple, green or red. We observed a high and unvaried hydrophobicity of the leaves from the beginning of the vegetative phase (between the end of March and the beginning of April) until when it blooms (middle of May-July). The pollen is indeed sticky and hydrophilic and modifies the wetting properties of the leaves. The leaves were collected in the Fondazione Bruno Kessler garden (Trento, Italy) in the period 26 April-8 May by gently cutting at the petiole and stored in sterile boxes (micro-electronic grade). Experimental tests were done on the day of collection.

Method details

Microscopy and wetting characterization of the leaves

The highly hydrophobic and superhydrophobic properties of the leaves (Figure 1B) are often conferred by the presence of microstructures covered with nano-structured epicuticular wax.1 Our first observations of the leaf structure were done with an optical microscope (AmScope, model SME-F8BH, 10× objective coupled with a eyepiece camera model MD130). In Figures 1C and 1D, the images in transmitted and reflected light, respectively. It is clearly seen that the microstructures (bumps) are located in correspondence with the cells. We evaluated the height of the microstructures by optical profilometry (Leica, DCM 3D). In Figures 1E and 1F we show a topographic map and the profile on a section, respectively. The small aspect ratio of the bumps (height/width ≅ 0.1) suggests that they do not play a decisive role in wettability (Wenzel’s roughness parameter rW≅ 1.02, calculated by the profilometer software). The parameters responsible for the low wettability of the leaf are instead the wax nanotubules scattered on the surface that we observed with a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope Jeol 7401-F (Figures 1G, 1H; S1A and S1B); the dry leaves were secured on the sample holder with a conductive tape and no metallic layer was applied. Figure S1C elucidates the micro structure morphology of the dry leaf observed in SEM images. We measured a tubules diameter Dt = 155.6 ± 18.5 nm and length of lt = 653.2 ± 115.1 nm (sample size is 10). The density is ∼10 tubules/μm2. The shape and size of the single nanotubules are very similar to the nonacosanol wax tubules of the Nelumbo nucifera6 (Lotus), Thalictrum flavum glaucum1 and Tropaeolum majus7 leaves while the density and arrangement are more reminiscent of those of the last two. Self-assembly processes of wax molecules lead to crystalline three-dimensional micro- and nano-structures that emerge from an underlying wax film, as documented by Atomic Force Microscopy.5,7

We characterized the advancing and receding contact angles of the Cotinus c. leaf by approaching a 230 μm diameter syringe and inflating and deflating, respectively, a drop of deionized water at a rate of 3 μL/min using a syringe pump (Pump 11 Elite, Harvard apparatus), recording with a digital microscope (Dinolite AM7915MZTL) and analysing the images with DropSnake, an ImageJ plugin. We called these contact angles ‘macro’ because the droplets diameter is in the order of 1-2 mm. ϑa,macro = 156.7 ± 6.5° (sample size is 7) and ϑr,macro = 127.3 ± 4.8° (sample size is 4), with a contact angle hysteresis Δϑmacro∼ 30°. The ‘micro’ contact angles (droplet diameter of tens of microns) measured from the sliding on frost recordings, as we explain in the next paragraph, are instead ϑa,micro = 139.2 ± 3.3° (sample size = 3) and ϑr,micro = 119.4 ± 11.9° (sample size = 7), with a contact angle hysteresis Δϑmicro∼ 20°. Note that ϑa,micro is measured as the droplets are slowly growing by condensation while ϑr,micro as the contact line moves at velocity of tens/hundreds of mm/s and thus may be affected by inertial effects. The ‘micro’ contact angles will be employed in the SoF modeling because are more characteristic of the droplets at play.

The dynamic contact angles of a “flat” layer of melted and rapidly cooled Lotus wax2 are ϑa = 162°, ϑr = 91°. The high ϑa indicates a not ideally flat surface, being the limit ∼ 120°.50,51 However, nonacosanol wax shows a high intrinsic hydrophobicity. Let us assume that also the Cotinus’ wax is nonacosanol, given the close appearance of the nanotubules with the three mentioned leaves. Given the high hydrophobicity of the wax, along with the advancing and receding contact angles on the Cotinus leaf, we can discern between the wetting states of the macro and micro droplets. Concerning the 'macro' droplets, the large advancing angle and the 30° hysteresis may indicate a Cassie state with high solid liquid area fraction, given that only the nano roughness plays the main role.52,53 For the micro droplets, a pure Wenzel state cannot be assumed as frequent CICDJ are observed, even for droplets with a diameter <10 μm (see Figure 2). Dew droplets growing on nanostructured surfaces must be in a Cassie or partial Cassie state to exhibit jumps, as observed with an Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope.54 The 20° hysteresis can be related to both high solid liquid area fraction and a non-pure Cassie state.55 It is plausible to consider a mixed state: Wenzel state in the central part (where the droplet nucleates between the nanostructures) and Cassie state in the outer part of the contact area.56,57 Also, micro droplets have a higher Laplace pressure than macro droplets, which could imply greater partial penetration of the menisci into the voids of the nanostructures and thus a reduction in contact angles.

Environmental chamber, imaging and analysis

We performed the condensation frosting experiments in a custom-made setup (see Figure S2 for the setup scheme) according to the following procedure. We introduce the desired humid air in a chamber (volume of 800 cm3) by mixing a dry air flux with a wet air one. The wet air flux is humidified in deionized water (18 MΩ-cm) through a bubbler. Each flux is set with a flow meter (FR2000, Key instruments) and the total flux is 800 sccm. The relative humidity (RH) and temperature of the mixed air (Ta) are measured with an Arduino BME280 sensor (accuracies ±3% and ±1°C) placed at the chamber inlet. The cold plate inside the chamber is cooled down to the plate temperature (T) by a thermostatic bath and two Peltier stages (measured with a thin film PT100 thermocouple, RS pro, class B accuracy). The PT100 is in thermal contact with the plate through a soft thermal pad (T-flex 600 Series Thermal Gap Filler, Laird Technologies, thickness of 1 mm, thermal conductivity of 3 W/mK). For the various T investigated, the ambient vapor pressure (Pvap) is set in order to have a constant saturation ratio s=Pvap/Pvap,sat(T) = 2.8 on the plate that assures a similar droplets nucleation density, where Pvap,sat(T) is the saturation vapor pressure at T. A good thermal contact is required and the placing of the leaf on the cold plate is not straightforward as for flat samples. Accordingly, the method we adopted to solve these difficulties is the following: i) to pour a layer (average thickness of 2 mm) of thermal gel (silicon oil based, thermal conductivity of 3 W/mK) on the plate with a syringe; (ii) place the leaf on the gel and; iii) press the leaf onto the gel, with a gentle stream of compressed air (clean room grade). The air flux flattens the leaf and shapes the gel underneath that achieve ∼1 mm thickness, ensuring full thermal contact. Given the small thermal inertia of the leaf (sub-millimetre thickness), its surface temperature is reasonably close to the measured T. Then, we cool the chamber while introducing dry air. When the desired T is reached, we introduce humid air and condensation starts. The chamber is equipped with an upper and a lateral glass window. We carried out experiments with the plate in horizontal position and recorded both from the top with a Dinolite microscope (model AM7915MZTL - EDGE) and from the side with a high-speed camera (Phantom V640, Vision Research) coupled to a microscopy objective (50X or 20x Mitutoyo Plan Apo infinity corrected, long working distance) through a tube lens (InfiniTube Ultima). A LED light (MULTILED QT, GSVITEC) placed outside the chamber, on the back of the samples with respect to the video camera, assures contrast in the high frame rate recordings. The images extracted from the videos were analyzed in ImageJ.

In the literature, there are several ways to measure the frost velocity (vfrost) but there is not a univocal way. To calculate vfrost on observation windows of few hundreds of microns, some authors divide either the length18 or the diagonal58 or the square root of the area22 by the time in which all droplets in the observed area are frozen (frost velocity of the order of tens of μm/s). Other authors, quantify the frosting rapidity as an area covered by frost per second (μm2/s).23,59 Others divide the area per second by the perimeter of the frost front (μm/s).21,60 In our experiments, given the large field of view of the top microscope (2783 μm × 2087 μm), several fronts with different velocities are observed (non homogeneous spreading). The faster fronts follow paths along where S is very frequently <1. In the other zones where S> 1, the droplets completely evaporate, a depletion zone forms and the propagation is slower. For each experiment, we identified some (3–6) directions along which the dendritic/ice-bridging front grows faster, measured their length (l) every 100 s, calculated their instantaneous velocity as v=l/100s and the temporal mean vt. We calculated vfrost as the mean of the various vt ± SD.27 The mean ice bridge velocity (vb) is instead calculated as the mean for several individual ice bridges by analysing the videos each 5-20 s. Sample size is 10–17. The error bars are standard deviations. We calculated vfrost with the faster fronts as they are a series of successful ice bridging events and, as we shall see, are representative of the SoF effect on vfrost/vbridge. The error bars of vfrost/vbridge are propagated standard deviations. Moreover, the faster fronts determines the millimetric scale connections between frost fronts started from different ice nucleation sites and are the weak link for the anti-frosting and anti-icing properties of a surface, e.g., the repellence to supercooled impacting droplets.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For both contact angles (ϑa,macro, ϑr,macro, ϑa,micro, ϑr,micro, ϑa,ice and ϑfin) and ice velocities (vfrost, vbridge, vfrost/vbridge and vd) the values are the means ± SD. The sample size of each quantity is specified in the Methods details and Results and Discussion sections.
