Modulation of glutamate metabolism in Niemann-pick disease type C1 mice
Authors: Antony Cougnoux, Carolina S. Alvarez, Andrea Gutierrez Maria, Emelin Hernandez, Niamh X. Cawley, Cristin D. Davidson, Forbes D. Porter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Niemann-Pick disease, type C1 (NPC1) is a lysosomal disease that results in progressive loss of Purkinje neurons. Previous work has implicated dysregulation of glutamate signaling as a potential pathogenic mechanism. In this study, we explore the efficacy of AMPA receptor inhibition and the role of SLC1A6, a Purkinje neuron glutamate transporter, in NPC1 cerebellar pathology.

Introduction

NPC1 results from impaired or loss of function of the NPC1 protein leading to the endolysosomal accumulation of unesterified cholesterol and lipids [1]. This lipid accumulation is associated with changes in neurotransmitter receptor levels or cellular localization [2,3]. Dysfunctional glutamate signaling is associated with cognitive and motor impairments, two symptoms observed in individuals with NPC1. Our group and others have previously shown that glutamate signaling is dysregulated in NPC1 [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Purkinje neuron loss is a prominent feature of NPC1 neuropathology and underlies the cerebellar ataxia observed in NPC1. For this study, two approaches were used to modulate glutamate metabolism. Our first approach was to investigate the therapeutic potential of perampanel, a noncompetitive postsynaptic AMPA receptor antagonist which has been approved as an anticonvulsant in children [8]. Typically, glutamate dependent excitotoxicity is mediated by NMDA receptors; however, for Purkinje neurons, glutamate excitotoxicity is primarily mediated by AMPA receptors [9], and increased AMPA receptor activity and impaired internalization have been proposed to contribute to NPC1 neuropathology [2,6]. We hypothesized that inhibition of AMPA mediated glutamate signaling might increase survival. Our second approach was to evaluate the phenotype of Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice. Slc1a3 and Slc1a6 are the major glutamate transporters in the cerebellum. Whereas Slc1a3, a glutamate transporter induced by riluzole, is primarily expressed by astroglia, Slc1a6 expression is restricted to Purkinje neurons, notably in the fructose-bisphosphate aldolase C (Aldolase C, Aldoc) positive subpopulation which are resistant to neurodegeneration in NPC1 [10,11]. Slc1a6 is elevated in posterior cerebellar lobules, similar to Aldoc expression (SFig. 1 A, B). These observations suggest that increased Slc1a6 expression might be a protective factor in NPC1. Thus, we hypothesized that Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice would show increased loss of aldolase C positive Purkinje neurons and manifest a more severe phenotype than Npc1−/−:Slc1a6+/+ mice.

Methods

BALB/cNctr-Npc1m1M (strain 003092, hereafter referred to as Npc1−/−) mice were initially obtained from Jackson Laboratories and Slc1a6 mutant mice were generated by Applied StemCell. A null allele was produced by removing exon 4 of Slc1a6. The targeting strategy and genotyping methods are provided in supplemental fig. 2. Perampanel (Cayman, Ann Arbor, MI), was administered intraperitoneally every other day at 4 mg/kg starting on day 21. Phenotypic assessments, immunostaining for Purkinje neurons, microgliosis, and astrogliosis along with nuclei counterstaining were performed and analyzed from 7-week-old mice as described [5,12]. The statistical analyses were performed in R (4.4.1) using Survival (3.8–3), Tidyverse (2.0.0), LmerTest (3.1–3), Survminer (0.5.0), and ggsurvfit (1.1.0). LMER parameters: (Disease Score in Npc1−/−) ∼ (Sex + Genotype + Age2 + (1 | Mouse_ID)). GraphPad Prism was used for some graphical representations.

Results

Inhibition of AMPA receptor function in Npc1−/− mice

Perampanel is a non-competitive AMPA receptor antagonist. Given that Purkinje neuron glutamate neurotoxicity is mediated by AMPA receptors, we hypothesized that perampanel would slow Purkinje neuron loss dependent ataxia in BALB/cNctr-Npc1m1N homozygous null mice (Npc1−/−). Although we did not observe a difference in phenotypic progression (Fig. 1A), mice treated with perampanel lived significantly longer (median 77 versus 73 days, N = 13–14, p = 0.013; Fig. 1B) than untreated animals. Evaluation of Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice

Slc1a6 is a glutamate transporter specifically expressed by Purkinje neurons and the expression of Slc1a6 is higher in Purkinje neurons resistant to neurodegeneration in NPC1. Thus, we hypothesized that impaired function of SLC1A6 in Npc1−/− mice would result in increased loss of Purkinje neurons and a more severe phenotype. To evaluate this hypothesis, we generated and evaluated Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice. Contrary to what we initially expected, the phenotype of the Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice was less severe. Phenotypic progression was delayed (Fig. 1C) and survival (Fig. 1D) increased in Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice relative to Npc1−/−:Slc1a6+/+ mice. Median survival increased 113% from 76 to 86 days (p = 0.046). Slc1a6 heterozygosity did not significantly impact NPC1 disease progression, and sex contributed minimally (PSex=M = 0.32, PNpc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− < 0.01, PNpc1−/−:Slc1a6+/− = 0.63). No difference in survival was observed between Npc1−/−:Slc1a6+/− (78 ± 9 days, n = 13, p = 0.422) and Npc1−/−:Slc1a6+/+ mice. Neuropathological evaluation confirmed our initial postulate that Purkinje neuron density (Fig. 1E,F) would be decreased in the posterior cerebellar lobules following loss of Slc1a6. In contrast, we observed preservation of Aldolase C negative Purkinje neurons in the anterior cerebellar lobules of Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice (Fig. 1F and SFig. 1C). Increased preservation of anterior Purkinje neurons was associated with decreased gliosis (SFig. 3 A, B) and may explain the milder phenotype observed in Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice.

Discussion and conclusions

The goal of the above experiments was to expand our understanding of the role of glutamate signaling and neurotoxicity in NPC1. We initially explored the therapeutic potential of perampanel, an AMPA receptor antagonist, in NPC1 mice. Although we did not observe a significate effect on phenotypic progression, our current data show a small but significant increase in lifespan of female Npc1−/− mice treated with perampanel. The sex difference observed in perampanel response could be related to the known sex difference in the metabolism of perampanel, with lower doses being more effective in females [13,14]. The limited solubility of perampanel precluded dose escalation to determine if we could establish efficacy in male mice. Although the magnitude of the effect was limited in the NPC1 mouse model and additional work will be required to translate these findings, our data suggest that AMPA mediated neurotoxicity contributes to NPC1 neuropathology and that inhibition of AMPA receptor signaling may have a positive therapeutic benefit in NPC1.

We also modulated glutamate metabolism by impairing SLC1A6 (EAAT4) activity. SLC1A6 is a glutamate transporter specifically expressed by Purkinje neurons [10,11]. Increased Slc1a6 expression correlates with increased aldolase C (Aldoc) expression (SFig. 1). Aldolase C positive Purkinje neurons have increased resistance to cell death in Npc1−/− mice [10,11]. Thus, we hypothesized that lack of SLC1A6 mediated reduction of extracellular glutamate would lead to persistent glutamate signaling that would be detrimental to Purkinje neurons. Consistent with our hypothesis and supporting the concept of glutamate neurotoxicity contribution to the NPC1 neuropathology, we observed increased Purkinje neuron loss in posterior cerebellar lobules in Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice. However, we did not anticipate that the Npc1−/−:Slc1a6−/− mice would have delayed onset of neurological impairment and prolonged survival. This milder neurological phenotype is consistent with increased aldolase C negative Purkinje neuron survival in anterior cerebellar lobules. We suspect that this may be due to differential sensitivity of Purkinje neurons to loss of SLC1A6 function. Perkins et al. [11] showed that loss of SLC1A6 function has a more profound effect on aldolase C positive Purkinje neuron survival in posterior lobules and that the SLC1A3 glutamate transporter, which expressed primarily by astrocytes, is protective of anterior aldolase C positive Purkinje neurons. We previously have shown that Slc1a6 expression is decreased in NPC1 astrocytes [5]. Further work would be necessary to characterize the independent and combined effects of altering SLC1A6 and SLC1A3 function and expression level on survival of Purkinje neuron aldolase C subtypes. In association with decreased neuronal loss in anterior lobules, a similar trend for gliosis was observed. This would be consistent with the concept that the observed gliosis is exacerbated by Purkinje cell loss [15]. In conclusion, our data supports the concept that glutamate neurotoxicity contributes to Purkinje neuron loss in NPC1 and suggests that other factors in addition to increased expression of Slc1a6 in aldolase C positive Purkinje neurons contribute to the increased survival of this neuron subpopulation.
