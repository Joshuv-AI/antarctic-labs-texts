Synthesis of a temperature responsive semi-inter-penetrating polymer network of poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum enriched with umbilical cord blood serum for wound healing
Authors: Reyhaneh Ranjbar, Jaleh Varshosaz, Fatemeh Baneshi, Mohsen Minaiyan, Mina Mirian, Majid Tabbakhian, Salar Nasr Esfahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human umbilical cord blood serum (UCBS) represents a potential reservoir of growth factors, neurotrophic factors, and cytokines, with anti-inflammatory properties. This study aimed to synthesize a temperature sensitive interpenetrating polymer network (semi-IPN) of diethyl acrylamide and xanthan gum enriched with UCBS for damaged skin tissue. The advantages of 3D IPN hydrogel are enhanced material-exchange, biocompatibility, and mechanical properties adjustability. The semi-IPN containing 1% xanthan gum exhibited the lower critical solution temperature of about 32 °C. 70% of the loaded UCBS in hydrogel was released within 24 h. The semi-IPN dramatically promoted cells proliferation and adhesion. 99% wound closure happened by the hydrogel loaded UCBS. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses indicated that the gel reduced inflammation, downregulated surface CD31 markers, increased collagen density, and accelerated re-epithelialization in the healed tissue, suggesting improved tissue regeneration. These findings highlight the strong potential of semi-IPN diethyl acrylamide-xanthan gum hydrogel dressing for effective management of open wounds.

Introduction

The umbilical cord (UC) is a fetal tissue that is typically discarded after birth, despite being a rich reservoir of biologically valuable components. During gestation, the UC functions as a lifeline between the mother and fetus, delivering oxygen and essential nutrients from maternal blood to the fetus, while simultaneously transporting fetal metabolic waste back to the maternal circulation.

Umbilical cord blood (UCB) contains a high concentration of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. Beyond these, the umbilical cord itself also harbors several classes of non-hematopoietic stem and progenitor populations, such as mesenchymal stromal cells, endothelial progenitor cells, neural progenitor cells, multilineage progenitor cells, and pluripotent somatic stem cells (Erices et al., 2000). As a result, the umbilical cord serves as an excellent biological source for applications in tissue regeneration, allogeneic transplantation, and other regenerative medicine therapies (Erices et al., 2000).

A significant component of UCB is the liquid portion known as umbilical cord serum (UCS) and umbilical cord plasma (UCP). The main difference between these two fluids lies in the presence of coagulation factors; serum lacks these factors, while plasma contains them. UCS is a rich source of growth factors, neurotrophic factors, and cytokines, which exhibit properties beneficial for wound healing, anti-inflammation, anti-aging, and anti-apoptosis (Erices et al., 2000).

Growth factors belong to a family of signaling molecules that are vital stimulators for cell proliferation and regulation of cellular activities as well as tissue repair. Among different biological sources that prevail in such factors—including peripheral blood serum and platelets derivatives—the umbilical cord serum (UCS) is considered unique with exceedingly high concentration of many potent growth factors including VEGF, EGF, PDGF, FGFs, TGFs, HGF, IGF and NGF as well as cytokines, chemokines, neurotrophic factors (Erices et al., 2000). This abundant bioactivity endows UCS as a particularly important element in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering interventions.

Hydrogels are networks of polymer chains with a high capacity to absorb water. When placed in an aqueous environment, they can absorb water and swell, maintaining the volume of water absorbed within the polymer network. They have a unique three-dimensional network structure, with the ability to exchange materials, good biodegradability, and adjustable mechanical properties, providing a suitable microenvironment. Hydrogels can be based on natural or synthetic materials, or a mixture of both (Wang et al., 2022).

Interpenetrating polymeric network (IPN) hydrogels represent a class of materials in which two or more polymer networks crosslinked with one other are interwoven at the molecular level and have found wide spread applications due to their enhanced mechanical strength and adjustable swelling behavior compared to that of typical single-network hydrogels. The family of semi-IPN hydrogels, in which one polymer is crosslinked and the other is physically trapped, are particularly desired for biomedical application. They retain structural integrity and are able to provide additional functions, e.g. better drug encapsulation and release. In this work, we examine a semi-IPN comprised of poly(diethyl acrylamide), a thermoresponsive polymer with an LCPST close to physiological, and xanthan gum (XG), a natural polysaccharide characterized by its biocompatibility and shear-thinning behavior as well as for its potential use in ECM mimicking. This additive combination is intended to give the hydrogel with best rheological properties for dressing wound. In addition, the hydrogel is supplemented with umbilical cord blood serum (UCBS) that contains growth factors and cytokines that are known to promote wound healing. In comparison to a passive scaffold the bioactive dressings are capable of actively promoting wound healing (Maity et al., 2020).

Polymer scaffolds are very important for tissue engineering applications because they can simulate structural role of biological materials in vivo, such as supporting and regulating cell actions, permeating nutrition and signaling factor to cells. Injectable hydrogels are less invasive than pre-formed scaffolds, which enables the in situ application of cell–polymer constructs into wound bed (Uppuluri et al., 2022). Their high-water-content and biocompatible nature have the ability to maintain a moist environment, promoting cell growth, differentiation, migration; preventing scab formation; as well as reducing wear and tear and pain.

Thermosensitive hydrogels are especially beneficial for skin applications due to their sol-gel transition at body temperature, facilitating application and in situ formation of a protective, conformal layer. A large number of thermosensitive systems including Pluronic F127 (Zhang et al., 2023) and its derivatives (Li et al., 2023), poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (Mecwan et al., 2023) (Chu et al., 2022), chitosan/collagen (Liang et al., 2022), among others, have been investigated for wound healing application (Hu et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2023; Lan et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2023).

Poly(diethyl acrylamide) (PDEAAm) is an attractive thermosensitive polymer. The IPN of poly(dimethyl aminoethyl methacrylate) has been used to retard the release of drugs (example, aminophylline), and a more compact crosslinked network were realized as compared with single networks (Zhang et al., 2011). In addition, IPNs containing hydrophilic polymers such as poly(dimethyl acrylamide) and xanthan gum have better water retention and gentler dehydration behavior (Hanyková et al., 2020).

Therefore, this study aimed to engineer a bioactive wound dressing by loading human umbilical cord blood serum (UCBS) into a thermosensitive semi-IPN hydrogel composed of PDEAAm and xanthan gum. We hypothesized that this semi-IPN network would provide controlled release of UCBS-derived growth factors, thereby enhancing their retention and prolonged therapeutic effect at the wound site, while offering a biocompatible and biodegradable scaffold.

Methods and materials

Materials

Diethyl acrylamide was purchased from TCI Company (Japan), ammonium persulphate (APS), tetramethyl ethylenediamine (TEMED), 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) and dialysis membrane were from Sigma Company (US). Xanthan gum was provided from Farabi Pharmaceutical Company (Iran). Alpha Cream (containing Lowsonia inermis extract) was from Rejuderm Company (Iran), human umbilical cord serum was purchased from Royian Institute (Iran). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution were BioIDEA (Iran).

Preparation of semi-IPN hydrogel of poly (diethyl acrylamide) and xanthan gum

A 2% by weight solution of xanthan gum was prepared and different amounts of this solution (0, 0.5, 1, 2 ml) were added to the reaction vessel along with 160 μl of diethyl acrylamide and stirred for 30 min. Then the reaction medium was degassed under nitrogen gas and 100 μl of 0.2% by weight ammonium persulfate solution along with 10 μl of TEMED was added to the reaction mixture. The mixture was stirred continuously and reacted for 24 h at room temperature. Then, dialysis was performed in deionized water for three days to remove the remaining unreacted molecules. For loading the hydrogel with UCBS, 8 g of semi-IPN hydrogel was thoroughly mixed with 2 ml of UCBS on a magnetic stirrer at 100 rpm speed.

Characterization of the semi-IPN hydrogel

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The identification of functional groups and the chemical bonds created were assessed using FTIR. In this experiment, the samples were combined with pure KBr and formed into thin discs. Absorption peaks were obtained in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 using FTIR spectroscopy (FTIR-8400, Shimadzu Co. Japan) with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and a wavelength range of 4000–500 cm−1.

1H NMR

The 1H NMR spectra of diethyl acrylamide monomer and polymer samples were recorded in D2O as solvent using a 400 MHz spectrometer (AV-400 Bruker, Germany).

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC)

Gel permeation chromatography in an aqueous medium was performed using a Waters 1515 pump and a Shodex SB-805 HQ column to measure the molecular weight of polymers. The data regarding the polymer's molecular weight distribution and related parameters were processed and extracted using the instrument's software.

Rheological properties

Rheological behavior of four formulations of semi-IPN with different contents of xanthan gum was studied by a dynamic rheometer (MCR, Anton Paar, Germany) with concentric cylinder geometry, CC27, with bob and cup diameters of 26.66 and 28.92 mm, respectively at 37 °C. Semi-IPN hydrogels underwent an oscillatory temperature sweep test with a constant frequency of 1 Hz and 1% amplitude gamma in the 25–40 °C range. The rate of temperature increase was 1 °C per minute. Temperature-dependent measurements of the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") were made. It was believed that the gel's melting or phase transition temperatures were situated where G' and G' intersected (Liu et al., 2020).

Inject-ability

A 5 ml syringe with needle (18 gauge) containing the hydrogel sample was positioned between the two jaws of the testing device. A load cell with a maximum capacity of 50 N was selected, as higher forces are not suitable for manual injection. The syringe plunger was compressed at a rate of 0.4 mm/s, and the peak force observed in the plateau region of the resulting force-displacement curve was recorded as the injection force (FMAX). Each hydrogel formulation was tested three times, and the average maximum force needed to expel the sample was determined. A formulation was deemed optimal if it required the lowest injection force, gelled in the shortest time, and had a phase transition temperature around 37 °C with minimal viscosity. For injectable hydrogels, the maximum permissible injection force is 30 N (Panyamao et al., 2020).

Gelation point

The turbidimetric experiment was conducted to determine the phase transition temperature or Lower Critical Solution Temperature (LCST) in semi-IPN hydrogels using a spectrophotometer. The samples were analyzed at a wavelength of 400 nm, as changes in UV absorption. This wavelength effectively reflects the phase transition of the hydrogel by increase in turbidity (Ribeiro et al., 2021).

The temperature of the samples was incrementally raised from approximately 25 °C to 35 °C, and the UV absorbance was recorded at each temperature step. The UV absorbance data were plotted as a function of temperature to identify the temperature at which the phase transition (LCST) occurs (Sawada and Takahashi, 2010).

Swelling

An initial weight of the semi-IPN hydrogels (Wi) was placed on filter paper with the weight of Wf. The samples were then positioned on a floating sponge within a phosphate buffer solution (PBS, pH 7.4). Periodically, the filters with the swollen hydrogels were weighed at various time intervals, and the combined weight of the swollen hydrogels and filter paper was recorded as (Ww). The Swelling Ratio was then calculated using the following equation (Song et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2021):(1)Swelling%=Ww−Wi−Wf/Wi×100

In vitro degradation

To evaluate the degradability of the optimized hydrogel, samples were placed in separate vials for analysis. Each vial was filled with 1 ml of PBS at three different pH (5, 7.4, 8.5), and kept at 37 °C for the duration of the study. A different vial was used for each time point. After 5, 10, 15, and 20 days, the PBS was completely removed, and the hydrogel in each vial was weighed. The remaining hydrogel mass was determined by calculating the weight difference for each sample. Degradation was expressed as the percentage of hydrogel mass remaining relative to the initial mass, with degradability calculated using Eq. (2):(2)Degradition%=Wo−WtWo×100

In this equation, W₀ represents the sample's initial weight, while Wt denotes the weight after certain time intervals.

UCBS release from semi-IPN hydrogels

The quantity of total proteins present in UCBS was determined using bicinconinic acid (BCA) kit (Kiazist, Iran) and analyzed with a microplate reader at 520–570 nm according to method provided by the manufacturer. To study the release of proteins of the UCBS from semi-IPN hydrogels a Franz cell containing 25 ml PBS (pH 7.4) and 0.5 ml Tween 20 to prevent the adhesion of UCBS proteins to the container walls was used. Filter paper was spread over the surface of the Franz cell and 1 ml semi-IPN hydrogel enriched with UCBS was placed to the filter paper at 37 °C. After incubation, 100 μl of the incubated solution was removed at predetermined time intervals and the released proteins in the PBS solution were quantified using BCA kit according to manufacturer's recommended protocol.

Cell proliferation test

The MTT method was used to evaluate the percentage of cell viability on semi-IPN hydrogels. At first, the scaffolds underwent sterilization using an autoclave at 120 °C, and a direct contact assay was employed to assess cell growth. In this research, L929 fibroblast cells from the Pasteur Institute of Iran's cell bank were utilized. Upon thawing, the cells were placed in a flask with RPMI culture medium (excluding 10% FBS), along with 100 U/ml of penicillin and 100 μg/ml of streptomycin. The flask was subsequently put in an incubator at 37 °C with a humidity level of 90% and an oxygen concentration of 5%. The culture medium was changed every three days. To assess the cell proliferation rate, 1 × 104 cells and 1 ml of culture medium were introduced into each well of a 24-well cell culture plate, which were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to enable cell adherence to the sample. After confirming cell adhesion, a defined volume of culture medium was added to each well, with three wells devoid of hydrogel acting as controls. After 24, 72 and 120 h, MTT (5 mg/ml solution) was added as much as 50 μl into each well for 4 h, and the culture medium in the well was removed as thoroughly as possible. The solution on the cells was eliminated after 4 h, and isopropanol was utilized to dissolve the generated purple crystals. To facilitate the dissolution of the MTT precipitate, the plate was placed on a shaker for 15 min, and the resulting solution was then moved to a 96-well plate. The amount of the dissolved substance in isopropanol was assessed using a plate reader set at a wavelength of 570 nm. Wells containing a greater cell density demonstrated increased optical density (OD) in comparison to wells with reduced cell numbers. The percentage of cell viability was calculated using Eq. (3) (Wang et al., 2017):(3)Cell viability percentage%=ODof sample/ODof control×100

The studied groups in cell proliferation test included: I) Control sample (cells in a polystyrene culture plate without hydrogel), II) blank semi-IPN hydrogel (25 and 50 μl hydrogel (1% xanthan gum)) and III) semi-IPN hydrogel 25 μl containing UCBS 5 μl UCBS and 50 μl hydrogel (1% xanthan gum) containing 10 μl UCBS.

Cell adhesion

For cell adhesion assay, the sterilized semi-IPN hydrogels were initially placed in a 24-well plate. Then, 2 × 104 cells were transferred to each well and incubated (4–5 h). Once the cells attached, the culture medium volume of each well was determined and the required culture medium was added. Then the samples were fixed using a 3.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 2 h, the fixing agent was then removed. The samples were rinsed twice with distilled water and dehydrated by passing through 50, 60, 70, 80, and 96% ethanol. In addition, cell morphology and adhesion on the surface of semi-IPN hydrogels were studied by Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (Quanta FEG 250, Spain). The pore size of hydrogels was measured using Image J software.

Antibacterial activity

Agar diffusion method was performed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the semi-IPN hydrogel (1% xanthan gum) containing 20% UCBS against Escherichia coli (E. coli) (gram-negative) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) (gram-positive). First, 15 μl of bacterial suspension (0.5 McFarland) was evenly cultured on agar and the hydrogel with a diameter of 10 mm was placed on the petri dishes. The petri dishes were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h (Mahmoodi et al., 2021). After that, petri dishes were photographed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of hydrogels by measuring the inhibition zones around them.

Animal studies

Eighteen male Wistar rats (weighing 220–240 g) at three months of age are purchased from the animal housing facility of the School of Pharmacy and were kept under controlled conditions of temperature at 25 °C and humidity at 55% with a light/dark cycle of 12 h each. During the experiment, standard tap water and food were provided to the animals. The experimental protocol was performed according to the animal welfare act at the animal house of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (ethical code # IR.MUI.AEC.1402.086). The animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injecting of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Prior to induction of the wound, the hair from the dorsum of the rats was removed by depilatory cream, and a circular full-thickness wound (1 cm2) was produced on the backs of the rats by a biopsy punch. To avoid any pain before anesthesia administration and skin punch procedures, 2–4% lidocaine gel was applied topically. The anesthetic effect manifests within five minutes of application. To avoid possible infections, 40000 units penicillin G and 5 mg gentamicin / kg of body weight were given intramuscularly on subsequent days until 5 days. The health condition of the animals was determined on the basis of alterations in body weight, appearance, water consumption, feeding behavior, and any deviation in their behavior during the course of the study.

The rats were divided into 3 groups each containing 6 rats. The studied groups were as follows: I) Untreated animals, II) animals treated with blank semi-IPN hydrogel, and III) animals treated with semi-IPN hydrogel containing UCBS. Treatment began on day zero or the day of wound creation, and the wounds in the treated groups were covered with 1 ml of semi-IPN hydrogel containing 20% UCBS every day. After 3, 7, and 14 days, photographs of the wound surface were taken. Additionally, after 14 days, the rats were euthanized with a carbon dioxide chamber, and the tissue samples around the wound were collected to perform histopathological tests, with H&E and Masson's trichrome staining. The evaluation of the closure of the wounds and pathological examinations, including granulation tissue formation, angiogenesis (by CD31 staining), presence of collagen bundles, and ultimately the presence of skin appendages, were among the criteria assessed for wound healing. To assess animal health and prevent distress induced by the pharmacological model, various health indices were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

The statistical comparison of the data was carried out through the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. All tests were performed in triplicate and the results were reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Results and discussion

Characterization of semi-IPN hydrogels

Fig. 1a shows the FTIR spectra of diethyl acrylamide monomer, plain poly (diethyl acrylamide, xanthan gum and semi-IPN hydrogel of poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum. In the monomer of diethyl acrylamide spectrum the peak at 979 cm−1 is assigned to the vinyl group (Maheswari et al., 2014). The peaks at 2975 cm−1 and 2931 cm−1 are attributed to the CH stretching vibrations of CH₃ group and CH₂ groups, respectively (Barron et al., 2016; JagadeeshBabu et al., 2011). The strong peak observed at 1640 cm−1 relates to the stretching vibration of the carbonyl (C=O) functional group (Chen et al., 2010; Qi et al., 2016). Symmetric CH bending vibrations are attributed to the bands at 1430 cm−1 and 1359 cm−1. The CN stretching of the monomer appears at 1260 cm−1 (Chen et al., 2009). The absence of the specific peak of 977 cm−1 and the obvious variation at 1640 cm−1 in the spectrum of poly (diethyl acrylamide) illustrate that the polymerization of the monomer indeed occurs (Fig. 1a). However, the slight shifts in the peaks of monomer during polymerization of diethyl acrylamide may be accompanied by some evidence to attribute them to cross-linking.Fig. 1a) FTIR spectra of diethyl acrylamide monomer, poly (diethyl acrylamide), xanthan gum and semi-IPN of poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum, b) H NMR of diethyl acrylamide monomer and c) poly (diethyl acrylamide).Fig. 1

a) FTIR spectra of diethyl acrylamide monomer, poly (diethyl acrylamide), xanthan gum and semi-IPN of poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum, b) H NMR of diethyl acrylamide monomer and c) poly (diethyl acrylamide).

Spectrum of xanthan gum, which is composed of polysaccharides and several functional groups, shows a wide and strong band at 3200–3500 cm−1. This region belongs to OH stretching of hydroxyl groups. A band at around 2900 cm−1 relates to CH stretching vibrations in sugar rings. A band at ∼1600 cm−1 is related to stretching vibrations of carboxylic acid carbonyl (C=O) groups. Other characteristic bands in the range of 1400 cm−1 and of 1000–1200 cm−1 are assigned to symmetric and asymmetric O–C–O vibrations in polymeric rings of xanthan gum (Faria et al., 2011) (Fig. 1a).

The FTIR spectrum of the semi-IPN hydrogel 1% xanthan gum shows a superposition of bands of the two polymers. The wide band at 3200–3500 cm−1 is related to the xanthan OH stretch and OH group of poly (diethyl acrylamide) with a slight broadening originating from H-bonding. The carbonyl (C=O) band at around 1620 cm−1 shows minor changes in intensity and position, which could be due to the integration of xanthan in the polymer network. Weakening the intensity of vinyl band (at 979 cm−1) indicates full monomer polymerization in the presence of xanthan. CH stretching at 2900–3000 cm−1 and C–O–C stretching at 1000–1200 cm−1 shows the existence of two contributing parts in the hydrogel (Likhitha et al., 2014; Peighambardoust et al., 2022) (Fig. 1a).

These changes of band position and intensity on the FTIR spectra indicate the formation of a semi-IPN structure. The hydrogen bonds formed between the functional groups of xanthan gum and poly (diethyl acrylamide) could promote the physical interactions, which would be beneficial for maintaining the integrity of the hydrogel.

Some very evident changes in the H NMR spectrum are observed implicating the formation of polymer on the polymerization of diethyl acrylamide monomer into poly (diethyl acrylamide). These include; I) the signal attributed to the double bond hydrogens between 1.5 and 2.5 ppm are disappeared as these protons are consumed in the polymerization. II) formation of polymer chain signals of backbone hydrogens can have different peak form and intensity, mostly broadened due to lower mobility of molecules and larger size (Fig. 1b, c).

Altogether, as described by Miguel, et al. (Miguel et al., 2014), these H NMR spectral changes provide evidence of polymerization, expenditure of reactive monomer groups, and the formation of rigid sections, resulting in broadening of peaks and the appearance of new regions in the spectra. Further signals broadening and dispersing are indicative of post-polymerization structural rearrangement. These modifications originate from the serial join of monomers in long polymer chains and the modified molecular dynamics of the polymer compared to the monomer (Fig. 1b, c).

GPC for determination of molecular weight of poly (diethyl acrylamide)

At the retention time of about 40 min, the chromatogram exhibited a peak, which dominated, indicating that the polymer exhibited a particular molecular weight. The molecular weight distribution of the poly (diethyl acrylamide) was revealed by its GPC data; the number average molecular weight (Mn) = 468.82 kDa and the weight average molecular weight (Mw) = 558.94 kDa. As a rule, the Mw is larger than Mn because the Mw gives more importance to heavy polymer chains, which increases their contribution to the average. The PDI (Mw/Mn) calculated was 1.193. This value indicates a rather narrow distribution in molecular weight, but there is still some dispersion present. Such a variation is not atypical and would be expected in many polymers, and simply indicates that the sample contains molecules of different molecular weight within controlled limits. This feature is essential for polymer applications, especially in areas that demand consistency in molecular characteristics, such as advanced material development or biomedical engineering.

Morphology and porosity of semi-IPNs

The SEM images of freeze-dried poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum semi-IPN hydrogels with different xanthan contents at ×200 magnification are presented in Fig. 2. The macro pores of hydrogels in semi-IPN hydrogels with 0.5, 1 and 2% of xanthan gum (Fig. 2b, c, and d) are larger than that of xanthan free hydrogel of poly (diethyl acrylamide) (Fig. 2a), which may result in the higher swelling ratio. In other words, the higher hydrophilicity of hydrogel due to larger amount of xanthan gum will lead to higher porosity, (hydrogel without xanthan gum had 52.3% porosity, 0.5% xanthan gum had 61.4% porosity and 1% xanthan gum showed 65.3% porosity). This is suitable for nutrition, adhesion, growth, and cell proliferation through proper incubation. The results indicate that water can easily penetrate the hydrogel network via polysaccharide chains, thereby increasing the swelling of the hydrogel network. Consequently, small molecules like water can readily move within the hydrogel and remain in these pores. However, at a 2% xanthan level, the network pores were slightly covered and filled by xanthan gum. It appears that increasing the xanthan level to 2% led to densification of the hydrogel network and a reduction in pore size, (2% xanthan gum 56.7%). Previous studies have also confirmed that with higher xanthan gum content, more compact pore sizes are formed compared to its lower concentrations (Jaberi et al., 2020). Phase transition temperature, gelation point and inject-ability

In the graph depicting UV absorbance at 400 nm relative to temperature, a significant alteration in absorbance was observed at a specific temperature (Fig. 3). This notable rise in turbidity indicates the phase transition temperature from sol to gel, implying that the liquid state shifts from a soluble form to a gel network, and the increase in turbidity correlates with a rise in UV absorbance. With the increase of the xanthan gum proportion from 0 to 1%, the semi-IPN hydrogel also exhibits an increase in the hydrophilicity, and thus the LCST (Lower Critical Solution Temperature) or sol-gel phase transition temperature increase accordingly. However, for higher xanthan concentration (around 2%) the overall structural change is compaction (JagadeeshBabu et al., 2011). Although more hydrophilic, such a structural densification resulted in a decrease of the gelation point. The underlying principle is that the smaller interface results in the reduced distance of the polymer chains which will induce the formation of new intermolecular bonds. This newly created connections promote the sol-gel- phase transition while lowering the phase transition temperature. Therefore, as a general trend, it is expected that an increase in the xanthan content should increase the hydrophilicity, which in most cases will also increase the LCST, while increased structural compaction at higher concentrations favors more intermolecular cross-linking and a resultant decrease in the transition temperature.Fig. 3a) The change of UV absorbance at 400 nm by changing the temperature in poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum semi-IPN hydrogel with different xanthan contents, b) injectability and c, d) the sol-gel transition of the semi-IPN hydrogel with 1% xanthan gum.Fig. 3

a) The change of UV absorbance at 400 nm by changing the temperature in poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum semi-IPN hydrogel with different xanthan contents, b) injectability and c, d) the sol-gel transition of the semi-IPN hydrogel with 1% xanthan gum.

Inject-ability testing revealed that increasing the concentration of xanthan gum required greater injection pressure and force to expel the prepared solutions from syringes. All samples exhibited injection forces and pressures below 30 N and 0.15 MPa, respectively, indicating they could be easily administered without specialized devices. Typically, injection forces for clinical applications are less than 30 N (Burckbuchler et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2018). however, the 2% xanthan gum sample approached the threshold of 30 N. The measured injection forces were as follows: 0% xanthan gum: 3.46 ± 0.62 N, 0.5% xanthan gum: 4.87 ± 0.40 N, 1% xanthan gum: 9.57 ± 1.05 N, and 2% xanthan gum: 22.45 ± 2.29 N. Results from rheological, inject-ability, or syringe ability tests demonstrated that the 1% xanthan hydrogel was more suitable for further testing compared to other hydrogels. The Fig. 3b illustrates the inject-ability of the 1% xanthan sample from a syringe. Fig. 3c, d depict the sol-gel transition of hydrogels (from liquid to semi-solid/gel state) as determined by the tube inversion method. The freshly prepared 1% xanthan gum sample behaved like a clear, low-viscosity solution at low temperatures (Fig. 3c). After several minutes of incubation at 37.5 °C, it transformed into an opaque, white gel (Fig. 3d). This sol-gel transition is attributed to the thermosensitive properties of diethyl acrylamide and xanthan gum in the formulation, which respond to body temperature.

Rheological properties of semi-IPN hydrogels

The storage modulus (G'), and the loss modulus (G") were determined as useful parameters to probe the elastic and viscous behavior of the hydrogels at the applied strain. The strain was changed from 0.1% to 100% to determine the linear viscoelastic region, yield point, and structural failure region of the hydrogels. The resulting curves (in which the moduli G' and G" are represented as a function of the strain are shown in Fig. 4a. Storage Modulus (G') represents the elastic behavior and stiffness of the material, while loss modulus (G") reflects the viscous characteristics and energy dissipation. In all semi-IPN hydrogels, at low strain ranges, both moduli (G' and G") remained relatively constant and high, signifying a linear viscoelastic region and structural stability. This observation indicates that the samples exhibited elastic behavior under these conditions, with the internal polymer network remaining intact. As the strain increased, a significant reduction in both moduli was observed, marking the transition of the samples into the nonlinear regime and the onset of structural network degradation (i.e., structural failure). This decline reflects the loss of elastic properties and the initiation of non-recoverable flow behavior. The differences in the magnitudes of G' and G" among the four hydrogels highlight the influence of their distinct polymer compositions, so that in hydrogels without xanthan gum and the semi-IPN hydrogel with 0.5% xanthan gum, the lowest storage modulus (G') was seen, indicative of a relatively weaker structure and reduced stiffness. Their lower loss modulus (G") further suggests diminished viscous dissipation or a less cohesive network (Fig. 4a). In the semi-IPN hydrogel containing 1% xanthan gum, the observed higher G' and G" values, when compared to the hydrogel with either no xanthan gum or 0.5% xanthan gum, suggest an improvement in structural rigidity and a greater dominance of elasticity (Fig. 4a). The semi-IPN hydrogel with 2% xanthan gum displayed the highest storage modulus (G'), signifying superior stiffness and mechanical strength within the group. This result is likely attributed to the increased xanthan content in its formulation (Fig. 4a). Fig. 4b shows the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") recorded under gradually increasing of shear stress to determine the structural breakdown threshold, the point at which the polymer network destabilizes and disintegrates. As this figure shows in hydrogel lacking xanthan gum [i.e., just poly (diethyl acrylamide)] the lowest resistance to shear stress was seen. Both G' and G" remained stable up to approximately 1000 Pa, with negligible reduction. Structural breakdown initiated at ∼1000 Pa, marked by a significant decline in G' (Fig. 4b). The semi-IPN hydrogel containing 0.5% xanthan gum demonstrated superior structural resistance compared to the hydrogel lacking xanthan gum, with disintegration onset occurring at ∼4000 Pa (Fig. 4b). The semi-IPN hydrogel containing 1% xanthan gum showed a sharp reduction in G' and G" at ∼10,000 Pa, indicating reduced structural stability. This sample displayed higher shear tolerance and lower sensitivity to applied loads (Fig. 4b). Eventually, increasing the xanthan gum to 2% in semi-IPN hydrogel exhibited shear resistance comparable to the sample containing 1% xanthan gum. Structural failure began at ∼10,000 Pa, followed by rapid moduli decline (Fig. 4b). A stronger polymer composition and more cohesive molecular network require higher shear stress to induce structural disintegration. Analysis of the structural breakdown thresholds across the four formulations revealed that increasing xanthan content enhances hydrogel strength up to 1%. However, beyond this concentration, no incremental improvement in mechanical robustness was observed (Fig. 4b).

Fig. 4c indicates changes of the storage modulus (G'), loss modulus (G"), and complex viscosity (η*) as a function of frequency within the range of 0.1 to 100 Hz. According to the graphs, it is observed that in all formulations, the complex viscosity (η*) is very high at low frequencies and decreases significantly with increasing frequency, indicating the pseudo viscous behavior of hydrogels. Complex viscosity (η*) is a rheological parameter that reflects the viscoelastic response of samples to oscillatory deformations at varying frequencies. Complex viscosity is a parameter that describes the combined effects of viscosity (flow-ability) and elasticity of the material under dynamic experimental conditions. In fact, when a sample is subjected to an oscillatory stress at a specific frequency, its response is a combination of elastic behavior (linear and energy storage) and viscous behavior (energy dissipation). Complex viscosity is a measure of the sample's resistance to oscillatory flow, which includes both the energy storage components (elastic) and the energy dissipation components (viscous). The values of complex viscosity (η*) and its decrease with increasing frequency (frequency-dependent) indicate that the samples exhibit non-Newtonian viscoelastic behavior. The decrease in complex viscosity with increasing frequency indicates that the samples become softer under faster movement or dynamic loading conditions. Given that the test was performed at 37 °C (above the phase transition temperature of the hydrogels as shown in Fig. 3), the higher storage modulus (G') compared to the loss modulus (G") (Fig. 4c) in all samples indicates that the dominant behavior of the material is more elastic than viscous.

The storage modulus (G′) refers to the material's capacity to store energy (elasticity), meaning how well the material is capable of storing the input energy as a spring and to return to the initial state after removal of force. Conversely, G′′ represents the loss modulus, which indicates the energy loss or the non-recoverable energy within the material. It reflects how the material undergoes permanent deformation and the energy loss, which is dissipated as heat, similar to a viscous liquid. In Fig. 4c, where G′ exceeds G′′, the samples demonstrate that they exhibit elastic solid characteristics to a greater degree and viscous liquid properties to a lesser degree. This type of response is usually found in systems like gels, hydrogels or cross-linked polymers that have an organized and stable molecular structure, so that they can restore their initial shape. It ensures that G′ outperformed G′′, specifically, all samples predominantly exhibit elastic-solid state characteristics, possessing the capability to store energy while maintaining a highly stabilized network as much as possible. Increasing the xanthan percentage enhanced this elastic behavior and strengthens the network (Fig. 4c).

Swelling behavior of semi-IPN hydrogels

Swelling is the characteristic property of hydrogels that results from the absorption of solvent into the polymer network and the resulting macroscopic expansion of the hydrogel structure. This feature is especially important for use in tissue engineering as a manner for supplying a moist environment that allows for diffusion of nutrients to implanted cells upon in vitro incubation (Kankala et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 5a all hydrogel formulations reached over 80% of their maximum swelling within the first hour. Hydrogels can hold a lot of water within their 3D structure without dissolving in the surrounding medium. The amount of water held within the hydrogel in its swollen state is determined by the properties of the polymeric system. Hydrogels have hydrophilic functional groups along their polymer backbone, which have a high affinity for water molecules and can absorb and hold water within their structure (Khan et al., 2021). Adding xanthan gum to a clean hydrogel makes it swell more than the plain hydrogel (Fig. 5a). In fact, the more xanthan gum in the hydrogel, the more water it can absorb and swell. Notably, the more xanthan gum in the hydrogel structure, the higher the swelling capacity and water uptake. This can be attributed to the higher concentration of hydrophilic moieties (hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) associated with higher polysaccharide content, which can interact with water molecules and increase water absorption (Miguel et al., 2014; Pasparakis and Bouropoulos, 2006). Therefore, among the hydrogels tested, semi-IPN hydrogel with 2% xanthan gum had the highest water uptake followed by hydrogel with 1% and 0.5% xanthan gum and the least swelling was seen in the plain hydrogels (without xanthan) as shown in Fig. 5a. Prior investigations have reported analogous findings for other polysaccharide-based hydrogels (da Silva et al., 2016). Overall, all the fabricated hydrogels demonstrated the capacity to provide a moist environment and enhance cellular adhesion, owing to their inherent hydrophilic nature, while simultaneously preventing the accumulation of exudates at the wound site (Smeds and Grinstaff, 2001; Shahbuddin et al., 2013).Fig. 5a) Swelling behavior, b) degradation rate of semi-IPN hydrogel with different xanthan gum content, c) degradation rate of semi-IPN hydrogel with 1% xanthan gum in different pH and d) umbilical cord blood serum release from poly (diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum semi-IPN hydrogel with different xanthan contents.Fig. 5

Degradation pattern of semi-IPN hydrogels

To investigate the degradability of hydrogels with varying xanthan gum contents, samples were incubated in phosphate buffer at 37.5 °C for 3, 7, 14, and 20 days at three different pH values: skin pH (5.0), physiological pH (7.4), and alkaline pH (8.5). The effect of different contents of xanthan gum gum in semi-IPN hydrogels on degradation rate was also studied. The sample without xanthan gum (0%) exhibited significantly slower degradation (Fig. 5b), with only 40% of the total hydrogel weight lost after 20 days. This slower degradation occurred because the absence of xanthan gum, a polysaccharide compound, allowed the phosphate buffer to more easily penetrate and leach out of the hydrogel. Additionally, our experiment revealed that the physical crosslinking between DEAAM molecules was stronger than that between DEAAM and xanthan gum. The weaker physical crosslinking observed with xanthan gum suggests that the interactions between DEAAM and xanthan are not as robust. We found that increasing the xanthan gum content reduced the degradation rate, which is attributed to the formation of a more cohesive network structure (Disha et al., 2016). The degradation rate reduced under weak acid condition (pH 5.0) (Fig. 5c). This is due to the partial protonation of the carboxylate groups in the xanthan gum structure. The decrease of negative charge in polymer chain facilitated the lower electrostatic repulsion among chains which resulted a denser network structure with less swelling and limited water penetration into hydrogel matrix that leads to slower degradation. In contrast, under mildly alkaline conditions (pH 8.5), the increased ionization degree of carboxyl groups enhanced electrostatic repulsion, leading to a more open network, increased water uptake, and facilitated penetration of the medium into the hydrogel structure, which ultimately resulted in faster degradation (Fig. 5c). The observed behavior at physiological pH (7.4) represented an intermediate state between these two conditions. Biodegradation studies in the presence of papain and/or collagenase are also suggested for future studies.

UCBS release profiles from hydrogels

To evaluate the potential application of the poly(diethyl acrylamide)-xanthan gum semi-IPN hydrogel containing UCBS, its release behavior from hydrogels with varying xanthan gum ratios was measured using a Franz diffusion cell at 37.5 °C and analyzed via the BCA assay kit, as illustrated in the Fig. 5d. Growth factors and other loaded API are typically released from dehydrated hydrogel polymer matrices through a swelling- and diffusion-controlled mechanism, involving rapid water absorption, which induces drug desorption. Upon water penetration into the drug-loaded hydrogel matrix, the drug diffuses through the swollen region into the external environment (Khan et al., 202`1). The effect of xanthan gum concentration in semi-IPN hydrogels was investigated as the key variable on UCBS release rate. Higher xanthan concentrations are expected to form a denser polymer network, significantly slowing UCBS release. Consequently, at identical time points, the cumulative released serum in PBS from formulations with higher xanthan content was lower. The release profiles were as follows: hydrogels containing 2%, 1%, 0.5% and 0% xanthan gum released about 70% of the loaded UCBS after 48 h, 24 h, 12 h, and 3 h, respectively which was statistically significant different (P < 0.0001). Overall, semi-IPN hydrogel matrices, comprising xanthan gum in their networks integrated with poly (diethyl acrylamide) molecular pores, demonstrated superior slow and controlled release compared to plain poly (diethyl acrylamide) gel. The presence of poly (diethyl acrylamide) facilitated cross-linking with the xanthan gum, providing a driving force for dispersing and loading UCBS molecules (Guo and Ma, 2014; Zhang et al., 2011). The pore effect was evident, consistent with findings reported in the literature (Liu et al., 2013).

Cell adhesion

This test is done to show cell compatibility of the hydrogels. Fig. 6c, d show L929 cell adhesion on blank and UCBS containing semi-IPN hydrogels, respectively. The blank semi-IPN hydrogel showed cell adhesion, which can be explained by the existence of xanthan in the hydrogel skeleton and as an appropriate template for cell proliferation. Finally, the adhesion and spreading of cells was significantly more pronounced in the UCBS (Umbilical cord blood serum)- incorporated hydrogel vs. that of an UCBS-free hydrogel, showing a fibroblast-like morphology. The rise in cell numbers is attributed to the existence of UCBS that promotes cell growth (Wang et al., 2009). The interaction of cells with hydrogels significantly affects cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation. Cell adhesion to the scaffold can be specific to the type of cell and may depend on the interaction of specific cell receptors with ligands that are part of the scaffold or adsorbed onto it. Inappropriate interactions can lead to undesirable tissue formation (Ho et al., 2022).

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial properties of semi-IPN ethyl acrylamide-xanthan gum (1% xanthan gum) hydrogel containing 20% UCBC were studied against E. coli and S. aureus and the results are shown in (Fig. 6e, f). The lack of an inhibition zone in the agar diffusion test suggests that the prepared hydrogel does not have noticeable antibacterial activity. This is likely because UCBC, xanthan gum and polymerized diethyl acrylamide do not inherently possess antimicrobial properties. In addition, the hydrogel's structure may limit its ability to diffuse through the agar, which could further reduce the release of any active components in agar medium.

In both the Alpha ointment and hydrogel-UCBS groups, particularly the latter, a higher collagen deposition with improved organization and thickness of collagen fibers was observed, indicating progression of the healing process toward the maturation stage. Marked neovascularization represents a defining characteristic of the early stages of wound healing. As recovery advances, most newly generated capillaries experience selective apoptotic elimination. This vascular regression persists until the density of vessels reaches levels similar to those found in healthy tissue. The formation of new blood vessels was identified using the endothelial cell marker CD31 (PECAM-1). CD31 immunohistochemical staining was utilized to identify the endothelial cells surrounding the wound bed on day 14 after the intervention. Immature blood vessels, comprising capillaries, arterioles, and venules, exhibited positive staining with CD31, displaying distinctly brown membrane or granular appearances. The ongoing presence of these brown granules on day 14 signifies continued angiogenesis and implies a prolonged healing process. Quantification showed a notably greater density of CD31-positive endothelial cells in the control group than in the Hydrogel-UCBS and Alpha ointment treatment groups. Significantly, the Hydrogel-UCBS group exhibited the least density of brown granules. These findings suggest that the Hydrogel-UCBS dressing notably speeds up tissue healing, as shown by lower neovascularization markers compared to both control and comparator groups at this time point.

In 2015, Parazzi et al. (Parazzi et al., 2015) discovered elevated levels of hormones and tissue-repairing molecules in umbilical cord blood-derived platelet gel, which contained higher concentrations of several angiogenic factors compared to peripheral blood. In the current study, histopathological evaluations revealed that topical application of hydrogel-UCBS accelerated wound healing in the treatment group compared to the untreated group. Immunohistochemical assessments demonstrated enhanced angiogenesis in the hydrogel-UCBS group relative to the untreated group. Increased infiltration of mononuclear inflammatory cells, attributed to the production of growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, pro-inflammatory mediators, and antimicrobial effects, further accelerated cutaneous wound healing. These findings align with the study of Damsi et al. (João De Masi et al., 2016), who investigated the role of growth factors in skin wound repair in rats.

Conclusion

We developed and thoroughly characterized a diethyl acrylamide hydrogel combined with various concentrations of xanthan gum (0%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2%) for potential use as a wound-dressing drug. Considering the overall performance, the 1% xanthan gum hydrogel was chosen as the optimal formulation, providing a favorable balance between swelling capacity, degradation rate, rheological properties and controlled release of UCBC. In vivo studies revealed that this formulation significantly enhanced the healing process, nearly closing the wound by day 14. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses indicated that the gel reduced inflammation, downregulated surface CD31 markers, increased collagen density, and accelerated re-epithelialization in the healed tissue, suggesting improved tissue regeneration. These findings suggest that the 1% xanthan gum hydrogel could provide a supportive physiological and biological microenvironment, highlighting its great potential as an advanced wound dressing in clinical applications.
