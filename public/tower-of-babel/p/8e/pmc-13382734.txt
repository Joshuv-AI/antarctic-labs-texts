Protocol for simultaneous non-invasive electrocardiogram and respiratory plethysmography recordings in mice
Authors: Nicholas Y. Iwakoshi, Stephen Salloum, Kevin J. Cummings, Erik J. Behringer, Christopher G. Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for non-invasive simultaneous recording of electrocardiogram (ECG) and breathing in conscious mice. We describe steps for securing surface ECG electrodes with kinesiology tape, lightly sedating mice for lead placement, and monitoring via plethysmography. Heart (electrocardiogram) and lung signals (air flow) were synchronously acquired, enabling accurate, minimally invasive cardiopulmonary phenotyping for neuroscience research and predictive therapeutic interventions (pharmacological, genetic, and engineering), without surgical implantation or confounding anesthesia.

Before you begin

This protocol explains how to record ECG and respiratory signals in conscious mice using surface electrodes and whole-body plethysmography. Mice are briefly anesthetized with isoflurane only to allow safe and accurate placement of ECG leads. Once fully recovered from anesthesia, the awake mouse is placed in the plethysmography chamber for data collection. This system works well for tracking heart and lung function in models of aging, neurological disease, or drug exposure.

Before conducting the following experiments, ensure that all animal care use and protocols are approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and performed in accord with the National Research Council’s “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” (8th Edition, 2011) and the ARRIVE guidelines. For this manuscript, the IACUC of Loma Linda University has approved the described protocol for male and female C57BL/6J and 3xTg-AD mice (age range: 2 to 30 months). Before starting, check that the plethysmography chamber, flow meter, and data acquisition software are all up to date in maintenance and operation.

To prepare the ECG leads, place them on a strip of kinesiology tape. Write the mouse’s ID at the edge of the tape using a fine-tip marker. This ensures that the ID stays visible, which is helpful if one is also recording video.

Since kinesiology tape is typically wide relative to the size scale of mouse anatomy, cut it lengthwise to better fit around the chest. Wrap the tape securely around the thorax, tight enough to hold the leads in place but loose enough to avoid interfering with breathing.

Finally, make sure your isoflurane anesthesia system is running properly and that all personnel are trained and comfortable with the steps involved in the protocol, especially post-anesthesia care both before and after acquiring the plethysmography and ECG recordings. Set up the recovery area in advance so everything’s ready once the recording is done.

Innovation

Previous studies often recorded only respiratory activity using whole-body plethysmography (WBP), or estimated heart rate from electromyographic signals without capturing full ECG waveforms.1,2 In contrast, our method simultaneously records high-fidelity electrocardiograms (ECG; P–QRS–T complexes) and respiratory signals in conscious, freely moving mice. This dual-stream setup enables accurate beat-to-breath synchrony analysis under physiologically relevant conditions.

Earlier approaches, such as Chen et al. (2013), used surface silver electrodes to extract interbeat intervals to capture full cardiac waveforms within a semi-restrained setup conducting plethysmography with a head-out system.1 Davis et al. (2019) relied on nuchal electromyographic signals, which excluded ECG morphology and limited arrhythmia detection.2

Our protocol captures true cardiac electrical activity synchronized with thoracic pressure oscillations. This allows precise quantification of cardiorespiratory coupling (CRC) metrics, including respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), spectral coherence, and beat-to-breath phase alignment in unrestrained awake animals.3,4,5

We also apply strict environmental controls, such as chamber temperature stability (±0.5 °C) and humidity monitoring, to reduce physiological drift and stress artifacts. These factors often introduced variability in earlier WBP systems.6

Importantly, this method avoids surgery and supports monitoring of autonomic function without requiring telemetric ECG implants.7,8,9 This is especially valuable in studies of aging, sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), and sleep apnea, where repeated surgical instrumentation may alter physiological outcomes.10,11

By combining non-invasive plethysmography with direct ECG in awake, unrestrained mice, our approach enables high-resolution CRC analysis across developmental stages, circadian rhythms, and disease models, from sleep-related disorders to neurodegeneration.12,13

Expected outcomes

This method enables non-invasive assessment of autonomic balance and cardiopulmonary adaptation across multiple research paradigms. It is particularly suited for: pharmacological testing (dose-dependent bradycardia or respiratory depression following cholinergic or serotonergic drugs2), aging-related studies assessing declines in heart–lung coordination or circadian variability,14 neurodegenerative and autonomic dysfunction models (including serotonin-deficient, TPH2, or 3xTg-AD mice15), and environmental or hypoxia–hypercapnia paradigms (evaluating gas mixture impacts on ventilatory control).16,17 Acquiring both plethysmography and ECG data simultaneously allows you to observe changes in both cardiac and pulmonary function in real time and compare changes within the same animal longitudinally. Quantification and statistical analysis

Once data are collected, multiple avenues exist for analysis, but here we present a general workflow with modular software tools that can be adapted in accord with the investigator’s needs. Both breathing and ECG waveforms can be assessed using basic filtering and detrending as the first steps in signal analysis.

After observing the raw data, the signal-to-noise ratio and baseline fluctuations will determine if filtering and detrending are needed. Bandpass filters allow for removal of non-physiological frequencies (this can vary depending on strain) and Savitzky-Golay filters help retain peaks within a waveform for later analysis. Before and after filtering your data we suggest calculating a Fast-Fourier Transform (we use L. Bluestein’s method) to identify signal frequencies and assess noise and evaluate how filtering affects the signal frequencies and amplitude.18

Due to the animal’s movement in the plethysmography chamber, baseline fluctuations are likely to be observed in the ECG and burst of activity can be seen in the plethysmography recordings. We have found that rolling detrending has worked best for our datasets. Once the raw signal is filtered, running a signal peak detection or a waveform detection function is next.

When assessing the breathing data, detecting peaks and troughs quantifies the number of breaths the animal has taken per minute and calculates the minute ventilation and volume per breath (this requires a pulse with known volume to obtain the area under the curve per volume).

Analyzing ECG waves uses the same method but focuses on detecting R waves with peak detection algorithms. When running the peak detection make sure to tune the parameters used and graph detected peaks to confirm the peak detection algorithm is accurately acquiring the signal peaks. Within most peak detection algorithms, the prominence (height of the peak in relation to nearby points), height (a threshold for signal amplitude, often a percentage of the maximum amplitude), and distance (the minimum time or points between peaks) are the levers used to define the peak detection parameters. We recommend starting with a prominence of 80% and a height of 75%. Distance may vary with the sampling rate, and all of these parameters will need to be tuned depending on your signal-to-noise ratio. Once the peak detection is completed, you can assess differences between animals or groups using time domain, frequency domain, and non-linear metrics. Time domain metrics provide insight into changes in overall heartbeat/breathing rate. Frequency metrics allow for detection of changes in underlying autonomic control. Non-linear metrics can provide insight into how normal signal patterns change while locating abnormal occurrences. Considering the movement of the animals within the chamber, a correlation between sudden changes in heartbeat and breathing can be used to roughly assess the frequency of large movements within the chamber. This correlation can be paired with video of the animals within the chamber for better assessment of how movement artifacts are affecting your signal and changes in behavior within the chamber between groups or exposures.

Limitations

This protocol is subject to technical, physiological, and environmental limitations. Technical limitations include signal quality deterioration, chamber dead space effects, temperature and humidity sensitivity, single-lead ECG constraints, signal noise artifact, and indirect tidal volume measurements.

Signal quality deterioration occurs when after 25–30 min the electrode gel may undergo the gel-sol transition leading to progressive ECG signal loss or increased noise artifacts.19 Electrode gel drying also reduces conductivity during extended recordings.14 Within the 40 cm3 chamber, volume introduces 2–3 s latency during gas-switch experiments (chamber dead space effects), limiting rapid kinetic analysis of respiratory responses.16,20 Plethysmography measurements are influenced by temperature fluctuations and humidity changes within the sealed chamber environment, potentially confounding respiratory volume calculations.17,20 The two-electrode configuration provides limited cardiac rhythm analysis compared to multi-lead systems, potentially missing complex arrhythmias.15,19 Electrical noise from nearby devices, fluorescent lighting, and inadequate grounding can introduce 50/60 Hz artifacts in ECG recordings.14 Whole-body plethysmography measures pressure changes rather than direct airflow, which may not accurately reflect true tidal volumes under all physiological conditions.16,17

A combination with each animal’s own unique behavioral patterns, size, and strain can affect plethysmography measurements. Movement artifacts and stress behaviors can make the data more difficult to analyze. Artifacts caused by movement can be detected and removed during analysis and the animal’s cardiovascular stress response can be minimized using habituation protocols.6,15,19,21 Besides the animal’s reaction to the chamber rodent strain can affect baseline cardiopulmonary variability.15 Regardless of mouse strain this protocol is optimized for adult C57BL/6 mice (20–30 g); very young (<6 weeks) or obese (>35 g) animals may not accommodate proper kinesiology tape placement. Accommodation for larger animals or rats can be made if larger diameter materials for the chamber are used and the size of the kinesiology tape is adjusted.

Maintaining consistent environmental conditions can minimize changes in behavior between recordings. Differences in ambient noise and vibrations can be hard to account for but acclimating the animals to the chamber for 10–45 min in three separate sessions can help minimize strong behavioral reactions to the new environment.

Problem 1

Respiratory distress makes the breathing trace abnormal and unstable. The signal may show repeated sigh-like breaths, irregular spacing between peaks, prolonged pauses, or loss of a stable rhythm. This can also be seen visually as increased breathing effort, gasping-like movements, or persistent restlessness. A single irregular breath can occur after movement, but intervention is needed if these changes persist for several breaths or repeat over time. This is usually caused by a wrap that is too tight, stress from the environment, or sensitivity to the gas condition (related to Steps 8 and 20).

Potential solution

Problem 3

Inconsistent heart rate measurements usually are observed when the ECG signal does not show clearly distinguishable and repeatable R waves, or when the calculated heart rate changes suddenly even though the waveform does not. In a high-quality recording, the R waves should be easy to identify and heart rate should remain stable or change gradually. If irregular values appear mainly during movement, noise, or poor peak detection, the problem is more likely technical than physiologic. A true rhythm abnormality is more likely to remain visible across consecutive beats in a stable ECG trace (related to Step 2, 3, 8, 20).

Potential solution
