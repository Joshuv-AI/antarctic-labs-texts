Differentiating Ocular Pathogenic Staphylococcus epidermidis Isolates Using a Human Corneal Epithelial Cell Coculture Model
Authors: Qiankun Chen, Yuan Wei, Jinding Pang, Zhenyu Wei, Yan Peng, Ahyan Ilman Qudsi, Zijun Zhang, Xinxin Lu, Zhiqun Wang, Yang Zhang, Kexin Chen, Qingfeng Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop an in vitro human corneal epithelial cell (HCEC) coculture model for differentiating pathogenic from nonpathogenic ocular Staphylococcus epidermidis isolates.MethodsHCECs were cocultured with S. epidermidis strains (104–108 CFU/mL) for 12 to 72 hours. Cell viability was assessed using calcein AM staining. Seventeen strains were evaluated in this model and validated in mouse keratitis models. Cytotoxicity profiles of pathogenic versus nonpathogenic strains were compared, and receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to evaluate HCEC viability as a predictor of pathogenicity.ResultsA 24-hour HCEC coculture assay reliably quantified bacterial cytotoxicity across concentrations. In vivo, 12 of 17 strains (70.6%) were pathogenic in mouse keratitis models. In vitro, cytotoxicity was detected in 50.0% of pathogenic strains (6/12) at 104 CFU/mL, 58.3% (7/12) at 105 CFU/mL, and 83.3% (10/12) at 106 CFU/mL, whereas no nonpathogenic strains induced cytotoxicity at ≤106 CFU/mL. At 106 CFU/mL, HCEC viability was significantly lower with pathogenic than nonpathogenic strains (P = 0.006). Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified a 91.36% viability cutoff at 106 CFU/mL of S. epidermidis to distinguish pathogenic strains, yielding an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.9167, with 100% specificity and 91.7% sensitivity.ConclusionsThis HCEC coculture assay provides a rapid and reliable method to distinguish pathogenic from nonpathogenic S. epidermidis ocular isolates, with strong concordance with in vivo keratitis outcomes.Translational RelevanceThis model offers a practical diagnostic tool to identify clinically relevant S. epidermidis strains, supporting earlier and more accurate diagnosis of bacterial keratitis and guiding targeted therapy.

Introduction

Bacterial keratitis (BK) remains one of the leading causes of corneal infection worldwide, significantly contributing to ocular morbidity in both developed and developing countries.1 Staphylococcus epidermidis is the most frequently isolated bacterial species from the cornea, comprising 27.4% to 42.3% of BK cases.2–8 As an opportunistic pathogen, S. epidermidis is a ubiquitous component of the conjunctival and skin microbiota.9,10 The commensal presence of this bacterium complicates the interpretation of its isolation from keratitis cases, because it may represent colonization or contamination rather than a true infectious process. Consequently, clinical isolates may be misinterpreted as nonpathogenic, leading to diagnostic and therapeutic delays.11 Infection caused by S. epidermidis may lead to severe corneal inflammation, perforation, and endophthalmitis, potentially resulting in irreversible vision loss if not treated promptly.12 Furthermore, prolonged or inappropriate treatment often promotes the emergence of drug-resistant strains.13,14 A rapid and accurate diagnosis ensures favorable clinical outcomes in S. epidermidis keratitis. Until now, no definitive laboratory method has reliably differentiated pathogenic S. epidermidis from commensal or contaminating strains.

Unlike Staphylococcus aureus, S. epidermidis lacks prominent virulence factors, complicating the evaluation of its pathogenicity.15 Animal models have traditionally been used to assess the virulence and pathogenicity of these bacteria.16,17 Biofilm formation is recognized as a virulence determinant, but its predictive accuracy in animal studies remains limited.17 Host–microbe interaction studies have emerged as a valuable approach to elucidate microbial pathogenicity.18 Coculturing with A549 cells demonstrated that pathogenic Pseudomonas aeruginosa induces significant cell death, but not S. epidermidis.19 Similarly, primary human skin fibroblast cells exhibited marked viability reduction when exposed to Staphylococcus haemolyticus or S. aureus.20 In the field of corneal infection, human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) have been extensively used as an in vitro model to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying keratitis caused by pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and Corynebacterium pseudodiphtheriticum.21–24 Using HCECs in this context facilitates detecting cellular responses to bacterial infection and evaluating potential therapeutic strategies within a controlled laboratory setting. However, no established bacterial–cell coculture model exists to assess the pathogenicity of S. epidermidis.

In this study, we aimed to develop a coculture model using HCECs and S. epidermidis to assess the pathogenicity of ocular isolates. Mouse keratitis models served as the reference standard to validate the accuracy of this model in differentiating pathogenic from nonpathogenic strains.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital (TRECKY2021-024), this study was conducted at the Beijing Tongren Hospital from September 2021 to October 2024. It comprised two stages: establishing an HCEC-S. epidermidis coculture model and evaluating HCEC viability within this model to distinguish pathogenic isolates.

Establishment of HCECs and S. epidermidis Coculture Model

S. epidermidis Preparation

To develop the coculture model, three strains were used: reference strain ATCC 35984, a clinical strain from corneal infection, and a commensal strain from normal conjunctiva. ATCC 35984 was procured from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center. The clinical strain was isolated from patients diagnosed with BK based on clinical manifestations and confirmed by positive results from corneal scraping smears and microbial culture at the cornea department of Beijing Tongren Hospital. Bacterial strains were cultivated on tryptic soy agar and incubated at 37°C for 48 hours. Strain identification was confirmed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry.25 Colonies were harvested and suspended in sterile normal saline, with the optical density at 600 nm adjusted to 1.0. The suspensions were subsequently diluted to obtain final concentrations ranging from 104 to 108 CFU/mL for further experiments.

Cell Culture

HCECs (crisprbio, Beijing, China) were cultured in 24-well plates using Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, South San Francisco, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin.26 HCECs were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 2 days, and the cells were passaged every 3 to 4 days when they reached 80% to 90% confluence. HCECs were identified by strong K12 expression and the absence of p63 expression (Supplementary Figure S1), consistent with a previous study.27

Coculture Model Establishment

HCECs were cultured to 90% confluence, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then incubated in antibiotic-free Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. S. epidermidis was subsequently added to HCECs. To evaluate the effect of incubation time, HCECs were cocultured with S. epidermidis at 107 CFU/mL for 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 hours. Additionally, the interaction was assessed using bacterial concentrations of 104, 105, 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL for 24 hours in an incubator (37°C, 5% CO2), with sterile PBS serving as the control. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Viability Assay

After 24 hours of coculture with S. epidermidis, HCEC viability was assessed using the Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit for Animal Live & Dead Cells (Biotium, Hayward, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.28 Briefly, the cells were washed three times with sterile PBS and incubated with 2 µM calcein acetoxymethyl ester (calcein AM) for 30 minutes at room temperature in a dark environment. Then, live cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX50, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a fluorescein isothiocyanate filter and contrast phase set. Cell counts were performed in three randomly selected high-power fields from the central wells using ImageJ software. Cell viability was calculated as (mean cell count in the treated group/mean cell count in the PBS group) × 100%.

Evaluation of the Coculture for Identifying Pathogenic S. epidermidis

S. epidermidis Strains

For the validation experiment, 17 S. epidermidis strains were tested: 13 strains isolated from 13 individual patients with BK, 3 strains from the conjunctivae of healthy volunteers without keratitis, and the reference strain ATCC 35984.

Animals

A total of forty-six 8-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility. The mice were provided with standard rodent chow and allowed free access to drinking water. All animal experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Establishment of S. epidermidis Keratitis Mouse Model

S. epidermidis strains were cultured on tryptic soy agar at 37°C for 48 hours, after which colonies were harvested and suspended in sterile normal saline to a final concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. After anesthesia induction, corneal infection was induced via an intrastromal injection of 1 µL bacterial suspension containing approximately 1000 CFU of S. epidermidis into the right eyes of mice using a sterile microliter syringe (Hamilton Company, Bonaduz, Switzerland), as previously described.29 Seventeen strains were injected into the corneas of 34 mice (2 mice per strain). Two mice injected with sterile saline served as negative controls. After 24 hours, corneal conditions were detected using a slit-lamp biomicroscope. To assess the integrity of the epithelium, 1 µL of 0.1% sodium fluorescein (Yuanye Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was instilled into the conjunctival sac, and corneal staining was visualized under cobalt blue illumination.28

Pathogenicity Assessment of S. epidermidis in Mice

The S. epidermidis strains were classified based on their ability to induce keratitis, characterized by corneal infiltration and positive fluorescein staining. Those stains that could cause keratitis were deemed pathogenic. Strains that failed to induce keratitis were confirmed as nonpathogenic after a secondary validation using an additional cohort of mice (2 mice per strain). The severity of corneal infection was quantified using a standardized grading system comprising three distinct parameters: (1) area of opacity, (2) density of opacity, and (3) irregularity of surface, with each parameter scored on a 4-point scale (0 [normal] to 4 [most severe]) according to our previous study.30 The total score ranged from 0 (normal) to 12 (most severe).

Pathogenicity Assessment of S. epidermidis Using the Coculture Model

A heatmap was generated to visualize the effects of bacterial concentrations (10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶, 10⁷, and 10⁸ CFU/mL) on HCEC viability across all strains, which were classified based on pathogenicity as determined by the animal keratitis models (pathogenic and nonpathogenic groups). Comparative analyses were then performed at each bacterial concentration to identify the threshold at which significant differences in HCEC viability emerged between the two groups. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied to evaluate the predictive performance of HCEC viability in discriminating pathogenic strains and to determine the optimal concentration and viability cut-off value for classification.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; San Diego, CA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For group comparisons, parametric data were analyzed using the Student t test (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (multiple groups). Nonparametric data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the coculture model in distinguishing pathogenic from nonpathogenic S. epidermidis strains. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), specificity, and sensitivity were calculated. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Establishment and Optimization of a HCECs and S. epidermidis Coculture Model

Coculture Duration

HCECs cocultured with S. epidermidis for 12 to 72 hours showed time-dependent cytotoxicity at 24, 36, 48, and 72 hours for both ATCC 35984 and the clinical strain (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the strain from normal conjunctiva did not exhibit cytotoxicity at any time point. Consequently, a 24-hour duration was chosen for subsequent experiments to evaluate S. epidermidis cytotoxicity.

Bacterial Concentration

ATCC 35984 induced apparent HCEC destruction at concentrations of 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL in the phase-contrast set (Fig. 1B). The clinical strain isolated from an infectious keratitis caused a progressive decrease in HCEC density at concentrations of 104, 105, 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL. No apparent cell destruction was observed in HCECs cocultured with the S. epidermidis strain from normal conjunctiva in these concentrations.

Observation Methods

HCECs were observed after exposure to S. epidermidis under the light microscope with the phase-contrast mode and calcein AM staining via fluorescent microscope. Compared with the phase-contrast mode, fluorescent images provided clearer cell morphology, facilitating more accurate observation and quantification of live cells. Calcein AM staining revealed that HCEC viability decreased significantly when exposed to 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL of ATCC 35984 (P = 0.0003, P < 0.0001, and P < 0.0001, respectively) (Figs. 1C–D) and to 104, 105, 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL of the strain from keratitis (P = 0.0115, P = 0.0027, P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, and P < 0.0001, respectively) (Figs. 1C and 1F). There was no significant difference between the groups treated with the normal conjunctival strain and the PBS group (all P > 0.05) (Figs. 1C and 1E).

Validation of the Pathogenicity in S. epidermidis Strains

Pathogenicity of S. epidermidis Strains in Mouse Models

Of the 17 strains evaluated, 12 (70.6%) were classified as pathogenic, because they induced keratitis in mouse models with characteristic features of corneal infection, including cellular infiltration, corneal opacity, ulceration, and hypopyon formation (Supplementary Table S1). In contrast, the remaining five strains (29.4%) did not induce keratitis, exhibiting corneal transparency or only mild cloudiness after inoculation, and were therefore designated nonpathogenic (Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, comparative analyses of corneal scores in the pathogenic and nonpathogenic groups were also conducted. The pathogenic group had higher scores in the area and density of corneal opacity, surface irregularity, and the total score (P < 0.0001, P = 0.0002, P = 0.0002, and P = 0.0003, respectively) (Fig. 2).

Comparison of clinical scores between pathogenic and nonpathogenic groups. (A) Area of corneal opacity. (B) Density of corneal opacity. (C) Surface irregularity. (D) Total composite score. ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Dose-dependent Cytotoxicity of S. epidermidis Strains on HCECs

The concentrations that reduced HCEC viability in 24 hours were assessed in all 17 S. epidermidis strains. Overall, 35.3% (6/17), 41.2% (7/17), 58.8% (10/17), 88.2% (15/17), and 94.1% (16/17) strains of S. epidermidis exhibited cytotoxic effects on HCECs at concentrations of 104, 105, 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL, respectively (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). A marked difference in cytotoxicity was observed between pathogenic and nonpathogenic strains. None of the nonpathogenic strains was cytotoxic at concentrations of up to 106 CFU/mL (Fig. 3A). At higher bacterial loads, cytotoxicity emerged, affecting 60.0% (3/5) of these strains at 107 CFU/mL and 80.0% (4/5) at 108 CFU/mL (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the 12 pathogenic strains demonstrated a pronounced, dose-dependent effect: cytotoxicity was detected in 50.0% (6/12) at 104 CFU/mL, 58.3% (7/12) at 105 CFU/mL, and 83.3% (10/12) at 106 CFU/mL (Figs. 3B and 3C). At concentrations of 107 CFU/mL and higher, all pathogenic strains (100%, 12/12) caused significant loss of HCEC viability (all P < 0.05) (Figs. 3B and 3C).

HCEC viability after exposure to 17 S. epidermidis strains at varying concentrations. (A) Nonpathogenic strains. (B, C) Pathogenic strains. Asterisks indicate a significant decrease in cell viability compared with the PBS control. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ***P < 0.0001.

Pathogenic S. epidermidis Strains Reduced HCEC Viability

The viability of HCECs following exposure to 12 pathogenic and 5 nonpathogenic S. epidermidis strains at various concentrations was summarized in the heatmap (Fig. 4A). Specifically, HCEC viability decreased progressively when exposed to 104, 105, 106, 107, and 108 CFU/mL of pathogenic S. epidermidis. Notably, HCEC viability was significantly reduced when exposed to 106 CFU/mL of the pathogenic group compared with the nonpathogenic group (P = 0.0061) (Fig. 3D). No significant differences in HCEC viability were observed at 104, 105, 107, and 108 CFU/mL concentrations between the 2 groups (P = 0.1928, 0.0572, 0.1627, and 0.7794, respectively) (Figs. 4B, 4C, 4E, and 4F).

ROC analysis of the diagnostic performance of HCEC viability at (G) 104, (H) 105, (I) 106, (J) 107, and (K) 108 CFU/mL in distinguishing the pathogenic S. epidermidis strains. ns, not significant. **P < 0.01.

Diagnostic Performance of HCEC Viability for Pathogenic S. epidermidis Strains

The discriminatory accuracy of HCEC viability in identifying pathogenic S. epidermidis strains was assessed using ROC analysis. At 106 CFU/mL of bacteria, an HCEC viability cutoff of < 91.36% yielded the best performance (AUC = 0.917), with 100% specificity and 91.7% sensitivity (P = 0.0084) (Fig. 4I). In comparison, the diagnostic performance at other bacterial concentrations was lower, with AUC values of 0.800 (104 CFU/mL), 0.7667 (105 CFU/mL), 0.7250 (107 CFU/mL), and 0.5667 (108 CFU/mL), none of which reached statistical significance (all P > 0.05) (Figs. 4G, 4H, 4J, and 4K).

Discussion

In the current study, we developed a novel and efficient HCECs S. epidermidis model to differentiate the pathogenicity of clinical isolates. Pathogenic S. epidermidis strains exhibited significant cytotoxicity toward HCECs, with 106 CFU/mL identified as the optimal threshold for distinguishing pathogenic from nonpathogenic strains. This coculture model provides a practical tool to distinguish pathogenic from commensal isolates, particularly in clinical scenarios where S. epidermidis is isolated from keratitis cases but its pathogenic role is uncertain, thereby enabling more accurate diagnosis and management of S. epidermidis keratitis.

We provided a highly accurate cell–microbe coculture model to distinguish pathogenic S. epidermidis strains. A previous study used A549 cell viability assays to differentiate pathogenic bacteria, including Acinetobacter baylyi, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus agalactiae, and P. aeruginosa, from nonpathogenic S. epidermidis strains.19 However, these assays were limited by short incubation periods and low bacterial loads, resulting in subtle morphological changes (loss of cell adherence and tight junctions) without significant reductions in cell density.19 Our study demonstrated an evident decrease in HCEC viability after infection with pathogenic S. epidermidis, facilitating more straightforward observation and identification.

The pathogenicity of S. epidermidis is multifactorial, involving biofilm formation, immune evasion mechanisms, virulence factors, and antimicrobial resistance. The ability to produce biofilms has been proposed as a distinguishing feature between pathogenic and commensal S. epidermidis.17,31 However, 44% of isolates from the normal conjunctival sac of precataract patients also formed biofilms and harbored the biofilm gene icaA.32 This observation suggests that biofilm formation alone cannot reliably determine the pathogenicity of ocular surface S. epidermidis isolations. S. epidermidis expresses an S. aureus-type wall teichoic acid, facilitating a transition from a commensal to a pathogenic lifestyle.33 Additionally, slime production, a potential virulence factor, was reported in 42.9% of pathogenic strains and 24.1% of commensal strains.34 Furthermore, ocular infectious isolates differ significantly from healthy conjunctival isolates in terms of genotype (icaA, icaD, IS256, and agr locus polymorphisms) and phenotype (biofilm production).35 However, another study reported overlapping molecular types and phenotypic traits between isolates from healthy conjunctiva and ocular infections.36 Both studies demonstrated that infectious isolates exhibited higher antimicrobial resistance.35–36 Despite these insights, it remains challenging to determine S. epidermidis pathogenicity based on a single virulence factor.

Our findings demonstrated that S. epidermidis cytotoxicity to HCECs reflected a comprehensive pathogenic effect, which could be directly observed under a microscope. Interestingly, we found that only pathogenic S. epidermidis strains induced a significant decrease in HCEC viability at concentrations of 106 CFU/mL or lower, whereas nonpathogenic strains did not. This concentration represented the optimal threshold for HCEC viability to distinguish pathogenic from nonpathogenic S. epidermidis strains with high accuracy (AUC = 0.917).

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively limited, and further validation in larger, multicenter studies will be necessary to confirm our findings. Second, the isolates evaluated were primarily from the ocular surface, and additional studies are required to assess the pathogenicity of S. epidermidis isolates from other body sites, such as skin and catheters, to broaden the model's universality. Third, the current study primarily focused on clinical applications to identify pathogenic S. epidermidis strains, and the molecular mechanisms underlying pathogenicity require further investigation. Finally, the retrospective collection of S. epidermidis strains is another limitation, necessitating future prospective studies to confirm our findings and facilitate their translation into clinical practice.

Conclusions

We designed a simple yet effective model for determining the pathogenicity of S. epidermidis isolates from keratitis, which could aid in the accurate diagnosis of S. epidermidis-related ocular infections. Furthermore, our findings provide a framework for evaluating the pathogenicity of opportunistic pathogens, offering a valuable tool for both clinical and research applications.
