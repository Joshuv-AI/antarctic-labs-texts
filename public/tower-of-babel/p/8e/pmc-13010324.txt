Catalytic Methods for the Transfer of Nonstabilized Carbenes
Authors: Emily S. Arnold, Isaiah K. Eckart-Frank, Sidney M. Wilkerson-Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transition-metal catalysis has fundamentally transformed the way we construct molecules because of the ability of d-block elements to couple two structurally complex molecular fragments. Suzuki and Negishi-type cross-coupling methods are prevalent in catalysis; however, reactions involving metal-carbene alkene couplings (MCAC) are also well-represented in the fragment coupling toolbox because of their ability to construct two bonds simultaneously. Despite this clear utility, it is still challenging to use nonstabilized carbene intermediates (i.e., those with only alkyl groups) in cyclopropanation and C–H insertion reactions, although alkyl cyclopropanes are prevalent in natural products and pharmaceuticals. This perspective article summarizes catalytic methods that have addressed the challenges involved in the transfer of nonstabilized carbene groups to alkenes and other traps.

Introduction

Fragment coupling is a fundamental part of the synthetic organic chemistry toolbox, because the ability to join two fragments enables the strategic implementation of convergent synthetic routes and is an inherently modular process. These reactions become more attractive when viewed through the lens of transition-metal catalysis, since transition-metal-catalyzed reactions can help improve reaction rate, regioselectivity, diastereoselectivity, and enantioselectivity, while providing opportunities for the development of new activation modes of common functional groups. − Carbene intermediates − are unique in this regard, since they enable fundamentally distinct fragment coupling reactions compared to the more prevalent cross-coupling reactions that construct, for example, C­(sp2)–C­(sp2) and C­(sp2)–N bonds. The ability of carbene intermediates to undergo addition reactions with π-bonds or insertion reactions with σ-bonds makes them versatile components of the organic chemistry toolbox for joining two fragments (Figure ) in a manner that increases sp3 content.

Coupling carbenes with alkyl substitution is challenging but enabling for drug development and agrochemistry.

The purpose of this Perspective article is to summarize recent advancements in the catalytic transfer of nonstabilized carbenes bearing alkyl groups, because this aspect of metal-catalyzed carbene transfer chemistry has been especially challenging. The prevalence of alkylated cyclopropanes in natural products, pyrethroid agrochemicals, and in drugs like nirmatrelvir, has underscored the need for this type of carbene transfer reaction (Figure ). Though nonstabilized carbenes were originally defined by Charette as carbenes that only bear alkyl substitution, we will cover some metal-catalyzed methylenations to provide context for the chronological development of nonstabilized carbene reactions. The article is divided into sections by transition-metal identity, and each section is then organized by the type of transformation (cyclopropanation reactions, insertion reactions, and other transformations), which are then organized chronologically. Within each section pertinent literature on the analogous stoichiometric transformations are included for the reader, when appropriate, to provide context on prior work developed before the catalytic transformation. The field of carbene chemistry is vast, and many excellent contributions cannot be included in this noncomprehensive Perspective article due to space. This Perspective will be focused on studies with multiple examples of metal carbene (or carbenoid) reactivity. We will not cover the metathesis reactivity of metal alkylidenes, , the reactivity of gold carbenes, − enyne metathesis and related cycloisomerizations, or strained ring isomerizations in detail, since multiple reviews and book chapters exist for these topics. − The reactivity of transition metals belonging to groups 3–5, − discussions of reactions using atomic carbon equivalents, − and carbenes with one or more fluorinated alkyl groups also will be excluded. − Before beginning this Perspective, it is useful to briefly discuss the nature and reactivity of both free and metal-bound carbenes. Doing so will help the reader appreciate some of the idiosyncrasies of these reactive intermediates and the logic behind the development of the contemporary strategies to harness nonstabilized carbenes.

Metal Carbenes Versus Carbenoids–Nomenclature

The term carbene refers to an unbound (free) divalent carbon species (Figure , top). , The word carbenoid was first put forth by Closs and Moss to describe the structures of reactive intermediates that can produce cyclopropane products, “without necessarily being free divalent carbon species.” So, the difference between a carbene and carbenoid primarily depends on whether the leaving group is still bound to a tetrahedral carbon atom. Accordingly, a metal carbene would best be described as an intermediate whose structure is a divalent carbon atom bound to a metal center, whereas a metal carbenoid is a metal bonded to a carbon atom whose leaving group is still attached. For metals such as Rh, , Cu, and Au, structural, solution, and/or computational studies have confirmed the intermediacy of a reactive metal carbene. α-Lithiohalides and zinc carbenoids are textbook examples of carbenoids (Figure , bottom). Ylides, which bear a formal negative charge on carbon and a formal positive charge on the leaving group, are not classically considered carbenoids (Figure , bottom). However, certain ylides are still useful and can produce metal carbenes upon coordination to a transition metal, which makes them valuable metal-carbene precursors. Throughout this perspective article, we will try to adhere to the original definitions of these terms put forth in the literature.

Electronic Properties of Free Carbenes

There are two primary electronic configurations for carbenes that have a profound impact on structure and reactivity, the S0(σ2) state (singlet carbenes) and the T1(σ1p1) state (triplet carbenes) (Figure A). Singlet carbenes possess an empty p-orbital and a filled sp2-hybrid orbital with a bond angle of 102° for the parent carbene, methylene (1CH2). When substituted, carbene intermediates can be classified as electrophilic, nucleophilic, or ambiphilic. , Triplet carbenes carry an unpaired electron in each orbital, exhibit wider bond angles (137° in 3CH2) and typically react as 1,1-diradicals. The singlet–triplet energy difference in carbenes depends on the substituents and has a profound impact on the reactivity and the choice of catalyst in these systems.

Orbital interaction diagram depicting the effect of lone pair-donor groups on carbene electronic configuration. (B). Representative dialkyl carbene, donor-carbene and N-heterocyclic carbene.

Pertinent to this review, simple alkyl carbenes lacking stabilizing groups are typically ground state singlets. Methylene, di­(tert-butyl)­carbene, and di­(1-adamantyl)­carbene are notable exceptions. Carbenes bearing resonance donating X groups (e.g., NR2, OR, Cl) are also usually ground state singlet carbenes, since the lone pairs on these atoms interact with the free p-orbital on the carbene, which increases the HOMO-LUMO gap and thus the singlet−triplet gap. When two very strong resonance donor groups like O or N are placed on the carbene carbon atom, the resulting carbenes are exceptionally stable and are best described as a nucleophilic carbenes (Figure B). , Diarylcarbenes may possess either singlet or triplet ground state character; the steric and electronic characteristics of the aryl groups play a significant role in this determination.

Generation of Metal Carbenes and a Survey of Carbene Precursors

Carbenes can be regarded as synthons bearing a formal positive and negative charge on the same carbon atom, so molecules that are formal carbanions containing an α-leaving group are synthetic equivalents for these reactive intermediates (α-elimination step) (Figure A). − From this perspective, it becomes apparent that carbenes are synthetically related to other plus minus reagents, such as ylides and diazo compounds. The progenitors to carbene intermediates are often called carbene precursors or carbene equivalents. The nature of the carbene precursor and its synthetic accessibility can limit the types of metal carbenes obtained. Indeed, one major challenge that has impeded the development of transformations using nonstabilized metal carbenes was the identification of suitable carbene precursors.

Obtaining nonstabilized carbenes is difficult using traditional diazo- or ylide-based approaches. (A). Synthetic logic for obtaining metal carbenes used in catalysis. (B). General two electron arrow-pushing mechanism for metal carbene formation. (C). Common precursors for diazo compounds. (D). Examples of ylides used as metal carbene precursors.

One of the most frequently employed methods to obtain metal carbenes intermediates used in catalysis is the reaction of a suitable carbene precursor with a transition metal (i.e., a polar two-electron arrow-pushing mechanism). When a transition metal binds to the precursor, back bonding from metal d-orbitals helps liberate the leaving group and stabilizes the resulting metal carbene (Figure B). The decomposition of diazo compounds is a well-studied way to generate metal carbenes for catalysis, since N2 is an excellent leaving group. Diazoacetates and related compounds are typically obtained using the Regitz diazo transfer reaction, , from diazirines using heat or light, from N-sulfonyl hydrazones (Bamford-Stevens method), − through the oxidation of NH2 hydrazones, or from N-sulfonyltriazole reagents (Folkin approach) (Figure C). ,

Sulfonium, − sulfoxonium ylides, , and iodonium ylides − have all been used to generate metal carbenes catalytically (Figure D). In contrast, ammonium, pyridinium, phosphonium, , and telluronium ylides − have all been used to effect [2 + 1] cycloadditions, but rarely are these ylides used to generate metal carbenes. In fact, the ylides themselves are typically generated from a metal carbene intermediate. Catalytic methods leveraging these precursors to generate nonstabilized carbenes, beyond methylenation, are sparse.

Most methods to generate carbene synthetic equivalents necessitate the use of resonance stabilizing groups (esters, arenes, carbonyls, etc.) to impart stability to the reagent. Hydrazone reagents are unique in this regard, since they are synthesized using condensation reactions from the requisite aldehyde or ketone, which can be either aromatic or aliphatic. Therefore, catalysis with nonstabilized carbenes requires nonconventional carbene precursors and fundamentally different mechanisms of reactivity to surmount these challenges.

The recent implementation of redox approaches has enabled significant advancements in the generation and stabilization of nonstabilized metal carbenes, and many of the examples below reflect the increasing prevalence of these methods for carbene catalysis. For example, net reductive, metalloradical and photoredox catalysis have enabled the use of nonconventional carbene precursors such as aldehydes and broadened the use of traditional carbenoid precursors like dihaloalkanes (vide infra). Critical to the success of these radical approaches was the rational design of ligands to support radical intermediates.

Metal Carbene Classification

Metal carbene complexes have been historically classified into two categories based on the nature of the transition metal and the spin state of the free carbene–Schrock-type or Fischer-type carbenes. Schrock carbene complexes involve high-valent, early transition metals (typically groups 4–6) with alkyl-substituted triplet carbenes and are nucleophilic on carbon. Fischer carbenes are characterized by low-valent, late transition metals (typically groups 6–8) with a heteroatom-stabilized singlet carbene and are electrophilic on carbon. While these two categories of carbenes have served as helpful organizational tools, the electronics of metal carbene complexes is much more complex and exists on a continuum between these two extremes depending on the identity of the transition metal, the supporting ligands, and carbene structure. Indeed, recent literature suggests nonheteroatom stabilized Fischer carbene complexes as a distinct category of “hybrid” carbenes. − As the name suggests, hybrid carbene complexes have characteristics of both Schrock and Fischer carbenes and are typically late transition metals in low oxidation states with alkyl substitution on the carbene carbon and lacking a resonance donating atom (e.g., Ru alkylidenes). Additionally, metalloradicals (metal carbene radicals) and metal N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) complexes do not fit nicely into the traditional categories.

Metal carbenes have also been classified based on the functional groups bound to the divalent carbon atom. Substituents on the carbene species can be broadly classified as donor (aryl or heteroatom), acceptor (electron-withdrawing group, EWG), donor/acceptor, or nonstabilized (alkyl). The identity of the substituents on the carbene precursor impacts its reactivity and how they are handled. − For example, thermogravimetric analysis of low molecular weight diazo compounds reveals alkyl-substituted diazo compounds display highly exothermic and energetic decomposition profiles. Thus, the generation of high concentrations of these reagents in solution should be avoided, and flow techniques should be considered. − Conversely, the improved reactivity profile of resonance-stabilized diazo compounds has led to highly predictable and well-understood reactivity with numerous transition metal catalysts. Early successes using resonance-stabilized diazos, like that employed in the synthesis of thienamycin by Merck, helped to propel such reactions forward.

General Reactivity Modes of Metal Carbenes

Due to the electronic configuration of metal carbenes, these reactive intermediates can undergo myriad transformations that mirror the elementary reactivity of unbound carbenes (Figure ). The reactivity paradigms most pertinent to this review are those that transfer a carbene group (Figure , Path 1 and Path 2). Therefore, we will focus on reactions that form two bonds to the same carbon atom such as cyclopropanation and insertion reactions (both C–H and heteroatom–H) and other types of 1,1-insertion reactions. A special type of 1,1-insertion reaction occurs when a carbene equivalent, like a diazo compound, engages an organometallic species bearing a transferable ligand. The resulting metal carbene transfers a group (typically aryl) from the metal to the carbene carbon, resulting in a new organometallic intermediate that is capable of downstream transformations (Figure , Path 3). When α-hydrogen atoms are available, metal carbenes can also undergo fast 1,2-hydride shifts (Figure , Path 4). The corresponding alkyl shifts can also take place from adjacent quaternary centers, provided they are suitably functionalized.

Summary of typical carbene reactivity relevant to this review.

Given this background, it is not difficult to understand why the development of catalytic methods for the transfer of nonstabilized carbenes has been sluggish when compared to their resonance-stabilized counterparts. Indeed, it was the correct choice of the transition metal, lack of suitable carbene precursors, and unwanted side reactivity that have plagued developing transformations with these types of carbenes using two-electron logic. Therefore, fundamentally new approaches are needed to harness the reactivity of metal carbenes that lack stabilizing groups. Numerous exciting transformations are currently being developed. Radical approaches to carbenes leveraging alkyl halides and transition metals under reducing conditions have been an extraordinarily effective method to address this problem. Methods using hydrazone reagents are also promising strategies, since nonstabilized metal carbenes can be readily obtained from the corresponding alkyl-substituted diazo compounds generated in situ. The catalytic generation of metal carbenes using carbonyl compounds (e.g., aldehydes) showcases the benefits of using nontraditional metal carbene precursors.

Group 6: Cr-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

The history of group 6 metal carbenes is extensive but has primarily centered on intermediates containing α-O or N heteroatom donating groups. , However, studies by Takai and co-workers in 2003 established chromium carbenes as viable intermediates for iodocyclopropanations. Using stoichiometric Cr­(II) with iodoform, the authors found that addition of an amine such as TMEDA greatly enhanced the efficiency of the cyclopropanation reaction. Based on these results, the authors proposed that a chromium carbene mechanism, which would proceed by a metallacyclobutane intermediate, may be operative. The same group has extended the scope of the stoichiometric chromium reactions to obtain silyl cyclopropanes, boryl cyclopropanes, stannyl cyclopropanes, and bicyclo[3.1.0]­hexanes. − Similarly, Mashima, Tsurugi, and co-workers have developed Cr-catalyzed cyclopropanations using bromoform as the carbene precursor to produce bromocyclopropanes, which was enabled by employing 2,3,5,6-tetramethyl-1,4-bis­(trimethylsilyl)-1,4-dihydropyrazine as a reductant.

Wang and co-workers developed an asymmetric chromium cyclopropanation using geminal dihaloalkanes as nonstabilized carbene precursors (Scheme A). The authors proposed that the reaction proceeds through a radical-polar crossover mechanism and did not determine whether the alkyl chromium intermediate formed a carbene or a carbenoid. Only terminal alkenes, specifically 2-substituted acrylic amides, were shown to be effective reaction partners.

Group 8: Fe-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

The first cyclopropanation reaction using an iron alkylidene complex was reported in 1966 by Pettit and Jolly. Continued work throughout the latter half of the twentieth century by Brookhart and others expanded the cyclopropanation reactivity of these complexes (Scheme ). − Helquist and Brandt found iron sulfonium salts were also effective for cyclopropanation. The metal carbenes were typically synthesized using multistep procedures involving activation of low valent iron methylsulfides or iron acyl complexes with Me3OBF4 or TMSOTf. So, the use of these iron complexes was limited to stochiometric reactivity. Subsequent advances in catalyst development have led to a dramatic increase in the number of Fe-catalyzed cyclopropanations and other carbene transfer reactions. Work by Kodadek and Woo in 1995, Carreira in 2012, and Hergenrother and Svec in 2019 were important milestones in iron carbene catalysis and established that iron porphyrin complexes can catalyze cyclopropanations of styrenes using ethyl diazoacetate and diazomethane, respectively. −

Selected Examples of Iron Alkylidene Complexes

Recent work by the Nagib group showed that iron salts are capable of forming nonstabilized metal carbenes from benzoyloxy zinc carbenoids derived from aliphatic aldehydes in cyclopropanations, X–H insertions, and heterocyclizations (Scheme ). In their initial report, Nagib and co-workers subjected an aliphatic aldehyde to benzoyl bromide with catalytic zinc bromide, to afford a benzoyloxy bromide. Zinc metal and lithium chloride generate a zinc carbenoid, which transmetalates with an iron­(II) salt and generates an iron carbenoid. Extrusion of benzoate affords the iron carbene, which engages styrene to produce a cyclopropane and regenerate the iron­(II) salt.

More recently, the group has utilized iron porphyrin catalysts rather than FeCl2 to improve the generality of this methodology; cyclopropanes of unactivated and electron-poor alkenes can be accessed with increased efficiency, but again, with low diastereoselectivity (Scheme A). Fe-Catalyzed Cyclopropanations and Cross-Metathesis

In 2024, the MacMillan group reported a photoredox approach to generate nonstabilized iron carbenes (Scheme A). The authors were able to use a variety of carboxylic acids and alcohols as carbene precursors with an iron porphyrin complex to achieve both cyclopropanations and X–H bond insertions. The scope of alkene partners primarily consists of styrene derivatives and alkenes bearing α-heteroatoms. The Guo group has also recently developed a photoredox approach toward cyclopropanation with nonstabilized iron carbenes; this method enables the direct use of aldehydes with iron in a photoredox system to synthesize cyclopropanes (Scheme B). This system is quite similar to that developed by MacMillan and co-workers, but aldehydes are used as carbene precursors rather than carboxylic acids. The scope of alkene partners is also similar; however, the scope of substitution on the carbene precursors is broader.

Photoredox Iron-Catalyzed Cyclopropanations

The Wang group has since published a method to access iron carbenes directly from aldehydes without the use of photoredox chemistry (Scheme ). The authors propose a mechanism where an alkyl iron species is produced from an α-siloxy radical generated in situ. This alkyl iron species is then reduced to produce an iron carbene, which goes on to react with an alkene to form cyclopropane products. A wide range of cyclopropanes could be produced with respect to both the carbene precursor and the alkene; however, the scope does not include any examples of trisubstituted alkenes. The authors found that similar reaction conditions could also be used for Si–H insertion.

Iron-Catalyzed Cyclopropanations from Aldehydes

Xu, Lang, Xiong, and co-workers found that using an iron porphyrin catalyst, it was possible to generate the iron carbenes through electrochemical activation of aldehydes (Scheme ). The authors also reported a limited scope of X–H insertions. The optimized conditions for electrocatalytic activation of aldehydes employed a graphite carbon plate anode and a reticulated vitreous carbon (RVC) cathode under constant current. Mechanistic studies indicated that the cyclopropanation occurs through Fe­(I), in contrast to other iron porphyrin systems in which Fe­(II) is the active species. The authors propose a mechanism in which iron­(III) is reduced sequentially at the cathode to iron­(I). The low valent iron­(I) intermediate then adds into the aldehyde, forming a hydroxyalkyl iron­(III) species. After single-electron reduction, α-elimination occurs to produce the iron carbene. Subsequent carbene transfer yields the cyclopropane products. The scope of aldehydes is primarily limited to primary aliphatic aldehydes and requires a large excess (5.0 equiv) of these reagents. Additionally, there are few examples of alkenes with 1,1-disubstitution and no examples of alkenes with 1,2-disubsititution reported. Other reports of aldehydes as carbene precursors for cyclopropanation and homocoupling have since been published.

An Electrocatalytic Iron-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation

Functional groups other than aldehydes have been used as carbene precursors to generate iron carbenes. Zhang, Ning, and co-workers recently developed an iron-catalyzed cyclopropanation using a triftosyl hydrazone as the carbene precursor (Scheme ). Using a hydrazone derived from dimethoxyacetaldehyde, the authors found that the cyclopropanation reaction was effective for a wide range of olefins. Using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, an electrophilic doublet iron carbene was suggested to be a likely intermediate. The scope of this method, however, is limited–there are no examples with 1,2- or trisubstituted alkenes shown.

Fe-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation with a Hydrazone Carbene Precursor

Dihaloalkanes can also be used as precursors to iron carbenes to accomplish net reductive carbene transfer reactions. Wang and co-workers leveraged gem-dihaloalkanes for B–H and Si–H insertions, leading to a variety of organoboranes and organosilanes (Scheme ). In the proposed catalytic cycle, halogen transfer to Fe­(TPP) results in an α-chloroalkyl radical. This radical is then captured by Fe­(TPP) and subsequent reduction affords the iron carbene. The X–H insertion step is fast and concerted, based on deuterium labeling, crossover, and kinetic isotope effect (KIE) studies.

Group 8: Ru-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Ruthenium alkylidene complexes are well-known catalysts for olefin metathesis and cycloaddition reactions. − In 1987, Brookhart and co-workers found that cyclopropylcarbene complexes of iron and ruthenium are effective in carbene transfer reactions. However, this transformation was not catalytic, like iron-mediated carbene transfer reactions studied at that time. Currently, there are few examples using ruthenium carbenes catalytically to transfer nonstabilized carbenes beyond metathesis reactivity.

Building on previous work, the Che group found that alkyl hydrazones were suitable carbene precursors for C–H insertion reactions to produce substituted tetrahydrofurans and pyrrolidines using ruthenium porphyrin catalysts (Scheme ). The authors of this study conceptualize the reaction as a reductive coupling between CO and C–H bonds, since hydrazones are synthesized by condensation reactions of aldehydes or ketones with hydrazides.

Mechanism and Selected Examples of Ruthenium-Catalyzed C–H Insertion

Fürstner and co-workers have shown that alkynes can undergo geminal hydrogenations to form nonstabilized carbenes using ruthenium. The ruthenium piano–stool complexes display carbene reactivity and can cyclize to form cyclopentenes and bicyclo[3.1.0]­hexanes or undergo sigmatropic rearrangements to form linear alkenes (Scheme ). Although the carbenes formed are not stabilized by resonance, coordination of ruthenium by a pendent methoxy group on the β-carbon provides some stabilization. −

Carbenes Produced by gem-Hydrogenation

Sun and co-workers in 2022 found that the use of ruthenium catalysts with enynes enabled a gem-hydroborative cyclization to form bicyclo[3.1.0]­hexanes (Scheme ). The authors proposed two distinct mechanisms for the reaction. In the first mechanism, the hydroboration occurs first, to produce a ruthenacyclopropene and then a ruthenium alkylidene, which then forms a ruthenacyclobutane and undergoes reductive elimination to form the product. In the second proposed mechanism, the ruthenium carbene is formed first, before the addition of HBpin to ruthenium or to the substrate. Addition of HBpin then occurs across the ruthenium carbene bond and reductive elimination produces the bicyclo[3.1.0]­hexane product.

Group 9: Co-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Cobalt porphyrin catalysts have been investigated extensively in metalloradical catalysis (MRC) for carbene transfer with much of the pioneering work using donor and acceptor carbenes being accomplished by the Zhang and de Bruin groups. ,− Early examples of work using other cobalt catalysts include those by Otsuka, Nakamura, and co-workers in the 1970s, which showed that the use of dioximato-based ligands with cobalt could promote cyclopropanations using diazoesters. − Additionally, in the late 1990s and early 2000s Yamada, Katsuki, and others began studying ketoiminato-based salen ligands with Co, , and in 2002 Yamada and co-workers found that in contrast to analogous copper complexes in which the metal-carbene bond is a double bond, in cobalt carbene complexes the Co–C bond is better represented as a single bond with radical character on the ester moiety.

Several groups have developed methods to synthesize heterocycles using nonstabilized carbenes with Co porphyrin catalysis. The Che group found the use of N-tosylhydrazones as diazoalkane precursors enables the intramolecular cyclopropanation of indoles and pyrroles to form polycyclic heterocycles (Scheme ). The reaction using indole traps tolerated a broad range of substitution, including a range of alkyl or aryl substituents at the bridgehead positions of the cyclopropane products; however, the substitution patterns are slightly more limited with pyrroles.

Co-Catalyzed Cyclopropanations of Indoles and Pyrroles

Che and co-workers were also able to use hydrazones as carbene precursors with Co­(TPP) to perform intramolecular Buchner cyclopropanation reactions (Scheme ). Using this chemistry, the authors were able to access polycycles which would be difficult to synthesize by other means.

By further developing their pyrrolidine cyclopropanations, the Che group was able to access polysubstituted pyrrolizidines and pyrrolizines (Scheme ). The reaction involves hydrazone formation in situ, followed by a cyclopropanation-ring-opening cascade sequence. Cyclopropanation-Ring-Opening Cascade Reaction of Pyrroles via Cobalt Carbenes

A major development in the field occurred in 2018, when the Uyeda group introduced Co catalysts for reductive cyclopropanation reactions using 1,1-dihaloalkanes as carbene precursors. The decision to move from redox neutral to net reductive reaction sequences was broadly enabling for nonstabilized carbene catalysis and enabled by the development of redox active pyridine diimine (PDI) and oxazoline iminopyridine (OIP) ligands. In the general proposed mechanism, a cobalt complex undergoes oxidative addition with a gem-dichloroalkane to afford a Co carbenoid. This cobalt complex transfers the dialkyl carbene to an alkene to produce a cyclopropane and a dichlorocobalt species. Electron transfer by a terminal reductant regenerates the catalyst. The Uyeda group has leveraged this strategy to transfer methylene groups from dibromomethane , and dialkyl carbene groups derived from gem-dichloroalkanes in racemic (Generations 1 and 2, Scheme ) and enantioselective versions of the cyclopropanation reaction (Generation 3, Scheme ). , The scope of alkenes that react with the cobalt carbenoid is quite broad; unactivated, cyclic, electron-deficient alkenes, and dienes all participate in the reaction. In addition to transfer of dimethyl carbenes, this cobalt-catalyzed approach is effective in cyclopropanations to afford strained spirocycles. However, the scope of dihaloalkanes is limited to dihalomethanes, dihalopropanes, and a few 1,1-dihalocycloalkanes.

Key to the success of these reactions is the nature of the reactive cobalt carbenoid. Though this intermediate is generated under reducing conditions using cobalt, a metalloradical intermediate is not involved. Instead, the authors describe a reactive cationic cobalt carbenoid, formed with assistance from zinc halides in the reaction mixture, as being a transition metal analog of the Simmons Smith reagent. Recent efforts from the Köenigs group have investigated the use of dialkyl hydrazones as nonstabilized Co carbene precursors in the cyclopropanation of styrenes to afford aryl cyclopropanes (Scheme ). Sulfonyl hydrazones have also been used as cobalt metalloradical precursors for intramolecular C–H alkylations. Mechanism and Selected Examples of MRC C–H Alkylation Reactions with Cobalt

Pitre and co-workers have published a methylenation reaction using vitamin B12 and dichloromethane using photocatalysis and propose a SN2-type nucleophilic substitution with CH2Cl2 as a key mechanistic step.

The de Bruin group was able to leverage similar reactivity to produce substituted piperidines (Scheme ). Mechanistic studies found that the desired piperidine products are formed by a 1,6-HAT process from the cobalt­(III)-carbene species. The resulting stabilized radical undergoes a rebound process to yield product. However, a competitive 1,2-β-hydrogen transfer step leads to linear alkenes as a side-product of the reaction. The authors note that the stability of the radical species formed at the α position to nitrogen significantly affects the ratio of heterocycle to linear alkene.

Co-Catalyzed C–H Alkylation Reactions to form Piperidines

Recent work by Wang and co-workers has found that gem-dibromocyclopropanes can be used as nonstabilized carbene precursors to make allenes. The reaction conditions include a cobalt­(II) phthalocyanine, an iridium photocatalyst, and blue light, and the authors suggest a cyclopropyl carbene as a key intermediate.

Group 9: Rhodium-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Rhodium carbenes have been used extensively in cyclopropanation, C–H insertion, and X–H insertion reactions, among others. Much of the existing rhodium carbene chemistry uses diazo compounds as carbene precursors. ,,, The mechanism for diazo decomposition was first proposed by Yates in 1952 with copper and has generally been accepted for rhodium as well. For Rh­(II), the diazo adds to the coordinatively unsaturated axial position. The diazonium intermediate has been observed by optical spectroscopy and NMR. Dinitrogen is then extruded from the diazonium to form the metal carbene; this step is thought to be rate-limiting and irreversible for cyclopropanation reactions. Hengge, Wu, and co-workers later measured a large and normal nitrogen-15 kinetic isotope effect (KIE) (approximately 1.035 ± 0.003 at 0 °C) lending support for the above mechanism. The large magnitude of the KIE indicates a significant amount of C–N bond cleavage occurs during the transition state of the rate-determining step.

Most existing rhodium carbene chemistry was developed using stabilized carbenes; the use of nonstabilized carbenes with rhodium is quite rare and an underdeveloped area because of the propensity of alkyl substituted Rh­(II) carbenes to undergo 1,2-hydride shifts. Work by Fox and co-workers is noteworthy, since these studies serve as early attempts to harness alkyl groups on Rh­(II)-carbenes and avoid β-hydride elimination by using bulky catalysts at low temperatures.

Zhou and Wang disclosed a rhodium-catalyzed intramolecular cyclopropanation using alkyl trisylhydrazones in 2023 (Scheme ). The authors propose that the hydrazone decomposes to the diazo under the reaction conditions and with loss of dinitrogen, a rhodium carbene is formed. This carbene then undergoes an intramolecular cyclopropanation with a tethered alkene to form the bicyclo[3.1.0]­hexane products. A one-pot method starting from the aldehyde was also effective.

Rh-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation Using Hydrazones

In 2012 de Bruin and co-workers reported the use of sulfonium ylides as nonstabilized carbene precursors with rhodium for C1 polymerization reactions to produce polymethylene (Scheme ). This can be conceptualized as a series of carbene insertion reactions. The authors found that rhodium complexes containing bulkier dienes were most effective. Using a combination of ethyl diazoacetate and a sulfonium ylide, a diblock copolymer could be obtained.

Rh-Catalyzed C1 Polymerizations

Nagaiah and co-workers found in 2016 that rhodium­(II) is an effective catalyst for N–H bond insertion with amides and N-tosylhydrazones (Scheme ). The majority of the scope consists of stabilized carbene precursors, namely aryl hydrazones. However, alkyl hydrazones were also effective in the reaction, with no reduction in yields. The first step in the mechanism is proposed to be oxidation of the dihydroquinazolinone to the quinazolinone mediated by rhodium. The hydrazone undergoes decomposition to the diazo, which forms the rhodium carbene upon extrusion of dinitrogen. This carbene intermediate then undergoes insertion into the N–H bond of the amide, which the authors note is likely a stepwise process. The ylide formed after N–H insertion then undergoes a proton transfer to give product.

Rh-Catalyzed N–H Insertion into Amides

Many of the existing studies using nonstabilized carbenes with rhodium catalysis primarily consist of cycloisomerization and cascade reactions, although limited examples of other work can be found. Stoltz and May in 2002 disclosed the use of Eschenmoser hydrazones as carbene precursors with rhodium catalysts for a tandem Bamford-Stevens/Claisen rearrangement (Scheme ). The authors propose that the Eschenmoser hydrazones used in the reaction decompose with heat to form diazo intermediates, which are intercepted by the rhodium catalyst to form rhodium carbene. A 1,2-hydride migration then occurs to form a 1,5-diene stereoselectively, and a subsequent Claisen rearrangement yields the aldehyde products. Only one example of a nonstabilized carbene precursor (in this case, a dialkyl hydrazone) is included in this report, and although the reaction proceeds in good yield, elevated temperatures are required.

Rh-Catalyzed Tandem Bamford-Stevens/Claisen Rearrangement

In 2015, Pla-Quintana and co-workers published a diyne cascade reaction using N-arylsulfonylhydrazones with rhodium catalysis (Scheme ). The carbenes formed in this system are not stabilized in a traditional sense, although the authors describe them as donor carbenoids. The authors propose a mechanism in which the hydrazone first decomposes to form the rhodium carbene, which then complexes with the tethered alkyne. Through an electrocyclic process, the alkyne is added to the carbene and the newly formed rhodacyclobutene undergoes ring-opening to form a vinyl rhodium carbene. This vinyl rhodium carbene then reacts with the second alkyne to form a second vinyl rhodium carbene, and after a migratory insertion and attack by a sulfinate anion, the cyclized product is released. Although the reaction is enantioselective, the authors do not assign the absolute stereochemistry of the products.

Enantioselective Rh-Catalyzed Cascade Reaction with Arylsulfonylhydrazones

The same group, in 2016, disclosed an enantioselective rhodium-catalyzed cascade cyclopropanation (Scheme ). The major difference between the diyne cascade reaction above and the cyclopropanation described here is the use of hydrogen gas to facilitate the formation of active catalyst–a rhodium hydride. The reaction proceeds through a similar mechanism as above, although an intramolecular cyclopropanation step occurs to produce a vinylcyclopropane to turnover the catalyst. The authors note that substitution on the tethered alkene has a substantial effect on the reaction; even a methyl group at the terminal position of the olefin decreases yields significantly.

Enantioselective Rh-Catalyzed Cascade Cyclopropanation with Arylsulfonylhydrazones

In 2015, May and Le described the use of alkyl hydrazones with rhodium catalysis to form polycyclic products and noted that cyclopropenes were intermediates formed during the reaction (Scheme ). The authors propose that first rhodium assists in the decomposition of the hydrazone to form a rhodium carbene. Cyclopropenation of the tethered alkyne occurs and the cyclopropene intermediate then isomerizes to form a vinyl rhodium carbene. C–H insertion finally forms the polycyclic product.

Rh-Catalyzed Synthesis of Polycyclic Scaffolds

Burns and co-workers in 2021 used N-sulfonyl-1,2,3-triazoles as vicinal dicarbene equivalents with rhodium catalysis to produce cylobutenes and proposed a cyclopropyl rhodium carbene as an intermediate. Wang and Huo later found that cyclopropyl N-tosylhydrazones undergo ring expansions with Rh­(II) catalysis (Scheme ). The majority of the scope consists of aryl hydrazones, which produce stabilized carbenes, but the authors found that alkyl hydrazones were also competent in the reaction, although the isolated yields of products were low. Hydrazones with substitution on the cyclopropyl ring were also tolerated.

Group 10: Nickel-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

In 1973, Noyori reported that copper, silver, rhodium, and other metals catalyze the ring-opening isomerization of bicyclo[1.1.0]­butanes through an electrophilic metal carbene, while nickel forms nucleophilic carbenes that can undergo other reactions such as cyclopropanation (Scheme ). Noyori also found that metal carbenes formed from bicyclo[1.1.0] butane typically have significant ylide (nickel, rhodium) or inverse ylide (silver, copper) character. Soon after the 1973 report, Noyori and co-workers disclosed that nickel carbenes from bicyclo[1.1.0]­butanes reacted with 1,1-disubsituted alkenes such as methyl fumarate to form cyclopropanes stereospecifically. , This result indicates that the cyclopropanation likely occurs through a pathway involving the formation of a metallacyclobutane and a subsequent concerted reductive elimination. Although bicyclo[1.1.0]­butane is a relatively unattractive carbene precursor due to its difficult synthesis, this work is an early example of nickel-carbene cyclopropanation reactivity and the discovery that nickel carbenes are nucleophilic was an important mechanistic insight. Later work by Aggarwal and co-workers used another strained ring, [1.1.1]­propellane, with nickel catalysis, building off this early Noyori work.

Bicyclo­[1.1.0]­butane as a Carbene Precursor with Ni(0)

Otsuka and Ibers disclosed in 1977 that diazomethane reacts with substituted olefins in the presence of Ni(0) or Pd(0) to form the corresponding cyclopropanes (Scheme ). The authors note that the nickel-catalyzed cyclopropanation is only effective in good yields with electron-poor olefins such as methyl acrylate, which is likely due to the nucleophilicity of the nickel carbene. Although pyrazolines form between diazomethane and olefins in the absence of metal catalyst, these pyrazolines do not ring contract to form cyclopropane even in the presence of metal.

Ni­(0)-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation with Diazomethane

In 1983, Kanai and co-workers reported the cyclopropanation of alkenes using dihalomethanes as carbene precursors with a Ni(0) catalyst (Scheme ). The authors propose that the dihaloalkane undergoes oxidative addition with the Ni(0) catalyst. Subsequent dehalogenation with a reduced Ni species or zinc produces a nickel alkylidene intermediate. After coordination of the alkene, a nickelacyclobutane is formed. Reductive elimination then provides the cyclopropane product. Studies from Moret, Hillhouse, and others support this mechanism. − The reaction is limited to dibromomethane and diiodomethane as carbene precursors and only electron-deficient alkenes are effective.

Ni-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation Using Dihalomethanes

There are limited examples of transferring nonstabilized carbenes using nickel catalysis; however, fundamental studies by Hillhouse in the 2000s provided insight into the general reactivity of nickel carbenes (Scheme ). − Hillhouse and co-workers subjected a Ni(0) bis­(di-tert-butylphosphino)­ethane complex to diphenyldiazomethane and observed the formation of a nickel diazirine complex. Upon treatment with Sm­(OTf)3, the intermediate nickel diazirine extrudes N2 and a nickel carbene is produced, which was characterized by X-ray crystallographic analysis. Additionally, heating the nickel carbene complex in the presence of ethylene afforded 1,1-diphenylcyclopropane. These nickel(0) diphenyl carbene complexes were shown to be nucleophilic on carbon instead of electrophilic, which is atypical for late transition metals and in agreement with the conclusions drawn by Noyori in the 1970s. This is hypothesized to be due to the low electronegativity of nickel, which polarizes the bond toward carbon.

Synthesis of a Stable Nickel Carbene

Chen and co-workers in 2015 disclosed a nickel-catalyzed cyclopropanation reaction which uses tetramethylammonium triflate as the carbene precursor (Scheme ). In this system, the lithiated ammonium salt is generated by reaction of methyl tetramethylammonium triflate with n-BuLi. The authors propose that the lithium carbenoid adds to nickel to form a bridged nickel–lithium-methyl trimethylammonium species and, upon elimination of trimethylamine, the nickel carbene. Later mechanistic studies by the same group found the cyclopropanation likely occurred by a similar mechanism to that proposed by Kanai and co-workers, invoking the formation of a nickelacyclobutane and subsequent reductive elimination to release product. The reaction is very limited, as only norbornene and 4-phenyl-1-butene are reported to yield significant amounts of cyclopropane products. Homocoupling is a prevalent and unproductive side reaction in the catalytic cycle.

Ni-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation with an Ylide Precursor

Work from the Uyeda group utilized a dinuclear nickel catalyst to accomplish a reductive cyclopropanation of alkenes using dichloromethane as a carbene precursor; however, this catalyst was unable to react with 1,1-diahaloalkanes to transfer nonstabilized carbenes (Scheme ). Although only methylene transfer is possible with this system, a variety of alkenes are effective in the cyclopropanation, including 1,1-disubstituted alkenes, a small selection of heterocycles and carbocycles, and activated alkenes such as styrene derivatives and α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds. The Uyeda group has also accomplished cyclooligomerizations of enones through iterative metallacycle ring expansions using a similar system.

Ni-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation Using Dichoromethane as a Carbene Precursor

The Uyeda group then published a report on enantioselective methylenation of electron-deficient olefins taking advantage of nickel carbene electronics with a chiral catalyst (Scheme ). The scope of alkenes was limited to those containing α-aryl groups or α-electron-withdrawing groups. The authors observed that the cyclopropanation was not stereospecific, indicating a stepwise mechanism. To account for the stereochemical outcome of the reaction, two mechanistic possibilities were proposed: the first is a retro-[2 + 2] step that cleaves the Cα–Cβ bond and a subsequent [2 + 2] step that recombines the two fragments in the trans configuration. The second possibility is a reversible β-hydride elimination followed by reinsertion. The mechanism of the reaction has still not been definitively determined, although the authors note that a nickelacyclobutane mechanism was calculated by DFT to be lower in energy than a radical mechanism.

Enantioselective Ni-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation Using Dichoromethane as a Carbene Precursor

Lloret-Fillol and co-workers have also used dichloromethane as the source of a methylene unit for cyclopropanation. In the Lloret-Fillol system, an iridium photocatalyst and a nickel complex function in a dual catalytic system to effect cyclopropanation of various alkenes. However, the authors do not invoke metal carbenes as intermediates in the proposed mechanism.

Although Julia has reported nickel-catalyzed cyclopropanations of styrenes, recent work by the Wilkerson-Hill group has shown that sulfone anions are able to cyclopropanate styrene derivatives in the absence of nickel.

In 2025, the Noël group published an electrochemical cyclopropanation using a nickel­(II) precatalyst (Scheme ). The reaction uses dichloroalkanes as carbene precursors. The authors propose that the nickel­(II) precatalyst is electrochemically reduced to nickel(0). Further reduction at the cathode in the presence of the dichloroalkane produces the nickel carbene intermediate, which then forms a nickelacyclobutane intermediate through cycloaddition with the alkene. The nickelacyclobutane then undergoes reductive elimination to produce the cyclopropane. Notably, the use of cyclic enones as the alkene partner results in the corresponding β-methylated products rather than cyclopropanes. Additionally, the scope of carbene precursor is quite limited, as only dichloromethane, d 2-dichloromethane, chloroform, d 1-chloroform, and 1,1-dichloroethane were used in the reaction. The scope of alkenes was also limited to styrene derivatives and alkenes containing α-electron-withdrawing groups.

Electrochemical Ni-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation

Waddell in 1990 found that [1.1.1]­propellane could also act as a metallocarbene precursor. Aggarwal and co-workers have capitalized on that knowledge by using [1.1.1]­propellane with nickel catalysis for cyclopropanations (Scheme ). The authors found that the reaction is highly stereospecific and propose a mechanism where ring-opening of the propellane affords a nickel(0) carbene intermediate. Subsequent formation of a nickelacyclobutane with the alkene and reductive elimination afford the spirocyclic products. Like other nickel-catalyzed cyclopropanations discussed here, the scope of alkenes was also limited to styrene derivatives and alkenes containing α-electron-withdrawing groups.

[1.1.1]­propellane as Carbene Precursor with Ni

In 2012, Hirano and Miura disclosed a nickel-catalyzed alkylation of azoles using nonstabilized carbene intermediates derived from hydrazones. They propose the formal C–H insertion occurs from 1,2-aryl migration of an aryl nickel­(II) intermediate into the carbene carbon followed by protodemetalation to produce the alkylated heterocyclic products (Scheme ).

Group 10: Palladium-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Historically, cyclopropanations with palladium catalysis commonly employ diazomethane, diazotrimethylsilane, or ethyl diazoacetate as carbene precursors. Notably, palladium­(II) precatalysts such as Pd­(OAc)2 and PdCl2 are effective for cyclopropanations with diazomethane as a carbene precursor and in fact are often more effective with diazomethane than with ethyl diazoacetate. − Conversely, other metals commonly used for cyclopropanation reactions, such as rhodium and copper, are much more effective with ethyl diazoacetate and typically perform much less effectively with diazomethane. Cyclopropanation with diazomethane has been reviewed extensively in the past and therefore this perspective will not include a comprehensive discussion of the use of diazomethane as a carbene precursor with palladium.

However, to provide relevant context, a brief discussion of the historical use of diazomethane with palladium catalysis is included here. Paulissen and co-workers in 1972 disclosed a palladium-catalyzed cyclopropanation reaction, using styrenes as the olefin partner. Work by Vorbrüggen and co-workers extended this system to α,β-unsaturated carbonyls as the olefin partners, and Suda in 1981 found that Pd­(OAc)2 is also effective for the cyclopropanation of various terminal alkenes. , Denmark and co-workers in 1997 disclosed a system for cyclopropanation using palladium­(II) and bis­(oxazoline) ligands with diazomethane (Scheme ). While the structure of the bis­(oxazoline) ligand had a significant impact on the yields of cyclopropanation, no enantioselectivity was obtained in the reaction using chiral nonracemic ligands. The authors propose that the reaction takes place through a Pd­(II) carbene or carbenoid intermediate, potentially through a metallacyclobutane pathway. The authors also suggest that the lack of asymmetric induction is due to partial or full ligand disassociation during the reaction.

Pd-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation without Asymmetric Induction

Palladium catalysts have been extensively studied and reviewed for their ability to form nonstabilized carbenes derived from hydrazones due to facile 1,2-migrations of aryl Pd­(II) carbenes. ,, This type of reactivity, which has been extensively studied by Barluenga, Valdes, and Wang, will not be comprehensively discussed in this review. Seminal work by Van Vranken using trimethylsilyldiazomethane established cross-coupling with palladium carbenes. In 2007, Barluenga and Valdés reported the use of dialkyl hydrazone reagents with an aryl bromide using palladium catalysis to yield aryl alkene products after β-H elimination (Scheme A). This study established the use of resonance stabilized and nonstabilized carbenes in palladium catalysis. Pd­(II) carbenes bearing a migrating group (aryl, alkenyl, alkynyl, benzyl), obtained from either oxidative addition or transmetalation, can undergo the well-established 1,2-migration followed by β-H elimination.

General Reactivity of Palladium with Non-stabilized Carbenes

To favor pathways beyond rapid β-H elimination (e.g., ring-formation, reductive arylations or other cascade processes), researchers have utilized benzaldehyde-derived compounds lacking β-hydrogen atoms (Scheme B). Thus, large swaths of the reported reactivity of palladium carbenes still rely on resonance stabilized carbene intermediates and are primarily on using the 1,2-migration step. Less attention has been given to traditional carbene transformations such as cyclopropanation reactions and X–H insertions using nonstabilized palladium carbenes (Scheme C). This gap in the literature is due to a lack of strategies to outcompete elimination pathways that occur by 1,2-aryl, -alkyl or –H migrations upon formation of a Pd carbene. However, these observations provide opportunities to discover new strategies to overcome the undesired side reactivity and experimentally probe the fundamental nature of palladium carbenes.

In 2002, Markó and co-workers reported the use of Pd­(OAc)2 with diazoethane to cyclopropanate vinylboronates. The authors note that the cyclopropanation occurs with good diastereo- and chemoselectivity and diazobutane is also suitable as a carbene precursor. Dienes are also tolerated and produce the monocyclopropane.

Mechanistically, cyclopropanations using palladium carbenes are distinct from those using other metal carbenes such as rhodium. Most notably, olefin precoordination to the metal has been found to be an important aspect. − Studies by Straub and Marko have found that palladium-catalyzed cyclopropanations likely occur through a Pd(0) resting state; these studies and others suggest the intermediacy of a palladacyclobutane and a subsequent reductive elimination to release the cyclopropane product (Scheme ). Notably, Ortuño and co-workers have found that in the presence of diazomethane, palladium acetate rapidly decomposes to Pd(0) nanoparticles. These nanoparticles were observed to be active catalysts for cyclopropanations.

General Mechanism Based on Quantum Chemical Calculations and Kinetic Experiments

Several examples of transformations using nonstabilized palladium carbenes have been published in recent years. − Much of this reactivity relates to cross-coupling, 1,2-migration of aryl and other groups, and migratory insertion into the carbene carbon, which have been covered extensively in previous reviews and therefore will not be discussed in detail here. ,,,,

In 2023, the Wilkerson-Hill group reported the use of alkyl N-tosyl hydrazones with palladium catalysis to form spirocyclic cyclobutanes (Scheme ). The authors propose that the formation of the cyclobutene products occurs through the formation of a palladium carbene and a subsequent 1,2-migration. A syn-carbopalladation and reductive elimination then release the spirocyclic products. During this work, the authors noted that the identity of the migrating group and the oxidation level on palladium had a significant effect on product selectivity.

Migrating Groups on Pd Impact Product Selectivity

Very recently, the Wilkerson–Hill group reported a method for the intramolecular cyclopropanation of alkenes to obtain [2.1.0]-bicyclopentanes (housanes) (Scheme ). Upon reacting α,α-dialkyl-α-allyl hydrazones with NaOtBu in the presence of a Pd(0) precatalyst, housanes were obtained in good to excellent yield. The authors found that a precomplexed palladium NHC precatalyst developed by Beller and co-workers provided the most consistent reactivity. Additionally, the authors showed that the reactivity of these palladium(0) carbenes could be modulated by changing the ligand. When a phosphoramidite, rac-MonoPhos, was used as a ligand, the reaction produced vinylcyclopropane products through an intramolecular C–H insertion (8 examples). Preliminary mechanistic studies suggested the reactions proceeded through the intermediacy of a palladium(0) carbene, generated by reaction of the diazo intermediate with palladium. The authors conducted deuterium labeling studies which showed no scrambling during the C–H insertion process. Additionally, when (R)-MonoPhos was used, the products were obtained in 31% ee. This represents one of only a few examples of enantioselective C–H insertion using nonstabilized Pd-carbenes. −

Divergent Reactivity of Pd Carbenes: Cyclopropanation and C–H Insertion

A recent report from Xia, Yan, and co-workers uses alkyl hydrazones as carbene precursors to form alkylidene cyclobutanes enantioselectively through a carbene coupling and represents another example of enantioselective palladium catalysis using nonstabilized carbene precursors. The reaction leverages a stereoselective 1,2-aryl migration elementary step.

The Wang group in 2018 reported a formal Si–H insertion reaction using tosylhydrazones as diazo precursors with palladium (Scheme ). Although this reactivity is proposed to occur through a migratory insertion into the carbene, as a formal X–H insertion reaction with a nonstabilized carbene a detailed description is warranted. The authors propose that the silane undergoes an oxidative addition to palladium. The generated palladium­(II) species reacts with the diazo formed in situ from hydrazone decomposition to form a palladium­(II) carbene. Next, either the hydride may migrate into the carbon of the carbene to form an alkyl palladium species, or migratory insertion of the silyl group into the carbene may occur to give an alkyl palladium hydride. Reductive elimination then occurs to release the alkylsilane products. Double Si–H insertion products could be obtained in the reaction also.

Group 10: Platinum-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Platinum, like gold and other 5d metals, forms carbenes with particularly high bond energies, according to ab initio calculations. Platinum carbenes are typically described as Fischer carbenes, and the Pt–C­(sp2) bond has significant single-bond character (Scheme ). This may be attributed to poor orbital overlap and high d-electron counts. In fact, the amount of single-bond character is significant enough that carbenoid ligands bound to platinum can be represented as metal-stabilized carbocations and the structural data supports these observations as well.

Platinum Carbenoids May be Represented Multiple Ways

Carmona and co-workers in 2012 achieved the synthesis of a Pt­(II) carbene complex and demonstrated its high electrophilicity (Scheme ). Templeton and co-workers synthesized a Pt­(IV) ethylidene complex shortly after. The observed 13C NMR shift of the carbene carbon is remarkably downfield (δ = 499.8 ppm in CD2Cl2) and implies the Pt­(IV) carbene is highly electrophilic, with significant cationic character, and that the platinum center is weakly π-back-bonding to the carbene. In contrast, Iluc and co-workers have synthesized and characterized a diaryl platinum carbene with high π character, short M–C bond lengths, and hindered rotation around the platinum carbene bond, indicating a platinum carbene with double bond character. These differences in carbene binding and Pt–C bond character are likely due to the differences in Pt-oxidation states and coordination. Methods using nonstabilized Pt carbenes in catalysis are mostly comprised of cycloisomerization reactions with alkynes as starting materials. −

Group 11: Copper-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Although copper-catalyzed cyclopropanations are well-precedented, to our knowledge very few of the existing systems are effective for transferring nonstabilized carbenes. However, mechanistic work done on systems using stabilized carbenes is informative. In 1972 Kochi and Salomon found that the coordination of olefins to copper is highly sensitive to the nature of the ligands on the metal. Additionally, the authors found that the copper carbenoid (organocopper diazonium salts), which are potential intermediates in the reaction, are not stable and were unable to be detected by 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Straub and co-workers were first to synthesize and spectroscopically characterize a copper­(I) carbene complex (Scheme ). Later mechanistic studies by Straub and co-workers found that copper-catalyzed cyclopropanations likely proceed through a one-step, intermolecular, direct carbene transfer process. The authors also found that the copper carbene must be stabilized by backbonding. Metallacyclobutane intermediates have been invoked for copper-catalyzed cyclopropanations by others, and later computational work by Howell in 2006 found that cupracyclobutanes are stable intermediates in the catalytic cycle in most cases.

First Characterized copper­(I) Carbene Species

In 2004 Warren and Dai synthesized a dicopper carbene complex [(β-diketoiminato)­Cu]2(μ-CPh2). Reaction of the dicopper carbene complex with styrene produced the cyclopropane in quantitative yield. Upon synthesis of the terminal carbene complex (β-diketoiminato)Cu = CPh2, the authors found this complex was also reactive with styrenes to produce cyclopropane products. A Hammett study revealed the copper carbene complex to have significant electrophilic character. A later study by Hoffman and co-workers supports the conclusion that copper carbenes are electrophilic.

Sulfonium ylides have been used as precursors to nonstabilized carbenes with copper. In 1974 Cohen and co-workers reported that diphenyl methyl sulfonium ylide in the presence of copper­(II) acetylacetonate was competent in a cyclopropanation reaction with olefins (Scheme ). Although the reaction was better performing when the ylide was generated in situ from methyldiphenylsulfonium tetrafluoroborate and strong base, the use of preformed ylide was also effective.

Cu­(II)-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation with a Sulfur Ylide

Julia and co-workers found in 1991 that the use of copper­(II) bis­(3-n-pentylacetylacetonate) as a catalyst with diphenyl methyl sulfonium ylide enabled the use of milder conditions (Scheme ). This system also resulted in higher yields for some substrates. Only diphenyl methyl sulfonium ylide was used as a carbene precursor, and the alkene scope was also relatively limited.

A Modified System for Cu­(II)-Catalyzed Cyclopropanation with a Sulfur Ylide

In 2024 Betley and co-workers disclosed the synthesis and characterization of copper benzylidene complexes. Although these are not strictly nonstabilized carbenes, this work is relevant here as a rare example of isolated copper carbenes shown to display C–H insertion reactivity. The authors demonstrated that the copper benzylidene complexes can be characterized as Fischer carbenes, with electrophilic character. The carbenes react with styrene to form cyclopropanes and undergo C–H insertion with tetrahydrofuran to form the alkylated product.

In contrast to the other metals in this perspective, most copper-catalyzed reactions with nonstabilized carbenes are cross-couplings or alkylations. − In 2004, Cruz-Almanza and co-workers found that Cu­(II) was effective with α-diazo compounds and tosylhydrazones to alkylate various imidazoles (Scheme ). The authors propose the formation of a copper carbene intermediate, which then reacts with the imidazole to yield alkylated products. However, the scope includes only one example of a nonstabilized carbene precursor.

Alkylation of Imidazole with a Cu­(I)-Carbene

The Wang group has reported several copper-catalyzed couplings which use hydrazones as carbene precursors with alkynes. The first report, in 2011, found that substituted allenes could be obtained using alkynes and N-tosylhydrazones with catalytic copper (Scheme ). The authors propose a mechanism where first a copper acetylide is formed, which reacts with diazo generated from the tosylhydrazone to form a copper carbene species. Migratory insertion produces an intermediate which is protonated to release the product and regenerate copper­(I). Later computational studies by Bai, Lan, and co-workers suggest a mechanism in which direct nucleophilic attack of the protonated alkyne occurs on the copper carbene rather than the formation of a copper acetylide. This forms a vinyl cation, which is deprotonated to release the product. Although most examples utilize aryl hydrazones, alkyl hydrazones also work, although in lower yields.

Cu-Catalyzed Alkyne-Hydrazone Coupling

In 2013, the Wang group reported a simplified method for the alkyne-hydrazone coupling chemistry (Scheme ). While the previous system required the use of a complex bisoxazoline ligand, the simplified conditions utilize copper­(I) iodide without added ligand. The scope of alkyl hydrazones in this second-generation system is also slightly expanded, and yields are improved in some cases.

Updated Alkyne-Hydrazone Coupling Method

Almost concurrently, the same group disclosed a method for synthesizing allyl allenes (Scheme ). By modifying the previous conditions to include a stronger base and allyl halides, the reaction becomes a three-component coupling which produces tri- and tetra-substituted allenes. However, the scope of hydrazones without aryl substitution is limited.

The Wang group found that the use of alkynes with silyl substitution as coupling partners with tosylhydrazones resulted in alkynylation products rather than in allenes (Scheme ). Tosylhydrazones have also been used as carbene precursors in coupling reactions with amines and amides. In 2011, Alami and co-workers reported a copper-catalyzed reductive coupling of hydrazones with amines to form α-branched amines (Scheme ). The authors propose a mechanism in which an amine undergoes N–H insertion with the copper carbene formed from the hydrazone. A proton transfer then produces the amine product. The scope of hydrazones without aryl substitution is quite limited and anilines do not produce product in this system. The Alami group later modified their reductive coupling system to enable the synthesis of α-aryl amines using anilines as coupling partners. The authors found that the use of rac-BINAP as ligand and the addition of water were necessary for an effective coupling between hydrazones and anilines. However, hydrazones without aryl substitution (which would form nonstabilized copper carbenes as intermediates) do not work under the modified conditions.

Cu-Catalyzed Reductive Coupling with Amines

Yadav and Yadav in 2014 found that tosylhydrazones made in situ could be coupled with phthalimide using copper catalysis and subsequently deprotected to obtain primary amines in a modification of the Gabriel amine synthesis (Scheme ). The mechanism proposed by the authors involves the formation of a copper carbene after decomposition of the hydrazone and subsequent N–H insertion of the carbene into phthalimide yields protected amines. These products can then be deprotected to access the primary amines. The series of three steps that make up this procedure (i.e., condensation, coupling with phthalimide, and deprotection) can be performed in one pot. Again, the scope of hydrazones without aryl substitution is quite limited, however the alkyl hydrazones are equally or more effective in the reaction than the aryl hydrazones.

A Copper-Catalyzed Modified Gabriel Amine Synthesis

In 2015 Wang, Han, and Xu reported reaction conditions for the reductive coupling of amides and tosylhdrazones to produce secondary amides (Scheme ). The authors propose the formation of a copper carbene from the hydrazone. In contrast to previously discussed systems, nucleophilic attack of the amide anion on the carbene generates an anionic copper species, which undergoes a proton transfer to release the product. Although aliphatic hydrazones were less reactive than aryl hydrazones, additional tetrabutylammonium iodide, which may be acting as a phase-transfer catalyst, enabled the reaction to proceed in good yields. Although this system is limited to aldehyde-derived hydrazones, the same authors later reported a modification which enabled the synthesis of tertiary amides using ketone-derived hydrazones. However, this modified system is not effective with aliphatic hydrazones, which produce nonstabilized carbene precursors.

In 2015 the same group found that hydrazones could be used in a copper-catalyzed direct C–H bond functionalization of polyfluoroarenes (Scheme ). The mechanism proposed by the authors is similar to that proposed for the alkylation of iminopyridinium ylides above. It begins with the deprotonation of the polyfluoroarene, which undergoes transmetalation with the copper­(I) catalyst. This aryl copper species reacts with the diazo produced from base-catalyzed decomposition of the hydrazone to produce the copper carbene. Subsequent migratory insertion and protonation occur to yield the alkylated polyfluoroarene products. Although aliphatic hydrazones are competent in the reaction, the scope of nonstabilized carbene precursors given in this report is limited.

C–H Functionalization of Polyfluoroarenes

Wang and co-workers later reported that hydrazones were effective in a desulfinative coupling with sulfones to produce terminal alkenes (Scheme ). The proposed mechanism consists of deprotonation of dimethyl sulfone by the base and transmetalation with copper. This copper species then reacts with the diazo produced from base-catalyzed decomposition of the hydrazone to produce the copper carbene, migratory insertion occurs to form an alkyl copper species, and desulfination then releases the product alkenes. Both aryl and alkyl hydrazones are effective in the reaction, and the reaction was also tolerant of other sulfones, although the authors did not expand the sulfone scope significantly at the time.

In 2024, Zhang and co-workers developed a three-component coupling using [1.1.1]­propellanes as carbene precursors (Scheme ). Using organozinc reagents, 3,3-difluoroallyl sulfonium salts, and copper catalysis, exocyclic cyclobutenes and bicyclo[1.1.1]­pentanes were obtained. Silver-Catalyzed Carbene Transfer Reactions

Silver carbenes are highly electrophilic, due in part to silver’s π-acidity. These complexes have been typically described as Fischer carbenes. − The highly electrophilic nature of silver carbenes also results in a relative lack of information relating to their structure; these complexes have been challenging to isolate and characterize although the Straub and Fürstner groups have had some success. ,

Despite the difficulties of isolation, silver carbenes in particular have historically been implicated in classical diazo chemistry such as the Wolff rearrangement. Additionally, due to their highly electrophilic nature, silver carbenes are effective in C–H insertion reactions; the use of a silver precatalyst with a tris­(pyrazolyl)­borate ligand enabled a C–H insertion reaction to occur with methane gas. Although they have found use in cyclopropanations, C–H and X–H insertions, and other transformations, to the best of our knowledge there is very limited use of nonstabilized silver carbenes. −

In 2023, Hong and co-workers found that [1.1.1]­propellane is a nonstabilized carbene precursor using a silver salt as the metal source (Scheme ). The authors also leveraged this reaction to develop a three-component coupling with cyclic ethers and anilines or alcohols. The mechanism proposed in this report involves the initial ring-opening of the [1.1.1]­propellane with silver to form a silver carbene. Nucleophilic addition of a cyclic ether occurs to produce a silver-bound oxonium ion. The third coupling partner, an aniline or alcohol, is then able to initiate ring-opening of the cyclic ether. Subsequent deprotonation and protodemetalation yields the products.

Biocatalysis

To the best of our knowledge there are currently no published reports of biocatalytic carbene transfer with nonstabilized carbenes. In 2012 the Arnold group reported a cyclopropanation using engineered cytochrome P450 enzymes with ethyl diazoacetate as the carbene precursor and styrenes as the olefin partners. Later reports from Fasan and co-workers utilized engineered Mb enzymes for cyclopropanations, including intramolecularly cyclopropanation, to produce bicyclo[3.1.0]­hexanes. − Hartwig and co-workers have reported the use of iridium-containing enzymes derived from CYP119 for cyclopropanations as well. , While the Arnold, Fasan, and Hartwig groups have each achieved high enantioselectivities as well, these reports invariably use diazos with esters or other stabilizing groups at the α-position as the carbene precursors. A notable example is a communication by Arnold and co-workers in 2019, which disclosed the use of engineered cytochrome P450s with fluoroalkyl groups for C–H insertion. The authors found the engineered enzymes efficiently catalyzed an insertion reaction into α-amino C–H bonds with high enantioselectivity using perfluorinated alkyl diazos as carbene precursors.

Conclusion: Opportunities in the Catalytic Transfer of Nonstabilized Carbenes

Despite challenges in their generation, stability, and selective reactivity, nonstabilized metal carbenes are important intermediates in catalysis. The transfer of nonstabilized carbenes with transition metal catalysis yields products with fundamentally different substitution patterns than well-understood donor/acceptor carbene precursors. Further development of nonstabilized carbene transfer moves the field of cyclopropanation chemistry beyond methylenations. A deeper understanding of factors that impact this reactivity, such as metal identity, oxidation state, ligand effects, and substitution, will enable new transformations.

The development of new activation modes (radical approaches) for vetted carbenoid precursors (e.g., dihaloalkanes) has been broadly enabling to the field of nonstabilized carbene chemistry and has been spurred on by the rational design of novel ligands that stabilize radical intermediates. We expect further ligand design and refinement to enable progress in this area. We anticipate the development of novel and atom-economical carbene precursors will also be beneficial to this area of research. A greater mechanistic understanding will undoubtedly play a major role in future developments in nonstabilized carbene transfer, since it may enable new carbene precursors, new modes of reactivity, or the improvement and expansion of current catalytic systems. Specifically, deeper discussions of product distributions and quantification of byproducts arising from competitive metal carbene pathways (e.g., 1,2-hydride shifts) will be broadly enabling to reaction development. Finally, given the tremendous impact of biocatalysis on the reactivity of stabilized carbenes, we anticipate these strategies will undoubtedly have a positive impact on the transfer of nonstabilized carbenes. More specifically, we look forward to the expansion of the high enantioselectivities and turnovers already achieved with stabilized carbenes to future chemistry developed with nonstabilized precursors.
