AHSA1 as a prognostic biomarker and potential immunotherapeutic target in HNSCC: integrative bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq analyses and experimental validation
Authors: Feihan Gu, Erli Wu, Xiaorong Yan, Wenkai Huang, Siyuan Chen, Weimin Bao, Wenhua Xu, Yuanyin Wang, Ran Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeat shock 90 kDa protein ATPase homolog 1 (AHSA1), a chaperone of heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90), is upregulated in various malignancies, where it promotes the invasion, migration, and proliferation of cancer cells. Nevertheless, the precise function of AHSA1 in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) has yet to be investigated.MethodsTo comprehensively investigate AHSA1 profiles of expression in HNSCC, various techniques including bioinformatics analysis of public databases and qRT-PCR were utilized. To evaluate AHSA1’s clinical significance in HNSCC, survival analysis was performed using Cox regression models and Kaplan-Meier curves. The associations between AHSA1 expression, immune microenvironment characteristics, and drug sensitivity were analyzed through bioinformatics approaches. The cellular localization and potential functions of AHSA1 in HNSCC were further investigated through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis. Ultimately, both in vitro and in vivo experimental techniques were used to examine the biological functions of AHSA1 in HNSCC tumor growth and metastasis.ResultsIn HNSCC, AHSA1 expression was markedly upregulated and showed strong associations with clinicopathological factors such as disease stage, TP53 mutation status, and HPV infection status. Survival analysis identified AHSA1 as an independent prognostic biomarker, with elevated expression correlating with adverse clinical outcomes. Notably, an inverse relationship was observed between AHSA1 levels and tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations. Drug sensitivity analysis revealed enhanced therapeutic responsiveness to multiple chemotherapeutic agents in tumors with high AHSA1 expression. Functional validation experiments demonstrated that AHSA1 silencing suppressed tumor growth in HNSCC mice and significantly suppressed cellular proliferation, migration, and invasive potential.ConclusionIn HNSCC patients, AHSA1 might be a useful biomarker for prognostic evaluation and guidance for immunotherapy. Furthermore, inhibition of AHSA1 could effectively suppress tumor growth, invasion, and migration, underscoring its potential for HNSCC treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14833-2.Keywords: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, AHSA1, Immune infiltration, Prognosis, TCGA

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a prevalent malignant tumor that can arise in various regions, including the oral cavity, throat, larynx, lips, salivary glands, and nasal cavity [1]. Globally, HNSCC ranks as the seventh most prevalent cancer type [2]. Current clinical guidelines recommend a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach for the treatment of HNSCC [3]. However, HNSCC patients frequently have locally advanced illness at presentation, with high rates of metastasis, local recurrence, and resistance to standard chemotherapy, especially in cervical lymph nodes. Consequently, the treatment failure rate and mortality rate remain high among patients with advanced HNSCC [4]. It is worth noting that despite remarkable advancements in immunotherapy that have substantially enhanced overall survival and quality of life in HNSCC patients, clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory, with persistently low 5-year survival rates and limited therapeutic benefits observed in specific patient subgroups [5]. Therefore, a better comprehension of the molecular processes behind the development of HNSCC and the discovery of new therapeutic targets linked to prognosis and immune infiltration are essential for creating more potent treatment plans.

As a key element of the chaperone system, heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) is essential for preserving the conformational stability of a number of oncogenic client proteins, such as human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2), cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4), and rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF) [6, 7]. As a co-chaperone of HSP90, activator of 90 kDa heat shock protein ATPase homolog 1 (AHSA1) enhances HSP90 function by stimulating its ATPase activity through the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [8]. Multiple pieces of evidence have shown that AHSA1 promotes tumor progression, with demonstrated roles in invasion, migration, and proliferation across multiple cancers including myeloma, breast cancer, colorectal adenocarcinoma, osteosarcoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [9–13]. For example, AHSA1 was found to be increased in osteosarcoma, and its inhibition reduces malignant behaviors like invasion, migration, and proliferation by altering the Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis [14]. Furthermore, Gao et al. [10] reported that AHSA1 upregulation contributed to HCC progression by modulating cell cycle-related proteins. The above findings highlight the multifaceted roles of AHSA1 in cancer progression. Nevertheless, its precise function and underlying processes in HNSCC remain unknown.

In this study, we used a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to investigate the prognostic significance of AHSA1 and its impact on immune infiltration in HNSCC. Initially, we conducted comparative analyses of AHSA1 expression profiles between malignant and normal tissue samples, followed by systematic evaluation of its associations with clinicopathological parameters in HNSCC patients. Subsequently, we investigated the prognostic significance of AHSA1 in HNSCC and comprehensively characterized the tumor immune microenvironment using multiple bioinformatics algorithms. Lastly, in HNSCC, we investigated the functions of AHSA1 in tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. Our research indicates that AHSA1 may play a role in the initiation and spread of HNSCC and could be a useful prognostic biomarker, offering important new information for HNSCC diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methods

Data collection and gene expression analysis

The web server TIMER2 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) is utilized to detect the expression of AHSA1 in pan-cancer [15]. Subsequently, we analyzed two HNSCC transcriptomic datasets (GSE13601 and GSE30784) from the GEO repository to characterize AHSA1 expression patterns [16]. Then, UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) was applied for analyzing AHSA1 expression across various clinical classifications, including TP53 mutation status, HPV infection status, cancer stage, lymph node metastasis, gender, tumor grade, age, and ethnic subgroups [17].

Prognostic value analysis

To assess AHSA1’s prognostic significance in HNSCC, we employed GEPIA2.0 and Kaplan-Meier analysis to evaluate survival and confirmed results using the GSE41613 dataset from GEO. Also, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to ascertain the independent prognostic value of AHSA1 in HNSCC.

AHSA1-related gene identification and functional enrichment analysis

Using TCGA-HNSCC data and LinkedOmics, we conducted a thorough co-expression analysis of AHSA1 while enforcing strict thresholds (|cor| >0.4, FDR < 0.05) [18]. The limma R package was used to perform differential gene expression analysis on HNSCC samples that were classified into groups with high and low levels of AHSA1 expression (|logFC| >1.0, FDR < 0.05). Intersecting genes from co-expression and differential analyses were identified as AHSA1-associated genes and subsequently subjected to functional annotation using the “clusterProfiler” software for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis [19].

Immune infiltration analysis

A detailed analysis of the relationship between AHSA1 expression and immune cell infiltration in HNSCC was conducted using six computational algorithms: QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, EPIC, TIMER, CIBERSORT-ABS, and XCELL [20–22]. The “ESTIMATE” package was utilized to evaluate the TME status of the HNSCC samples [23]. Additionally, to find differences in immune function between the groups, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was also carried out. The several immunological pathways that were enriched between the groups with high and low expression of AHSA1 were evaluated using the “GSVA” software.

Assessment of immunological checkpoint genes and chemotherapy sensitivity

We used TIMER2 to examine the relationship between AHSA1 and immune checkpoint gene expression considering immune checkpoint inhibitors are essential for immunotherapy. To assess AHSA1’s impact on therapeutic response, we employed the “pRRophetic” package for concentration (IC50) prediction of chemotherapeutic agents in HNSCC patients [24, 25] and utilized CellMiner to evaluate drug sensitivity patterns across NCI-60 cancer cell lines based on AHSA1 expression levels [26].

scRNA-seq data processing

To characterize AHSA1 expression at single-cell levels in HNSCC, we conducted an analysis of the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE172577, which included six OSCC patient samples. First, the “LogNormalize” function in the “Seurat” software package was used to process and normalize the scRNA-seq data [27]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed in accordance with the published tutorial after batch effects between various data were rectified using the “Harmony” package [28]. The data was clustered using the t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) technique, and the marker genes for each cluster were located using the “FindAllMarkers” program. Cell type annotation was accomplished by integrating the CellMarker database with established cell type-specific markers. Furthermore, the “CellChat” package was employed to identify potential cell-cell communication differences between AHSA1-negative and AHSA1-positive cell types, aiming to elucidate the role of AHSA1 in the pathogenesis of HNSCC.

HNSCC patients and clinical samples

In this study, paired tissue samples were collected from 26 patients diagnosed with HNSCC who underwent surgery at the Stomatological Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Tumor sites included various anatomical regions of the head and neck, such as the oral cavity (tongue, gingiva, buccal mucosa, and floor of mouth), as well as non-oral sites like the larynx and hypopharynx. Freshly resected tumor tissues and the corresponding normal adjacent tissues (NATs) were rinsed with saline immediately after excision, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent paraffin embedding, protein extraction, and RNA isolation. All procedures were performed in strict accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and approved protocols.

Immunohistochemical staining

To investigate the expression differences of AHSA1 between healthy and HNSCC oral tissues, we performed immunohistochemical staining. The healthy and HNSCC samples were processed through paraffin embedding and dehydration. After dewaxing and rehydration, antigen retrieval was conducted using citrate buffer. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% BSA. The sections were incubated with the primary antibody overnight, followed by a 1-hour incubation with the secondary antibody. Detection was carried out using DAB, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the slides were examined under a light microscope. The immunohistochemical staining results were quantified using ImageJ software. For each sample, five randomly selected high-power fields were analyzed. The IHC Toolbox plugin was employed to separate DAB signals from hematoxylin counterstaining and to calculate both the positively stained area and mean optical density (MOD). All image analyses were performed under identical settings to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Cultures of cells and cell lines

The study utilized SCC9 and Cal27 cell lines obtained from HyCyte (China), along with HOK, FADU, HN6, and HN4 cells provided by Professor Xiang Li (Nanjing Medical University, China). Cal27, HOK, FADU, HN6, and HN4 cells were cultured in DMEM (Wisent, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (Wisent, China) under 37 °C and 5% CO₂ conditions. SCC9 cells were cultured in their own proprietary medium (HyCyte, China).

RNA extraction and qPCR

The TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to separate total RNA from tissue samples and cell lines. To create cDNA, reverse transcription was carried out using the Takara PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Cat. #RR047A). Quantitative real-time PCR was used to quantify gene expression, with the 2-ΔΔCt method used to calculate relative expression levels and GAPDH serving as the endogenous control. Supplementary Table 1 provides specific primer sequence information.

Lentivirus transduction

A lentiviral vector targeting AHSA1 gene silencing was designed and constructed by Anhui General Gene Technology based on the AHSA1 sequence. Three short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting AHSA1 (sh-AHSA1-1, sh-AHSA1-2, and sh-AHSA1-3), along with a negative control (sh-NC), were synthesized for gene silencing. The sequences were as follows: sh-AHSA1-1: 5’-GGATCCGCATCCATTAACAATCGCAAATTCAAGAGATTTGCGATTGTTAATGGATGCTTTTTGGTACC-3’; sh-AHSA1-2: 5’-GGATCCGCCCTGAGAAACATATTGTGATTTCAAGAGAATCACAATATGTTTCTCAGGGTTTTTGGTACC-3’; and sh-AHSA1-3: 5’-GGATCCGTAAGCTTGATGGAGAGGCATTTCAAGAGAATGCCTCTCCATCAAGCTTACTTTTTGGTACC-3’.Enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) expression, which was incorporated into the transfer plasmid, was used to track the effectiveness of transfection. Stable transfectants were selected using puromycin resistance, and all functional assays were conducted within a 2-week post-infection period to ensure experimental consistency.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

After being seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 10³ cells per well, SCC9 and CAL27 cells were cultivated for 1, 2, 3, and 4 days. The CCK-8 assay was used to measure cell viability. A microplate reader was used to measure the optical density (OD) at 450 nm following two hours of incubation with CCK-8 solution at 37 °C. Every experiment was carried out three times.

Wound healing assay

Use a 6-well plate to inoculate transfected cells and after 48 h, scrape the cells utilizing a pipette tip of 100 µl. After using phosphate buffer solution (PBS) to clean the cell surface, cell debris will be eliminated. After that, the cells were observed, captured on camera, and their locations in the images were recorded using a 100x magnification microscope. Following 24-hour incubation under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), wound healing progression was documented through microscopic imaging, and migration distances were quantified using ImageJ software for comparative analysis.

Colony formation assay

Transfected Cal27 and SCC9 cells were cultivated for 10–15 days at standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) after being seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well in 6-well plates. After being washed with PBS, colonies were fixed for 30 min with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained for 10 min with 0.1% crystal violet, and then quantified for comparison.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Transfected SCC9 and CAL27 cells were cultivated in the upper chamber of 24-well Transwell plates (8 μm pore size, BD Biosciences) for migration tests, while the lower chamber contained 600 µL of 10% FBS medium. Following a 24-hour incubation period, migrating cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. An inverted microscope was used to quantify the cells.

Xenograft model

The nude mice (female, aged 4–5 weeks, weighing 14–16 g) used in this study were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co., LTD. Twelve naked mice were split into two groups—six in each of the sh-NC and sh-AHSA1 groups—at random. After SCC9 cells were transfected steadily, they were cultivated to the logarithmic growth phase. Alcohol was applied to the skin under the right axilla, and then the cell suspension was injected using a syringe. Ensure that the nude mice are housed under SPF-rated conditions with constant temperature and humidity, alternating light and dark for 12 h each, and change the supplement regularly to ensure that the nude mice are fed ad libitum with feed and water. Following injection, daily attentive observation of the naked mice’s development and health is necessary. Every three days, the size of the tumor was measured, and the volume was computed using the formula V (mm³) = (width)² × length/2. Mice were put to sleep with isoflurane after a 28-day trial period, and they were killed by cervical dislocation. Tumors that were removed were weighed and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for further examination.

Statistical analysis

Clinicopathological correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test, while differential expression analysis across clinical subgroups was performed using the “edgeR” package with a quasi-likelihood F-test. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare TME scores, immune checkpoint expression, and drug sensitivity (IC50) between AHSA1 high- and low-expression groups. AHSA1 expression levels and immune cell infiltration patterns were evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Results

AHSA1 is highly expressed in HNSCC

Differential AHSA1 expression between tumor and normal tissues was seen in our pan-cancer analysis for a number of malignancies, including cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), and HNSCC (Fig. 1A). TCGA data analysis of 44 HNSCC samples confirmed significant AHSA1 upregulation compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1B). Consistent results were obtained from GEO datasets (GSE13601 and GSE30784), supporting AHSA1 overexpression in HNSCC (Figs. 1C-D). Subsequently, to validate our findings, we performed qRT-PCR analysis on 26 paired HNSCC tissue samples, which confirmed a significantly higher expression of AHSA1 in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1E). Immunohistochemical staining further demonstrated that AHSA1 protein levels were significantly elevated in HNSCC tissues compared to normal oral mucosa (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Fig. 1).

AHSA1 expression is associated with clinicopathological features of HNSCC

Subsequently, we investigated the association between AHSA1 expression and various clinicopathological features of HNSCC. Subgroup analysis based on UALCAN data revealed that AHSA1 was significantly upregulated across multiple clinical subgroups, including patients with TP53 mutations, HPV-positive status, metastatic disease, and those stratified by diverse demographic and pathological characteristics (Figs. 2A–F).

AHSA1’s prognostic significance in HNSCC

Subsequently, the impact of AHSA1 on the overall survival of patients with HNSCC was next investigated using the Kaplan-Meier plotter. According to survival analysis, patients with HNSCC who expressed high levels of AHSA1 had a significantly lower overall survival rate (HR = 1.56, p = 0.0015; Fig. 3A). The GSE41613 dataset substantiated this predictive connection (p = 0.037; Fig. 3B), and GEPIA2.0 analysis verified it (Fig. 3C). We then examined the relationship between AHSA1 and other clinical grades of patients with HNSCC. The findings showed that AHSA1 had a high predictive value at all clinical stages, especially in cases with advanced HNSCC (Figs. 3D-F). AHSA1 was consistently found to be an independent predictive factor in HNSCC by both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis of TCGA-HNSCC data (Figs. 3G-H).

Analysis of AHSA1-related genes

Specific signaling pathways associated with AHSA1-related genes were investigated using GSEA. The results revealed that AHSA1 exhibited a positive correlation with several pathways, including the regulation of beta cell development, DNA replication pre-initiation, and chromatin-modifying enzymes. In contrast, several immune-related pathways, including the B cell receptor signaling pathway, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and the T cell receptor signaling system, were negatively correlated with AHSA1 (Fig. 4A-F). The co-expression profile of AHSA1 in HNSCC was next investigated using the LinkedOmics database, which identified 666 genes that co-express AHSA1 (Fig. 4G-H). 1,426 genes that were differentially expressed between groups with high and low AHSA1 expression were found by comparative analysis (Fig. 4I). Following the integration of these datasets, 2,081 distinct AHSA1-associated genes were identified and subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. The findings showed a notable enrichment in immune-related processes, including Th17 cell development, cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, IL-17 signaling, and molecular mediators of immune response (Fig. 4J-K). All of these results point to AHSA1 as a possible key immunomodulator in HNSCC, affecting tumor immunogenicity and immunotherapy response via a variety of molecular pathways.

The connection between the TME and AHSA1

To further investigate the impact of AHSA1 on the TME, we comprehensively analyzed immune cell infiltration patterns in HNSCC. Our findings revealed significant negative correlations between AHSA1 expression and infiltration levels of multiple immune cell populations, including Tregs, CD4 + T cells, neutrophils, CD8 + T cells, macrophages, B cells, and NK cells (Fig. 5A). Comparative analysis of TME characteristics showed significantly reduced immune and ESTIMATE scores in AHSA1 high-expression patients compared to their low-expression counterparts (Fig. 5B-C). ssGSEA further demonstrated enhanced immune response activity in the AHSA1 low-expression group, suggesting an immunosuppressive role of AHSA1 in HNSCC (Fig. 5D-E).

Treatment prospects for HNSCC with AHSA1

We used TIMER2 to investigate the relationship between AHSA1 expression and important immune checkpoint regulators, such as TIGIT, PDCD1, CD244, CD27, IDO2, ICOS, CD28, and CD48. These immune checkpoint genes in HNSCC were found to have substantial negative relationships with AHSA1 levels. In addition, this inverse correlation was more pronounced in HPV-positive patients, particularly for CD27, CD48, and IDO2 expression (Fig. 6A-B). We also examined the chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity of the AHSA1 high- and low-expression groups using the “pRRophetic” software. The analysis revealed that several chemotherapy agents, including A-770,041, belinostat, BHG712, CAL-101, CGP-60,474, parthenolide, rapamycin, and navitoclax, exhibited greater sensitivity in patients with low AHSA1 expression (Fig. 7A-H). CellMiner analysis further revealed positive correlations between AHSA1 expression and sensitivity to seliciclib, vorinostat, nelarabine, and 5-fluorodeoxyuridine (Fig. 7I).

First, we performed quality control and data processing on the scRNA-seq data (GSE172577) and used the “harmony” package to remove batch effects between different samples (Fig. 8A). The distribution of six distinct HNSCC samples within the t-SNE map is illustrated in Fig. 8B. Through comprehensive analysis of cell surface markers, we identified and characterized 10 distinct cell populations within the HNSCC microenvironment, including epithelial cells, T cells, myeloid cells, adenocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, B cells, plasma B cells, vascular wall cells, and mast cells (Fig. 8C). Figure 8D showed the expression of recognized lineage markers in each of the ten main HNSCC cell clusters. Cellular correlation analysis revealed positive correlations in expression pattern across all cell types except epithelial cells (Fig. 8E). In addition, Fig. 8F revealed significant variations in the proportions of different cell types across patient samples, highlighting the presence of intertumoral heterogeneity. Lastly, gene localization analysis revealed that AHSA1 was mainly expressed in T cells, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, etc. (Fig. 8G-H). The broad expression of AHSA1 in a variety of immune cell types offers compelling evidence for its possible involvement in the TME of HNSCC.

To further explore the intercellular communication differences based on AHSA1 expression, we utilized the “CellChat” package to analyze interactions between AHSA1-positive and AHSA1-negative populations within T cells and epithelial cells, the primary cell types expressing AHSA1. Our analysis revealed that compared with AHSA1-T cells, AHSA1 + T cells exhibit significantly enhanced interaction ability with other cells (Fig. 9A). Compared to AHSA1-T cells, AHSA1 + T cells exhibit significantly enhanced incoming and outgoing signaling activities (Figs. 9B-C). Almost all cell types including both immune and non-immune showed a stronger tendency to send signals to AHSA1 + T cells. Moreover, AHSA1 + T cells also demonstrated an increased capacity to transmit signals to other cells (Fig. 9D). A similar pattern was observed in epithelial cells (Fig. 9E). AHSA1 + epithelial cells displayed stronger input and output signaling abilities than their AHSA1-counterparts (Figs. 9F-G). Likewise, nearly all cell types exhibited a higher capacity to communicate with AHSA1 + epithelial cells, which in turn also showed an enhanced ability to send signals to other cell populations (Fig. 9H). These findings highlight the potential role of AHSA1 in modulating the TME of HNSCC, and suggest that AHSA1 may represent a promising therapeutic target for HNSCC intervention.

AHSA1 knockdown suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion of HNSCC cells

To investigate AHSA1’s oncogenic potential in HNSCC, we first evaluated its expression patterns across cell lines. Three HNSCC cell lines (SCC9, CAL27, and HN4) showed significantly elevated AHSA1 levels compared to HOK (Fig. 10A). Based on this expression profile, we selected CAL27 and SCC9 for functional studies. Using lentiviral-mediated RNA interference, we established stable AHSA1 knockdown models, with transfection efficiency confirmed by qRT-PCR. Among the three shRNA constructs, sh-AHSA1-1 exhibited the highest silencing efficiency in both CAL27 and SCC9 cells (Figs. 10B-C), and was therefore chosen for subsequent experiments. According to the results of the CCK-8 experiment, AHSA1 knockdown markedly reduced the ability of both CAL27 and SCC9 cells to proliferate in comparison to the control groups (Fig. 11A). This anti-proliferative effect was further confirmed through colony formation assays, which showed a marked reduction in colony numbers following AHSA1 silencing (Fig. 11B). To investigate AHSA1’s role in tumor metastasis, we performed a series of migration and invasion assays. Wound healing experiments (Fig. 11C) revealed significantly slower wound closure rates in sh-AHSA1-transfected cells compared to controls. In accordance with these results, AHSA1 knockdown significantly decreased the migratory and invasive capacity of HNSCC cells, according to Transwell migration and invasion experiments (Fig. 11D-E). To validate these in vitro observations, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using SCC9 cells. As shown in Figs. 12A-D, mice implanted with sh-AHSA1-transfected cells exhibited significantly slower tumor growth rates, smaller tumor volumes, and reduced tumor weights compared to control groups (p < 0.001). These in vivo outcomes supported our in vitro research, highlighting the critical function of AHSA1 in advancing the development of HNSCC.

Discussion

As one of the most common cancers, HNSCC is frequently found in different parts of the head and neck and is distinguished by its aggressive biological behavior. The high mortality rate in HNSCC is largely attributed to frequent tumor recurrence, particularly in advanced-stage cases [29, 30]. While surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality, multimodal approaches combining surgery with radiotherapy and chemotherapy have become standard practice to improve clinical outcomes [31]. Immunotherapy, especially immune checkpoint inhibitors, has recently emerged and transformed the treatment of cancer by reviving endogenous T cell responses against tumor antigens, showing notable therapeutic advantages for a variety of cancers [32]. In a variety of tumor types, checkpoint inhibitors have shown outstanding effectiveness when used alone or in conjunction with other therapies [33]. Consequently, identifying reliable biomarkers capable of accurately predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy in HNSCC is crucial for enhancing treatment outcomes and enabling personalized therapeutic strategies.

AHSA1 is a critical co-chaperone involved in the folding cycle and conformational regulation of Hsp90. Their mutually beneficial relationship raises intracellular ATPase activity and is necessary for a number of physiological processes, such as hormone transmission, cell survival, apoptosis, and cell cycle regulation [34]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Hsp90 client proteins are indispensable for cancer cell survival and proliferation [35]. Recent studies have revealed intriguing connections between AHSA1 expression and key immunotherapeutic parameters, including immune checkpoint expression, tumor mutational burden, and immune cell infiltration patterns. Notably, AHSA1 expression shows significant negative correlations with myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and CD4 + T cell infiltration across multiple cancer types. Its potential as a prognostic biomarker has been demonstrated in HCC, where it influences cell migration and proliferation [36]. Mechanistic studies have further elucidated AHSA1’s oncogenic roles. Shao et al. [14] demonstrated that AHSA1 influences cell invasion, migration, apoptosis, and proliferation through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Similarly, Gao et al. [10] demonstrated that AHSA1 is a predictive biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma and affects the course of the disease via influencing the TGF-β/Akt-Cyclin D1/CDK6 axis. Our findings align with prior research indicating that AHSA1 is highly expressed in various cancers, including HNSCC. This observation was corroborated through analysis of the TCGA and GEO datasets. Additionally, qRT-PCR tests verified that AHSA1 expression was upregulated in HNSCC tissues and cell lines. Ultimately, we used a number of functional tests to verify that inhibiting AHSA1 successfully inhibits HNSCC cell invasion, migration, and proliferation. The consistent anti-tumor effects seen in various experimental systems provide compelling evidence for AHSA1’s potential as a therapeutic target for the treatment of HNSCC.

Our analysis revealed significant associations between AHSA1 expression and key clinicopathological characteristics of HNSCC, including tumor grade, clinical stage, HPV infection status, and TP53 mutation status. The p53 protein, which is encoded by the crucial tumor suppressor TP53 gene, is crucial for preserving genomic integrity. Its primary functions include DNA damage repair, cell cycle regulation, induction of apoptosis, and promotion of cellular senescence. Mutations or functional inactivation of TP53 lead to the loss of its tumor-suppressive capabilities, thereby facilitating tumorigenesis and cancer progression [37]. Additionally, mutations in p53 activate the AHSA1/Hsp90 ATPase axis, which stimulates Wnt signaling transduction and upregulates the expression of Wnt target genes, potentially contributing to tumor development [38]. Analysis of TCGA and GEO datasets demonstrated that elevated AHSA1 expression correlates with significantly reduced overall survival in HNSCC patients. In both univariate and multivariate Cox regression studies, AHSA1 was found to be an independent predictor of HNSCC outcome. Comprehensive GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of AHSA1-associated genes was conducted in order to clarify the molecular pathways behind AHSA1. The findings showed a notable enrichment in immunology-related pathways, such as the production of immunoglobulins, Th17 cell differentiation, IL-17 signaling, and immune mediators. These findings suggest AHSA1’s potential role in modulating tumor immune regulation and surveillance mechanisms in HNSCC.

Immune cell infiltration within the TME significantly influences immunotherapy response, treatment outcomes, and tumor behavior. Our immune infiltration analysis revealed significant negative correlations between AHSA1 expression and various immune cell populations, including CD4 + T cells, neutrophils, CD8 + T cells, macrophages, B cells, Tregs, and NK cells. By controlling lymphocyte activity and tumor cell proliferation, Tregs can aid in the pathophysiology of some malignancies and play a significant role in tissue healing and immune system balance within tumors [39, 40]. B lymphocytes exhibit a dual role in HNSCC. On one hand, they enhance anti-tumor immune responses through antibody production; on the other hand, they may facilitate tumor immune escape by secreting immunosuppressive factors, such as IL-10, within the tumor microenvironment [41]. The main cytotoxic effectors, CD8 + T cells, have strong anti-tumor activity by recognizing tumor antigens from histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules via the T-cell receptor (TCR). Upon activation, these cells release cytotoxic mediators, including perforin and granzymes, inducing tumor cell apoptosis [42, 43]. Neutrophils can prevent distant tumor spread by creating neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which capture and destroy tumor cells in the bloodstream. However, their anti-tumor activity is modulated by various factors within the TME, and their functional roles may vary depending on tumor type and microenvironmental conditions [44]. Moreover, our study demonstrated that high AHSA1 expression correlated with lower immune and ESTIMATE scores. These findings suggest that a TME with elevated AHSA1 expression possesses notable immunosuppressive features, which could potentially promote tumor progression and metastasis.

In recent years, immunotherapy has significantly advanced our approach to cancer treatment. Checkpoint inhibitors, in particular, have been the focus of clinical development, demonstrating remarkable success as either monotherapy or combination therapies across various tumor types [45]. Our study revealed significant correlations between AHSA1 expression and key immune checkpoint molecules, including TIGIT, CD27, CD28, and PDCD1. CD244, an immunomodulatory receptor found on various immune cells, contributes to the overall immunosuppressive milieu. It has been shown that CD244 inhibits the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, thereby modulating anti-cancer immune responses. Recent studies have identified CD244 as an innovative target for immunotherapy in HNSCC [32, 46]. This study showed that elevated AHSA1 expression was related to downregulation of PDCD1, which encodes PD-1, a critical immune checkpoint protein that suppresses T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses through PD-L1/PD-L2 interactions [47]. Interestingly, these associations were particularly pronounced in HPV-positive HNSCC patients, suggesting enhanced immunotherapy responsiveness in this subgroup with high AHSA1 expression. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that individuals with lower AHSA1 expression were more sensitive to the effects of belinostat, parthenolide, rapamycin, and navitoclax. We also identified several potential therapeutic agents (seliciclib, vorinostat, 5-fluorodeoxyuridine, and nelarabine) that may be effective against HNSCC tumors.

This study has several shortcomings that need to be noted. First, there was a relatively limited sample size in the original dataset used in this work, and future research is required to better evaluate the prognostic usefulness of AHSA1 in larger and more diverse clinical cohorts. Second, the absence of post-translational modification data in our analyses limits our understanding of their potential impact on AHSA1 function. Additionally, although AHSA1 overexpression correlates with TME alterations, direct mechanistic evidence linking AHSA1 to specific TME modifications remains lacking. The precise impact of AHSA1 on the TME remains unclear and warrants more study. To better comprehend the function of AHSA1 in immunotherapy response, comprehensive immunohistochemical analyses or protein sequencing should be conducted, supported by preclinical and functional studies to validate these associations. Moreover, before AHSA1 can be considered a viable therapeutic target, further research is necessary to enhance its clinical applicability. Lastly, although our in vivo and ex vivo results show that AHSA1 promotes tumor growth in HNSCC, more mechanistic research is necessary to completely clarify its molecular pathways and functional roles in HNSCC pathogenesis.

To address the current limitations and further elucidate the role of AHSA1 in HNSCC, future studies should focus on several key aspects. First, expanding clinical sample sizes across diverse populations will be essential to validate AHSA1’s prognostic and predictive value robustly. Second, investigating post-translational modifications of AHSA1, such as phosphorylation or acetylation, could uncover additional regulatory layers affecting its function. Third, comprehensive immunohistochemical and spatial transcriptomic analyses on patient specimens will help delineate the spatial and cellular context of AHSA1 expression and its association with immune infiltration. Lastly, preclinical evaluation of AHSA1-targeted therapies, alone or in combination with established immunotherapies, is critical to assess therapeutic efficacy and safety, thereby facilitating translation into clinical applications.

Conclusion

By integrating bioinformatics analysis with in vitro and in vivo experimental data, we systematically evaluated AHSA1 expression in HNSCC cell lines and tissues for the first time, exploring its potential role in tumor progression and clinical prognosis. Our findings reveal that AHSA1 is significantly overexpressed in HNSCC tissues and is closely associated with poor patient prognosis. Functionally, AHSA1 plays a multifaceted role in HNSCC, not only promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration but also modulating immune cell infiltration within the TME. Given its significant prognostic and functional implications, AHSA1 holds promise as a potential biomarker for clinical diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making, as well as a key target for future immunotherapy strategies.
