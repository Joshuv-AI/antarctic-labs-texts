Beyond deacetylation: crosstalk mechanisms and context-dependent regulation of sirtuin non-classical enzymatic functions in disease
Authors: Yiran Ma, Junli Chen, Ruixiao Song, Xiaolong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sirtuin (SIRT) family, long regarded as NAD⁺-dependent lysine deacetylases, is now recognized as a diverse superfamily of lysine deacylases with high substrate selectivity. Beyond classical deacetylation, sirtuin isoforms catalyze various non-classical enzymatic activities, including demyristoylation, desuccinylation, delactylation, and mono-ADP-ribosylation, and regulate cellular signaling via non-catalytic protein interactions. While the classification of sirtuins as a lysine deacylase superfamily is well-established, their integration into complex disease networks remains fragmented. This review synthesizes the context-dependent mechanisms of these non-classical functions across five major disease areas: metabolic syndrome, cancer, aging, neurodegeneration, and cardiovascular disease. We emphasize that the same non-classical sirtuin activity exerts context-dependent bidirectional effects, either protective or pathogenic, influenced by tissue type, cellular microenvironment, and substrate availability. We illustrate synergistic and antagonistic crosstalk among sirtuin members that supports precise metabolic regulation. We also summarize emerging therapeutic strategies targeting non-classical sirtuin activities, including small molecules, natural products, and biologics, and highlight key challenges: improving substrate selectivity, minimizing off-target effects, and promoting clinical translation. Finally, we propose three critical research directions: clarifying dynamic mechanisms of substrate selectivity, developing condition-specific targeting approaches, and advancing high-resolution detection of post-translational modifications. This review provides a paradigm shift in understanding sirtuin biology and lays the molecular foundation for precision therapies against metabolic and age-related diseases.

Introduction

The history of the sirtuin family traces back to 1979 with the discovery of the Silent Information Regulator 2 (SIR2) gene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Rine et al. 1979). Initially identified for its essential role in repressing transcription at yeast mating-type loci, SIR2 was later discovered to be a critical genetic determinant that extends replicative lifespan (Kaeberlein et al. 1999). This landmark connection between gene silencing and aging sparked intense biomedical interest, leading to the identification of its seven mammalian homologs, SIRT1–7 (Frye 1999; Frye 2000).

Structurally, all seven mammalian sirtuins share a highly conserved catalytic core domain (approximately 275 amino acids) comprised of a large Rossmann-fold domain tailored for NAD⁺ binding and a smaller, structurally diverse zinc-binding domain (Min et al. 2001). The classical enzymatic reaction takes place in the cleft between these two domains, where sirtuins couple the deacetylation of target lysine residues to the hydrolysis of NAD⁺. This process yields the deacetylated protein along with nicotinamide (NAM) and a unique metabolite, O-acetyl-ADP-ribose (O-AADPR) (Min et al. 2001; Tanner et al. 2000). Notably, O-AADPR acts as an intracellular second messenger by interacting with macrodomain-containing proteins and ion channels to regulate genomic silencing and stress responses (Kustatscher et al. 2005; Grubisha et al. 2006). Crucially, when sirtuins catalyze non-classical deacylation reactions, they generate corresponding diverse O-acyl-ADP-ribose derivatives (such as O-succinyl- or O-malonyl-ADPr) (Du et al. 2011). This vastly expands the repertoire of sirtuin-mediated secondary signaling molecules, while NAM serves as a potent endogenous inhibitor providing negative feedback regulation (Bitterman et al. 2002). Furthermore, this strict dependence on NAD⁺ cleavage inherently positions sirtuins as fundamental metabolic sensors, coupling cellular energy status to complex protein regulation (Imai et al. 2000).

Despite this shared foundational architecture, the sirtuin family exhibits profound structural diversity that dictates their multifaceted biological roles. Much of this functional divergence stems from their highly variable N- and C-terminal extensions, which strictly govern their distinct subcellular localizations (Zhao et al. 2003). Specifically, SIRT1, SIRT6, and SIRT7 are predominantly localized to the nucleus (with SIRT7 specifically enriched in the nucleolus); SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 reside primarily in the mitochondria; whereas SIRT2 is predominantly cytosolic, though it can shuttle to the nucleus under certain conditions (Michishita et al. 2005).

Beyond these terminal variations that define their spatial distribution, the sirtuin family exhibits highly specialized adaptations within their catalytic pockets that dictate their non-classical capabilities. While classical sirtuins possess relatively compact hydrophobic pockets tailored for acetyl groups, evolutionary divergence has reshaped these active sites across different members. For example, some members (such as SIRT6) have evolved elongated hydrophobic pockets to accommodate long-chain fatty acids (Pan et al. 2011; Feldman et al. 2013), while others (like SIRT5) have incorporated positively charged regions to capture acidic acyl modifications (Du et al. 2011). Additionally, unique structural adaptations, such as the semi-hydrophilic cage in SIRT3 (Fan et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025) or the secondary hydrophobic channel in SIRT4 (Betsinger and Cristea 2019), enable the precise recognition of bulky or highly specific metabolic intermediates. (The specific key residues and detailed molecular mechanisms driving these interactions are discussed comprehensively in their respective sections below.) Together, these structural paradigm shifts—ranging from terminal extensions to specialized pocket architectures—form the biochemical foundation for the diverse non-classical enzymatic activities explored in this review.

Operating from these distinct subcellular compartments, sirtuins profoundly regulate core life activities including DNA damage repair (Groselj et al. 2013), inflammation (Xu et al. 2025), oxidative stress (Xu et al. 2025), immune regulation (Ellmeier and Seiser 2018), and metabolism (Covington and Bajpeyi 2016). Historically, various members of the sirtuin family have been shown to drive this extensive regulatory network primarily through the classical deacetylation of histones (e.g., H3K9 (Vaquero et al. 2004), H4K16 (Vaquero et al. 2006), and non-histone proteins (e.g., p53 (Vaziri et al. 2001; Smith 2002), FOXO (Brunet et al. 2004; Giannakou and Partridge 2004), Ku70 (Cohen et al. 2004), PGC-1α (Gerhart-Hines et al. 2007)). This deacetylase-centered paradigm has established a conceptual foundation for analyzing diseases associated with metabolic dysregulation, such as metabolic syndrome (Bordone and Guarente 2005), cardiovascular metabolic dysfunction (Winnik et al. 2015), cancer-associated metabolic reprogramming (Shen et al. 2024), aging-related metabolic decline, and neurodegenerative metabolic disorders (Longo and Kennedy 2006; Kim et al. 2007; Lavu et al. 2008). However, this framework largely relies on relatively narrow functional classifications and linear mechanistic relationships. As understanding of enzymatic mechanisms has advanced, it has become clear that this single-catalytic-activity model cannot fully account for the complex roles sirtuins play in metabolic homeostasis and disease.

Driven by advances in identification technologies such as mass spectrometry, the field of functional proteomics has uncovered a diverse array of lysine acyl modifications beyond acetylation, including propionyl, butyryl, succinyl, and malonyl groups, among others (Phillips 1963; Brandi et al. 2022; Wang and Lin 2021; Komaniecki and Lin 2021; Zhao et al. 2018). In this context, a vast and diverse new research field focusing on the non-classical functions of sirtuins is gradually emerging. Compared to the strong deacetylase activity of SIRT1-3 (Wu et al. 2022), SIRT4-7 only exhibits weak or undetectable deacetylase activity in various in vitro assays (Mathias et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2017; Tennen et al. 2010). A landmark study by Du et al. in 2011 revealed that SIRT5 has a catalytic efficiency for acidic acyl modifications such as succinylation and malonylation that is hundreds of times higher than its efficiency for acetylation, while its deacetylation catalytic efficiency is approximately 500 times lower than that of SIRT1 (Du et al., 2011). Subsequently, Feldman and Jiang et al. independently reported that SIRT6 exhibits strong deacylation activity (demyristoylation and depalmitoylation) when activated by long-chain fatty acids (Feldman et al. 2013; Jiang et al. 2013), while SIRT2 shows efficient demyristoylation activity that directly regulates oncogenes such as K-Ras (Jing et al. n.d); Wang et al. 2022). More recent breakthroughs have further expanded the known enzymatic scope of sirtuins: Mathias et al. identified SIRT4 as a unique lipoamidase targeting the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (Mathias et al. 2014), and since 2022, multiple research groups have confirmed the delactylation activity in SIRT1, SIRT2, SIRT3, and SIRT6 (Fan et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2023; Du et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). Advances in proteomics have now identified approximately 20 distinct types of lysine acylation, which serve not only as metabolic byproducts but also as critical signals linking metabolism to gene expression (Shang et al. 2022; Sheng et al. 2025). To understand the biological significance of these modifications, it is essential to ask why such profound chemical diversity arises within cells. Recently, a crucial conceptual shift has emerged in the field, recognizing that this acylation landscape is largely dictated by intracellular metabolic flux and the accumulation of reactive metabolic intermediates (Keenan et al. 2021; Zhang and Schroeder 2025). Depending on nutrient availability, subcellular compartmentalization, and metabolic stress, intermediates such as various acyl-CoAs (e.g., succinyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA) can profoundly accumulate. Notably, due to their high intrinsic chemical reactivity, these metabolites frequently modify protein lysine residues through direct, non-enzymatic chemical acylation, bypassing canonical enzymatic “writers” to serve as critical signals linking metabolism to gene expression (Keenan et al. 2021; Zhang and Schroeder 2025).

As established by seminal biochemical and structural studies (He et al. 2012; Bheda et al. 2016) and comprehensively reviewed in recent years (Wu et al. 2022), the sirtuin family has evolved well beyond mere deacetylases. To manage the cell’s dynamic metabolic footprint, they function as a versatile “lysine deacylase and mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase superfamily”. Through fine-tuned structural variations in their active sites, each member has acquired precise substrate selectivity for specific acyl modifications. This structural adaptation enables them to act as dedicated “erasers”, sensing intracellular metabolic intermediates and translating their fluctuations into definitive regulatory signals. Consequently, the frontier of current research lies not in simply cataloging this enzymatic diversity, but in rigorously deciphering its complex physiological integration.

The exploration of non-classical sirtuin functions has also revealed a striking complexity: the same non-classical activity of a given sirtuin can produce opposite biological effects depending on the disease context or tissue type. For instance, SIRT5, a major mitochondrial desuccinylase, exhibits heterogeneous roles in cancer. It promotes the progression of hematological malignancies and certain solid tumors while suppressing hepatocellular, renal, pancreatic, and lung carcinomas. In addition, it displays both pro- and anti-tumor effects in breast, prostate, liver, and gastric cancers. This functional divergence arises from tissue-specific expression patterns and the specialization of its target substrates (Fabbrizi et al. 2023; Ke et al. 2025). Additionally, sirtuins often function synergistically through regulatory networks, shared signaling pathways, or cross-regulation of substrates. For example, in aging hearts subjected to ischemic stress, nuclear SIRT1 translocates to mitochondria and recruits SIRT3 to form protective complexes (Zhang et al. 2023). In the maintenance of genomic stability, SIRT6 interacts with PARP1 at lysine 521, enhancing its activity to facilitate double-strand break (DSB) repair (Mao et al. 2011); this process is further activated by JNK-mediated phosphorylation of SIRT6 at serine 10 (Meter et al. 2016). Similarly, in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), SIRT7 is recruited to DNA damage sites via PARP1, where it modulates H3K18 acetylation and influences 53BP1 recruitment, thereby affecting the efficiency of non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) (Vazquez et al. 2016).

In summary, binary classifications of sirtuin functions no longer meet the demands of scientific rigor, posing significant challenges to traditional single-target, single-effect drug development paradigms. Research into the non-classical functions of sirtuins stands at a critical juncture marked by both opportunity and controversy, necessitating systematic and high-precision investigation. Given the vast number of publications in the sirtuin field, an exhaustive summary is not feasible in one review. Building upon this foundational concept of the deacylase superfamily, this article focuses on a selectively curated set of representative studies to rigorously and insightfully synthesize how these non-classical modifications act in highly context-dependent manners. Specifically, we outline: (1) the biochemical characteristics and substrate profiles associated with each non-classical activity; (2) their context-dependent, bidirectional roles in disease biology and the dynamic crosstalk among sirtuin family members; and (3) current therapeutic strategies and associated challenges. Our goal is to provide a clear conceptual framework that enhances the understanding of this emerging aspect of the sirtuin enzymatic landscape and its potential for clinical translation, offering new insights and identifying promising targets for the development of therapies aimed at aging and metabolic disorders.

The map of non-classical functions of the Sirtuin family

Based on substrate origin, chemical properties, and functional roles, we classify the non-classical functions of the sirtuin family into four major types (Fig. 1): (1) Broad-spectrum deacylation—as “metabolic scavengers”, these sirtuins remove short-chain acyl groups derived from glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and the TCA cycle; (2) Long-chain fatty acid deacylation—as “signal regulators”, they modulate protein localization and activity by removing lipid anchors; (3) Inhibitory modification transfer and cofactor hydrolysis—as “molecular switches”, they directly inhibit target proteins; (4) Non-catalytic protein interactions—as “protein scaffolds”, they regulate signaling networks independent of enzymatic activity. Broad-spectrum removal of metabolic-derived acyl groups

Lysine acylation, a key class of post-translational modifications (PTMs), is extensively involved in cellular metabolism, gene transcription, signal transduction, and genomic stability. Its dynamic equilibrium is maintained by the coordinated actions of acyltransferases and deacylases (Hao et al. 2024; Brownell et al. 1996; Kleff et al. 1995; Taunton et al. 1996; Owen et al. 2000). Advances in proteomics have markedly expanded the substrate repertoire of sirtuin-mediated deacylation, extending beyond classical deacetylation to encompass short-chain, acidic, ketone body-derived, and long-chain fatty acyl modifications. This expansion underscores structural specificity and functional diversification, positioning sirtuins as central hubs linking metabolic states to protein function. This section systematically outlines the mechanisms and functional roles of sirtuin family members in removing various lysine acyl modifications, providing a theoretical foundation for understanding their physiological and pathological significance and identifying potential intervention targets.

Removal of neutral short-chain acyl groups

Neutral short-chain acyl modifications—including propionylation, butyrylation, crotonylation, and lactylation—are widely distributed across prokaryotes and eukaryotes. These modifications serve as crucial links between cellular metabolic states and epigenetic regulation, with their dynamic balance essential for maintaining normal cellular functions (Dang et al. 2022). The acyl donors for these modifications are typically metabolic intermediates derived from glucose, amino acids, or fatty acids (e.g., propionyl-CoA, butyryl-CoA), which exert biological effects through covalent attachment to lysine residues. Sirtuins function as the primary “erasers” of neutral short-chain acyl modifications, utilizing unique catalytic pocket structures to specifically recognize and remove distinct acyl groups.

De-propionylation

Lysine propionylation (Kpr) was first identified in 2007 via HPLC/MS/MS proteomic analysis (Chen et al. 2007). Propionyl-CoA, the direct donor for this modification, is generated from odd-chain fatty acid and amino acid catabolism (Chen et al. 2007). The covalent attachment of the propionyl group occurs via both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms. While classical histone acetyltransferases (HATs), such as p300 and CBP, act as propionyltransferases, the intrinsic chemical reactivity of propionyl-CoA also drives spontaneous acylation (Chen et al. 2007). Kpr modulates cellular and organelle functions by influencing DNA affinity, protein stability, and enzymatic activity (Shi et al. 2024). SIRT1, SIRT3, SIRT5, SIRT6, and SIRT7 have been confirmed to remove propionyl groups in an NAD⁺-dependent manner (Cheng et al. 2009; Nahomi et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2023; Kibria et al. 2025). Synergistic interactions among these sirtuins have also been observed: SIRT7-mediated deacylation at K368 enhances SIRT1’s ability to depropionylate the transcription factor SP7/Osterix (OSX), thereby increasing OSX transactivation activity and promoting osteogenesis (Fukuda et al. 2018). Recent studies indicate that sirtuin-mediated depropionylation regulates gene transcription (Shi et al. 2023) and oxidative stress (Ding et al. 2021); however, the underlying mechanisms require further elucidation.

De-butyrylation

Lysine butyrylation (Kbu) was also identified in 2007 (Chen et al. 2007). Its substrate, butyryl-CoA, is an intermediate of fatty acid β-oxidation and structurally resembles propionyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA (Yang et al. 2021). Similar to propionylation, lysine butyrylation is established through a dual mechanism: it can be enzymatically catalyzed by promiscuous HATs or generated through non-enzymatic spontaneous reactions driven by butyryl-CoA (Chen et al. 2007). Although sirtuin family members can remove butyryl groups, they exhibit varying selectivity: SIRT1 and SIRT3 display weak debutyrylase activity compared to their deacetylase activity, whereas SIRT2 and SIRT6 exhibit stronger activity; SIRT7 shows comparable activity for both acyl groups (Tanabe et al. 2018). Lysine residue positioning can influence SIRT7-mediated debutyrylation: the C-terminal basic region of SIRT7 binds nucleosomal DNA, enhancing debutyrylation at H3K18 relative to other sites (Tanabe et al. 2018). Reports on sirtuin-mediated debutyrylation remain limited, underscoring the need for further investigation into Kbu and its deacylases to clarify their roles in relevant proteins and diseases.

De-crotonylation

Lysine crotonylation (Kcr) was identified in 2011 via mass spectrometry in HeLa cells and mouse sperm cells (Tan et al. 2011). Unlike modifications dominated by chemical reactivity, lysine crotonylation is predominantly an enzymatic process. Well-characterized acetyltransferases, particularly p300/CBP, exhibit robust crotonyltransferase activity, actively transferring the crotonyl group to target lysines (Sabari et al. 2018). Feldman et al. (Feldman et al. 2013) demonstrated that SIRT1 and SIRT2 catalyze removal of crotonyl modifications from histone H3K9 peptides in vitro. Subsequently, Bao et al. (n.d.) showed that SIRT1, SIRT2, and SIRT3 recognize H3K4cr through π-π stacking interactions between phenylalanine (Phe180) in their catalytic pockets and the crotonyl group. SIRT3 also regulates histone Kcr dynamics and gene transcription in live cells. However, Wei et al. (Wei et al. 2017) reported that class I HDACs, rather than sirtuins, are the primary decrotonylases, with SIRT1 exhibiting only limited decrotonylase activity. SIRT6 also displays potential decrotonylase activity, as its knockdown significantly enhances H3K27 crotonylation (Liao et al. 2022).

Sirtuin-mediated decrotonylation primarily participates in cellular metabolism and gene expression regulation. In a mouse model of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), reduced crotonylation at K58, K151, K212, and K345 of isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) impaired TCA cycle function, with SIRT7 involved in this dynamic regulation (Liu et al. 2025). Under replicative stress, dynamic conversion between histone H2AK119cr and H2AK119ub maintains genomic stability by regulating transcription-replication conflicts (TRCs). Specifically, SIRT1-mediated removal of H2AK119cr is a prerequisite for BMI1-mediated addition of H2AK119ub. Accumulation of H2AK119ub at reversed replication forks suppresses transcription, reducing TRCs and associated R-loop formation and DNA double-strand breaks, thereby protecting cells from genomic damage (Hao et al. 2022).

De-lactylation

Lysine lactylation (Kla) was identified in histones in 2019 (Zhang et al. 2019). This modification is primarily enzymatic. Recent studies have identified p300 as a major lactyl-transferase, directly coupling cellular lactate accumulation to epigenetic regulation by enzymatically adding lactyl groups to target proteins (Zhang et al. 2019). Among sirtuins, SIRT3 exhibits the highest delactylase activity, particularly at H4K16 (Fan et al. 2023). Crystal structure analyses reveal that the SIRT3 catalytic pocket possesses both hydrophobic and hydrophilic characteristics: residues F180, I230, I291, and V292 form a preconfigured hydrophobic cage that accommodates the hydrocarbon portion of lactate, while a hydrogen bond network involving water molecules facilitates interactions with the hydrophilic portion of lactyl-lysine peptides (Fan et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025). In addition to SIRT1, SIRT2, SIRT3, and SIRT6 also exhibit histone lysine delactylase activity (Du et al. 2024; Jennings et al. 2021; Nickel et al. 2025). SIRT2 localizes to the cytoplasm and may mediate delactylation of non-histone proteins (Rho and Hay 2025).

In summary, sirtuin family members participate in removing Kpr, Kbu, Kcr, and Kla in an NAD⁺-dependent manner, exhibiting notable functional differentiation. Depropionylation relies on synergistic sirtuin interactions, as exemplified by SIRT7 enhancing SIRT1-mediated OSX regulation to achieve precise modulation of osteogenesis. Debutyrylase activities vary significantly among sirtuin members, with SIRT2 and SIRT6 showing the highest activity, and SIRT7 activity influenced by nucleosomal DNA binding. Decrotonylation exhibits functional differences between sirtuins (SIRT1/2/3/6) and class I HDACs, with the latter serving as the primary decrotonylases. Functionally, these modifications may serve as key hubs linking cellular metabolism to functional homeostasis by regulating metabolic stability, genomic integrity, and gene transcription. However, systematic correlational evidence remains limited; further research is needed to identify specific substrates and elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Removal of acidic short-chain acyl groups

Beyond neutral acetylation, the lysine acylation landscape includes negatively charged modifications, primarily succinylation, malonylation, and glutarylation (Hao et al. 2024). These PTMs resemble lysine acetylation but differ in hydrophobicity, charge, or hydrocarbon chain length (Sabari et al. 2017). Succinylation and malonylation are widely distributed: succinylation is enriched on mitochondrial proteins, whereas malonylation predominates on cytoplasmic proteins (Liu et al. 2020). Under physiological pH, these modifications reverse lysine charge from positive (+ 1) to negative (− 1), a change substantially greater than that induced by other acylations (Sabari et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2023). The key breakthrough beyond classical deacetylation was the discovery that SIRT5 specifically catalyzes removal of these acidic acyl groups. SIRT7 has also been reported to exhibit desuccinylase activity (Li et al. 2016).

De-succinylation

Lysine succinylation (Ksuc) was initially discovered in 2011 in E. coli isocitrate dehydrogenase via mass spectrometry. This modification covalently attaches succinyl groups from the TCA cycle intermediate succinyl-CoA to lysine residues, significantly altering protein charge state and functional activity (Zhang et al. 2011). While specific succinyltransferases may exist, lysine succinylation is widely considered to occur predominantly via non-enzymatic mechanisms. The high chemical reactivity of the succinyl-CoA thioester bond, combined with its localized accumulation and the slightly alkaline pH within the mitochondrial matrix, creates a highly favorable microenvironment for spontaneous chemical succinylation (Wagner and Hirschey 2014). To prevent excessive accumulation of this modification, its reversibility is tightly controlled by desuccinylases, primarily the well-documented SIRT5 (Wagner and Hirschey 2014). The critical regulatory role of SIRT5 is underscored by proteomic analyses in mouse cells, which mapped 2,565 succinylation sites across 779 mostly mitochondrial proteins, the vast majority of which were profoundly hyper-succinylated following SIRT5 depletion (Park et al. 2013; Rardin et al. 2013).

SIRT5’s efficient desuccinylase activity relies on unique structural features and substrate recognition mechanisms. Du et al. discovered that the negatively charged sulfonic group of the buffering molecule CHES occupies the active pocket of SIRT5, forming electrostatic interactions with Tyr102 and Arg105, suggesting that SIRT5 preferentially recognizes negatively charged acyl groups (Du et al. 2011). Subsequent enzymatic assays confirmed that SIRT5 hydrolyzes succinyl and malonyl peptides 29- to 1,000-fold more efficiently than acetyl peptides (Du et al. 2011). Tyr102 and Arg105 form a positively charged gate that specifically captures negatively charged carboxylic side chains of succinyl or malonyl groups via salt bridges and hydrogen bonds; substitution of these residues markedly increases Km values and reduces catalytic efficiency (Du et al. 2011). Additionally, Ala86 in the SIRT5 substrate-binding pocket provides larger spatial accommodation for bulky acylated lysine substrates, conferring broader substrate selectivity (Yang et al. 2017).

Du et al. confirmed that TCA cycle intermediates (succinyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA) serve as direct donors in SIRT5-catalyzed reactions. SIRT5 downregulation leads to excessive succinylation of succinyl-CoA synthetase subunit β (SUCLA2) at K118, inhibiting its activity and triggering a vicious cycle of succinyl-CoA accumulation and SUCLA2 hyper-succinylation, resulting in TCA cycle disruption and mitochondrial damage; SIRT5 overexpression reverses this process (Liu et al. 2025). Recent studies have further elucidated SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation in regulating the TCA cycle, electron transport chain, and fatty acid metabolism. Mass spectrometry identified 933 succinylation sites on 366 proteins in fibroblasts, involved in multiple metabolic pathways including TCA cycle, fatty acid oxidation, and cell adhesion; succinylation at these sites increases upon SIRT5 loss (Gut et al. 2020). Specifically, SIRT5 desuccinylates ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein 2 (UQCRC2) at K250, facilitating its translocation from cytoplasm to mitochondria, enhancing mitochondrial respiratory complex III activity, and maintaining electron transport and TCA cycle flux (SIRT5 -mediated desuccinylation of UQCRC2 attenuates osteogenic differentiation of aged BM-MSCs through impairing mitochondrial homeostasis, 2025). SIRT5 also enhances ketogenic rate-limiting enzyme HMGCS2 activity by desuccinylating key sites K211 and K358, regulating fatty acid oxidation and ketone body synthesis (Xu et al. 2023). Similarly, SIRT5 desuccinylates the fatty acid β-oxidation enzyme HADHA at K644 to maintain its activity; SIRT5 inhibition induces widespread succinylation of fatty acid oxidation enzymes, causing metabolic impairment (Wang et al. 2025). Notably, both SIRT3 and SIRT5 target very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (VLCAD): SIRT3 stabilizes the FAD cofactor via deacetylation at K507, while SIRT5 stabilizes it via desuccinylation at K482, K492, and K507, maintaining VLCAD binding to cardiolipin and facilitating its recruitment back to the mitochondrial membrane (Zhang et al. 2015). This crosstalk may significantly regulate fatty acid β-oxidation. Additionally, SIRT5 is closely associated with urea cycle (Teng et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024), glycolysis (Zhang et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024), and oxidative stress regulation (Lin et al. 2013), playing important roles in intracellular energy metabolism, oxidative stress, signal transduction, and apoptosis.

Early studies identified SIRT7 as a nuclear-localized histone desuccinylase recruited to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) in a PARP1-dependent manner, catalyzing H3K122 desuccinylation to promote chromatin condensation and facilitate DNA repair (Li et al. 2016). Yuan et al. found that SIRT7 desuccinylates protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) at K387, enhancing its methyltransferase activity and influencing SREBP1a methylation, thereby promoting lipid metabolic reprogramming and tumor growth and metastasis (Yuan et al. 2022). However, existing research primarily focuses on SIRT7 deacetylase activity, with reports on its desuccinylase function mainly limited to roles in gene expression and lipid metabolism (Hu et al. 2025). Characterizing its structural properties, substrate interactions, and enzymatic mechanisms, as well as exploring its additional functions in gene expression regulation and emerging roles in metabolism and signal transduction, will be important directions for future studies.

De-malonylation

Lysine malonylation (Kmal) was identified in 2011 using SILAC and high-throughput mass spectrometry (Peng et al. 2011). Much like succinylation, lysine malonylation is primarily driven by non-enzymatic mechanisms, where malonyl-CoA acts as a reactive electrophile that spontaneously modifies lysine residues without the need for acyltransferase catalysis (Wagner and Hirschey 2014). Peng et al. measured demalonylase activity across all HDACs and found that SIRT5 not only catalyzes desuccinylation but also possesses demalonylase activity, whereas its deacetylase activity was negligible (Peng et al. 2011). Activity studies indicate that among the SIRT family, only SIRT5 exhibits specific demalonylase capability (Du et al. 2011). As discussed above, SIRT5 recognizes negatively charged acyl groups via Tyr102 and Arg105-mediated salt bridges and hydrogen bonds. At the subcellular level, SIRT5-targeted desuccinylation sites are primarily mitochondrial, whereas demalonylation sites are mainly cytoplasmic, with only a minor fraction localized to mitochondria (Rardin et al. 2013; Nishida et al. 2015). Functionally, while SIRT5-mediated demalonylation participates in fatty acid metabolism (Liu et al. 2025), oxidative stress (Wei et al. 2024), and carbohydrate metabolism (Wang et al. 2022), cytoplasmic demalonylation primarily regulates glycolysis and the urea cycle, whereas mitochondrial desuccinylation predominantly enhances oxidative phosphorylation (Nishida et al. 2015; Du et al. 2018).

Proteomic analysis indicates that SIRT5 preferentially demalonylates the glycolytic enzyme glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), promoting glycolysis. Substituting GAPDH Lys184 with glutamic acid to mimic malonylation inhibits GAPDH activity, indicating that normal enzyme function requires SIRT5-mediated demalonylation (Nishida et al. 2015). Moreover, SIRT5 inhibits negative feedback regulation of glycolysis, a process modulated by malonyl-CoA, the malonyl donor for lysine malonylation (Nishida et al. 2015; Bowman et al. 2017). Pathway enrichment and gene function prediction analyses reveal that SIRT5 demalonylation is involved not only in metabolic pathways but also in eukaryotic translation elongation, amino acid and derivative metabolism, and selenoamino acid metabolism (Nahálková 2023). He et al. discovered that SIRT5 interacts with DEAD-box helicase 3 (DDX3), demalonylating it at K66, K130, and K162, thereby enhancing TBK1-IRF3 signaling pathway activation (He et al. 2021).

De-glutarylation

Lysine glutarylation (Kglu) originates from the degradative metabolites of lysine and tryptophan, specifically glutaryl-CoA (Tan et al. 2014). This modification is similarly generated through predominantly non-enzymatic pathways, relying on the spontaneous chemical reactivity of glutaryl-CoA (Wagner and Hirschey 2014). Berndt et al. used antibodies specifically recognizing glutarylated lysine for Western blot analysis and detected protein glutarylation across various prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms; in vitro assays confirmed that SIRT5 is the only sirtuin family member with deglutarylase activity (Berndt n.d.). Tan et al. independently confirmed SIRT5 deglutarylase activity and identified 683 Kglu sites across 191 proteins, highly enriched in metabolic enzymes and mitochondrial proteins. SIRT5-deficient mice exhibited elevated levels of glutarylated mitochondrial proteins, indicating that SIRT5-mediated deglutarylation may regulate mitochondrial function (Tan et al. 2014).

SIRT5-mediated deglutarylation regulates amino acid metabolism and partially restores glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase (GCDH) activity by approximately 30–50%. SIRT5 deficiency significantly alters lysine oxidation capacity and levels of various amino acids and their metabolic intermediates in HEK293T cells and mouse livers (Bhatt et al. 2022). Additionally, deglutarylation acts on glutamate dehydrogenase-1 (GLUD1), contributing to TCA cycle regulation (Wang et al. 2018). Notably, SIRT5 performs both desuccinylation on IDH2 and deglutarylation on G6PD, collectively activating NADPH-producing metabolic enzymes, maintaining NADPH homeostasis, sustaining the GSH-mediated antioxidant network, and reducing ROS levels (Zhou et al. 2016).

In summary, SIRT5, owing to its unique structural characteristics, serves as a key regulatory enzyme for Ksuc, Kmal, and Kglu. The SIRT5 catalytic pocket captures negatively charged acyl side chains via salt bridges and hydrogen bonds formed by Tyr102 and Arg105, while Ala86 in the substrate-binding pocket broadens substrate specificity by providing spatial adaptability. Functionally, SIRT5 primarily regulates TCA cycle–electron transport chain coupling through mitochondrial desuccinylation, modulates amino acid metabolism via deglutarylation, and governs glycolysis and signaling pathway regulation through cytoplasmic demalonylation. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests a potential role for SIRT7 in Ksuc regulation, but its structural basis, molecular mechanisms, and physiological functions require further investigation.

SIRT4: an emerging regulator of acidic acylation

Although SIRT5 has traditionally been viewed as the predominant regulator of acidic acylation, recent evidence indicates that SIRT4 also plays a pivotal role in this domain. Landmark biochemical studies have revealed that SIRT4 possesses potent deacylase activity specifically directed toward complex, negatively charged short-chain acylations, such as hydroxymethylglutarylation (Khmg), methylmalonylation (Kmmal), and glutarylation (Kglu) (Anderson et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2024). Notably, SIRT4 exhibits high catalytic efficiency for these dicarboxylic acyl groups, particularly in the context of branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism. For instance, SIRT4-mediated de-Kmmal and de-Khmg activities are essential for regulating leucine-stimulated insulin secretion, suggesting that SIRT4 serves as a specialized metabolic sensor in mitochondria (Anderson et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2024). These findings position SIRT4 alongside SIRT5 as a key guardian of mitochondrial acyl-CoA homeostasis, potentially through complementary or tissue-specific mechanisms.

Removal of ketone body- and branched-chain amino acid-derived acyl groups

These modifications represent a novel class of metabolic intermediate-derived PTMs, primarily including lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) and lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb). The acyl donor for Kbhb is β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), a key product of ketone body metabolism (Zhou et al. 2022), while Khib derives from 2-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA (Dai et al. 2014). Both modifications regulate gene transcription and metabolic homeostasis via hydrogen bond formation mediated by their hydroxyl groups (Sabari et al. 2017). Mechanistically, the addition of these ketone body-derived modifications is largely enzymatic. Notably, p300 serves as a key writer capable of catalyzing the enzymatic addition of β-hydroxybutyrate to lysine residues (Kbhb), linking nutritional states directly to epigenetic remodeling (Huang et al. 2018). SIRT3 is currently the primary enzyme responsible for removing these modifications and is closely linked to glycolysis and gene transcription regulation.

Khib and Kbhb were identified in histones in 2014 and 2016, respectively (Dai et al. 2014; Metabolic regulation of gene expression by histone lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation, 2016). In vitro biochemical assays utilizing histone peptides as substrates have demonstrated that SIRT3 selectively removes Kbhb at H3K4, K9, K18, K23, K27, and H4K16, but not at H4K5, K8, or K12, whereas HDAC3 exhibits no such selectivity (Zhang et al. 2019). However, because SIRT3 is canonically recognized as a predominantly mitochondrial enzyme, the physiological relevance of its potential nuclear activity on histones in vivo remains highly debated. Nevertheless, these in vitro peptide studies provide critical structural insights into SIRT3’s enzymatic pocket. Given that R- and S-type Kbhb have distinct metabolic origins, this chiral preference positions SIRT3 as a unique link between metabolic environments and epigenetic regulation. Further studies indicate that the β-backbone formed by Kbhb and adjacent amino acid main chains plays a predominant role in SIRT3 substrate recognition. In glycine-flanking motifs, the simplicity of the glycine side chain increases spatial conformational flexibility, partially hindering SIRT3 binding (Zhang et al. 2019; Abmayr and Workman 2019).

MS and Western blot analyses revealed significantly elevated Khib levels on histones H3 and H4 in embryonic kidneys of SIRT3-deficient mice, demonstrating that SIRT3 possesses de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylase activity during embryogenesis (Perico et al. 2021). In adult kidneys, however, SIRT3 lacks this activity and functions primarily as a mitochondrial deacetylase (Perico et al. 2021). Enzymatic assays indicate that SIRT3-mediated de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation is closely linked to glycolysis: SIRT3 loss in embryonic kidneys elevates Khib levels on phosphofructokinase (PFK), promoting glycolysis. Additionally, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity increases in SIRT3-deficient embryonic kidneys, enhancing glycolytic flux and correlating with reduced nephron progenitor cells and impaired nephrogenesis (Perico et al. 2021).

In summary, SIRT3 regulates Kbhb and Khib through distinct mechanisms. For Kbhb, its hydrogen-bonded hydrophobic pocket specifically recognizes S-configured substrates and discriminates histone sites via β-backbone interactions, exhibiting strict chiral and site selectivity. For Khib, SIRT3 functions only in embryonic kidneys, and its loss enhances glycolysis, impairing nephrogenesis. For Kla (discussed earlier), SIRT3 utilizes a semi-hydrophobic, semi-hydrophilic catalytic pocket and hydrophobic cage, conferring higher activity than other sirtuins. Currently, direct evidence linking these modifications to specific metabolic pathways and their functional roles remains limited, and the underlying molecular mechanisms are incompletely understood.

Long-chain fatty acid deacylation and signal regulation

Long-chain fatty acid acylation anchors proteins to cell membranes via hydrophobic interactions, participating in signal transduction, membrane transport, and protein interactions (Jiang et al. 2018). Current understanding of sirtuin-mediated catalysis of these PTMs derives primarily from studies of N-terminal glycine myristoylation. Lysine myristoylation is strictly an enzymatic process. Beyond N-terminal modifications, internal lysine myristoylation is orchestrated by N-myristoyltransferases (NMT1 and NMT2), which utilize myristoyl-CoA as the activated lipid donor (Kosciuk et al. 2020). Sirtuins also remove palmitoyl, octanoyl, decanoyl, and dodecanoyl groups (Feldman et al. 2013; Kibria et al. 2025). Unlike SIRT5-mediated short-chain acyl modifications, long-chain deacylation is mainly regulated by SIRT6 and SIRT2 (Liu et al. 2015; Xie et al. 2019).

De-myristoylation

Beyond its deacetylase activity, SIRT6 catalyzes long-chain fatty acid deacylation, serving as a deacylase for TNF-α and various secreted proteins (Jiang et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2016). SIRT6 specificity for long-chain acyl groups stems from its unique structural features: crystal structure analysis reveals an elongated active site between its Rossmann-fold and Zn2⁺-binding domains, forming the longest hydrophobic pocket among the sirtuin family (Pan et al. 2011). This structural characteristic favors long-chain acyl binding, while its deacetylase activity is ~ 1,000-fold weaker than classical sirtuin deacetylases and requires free fatty acids (FFAs) (Feldman et al. 2013).

SIRT2 also exhibits long-chain deacylase activity. Kinetic studies demonstrate that SIRT2 removes myristoyl groups from diverse peptide sequences with catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) approximately five-fold higher than its deacetylase activity (Xie et al. 2019). Crystal structure analysis reveals a large hydrophobic pocket in SIRT2 that accommodates myristoyl groups (Xie et al. 2019). Comparative overlay analysis of SIRT2 and SIRT6 shows differences in the Zn2⁺-binding subdomain and hydrophobic residues forming the acyl pocket: e.g., SIRT6 Met134 corresponds to SIRT2 Phe190, while SIRT6 Asn2 and Trp69 occupy the position of SIRT2 Phe119 (Teng et al. 2015). Beyond these variations, both enzymes exhibit generally similar structures for accommodating long-chain acyl groups.

Although the full scope of this catalytic activity remains poorly understood, existing studies reveal significant functions of SIRT6 and SIRT2 in protein secretion, membrane-associated processes, and cell signaling, particularly in regulating Ras family proteins. Regarding protein secretion, Feng et al. confirmed VEGFA as a SIRT6 substrate using CLICK-IT assays. SIRT6 promotes VEGFA secretion via demyristoylation, inducing endothelial cell migration and angiogenesis (Feng et al. 2025). Subsequently, the team developed an enrichment technique for lysine-myristoylated peptides and identified a SIRT6 mutant (H133Y) with high myristoyl affinity but impaired demyristoylase activity. Further studies revealed that SIRT6 demyristoylates ATF2 at K296, activating this transcription factor and regulating its nucleocytoplasmic translocation. This decreases PRKCD expression, forming the SIRT6/Myr-ATF2/PRKCD/VE-cadherin axis that enhances endothelial barrier integrity under elevated myristic acid conditions (Feng et al. 2025).

Zhang et al. generated a SIRT6[G60A] mutant with undetectable deacetylase activity both in vitro and in vivo, while retaining intact demyristoylase activity (Zhang et al. 2016). Using this tool, they demonstrated functional divergence of SIRT6 enzymatic activities: demyristoylation promotes secretion of proteins such as TNF-α, whereas deacetylation inhibits gene transcription. Wang et al. (Wang et al. 2022) found that Shigella infection-induced Golgi stress upregulates SIRT2 via transcription factor CREB3. Upregulated SIRT2 suppresses secretion of the Shigella virulence factor IcsB by removing acyl modifications, thereby enhancing host innate immunity.

In membrane-associated processes and signal transduction, Zhang et al. used the SIRT6[G60A] mutant to confirm that deacylation is critical for SIRT6 tumor-suppressive function (Zhang et al. 2017). Mechanistically, SIRT6 targets Ras family member R-Ras2, removing its lysine fatty acyl group. Deacylated R-Ras2 exhibits reduced binding affinity for plasma membrane and PI3K catalytic subunit p110α, inhibiting Akt-Thr308 phosphorylation and attenuating PI3K-Akt signaling and cell proliferation. SIRT2-mediated deacylation also regulates other Ras family proteins, demonstrating potential anticancer activity. Jing et al. first identified K-Ras4a as a SIRT2 deacylation substrate; deacylation alters K-Ras4a membrane localization and enhances its interaction with A-Raf, promoting cellular transformation (Jing et al. n.d.). Wang et al. further showed that SIRT2 mediates deacylation of multiple IcsB substrate proteins, including Ras family members RalA, RalB, Rheb, and R-Ras (Wang et al. 2022). Additionally, Kosciuk et al. demonstrated via overexpression, 32P-NAD⁺ assays, and co-IP experiments that SIRT2 removes myristoylation at ARF6 K3, regulating its GTPase activity and modulating signal transduction (Kosciuk et al. 2020).

Similarly, SIRT3 (Gai et al. 2016), SIRT4 (Kumar and Lombard 2017), and SIRT7 (Kibria et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2017) possess deacylase capabilities and participate in regulating leucine metabolism, insulin secretion (Anderson et al. 2017), and lipid metabolism (Talwadekar et al. 2023).

Inhibitory modification transfer and cofactor hydrolysis

Beyond their well-characterized deacylase activity, sirtuins also function as “writers” of inhibitory modifications via mono-ADP-ribosylation, enabling targeted inactivation of proteins involved in cellular metabolism, signal transduction, and gene expression regulation.

Mono-ADP-ribosylation transfer

Mono-ADP-ribosylation (MARylation) activity was first identified in the 1960 s (Chambon et al. 1966). Unlike classical NAD⁺-dependent deacetylation, sirtuin-mediated MARylation directly transfers ADP-ribose from NAD⁺ to target proteins via nucleophilic attack, without generating O-acetyl-ADP-ribose. MARylation is an exclusively enzymatic modification. It is catalyzed by specific writers—primarily members of the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) family, also known as ARTDs—which facilitate the transfer of ADP-ribose from NAD⁺ onto target amino acid residues (Lüscher et al. 2022). Nicotinamide strongly inhibits this reaction (Lüscher et al. 2022; Suskiewicz et al. 2023; Kolb and Burkart 1999). For most sirtuins, MARylation activity is substantially lower than deacetylase activity, and some subtypes exhibit functional bias: SIRT4 and SIRT6 primarily display MARylation activity with negligible deacetylation (Wu et al. 2022; Tao et al. 2023).

Although SIRT6 exhibits robust deacetylase activity, its first identified enzymatic function was NAD⁺-dependent MARylation (Liszt et al. 2005). This activity plays a significant role in DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair. SIRT6 catalyzes ADP-ribosylation of PARP1 at K521 (Mao et al. 2011) and mono-ADP-ribosylates KAP1 at the 5’ UTR of long interspersed nuclear elements 1 (L1), inhibiting L1 activity and participating in DNA damage repair (Meter et al. 2014). Lysine demethylase 2 A (KDM2A) is also a SIRT6 substrate: MARylation increases H3K36me2 levels at DNA damage sites, promoting H3K9 trimethylation and enhancing DSB repair (Rezazadeh et al. 2020). Additionally, SIRT6 mono-ADP-ribosylates BAF170 at K312 during oxidative stress, recruiting BAF170 to the heme oxygenase 1 enhancer and promoting RNA polymerase II recruitment, thereby activating HO-1 transcription and protecting cells from oxidative stress (Rezazadeh et al. 2019).

SIRT4 MARylation primarily regulates amino acid metabolism. Early studies showed that SIRT4 inhibits glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) via ADP-ribosylation, suppressing glutamate metabolism and insulin secretion in pancreatic β-cells (Haigis et al. 2006; Argmann and Auwerx 2006). SIRT4 deficiency induces insulin secretion in insulinoma cells, mouse pancreatic β-cells, and pancreatic cells from calorie-restricted mice, associated with GDH activation-mediated amino acid upregulation (Haigis et al. 2006; Chen et al. 2010). Recent studies indicate that SIRT4 regulates GDH activity in hematopoietic stem cells, inhibiting glutamine metabolism and ATP levels, thereby suppressing hepatic stellate cell activation and protecting against liver fibrosis (Yin et al. 2022). This enzymatic activity also regulates oxidative metabolism: Wadsworth et al. found that 2-hydroxybutyrate (2HB) alleviates muscle fatigue, improves oxidative capacity, and enhances exercise performance via SIRT4-dependent metabolic feedback. 2HB inhibits BCAT2, increasing the NAD⁺/NADH ratio, which SIRT4 senses and responds to by redistributing protein ADP-ribosylation (decreased nuclear, increased cytoplasmic) and reducing C/EBPβ ADP-ribosylation. This promotes C/EBPβ binding to BCAA degradation gene promoters, enhancing oxidative metabolism (Wadsworth et al. n.d.). Despite these initial findings, the physiological relevance of SIRT4-mediated MARylation has been subject to significant scrutiny. Du et al. (2009) reported that full-length human SIRT4 lacked detectable mADP-ribosyltransferase activity toward GDH in NAD+ assays (Du et al. 2009). In light of the recent discovery of its potent deacylase functions (see Sect. "SIRT4: An emerging regulator of acidic acylation"), it is increasingly proposed that the metabolic regulatory effects previously attributed to MARylation might, in fact, be driven by the removal of acidic acyl groups. This shift in understanding underscores the complexity of SIRT4’s catalytic profile and highlights the need for further validation of its MARylation substrates in vivo.

SIRT7 exhibits robust spontaneous MARylation activity independent of acetylated substrates, surpassing its deacetylase activity (Mitra and Dey 2020). Although SIRT7 cannot modify histones H3 and H4, it specifically modifies transcription factors p53, ELK4, and PARP1, suggesting roles in tumorigenesis and DNA damage response. SIRT7 enhances PARP1 activity via ADP-ribosylation, promoting DNA repair (Mitra and Dey 2020).

In summary, sirtuin-mediated MARylation fundamentally differs from classical deacetylation. Mechanistically, deacetylation hydrolyzes NAD⁺ to remove acyl groups and generate O-acetyl-ADP-ribose, whereas MARylation directly transfers ADP-ribose to target proteins without generating this product and is strongly inhibited by nicotinamide. Functionally, deacetylation fine-tunes protein activity, while MARylation acts as a “functional switch”. SIRT4 and SIRT6 exhibit pronounced functional bias, playing irreplaceable roles in DNA repair, metabolic homeostasis, and stress responses.

Fatty acylamide hydrolysis

Sirtuin acylamide hydrolytic activity exhibits member specificity and substrate selectivity. SIRT4, a representative example, directly hydrolyzes the acylamide cofactor in the DLAT E2 component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, inhibiting its activity and regulating energy metabolism (Mathias et al. 2014). Crystal structures reveal that SIRT4 possesses an additional hydrophobic channel outside the classical catalytic pocket that accommodates long-chain lipoic acid groups, providing spatial basis for its high selectivity (Betsinger and Cristea 2019). The lipoamide cofactor targeted by SIRT4 is added via a highly regulated enzymatic relay. This process is mediated by lipoyltransferases, such as LIPT1 and LIPT2, which covalently attach the lipoic acid group to the E2 subunits of metabolic complexes (Solmonson and DeBerardinis 2018). However, the physiological role of this function requires further elucidation. In contrast, SIRT3, although targeting PDHc to regulate mitochondrial metabolism, lacks acylamide hydrolytic activity. Instead, SIRT3 targets the PDHc E1α subunit (PDHA1) via classical deacetylase activity, removing inhibitory lysine acetylation, thereby activating PDHc and promoting glucose oxidation (Jing et al. 2013).

Regulation of non-catalytic protein interactions

Sirtuin family functions extend beyond catalytic activity. For instance, the SIRT2 splice variant (isoform 5) lacks deacetylase activity entirely yet retains protein interaction capability through binding to p300 (Rack et al. 2014). This enzymatic activity-independent regulatory mode alters protein conformation via direct binding or serves as a protein scaffold, participating in cytoskeletal remodeling and membrane transport, thereby offering new perspectives on cellular signaling.

Among these non-catalytic regulatory networks, SIRT1-related mechanisms are relatively well characterized. In cerebellar granule neurons (CGNs), SIRT1 protects against low potassium-induced apoptosis, and catalytically inactive SIRT1 mutants (H363Y and H355A) retain this neuroprotective function, indicating independence from deacetylase activity. This protection may involve mediating protein complex formation or scaffold protein functions (Pfister et al. 2008). In signaling regulation, SIRT1 directly interacts with TGF-β pathway protein Smad2 via its non-catalytic N-terminal domain. The catalytically inactive H363Y mutant exhibits identical interaction patterns to wild-type SIRT1, confirming that this interaction is not mediated by deacetylation (García-Vizcaíno et al. 2017). Another critical protein–protein interaction network involves SIRT1 and Deleted in Breast Cancer 1 (DBC1). DBC1 functions as a prominent negative regulator of SIRT1 by directly binding to it and inhibiting its catalytic activity (Kim et al. 2008). Notably, the structural dynamics of this complex demonstrate how non-catalytic binding interfaces can be pharmacologically targeted. For example, carboxamide-based SIRT1 inhibitors have been shown to effectively block the SIRT1-DBC1 interaction through an acetylation-independent mechanism (Hubbard et al. 2013). This co-regulation emphasizes that manipulating SIRT1’s protein binding capabilities, independent of its substrate acetylation status, represents a viable regulatory strategy. Additionally, SIRT1 directly binds the small GTPase RhoA, stabilizing its GDP-bound inactive state. This inhibits RhoA/ROCK pathway activity (Ahn et al. 2015) and reduces F-actin polymerization (Feng et al. 2013), thereby decreasing cytoskeletal rigidity and delaying cellular aging. SIRT1 also forms a complex with FOXO3a and NRF1 on the SIRT6 promoter, positively regulating SIRT6 expression through a potentially catalytic activity-independent mechanism (Hs et al. 2010).

SIRT7 exhibits similar non-catalytic regulation by directly interacting with hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF)−1α and HIF-2α, reducing their protein levels and inhibiting transcriptional activity. Catalytically inactive SIRT7 mutants or those lacking the deacetylase domain retain this effect, which remains unaffected by nicotinamide (Hubbi et al. 2013).

Non-classical sirtuin functions in disease

Metabolic syndrome: tissue-specific regulation by SIRT5 non-classical activities

Metabolic syndrome (MS) is characterized by concurrent obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, increasing cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes (T2D) risk (Rochlani et al. 2017). Central obesity and insulin resistance are key drivers, closely linked to systemic energy dysregulation and mitochondrial dysfunction (Roden and Shulman 2019; Kahn et al. 2006). As a mitochondrial deacetylase with expanding non-classical deacylase activities (e.g., desuccinylation, demalonylation), SIRT5 has emerged as a critical regulator of mitochondrial quality control and metabolic homeostasis. Core metabolic organs: tissue heterogeneity in SIRT5 function

The liver, pancreas, heart, and kidneys are core visceral organs maintaining metabolic homeostasis. In type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and its complications, SIRT5 exhibits significant tissue-specific functional heterogeneity.

In liver, heart, and kidney, SIRT5 primarily exerts protective effects. Using a liver-specific SIRT5 overexpression ob/ob mouse model (ob/ob-SIRT5 OE) generated by CRISPR/Cas9, Du et al. observed reduced malonylation and succinylation, improved glycolysis, inhibited gluconeogenesis, enhanced fatty acid oxidation, and attenuated hepatic steatosis (Du et al. 2018). In T2D complications, SIRT5 protection is similarly significant. Wu et al. found that SIRT5 downregulation in diabetic mice exacerbates cardiac dysfunction, hypertrophy, and lipotoxicity, whereas SIRT5 overexpression ameliorates these changes (Wu et al. 2024). Mechanistically, SIRT5 deficiency impairs fatty acid oxidation (FAO) via succinylation of CPT2 at Lys424, inactivating its enzymatic activity and causing acylcarnitine accumulation, leading to diabetic cardiac lipotoxicity (Wu et al. 2024). Wei et al. reported that SIRT5 deficiency exacerbates myocardial injury in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) mice, inducing oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cardiomyocyte senescence, necroptosis, and DNA damage; conversely, SIRT5 overexpression alleviates these effects. This process involves SIRT5-mediated malonylation of glutathione S-transferase π1 (GSTP1) (Wei et al. 2024), further linking SIRT5 to cardiac metabolism. Additionally, Bai et al. found that SIRT5 overexpression reduces malonylation by ~ 80% in renal proximal tubules of T2D BKS db/db mice. The affected proteins are associated with glycolysis and FAO, and reduced malonylation promotes both processes, contributing to metabolic reprogramming in diabetic kidney disease (DKD) (Baek et al. 2023).

Adipose tissue: a key switch for energy expenditure and thermogenesis

As an endocrine and energy-storing organ distributed throughout the body, adipose tissue is a key node for SIRT5-mediated regulation of systemic energy expenditure. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is mitochondria-rich and critical for energy expenditure, thermogenesis, and glucose homeostasis. Early studies found that mitochondrial protein Ksuc and Kmal are highly expressed in BAT and regulated by SIRT5. Uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), a key thermogenic protein in BAT, exhibits significantly increased succinylation at K56 and K151 upon SIRT5 loss. This succinylation shortens UCP1 protein half-life and reduces its uncoupled respiratory capacity, impairing BAT thermogenic function (Wang et al. 2019). Zhai et al. observed similar results in a browning white adipose tissue mouse model and further elucidated mechanisms: SIRT5 reduces lipid synthesis in white adipose tissue by inhibiting succinylation, regulates C/EBPβ expression, affects UCP1 transcription, and promotes white adipose tissue browning, offering a new target for improving lipid metabolic disorders (Zhai et al. 2024).

Structural tissues: local metabolism and degenerative changes

SIRT5 function extends beyond classical metabolic organs to impact structural tissue pathophysiology. Obesity is a recognized risk factor for osteoarthritis (OA), with mechanisms linked to chondrocyte metabolic dysregulation. Studies found markedly elevated Kmal levels in cartilage of obese mice and in chondrocytes treated with high glucose and insulin. SIRT5 is expressed in chondrocytes in an age-dependent manner and influences Kmal levels and chondrocyte metabolism following joint injury. SIRT5 deficiency decreases glycolytic rate and basal mitochondrial respiration in chondrocytes, contributing to OA pathogenesis (Zhu et al. 2021).

In summary, SIRT5 exhibits highly tissue-specific functional profiles in metabolic syndrome and related diseases through its non-classical enzymatic activities (demalonylation, desuccinylation). It primarily exerts protective effects in core visceral organs, adipose tissue, and cartilage by removing pathogenic acyl modifications to maintain energy homeostasis and counteract tissue damage. However, its function in pancreatic β-cells appears potentially inhibitory, highlighting the complexity and context dependence of its roles. These findings collectively reveal that non-classical protein acylation serves as a key mediator transmitting metabolic stress signals, with SIRT5 as a central regulatory node responding to these signals. The synergistic regulatory networks of non-classical enzymatic modifications across different metabolic organs and their correlation with metabolic syndrome progression require further elucidation.

Cancer-associated metabolic reprogramming: bidirectional regulation by non-classical sirtuin functions

Cancer is increasingly recognized as a disease of metabolic reprogramming, wherein tumor cells rewire metabolic networks to support rapid proliferation, hypoxic survival, and metastasis. The Warburg effect—enhanced glycolysis even under aerobic conditions—exemplifies this transformation and has been linked to aberrant lysine acylations (e.g., succinylation, malonylation, lactylation) that directly regulate metabolic enzyme activity and gene transcription. Through their non-classical enzymatic functions, sirtuin family members play dual and context-dependent roles in cancer metabolism: they can act as metabolic tumor suppressors by reversing the Warburg effect and restoring oxidative phosphorylation, or conversely, promote tumor progression by enabling metabolic flexibility under nutrient stress. SIRT1 and SIRT3: broad-spectrum anticancer effects via delactylation and debutyrylation

The clinical and genetic profiling of SIRT1 and SIRT3 provides a compelling framework for their roles as fundamental tumor suppressors in human oncology. Genomic characterization across diverse cancer lineages has unveiled frequent allelic loss and somatic mutations at both the SIRT1 and SIRT3 loci (Han et al. 2013; Kim et al. 2010), findings substantiated by clinical evidence where their expression levels serve as critical determinants of patient prognosis in gastrointestinal and breast cancers (Liu et al. 2025; Finley et al. 2011). While these tumor-suppressive capacities have long been anchored in classical deacetylation—such as SIRT1-mediated p53 regulation and SIRT3-mediated mitochondrial ROS control (Han et al. 2013; Kim et al. 2010)—their broader impact is increasingly attributed to a dual-pronged mechanism driven by non-classical enzymatic activities. Through this non-classical repertoire, SIRT1 and SIRT3 suppress malignancy by directly targeting metabolic enzymes to reverse tumor-specific metabolic advantages and by modifying histones to induce comprehensive epigenetic reprogramming.

Specifically, lysine lactylation (Kla), a post-translational modification inextricably linked to the Warburg effect, is aberrantly elevated in tumors and promotes malignant phenotypes (Sui et al. 2025). Although SIRT3 plays context-dependent roles across cancer types, it predominantly acts as a tumor suppressor. SIRT3 deficiency induces the Warburg effect, promoting tumorigenesis (Haigis et al. 2012). Malate enzyme 2 (ME2), which converts malate to pyruvate and NADPH, is inhibited by SIRT3-mediated K352 delactylation, disrupting mitochondrial redox balance, reducing NADP⁺/NADPH ratio and ROS levels, and suppressing colorectal cancer (CRC) cell metabolism and growth; the catalytically inactive H248Y mutant lacks this effect (Li et al. 2025).

Lysine crotonylation (Kcr) is also linked to tumor progression. H3K27cr is upregulated in metastatic CRC tissues and correlates with advanced clinical stages (Liao et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). LINC00922 binds SIRT3, preventing its recruitment to the ETS1 promoter, increasing H3K27cr levels, activating ETS1 transcription, and promoting CRC invasion and migration. Conversely, SIRT3 overexpression reduces H3K27cr and inhibits CRC progression (Liao et al. 2023).

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intracellular Kla levels are elevated, and SIRT3 expression negatively correlates with Kla (Jin et al. 2023). Quantitative proteomics identified 52 potential SIRT3-regulated lactylated proteins, including CCNE2. Lactylation at CCNE2 K347/K348 correlates with poor HCC prognosis (Jin et al. 2023).

In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), SIRT3 exerts dual catalytic effects on H3K9la in nucleus and mitochondria, regulating tumor suppressor gene transcription and inhibiting ESCC proliferation, migration, and invasion (Chen et al. 2025).

In gliomas, SIRT1 targets polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1). PTBP1 K436 lactylation in glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) weakens its interaction with E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21, inhibiting proteasomal degradation and promoting glycolytic gene expression. SIRT1 reverses this by delactylating K436 (Zhou et al. 2025).

In gastric cancer (GC), SIRT1 delactylates histones. SIRT1 deficiency elevates H3K18la, activating lncRNA H19 transcription and enhancing glycolysis, forming a positive feedback loop promoting GC proliferation. SIRT1 overexpression reduces H3K18la, disrupting this loop and inhibiting GC progression (Tsukihara et al. 2025).

In summary, SIRT3 targets mitochondrial metabolic enzymes (ME2, CCNE2) and histones (H3K9, H3K27) to influence metabolic reprogramming and gene transcription, whereas SIRT1 regulates RNA-binding proteins (PTBP1) and histones (H3K18). Both remove lactylation from key substrates, reversing the Warburg effect and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.

SIRT4 and SIRT6: metabolism and DNA repair regulation via mono-ADP-ribosylation

In contrast to SIRT1 and SIRT3, which function primarily as deacetylases, SIRT4 and SIRT6 exert their effects mainly through Mono-ADP-Ribosylation (MARylation.) This activity underpins their roles as cellular stabilizers, with a refined division of labor: SIRT4 maintains metabolic homeostasis, while SIRT6 safeguards genomic stability.

SIRT4 MARylation is critical for metabolic homeostasis in HCC. SIRT4 modifies MAT2A, a key enzyme in methionine metabolism, via MARylation. Disruption of this process blocks the mTORC1-c-Myc-SIRT4 axis, leading to methionine metabolic dysregulation and creating a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment for HCC progression (Zhao et al. 2022).

SIRT6 MARylation primarily targets DNA repair proteins, influencing tumor cell survival and treatment sensitivity. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), CRISPRi screening revealed that targeting HDAC8 and SIRT6 enhances sensitivity to the NAMPT inhibitor KPT-9274 (Zhang et al. 2021). Mechanistically, SIRT6 inhibition eliminates PARP1 MARylation, increasing DNA damage sensitivity and impairing DNA repair (Zhang et al. 2021). In acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), SIRT6 MARylation activates PARP1, promoting HMGB1 poly-ADP-ribosylation and acetylation, facilitating HMGB1 translocation and autophagy (Kong et al. 2020). SIRT6 overexpression also correlates with ERK1/2 phosphorylation and MMP9 upregulation, enhancing non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) migration and invasion (Bai et al. 2016). SIRT6 knockdown induces G2/M arrest, downregulates histone variant genes, and inhibits HCC progression (Lee et al. 2016). Similarly, SIRT6 knockdown reduces melanoma growth, survival, and colony formation, inducing G1 arrest and senescence (Garcia-Peterson et al. 2017). Additionally, SIRT6 elevates intracellular ADP-ribose, activating TRPM2/NFAT signaling and promoting IL-8/TNF-α expression, enhancing pancreatic cancer cell migration (Bauer et al. 2012).

SIRT6 also exhibits non-deacetylase-dependent tumor-suppressive effects. In PI3K pathway-activated tumors (e.g., breast cancer), SIRT6 overexpression inhibits tumor growth and stemness; the deacetylase-dead D63Y mutant retains wild-type effects (Ioris et al. 2017). Mechanistically, rather than altering upstream AKT phosphorylation, this catalytically inactive SIRT6 functions as a transcriptional corepressor. It localizes to chromatin to dampen the transcription of downstream PI3K target genes. This suggests that SIRT6 mediates tumor suppression by acting as a structural scaffold to recruit other repressor complexes or by directly engaging in protein–protein interactions with transcription factors, even though the precise protein interactors in this specific PI3K-driven context remain to be fully elucidated (Ioris et al. 2017). In HBV-associated HCC, HBx disrupts SIRT6-PARP1 binding, inhibiting MARylation required for DNA repair and promoting HCC development (Na et al. 2016).

In conclusion, SIRT4 and SIRT6 anchor cellular homeostasis through MARylation-mediated metabolic regulation and genomic maintenance. The functional patterns of SIRT4 in suppressing tumor progression by modulating key metabolic enzymes (e.g., MAT2A), alongside the regulatory role of SIRT6 over PARP1 in DNA repair, represent robust mechanistic frameworks. Nevertheless, the functional duality of SIRT6—acting as either a tumor suppressor or an oncogene depending on the biological context—remains a pivotal unresolved issue. Future research must further elucidate how SIRT6 functions as a transcriptional co-repressor scaffold independent of its enzymatic activity, as well as the substrate specificity of its MARylation within complex tumor microenvironments.

SIRT5 and SIRT7: bidirectional regulation via desuccinylation and demalonylation

SIRT5 and SIRT7 are the most controversial sirtuin members, exhibiting opposing bidirectional regulatory roles through desuccinylation and demalonylation across various cancers. They act as both tumor suppressors and oncogenes, with contradictory evidence even within the same cancer type. Understanding this bidirectionality is key to revealing their true biological functions and developing precision therapeutic targets.

Tumor-suppressive functions: repairing core metabolism and inhibiting immune evasion

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), gastric cancer (GC), and prostate cancer (PCa), SIRT5 and SIRT7 primarily function as tumor suppressors by desuccinylating metabolic enzymes and epigenetic regulators.

In HCC, high succinylation correlates with poor prognosis (Bai et al. 2022). SIRT5 is downregulated in HCC tissues, promoting bile acid secretion via increased succinylation, inducing M2 macrophage polarization, and creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment favoring tumor-initiating cells (Sun et al. 2022). SIRT5 desuccinylates citrate synthase (CS) at K375, reducing mitochondrial activity and ATP in HepG2 cells, increasing ROS and apoptosis, and inhibiting HCC proliferation (Cao et al. 2025). SIRT7 interacts with HBV core protein, binds cccDNA, and catalyzes H3K122 desuccinylation, cooperating with SUV39H1 and SETD2 to induce HBV transcriptional silencing (Yu et al. 2021).

In GC, SIRT5 is downregulated (Wang et al. 2019). SIRT5 overexpression desuccinylates S100A10 at K47, reducing its stability and inhibiting GC invasion and migration (Wang et al. 2019). SIRT5 also desuccinylates OGDH, disrupting mitochondrial function, reducing ATP, increasing ROS, and inhibiting GC growth (Lu et al. 2019).

In advanced PCa, SIRT5 is reduced and correlates with poor survival (Kwon et al. 2023). LDHA-K118su increases with PCa progression and correlates with enhanced migration and invasion (Kwon et al. 2023).

In summary, SIRT5 and SIRT7 exert robust tumor-suppressive effects primarily through desuccinylation, focusing on metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation. The mechanisms by which SIRT5 inhibits key enzymes like CS and LDHA, and SIRT7 mediates epigenetic silencing of viral transcription, have emerged as reliable functional models. Nevertheless, the substrate specificity of these deacylases across different tissues remains to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, their complex roles in modulating the immune microenvironment require deeper validation to determine their broad applicability in clinical interventions.

Oncogenic functions: metabolic adaptation, therapy resistance, and immune microenvironment remodeling

Conversely, SIRT5 and SIRT7 promote oncogenesis in CRC, NSCLC, AML, pancreatic cancer, and bladder cancer by enabling tumor cell survival under nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and chemotherapy.

In CRC, SIRT5 expression correlates with poor prognosis (Shi et al. 2019). SIRT5 activates GLUD1 via demalonylation, promoting glutamine entry into the TCA cycle and accelerating CRC progression (Wang et al. 2018). Under glutamine deprivation, SIRT5 desuccinylates ME2 at K346, enhancing mitochondrial respiration and sustaining CRC proliferation (Teng et al. 2024). SIRT5 desuccinylates PPA2 at K176, reducing NEDD4-mediated HIF-1α ubiquitination, enhancing HIF-1α stability under hypoxia, and promoting glycolysis and metastasis (Zhang et al. 2025). SIRT5 also activates transketolase (TKT) via demalonylation, promoting the non-oxidative pentose phosphate pathway and nucleotide synthesis, protecting CRC cells from DNA damage (Wang et al. 2022).

In NSCLC, SIRT5 is overexpressed and correlates with poor survival (Lu et al. 2014). NRF2 positively correlates with SIRT5 mRNA in cancer tissues (Lu et al. 2014). SIRT5 desuccinylates ACAT1 in EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), activating NRF2, inhibiting CCL5/CXCL10 secretion, reducing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Shouhan et al. 2024). SIRT5 also desuccinylates SUCLG2 at K93, promoting TRIM21-mediated lysosomal degradation, upregulating succinyl-CoA and protein succinylation, and facilitating LUAD proliferation (Hu et al. 2023).

In AML, SIRT5 is a survival gene (Yan et al. 2021). SIRT5 knockdown increases succinylation, malonylation, and acetylation, but in SIRT5-dependent lines, OXPHOS, ECAR, and NAD⁺/NADH decline due to disrupted glutamine metabolism (Yan et al. 2021).

In breast cancer (BCa), SIRT5 is overexpressed and correlates with poor prognosis (Negrón Abril et al. 2021). SIRT5 loss hyper-succinylates IDH2 and other metabolic enzymes, increasing oxidative stress and inhibiting BCa proliferation (Negrón Abril et al. 2021). SIRT5 desuccinylates GLS at K164, protecting it from ubiquitination and degradation, promoting glutamine metabolism and BCa proliferation (Greene et al. 2019). SIRT5 desuccinylates MTHFD2, reducing BCa senescence and enhancing chemotherapy resistance (Xianhong et al. 2025).

SIRT5/7 also mediate therapy resistance. SIRT5 desuccinylates HADHA at K644, promoting FAO and driving AML progression and resistance to venetoclax/azacitidine (Wang et al. 2025). SIRT5 inhibition impairs OXPHOS and ATP production, enhancing venetoclax-mediated metabolic suppression and AML cell death (Wang et al. 2025). In pancreatic cancer, SIRT7 desuccinylates H3K122 at the GLUT3 enhancer, inhibiting GLUT3 transcription and promoting gemcitabine resistance (Chen et al. 2024). Fasting-mimicking conditions upregulate SIRT7, enhancing cancer cell survival under stress (Chen et al. 2024). In bladder cancer, high SIRT7 and EZH2 expression correlates with poor platinum-based chemotherapy response. SIRT7 enhances EZH2 activity by reducing its succinylation, co-regulating H3K18ac and H3K27me3 at the RND3 promoter, inhibiting RND3 expression (Cao et al. 2024). Targeting SIRT7/EZH2 may enhance platinum efficacy in bladder cancer.

In summary, SIRT5 and SIRT7 exert pivotal oncogenic functions by leveraging non-deacetylation modifications—specifically desuccinylation and demalonylation—to facilitate metabolic reprogramming, hypoxia adaptation, and immune evasion. The mechanistic patterns of SIRT5 in optimizing glutamine metabolism via GLUD1, ME2, and GLS, as well as its role in supporting metabolic adaptation by stabilizing HIF-1α, have demonstrated significant robustness. Nevertheless, the precise “context-dependent” mechanisms governing the functional switch between tumor-suppressive and oncogenic roles of these sirtuins remain an unresolved puzzle. Furthermore, developing effective synergistic therapeutic strategies that target SIRT5/7-mediated resistance—such as resistance to venetoclax or platinum-based agents—remains a challenging frontier for future clinical translation.

Dissecting functional contradictions: ccRCC, breast cancer, and thyroid cancer

The bidirectionality of SIRT5/7 across cancer types is perplexing, and in ccRCC, BCa, and thyroid cancer, this paradox manifests within the same cancer type, providing a “scalpel” to dissect underlying mechanisms.

In ccRCC, SIRT5 promotes proliferation by inhibiting SDHA succinylation and SDH interaction (Ma et al. 2019). SIRT5 upregulation also confers sunitinib resistance by regulating IDH2 K413 succinylation, enhancing mitochondrial metabolism and antioxidant capacity (Meng et al. 2021). However, SIRT5 correlates with good prognosis in ccRCC (Tan et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2021) and is downregulated in ccRCC tissues, negatively correlating with TNM stage and grade (Yihan et al. 2021). SIRT5 overexpression desuccinylates PDHA1 at K351, activating PDC, enhancing TCA cycle, inhibiting the Warburg effect, and suppressing tumor growth (Yihan et al. 2021).

In breast cancer, SIRT7 is elevated and correlates with poor prognosis (Huo et al. 2020), yet paradoxically, low SIRT7/IDH1 predicts poor outcome (Su et al. 2021). SIRT7 maintains IDH1 expression via SREBP1, sustaining α-KG levels, inhibiting glycolysis and the Warburg effect (Su et al. 2021). The catalytically inactive H187Y mutant retains wild-type effects on IDH1 promoter activity, indicating deacetylase-independent mechanisms (Su et al. 2021). Mechanistically, rather than relying on its enzymatic activity, SIRT7 physically interacts with SREBP1 and acts as a transcriptional co-activator, facilitating the direct recruitment of SREBP1 to the IDH1 promoter to drive gene expression (Su et al. 2021). Additionally, MCM6-Kcr increases in invasive breast cancer, inducing DNA replication stress and apoptosis, which is counteracted by SIRT7-mediated debutyrylation and RNF8-mediated ubiquitination (Song et al. 2025).

In thyroid cancer, SIRT5 desuccinylates LDHA, inhibiting glycolysis in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) cells. lncRNA GLTC disrupts this interaction, increasing LDHA K155 succinylation, promoting glycolysis and PTC viability (Shi et al. 2023). GLTC-enhanced LDHA succinylation also promotes NAD⁺ production, contributing to radioactive iodine (RAI) resistance; modulating SIRT5-LDHA interaction may restore RAI sensitivity (Shi et al. 2023). SIRT7 promotes PTC proliferation by desuccinylating and destabilizing LATS1 (Li and Pu 2025). SIRT7 also desuccinylates KIF23 at K537, enhancing its stability and promoting anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) proliferation and migration (Wu et al. 2024).

In summary, the functional contradictions of SIRT5 and SIRT7 within ccRCC, BCa, and thyroid cancer underscore a biological impact heavily dependent on substrate selectivity and non-enzymatic activities. Robust mechanistic patterns have been identified, such as SIRT5 suppressing the Warburg effect via PDHA1 desuccinylation and SIRT7 acting as a transcriptional co-activator to maintain IDH1 expression. Conversely, their roles in promoting progression by stabilizing SDHA or KIF23 are equally substantiated. These paradoxes suggest that sirtuins may flip their functional polarity by switching targeted substrates or utilizing protein–protein interactions (e.g., scaffolding) under varying metabolic stresses or genetic backgrounds. Future research must identify the molecular switches that trigger these functional shifts and determine which mode predominates within specific clinical subtypes.

Aging-related metabolic decline and neurodegenerative disorders: bidirectional protection and damage via non-classical functions

Aging is fundamentally a process of progressive metabolic decline (Sun et al. 2025), characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction (Miwa et al. 2022), disrupted proteostasis (Ding et al. 2025), and chronic low-grade inflammation (Fu et al. 2025). Neurodegenerative diseases, as typical age-related disorders, share common metabolic dysregulation mechanisms with systemic aging, including impaired glucose metabolism, oxidative stress, and defective autophagy (López-Otín et al. 2023). The sirtuin family, as NAD⁺-dependent enzymes, functions as critical metabolic sensors integrating cellular energy status with proteostasis and genomic stability (Grabowska et al. 2017). The physiological significance of sirtuins in modulating mammalian aging has been profoundly illustrated by studies. For instance, hypothalamus-specific overexpression of SIRT1 (the BRASTO mouse model) has been shown to significantly extend lifespan and delay age-related physiological decline by enhancing systemic metabolism and physical activity (Satoh et al. 2013). Similarly, whole-body (pan) overexpression of SIRT6 has been demonstrated to extend the lifespan of mice, a phenotype characterized by improved metabolic homeostasis and reduced frailty (Kanfi et al. 2012). These landmark findings establish a robust link between sirtuin activity and systemic longevity, providing a foundation for understanding their complex roles in aging-related pathologies. However, sirtuin non-classical functions exhibit paradoxical roles in the aging brain. While certain members confer consistent neuroprotection, others demonstrate a profound functional dichotomy, shifting between protective and pathogenic states dictated by the metabolic microenvironment and cell-specific context.

SIRT6: systemic anti-aging via mono-ADP-ribosylation

SIRT6 is a key regulator of aging (Kanfi et al. 2012; Chang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2018), and its functional status closely correlates with lifespan (Ramteke et al. 2025). SIRT6 overexpression delays cellular aging and extends healthy lifespan by reducing age-related DNA damage, maintaining telomere integrity, preserving metabolic homeostasis, and regulating oxidative stress (Park et al. 2025; Roichman et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2022). In aged mice, SIRT6 is reduced in muscle tissue; SIRT6 knockout induces premature neuromuscular junction (NMJ) degeneration, characterized by structural degradation, impaired transmission, and motor decline (Zhang et al. 2024). Mechanistically, SIRT6 mono-ADP-ribosylates transcription factor YY1, promoting its dissociation from the dystrophin promoter and enabling YY1 binding to SMURF2 E3 ligase for degradation, thereby maintaining dystrophin expression and delaying NMJ degeneration (Zhang et al. 2024).

A targeted sequencing study of SIRT6 in 450 German-Jewish centenarians and 550 controls identified enrichment of two linked substitutions (N308K/A313S) in centenarian SIRT6 alleles (centSIRT6) (Simon et al. 2022). This allele exhibits enhanced MARylation and reduced deacetylase activity in vitro, strengthening SIRT6-lamin A/C interaction, promoting DNA repair, inhibiting transposable elements, enhancing antioxidant capacity, and more effectively inducing cancer cell death, contributing to extended human lifespan (Simon et al. 2022).

Additionally, SIRT6 may reduce transcription factor Sp1 protein levels via direct binding, decreasing Sp1-mediated activation of Slc7a11, reducing cystine uptake, activating cystathionine γ-lyase (Cgl), and promoting cysteine-to-H₂S conversion, exerting anti-aging effects (Touitou et al. 2025). Notably, the catalytically inactive H133Y mutant retains this effect, independent of deacetylase function (Touitou et al. 2025).

In summary, SIRT6-mediated MARylation for genomic maintenance and metabolic regulation constitutes a robust paradigm for systemic anti-aging. Key mechanisms, such as SIRT6 modifying YY1 to preserve neuromuscular junction integrity and the centenarian SIRT6 allele (centSIRT6) enhancing DNA repair via elevated MARylation, have been well-substantiated. Nevertheless, the ability of SIRT6 to modulate Sp1 and H₂S metabolism independent of its catalytic activity (as evidenced by the H133Y mutant) highlights its complexity as a transcriptional co-regulator. Future research must delineate the relative contributions of its enzymatic versus scaffolding functions across various aging tissues and explore strategies to pharmacologically mimic the centSIRT6 variant for clinical interventions.

SIRT5: bidirectional effects of desuccinylation in neuroprotection and neurodamage

Unlike SIRT6’s unidirectional protection, SIRT5 exhibits highly context-dependent bidirectional regulation in age-related neurodegenerative diseases: it acts as a protective factor in chronic neurodegeneration (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, AD) (Wu et al. 2021) but as a pro-damage factor in acute neurological injuries (e.g., ischemic stroke) (Xia et al. 2022), posing challenges to understanding its true functions in the nervous system.

Protective role in neuroinflammation and chronic metabolic imbalance

Lysine succinylation correlates with age-related diseases such as ovarian insufficiency, osteoporosis, and brain injury (Le et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2019; Xiao et al. 2021). CNS aging is closely linked to SIRT5-regulated succinylation. In LPS-induced neuroinflammation and SIRT5 knockdown BV2 microglial models, succinylation increases, mitochondrial adaptability declines, OXPHOS weakens, and glycolysis enhances (Zhao et al. 2024). Under inflammatory stimulation, these changes exacerbate, increasing ROS, lipid droplet accumulation, and lipid peroxidation (Zhao et al. 2024).

Zavileyskiy et al. quantified 43 acetylation sites in epileptic rat cortex, measuring NAD⁺, SIRT2/3/5 expression, and metabolic enzyme activity (Zavileyskiy et al. 2022). Chronic epilepsy showed no significant changes in acetylation, PDH activity, SIRT2, or NAD⁺, but SIRT3/5 and OGDH activity were reduced, consistent with Aleshin et al. (Aleshin et al. 2023). Succinylation and malonylation negatively correlated with SIRT5, while acetylation positively correlated with SIRT2 (Zavileyskiy et al. 2022), suggesting synergistic sirtuin actions in seizure activity.

SIRT5, as a key desuccinylase, is closely associated with neurodegeneration (Kumar and Lombard 2015; Li and Liu 2016; Lian et al. 2023). In AD mouse brains, SIRT5 is downregulated. SIRT5 overexpression inhibits neuroinflammation and microglial/astrocyte activation by activating autophagy, alleviating oxidative stress and apoptosis-induced brain damage (Wu et al. 2021). SIRT5-catalyzed RAB7A desuccinylation improves cadmium-induced autophagic flux blockage and AD-like pathogenesis. Cadmium exposure reduces SIRT5, increases RAB7A K31 succinylation, and blocks autophagic flux; SIRT5 overexpression alleviates this, reducing Aβ deposition and memory deficits in cadmium-exposed FAD4T mice (Deng et al. 2024).

In summary, SIRT5 exerts robust neuroprotective effects in chronic neurodegeneration by maintaining mitochondrial function and optimizing autophagic flux through desuccinylation (e.g., of RAB7A). This paradigm is well-substantiated in alleviating Aβ deposition and neuroinflammation. However, the functional polarization of SIRT5 between chronic protection and acute injury (e.g., ischemic stroke) remains a pivotal unresolved paradox. Future research must focus on identifying the environmental cues that trigger this bidirectionality to determine the optimal timing for precise intervention across various neuropathological stages.

Pro-damage effects in acute ischemic neuroinjury

In ischemic stroke, SIRT5’s role is completely reversed. Xia et al. found that SIRT5 increases in microglia during early ischemic stroke, and SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation promotes neuroinflammation and neuronal damage (Xia et al. 2022). Mechanistically, SIRT5 desuccinylates Annexin A1 (ANXA1) at K166, reducing its SUMOylation, promoting nuclear translocation, preventing membrane recruitment and secretion, leading to microglial hyperactivation, pro-inflammatory cytokine overexpression, and exacerbating neuronal damage after ischemic stroke (Xia et al. 2022).

Possible sources of bidirectional effects: substrates, temporal phases, and cellular environments

The contradictory mechanisms of SIRT5 in AD and ischemic stroke remain incompletely elucidated. Based on existing evidence, reasonable inferences include: (1) Substrate selectivity: RAB7A desuccinylation activates autophagy-lysosomal pathway for pathological protein clearance, whereas ANXA1 desuccinylation blocks its anti-inflammatory function and promotes microglial hyperactivation, indicating SIRT5 functional direction depends on substrate accessibility in specific pathological environments. (2) Disease phases and inflammation intensity: AD is chronic low-grade inflammation, where SIRT5 exerts long-term protection via metabolic homeostasis and autophagy; ischemic stroke involves acute inflammatory storm, where rapid SIRT5 upregulation in microglia may be “hijacked” to amplify acute damage responses. (3) Cell type dependency: SIRT5 protection is primarily neuronal, while pro-damage effects are microglial; metabolic and inflammatory differences between cell types may dictate SIRT5 functional output. (4) Sirtuin family synergy: Epilepsy studies show complex correlations among SIRT2/3/5 expression, suggesting potential substrate competition or functional complementation, though roles in AD and stroke require clarification.

Cardiovascular diseases: cardiac metabolic homeostasis regulated by Sirtuin-mediated deacylation

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD)—including atherosclerosis, myocardial diseases, valvular heart diseases, heart failure, arrhythmias, and hypertensive heart disease—remain leading causes of death worldwide (Martin et al. 2025). Emerging post-translational modifications (PTMs) function as key metabolic sensors regulating cardiac homeostasis, and their dysregulation directly contributes to CVD pathogenesis (Sun et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025). Among these, succinylation, malonylation, and their regulation by SIRT5 are closely linked to cardiac metabolism and function.

Succinylation and succinyl-CoA are abundant in the heart and enriched in metabolic pathways (Boylston et al. 2015; Sadhukhan et al. 2016). As noted, SIRT5 alleviates diabetes-induced cardiac damage by regulating succinylation and malonylation substrates (Wei et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024). SIRT5 knockout induces cardiac succinylation accumulation, leading to fatty acid metabolism defects, increased glycolysis, reduced ATP production, and ultimately ischemia–reperfusion injury and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (Boylston et al. 2015; Sadhukhan et al. 2016).

Cardiac glucotoxicity, electron transport chain imbalance, and oxidative stress are linked to the SIRT3-AGO2-CYTB axis (Zhan et al. 2024). In diabetic cardiomyocytes, mitochondrial AGO2 is reduced, decreasing electron transport chain (ETC) subunits and disrupting mitochondrial function. Conversely, AGO2 overexpression restores these subunits—particularly cytochrome b (CYTB)—thereby reducing ROS and alleviating diabetic cardiac dysfunction (Zhan et al. 2024). Mechanistically, the cytosolic short isoform of SIRT3 mediates AGO2 mitochondrial translocation via demalonylation. Under diabetic conditions, increased AGO2 malonylation impairs its translocation; SIRT3 removes this modification, restoring AGO2 mitochondrial localization. Once localized, AGO2 recruits translation elongation factor TUFM to enhance mitochondrial gene translation and regulate CYTB expression (Zhan et al. 2024).

Peoples et al. used a cardiac-specific Slc25a3 knockout mouse model to induce impaired mitochondrial ATP synthesis. In damaged cardiac mitochondria, SIRT3 was reduced, malonyl-CoA increased, and SIRT5 acetylation elevated, leading to increased IDH2 acetylation and malonylation (Peoples et al. 2021). Crosstalk exists between these modifications: SIRT5 acetylation at K203 reduces its demalonylase activity, indicating that acetylation regulates malonylation levels (Peoples et al. 2021). Although malonylation inhibits IDH2 while acetylation enhances it, the net effect of both modifications ultimately increases IDH2 activity, contrasting with the view that disrupted mitochondrial deacetylation inhibits metabolic processes (Peoples et al. 2021).

Research on sirtuin decrotonylation and mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase activity in CVD remains limited. SIRT1 deficiency significantly upregulates Kcr at SERCA2a K120, affecting PPAR signaling pathway proteins (reduced PPARα, increased PPARβ/δ and APOA1), inducing metabolic disorders, cardiac hypertrophy, cardiomyocyte ultrastructural abnormalities, heart dysfunction, and arrhythmias (Chen et al. 2023). Under glucose starvation, SIRT7 binds histone variant mH2A1.1 via its own ADP-ribosylation modification and repositions to intergenic chromatin regions, promoting mH2A1 enrichment in neighboring genes and regulating genes involved in aging, autophagy, and metabolism (Simonet et al. 2020). SIRT7 deletion in mice under caloric restriction induces abnormal gene expression profiles, impaired autophagy, premature aging, and cardiac hypertrophy (Simonet et al. 2020).

In summary, SIRT5 protects against diabetes-related cardiac injury through succinylation and malonylation regulation, forming unique crosstalk with SIRT3: demalonylating CPT2 and desuccinylating GSTP1 alleviates myocardial damage. However, SIRT5 acetylation reduces its demalonylase capacity, and together with reduced SIRT3, leads to IDH2 acetylation/malonylation accumulation, ultimately enhancing IDH2 activity. SIRT3 protects by demalonylating AGO2, maintaining cardiac mitochondrial function. Additionally, SIRT1 and SIRT7 exert protective effects in CVD through decrotonylation and mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase activities, though relevant substrates and pathways require further elucidation.

Current research has yielded a highly diversified landscape of modulators. Tool compounds represent the foundational tier of this pharmacological landscape, serving as indispensable probes for validating basic biological mechanisms. Molecules such as the specific SIRT5 inhibitor MC3482 (Xia et al. 2024) and the pyrimidine derivative Compound 58 (Mo et al. 2023) exhibit robust target engagement in vitro and in localized acute injury models (e.g., ischemic stroke and pyogenic acute kidney injury). These agents are critical for deciphering the precise cellular consequences of inhibiting specific acylations, such as ANXA1 desuccinylation. However, their utility remains largely confined to the laboratory. They lack the optimized pharmacokinetic properties, metabolic stability, and systemic safety profiles required for human use, necessitating significant structural refinement before progressing down the drug development pipeline.

Advancing further along the translational spectrum are preclinical leads, which have demonstrated robust in vivo efficacy across systemic disease models. Synthetic small molecules within this category offer favorable physicochemical properties and specific pathway modulation. For example, the SIRT5-specific activator MC3215 effectively reduces systemic protein malonylation to alleviate myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (Castiello et al. 2025), while the SIRT6 activator MDL-800 serves as a powerful preclinical model for reversing temozolomide resistance in glioblastoma via the SIRT6-H3K9la-MGMT axis (Zou et al. 2025). Concurrently, natural products like Quercetin (Zhou et al. 2023; Chang et al. 2021), Curcumin (Xu et al. 2025), and Kaempferol (Song et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025) function as versatile preclinical leads with high biocompatibility. By directly binding to sirtuins or upregulating their expression, these compounds exhibit multi-target synergistic effects in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and osteoarthritis models. Despite their potential, the clinical progression of these natural preclinical leads is frequently bottlenecked by poor bioavailability and insufficient tissue-specific targeting. Furthermore, biological modalities, such as engineered extracellular vesicles designed to modulate SIRT5/p53 pathways (Wagner and Hirschey 2014), represent an emerging class of preclinical leads offering natural carrier mechanisms for precise signal delivery.

The most advanced tier comprises clinically meaningful candidates, which present immediate translational value through either ongoing clinical trials or drug repurposing strategies. Repurposing FDA-approved drugs that inadvertently modulate sirtuin non-classical activities circumvents the lengthy safety evaluation phases of traditional drug discovery. Notably, the local anesthetic Lidocaine has been shown to inhibit lung cancer progression by altering SIRT5-mediated HIST1H2BL desuccinylation (Chen et al. 2024). Similarly, the cholesterol-lowering agent Cholestyramine rescues SIRT5-deficient immunosuppressive microenvironments in hepatocellular carcinoma (Sun et al. 2022), and Dichloroacetic acid (DCA) dynamically regulates the succinylome in colorectal cancer (Zhu et al. 2016). Beyond repurposed drugs, clinical-stage metabolic precursors and modulators, such as Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) for aging-related neuromuscular decline (Zhang et al. 2024) and the combination of the SIRT1 activator SRT2104 with oxamic acid for gastric cancer (Tsukihara et al. 2025), represent candidates with established safety profiles in humans, positioning them at the forefront of clinical translation.

Despite the promise of therapies targeting sirtuins’ non-classical activities, current research must overcome several substantial hurdles:

A fundamental biological paradox in this field lies in the low stoichiometry of non-classical modifications (Wagner and Hirschey 2014). Quantitative proteomics has revealed that the global stoichiometry of modifications such as lysine succinylation (Ksuc) or malonylation is generally very low, often modifying only a small fraction of the total target protein pool (Weinert et al. 2013; Baeza et al. 2016). This raises a critical question: how can such low-abundance modifications exert significant physiological and pathological effects? Current paradigms suggest several mechanisms to explain this discrepancy: (1) Localized concentration: while overall cellular stoichiometry is low, the modification levels within specific subcellular microdomains (e.g., mitochondrial matrix) or at defined chromatin loci can be exceptionally high (Wagner and Hirschey 2014; Kosciuk et al. 2019). (2) Allosteric and catalytic targeting: these acylations frequently occur at critical active sites of rate-limiting metabolic enzymes. In highly sensitive metabolic networks, inhibiting even a minor fraction of a rate-limiting bottleneck can trigger an amplified disruption in downstream metabolic fluxes (Baeza et al. 2016). (3) Complex inactivation: for multi-subunit protein complexes, the modification of a single subunit may be sufficient to structurally alter and incapacitate the entire macromolecular machinery (Narita et al. 2019). (4) Dynamic flux: the rapid temporal turnover of these modifications, driven by the constant interplay between spontaneous acylation and sirtuin-mediated deacylation, may hold more regulatory significance than their static steady-state levels (Wagner and Hirschey 2014). Therefore, despite their low global stoichiometry, these modifications act as highly sensitive, context-dependent signaling switches.

Beyond stoichiometry, substrate specificity ambiguity and pathway crosstalk present significant hurdles. Sirtuin family members exhibit functional multiplicity, simultaneously mediating various non-classical enzymatic activities. SIRT5, for example, participates in desuccinylation, demalonylation, and deglutarylation, forming complex signaling networks (Carrico et al. 2018). Single-target interventions block or activate only selected modification pathways, making complete pathway control difficult. Compensatory pathway activation may diminish therapeutic effects or trigger unexpected physiological disturbances. Critically, the mechanisms of action for most intervention molecules remain under exploration—specific binding sites, conformational impacts, and selective regulation of distinct modification pathways lack clear molecular models, necessitating high-resolution structural biology and multi-omics analyses (Dai et al. 2018).Compounding these mechanistic hurdles are the inadequate pharmacokinetic properties of current intervention molecules. Many small-molecule compounds exhibit low subtype specificity, carrying off-target risks and potential toxicity that limit clinical safety (Carafa et al. 2016). Natural products, despite low toxicity and multi-target regulation, commonly suffer from low bioavailability, poor metabolic stability, and insufficient tissue targeting, hindering precise disease-site accumulation and narrowing therapeutic windows.

Furthermore, the lack of specific biomarkers and high-resolution detection technologies poses a major barrier to research advancement and contributes to clinical trial failures. Clinically, no biomarkers accurately reflect sirtuin non-classical enzymatic activities or drug targeting efficiency, preventing effective monitoring of in vivo target engagement, modification pathway regulation, or correlation with disease progression (Dai et al. 2018; Curry et al. 2021). Moreover, low abundance and high dynamic variability of sirtuin-mediated non-classical modifications (e.g., Kcr, Kmal) exceed conventional detection sensitivity thresholds (Kosciuk et al. 2019). Existing methods for monitoring specific acylations—primarily targeted mass spectrometry and antibody-based Western blotting—face significant hurdles. The development of specific antibodies for low-abundance modifications lags, and cross-reactivity often distorts results. This detection dilemma directly impedes high-throughput drug screening and preclinical target engagement assessments.

To circumvent these bottlenecks, the field must increasingly adopt substrate-agnostic assays. Because all sirtuin-catalyzed deacylation reactions—regardless of the specific acyl group—consistently consume NAD⁺ and produce nicotinamide (NAM) alongside O-acyl-ADP-ribose derivatives, measuring these universal metabolites provides a unified screening strategy. For example, the PNC1-OPT assay offers a robust, fluorometric method to quantify NAM release, serving as a highly effective, substrate-agnostic platform for high-throughput screening of sirtuin activators and inhibitors (Hubbard and Sinclair 2013). Similarly, mass spectrometry can be repurposed to directly monitor NAM production or NAD⁺ depletion, bypassing the need for elusive modification-specific peptide substrates (Trammell and Brenner 2013). Integrating these metabolite-centric detection strategies will be crucial for circumventing current analytical limitations and accelerating the discovery of novel compounds targeting sirtuin non-classical activities. Consequently, addressing these detection challenges and validating clinical value remain critical directions for future breakthroughs.

Ultimately, clinical translational research lags severely, with a substantial gap between basic research and clinical applications. Most studies remain at cellular and animal model stages, lacking clinical sample validation and trial data support. The influence of disease heterogeneity and individual differences on treatment efficacy remains undefined.

Future perspectives and development directions

Over two decades of research have revealed increasing complexity and apparent contradictions in sirtuin biology: the same family member can exhibit opposite functions in different diseases and even switch roles at different disease stages. This seeming chaos reflects the context-dependent and dynamic nature of biological systems. Therefore, future sirtuin research should move beyond asking “is SIRT X good or bad” to address a more fundamental question: under what conditions does sirtuin functional direction shift, and can we predict and manipulate this transition? Based on this understanding, we propose three key breakthrough directions.

Elucidating dynamic regulatory mechanisms of substrate selectivity

Why does the same sirtuin modify different substrates—or exhibit distinct modification outcomes for the same substrate—under varying contexts? Current studies predominantly identify sirtuin substrates in vitro or under single conditions, yet how substrate selectivity is dynamically regulated in physiological and pathological environments remains unclear (Wu et al. 2022; Du et al. 2024; Bheda et al. 2016). The key lies in systematically analyzing molecular determinants governing substrate selectivity. Factors influencing sirtuin-substrate interaction specificity and efficiency include subcellular NAD⁺ and acyl-CoA concentrations (Madsen et al. 2016; North and Sinclair 2007), sirtuin post-translational modifications (e.g., SIRT5 K203 acetylation reducing demalonylase activity) (Peoples et al. 2021), and changes in chaperone protein expression (Li et al. 2023). These parameters require quantitative assessment across different cell types and metabolic states.

Proximity labeling technologies (e.g., BioID, APEX2) combined with time-resolved mass spectrometry can track dynamic changes in the sirtuin interactome during disease progression, identifying critical transition points for functional switching (Munter et al. 2017; Singer-Krüger et al. 2020). Microfluidic and single-cell imaging technologies can quantify relationships between these parameters and sirtuin activity at single-cell resolution, establishing thresholds where function shifts from protective to detrimental (Wang et al. 2015). Accumulation of such quantitative data will enable predictive models of substrate selectivity and inform clinical intervention timing and dosage ranges.

Additionally, crosstalk among sirtuin family members warrants investigation. High-throughput interaction profiling and combinatorial genetic perturbations can systematically dissect physical interactions and functional synergy or antagonism among sirtuin members, revealing how the sirtuin network operates as an integrated system responding to metabolic perturbations (Lee et al. 2014; Meng et al. 2020).

Developing context-dependent precision targeting strategies

Limited efficacy of existing sirtuin modulators in clinical trials largely stems from insufficient selectivity and lack of context dependence. Developing targeted drugs that exert effects only under specific conditions is critical. Substrate-selective allosteric modulators represent a key breakthrough. Unlike traditional active-site inhibitors, allosteric modulators bind allosteric sites to selectively enhance or weaken sirtuin affinity for particular substrates (Zhang et al. 2022). Leveraging AlphaFold-predicted sirtuin–substrate complex structures, combined with virtual screening and high-throughput biochemical validation, holds promise for achieving substrate-selective regulation (Abramson et al. 2024). Additionally, PROTAC-based selective degradation technologies enable conditional elimination of specific sirtuin members (Huang et al. 2024).

Tissue-specific delivery systems are equally critical. Coupling lipid nanoparticles with cell-specific ligands or using AAV vectors for tissue-specific expression can achieve differential regulation across tissues, resolving paradoxes where activation is needed in one tissue but inhibition in another (Ross et al. 2022).

Companion diagnostic systems are prerequisites for precision therapy. Multi-omics analyses of liquid or tissue biopsies—measuring acylation markers, sirtuin expression levels, and NAD⁺/NADH ratios—can predict patient responses to specific modulators, enabling pretreatment patient stratification (Hu et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2025; Braidy et al. 2013; Schirmer et al. 2012).

Breaking technical bottlenecks in novel PTM detection

Research on enzymatic activities such as depropionylation, debutyrylation, and deglutarylation has lagged due to technical limitations: lack of unified detection methods and standards, low modification abundance requiring higher sensitivity, and absence of highly specific enrichment tools (Shui et al. 2023; Weyh et al. 2024). Development of highly specific pan-modification antibodies is the current bottleneck. For structurally similar modifications (e.g., acetylation, propionylation, butyrylation), antibodies capable of precise discrimination are needed, along with optimized enrichment processes to improve detection sensitivity for low-abundance modifications. In mass spectrometry, parameter optimization and refined data analysis algorithms are required to accurately differentiate fragmentation patterns of structurally similar modifications. Establishing standardized, multi-center validated operating procedures to ensure cross-laboratory data comparability is fundamental to advancing the field. Additionally, developing real-time detection tools based on chemical labeling or biosensors will enable monitoring of dynamic PTM changes in living cells, shifting from “static snapshots” to “dynamic tracking”. These technological breakthroughs will provide essential tools for systematically elucidating the biological functions of novel enzymatic activities and their roles in disease (Gao et al. 2025).

Conclusion

The discovery of sirtuin non-classical enzymatic functions has redefined this family from histone deacetylases to central metabolic sensors that integrate cellular NAD⁺ status, acyl-CoA availability, and substrate supply to fine-tune metabolic networks across health and disease. However, the bidirectional roles of the same non-classical activity across different disease contexts reveal a fundamental insight: these activities did not evolve to execute fixed biological functions, but rather act as dynamic metabolic modulators, flexibly adjusting substrate selection and modification direction in response to cellular metabolic microenvironments—from hepatic glucose/lipid metabolism in metabolic syndrome, to cancer-associated aerobic glycolysis, to age-related mitochondrial decline, to myocardial lipotoxicity in diabetic cardiomyopathy. Recognizing this context dependence fundamentally transforms sirtuin-targeted therapeutic design—from pursuing “universal regulation” toward achieving “context-dependent metabolic precision modulation”. This shift requires both technological innovation and a conceptual transition from reductionism to systems-level thinking, and from linear causality to network dynamics. Ultimately, the goal of sirtuin research extends beyond developing new drugs to redefining our understanding of metabolic homeostasis across aging, metabolic diseases, and cancer, enabling precision medicine that dynamically regulates cellular metabolic fate according to each patient’s unique metabolic status, disease stage, and genetic background.
