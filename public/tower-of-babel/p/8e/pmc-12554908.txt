Increased proportion of “other hrHPV” genotypes other than 16 and 18/45 among women attending ART clinic in Northern Uganda☆
Authors: Frank Ssedyabane, Lugemwa Yvonne, Kamama Fahima, Komakech Simon, Ojosia Lawrence, Ebong James, Kadhumbula Silvester, Wasswa Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We determined the prevalence, genotype distribution, and associated factors for hrHPV among women living with HIV.•This was a cross sectional study (346 women).•There was an increased prevalence of hrHPV, especially those HPV genotypes categorized as “Other hrHPV”.•Unemployment was associated with increased odds of hrHPV among the study participants.Keywords: HIV, Cervical cancer, High-risk human papillomavirus, HPV vaccination

Introduction

Persistent hrHPV infections are a major factor in the development of cervical lesions and their progression to cervical cancer (Bosch et al., 2002). These viruses are categorized based on their virulence as either Low-Risk HPV (types 6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, 70, 72, and 81) or High-Risk HPV (types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68). Cervical cancer is the most common cancer among women living with HIV (Nang et al., 2023) implying an increased risk of HPV infection among women living with HIV (Dreyer, 2018, Nakisige et al., 2017, Nakisige et al., 2020, Temesgen et al., 2021).

There are over 570,000 new cervical cancer cases worldwide and corresponding 311,000 deaths (Zhao et al., 2021). The incidence and mortality rates due to cervical cancer are estimated to be 117,316 and 76,745 respectively (Sharma et al., 2022). The cervical cancer incidence in East Africa stands at 43/100,000 (Sankaranarayanan, 2014), and in Uganda, the age-standardized cervical cancer incidence is higher than the global average at 56.2/100,000 (WHO, 2018). Notably, a dot density map analysis for spatial distribution of cervical cancer cases in Gulu district in the northern region of Uganda reported about 6,000 cervical cancer cases per 100,000 women (Bingi et al., 2018).

Genital HPV infections are disproportionately distributed in many regions of the world, owing largely to many factors including socio-economic status (Sung et al., 2021). Significant ethno-geographic diversity has been found in Sub-Saharan African countries. Multiple hrHPV genotypes, such as HPV-16, 18, 35, and 52, are found in Africa and are responsible for the ongoing increase in cervical cancer rates (Mandiriri et al., 2020). In the Ugandan general population, the prevalence of hrHPV infection has been reported to range from 17 % (Asiimwe et al., 2008) to 39.1 % (Nakigozi et al., 2024). A systematic review and meta-analysis across sub-Saharan Africa reported the prevalence of hrHPV infection in the general population to be 55.1 % (Tchouaket et al., 2023).

With Uganda’s HIV prevalence said to be approximately 6 % (MoH U, 2019) and mostly affecting women (Bray et al., 2018, Ouma et al., 2021), there is a high cervical cancer burden as evidenced by increasing rates of hrHPV genotypes in several Ugandan regions (Nakisige et al., 2017, Banura et al., 2011). Therefore, understanding the distribution and prevalence of HPV genotypes in women living with HIV is crucial for guiding targeted vaccination strategies, screening programs, and clinical management. This study aimed to investigate the genotype specific prevalence of HPV and associated factors among women living with HIV in Uganda.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study from August to October of 2024.

Study setting

The study was carried out at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital (GRRH) in Gulu, the largest city in the Northern Region of Uganda with roughly 27,000 HIV-positive individuals. The hospital is located approximately 332 km (206 miles) north of Kampala, Uganda's capital and largest city. The geographical coordinates of GRRH are 02.777778 latitude and 32.297778 longitude.

It provides essential specialized healthcare services to the community in its catchment area which includes the districts of Amuru, Gulu, Kitgum, Lamwo, and Pader. The hospital runs a functional antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinic which routinely open Monday through Friday. The services offered at the clinic include laboratory services as well as cervical cancer screening services.

Study population

We studied all HIV-positive women at the ART who met the eligibility criteria and gave their written informed consent to participate in the study.

Sampling procedure

A convenient sampling procedure was used to obtain study participants working alongside a physician who identified the women at the ART who met the criteria. This was a non-probability sampling method where we consecutively recruited participants as they attended the clinic. This continued until a final sample size was obtained.

Inclusion criteria

All women who attended the ART clinic at GRRH during the study period, aged 18 years and above and provided written informed consent were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded all those women who reported to be undergoing their menstruation periods, those who were too ill to provide information as well as those who had undergone total hysterectomy.

Sample size estimation

We included a total of 346 participants. This was calculated using the Kish and Leslie (1965) formula using a 5 % margin of error, the prevalence of hrHPV among women living with HIV of 34.3 % (Nakigozi et al., 2024), and a z value of 1.96.

Demographic data collection

Demographic data were collected using a pretested questionnaire, and administered by a research assistant who was a qualified and experienced midwife stationed at the ART clinic. The data included variables such as age, residential region, religion, family planning practices and methods, educational attainment, marital status, history of blood pressure, history of diabetes, most recent Pap smear results, HIV viral load, duration on ART, occupation, tribe, age at sexual debut, knowledge on HPV among others. This data collection process followed the standard care procedures at the ART clinic and provision of written informed consent.

Collecting of cervical specimens

A well trained and experienced midwife collected the cervical specimens following standard operating procedures. The speculum was inserted into the vagina to expose the cervix and an endocervical brush was inserted, and rotated 360 degrees, after which it was withdrawn and inserted into a PreservCyt solution. This specimen was left to stand at room temperature until several specimens were transported to the laboratory and processed together as a batch.

HPV DNA testing

HPV DNA testing was performed using the GeneXpert HPV Assay, a qualitative in-vitro test for specific HPV genotypes. This test is based on the principle of real time polymerase chain reaction and detects the E6/E7 region of the viral DNA genome from 14 hrHPV types in a single analysis. The Cepheid GeneXpert instrument is a closed system, with automated sample processing, cell lysis, purification, nucleic acid amplification, and target sequence detection in clinical samples. This system uses real-time monitoring to ensure the specimen contains an adequate number of human cells for a qualitative assessment of HPV status. Probe Check Control (PCC) verifies reagent rehydration, PCR tube filling in the cartridge, probe integrity, and dye stability. Primers and probes for the detection of particular genotypes or pooled results are found in six color channels: “SAC primary” for sample adequacy control, “HPV 16 primary,” “HPV 18/45 primary” for pooled results, “P3 primary” for pooled results of HPV types 31, 33, 35, 52, or 58, “P4 primary” for pooled results of HPV types 51 or 59, and “P5 primary” for pooled results of HPV types 39, 56, 66, or 68.

Results Interpretation

For a hrHPV positive result, the designated hrHPV DNA shows a Ct (Cycle Threshold) within the accepted range, the fluorescence endpoint is above the predetermined threshold, the SAC is not applicable. Since HPV target amplification may impede this control, SAC is ignored and PCC has to pass. All probe check outcomes must pass.

For a HPV 16 positive result, the specific HPV 16 DNA exhibits a Ct value within the permissible range and the fluorescence endpoint surpasses the threshold setting. And the SAC is not applicable. The SAC is disregarded due to the possibility of interference from HPV target amplification and the PCC has to pass. Every probe check result has to be satisfactory.

For a HPV 18/45 positive result, the Ct value of HPV 18/45 DNA has to be within the permissible range, and the fluorescence endpoint is higher than the threshold. The SAC is not applicable. Because of possible interference from HPV target amplification, the SAC is ignored and the PCC has to pass. All probe check results must be successful.

For a positive result for “Other hrHPV”, the particular DNA of HPV 31, 33, 35, 39, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 or 68 exhibits a Ct value that falls within the acceptable range and a fluorescence endpoint that surpasses the specified threshold and the SAC is not applicable. Since HPV target amplification can occur without this control, the SAC is ignored. PCC has to pass and all probe check results must be within the acceptable criteria.

For a negative result, the Ct value of the targeted hrHPV DNA is outside the acceptable range, and the fluorescence endpoint is below the designated threshold. The SAC has to pass. When the SAC target is amplified using PCR, the fluorescent endpoint surpasses the threshold and the Ct value falls within the permissible range. The PCC also has to pass and every probe check results has to meet the necessary standards.

Data management and analysis

Following initial entry into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office Professional Plus 2013, version 15.0.4675.1003, Microsoft Inc, USA) we imported the data into STATA 17 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, United States). We used descriptive statistical measurements to describe the population using frequencies, means ± standard deviations (SDs) or median values for continuous variables. We also used frequencies and proportions to describe categorical variables. We used chi-square or t-tests to evaluate the relationships between variables. We also employed logistic regression (bivariate and multivariate) to derive associations between different variables and HPV positivity. The findings were displayed in tables as frequencies and percentages. The prevalence was presented as a percentage with the respective confidence interval. Associations between variables were presented as odds ratios and 95 % confidence intervals and P-values of <0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

We recruited 346 participants with a mean age of 37.1 years and most were aged 31–40 years (41.3 %). Majority belonged to the catholic religious denomination (53.2 %), were Acholi (85.3 %) by tribe, and had attained primary (42.2 %) or secondary education (37.3 %). Over half of the study participants were peasant farmers (55.5 %), and 60.7 % were married. All the participants were on the TLD (Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, Lamivudine and Dolutegravir) ART regimen while 67.1 % had been on ART for over 61 months. About 47.1 % reported to have ever been tested for HPV and 49.4 % reported a history of other STIs. HPV vaccination was reported in very few (1.7 %) participants. Most participants report to have had 2–3 lifetime sexual partners (59.2 %) while 74.3 % reported to have had their sexual debut between 16–19 years as detailed in Table 1.

Prevalence of HPV across different participant demographics

HPV prevalence did not significantly vary by age, education, religion, tribe, sexual behavior, or HPV vaccination status. However, occupation showed a significant distribution (p = 0.016), with unemployed individuals having a higher HPV positivity (30/103, 39.5 %). Additionally, unmarried participants and those with a shorter duration on ART showed higher HPV prevalence, though not significantly differing. A slightly higher proportion of HPV-positive individuals reported a history of other STIs and early sexual debut, but these distributions did not significant differ as shown in Table 2.

Factors associated with HPV

In multivariate analysis, being professionally employed was significantly associated with reduced odds of hrHPV infection (aOR = 0.293, 95 % CI: 0.092–0.931, p = 0.037). Although not statistically significant, individuals who were unemployed showed higher odds of hrHPV infection (aOR = 1.779, p = 0.086). Also longer ART duration (61 ≤ months) was associated with reduced odds (aOR = 0.512, p = 0.074) of hrHPV infection.

Infrequent alcohol consumption appeared associated with increased odds of hrHPV infection, particularly among non-drinkers (aOR = 5.193, p = 0.059) and rare drinkers (aOR = 5.321, p = 0.058), though these associations were borderline. Other variables, including age at sexual debut, number of sexual partners, HPV vaccination, STI history, and marital status, were not significantly associated with hrHPV infection as shown in Table 6.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to determine the prevalence of hrHPV genotypes and associated factors among women living with HIV at an ART clinic in Northern Uganda. We observed an overall prevalence of hrHPV to be 29.5 %. This prevalence was comparably high as seen in a similar study carried out in another HIV population by Nakigozi et al (Nakigozi et al., 2024) where the prevalence was 34.3 %. However, another study in another population in Mukono district, central Uganda, among women aged 25–49 years and receiving ART, the prevalence was as high as 39.8 % (Nantale et al., 2023). There was also a lower prevalence of hrHPV (16.67 %) among women of reproductive age at a rural teaching hospital in Western Uganda (Nang et al., 2023). It is important to note that this study was conducted among HIV positive population, and that HIV itself is a risk factor for HPV infection and its persistence (Bowden et al., 2023). This difference could be attributed to differences to the number life time sexual partners as reported in our study. A good fraction of our study population reported to have had 2–3 life time sexual partners which increases their chances of acquisition of hrHPV infection.

We observed increased proportions of HPV16 and HPV 18/45 among individuals below 30 years, those who had not received HPV vaccine and those who reported 2 to 3 lifetime sexual partners. The same observation was made among those who were married, those who had attained a maximum of secondary education as well as who had spent 0–12 months and 61 ≤ months on ART. There have been other observations in disagreement with ours concerning proportions of HPV16 and HPV 18/45. For instance, Liang et al (Liang et al., 2024) reported a population based prevalence of hrHPV of 9.8 % in the age group of 30–34 years and a prevalence of 3.6 % among those aged 60 years and above. However, there have also been previous studies that reported increased proportions of HPV16 and HPV 18/45 in individuals above 30 years of age (Klug et al., 2007, Luyten et al., 2009, Cuzick et al., 1999, Dareng et al., 2019, Torres-Poveda et al., 2019).

Generally, we present results of an increased proportion of “other hrHPV” genotypes among the study population. This reflects the fact that none of these genotypes are covered under the current HPV vaccination programs with the available vaccines. Also, the fact that the study population was generally young and moreover HIV positive, several HPV genotypes have a high likelihood to persist. In addition, the GeneXpert assay, as used in this study, groups eleven (Bingi et al., 2018) hrHPV genotypes under the ‘other hrHPV’ category, which potentially inflates their relative contribution.

We also report an increased prevalence of “other hrHPV” genotypes among participants aged 31–40 years, those who reported early sexual debut and those with history of other STIs. Studies from several European countries equally reported increased prevalence of hrHPV genotypes in this category (Luyten et al., 2009, Kjær et al., 2008, Coupé et al., 2008, Forslund et al., 2002). However, there are studies in Africa that have shown increased prevalence of HPV in women that are as young as 20 years and below (Cosmas et al., 2022). Earlier global estimates also indicated that the HPV infection prevalence was highest among women below 25 years of age (Smith et al., 2008) which reflects the average age of sexual debut (Weaver et al., 2011, Wellings et al., 2006). Studies in South Africa and Tanzania have reported much higher prevalence estimates among girls aged 16 to 22 years (66.7 % and 32.5 % respectively) (Mbulawa et al., 2018, Baisley et al., 2020). Several other studies have reported lower HPV prevalence estimates among virgin girls in Tanzania (Houlihan et al., 2016), young girls in India with varying socio-sexual lifestyles (Sharma et al., 2015) as well as young women in Burkina Faso (Traore et al., 2016). These differences may be attributed to differences in the prevalence of HIV and sexual behavior of the participants which increases their exposure to HPV (Peltzer and Pengpid, 2016).

We take note of the fact that almost all participants in this study were unvaccinated against hrHPV. Moreover, current vaccination efforts target young girls of 10 years and below (WHO, 2013). Also, early sexual debut has already been fronted as a factor that increases chances of acquiring the HPV infection (Weaver et al., 2011, Peltzer and Pengpid, 2016). A good number of participants in this study were observed to have a history of other sexually transmitted diseases, which could be responsible for increased prevalence of hrHPV. Some of these, including chlamydia have been shown to increase ones chances of HPV infection (Ssedyabane et al., 2019).

The observation of an increased prevalence of hrHPV among young participants unlike the old could be explained by higher rates of new sexual partnerships, greater biological susceptibility of the cervical epithelium in younger individuals and lack of prior immunity. It has been suggested earlier that early sexual debut exposes women to hrHPV (Bosch et al., 2002, Weaver et al., 2011, Peltzer and Pengpid, 2016, Winer et al., 2003, Durowade et al., 2017, Krüger-Kjær et al., 1998), moreover when their immune systems are immature hence increasing the chances of hrHPV infections and persistence (Baisley et al., 2020). Moreover, young people in Africa tend to engage in risky sexual behavior including multiple high risk sexual partners, and young age at sexual debut (Tchouaket et al., 2023, Delam et al., 2020).

The observation of a reduced prevalence as age advances is consistent with previous studies (Coupé et al., 2008, Bruni et al., 2010, De Vuyst et al., 2009, Jacobs et al., 2000). The possible explanation for this observation could be a likely reduction in the number of sexual partners and general sexual activity (Haversath et al., 2017, Hansen et al., 2020) as age increases, which reduces chances of HPV infection regardless of their vaccination status.

Unlike a shorter duration on ART, this study revealed that a longer ART duration (>= 61), as well as having a professional employment decreased study participants' risk of hrHPV infection. Studies conducted in Botswana and Kenya concurred that longer ART duration was linked to a lower chance of HPV infection in HIV-positive women (Kelly et al., 2017, Ermel et al., 2021). During HIV infection, and prior to ART initiation, or even early stages on ART, there is persistent chronic inflammation, antiretroviral toxicity and general cellular dysregulation and active HIV viral replication (Deeks et al., 2013) which increases chances of HPV infections. ART initiation and continued use over a long time can indeed reduce the likelihood of HPV infection since it boosts immune function and may aid the body to more efficiently clear HPV infection (de Vries and Steenbergen, 2018, Rositch et al., 2013). In fact several authors have gone ahead to recommend ART for immune reconstitution and viral suppression (Liu et al., 2018). Moreover, HPV clearance, as one advances on ART, is favored by an improved immunological and virological status, which further underscores the importance of ART in fighting infections including HPV (Beachler et al., 2012).

There was also an observed increase in hrHPV infection risk among infrequent alcohol consumers and those who reported to be unemployed, though not statistically significant. This has been equally reported by previous studies (Bowden et al., 2023, Schabath et al., 2015). Alcohol consumption has long been reported in previous studies as risk factor of HPV infection, which precedes cervical cancer (Ho et al., 1998, Goodman et al., 2008). In other populations in other parts of the world, alcohol consumption has been demonstrated to be associated with having multiple HPV genotypes not only in men (Nielsen et al., 2013) but also young women (Ho et al., 1998, Burkett et al., 1992, Bairati et al., 1994). There have also been previous studies that demonstrated no associations between alcohol consumption and HPV infection (Sikström et al., 1995, Tolstrup et al., 2006). Alcohol could be increasing one’s chances of HPV infection through other mechanisms. For instance, alcohol has been said to be a non-independent factor for HPV infection, since it is believed to be associated with risky sexual behavior in many populations (Olusanya et al., 2020).

Alcohol consumption is a very common practice among people of lower socioeconomic status and those who are unemployed (Crum et al., 1992, Thun et al., 1997). Alcohol is known to induce iron deficiency, hence blocking it’s would be protective contribution towards cervical cancer (Sanjoaquin et al., 2005). Therefore, through several mechanisms, iron deficiency can cause DNA hypomethylation (Baraona et al., 2001), an increase in which has been demonstrated to facilitate HPV-related cervical carcinogenesis (Powers, 2005, Ferguson et al.,1998). In addition, alcohol can activate cytochrome P450 2E1 leading to production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Pöschl et al., 2004) which themselves are associated with carcinogenesis (De Marco, 2013).

Alcohol, no matter the consumption level, is further considered to have a modulator effect on the functioning of one’s immune system, which often results in immune deficiencies, which eventually lead to increased susceptibility to infectious diseases (Dunne, 1989, Szabo, 1997, Cook, 1998, Diaz et al., 2002, Molina et al., 2010, Nelson and Kolls, 2002). Also, alcohol interferes with inflammatory and other specific immune responses to pathogens (Szabo, 1997).

Some of the limitations of this study include the fact that cross sectional design does not provide concrete evidence on statistical associations. We relied on logistic regression after controlling for other confounders to derive the associations provided in this study. Therefore, the associations presented here are not easily extrapolatable to the general population. We also did not explore statistical significance in differences between women positive for HPV 16/18/45 and those positive for other high-risk HPV genotypes. However, one of the strengths of this study is statistical power, which we considered while computing the sample size. We also employed standard collection, processing, analysis, and reporting methods for HPV DNA from cervical specimens.

Conclusion

Women on ART at Gulu RRH, Gulu district, had an elevated prevalence of hrHPV especially those HPV genotypes categorized as “Other hrHPV”. Unemployment was significantly associated with increased odds of hrHPV among the study participants. Given the slowly growing number of vaccinated women and the fact that screening recommendations are changing towards hrHPV testing, it is important to note that a considerable fraction of existing HPV genotypes are not covered by the existing vaccines. This calls for extended HPV genotyping during screening and customization of HPV vaccination to cover all HPV genotypes. Prospective studies are recommended to further unpack the category of “other hrHPV” and describe individual HPV genotypes and their distribution.
