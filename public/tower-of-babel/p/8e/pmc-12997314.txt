Comparable outcomes of HSCT up-front and after failure of IST in pediatric aplastic anemia in recent years
Authors: Felicia Andresen, Ayami Yoshimi, Peter Bader, Marc Bierings, Ivana Bod’ová, Victoria Bordon, Jochen Buechner, Birgit Burkhardt, Michael Dworzak, Miriam Erlacher, Krisztián Kállay, Veerle Labarque, Franco Locatelli, Andrea Malone, Tania Masmas, Ingo Müller, Peter Nöllke, Markus Schmugge, Katarzyna Pawelec, Petr Sedlacek, Julian Sevilla, Owen Smith, Jan Stary, Charlotte Niemeyer, Brigitte Strahm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Up-front HSCT from an MSD or a ≥9/10 HLA-MUD both achieved an excellent OS of 95%.•OS after HSCT after IST failure was inferior compared with up-front HSCT (83% vs 95%) but has improved over time (89% since 2015).

Introduction

Severe aplastic anemia (SAA) is a rare hematological disorder characterized by severe pancytopenia and markedly hypocellular bone marrow (BM) without significant dysplasia. Most cases of acquired SAA are thought to be caused by immune-mediated processes leading to the rapid destruction of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells.1 The main treatment options for patients with SAA include allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and immunosuppressive therapy (IST). HSCT is the preferred first-line treatment for children and adolescents with SAA with an available HLA-matched sibling donor (MSD), resulting in an excellent overall survival (OS) of >90% and event-free survival (EFS) of >85%.2,3 However, many patients lack a suitable MSD and generally receive IST consisting of antithymocyte globulin (ATG) and cyclosporine A with an overall response rate and OS of ∼50% to 70% and 90%, respectively.2,4, 5, 6, 7 Despite comparable OS of IST and MSD-HSCT, patients receiving IST are at risk for nonresponse, disease reoccurrence, and clonal evolution. Accordingly, EFS is significantly lower after IST than after MSD-HSCT.2,3,5 Patients failing IST have an indication for a second-line HSCT from an HLA-matched unrelated donor (UD; MUD), which has historically resulted in a variable OS and EFS, mostly attributed to graft failure (GF), graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and infections.3,8 More recent studies have reported improved outcomes of HSCT using an MUD or haploidentical donor, highlighting the potential role of alternative donor transplants in the treatment of SAA.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Here, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the outcomes of allogeneic HSCT in a consecutive cohort of 179 children and adolescents with SAA registered in the European Working Group of SAA in Childhood (EWOG-SAA) 2010 registry. Our analysis aims to compare the outcomes of patients with SAA who received a transplant up front and transplantation after failure of IST, thereby contributing to the optimization of treatment for pediatric SAA.

Methods

Study population

The EWOG-SAA 2010 registry (DRKS00000610) included 291 children and adolescents with SAA (age ≤18 years). The EWOG-SAA registry protocol provided recommendations for the standard of care. Of the 291 patients, 2 patients did not receive any treatment, and 14 patients did not have treatment data available. For the remaining 275 patients eligible for analysis, IST and HSCT were given as first-line treatment to 185 and 90 patients, respectively. Details on the response and outcome of 150 of the 185 patients receiving IST have been published previously.7 Of 185 patients who received IST, 94 (51%) received transplantation after IST failure. Five patients were excluded from the analysis: 1 patient with a fatal event during conditioning, and 4 patients with insufficient HSCT data. The final study cohort included 179 patients who received an allogeneic HSCT up front (n = 87) or after failure of IST (n = 92) between July 2011 and January 2022 (supplemental Figure 1). Data on patient characteristics, treatment before HSCT, HSCT procedure, and outcomes were prospectively collected and extracted from the electronic database of the EWOG-SAA registry. The registry was approved by the institutional review board of each participating institution. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians and patients, if applicable, according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnosis of AA

SAA was diagnosed in patients presenting with pancytopenia and hypoplastic BM without dysplasia, as described previously.13,14 The workup included BM aspirate and biopsy, cytogenetic analysis including metaphase cytogenetics and fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis of at least chromosomes 7 and 8. Cytogenetic analyses were performed according to standard procedures and described according to the International System for Human Cytogenetics Nomenclature.15 BM biopsy samples were assessed by a reference pathologist to exclude refractory cytopenia of childhood.13 Fanconi anemia was excluded in all patients by chromosomal breakage testing. Screening for other inherited BM failure syndromes was performed as clinically indicated. SAA was defined by a BM cellularity of <25% and decreased peripheral blood counts in at least 2 lineages (that is, absolute neutrophil count [ANC] of <0.5 × 109/L, platelet count of <20 × 109/L, and/or absolute reticulocyte count of <60 × 109/L), whereas very severe AA (VSAA) had to have an ANC of <0.2 × 109/L. Nonsevere AA (NSAA) was characterized by cytopenias not fulfilling the definition of SAA.14 Criteria for assessment of response to IST were defined as previously described.7

Definitions and statistical analysis

Primary end points were engraftment, cumulative incidence of acute GVHD (aGVHD), chronic GVHD (cGVHD), and GF, OS, and GVHD-free, relapse/rejection-free survival (GRFS). Neutrophil and platelet engraftment were defined as the first of 3 consecutive days with an ANC of >0.5 × 109/L and the first 7 days with a platelet count of >20 × 109/L without transfusion support, respectively. Primary GF was defined as absent hematopoietic recovery by day 42, and secondary GF as a decline in hematopoietic function requiring transfusion or granulocyte growth factor support after previous engraftment.16 aGVHD and cGVHD were diagnosed using established criteria.17 Patients with primary engraftment were considered at risk for aGVHD. Patients at risk for cGVHD had to show primary engraftment without any competing post-HSCT events before day 100. Pulmonary complications were defined as the presence of radiologic changes, oxygen support, or mechanical ventilation. Renal complications included creatinine elevation of grade ≥3 per common terminology criteria for adverse events, Fanconi syndrome, nephrotic syndrome, or hemodialysis or hemofiltration. Clonal evolution was defined as the presence of cytogenetic abnormalities in the absence of overt dysplasia or increased blast counts.18 OS was defined as the time between HSCT and death or last follow-up. GRFS was defined as the time from HSCT to death, primary or secondary GF, grade 3/4 aGVHD, moderate/severe cGVHD, or last follow-up. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test, and continuous variables using the Mann-Whitney test. Survival rates were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method, expressed as estimated probability with 95% confidence interval (CI), and compared using the log-rank test. Time-to-event outcomes, including aGVHD and cGVHD, were assessed with cumulative incidence curves and compared using the Gray test. Multivariate analysis for GRFS was performed using a cause-specific Cox model, including variables with P values < .15 in univariate analysis. P values were 2-sided, with significance set at < .05. P values >.1 were reported as not significant, whereas those between .05 and .1 were reported in detail. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 28.0.0 (IBM Corp) and NCSS 2004 (NCSS, Kaysville, UT).

Results

Patient and transplant characteristics

Over half of patients initially presented with VSAA (55%), whereas SAA and NSAA were diagnosed in 25% and 20% of cases, respectively. Of 35 patients with NSAA at diagnosis, 27 and 6 patients progressed to VSAA and SAA before treatment initiation, respectively. Two patients still had NSAA before commencing IST. The median interval between diagnosis and HSCT was 4.6 months (range, 0.7-54.9) and the median age at HSCT was 10.3 years (range, 0.9-18.7). Except for the sex distribution, patient characteristics were comparable among patients who received a transplant up front or those who received a transplant after failure of IST (Table 1). In contrast, there were significant differences in donor characteristics and conditioning regimens in both groups (Table 2). Most up-front HSCT procedures used an MSD (n = 61 [70%]) and the preferred conditioning regimen was cyclophosphamide (Cy) with or without ATG (n = 52 [60%]). In contrast, most patients in the post–IST failure group received a transplant from a UD (n = 81 [88%]), and fludarabine (Flu) and Cy with any type of serotherapy was the most common conditioning regimen (n = 70 [76%]). A haploidentical donor was used in 12 patients (7%; up-front, n = 3; after IST failure, n = 9). As recommended, BM was used in 82% of patients in the whole cohort, with a higher proportion of peripheral blood stem cells (PBSCs) HSCTs in the post–IST failure group (n = 22 [24%]). All other transplantation characteristics were comparable between the groups. In the post–IST failure group, 79 patients received a transplant due to nonresponse to IST (35 patients before day 180, 44 patients after day 180) and 10 patients had experienced relapse before HSCT. Additionally, 3 patients underwent HSCT due to clonal evolution with cytogenetic aberrations (monosomy 7, chromosome 7p deletion, and trisomy 8) without increase in BM blasts. Engraftment and GF

Initial engraftment was achieved in 168 patients (94%). Ten patients experienced primary GF (6%) and 1 patient died without engraftment on day 4 after HSCT (supplemental Table 1). Secondary GF was diagnosed in 7 patients (4%) at a median time of 107 days after HSCT (range, 61-684). Two patients had an early secondary GF classified as rejection, whereas the remaining patients had a declining graft function in the presence of a complete donor chimerism (n = 1) or mixed chimerism with predominantly allogeneic cells (n = 4; 80%-94% donor cells). Fourteen patients experiencing primary or secondary GF were alive at last follow-up after an unconditioned stem cell boost (n = 1) or a second allograft (n = 13), whereas 3 patients died before (n = 2) or after a second HSCT (n = 1). The 5-year cumulative incidence of any type of GF was 10% (95% CI, 6-15), with no difference for patients after up-front HSCT or HSCT after IST failure (up-front, 11% [95% CI, 6-20] vs after IST failure, 9% [95% CI, 4-17]; P value not significant; Figure 1A; supplemental Table 2). The use of a haploidentical donor significantly increased the risk of any type of GF in up-front and post–IST failure HSCT groups (Figure 1B; supplemental Table 2). GVHD

Of 169 patients at risk, 31 developed grade 2 to 4 aGVHD (18%), with 6 patients having grade 3 to 4 aGVHD (4%; supplemental Table 2). The cumulative incidence of grade 2 to 4 and grade 3 to 4 aGVHD was 18% (95% CI, 13-25) and 4% (95% CI, 2-8), respectively (Figure 1C). Of note, the probability of grade 2 to 4 aGVHD was not significantly influenced by the time point of HSCT, recipient age, stem cell source, total nucleated cell dose, donor type, conditioning regimen, type of serotherapy, or sex compatibility (supplemental Table 2). Of 162 patients at risk, 10 developed cGVHD (6%), with 5 individuals scoring mild and 5 scoring moderate/severe. The cumulative incidence of any type and moderate/severe cGVHD was 8% (95% CI, 4-15) and 3% (95% CI, 1-7), respectively (Figure 1D). Lastly, the probability of cGVHD did not differ between the up-front and post–IST failure group (up-front, 6% [95% CI, 3-14] vs after IST failure, 10% [95% CI, 4-24]; P value not significant).

OS and GRFS

With a median follow-up of 4.0 years (range, 0.2-10.7) after HSCT, 161 patients were alive at last follow-up. One patient was successfully treated for Hodgkin lymphoma that was diagnosed 6 years after HSCT. The cause of death was infection (n = 9), posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder (n = 3), thrombotic microangiopathy (n = 2), aGVHD (n = 2), and cGVHD (n = 2). For the whole cohort, the 5-year OS was 89% (95% CI, 84-94) (Figure 2A; Table 3). Among children who received a transplant up front, the OS among 61 patients who received a transplant from an MSD was superimposable to that of the 21 patients who received a transplant from a ≥9/10 HLA-MUD or matched family donor (MFD) other than an MSD (MSD, 95% [95% CI, 88-100] vs MUD/MFD, 95% [95% CI, 86-100]; Figure 2B). There was a significant difference in OS among patients who received a transplant up front and those who received a transplant after failure of IST (95% [95% CI, 89-100] vs 83% [95% CI, 75-91], P < .01). This difference remained significant for procedures performed before 2015 (up-front, 96% [95% CI, 87-100] vs after IST failure, 71% [95% CI, 54-88]; P = .01; Figure 2C). However, outcomes of patients who received a transplant after failure of IST improved over time and from 2015 onward there was no significant difference in OS between both groups (up-front, 97% [95% CI, 92-100] vs after IST failure, 89% [95% CI, 81-97]; P = .09; Figure 2D). In the post–IST failure group, OS was significantly better in patients who received a transplant at a younger age (<10 years, 95% [95% CI, 88-100] vs ≥10 years, 74% [95% CI, 61-87]; P < .01), and after failure of IST with Atgam (Atgam, 90% [95% CI, 82-98] vs Thymoglobulin, 65% [95% CI, 45-85]; P = .01). Although not statistically significant, the probability of OS improved steadily over time in the post–IST failure group (<2015, 71% [95% CI, 54-88] vs 2015-2017, 88% [95% CI, 77-99] vs ≥2018, 90% [95% CI, 79-100]; P value not significant). Of note, 15 of 28 patients who received a transplant before 2015 received IST with Thymoglobulin (54%), whereas Atgam was used in IST in most patients who received a transplant from 2015 onward (n = 55/64 [95% CI, 86%]). Furthermore, there was a gradual, although not significant decrease in OS associated with donor matching (MUD, 88% [95% CI, 78-98] vs 9/10 UD, 79% [95% CI, 61-97] vs other UD, 65% [95% CI, 40-90]; P = .08). The 5-year GRFS was 79% ([95% CI, 73-85]; Figure 2A), with no statistically significant difference between patients who received a transplant up front and after IST failure (up-front, 84% [95% CI, 76-92] vs after IST failure, 75% [95% CI, 66-84]; P value not significant; Table 4). In the up-front HSCT group, none of the assessed factors affected GRFS. In particular, GRFS of HSCT from an MSD and MUD was comparable (MSD, 83% [95% CI, 73-93] vs MUD, 83% [95% CI, 65-100]; P value not significant). We also performed a multivariate analysis of factors affecting GRFS in the post–IST failure HSCT group. The year of HSCT significantly affected GRFS (<2015 vs ≥2018: relative risk (RR), 3.57 [P = .08]; 2015-2017 vs ≥2018: RR, 4.61 [P = .02]). Impact of the conditioning regimen and ATG on GRFS in MSD-HSCT

Up-front MSD-HSCT after conditioning with high-dose Cy and ATG has long been the first-line treatment of choice in pediatric SAA. To study the impact of conditioning regimens on OS and GRFS in MSD-HSCT, we assessed a group of 61 patients who received an up-front transplant from an MSD. Patients received a conditioning regimen using Cy only (n = 20); Cy and ATG (n = 31); or Flu, Cy, and ATG (n = 10). OS was comparable between all 3 groups (Cy only, 94% [95% CI, 83-100] vs Cy/ATG, 97% [95% CI, 91-100] vs Flu/Cy/ATG, 100%; P value not significant). Furthermore, there were comparable incidences of GF, high-grade aGVHD, and cGVHD among patients conditioned with Cy only or Cy/ATG, whereas none of the patients conditioned with Flu/Cy/ATG developed high-grade aGVHD or cGVHD. GRFS was comparable for the 3 conditioning regimens (Cy only, 80% [95% CI, 62-98] vs Cy/ATG, 84% [95% CI, 70-98] vs Flu/Cy/ATG, 90% [95% CI, 71-100]; P value not significant; supplemental Figure 2).

Up-front vs post–IST failure MUD-HSCT

In the absence of an MSD, IST has long been the preferred treatment in pediatric SAA, but improved outcomes of UD HSCT have challenged this algorithm. We aimed to compare the outcomes of up-front and post–IST failure MUD-HSCT in our cohort. To reduce potential bias from Thymoglobulin use in IST, only patients who received a transplant after 2015, who had received Atgam in IST, were included in the analysis. Patients were included if they received a transplant from an MUD or MFD (≥9/10 HLA matching) with an unmanipulated BM or PBSC graft after a Flu/Cy conditioning regimen combined with serotherapy. This included 13 patients who received a transplant up front and 26 patients who received a transplant after IST failure. There was no significant difference in OS of patients who received a transplant up front and after IST failure (up-front, 92% [95% CI, 76-100] vs after IST failure, 84% [95% CI, 69-99]; P value not significant). GRFS was also comparable in both groups (up-front, 77% [95% CI, 54-100] vs after IST failure, 76% [95% CI, 59-93]; P value not significant; supplemental Figure 3). Pulmonary complications were reported in 2 patients after up-front HSCT (15%), with 1 patient passing away due to grade 4 aGVHD and pulmonary complications. In contrast, 8 patients in the post–IST failure HSCT group had pulmonary complications (31%; P value not significant), of whom 4 patients passed away, due to GF and pulmonary aspergillosis (n = 1), posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder (n = 1), thrombotic microangiopathy (n = 1), and acute respiratory distress syndrome and multiorgan failure (n = 1). Renal complications were rare after up-front HSCT (n = 1, 8%) but were reported in 4 patients after HSCT after IST failure (15%; P value not significant). Lastly, the percentage of patients experiencing viral infections was comparable between both groups (up-front, n = 6 [46%] vs after IST failure, n = 13 [50%]; P value not significant).

Discussion

We report the outcomes of a consecutive multicenter cohort of 179 patients with SAA in the EWOG-SAA 2010 registry that received an allogeneic HSCT up front or after failure of IST. Although this is a relatively large international pediatric cohort, the rarity of events in some subgroups and the retrospective nature of this study limit the ability to draw definitive conclusions. HSCT resulted in a 5-year OS of 89% in the whole cohort. Although the excellent outcomes of up-front HSCT using an MSD have been reported by several groups,2,3 it is remarkable that the OS of patients who received a transplant up front from an MUD/MFD with a ≥9/10 HLA match was comparable with that of the MSD-HSCT group in our cohort. Although the OS was clearly superior in patients who received a transplant up front compared with those who received a transplant after IST failure in the overall cohort (up-front, 95% vs after IST failure, 83%; P ≤ .01), improvements in the post–IST failure group over time reduced the previously significant impact of IST failure on OS from 2015 onward (≥2015 up-front, 97% vs after IST failure, 89%; P = .09). Additionally, younger age at HSCT and the use of a well-matched UD were associated with improved OS in the post–IST failure HSCT group. Moreover, IST with Atgam resulted in a better OS and GRFS compared with IST with Thymoglobulin. Although the use of horse ATG in IST has repeatedly been shown to result in superior outcomes and response rate of IST compared with rabbit ATG,6,7,19,20 little is known about its impact on transplant outcomes. In the IST setting, rabbit ATG has been shown to exhibit a longer-lasting and more potent immunosuppressive effect compared with horse ATG, resulting in a higher incidence of infectious complications.19,21,22 These effects might have contributed to the inferior outcome of HSCT after failure of IST with Thymoglobulin. However, most of our patients receiving IST with Thymoglobulin underwent a transplant before 2015, which may indicate a time-dependent effect. Other factors known to negatively affect transplant outcomes in SAA, such as the use of PBSCs3,10,23, 24, 25 and positive cytomegalovirus donor/recipient status,10,26, 27, 28 were not associated with inferior outcomes in our cohort, although this observation is limited by the small sample size. The adapted composite end point, GRFS, has been established to better assess transplant outcomes in SAA.29 The 5-year GRFS of the whole cohort was 79%. In contrast to the OS analysis, there was no significant difference in GRFS comparing the up-front HSCT (84%) and post–IST failure group (75%, P value not significant) due to higher rates of cGVHD in the up-front HSCT group. Similar to OS, GRFS was comparable between patients who received a transplant up front from an MSD and MUD (MSD, 83%; MUD, 83%). GRFS improved over time in the post–IST failure group, as shown in the multivariate analysis. Notably, in our subgroup analysis of MUD-HSCTs performed after 2015, OS and GRFS were comparable between up-front (OS, 92%; GRFS, 77%) and post–IST failure procedures (OS, 84%; GRFS, 76%).

In summary, these results corroborate the previously reported good outcomes of up-front MUD-HSCT.8,11,12,30,31 However, the improved outcomes of HSCT after IST failure demonstrate that pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory SAA can be better salvaged with MUD-HSCT in recent times. Based on these observations, the use of an MUD in up-front HSCT is now also a viable option, with OS and GRFS comparable with those achieved with MSD-HSCT. However, identification of a suitable MUD may delay treatment initiation, which is associated with a potential risk of severe complications. Accordingly, we suggest that up-front MUD-HSCT should only be considered if a suitable donor can be identified promptly (eg, within 2-3 months after SAA diagnosis). In contrast, IST is immediately available, and we recently reported an overall response rate of IST at day 180 of 22% and 42% among 150 patients receiving rabbit and horse ATG, respectively. OS was excellent among responders at day 180 of IST (100%). In nonresponders, only 1 fatal event occurred before HSCT. Additionally, nonresponders with an ANC of ≥0.2 × 109/L at day 90 had an excellent OS after HSCT compared with patients with an ANC of <0.2 × 109/L at day 90 (nonresponse with ANC of ≥0.2 × 109/L, 95% vs nonresponse with ANC of <0.2 × 109/L, 75%; P < .01), highlighting the role of early second-line HSCT in patients with persistent very severe neutropenia.7 Therefore, IST remains an important first-line treatment option that can be initiated immediately and spares responding patients from toxicities and late effects associated with transplantation, whereas patients who fail IST and subsequently undergo HSCT still achieve good OS and GRFS comparable, although not equivalent, with outcomes after up-front HSCT. Alternatively, up-front HSCT from a well-matched UD can be considered. A multicenter, randomized phase 3 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05600426) comparing up-front MUD-HSCT and IST in patients with newly diagnosed SAA will hopefully reveal more data to contribute to better guidance.32 Furthermore, HSCT from a haploidentical donor has also emerged as a potential up-front treatment strategy for SAA, with improved outcomes reported by some more recent studies.12,31,33 Our cohort included only 12 patients who received a transplant from a haploidentical donor with an excellent probability of OS (92%) but a high rate of GF, as observed in other studies. Although the use of a haploidentical donor increases the donor availability and immediate access to HSCT, larger patient cohorts, ideally in randomized multicenter studies with longer follow-up data, are needed to assess its role and to compare it with other treatment modalities.

A combination of high-dose Cy and ATG has long been the preferred conditioning regimen in MSD-HSCT.34, 35, 36 Given the toxicities of high-dose Cy, several studies combined Flu (120 mg/m2) with lower doses of Cy (30-50 mg/kg) and serotherapy in MSD and UD transplantations for SAA. These studies reported a variable OS ranging from 61% to 97%.37, 38, 39 A more recent study by Bejanyan et al reported an OS of 91% in patients receiving Cy/ATG and Flu/Cy/ATG, but a higher cumulative incidence of cGVHD with Cy/ATG compared to Flu/Cy/ATG.40 In contrast, in our subgroup analysis of 61 up-front MSD-HSCTs, the conditioning regimen (Cy only, Cy/ATG, and Flu/Cy/ATG) or the application of serotherapy (Cy only, Cy/ATG) did not significantly affect GRFS, although subgroup sizes were small. In view of these data and the expected lower long-term toxicity, the Flu/Cy/ATG regimen is an attractive alternative to the well-established Cy/ATG regimen in MSD-HSCT.

In summary, our data demonstrate favorable transplant outcomes in pediatric AA, with notable improvements both in up-front MUD-HSCT and HSCT after failure of IST. Together with the previously published outcomes of IST, these observations challenge established treatment algorithms favoring IST over HSCT in the absence of an MSD and instead call for a more elaborate treatment decision, taking additional factors such as active infections, transfusion refractoriness, and patient preferences into account. GRFS serves as a valuable composite end point by capturing GF and GVHD in addition to OS. However, long-term follow-up, including data on late effects, will be critical in determining the optimal donor, conditioning regimen, and timing of HSCT to achieve long-term hematopoietic function with minimal toxicity and excellent quality of life.
