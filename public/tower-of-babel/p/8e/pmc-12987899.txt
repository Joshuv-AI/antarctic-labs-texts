Successful Treatment of Linear Porokeratosis with Ivarmacitinib in an Adolescent: A Case Report
Authors: Zenan Tang, Yan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorokeratosis is a rare cutaneous disorder of keratinization, and its linear variant typically follows the lines of Blaschko. We report a case of a 15-year-old female patient with a 3-year history of a linear eruption on her right waist and thigh, accompanied by severe pruritus. Previous interventions, including topical corticosteroids, antihistamines, and oral prednisone, had failed to produce clinical improvement. The diagnosis of linear porokeratosis was confirmed after histopathological examination revealed the characteristic cornoid lamella. The patient was prescribed ivarmacitinib at a dose of 4 mg once daily. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported a significant reduction in pruritus, and clinical examination showed marked flattening of the papules, with no adverse events reported. Ivarmacitinib is a highly selective JAK1 inhibitor, and JAK1 plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases. Ivarmacitinib was successfully utilized for the first time in the treatment of an adolescent patient with linear porokeratosis. Although the precise mechanism by which ivarmacitinib addresses linear porokeratosis has yet to be definitively elucidated, this case provides clinicians with a novel perspective and an additional option regarding pharmacological selection and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: porokeratosis, ivarmacitinib, JAK inhibitors

Introduction

Porokeratosis is a rare disorder of keratinization.1 It encompasses several clinical variants, including disseminated superficial actinic porokeratosis (DSAP), porokeratosis of Mibelli, palmoplantar porokeratosis, and linear porokeratosis.2 Linear porokeratosis is a rare variant that typically follows the lines of Blaschko and can present at birth or develop later in adulthood.3 The treatment of linear porokeratosis has historically been challenging. Conventional therapies including topical corticosteroids, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), cryotherapy, and oral retinoids have demonstrated inconsistent efficacy, often being limited by adverse effects or high recurrence rates upon discontinuation.1 Recent advances in the understanding of its pathogenesis have led to more targeted approaches, such as the combination of topical cholesterol and statins (eg, lovastatin), aimed at correcting defects in the mevalonate pathway.4 However, the management of pruritus remains difficult and is often refractory to traditional antipruritic treatments, including antihistamines.5 Ivarmacitinib is a highly selective oral JAK1 inhibitor that has demonstrated efficacy and safety in Phase III clinical trials for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis and alopecia areata.6,7 Its superior selectivity for JAK1 over other JAK family members is expected to minimize off-target effects associated with JAK2 inhibition, such as neutropenia and anemia. Herein, we report a case of an adolescent with linear porokeratosis and severe, refractory pruritus who achieved significant improvement after treatment with ivarmacitinib. This case provides clinicians with a novel therapeutic option for managing this challenging condition.

Case Presentation

A 15-year-old female presented with a 3-year history of a linear eruption on her right waist and right thigh, with severe pruritus (VAS 8). She had been diagnosed with “eczema” or “linear epidermal nevus”. Previous interventions, including topical clobetasol propionate, various oral antihistamines, cryotherapy, and oral prednisone (30 mg once daily), failed to produce clinical improvement.

Physical examination showed linear keratotic and verrucous papules arranged in a band-like pattern on the right abdomen, lower back, upper thigh, and hip (Figure 1A–C). Histopathological examination revealed hyperkeratosis with focal parakeratosis, the presence of a cornoid lamella, acanthosis, and reticular ridges extending downward in a rod-like pattern. In dermis, focal lymphocytic infiltration was observed around small blood vessels. Based on the clinical presentation and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of linear porokeratosis was confirmed (Figure 1D). Laboratory investigations, including a complete blood count, biochemistry, hepatitis, tuberculosis, coagulation etc.were unremarkable.

(A–C) Baseline: Linear keratotic and verrucous papules on the right trunk and lower limb. (D) Histopathology: Characteristic cornoid lamella, hyperkeratosis, and focal lymphocytic infiltration. (E–G) Follow-up: Significant flattening of papules after 3 months of ivarmacitinib treatment.

The patient was prescribed Ivarmacitinib at a dose of 4 mg once daily. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported a significant reduction in pruritus (VAS 1). Clinical examination showed marked flattening of the papules. No adverse events reported. (Figure 1E–G).

Discussion

Linear porokeratosis must be differentiated from inflammatory linear verrucous epidermal nevus (ILVEN). Both conditions can present as linear papules arranged along Blaschko’s lines. ILVEN is a rare benign epidermal nevus that typically presents in infancy or early childhood, characterized by intensely pruritic, erythematous, scaly papules and plaques distributed along Blaschko’s lines.8 The characteristic histology of ILVEN shows psoriasiform acanthosis with alternating columns of parakeratosis and orthokeratosis, overlying corresponding areas of hypogranulosis and hypergranulosis, respectively.8 In the present case, although the patient presented with linear keratotic and verrucous papules along Blaschko’s lines, which overlapped clinically with ILVEN, histopathological examination was decisive. The presence of cornoid lamellae, defined as columns of parakeratosis within the stratum corneum, represents the diagnostic hallmark of porokeratosis and is not observed in ILVEN. Therefore, a definitive diagnosis of linear porokeratosis was established.

The treatment of linear porokeratosis remains challenging. Conventional therapies, including topical corticosteroids, retinoids, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), cryotherapy, and CO2 laser, have demonstrated varying degrees of efficacy.9 Specifically, 5-FU (0.5–5% cream) has shown effectiveness in some cases but often leads to intense inflammatory reactions. Topical Vitamin D derivatives and retinoids have provided modest and inconsistent results, sometimes requiring combination with cryotherapy to achieve better outcomes. Physical interventions (such as surgical excision, dermabrasion, and various laser therapies) are more suitable for localized lesions.1 Recent breakthroughs in understanding the pathogenesis of porokeratosis have introduced more targeted approaches. One significant advancement is the topical application of a cholesterol/statin combination (typically 2% lovastatin/2% cholesterol). This therapy aims to reverse the deficiency in the mevalonate pathway by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase to prevent the accumulation of cytotoxic metabolites while supplementing cholesterol to stabilize the extracellular lipid matrix and maintain keratinocyte integrity.4

The underlying mechanism of intractable pruritus in linear porokeratosis has been increasingly linked to a specific non-histaminergic cytokine signaling axis.5 In such cases, proliferated abnormal keratinocytes within the lesions are thought to secrete significant amounts of thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP). TSLP acts as a master regulator of Th2 immunity, fostering a microenvironment that promotes the dermal deposition of periostin and the upregulation of interleukin (IL)-31. Periostin further amplifies this Th2-mediated inflammation, while IL-31 serves as a potent pruritogenic cytokine that preferentially targets sensory neurons. Significantly, the itch in these patients is primarily non-histaminergic, supported by the observation that mast cell counts remain normal in lesional skin and clinical symptoms are often refractory to oral antihistamines.

Systemic treatments are increasingly targeting specific inflammatory pathways. While oral retinoids10 and cyclosporine A11 have been used to modulate epidermal differentiation and suppress inflammation, their effects are often temporary with frequent relapses upon discontinuation. Emerging biological agents, such as the IL-17A inhibitor secukinumab12 and the IL-1 inhibitor anakinra,13 have shown promise in specific recalcitrant cases. Furthermore, both omalizumab14 and dupilumab15 have been reported to achieve effective control of pruritus in patients with eruptive pruritic papular porokeratosis (EPPP). Most notably, JAK inhibitors have emerged as a highly effective option. Recent studies have demonstrated that abrocitinib16 and upadacitinib17 can achieve significant clinical efficacy in EPPP patients. To date, there has been no documented evidence regarding the application of JAK1 inhibitors specifically for the treatment of linear porokeratosis, and its use in the adolescent population remains entirely unexplored.

Ivarmacitinib is a highly selective oral JAK1 inhibitor. In vitro studies have demonstrated that its selectivity for JAK1 is 10-fold higher than for JAK2, 77-fold higher than for JAK3, and 420-fold higher than for TYK2. This high selectivity minimizes the inhibition of JAK2, thereby reducing the potential risk of neutropenia and anemia. Phase III clinical trials have already confirmed that Ivarmacitinib effectively alleviates symptoms of atopic dermatitis and alopecia areata.6,7 The successful use of Ivarmacitinib in this case provides a novel perspective for the management of porokeratosis. The exact mechanism by which ivarmacitinib improves linear porokeratosis remains unclear, and reports on the treatment of this condition are extremely limited. There have been published cases of successful treatment of generalized porokeratosis with JAK inhibitors. The pathogenesis of porokeratosis involves abnormal clonal proliferation and premature apoptosis of keratinocytes resulting from genetic mutations. Whether this process is dependent on the JAK STAT signaling pathway is also currently unknown. Based on previous case reports, it can be inferred that porokeratosis, particularly cases with prominent pruritus, may be associated with itch related inflammation.5 In our case, we observed that while the JAK inhibitor effectively controlled pruritus, the skin lesions also improved. Therefore, whether JAK inhibitors exert effects beyond their anti inflammatory properties in this context warrants further investigation.

When considering ivarmacitinib treatment for adolescent patients, safety considerations are of particular concern. Prior to treatment initiation, clinicians should perform appropriate baseline screening, including evaluation for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV infection status, as JAK inhibitors may increase susceptibility to infections.18 Active severe infections represent a contraindication for treatment initiation.18 During treatment, patients and their guardians should be informed to remain vigilant for signs of infection and to promptly report any suspicious symptoms. A Phase III clinical trial including adolescents aged 12 years and older with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis has demonstrated that ivarmacitinib significantly improves the signs and symptoms of moderate-to-severe AD in both adolescent and adult patients, showing a favorable benefit-risk profile.6 Given that experience with ivarmacitinib in the adolescent population remains limited, treatment should be initiated under the supervision of a specialist, with appropriate baseline evaluation and regular monitoring.

Conclusion

Ivarmacitinib was successfully utilized for the first time in the treatment of an adolescent patient with linear porokeratosis. Although the precise mechanism by which ivarmacitinib addresses linear porokeratosis has yet to be definitively elucidated, this case provides clinicians with a novel perspective and an additional option regarding pharmacological selection and therapeutic strategies.
