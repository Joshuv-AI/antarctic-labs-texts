Looking back to move forward: can historical clinical trial data and machine learning drive change in participant recruitment in anticipation of future value assessments?
Authors: Saswata Paul Choudhury, Abhirup Dutta Majumdar, Ronan Mahon, Sekhar Kumar Dutta, Subrata Bhattacharyya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug development is an expensive endeavor, with costs averaging $879.3 million and only 14.3% of them ultimately securing regulatory approval. One fundamental challenge is ensuring that the enrolled patient population in a clinical trial accurately reflects the broader target population’s baseline characteristics (BCx). This concern ties directly to the generalizability of study findings beyond the trial setting. If the trial population differs significantly from real-world patients in terms of BCx (e.g., age, comorbidities, disease severity), the applicability of the results to routine clinical practice may be limited. Both regulatory agencies and health technology assessment (HTA) bodies may question the generalizability and transportability of trial results to real-world settings. HTA bodies may further require robust comparative effectiveness estimates, which necessitate alignment with the patient populations of previous studies. Real-world data (RWD) can help assess and calibrate the representativeness of clinical trial populations, though limitations persist, including underrepresentation of key subgroups and missing biomarker data. Historical clinical trial data can offer valuable insights into BCx distributions and recruitment patterns, as well as enable more reliable estimates of comparative effectiveness. Machine learning (ML) can enhance this process by leveraging clustering methods to identify optimal BCx distributions, thereby improving trial generalizability. Moreover, Bayesian clustering frameworks that incorporate RWD can further refine these estimates, ensuring better alignment with real-world epidemiology. This paper proposes that, by combining insights from prior trials with RWD using ML techniques, researchers can more effectively capture population heterogeneity and design more generalizable, patient-centric recruitment strategies.Keywords: Clinical trials, Health technology assessment, Machine learning, Prior evidence

Introduction

Regulatory approval is a key milestone in drug development, while successful reimbursement and positive patient sentiment are equally crucial for ensuring market access. Demonstrating sufficient value to payers is essential for achieving reimbursement; without it, even innovative drugs may struggle to reach patients [1]. A lack of commercial viability can lead to the abandonment of potentially life-saving treatments, adversely impacting patients and caregivers. On average, developing a new drug costs approximately $879.3 million, factoring in both capital expenditures and the high attrition rates of clinical trials [2]. In fact, the likelihood of a new drug receiving regulatory approval is just 14.3% [3]. These statistics underscore the need for robust evidence generation at every stage of development.

A fundamental challenge in clinical trial design is ensuring that the distributions of baseline characteristics (BCx) of enrolled patients are reflective of the characteristics of the patients treated in routine clinical practice. Regulatory agencies and health technology assessment (HTA) bodies share these concerns, noting that in 65–97% of cases, trial populations may not represent the broader patient community [4]. This gap stems from the design of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which prioritize internal validity and often impose strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. As a result, trial populations are typically more homogeneous and may not capture the full spectrum of clinical and demographic diversity seen in routine care [5, 6]. In addition, HTA bodies rely on robust estimates of comparative effectiveness, which depend in part on the comparability of BCx across relevant trials. Ideally, the BCx in a new trial should reflect both the real-world population and the populations of prior trials to support valid comparisons and downstream decision-making.

To address these challenges proactively, it is crucial to optimize trial recruitment before trial begins, ensuring that the enrolled population is representative of target population and conducive to comparative analysis. Misalignment between the trial and target populations may cause challenges at the point of HTA. For example, the aducanumab trials raised concerns about the drug’s efficacy and the representativeness of patient subgroups. A major issue was the limited generalizability of the trial results due to discrepancies in baseline distributions, which ultimately led to a controversial Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in 2021 with significant restrictions on coverage by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services [7]. This case underscores the importance of recruiting a representative patient population.

Real-world data (RWD) can be leveraged to parameterize target BCx distributions and quantitatively assess the extent to which the trial-enrolled population represents the clinical and demographic profile of the intended treatment population. However, RWD has limitations. While analysis based on RWD can provide broader insights, databases based on private insurance claims may underrepresent patients who rely on public healthcare or lack insurance and country-specific datasets may fail to capture global diversity [8, 9]. Additionally, real-world databases often lack biomarker data relevant to a drug’s mechanism of action (e.g., PD-L1). This limitation of RWD was highlighted in a study that found only 15% of 220 US-based clinical trials published in high-impact journals could be feasibly replicated using RWD, underscoring the challenges of generalizing trial results to broader patient populations [10].

Given the limitations of relying solely on RWD and the need to support a robust comparative effectiveness analysis, it may be strategically advantageous to partially anchor the selection of optimal BCx on insights drawn from prior comparative studies. With over 22,000 phase III RCTs conducted in the USA alone, a vast repository of data remains underutilized [11]. This dataset offers immense potential for refining trial designs and improving generalizability. Prior trials provide valuable insights into recruitment patterns, accrual rates, and resource constraints across various disease areas. Exploring this wealth of data could help determine how well trial populations reflect the broader patient community. However, merely drawing insights from previous trials is insufficient; these findings must be actively integrated into the design of future studies. For example, Stein observed that despite extensive preclinical studies identifying key clinical trial design variables—such as dosing, treatment duration, treatment window, and sex- or age-related differences—two similar phase III trials on traumatic brain injury (TBI) proceeded without incorporating these type of insights [12]. This oversight may have contributed to their lack of success, as evidenced by the failure to meet the primary endpoints [13, 14].

Salanti et al. [15] highlighted the important role of network meta-analysis (NMA) in planning future trials, introducing a four-step framework to identify the optimal design for a new trial. This framework aims to update existing evidence while minimizing the required sample size by borrowing strength from available studies. Building on this concept, we propose in this paper how a quantitative framework based on ML algorithms could enhance the generalizability of a trial’s patient population during trial recruitment [15]. By leveraging data from multiple trials, inspired by NMA principles of borrowing strength, this approach can systematically identify patterns in BCx across studies. Such a framework could optimize the selection of a target population that more closely reflects the joint distribution of the combined patient population in previous studies. Although individual trials may have limited external validity, integrating data from multiple studies could yield a more comprehensive joint distribution of BCx, which, when combined with insights from RWD sources, may serve as a basis for an optimal trial BCx. Enhancing trial comparability in this way can also strengthen estimates of relative treatment effect, an essential factor in value assessment framework such as the new European Union's Joint Clinical Assessment regulation.

The way forward

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool in the last decade, with the potential to optimize trial designs, enhance diagnostic accuracy, predict patient outcomes, and personalize treatments. Despite challenges in its adoption, such as regulatory and privacy concerns, ML’s application in healthcare continues to grow and drive innovation [16, 17]. There is some caution about deploying ML-based approaches in data analysis, given concerns that they might underperform when operating on small datasets. However, it is important to distinguish between an algorithm’s ability to make accurate predictions and its capacity to uncover underlying patterns. While ML might struggle with precise predictions in such limited settings, in our opinion, it can still reveal meaningful historical patterns of the target population distribution. For example, Feighner et al. used unsupervised learnings such as cluster analysis to uncover distinct patient subgroups, demonstrating how clustering methods can be effectively utilized in clinical research [18]. Furthermore, unsupervised learnings applied to electronic health record (EHR) and genetic data from 11,210 patients identified three distinct subtypes of type II diabetes mellitus, each characterized by unique phenotypic profiles, potentially indicating differing therapeutic needs and variable responses to candidate treatments [19].

Methods like K-medoids can also be highly effective for identifying the representative point with the smallest average dissimilarity or the point most similar to others in a cluster [20]. One method for identifying the optimal BCx distribution is to simulate BCx values across various clustering configurations. By evaluating how effectively each clustering solution captures the diversity and central tendencies of the data, the configuration that best mirrors the underlying distribution can be identified. This simulation-based approach combined with K-medoid algorithm allows for a quantitative comparison of clustering outcomes, pinpointing the “most similar” BCx distribution that minimizes the distance between points. Furthermore, this method is particularly valuable when handling non-Euclidean distances or datasets with outliers, as it accommodates extreme heterogeneity in BCx across trials while ensuring the clustering solution remains robust. To optimize cluster selection, methods such as the elbow method, silhouette coefficient, and gap statistics can be used. Furthermore, probabilistic models like Gaussian mixture models offer greater flexibility in assigning clusters [21]. To assess the variability of these estimates, bootstrap techniques can generate confidence intervals around centroids, ensuring robustness, especially in cases with sparse data. This approach allows trial designers to align BCx across studies while maintaining scientific rigor.

Incorporating external data sources can further strengthen the clustering framework. RWD data, such as electronic health records, patient registries, and claims data, can be integrated into Bayesian cluster analysis frameworks as priors [22]. This integration not only strengthens the analysis but also helps model the between-trial heterogeneity, particularly when data from diverse populations (e.g., trials conducted on Asian populations versus global cohorts) are involved. This approach allows for pre-specification of cluster numbers as priors and can be particularly useful when comparing centroid distribution differences across these diverse clusters. Integrating RWD into Bayesian clustering strengthens the analysis by adding crucial prior information, including baseline event rates, treatment effects, and patient characteristics, which in turn offers a more detailed and comprehensive view of the population. Conceptually, clustering-based population segmentation may reveal substantial heterogeneity across prior trials, indicated by a high number of clusters, helping researchers assess whether data borrowing is appropriate or if emphasis should instead be placed on pivotal trials. If distinct subgroups with significant between-cluster differences are identified, borrowing data from all available trials might not be appropriate. In such cases, agglomerative hierarchical clustering can help prioritize data from trials with similar populations, reducing noise and improving the relevance of the analysis [23]. Alternatively, rather than including all available trials, analysts may prioritize pivotal trials with key comparators to ensure the analysis remains relevant and actionable, aligning with the strategic goals of drug manufacturers. For example, older trials may be less relevant for the assessment, and conducting a separate sensitivity analysis could be a suitable approach—although the decision would depend on the discretion of the analyst.

Moreover, this approach can potentially be extended to account for treatment effects by identifying studies with similar effect sizes and examining the BCx distributions observed in those studies. By clustering studies based on effect sizes and adjusting for relevant baseline factors, researchers can gain insights into the types of populations associated with specific outcomes. This helps determine whether the BCx linked to a particular effect size can serve as a reference or guide for future trial designs, ultimately supporting better-informed recruitment strategies and enhancing the generalizability of trial results. For instance, these findings can guide real-time monitoring of enrolled participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics throughout the recruitment period. Screening logs and interim analyses enable comparisons against predefined benchmarks of these factors. If underrepresentation of certain subgroups, such as women or ethnic minorities, is detected, recruitment strategies can be adjusted proactively. This may include extending the recruitment period, launching targeted outreach campaigns, or reallocating resources to improve access and boost enrollment from underrepresented populations.

Concluding remarks

Harmonizing evidence generation strategies between regulatory and payer bodies is essential throughout the product lifecycle. Insights from clinical trials across their sequential phases play a central role in shaping strategic decisions. However, no two trials are identical, as each has distinct objectives, estimand, and resource constraints. Regulatory decision-making focuses on evaluating benefits and risks against standard-of-care treatments, while payers must compare multiple comparators simultaneously, often drawing from multiple RCTs. This raises concerns about generalizability—while some trials within NMA may align with the target population, others may not. Existing methods adjust for cross-trial imbalances, yet the marginal distribution of baseline characteristics at the network level may still differ from the intended target population.

To address these challenges, integrating RWD can help refine trial population alignment and improve generalizability. RWD offers valuable insights that can complement clinical trial data and improve generalizability. This paper does not suggest replacing RWD with trial data but advocates for leveraging both sources to optimize trial recruitment and population alignment. ML techniques can enhance this process by capturing population heterogeneity and informing more robust trial designs. However, ML models depend heavily on the quality and completeness of their training data, requiring careful selection and validation. The “black box” nature of some ML algorithms also poses interpretability challenges, necessitating transparency and adherence to regulatory guidance.

As HTA and regulatory bodies refine their positions on ML applications, there is growing optimism that integrating ML-driven approaches with traditional evidence generation will enhance trial design, improve external validity, and better inform payer decision-making in developed countries.
