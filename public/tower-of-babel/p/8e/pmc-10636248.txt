Excessive daytime sleepiness versus sleepiness at the wheel, the need to differentiate global from situational sleepiness to better predict sleep-related accidents
Authors: Pierre Philip
Published: 2023

Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) is a very common symptom affecting 10% to 20% of the general population [1]. Despite EDS can present different forms (transient versus chronic) and can have different causes (sleep deprivation, sleep disorders), it leads to similar deleterious consequences in terms of daytime functioning. One of the most dangerous consequences of excessive daytime sleepiness is the risk of driving accidents due to vigilance alterations [2]. Indeed sleep-related accidents have been associated with a specific type of crash where the driver report inability to maintain the vehicle on the lane, particularly on monotonous road. EDS increases reaction time and therefore alters the ability of drivers to quickly correct vehicle trajectories [3]. Because of this, experimental and accidental studies have focused on deviations from the center of the road and inappropriate line crossings to quantify the sleep-related driving risk. Still, drivers' perception of their level of alertness/sleepiness remains the best strategy for drowsy driving campaigns.

Drowsy driving affects not only sleep-deprived drivers but also many patients with sleep disorders (i.e. obstructive sleep apnea syndrome) so researchers have developed simple tools to investigate subjective perceptions of sleepiness during their daily lives. One of the most widely used self-report instruments is the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [4], which explores, in eight very different conditions, the global severity of EDS. Each question explores the tendency to fall asleep in daily life situations involving very different behaviors, such as reading, watching TV, or sitting in a vehicle during a traffic jam. The ESS score has been correlated to the accidental risk and studies have shown that high levels of sleepiness (>15 points) are frequently associated with accidental risk. Of course, the validity of the ESS relies on the fact that drivers can perceive accurately EDS, especially in circumstances where they are not supposed to fall asleep like in front of a steering wheel. This is a very important issue because many drivers involved in sleep-related accidents argue that they were not responsible because they did not feel sleepiness rising. They pretend to end up in car crashes without being alerted of the danger but a lot of victims and their lawyers sue these drivers because sleepiness at the wheel is a very well-known cause of traffic accidents, which need to be avoided for a safe driving.

From a legal point of view, it is therefore very important to confirm the hypothesis that people falling asleep at the wheel are able to perceive specific situational sleepiness (i.e. behind the wheel) and therefore could be blamed for losing control of their vehicle. Anderson et al. [5] published in this issue of Sleep an experimental real-driving study showing that shiftwork drivers are clearly aware of severe sleepiness when driving a car under sleep deprivation. This study replicates in a real environment the first study performed by Reyner and Horne using a driving simulator ( [6]) 20 years ago. Both studies, under simulated and real driving conditions, confirm that sleepiness is well-perceived and incremental symptom, which impairs cognitive functions. Anderson’s study showed that drivers who are aware of their sleepiness and fight against the symptom have a much higher risk of driving impairment, therefore it is important to define how EDS and sleep-related driving risk should be investigated at a population level. Not only does this issue concern untreated patients but also patients who is living with chronic sleep disorders (obstructive sleep apnea syndrome or hypersomnia) which are not fully improved by treatments such as continuous positive airway pressure. For instance, it is estimated that about 10% of patients under continuous positive airway pressure remain sleepy and deserve an additional treatment to restore alertness [7]. Drugs like pitolisant or solriamfetol have received recently their FDA/EMA approval for treatment of residual EDS and criteria evaluating accidental risk will be of major importance to evaluate the ability to drive in these patients.

The ESS explores a wide range of behavioral conditions related to the occurrence of global EDS but is not focused on the situational sleepiness affecting directly the driving risk. Consequently, among its eight questions, only two relate to driving impairment. Drivers can have high levels of behavioral sleepiness in passive situations (watching TV, reading, resting in the middle of the afternoon . . . ) without experiencing situational sleepiness in situations involving driving or being in a car.

Philip et al have recently published an article [8] on a new scale (the Bordeaux sleepiness scale) specifically designed to investigate situational sleepiness during driving based on the intensity and frequency of appearance of the symptom. This 4-item scale shows a very good and linear predictive value of risk of inappropriate line crossings and traffic accidents and should be preferred to the ESS to explore driving risk in treated and untreated sleep-disordered patients.

Another important issue refers to the accuracy of answers in a context where legal issues can be problematic for the drivers. Research studies [9] have shown that the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test is associated with the occurrence of near misses and sleep-related accidents and can be used to double-check the concordance of subjective and objective scores in drivers suspected of sleepiness at the wheel.

Following the path of sleep researchers, the automobile industry has developed warning systems based on lane tracking. Furthermore, it is working hard towards developing systems to detect sleepiness at the wheel. This is of major importance for drivers: using semi-autonomous vehicles, which monitor and correct car weaving under alleviated human supervision. These technological developments will reinforce the conditions where people can fall asleep which can interfere with their ability to take back the wheel. Unexpectedly, autonomous vehicles will not solve the issue of sleepiness at the wheel but, actually, might worsen it.

In conclusion, EDS deserves better evaluation. Besides, the concept of situational sleepiness, acknowledged in recent publications, highlights the importance of precisely quantifying EDS in specific situations to better understand its potential hazards.
