Inpatient continuous thalamic stereo-electroencephalography stimulation in the evaluation of focal epilepsy: illustrative case
Authors: Bruno Hidalgo Monroy Lerma, Ghassan S Makhoul, Anas Reda, Addison C Cavender, Derek J Doss, C Price Withers, Kate Wang, Graham W Johnson, Chadd Funk, Angela N Crudele, Dario J Englot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIn patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) who are not candidates for resective surgery, thalamic neuromodulation targeting structures such as the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT), centromedian nucleus (CM), and pulvinar (PUL) has emerged as a promising therapy. The clinical investigation of these targets’ efficacy and side effect profiles is ongoing, complicating discussions between neurosurgeons, neurologists, and patients. The implementation of single-pulse and continuous stimulation protocols to conduct patient-specific side effect screens and generate prognostic biomarkers could offer guidance for target selection.OBSERVATIONSA 32-year-old male with extensive polymicrogyria and DRE underwent stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) with multisite thalamic sampling to inform neuromodulation target selection. Single-pulse stimulation was conducted to screen for acute sensorimotor effects, followed by continuous (12- to 24-hour) stimulation using clinically relevant parameters. Intolerable paresthesia occurred during CM single-pulse stimulation, leading to its exclusion as a target. Single-pulse and continuous stimulation of the ANT and PUL were well tolerated. PUL stimulation offered superior suppression of epileptiform discharges during sleep.LESSONSThese findings meaningfully informed the selection of the PUL as a neuromodulation target. The thalamus, whose many nuclei mediate distinct patterns of white matter tracts connecting distributed subcortical and cortical regions, is increasingly considered as a neuromodulation target in patients with focal drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) who are not candidates for resection.1–4 Pioneering clinical investigations of the therapeutic efficacy of open- and closed-loop thalamic stimulation in focal DRE have reported meaningful reductions in seizure burden.5–8 Although the thalamus is not considered the epileptogenic zone (EZ) itself, changes in its connectivity to the EZ are thought to contribute to seizure generation, and its white matter tracts serve as critical pathways for seizure propagation.9–12 Multisite thalamic stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) recordings during intracranial electrophysiological monitoring have correspondingly become widely adopted across medical centers, primarily in patients whose noninvasive workup disfavors resection.13–16 The widespread implementation of thalamic sampling during SEEG monitoring compels a critical evaluation of best practices to improve surgical decision-making, particularly when multiple thalamic nuclei are candidate targets.16 The anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) has been most extensively studied in focal DRE, with clinical studies demonstrating population-level evidence of seizure burden reduction and side effect incidence.5,17–19 However, recent evidence suggests that other targets such as the pulvinar (PUL) and centromedian nucleus (CM) of the thalamus may be more effective in some patients.20,21 This evidence reaffirms that seizure networks are variable and ought to be delineated in a patient-specific manner.22 The efficacy and side effects of CM stimulation have been characterized in generalized epilepsies, but its investigation in the treatment of focal DRE has been limited to case series and single-center trials.8,23–25 PUL stimulation has gained attention specifically for focal DRE but remains the least extensively studied of the discussed targets.21,23,25,26 This introduces ambiguity during the joint decision-making process between clinicians and patients, where solid evidence cannot be reliably referenced. Until such evidence accumulates, an approach to mitigate this ambiguity is to leverage thalamic SEEG to emulate therapeutic stimulation at each candidate target. Even in the presence of population-level evidence, such stimulation trials could offer complementary patient-specific data on efficacy and tolerability to aid target selection.

Here, we demonstrate the safety and feasibility of 12- and 24-hour trials of continuous stimulation of distinct thalamic nuclei using therapeutic parameters during SEEG evaluation in the epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU). We believe that continuous stimulation trials offer a unique opportunity to prospectively evaluate target suitability in a patient-specific manner under conditions that closely emulate long-term neuromodulation, prior to committing to permanent lead implantation. Such trials could enable the preemptive identification of targets at which the patient would not tolerate stimulation parameters with evidence of efficacy, forcing downtitration to potentially suboptimal parameters. Beyond screening for adverse effects, continuous stimulation trials enable the assessment of potential biomarkers of therapeutic response, such as the suppression of epileptiform discharges.9 Continuous stimulation for > 12 hours specifically enables the evaluation of side effects and generation of biomarkers across the sleep-wake cycle, which is known to modulate epileptic processes.27

Recent reports have commented on the integration of thalamic SEEG recordings, without the use of stimulation, into clinical decision-making.12,13 Others have proposed the utility of either single-pulse or continuous (< 12 hours) stimulation to quantify interictal epileptiform discharge (IED) suppression.28,29 To our knowledge, our case is the first to illustrate that the combination of single-pulse and continuous (12- to 24-hour) stimulation protocols trialing therapeutic parameters at multiple candidate thalamic nuclei is safe and feasible and can meaningfully contribute to discussions between neurosurgeons, neurologists, and patients regarding target selection.

Illustrative Case

History of Present Illness

A 32-year-old male with a past medical history including extensive right hemispheric polymicrogyria and DRE since 2016 presented for presurgical evaluation with SEEG. Vagus nerve stimulation therapy provided minimal benefit. The patient had consciousness-impairing seizures at least once per week, sometimes experiencing clusters of up to 10 per week. Two previous evaluations in the EMU with long-term video and scalp EEG monitoring suggested right-sided lateralization with evidence of variable sources of seizure onset across frontal, central, and temporal regions. MRI demonstrated extensive right-sided perisylvian and frontotemporal polymicrogyria (Fig. 1A and B), along with a small right-sided paraventricular nodular heterotopia (PVNH). PET and functional MRI studies similarly suggested right-sided lateralization but were inconclusive in localizing a focal EZ. SPECT demonstrated hypoperfusion of the right anterior insular and adjacent frontal operculum regions. Given inconclusive characterization of seizure onset through noninvasive measures, further evaluation through SEEG was recommended to inform surgical intervention through resection or neuromodulation. After multidisciplinary discussion, SEEG monitoring with coverage of the PVNH, right frontal and temporal lobes, operculum, and central regions along the polymicrogyria was recommended. Because of noninvasive evidence of diffuse or multifocal EZs, sampling of the PUL, CM, and ANT was also recommended to inform target selection if thalamic neuromodulation were pursued.

Preoperative coronal (A) and sagittal (B) T1-weighted MR images demonstrating right-sided perisylvian polymicrogyria (boxes). Intraoperative coronal (C) and sagittal (D) CT images coregistered to preoperative T1-weighted MR images to confirm SEEG electrode placement.

Thirteen SEEG electrode (Ad-Tech) trajectories were developed using WayPoint Navigator software (FHC) and implanted throughout the right hemisphere. All thalamic trajectories utilized electrodes with 5-mm spacing between contacts. The CM and ANT trajectories utilized 14-contact electrodes, whereas the PUL trajectory utilized a 12-contact electrode. Appropriate electrode placement within thalamic nuclei was confirmed intraoperatively by coregistering preoperative T1-weighted MR images with intraoperative CT images using CRAnialVault Explorer (CRAVE) software (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee) (Fig. 1C and D). Bipolar pairs within thalamic nuclei were confirmed postoperatively by manual registration and visual inspection using CRAVE (Fig. 2).

Postoperative confirmation of lead placement at the ANT (A), PUL (C), and CM (E) using an atlas of the thalamus. Dynamic shape models showing the contralateral thalamus (red) and bilateral ANT (B), PUL (D), and CM (F).

The patient’s home antiseizure medication regimen (cenobamate 300 mg daily, topiramate XR 200 mg twice daily) was not altered during his EMU stay. Thirty-three ictal events revealed a broad network involved in seizure onset, implicating the large region of right fronto-parieto-temporal polymicrogyria with involvement of the motor cortex along the posterior extent. There was a clear state dependence of the dominant epileptiform discharge; IEDs predominated during the wake state, whereas brief ictal rhythmic discharges (BIRDs) predominated during sleep. Thalamic SEEG demonstrated earlier and greater involvement of the PUL than the ANT and CM during seizures. Given the distributed and eloquent nature of the patient’s EZs, responsive neurostimulation (RNS) targeting the thalamus was favored over resection. To help guide selection of the thalamic target among the ANT, CM, and PUL, we designed and conducted the described two-phase stimulation assessment (Fig. 3A).

Single-Pulse Stimulation Assessment

Single-pulse stimulation was conducted to screen for acute sensorimotor side effects using the Natus Nicolet Cortical Stimulator. We delivered bipolar stimulation through trains of pulses with a 100-μsec pulse width and 30-second train duration, and uptitrated the pulse frequency from 30 to 50 to 100 Hz and amplitude from 1 to 3 to 5 mA (Fig. 3B). The goal was to uptitrate to parameters with strong clinical evidence of therapeutic efficacy.5 We were unable to exactly match parameters between single-pulse and continuous stimulation due to restrictions in the Natus system. We started at the lowest value for both parameters, then uptitrated amplitude first to determine the maximum value without sensorimotor effects. We then locked this amplitude value and repeated this process for frequency. We repeated this process for all thalamic nuclei, with 5-minute intervals between the stimulation of each nucleus. The patient was blinded to stimulation delivery and asked to report sensorimotor symptoms. If sensorimotor symptoms were observed at any frequency or amplitude, the thalamic nucleus was excluded and not assessed through continuous stimulation.

Paresthesia and a brief jolting pain along the right upper extremity were reported during CM stimulation at 50 Hz and 3 mA, leading to its exclusion from continuous stimulation trials. The patient did not report any sensorimotor symptoms during PUL and ANT stimulation at 100 Hz and 5 mA.

Continuous Stimulation Assessment

We then conducted continuous trials of stimulation with therapeutic parameters at each thalamic nucleus that was not excluded during phase 1 (Fig. 3A). The goal was to conduct 24-hour trials at each nucleus. However, due to technical difficulties, continuous stimulation of the ANT lasted 12 hours. This 12-hour period still spanned periods of the wake and sleep states. We adopted the most common stimulation parameters cited in one of the primary clinical trials demonstrating the therapeutic efficacy of thalamic deep brain stimulation: bipolar stimulation, 90-μsec pulse width, 3-mA amplitude, 145-Hz frequency, 1-minute train duration, and 5-minute intertrain interval (Fig. 3C).5 The continuous stimulation protocol was implemented using the Ripple Trellis software and delivered via the Ripple Explorer Summit neural interface system. The Ripple system was used during phase 2 to enable programmatic stimulation, which Natus does not support. The protocol was delivered continuously with a 1-minute on, 5-minute off duty cycle at one thalamic nucleus at a time, with a 1-hour interval between nuclei.

During continuous stimulation, the patient was evaluated every 2 hours for sensorimotor or subjective cognitive symptoms. The patient was also asked to write down any symptoms felt between evaluations. In the morning following a night of continuous stimulation, the patient was asked to subjectively report the quality of his sleep to screen for stimulation-induced disruption of sleep quality. The patient did not report sensorimotor or cognitive symptoms across any evaluations during continuous stimulation of either nucleus. He reported sleeping well both nights. The patient was blinded to the stimulation target, but not stimulation itself.

Epileptologists reported clusters of seizures throughout the patient’s 13 days in the EMU, with zero events on 7 days but a mean of 8.4 clinical or electrographic seizures on days with at least one seizure. No clinical seizures were observed during either trial. Six electrographic seizures with no clinical correlates were recorded during ANT stimulation, with durations ranging from several seconds to 6 minutes. The patient did not report his typical left facial and upper extremity aura during any of these events. One electrographic seizure with a clinical correlate was recorded during PUL stimulation, which lasted 8 seconds. This event occurred as the patient was falling asleep and was preceded by a cluster of IEDs, followed by the typical onset pattern observed during seizures throughout the patient’s EMU stay (Fig. 4). Video monitoring revealed that the patient was awoken during the onset of this event and pressed the seizure button but did not proceed to demonstrate clinical evidence of a seizure. On evaluation, the patient reported that he was awoken by his distinct sensory aura and that he had “never felt that aura without starting a cluster of seizures within a few moments.”

Evaluation of Epileptiform Discharges During Continuous Stimulation

A board-certified epileptologist recorded the rate of epileptiform discharges during each stimulation condition. Delivery of continuous stimulation for > 12 hours enabled the comparison of epileptiform discharge suppression during PUL and ANT stimulation, which accounted for the patient’s state-dependent differences in the dominant discharge. Accordingly, IEDs were compared during wake periods, and BIRDs were compared during sleep periods. All comparisons across stimulation conditions controlled for time of day. Waking and sleeping states were confirmed by an epileptologist using video intracranial EEG monitoring. IED rates at four of the suspected EZs were quantified through manual annotation of a 10-minute sample from each stimulation condition and a waking baseline (Fig. 5A). BIRDs were quantified across a 1-hour sample during each stimulation condition and two separate sleeping baselines. BIRD annotation included both the duration of each BIRD (Fig. 5B) and its rate per minute (Fig. 5C). A Poisson rate ratio test was used to evaluate if IED and BIRD rates during stimulation conditions were significantly different from those at the waking and sleeping baseline, respectively (Fig. 5A and C). A Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to test for significant differences in BIRD duration across stimulation conditions, followed by a post hoc pairwise Mann-Whitney U-test (Fig. 5B). A Bonferroni-Holm correction for multiple comparisons with an α of 0.05 was performed for all Poisson rate ratio and Mann-Whitney U-tests.

Quantification of epileptiform discharges across wake and sleep states. A: IED rate at four suspected EZs across stimulation (Stim.) conditions (Poisson rate ratio test, relative to wake baseline). B: Distribution of BIRD durations during each stimulation condition (Kruskal-Wallis p = 9.60 × 10−7; post hoc Mann-Whitney U-test: baseline vs PUL = 3.29 × 10−2; baseline vs ANT = 1.89 × 10−6; PUL vs ANT = 2.15 × 10−3). C: BIRD rate during each stimulation condition (Poisson rate ratio test, relative to sleep baseline). A Bonferroni-Holm correction for multiple comparison with an α of 0.05 was performed across all statistical tests. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

The patient’s IED rates from EZs at the middle frontal and superior temporal gyri were significantly suppressed during both PUL and ANT stimulation, relative to his waking baseline. The IED rate from an EZ in the primary sensory cortex was suppressed during stimulation of the PUL but not the ANT. IEDs from an EZ in the middle temporal gyrus were not significantly suppressed during stimulation of either target (Fig. 5A). BIRD duration was significantly shortened during stimulation of both the PUL and ANT (Fig. 5B). Although BIRD duration was shortest during ANT stimulation, there were significantly more episodes across the sampled hour. There was thus a marked increase in BIRD rate during ANT stimulation relative to sleep baseline, which was not observed during PUL stimulation (Fig. 5C). Overall, these data suggest that both PUL and ANT stimulation effectively suppress IEDs during the wake state, with a marginal advantage for PUL stimulation at the primary sensory cortex, and PUL stimulation is more effective at suppressing BIRDs during sleep.

Surgery and Outcome

Resective surgery was carefully considered but deemed a less favorable option given the extent of the EZ, which involved the extensive polymicrogyria as well as motor networks. Given the absence of population-level data on the tolerability and efficacy of thalamic stimulation targeting alternative structures, we conducted the described two-phase stimulation assessment to further inform our surgical decision-making. This allowed us to confidently exclude the CM as a potential target because the patient would be unlikely to tolerate stimulation parameters with evidence of therapeutic efficacy.5 The tolerability of the ANT and PUL across both phases of this protocol provided evidence that the patient would tolerate stimulation of these nuclei at therapeutic parameters. The reported evidence of epileptiform discharge suppression was taken into consideration alongside evidence of earlier ictal recruitment of the PUL relative to the ANT on SEEG recordings. After multidisciplinary discussion, the PUL was favored as the target for RNS lead placement.

The patient subsequently underwent implantation of an RNS device targeting the PUL. The patient was placed supine, the previously implanted bone anchors were exposed, and the custom stereotactic platform (FHC) was mounted on the head to mark the entry point for the electrode. The frame was removed, a burr hole was made, and the frame was remounted. The electrode positioner drives were used to guide the cannula and, subsequently, the electrode to the PUL. The patient recovered well in the postoperative unit and was discharged on postoperative day 2. Surgical outcome cannot yet be reported as the patient has not reached the first outcome milestone at 6 months.

Discussion

Observations

Thalamic SEEG monitoring during presurgical evaluation of patients with DRE has become widely implemented, but the use of stimulation to help guide surgical decision-making is an area of ongoing development. Our report is the first to illustrate a systematic assessment combining single-pulse and continuous stimulation to evaluate candidate neuromodulation targets during a thalamic SEEG study. This two-phase stimulation assessment produced valuable contributions to our clinical decision-making by offering a patient-specific screen for side effects and the collection of biomarkers that may help prognosticate therapeutic efficacy.

Sensorimotor symptoms are a known side effect of neuromodulation therapy and are typically managed after chronic device implantation by downtitrating stimulation parameters.24,30 However, this might result in the chronic delivery of suboptimal stimulation, hampering therapeutic efficacy. By identifying such intolerable sensorimotor symptoms induced by CM stimulation in this patient, we were able to fine-tune our presurgical evaluation and preemptively avoid targets at which therapeutic stimulation would not be tolerated.

The suppression of epileptiform discharges during thalamic stimulation is increasingly used as a proxy to assess a thalamic target’s influence on EZs.9,29 We noted a strong state dependence in this patient’s dominant epileptiform discharges, with IEDs predominating during the wake state and BIRDs during the sleep state. This may be reflective of distinct pathological epileptic processes across the sleep and wake states, corroborating the well-described bidirectional interaction between epilepsy and the sleep-wake cycle.27 Indeed, it is possible that therapeutic strategies designed based on waking data may be suboptimal to treat nocturnal seizures. With this in mind, we emphasize the importance of extending the continuous stimulation assessment to > 12 hours, allowing for the evaluation of side effects and biomarkers across both sleep and wake states. This patient tolerated both PUL and ANT stimulation well across the sleep and wake states. However, whereas IED rates during the wake state suggested only a marginal advantage in the efficacy of PUL stimulation over ANT stimulation, BIRD predominance during sleep suggested that PUL stimulation suppressed nocturnal epileptiform discharges more effectively. This distinction may not have been apparent with more limited stimulation durations.

It is important to note, however, that these biomarkers should not be overinterpreted. Because a single trial per candidate nucleus is conducted, a relative decrease in IED or seizure rates on a given day could be the product of well-known multidien fluctuations in these events.31 Moreover, the notion that IED modulation is a prognostic biomarker for neuromodulation efficacy requires further investigation. In our evaluation of this patient, these biomarkers offered data that was supplementary to our existing decision-making schema. In the future, continuous stimulation protocols could be repeated on multiple nonconsecutive days to collect more robust evidence of IED or seizure suppression.

The proposed workflow contains many facets that can be further developed and improved on. For example, the disruption of sleep is a potentially underreported side effect of neuromodulation therapy for DRE, where many patients have preexisting poor sleep quality.27 Sleep adverse effects have been reported during ANT stimulation.19,32 Monitoring patients in the EMU with a dual-scalp EEG-SEEG montage would allow for more rigorous monitoring of the integrity of the sleep cycle during nights in the EMU.

Lessons

The use of single-pulse and continuous stimulation assessments was valuable to us in the generation of data regarding side effects and efficacy to guide target selection among candidate thalamic nuclei. The performance of a patient-specific side effect screen can increase confidence in the selection of a novel target such as the PUL for both the clinician and the patient, offering a supplement while population-level evidence accumulates. Moreover, stimulation assessments can enable the timely identification of targets at which therapeutic stimulation would not be tolerated, such as CM in this patient. Finally, > 12-hour continuous stimulation assessments can generate biomarkers across the sleep-wake cycle, enabling a robust consideration of candidate targets’ state-dependent interactions with epileptic processes, as demonstrated in this patient.
