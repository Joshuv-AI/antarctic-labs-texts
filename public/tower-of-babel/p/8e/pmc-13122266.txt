Long‐Term Outcomes in Children With Congenital Cytomegalovirus Infection
Authors: Noriko Morimoto, Noriomi Suzuki, Kanako Imamura, Tomohiro Saikawa, Naoto Miyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesWe aimed to evaluate the long‐term auditory outcomes of early antiviral therapy with valganciclovir (VGCV) in children with congenital cytomegalovirus (cCMV) infection, regardless of initial symptomatology.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 56 pediatric patients (112 ears) diagnosed with cCMV infection between 2004 and 2022 at our institution. The participants were divided into those who received (treated group, n = 19) and did not receive (untreated group, n = 37) VGCV or ganciclovir therapy. Audiological assessments were conducted over a minimum 2‐year follow‐up period (range, 2–15 years). Furthermore, hearing thresholds were evaluated longitudinally, with a decline of ≥ 10 dB being defined as significant. The primary outcomes were hearing loss progression and age at onset.ResultsAt the final follow‐up, there were no significant between‐group differences in the prevalence or severity of hearing loss. However, fewer ears showed significant hearing deterioration in the treated group (26%) than in the untreated group (41%, p < 0.01). Furthermore, the magnitude of hearing decline was smaller in the treated group (35.0 ± 13.8 dB) than in the untreated group (67.4 ± 19.2 dB) (p < 0.002). Hearing loss in treated patients stabilized by the age of 3 years; in contrast, untreated patients experienced multiple deterioration episodes even after this age.ConclusionsEarly VGCV treatment may help stabilize hearing and reduce long‐term hearing deterioration in children with cCMV infections. This highlights the importance of early intervention and hearing surveillance, especially in untreated individuals.Level of Evidence4

Introduction

Congenital cytomegalovirus (cCMV) infection is the most common fetal viral infection, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 0.64% among all live births. Congenital hearing loss occurs in 1–2 per 1000 newborns, with cCMV infection being the second leading cause after hereditary hearing loss, accounting for approximately 20%–25% of all cases [1].

Clinically, cCMV infections can be classified as symptomatic or asymptomatic. Approximately 10%–15% of infants with cCMV infection exhibit clinical manifestations such as petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly, microcephaly, and hearing loss, whereas the remaining 85%–90% are asymptomatic at birth. Nonetheless, even asymptomatic infants are at risk of developing progressive or late‐onset sensorineural hearing loss.

Antiviral therapy, particularly valganciclovir (VGCV) therapy initiated within the first 2 months of life, may improve long‐term auditory function and neurodevelopmental outcomes in symptomatic infants [2, 3, 4]. Accordingly, a 6‐month course of early antiviral therapy is currently recommended for neonates with moderate‐to‐severe symptoms [5]. However, there is no evidence to support antiviral treatment in neonates with mild symptoms. Furthermore, antiviral treatment is not recommended for asymptomatic infants in light of potential adverse effects [6].

The natural history of cCMV‐related hearing loss includes a wide range of auditory changes. Specifically, some children experience gradual deterioration, whereas others show sudden and rapid decline in hearing. During a 10‐year follow‐up period, Foulon et al. reported that 22% of children with cCMV developed hearing loss with notable fluctuations, particularly during early childhood [7]. Moreover, even following initial hearing improvement with VGCV therapy, there were subsequent fluctuations in hearing thresholds.

In Japan, targeted CMV screening for newborns referred from the universal newborn hearing screening program is covered by the National Health Insurance. More recently, targeted CMV screening for infants referred after newborn hearing screening tests was implemented in April 2024. However, nationwide coverage remains limited, with an estimated implementation rate of approximately 30%. This indicates the increasing recognition of cCMV as a preventable cause of pediatric hearing loss and the importance of its early detection. Although randomized controlled trials have been conducted to evaluate the efficacy of antiviral treatment, ethical concerns and recruitment difficulties have led to the early termination of studies, such as the ValEar [8] and Concerto programs [9]. Although several clinical trials have assessed antiviral therapy in symptomatic infants, long‐term auditory outcomes in real‐world clinical settings remain poorly characterized. In routine clinical practice, CMV testing is often performed only after a diagnosis of hearing loss, typically using urine polymerase chain reaction or dried umbilical cord sample analysis. Consequently, asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic cases may not be identified early, and their long‐term hearing trajectories remain unclear. Therefore, studies reflecting actual clinical practice are necessary to better understand long‐term outcomes and optimize management strategies. Additionally, the long‐term effects of antiviral therapy in infants with normal hearing remain unclear. Finally, long‐term auditory outcomes following VGCV treatment remain unclear.

Accordingly, we aimed to investigate the efficacy of early VGCV therapy in preventing or mitigating hearing deterioration in children diagnosed with cCMV. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate long‐term auditory outcomes throughout adolescence to clarify the efficacy of antiviral therapy in cCMV cases, regardless of initial symptomatology.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cohort study included pediatric patients diagnosed with cCMV infection at our institution between 2004 and 2022. Among 66 confirmed cases, we included 56 children (112 ears) aged 2–20 years who underwent newborn hearing screening. All participants underwent audiological follow‐up for ≥ 2 years (range, 2–15 years). We excluded patients with other known causes of hearing loss (e.g., mumps and chronic otitis media).

The participants were divided into those who received (treated group, n = 19) and did not receive (untreated group, n = 37) ganciclovir (GCV) and/or VGCV therapy. The primary outcomes were the progression of hearing loss and age at onset. The secondary outcome was the association between hearing loss and the presence of neurological abnormalities.

Diagnosis of cCMV Infection

We confirmed cCMV infection by detecting CMV DNA in urine samples within 21 days of birth in suspected cCMV cases immediately after birth. For cases in which cCMV was suspected after 21 days, diagnosis was performed by extracting CMV DNA from dried umbilical cord samples, followed by quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction.

Treatment Protocol

Infants diagnosed with cCMV infection who presented with moderate‐to‐severe symptoms at birth, including microcephaly, intracranial calcifications, white matter abnormalities, or sensorineural hearing loss, were included in the treatment group if they received oral VGCV (16 mg/kg twice daily) or intravenous ganciclovir within the first 2 months of life. The duration of antiviral therapy (4 weeks to 6 months) depended on the clinical course and occurrence of adverse events.

The untreated group comprised asymptomatic infants (including those with normal hearing) and symptomatic infants who were diagnosed after the age of 2 months or did not receive antiviral therapy for various reasons.

Audiological Assessment

Audiological assessment was conducted using a combination of auditory brainstem response, auditory steady‐state response, and pure tone audiometry, depending on the age and developmental stage.

The average hearing thresholds at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz were calculated, with hearing loss severity being classified as mild (21–45 dB), moderate (46–70 dB), severe (71–90 dB), or profound (≥ 91 dB). Significant improvement or deterioration was indicated by a threshold change of ≥ 10 dB. For patients with otitis media with effusion, hearing is reassessed after treatment or tympanostomy tube insertion.

Statistical Analyses

Between‐group comparisons were performed using the chi‐square and Mann–Whitney U tests. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the time until hearing deterioration (defined as a threshold of ≥ 40 dB), with curve comparisons performed using the log‐rank and generalized Wilcoxon tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using the STAAT statistical application and Python (version 3.11) with custom scripts developed in accordance with standard survival analysis procedures.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Child Health and Development (approval number: NCCHD‐280). This retrospective observational study was conducted in accordance with the institutional ethical standards. Given that no personally identifiable information was used, the requirement for informed consent was waived; instead, we applied an opt‐out method that was approved by the ethics committee.

Results

Patient Characteristics

During the study period, 66 patients were diagnosed with cCMV infection. Among them, we excluded 10 patients who did not undergo newborn hearing screening. Accordingly, 56 patients (112 ears) were included in the final analysis. Among them, 19 and 37 patients were assigned to the treated and untreated groups, respectively. Among the 19 treated patients, 10 received VGCV monotherapy, 3 received GCV monotherapy, and 6 were managed with sequential therapy (1 week of intravenous GCV, followed by oral VGCV). Adverse events included concurrent hyperkalemia and neutropenia (two cases), hyperkalemia only (two cases), and elevated bilirubin (one case). Medication was ultimately discontinued in four cases because of adverse events, all occurring within the first 9 weeks of treatment. In one asymptomatic infant, therapy was stopped because of hyperkalemia after careful consideration of the risk–benefit balance, and no clinical complications were observed during follow‐up. These findings contribute to understanding the safety profile of antiviral therapy in clinical practice.

Hearing Status at Birth and Follow‐Up

At birth, hearing loss (unilateral or bilateral) was observed in 11 (57.8%) and 11 (29.7%) patients in the treated and untreated groups, respectively. At the final follow‐up, hearing loss (unilateral or bilateral) was observed in 13 (68.4%) and 27 (73.0%) patients in the treated and untreated groups, respectively. There was no significant between‐group difference in the prevalence of hearing loss at final follow‐up.

Timing of Hearing Deterioration

In the treated group, hearing deterioration was observed more frequently before the age of 3 years, with only one episode occurring thereafter (Figure 3). Contrastingly, in the untreated group, hearing loss was more frequently observed after the age of 3 years (median: 3.5 years, range: 1–13 years), with multiple episodes of hearing deterioration being observed (median: 5.0 years, range: 3–17 years).

Discussion

Our findings suggest that early antiviral therapy with VGCV may be associated with the timing and pattern of progression of hearing loss in children diagnosed with cCMV infection, even in those presenting with severe neurological abnormalities at birth. Notably, the treated group included infants with high medical complexity, such as ventriculomegaly, abnormal neuroimaging findings, and psychomotor developmental delays. However, there was no significant between‐group difference in long‐term hearing outcomes, which suggests the utility of antiviral therapy during the clinical course of hearing loss, especially in high‐risk cases.

There were between‐group differences in the timing and patterns of hearing loss. Specifically, hearing deterioration was more frequently observed before the age of 3 years in the treated group, with only one episode occurring. Contrastingly, in the untreated group, hearing loss was more frequently observed after the age of 3 years, with multiple episodes of hearing deterioration being observed. Among 24 infants treated with VGCV for 6 months within 2 months of birth, Morioka et al. reported that the proportion of ears with maintained or improved hearing increased from 52.5% at 6 post‐treatment months to 72.9% by the age of 3 years [10]. Contrastingly, McCrary et al. reported progressive hearing loss between the ages of 5 months and 4.3 years in a cohort of 16 children who underwent VGCV therapy for 6 weeks to 6 months [3]. Notably, they emphasized that shorter follow‐up periods may underestimate the progression of hearing loss, which highlights the need for long‐term observation when interpreting treatment effects [3]. Similarly, Chung et al. observed greater fluctuations in hearing thresholds in the untreated group than in the early‐treated group at the age of 2 years [9]. Lanzieri et al. observed sensorineural hearing loss in 71%, 59%, and 22% of treated symptomatic, untreated symptomatic, and untreated asymptomatic children with cCMV, respectively, with progression occurring regardless of treatment status [11]. In our study, though fewer cases of hearing deterioration were observed among patients receiving longer antiviral therapy, the differences did not reach statistical significance, likely because of the small sample size. Therefore, no definitive conclusions regarding the optimal treatment duration can be drawn from this study. Taken together, our findings suggest that antiviral therapy may be associated with early stabilization or short‐term modification of hearing outcomes, whereas long‐term auditory outcomes remain heterogeneous; hearing deterioration may still occur despite treatment.

Adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation occurred exclusively during the early treatment phase. Importantly, no subsequent neurological or auditory deterioration was observed after discontinuation. In one asymptomatic infant, therapy was stopped because of hyperkalemia after careful consideration of the risk–benefit balance, and no clinical complications were observed during follow‐up. These findings contribute to understanding the safety profile of antiviral therapy in clinical practice. Overall, our study provides real‐world data that reflect current clinical practice. By integrating evidence from short‐term and long‐term studies, our findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of antiviral therapy in cCMV, especially with respect to disease severity and the importance of extended follow‐up. The treated group experienced an early but relatively stable or gradual hearing decline, whereas the untreated group showed a prolonged risk of late‐onset, repeated, and sometimes sudden deteriorations. Notably, there were no significant between‐group differences in long‐term hearing outcomes, which can be attributed to the treated group comprising patients with congenital hearing loss. These findings highlight the importance of long‐term auditory monitoring and suggest that early antiviral therapy may be associated with improvement of hearing outcomes in patients with cCMV infection.

This study has some limitations. First, this study had a relatively small sample size; moreover, treatment allocation was not randomized and was strongly influenced by clinical severity. Infants with more severe cCMV infection were more likely to receive antiviral therapy, which resulted in substantial between‐group differences in baseline characteristics. Therefore, direct causal inference regarding treatment efficacy is limited. Second, the absence of a non‐treated severe cCMV group represents a significant limitation. Given that early diagnosis and treatment are typically performed for severe neonatal cases, the natural course of severe disease without intervention could not be evaluated. Third, the follow‐up duration varied among patients and may have been insufficient to capture long‐term hearing outcomes in all cases. Previous long‐term studies have suggested that hearing deterioration may occur several years after treatment; accordingly, a shorter follow‐up period may underestimate progressive hearing loss. Fourth, the treatment duration varied widely (4 weeks to 6 months), which limits the generalizability of our findings to the current standard of 6‐month VGCV therapy.

Our findings highlight the importance of early diagnosis, intervention, and continuous hearing follow‐up during adolescence in children with cCMV. Given the variability in cCMV‐related hearing loss and the possibility of spontaneous improvement in some cases, universal application of antiviral therapy in asymptomatic infants remains controversial. Detection of CMV DNA in the endolymph [12] supports the efficacy of VGCV against direct viral damage to the inner ear. However, the limited efficacy of VGCV initiated after the age of 1 month suggests that cytokine‐mediated mechanisms may be involved in late‐onset and progressive hearing loss [8]. In our cohort, long‐term hearing loss recurrence was more common in the untreated group than in the treated group. This indicates that the reduction in viral load following early treatment may mitigate the effects of residual virus in the inner ear and immunological responses.

These findings are particularly relevant within current CMV screening practices in Japan, where testing is typically triggered by referral after newborn hearing screening or by clinical suspicion rather than through universal screening. Under this framework, untreated children remain at risk of late‐onset and recurrent hearing deterioration, highlighting the importance of long‐term auditory monitoring. Larger prospective studies with longer follow‐up durations are warranted to establish optimal treatment criteria and identify infants most likely to benefit from antiviral intervention.

Conclusion

These results emphasize the need for continuous hearing monitoring and suggest a potential benefit of treatment in certain clinical contexts, rather than definitive efficacy.
