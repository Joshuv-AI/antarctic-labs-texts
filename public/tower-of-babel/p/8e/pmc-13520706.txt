Tailoring pedagogical methods to enhance student engagement through a student-faculty partnership in an undergraduate immunology course
Authors: Mustafa G. Mujtaba, Dayhana Justiniano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGetting real-time feedback on pedagogical methods used in the classroom can be a challenge due to a variety of reasons, including the classroom format, instructor lecturing styles, as well as weariness of students to engage instructors. However, a structured semester-long student-faculty partnership can serve as a powerful tool to enhance teaching effectiveness and student engagement. We describe here our version of a student-faculty partnership program (SFPP) model in an upper-level undergraduate Immunology course where a student from a non-science major was embedded in the course to observe classroom dynamics between students and instructor and provide formative feedback to the instructor throughout the semester. This collaboration led to several pedagogical adjustments suggested by the student-partner, including increased use of flipped classroom sessions, integration of lab experiments within lecture sessions, and the use of interactive tools, such as clickers for concept reinforcement and game apps for exam reviews. As a result, the course section implementing the SFPP model had high engagement in lectures and labs, increased performance on lab assignment submitted, and better satisfaction with the revised course format. The end of the semester student surveys and comments indicated student enthusiasm and comfort with the course content as a result of the revised instructional strategies. Thus, the SFPP model offers an adaptable approach for instructors seeking to create a more responsive, real-time, and student-centered learning environment in immunology and biology education.

INTRODUCTION

Classroom observations have been used to improve pedagogical methods, guide course design, and support instructor professional development (1–3). Traditionally, observations are conducted by peers or supervisors to assess teaching effectiveness and student engagement (4, 5). While these observations can provide valuable feedback, they are limited to a few sessions and may not capture the day-to-day nuances of student learning. End of the semester evaluations by classroom students also offer valuable information from the students’ perspective to improve future semester courses. However, they do not offer the opportunity to make real-time changes throughout the current active semester. In contrast, through student-faculty pedagogical partnership, the student partners are not enrolled as learners but are rather immersed in the learning environment as observers and can provide real-time, context-rich feedback (1, 6–10).

At Florida Gulf Coast University (FGCU), the Lucas Center’s Student-Faculty Partnership Program (SFPP) is a structured initiative designed to foster this collaborative dialog between a student observer and faculty with the goal of enhancing teaching and learning (11). The SFPP at FGCU follows a well-established approach (see references 12, 13 in that the embedded student observer is from a different major that is not part of the course roster, which allows for greater freedom of observation of the class activities and pedagogical methods as compared to other types of student-led observation methods (1, 6, 7, 12, 13). Essentially, the non-major student and faculty form a partnership to enhance instructional activities through feedback throughout the semester. The student partner’s role is to interact with classroom students, including performing lab and lecture activities with them and observing the classroom dynamics between faculty and students throughout the semester. Thus, the embedding of the student partner allows for continuous reflection and iterative improvement, making the feedback by the student partner more actionable and relevant.

In this study, a student from a non-science major was embedded in an upper-level Immunology course (PCB 4233C) as part of the SFPP, and their role was to observe real-time classroom dynamics and provide formative feedback to the instructor as evidenced by student partner’s observations of classroom activities and student engagement. The student partner in the SFPP had weekly discussion and training sessions with other student partners from across campus, which was led by the Lucas Center’s SFPP coordinator (Supplemental Material, Appendix 1). This initiative aims to improve student retention, engagement, and performance.

PROCEDURE

Course context and duration

The SFPP activity was implemented over a 15-week period in an upper-level combined lecture and laboratory Immunology course (PCB 4233C; n = 32 students) and compared to a control section of PCB 4233C (n = 32 students) both taught by the same instructor and meeting twice a week (2 h and 15 min per class). The courses were conducted in-person with online learning components via the university’s learning management system (LMS). The student partner for the Immunology course attended all sessions throughout the semester and had access to all assignments posted on the university’s LMS for the course.

Implementation of the SFPP

The role of the student partner as part of the SFPP included observing student interactions and engagement with the instructor and providing real-time and weekly feedback to the instructor. Details of the SFPP implementation at FGCU and the student partner’s weekly discussion and training sessions led by the Lucas Center’s SFPP coordinator are outlined in the Supplemental Material (Appendix 1). The impact of the SFPP was assessed through a comparative analysis with a control section of the Immunology course that did not implement the SFPP. The first 2 weeks of the semester were observational as the student partner became familiar with the instructor’s teaching pedagogy. Starting in the third week, the faculty and student partner met after class to discuss the classroom instruction and where improvements can be made. Based on the instructor’s discussions with the student partner, the instructor implemented the following changes to the classroom instruction:

increased use of flipped classroom sessions where classroom pedagogy shifts content delivery outside of class while reserving in-class time for active learning and discussion, which is particularly true for many STEM disciplines (14–16);

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the value of a structured, semester-long SFPP as a strategy for enhancing undergraduate immunology education. As part of the SFPP at FGCU, a student from a non-science major observes classroom interactions and provides ongoing feedback. In the Immunology course, this collaborative model led to several pedagogical enhancements as outlined above and in Fig. 1B. Although not statistically significant (P > 0.05), the SFPP Immunology course section had a slightly higher retention rate, greater engagement, and higher course assignment grades (Table S1). Furthermore, the end of the semester survey completion rate was high for the SFPP section (87.5%), and the student ratings (Likert scale) and the qualitative feedback were very favorable, indicating student enthusiasm for the new elements introduced due to the SFPP (Table S2). The following student comments are illustrative examples from the end of the semester student surveys (n = 32) that illuminate the positive impacts of the SFPP:

“Keeping it engaging by doing clicker questions and requiring quizzes before lectures made me actually read the PowerPoint. He always reviewed previous material before learning new stuff.”

These changes in the SFPP section of the Immunology course contributed to greater student engagement and align with existing literature demonstrating that active learning strategies can enhance student engagement in biology education (3, 14, 15, 19, 20). The implementation of the SFPP demonstrates that integrating real-time student feedback into course design can improve student learning experiences as the approach fosters a more adaptive and student-centered environment. These benefits of the SFPP are not limited to the context described here, as similar strategies could be applied across STEM disciplines and in varied instructional formats, including large lectures and hybrid courses. Some limitations of the SFPP in an upper-level undergraduate immunology course include a relatively small sample size and variability in student characteristics and institutional contexts as well as a non-randomized design. Future adaptations of this SFPP model could involve its impact on different student populations and academic settings as well as examining long-term effects on learning outcomes. Overall, the SFPP model presents a unique approach for faculty aiming to create more student-centered, active, and responsive learning environments in biology and other STEM courses.
