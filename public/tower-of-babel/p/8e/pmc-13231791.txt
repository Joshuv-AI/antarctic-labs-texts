Cariostatic effects of pediatric iron supplement formulations on streptococcus mutans–induced enamel demineralization: an in vitro study
Authors: Ahmed Mohamed Elmarakby, Shaimaa F K Habib, Nasser H Shaheen, Ali Abdulmajeed Barakat, Heba Arafa Abd Elwahab, Mohamed Ahmed Wakwak, Mohammed A AlSaleem, Rotana Marwan Abouseedou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIron deficiency anemia (IDA) is one of the most prevalent nutritional disorders in childhood, and oral iron supplements remain the standard therapy. Experimental evidence suggests that iron may inhibit cariogenic activity; however, the in-vitro cariostatic potential of commonly prescribed pediatric iron formulations remains insufficiently characterized.ObjectiveTo evaluate the in vitro cariostatic potential of four commercially available pediatric iron supplements on Streptococcus mutans–induced enamel demineralization using permanent human premolars.Materials and methodsA total of 120 extracted sound permanent human premolars were randomly allocated into six groups (n = 20): four iron supplement groups (Fre-in-sol, Ferotonic, Feromin, Ferose), a positive control (S. mutans + 10% sucrose), and a negative control. Artificial caries lesions were induced using S. mutans (strain 6715) with 10% sucrose under anaerobic conditions for 60 days. Enamel demineralization was assessed daily using ICDAS-II visual criteria, tactile examination, and DIAGNOdent laser fluorescence. Incidence data were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests (α = 0.05), while DIAGNOdent values and time-to-onset were summarized descriptively and analyzed statistically where applicable.ResultsAll specimens in the positive control group developed enamel demineralization by day 12 (20/20, 100%). In contrast, Fre-in-sol (2/20, 10%), Ferotonic (3/20, 15%), and Feromin (3/20, 15%) groups showed significantly reduced demineralization incidence compared with the positive control (p < 0.001), with delayed mean onset ranging from 29 to 47 days. The Ferose group demonstrated higher demineralization (16/20, 80%) and did not differ significantly from the positive control (p = 0.053). Mean end-of-study DIAGNOdent readings were lowest for Fre-in-sol (8.4 ± 2.3), Ferotonic (9.7 ± 2.6), and Feromin (9.1 ± 2.4), highest for the positive control (38.6 ± 4.1), and intermediate for Ferose (24.9 ± 3.8). Cavitation was observed only in the positive control (20%) and Ferose (5%) groups.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this in vitro study, ferrous sulfate–based pediatric iron formulations showed greater inhibition of S. mutans–induced enamel demineralization than ferric polymaltose. These findings indicate a formulation-dependent in vitro cariostatic potential and support further investigation under clinical conditions.Clinical significanceCertain pediatric iron supplements showed in vitro cariostatic effect and could potentially provide inhibition effect for caries activity in addition to correcting anemia. However, pediatric clinical studies are required before recommendations regarding formulation selection can be made.

Introduction

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is the most common micronutrient deficiency worldwide and disproportionately affects infants and preschool-aged children. Global reports indicate that nearly half of preschool children are anemic, with iron deficiency accounting for a large proportion of cases and leading to adverse effects on growth, neurocognitive development, behavior, and immunity [1, 2]. This public health burden is particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries and in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations [1, 2].

Early childhood caries (ECC) and severe ECC (S-ECC) are likewise prevalent in young children and share several risk factors with IDA, including poor diet quality, high intake of fermentable carbohydrates, and low socioeconomic status [3–5]. Multiple clinical and epidemiologic studies have demonstrated that children with S-ECC have significantly lower hemoglobin and ferritin levels and are at higher risk of IDA than caries-free peers [3–7]. Conversely, children with IDA are more likely to present with ECC, supporting a bidirectional relationship between nutritional deficiency and oral disease [3–7].

Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, multifactorial disease characterized by repeated cycles of enamel demineralization and remineralization at the tooth–biofilm interface [8, 9]. Among cariogenic microorganisms, Streptococcus mutans is considered a key pathogen due to its acidogenic and aciduric properties and its ability to synthesize extracellular polysaccharides via glucosyltransferase enzymes, which promote biofilm accumulation and sustained low plaque pH [8, 9].

Although fluoride remains the cornerstone of caries prevention and management due to its ability to enhance remineralization, inhibit demineralization, and reduce cariogenic biofilm activity, some heavy metals like iron may offer some sort of caries inhibition effects [10, 11]. Nevertheless, caries development is influenced by multiple interacting biological and environmental factors, and adjunctive strategies that modify enamel susceptibility or bacterial virulence may contribute additional benefit, particularly in high-risk pediatric populations [12].

Interest in the potential cariostatic role of iron has increased over recent decades. In vitro and in vivo studies have shown that iron salts can reduce enamel demineralization, modify bacterial metabolism, and alter physicochemical properties of the tooth surface [3–5]. Experimental models have suggested that specific iron formulations—particularly ferrous salts—may reduce lesion development under cariogenic challenge, while effects appear to vary according to formulation and exposure conditions. In most of that type of experimental models, permanent teeth were used as a standardized in vitro substrate to model enamel demineralization under controlled conditions, and this choice supports methodological consistency rather than direct clinical extrapolation to primary dentition [8–12]. However, the research gap remains that commercially available pediatric iron products (rather than isolated iron salts) have been less consistently evaluated under standardized, long-duration cariogenic conditions using outcomes that are interpretable within a clinically meaningful diagnostic framework. Therefore, conducting in vitro investigations is important to evaluate commercially available pediatric iron formulations, apply a prolonged duration cariogenic challenge, and integrates multiple assessment tools under a standardized Streptococcus mutans protocol.

Mechanistically, formulation-dependent differences are plausible. Ferrous sulfate formulations can release bioavailable ferrous ions that may interact with enamel mineral phases and interfere with S. mutans virulence-related pathways, whereas ferric polymaltose complexes are more strongly chelated, potentially limiting ionic iron availability at the enamel–biofilm interface [8–12]. These differences in valence state, solubility, and bioavailability provide the biological rationale for expecting distinct effects between ferrous sulfate–based and ferric polymaltose–based pediatric formulations [3, 5, 8–12].

Accurate detection of early (non-cavitated) carious lesions is essential for prevention-oriented management. ICDAS-II provides a standardized visual-tactile framework for grading early enamel changes and has demonstrated good validity and reproducibility [13]. DIAGNOdent laser fluorescence provides an adjunctive quantitative signal related to bacterial metabolites and has shown improved performance when interpreted alongside ICDAS-II rather than as a stand-alone method [14–17]. Although laboratory techniques such as microhardness testing, transverse microradiography, and quantitative light-induced fluorescence offer highly quantitative mineral loss outcomes, using ICDAS-II together with DIAGNOdent increases translational relevance by aligning experimental outcomes with tools commonly used to detect early lesions in clinical settings [18, 19].

In the present study, the four pediatric iron supplements were selected because they are commonly prescribed in pediatric practice in Saudi Arabia and represent distinct iron salt types (ferrous sulfate versus ferric polymaltose) in liquid formulations. Extracted permanent premolars were used as a standardized enamel substrate to ensure consistent surface characteristics and comparability across groups in this in vitro model. We also acknowledge that this model uses a single-species S. mutans challenge and does not reproduce the full complexity of the oral environment (multispecies biofilms, salivary flow, pellicle formation, and host factors). Nevertheless, this controlled design is appropriate for isolating formulation-dependent effects on enamel demineralization under standardized cariogenic conditions.

Therefore, the present in vitro study aimed to evaluate the cariostatic potential of four commonly prescribed pediatric iron supplements on Streptococcus mutans–induced enamel demineralization in extracted permanent human premolars using a standardized 60-day cariogenic model. Cariostatic potential was defined as delayed onset and/or reduced incidence of enamel demineralization (ICDAS-II), lower DIAGNOdent fluorescence readings indicative of reduced early mineral change, and reduced cavitation occurrence on tactile examination.

Null hypothesis (H0): There is no difference among the tested pediatric iron formulations and the positive control with respect to the incidence, timing, or severity of enamel demineralization or cavitation under standardized Streptococcus mutans–induced cariogenic conditions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This experimental in vitro study was designed to evaluate the formulation-dependent cariostatic potential of four commercially available pediatric iron supplements against Streptococcus mutans–induced enamel demineralization under standardized cariogenic conditions. The study protocol followed established artificial caries induction models and was structured to allow assessment of both early enamel changes and cavitation development over a prolonged observation period.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using statistical power analysis software (G*Power, version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The calculation was based on detecting differences in the incidence of enamel demineralization among experimental groups, which represented the primary categorical outcome of the study.

A one-way analysis of variance framework was selected for sample size estimation, assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.50), derived from previous in vitro cariology studies evaluating formulation-dependent differences in enamel demineralization under controlled cariogenic challenges. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, with a statistical power of 80% (1 − β = 0.80) and six experimental groups. Based on these parameters, a minimum of 20 specimens per group was required. Accordingly, a total of 120 extracted teeth were included in the study.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. alf.dent-2020037; dated 21 September 2020). Written informed consent for the use of extracted teeth for research purposes was obtained from all donors or their legal guardians. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and ethical guidelines.

Tooth selection, disinfection, and preparation

A total of 120 sound permanent human premolars, extracted for orthodontic reasons, were collected. Permanent premolars were selected to provide a standardized enamel substrate with sufficient thickness and surface uniformity, minimizing structural variability and reducing the risk of premature cavitation during prolonged cariogenic challenge. This selection was methodological and not intended to directly replicate primary dentition.

Immediately after extraction, teeth were cleaned from soft-tissue remnants using ultrasonic scaling and disinfected by immersion in 0.1% thymol solution for 24 h. Subsequently, specimens were stored in 0.9% saline solution at room temperature for no longer than 30 days prior to experimentation. The storage solution was replaced daily to prevent microbial contamination.

Each tooth was embedded in auto polymerizing acrylic resin (self-cure acrylic resin; Acrostone, Cairo, Egypt) up to the cementoenamel junction. A standardized enamel window (approximately 5 × 5 mm) was created on either the mesial or distal surface by coating the remaining crown surface with an acid-resistant transparent nail varnish (clear nail varnish; local commercial product). Specimens were then randomly allocated to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence.

Artificial cariogenic challenge model

Artificial caries lesions were induced using Streptococcus mutans strain 6715. Bacterial cultures were grown in Todd Hewitt Broth (THB; Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) under anaerobic conditions using an anaerobic jar system (anaerobic jar; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at 37 °C for 24 h.

For each specimen, 1.0 mL of bacterial suspension, 100 µL of 10% sucrose solution, and 100 µL of the assigned iron supplement were placed into individual wells of sterile 24-well plates (polystyrene culture plates; Corning, NY, USA). In the positive control group, iron supplements were replaced with sterile distilled water. In the negative control group, bacterial suspension was omitted.

Specimens were positioned vertically with the exposed enamel window fully immersed in the culture medium. The model represented a controlled planktonic cariogenic challenge designed to isolate formulation-dependent effects on enamel demineralization. Culture media were refreshed daily for 60 consecutive days, and all plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C throughout the experimental period. The prolonged 60-day exposure was selected to permit evaluation of both early enamel demineralization and cavitation development under sustained cariogenic stress.

Caries detection and outcome definitions

Visual assessment (ICDAS-II)

Daily visual examination was performed using the International Caries Detection and Assessment System II (ICDAS-II). Teeth were air-dried for 5 s prior to scoring. An ICDAS-II score ≥ 1 was defined as initial enamel demineralization.

Laser fluorescence assessment

Laser fluorescence measurements were performed daily using a DIAGNOdent device (DIAGNOdent 2095; KaVo, Biberach, Germany). The device was calibrated before each session using the manufacturer-supplied ceramic reference standard. Measurements were obtained from the center of the exposed enamel window. A DIAGNOdent value ≥ 10 was considered indicative of early enamel demineralization, consistent with published diagnostic accuracy studies.

Tactile examination

Cavitation was assessed using a blunt-tipped dental explorer under standardized illumination and magnification. A lesion was recorded as cavitated when a definite surface discontinuity was detected.

A tooth was classified as demineralized if it exhibited an ICDAS-II score ≥ 1 and/or a DIAGNOdent value ≥ 10.

Examiner calibration, blinding, and measurement precautions

Two independent examiners performed all visual, laser fluorescence, and tactile assessments. Prior to the experiment, examiners were calibrated using 30 extracted teeth not included in the study. Inter-examiner agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, with values ≥ 0.85 considered excellent.

To minimize measurement-related artefacts, DIAGNOdent assessments were non-destructive and performed at standardized locations. Tactile examination was limited to gentle probing solely to detect cavitation. Examiners were blinded to group allocation throughout the study period.

The primary outcome of the study was the incidence of enamel demineralization (presence/absence), defined based on ICDAS-II and/or DIAGNOdent criteria, and used for inferential statistical comparisons between groups.

Statistical analysis

The incidence of enamel demineralization was predefined as the primary outcome for inferential statistical analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Data distribution and assumptions

Prior to inferential analysis, continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Homogeneity of variances across groups was evaluated using Levene’s test. When assumptions of normality and homogeneity were satisfied, parametric tests were applied. In cases where assumptions were violated, appropriate non-parametric alternatives were considered.

Categorical outcomes

The primary outcome (incidence of enamel demineralization) was analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Cavitation outcomes were analyzed similarly but interpreted cautiously due to low event frequency. When expected cell counts were less than five, Fisher’s exact test was used. Given the low frequency of cavitation events, these outcomes were interpreted cautiously, and inferential conclusions were limited accordingly.

Continuous outcomes (DIAGNOdent values)

DIAGNOdent fluorescence values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons of end-of-study values were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) when parametric assumptions were met. Where significant overall differences were detected, Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons with adjustment for multiple testing. If normality assumptions were not satisfied, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by appropriate post-hoc comparisons was planned.

Time-dependent outcomes

Time to initial enamel demineralization and time to cavitation were defined as the number of days from baseline to first detection according to predefined criteria. Given the limited number of events in certain groups and absence of events in others, time-to-event data were summarized descriptively based on day of first detection. Survival modeling techniques were considered; however, due to sparse event distribution and lack of censoring variability, formal survival curve comparisons were not performed. This approach was deemed appropriate for the exploratory nature of the in vitro model.

Multiple comparisons

When multiple pairwise comparisons were conducted, adjustment for multiplicity was applied using conservative post-hoc procedures to reduce the risk of Type I error.

Results

Time to initial enamel demineralization

Initial enamel demineralization was defined as an ICDAS-II score ≥ 1 and/or a DIAGNOdent value ≥ 10, as specified in the Methods. Time to initial demineralization was recorded as the number of days from baseline to first detection.

The positive control group demonstrated the earliest onset of demineralization, with all specimens affected within the first 12 days. In contrast, specimens exposed to ferrous sulfate–based formulations showed delayed onset, with mean first-detection days ranging between 29 and 47 days (Table 2). The ferric polymaltose (Ferose) group exhibited earlier onset compared with the ferrous sulfate groups.

Cavitation incidence and time to cavitation

Cavitation was observed in 5 of 120 specimens (4.2%) during the 60-day experimental period. The highest cavitation incidence occurred in the positive control group (4/20, 20%), followed by the Ferose group (1/20, 5%). No cavitation was detected in the Fre-in-sol, Ferotonic, Feromin, or negative control groups (Table 5).

Agreement between visual and fluorescence assessments

Qualitative concordance was observed between ICDAS-II visual scores and DIAGNOdent fluorescence measurements. Groups with low visual demineralization incidence correspondingly demonstrated low fluorescence values, whereas higher visual lesion incidence was associated with elevated DIAGNOdent readings (Tables 2 and 4).

Discussion

The present in vitro study was designed to test the predefined null hypothesis (H0) that no differences exist among pediatric iron supplement formulations with respect to enamel demineralization outcomes under standardized cariogenic conditions. The observed formulation-dependent differences in demineralization onset, incidence, and DIAGNOdent fluorescence values represent deviations from H0 within an in vitro context. However, these findings should be interpreted as experimental observations rather than evidence sufficient to reject H0 in a clinical setting, and they require confirmation through in vivo and clinical investigations [19, 20].

Within the limitations of the experimental model, ferrous sulfate–based formulations were associated with delayed onset and reduced incidence of enamel demineralization compared with the ferric polymaltose formulation and the positive control. These outcomes were consistently supported by both visual ICDAS-II assessment and laser fluorescence measurements. The concordance between these diagnostic approaches was qualitative and supportive, enhancing internal consistency of the findings, but does not imply quantitative diagnostic agreement. Similar formulation-dependent trends have been reported in previous experimental studies examining iron–enamel interactions under cariogenic challenge [20–24].

Several biologically plausible mechanisms may explain the observed differences between iron formulations; however, these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study and must therefore be regarded as hypothetical. Prior research suggests that ferrous ions may interact with enamel hydroxyapatite, reduce enamel solubility, or interfere with Streptococcus mutans metabolism and glucosyltransferase-mediated extracellular polysaccharide synthesis. In contrast, ferric polymaltose complexes exhibit strong chelation, potentially limiting ionic iron availability at the enamel–biofilm interface. While supported by existing literature, these mechanistic explanations remain speculative in the context of the current findings [25–27].

The in vitro nature of the study inherently limits its clinical realism. Key intraoral factors—including salivary flow and buffering capacity, pellicle formation, multispecies biofilms, dietary variability, host immune responses, and oral clearance—were not replicated. Additionally, a standardized enamel substrate was used to enhance reproducibility, although this does not fully reflect the structural and compositional characteristics of primary teeth typically affected by early childhood caries. The use of undiluted iron formulations and a prolonged 60-day exposure period represent an extreme experimental condition designed to reveal formulation-dependent effects rather than simulate real-world clinical exposure [27–30].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The use of permanent premolars as a standardized enamel substrate enhances methodological consistency but does not fully represent the structural characteristics of primary teeth typically affected by early childhood caries. The experimental model employed a single-species Streptococcus mutans biofilm and therefore does not replicate the complexity of multispecies oral biofilms present in vivo. In addition, the use of undiluted iron formulations under prolonged exposure conditions represents a maximum-effect experimental model rather than typical clinical scenarios. Quantitative assessment methods for mineral loss, such as microhardness testing or transverse microradiography, were not included, and microbiological analyses were not performed. Furthermore, the relatively low number of cavitation events limited the statistical power for inferential analysis of cavitation outcomes. Collectively, these factors restrict the interpretation of the findings to controlled in vitro conditions and highlight the need for further in situ and clinical investigations.

The diagnostic approach employed in this study also warrants critical consideration. ICDAS-II and DIAGNOdent were selected due to their clinical familiarity and ability to detect early enamel changes; however, both methods have recognized limitations. Visual-tactile assessment is subject to examiner interpretation, while DIAGNOdent values depend on fluorescence thresholds and cannot directly quantify subsurface mineral loss. Well-established quantitative techniques such as surface or cross-sectional microhardness testing, transverse microradiography, and quantitative light-induced fluorescence provide more precise mineral loss measurements and lesion depth characterization. Incorporation of these methodologies in future studies would strengthen mechanistic interpretation and quantitative validation of formulation-dependent effects [31–35].

In addition to purely laboratory-based approaches, in situ experimental models offer an important intermediate step between in vitro studies and randomized clinical trials. In situ designs allow enamel specimens to be exposed to the natural oral environment, including saliva, pellicle formation, and multispecies biofilms, while maintaining experimental control. Previous in situ studies evaluating cariogenic and preventive interventions have demonstrated the value of this approach in bridging laboratory findings with clinical relevance. Future research assessing pediatric iron formulations using in situ models may provide more clinically meaningful insight prior to large-scale in vivo trials [36–42].

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It evaluated commercially available pediatric iron supplements rather than isolated iron salts, employed a prolonged and standardized cariogenic challenge, and combined clinically oriented diagnostic methods to assess formulation-dependent effects. This design enhances translational relevance and provides a structured framework for hypothesis generation regarding the interaction between pediatric iron supplementation and caries-related processes [10, 11].

From an educational and translational perspective, the findings support teaching objectives in preventive dentistry and pediatric oral health by illustrating how formulation differences may influence enamel responses under cariogenic stress. The study also underscores the importance of interdisciplinary awareness between dental and medical practitioners, without implying that formulation choices can currently be optimized based on in vitro evidence alone. Ultimately, the concept of a potential “dual benefit” of iron supplementation—addressing anemia while influencing caries development—should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires validation through in situ models, in vivo studies, and well-designed randomized clinical trials [43–50].

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro model, pediatric iron supplements demonstrated formulation-dependent differences in their influence on Streptococcus mutans–induced enamel demineralization. Ferrous sulfate–based formulations exhibited greater in vitro cariostatic potential under standardized experimental conditions, whereas the ferric polymaltose formulation showed limited protective effects. These findings address the study aim by indicating that the chemical form of iron may influence enamel responses during cariogenic challenges.

The observed formulation-dependent effects are restricted to controlled laboratory conditions and do not constitute clinical evidence. Potential biological explanations and broader clinical implications should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Further in situ, in vivo, and clinical investigations are required to determine whether these in vitro observations translate into clinically meaningful outcomes and to establish their relevance for pediatric oral health and anemia management.
