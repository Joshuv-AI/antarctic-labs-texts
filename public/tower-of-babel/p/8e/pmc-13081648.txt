Optimized genetic tracers for viral mediated neuronal projection mapping
Authors: Jennifer N. Dulin, Li Ye, Teresa Grider, Anael Rizzo, Ephron S. Rosenzweig, Janet Weber, Charu Ramakrishnan, Brian Hsueh, Karl Deisseroth, Mark H. Tuszynski, Daniel Gibbs, Gunnar H. D. Poplawski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mapping fine axonal projections with high sensitivity remains a major challenge in neuroanatomy. Here, we developed Codon Optimized Membrane Embedded Tracers (COMET), a family of genetically encoded, membrane-anchored fluorescent proteins optimized for adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery. COMET tracers—based on codon-optimized SuperFolder GFP (gCOMET) and TdTomato (rCOMET) fused to an H-RAS CaaX prenylation motif—enable robust plasma membrane targeting and enhance fluorescent signal localization to fine-caliber axons. In vivo, COMET tracers significantly outperformed conventional soluble fluorophores and chemical tracers in detecting corticospinal tract (CST) projections, revealing previously underappreciated collateralization and fine arborizations. Following spinal cord injury, COMET provided superior sensitivity for detecting regenerating and sprouting axons compared with biotinylated dextran amine (BDA). Importantly, COMET tracers retained bright native fluorescence after tissue clearing by CLARITY, enabling volumetric imaging of long-range projections without antibody amplification. COMET expands the available toolkit for high-resolution connectomics and regeneration studies, offering a versatile platform for sensitive, scalable, and cell-type-specific neuronal tracing in intact and injured mammalian nervous systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00894-z.

Introduction

Neuroanatomical tracing using light microscopy has been central to defining the organization of mammalian neural circuits connectivity [1, 2]. Recombinant adeno-associated viruses have become especially powerful tools for projection mapping because they enable precise genetic control of tracer expression, compatibility with cell-type-specific promoters [3–5], recombinase-dependent systems [6–8], and intersectional labeling strategies [9, 10]. As a result, AAV-based tracers have largely supplanted traditional chemical methods such as biotinylated dextran amine [11, 12], cholera toxin subunit B [13, 14], or plant lectins [15], and are now widely used for circuit mapping and regeneration studies across the mammalian nervous system.

Despite these advantages, a persistent limitation of conventional AAV-delivered cytosolic fluorescent proteins is their reduced sensitivity for labeling fine-caliber axons, distal collaterals, and sparse projections. Long-range axons often appear discontinuous or weakly labeled, and small-diameter branches can be missed entirely, leading to systematic underestimation of projection density and anatomical complexity. These limitations are particularly problematic in systems such as the corticospinal tract, where axons are long, thin, and sparsely distributed, and where regeneration or collateral sprouting after injury occurs at low density.

To address these challenges, membrane-targeted fluorescent tracers have gained increasing popularity in recent years. By anchoring fluorophores to the plasma membrane through farnesylation or related motifs, these approaches concentrate fluorescence along the axonal surface and improve signal-to-noise. Membrane-targeted AAV tracers have been applied in diverse contexts, including peripheral and enteric nervous system mapping using systemically delivered AAV-PHP.S hSyn1 tdTomato-farnesylated vectors [16], autonomic and cardiac circuit reconstruction using AAV-delivered farnesylated tdTomato reporters [17], and openly shared viral tracer workflows that leverage membrane localization for enhanced fiber delineation [18]. Collectively, these studies illustrate that membrane-targeted tracers have become an increasingly adopted component of the modern neuroanatomical toolkit.

However, despite their growing use, membrane-targeted tracers have rarely been subjected to direct, systematic comparison against conventional cytosolic fluorescent proteins or classical chemical tracers across demanding anatomical and injury paradigms. In most prior work, membrane-targeted reporters were employed as enabling tools rather than rigorously benchmarked tracing modalities. As a result, it has remained unclear to what extent membrane localization quantitatively improves detection sensitivity, whether it alters axonal transport properties, or whether enhanced sensitivity merely improves visualization of known anatomy rather than revealing previously inaccessible biological features.

Here, we set out to address this gap by developing and systematically evaluating Codon Optimized Membrane Embedded Tracers, or COMET, a family of AAV-compatible, membrane-anchored fluorescent proteins optimized for high-sensitivity projection mapping. COMET tracers are based on codon-optimized SuperFolder GFP (gCOMET) [19] and TdTomato (rCOMET) [20] fused to a C-terminal H-RAS CaaX prenylation motif [21], enabling robust plasma membrane localization while preserving efficient axonal transport.

Using direct side-by-side comparisons within the same animals and, in some cases, within the same neurons, we demonstrate that COMET tracers substantially outperform cytosolic fluorescent proteins and biotinylated dextran amine in detecting fine-caliber corticospinal axons, distal collaterals, and sparse regenerating fibers after spinal cord injury [22–25]. Importantly, the increased sensitivity of COMET tracers does not simply amplify known projections, but enables identification of new and previously elusive biology. Most notably, COMET consistently reveals the ventral corticospinal tract in intact adult mice, a pathway whose existence and extent have remained difficult to establish using conventional anterograde tracing methods but which has been suggested by transgenic labeling approaches [26–28]. These findings demonstrate that improved tracer sensitivity can uncover genuine anatomical features that are otherwise missed.

In addition, COMET tracers retain strong native fluorescence following whole-tissue clearing using CLARITY-based methods (such as CLARITY [29] or iDISCO [30]), enabling volumetric imaging of long-range projections without antibody amplification. Together, these results establish COMET as a quantitatively benchmarked, membrane-targeted tracer platform that enhances anatomical sensitivity, enables discovery of previously underappreciated corticospinal organization, and improves the accuracy of regeneration studies. By directly comparing membrane-anchored and conventional tracers across intact, injured, and cleared tissue, this work expands the experimental toolkit for high-resolution connectomics and neural repair research.

Materials and methods

Molecular biology

DNA encoding Codon Optimized Membrane Embedded Tracer (COMET) constructs were synthesized de novo by Genscript (Genscript Inc., NJ, USA) using the OptimumGene algorithm for human codon optimization. This algorithm minimizes non-functional repetitive sequences, cryptic splice sites, and CpG motifs that can impair expression. All COMET open reading frames were fused in-frame to the C-terminal H-RAS farnesylation motif (KLNPPDESGPGCMSCKCVLS) for plasma membrane anchoring [31], and a T2A ribosomal skip sequence for bicistronic expression [32].

Green fluorescent COMET constructs (gCOMET) were derived from SuperFolder GFP [19], and red variants (rCOMET) from TdTomato or dTomato [20]. Constructs were subcloned into a pAAV–CAGGS–MCS backbone for constitutive expression or into a pAAV-CAGGS-DIO-EGFP plasmid for Cre-dependent delivery. All viral vectors were produced by the Salk GT3 Core or the University of Pennsylvania Vector Core (AAV9-CamkII-Cre), and titers were determined by qPCR.

Primary neuron culture and transduction

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from E18 Sprague–Dawley rats and plated on poly-D-lysine-coated coverslips at 100,000 cells/cm2. Cells were maintained in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27, GlutaMAX, and penicillin/streptomycin. At DIV 5, neurons were transduced with AAV8-rCOMET at an MOI of ~1 × 105 GC/cell. Imaging was performed at DIV 12 (7 days post-transduction) to assess membrane localization and axonal transport (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Movie 2).

Immunocytochemistry and confocal imaging

Neurons expressing rCOMET were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and immunostained with anti-synaptophysin (1:500; Synaptic Systems), followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies. Coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold and imaged on an Olympus Fluoview 1000 confocal microscope. Colocalization with synaptophysin was assessed via line scan analysis and puncta overlap (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In vivo AAV delivery and wire knife lesion model

Adult female Sprague–Dawley rats (200–250 g) and C57BL/6 J mice (8–12 weeks) were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a stereotactic frame. AAV8 constructs were pressure-injected into motor cortex (AP: + 1.0 mm, ML: ± 2.0 mm, DV: − 1.5 mm) using a Picospritzer III (Parker Hannifin) and beveled glass micropipettes (~20–30 µm tip diameter). Injection volumes were 800–1000 nL/site and delivered at a rate of approximately 200 nL/min.

For spinal cord injury experiments (Fig. 2A), a dorsal column lesion at C4 was made using a wire knife (McHugh Milieux), inserted laterally through a dural slit, and retracted vertically to transect the dorsal columns including the corticospinal tract (CST). Immediately following lesioning, 300,000 neural progenitor cells derived from E14 F344 rat embryonic spinal cord in 2 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were grafted into the lesion cavity using a Hamilton syringe.

Biotinylated dextran amine tracing

For comparison with viral tracers, corticospinal neurons were labeled using biotinylated dextran amine (BDA; 10 kDa; Invitrogen). BDA was injected stereotactically into the motor cortex using the same coordinates, injection volumes, and surgical procedures as those used for AAV delivery. Tissue processing and visualization were performed in parallel with viral tracing experiments, and BDA-labeled axons were detected using streptavidin-conjugated fluorophores.

In vivo AAV injections for CLARITY

To assess tracer performance following tissue clearing, C57BL/6 J mice received AAV5-gCOMET and AAV5-EYFP (or AAV5-rCOMET and AAV5-TdTomato) injections into contralateral lateral hypothalamic nuclei. Stereotactic coordinates were AP: −1.5 mm, ML: ± 1.0 mm, relative to bregma and DV: −5.2 mm. Each site received 500–800 nL virus via Picospritzer III. Mice were perfused 3–4 weeks later for CLARITY processing and native fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3).

Tissue processing and immunohistochemistry

Animals were transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains and spinal cords were post-fixed overnight, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and sectioned at 50–100 µm using a Leica vibratome. BDA was detected using streptavidin–Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI or NeuN as appropriate.

For tissue clearing, samples were embedded in hydrogel monomer solution (4% acrylamide, 0.05% bisacrylamide, 4% PFA, and 0.25% VA-044 in PBS) and polymerized at 37 °C. Passive clearing was carried out in 4% SDS at 37 °C for 7–10 days. Cleared samples were washed thoroughly and mounted in RIMS for native fluorescence imaging.

Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) for anti-GFP detection

For experiments shown in Fig. 1C, fluorescence signal was enhanced using a tyramide signal amplification (TSA) protocol to maximize sensitivity for GFP detection. All steps were performed on free-floating tissue sections at room temperature unless otherwise indicated. Sections were first washed three times for 10 min each in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) and subjected to methanol antigen retrieval (100% methanol, 30 min at room temperature), followed by three additional TBS washes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation for 30 min in freshly prepared 0.6% H2O2 (20 µl 30% H2O2 per 1 ml TBS). Sections were then blocked in TBS-T (0.25% Triton X-100) containing 5% normal serum or BSA for 3 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C in TBS-T + 5% serum/BSA. For anti-GFP detection, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-GFP (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #5568S). After three 10-min TBS washes, tissue was incubated for 2–3 h at room temperature with biotinylated donkey anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:333; Jackson ImmunoResearch) prepared in 5% serum/TBS-T. After washing, sections were transferred to freshly prepared avidin–biotin complex (ABC) solution (Vector Labs ABC kit) consisting of 4 µl reagent A + 4 µl reagent B per 1 ml TBS-T and incubated 30 min on a gentle orbital shaker. Tissue was then washed three times in TBS and incubated for 30 min in biotinylated tyramide (1:2500) diluted in freshly prepared buffer (6 µl 30% H2O2 in 20 ml TBS). Note: biotinyl-tyramide was vortexed extensively before and after dilution to ensure proper suspension. Sections were then washed four times for 5 min each in TBS and incubated with streptavidin-conjugated fluorophore (e.g., Streptavidin-Alexa 488, 1:500; Thermo Fisher) together with secondary antibodies for additional targets where applicable, all diluted in TBS-T + 5% serum/BSA. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000). After final washes (3 × 10 min TBS), tissue was mounted onto glass slides and coverslipped with Mowiol 4–88 mounting medium. This amplification method provided a strong and uniform fluorescence signal along axons, allowing direct comparison between cytosolic and membrane-targeted GFP constructs independent of intrinsic fluorophore brightness.

Imaging and quantification

Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus Fluoview 1000 confocal microscope or Leica SP8 for cleared tissues. Axon density, diameter, and collateralization were quantified using Fiji/ImageJ in defined ROIs spanning spinal segments. CST axons were analyzed across transverse or sagittal sections for collateral density and regeneration.

Live transport dynamics were assessed in rCOMET-expressing neurons by time-lapse imaging (1 fps for 10 min) and analyzed by kymograph using KymoToolBox (FIJI). Mean anterograde vesicle velocity was 0.97 ± 0.31 µm s−1 (n = 72 vesicles). All data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism using unpaired t-tests or two-way ANOVA with post-hoc correction. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Membrane-targeted COMET tracers enhance axonal detection and tracing sensitivity

Membrane-tethered COMET tracers outperform both cytosolic fluorescent proteins and conventional chemical tracers for axonal labeling. By enhancing fluorescent signal localization to the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Movie 1), COMET enables more sensitive detection of fine axonal processes, reveals previously underestimated patterns of corticospinal connectivity, and improves quantitative mapping of projection density across the spinal cord.

To enhance axonal labeling, we fused codon-optimized Superfolder GFP (gCOMET) or TdTomato (rCOMET) to an H-RAS CaaX prenylation motif, enabling plasma membrane targeting via trans-Golgi trafficking (Fig. 1A). A soluble EGFP construct lacking membrane tethering served as a control. COMET tracers show equivalent axonal transport and membrane localization

To validate transport fidelity and membrane localization, we coinjected AAV8-gCOMET and AAV8-rCOMET into the same motor cortex hemisphere. Dual-color imaging of cervical spinal cord sections revealed extensive colocalization of CST axons (yellow signal), with ~99% of fibers colabeled by both tracers and only rare singly labeled axons (Fig. 1B, arrowheads). These results demonstrate that gCOMET and rCOMET exhibit comparable axonal transport kinetics and membrane targeting in vivo.

This equivalence allows the tracers to be used interchangeably or in combination for multi-color projection mapping without introducing labeling bias.

Membrane-anchored COMET enhances CST collateral detection compared with soluble EGFP

To evaluate the effect of membrane tethering on axonal labeling sensitivity, we compared AAV8-gCOMET and AAV8-EGFP injected at equal titers into contralateral motor cortices of the same animals. Transverse spinal cord sections from cervical to sacral levels revealed markedly more extensive CST collateralization with gCOMET, particularly in intermediate and ventral gray matter zones (Fig. 1C).

Quantification showed a 2–5-fold increase in labeled axonal profiles across all spinal levels with gCOMET, while the diameter of main tract axons remained unchanged. These results highlight that membrane anchoring substantially improves detection of fine CST collaterals (Fig. 1C, #/*) without altering axonal morphology and labeling intensity within the main tract (Fig. 1C, +). It is likely that the much-improved detection of long-range distal axonal projections is owing to active axonal transport of the COMET tracers compared with passive diffusion of the EGFP cytosolic tracer. To test this, we examined rCOMET transport along a rapidly moving vesicle population (Supplementary Movie 2), and found that rCOMET colocalizes with synaptophysin (Supplementary Fig. 2) and moves within axons at speeds (measured by kymograph analysis) consistent with kinesin dependent fast anterograde axonal transport (0.97 μm s−1 ± 0.31 μm s−1).

Importantly, gCOMET labeling reveals a far denser and more widespread pattern of CST innervation than previously appreciated, suggesting that earlier studies using cytosolic tracers significantly underestimated the complexity of corticospinal connectivity.

rCOMET outperforms conventional BDA tracing for detecting CST collaterals

To directly benchmark COMET tracers against traditional chemical tracers, we injected AAV8-rCOMET and BDA into contralateral motor cortices of rats (Fig. 1D). In cervical spinal cord sections, both tracers labeled the CST main tract (Fig. 1D, +); however, rCOMET revealed substantially more extensive branching into the intermediate and ventral gray matter (Fig. 1D, #/*). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that rCOMET detected twice as many CST axons in the main tract compared with BDA (Fig. 1D, + , bottom row left panel). Quantification of axon diameter shows that rCOMET significantly enhances detection of fine caliber axons compared with BDA (bottom row, middle panel). This labeling of small caliber axons results in greater labeling of collaterals it the ventral gray matter (Fig. 1D, #/*) which is quantified in the bottom row, right panel (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons). While rCOMET labeled a greater total number of CST axons than BDA within the main tract, large-caliber dorsal column axons were more prominently detected with BDA, whereas rCOMET revealed a substantial population of fine-caliber axons and collateral projections that were largely missed by BDA. Importantly, normalization to main tract axon counts (data not shown) did not abolish the enhanced detection observed with rCOMET, indicating that COMET improves sensitivity for fine axons rather than simply increasing the number of labeled corticospinal neurons. These results highlight the enhanced sensitivity of membrane-anchored COMET tracers for detecting fine axonal processes, and suggest that chemical tracers may systematically underestimate the density and complexity of long-range corticospinal projections. Because the goal of this study was to compare axonal detection sensitivity rather than viral transduction efficiency per se, we did not quantify labeled corticospinal neuron somata in the motor cortex. Instead, we used the cross-sectional area and axon counts of the CST main tract as downstream quantitative proxies for the size of the labeled corticospinal neuron population. Using these measures, we observed comparable main tract labeling between gCOMET and soluble GFP, and although rCOMET labeled a greater total number of CST axons than BDA, normalization to main tract axon counts did not abolish the enhanced detection of fine-caliber axons and collateral projections. These analyses indicate that the increased axon detection achieved with COMET reflects improved sensitivity for fine and distal axonal processes rather than differences in infection rate or neuronal labeling.

COMET enables high-sensitivity detection of regenerating CST axons after spinal cord injury

To assess whether COMET tracers improve visualization of regenerating axons, we used a cervical spinal cord injury and grafting model [22–25]. Adult rats received dorsal column lesions at the C4 level, followed by implantation of E14-derived neural progenitor cells into the lesion site (Fig. 2A). For direct within-cell comparison, AAV8-rCOMET (red) and BDA (green) were premixed and coinjected into the same motor cortex hemisphere, labeling corticospinal neurons with both tracers simultaneously. This approach allowed us to evaluate the relative sensitivity of each tracer for detecting axonal regeneration into the graft environment. rCOMET reveals extensive CST regeneration and collateral sprouting after spinal cord injury

Sagittal spinal cord sections through the lesion site revealed marked differences in tracer performance. While both rCOMET and BDA labeled CST axons entering the graft, rCOMET consistently detected far more regenerating fibers, including fine axonal arbors extending deep into the graft core (Fig. 2B). High-magnification views highlighted distinct regions: * = the graft–host interface, where axons regenerate into the grafted cells; ** = CST axon sprouting into spared host gray matter above the lesion; and *** = distal regions within the graft core. rCOMET-labeled axons were more abundant, exhibited finer branching, and extended over longer distances compared to BDA-labeled fibers. These results indicate that rCOMET enables more comprehensive detection of both regenerating axons penetrating into the graft and collateral sprouting into intact gray matter after injury.

High-resolution imaging reveals a hidden population of regenerating CST axons missed by BDA

High-resolution imaging of the graft clarified a critical difference between tracers. As expected, many CST axons labeled with BDA appeared to retract from the lesion site, terminating near the graft–host interface with retraction bulb-like morphologies (Fig. 2C arrow heads). This is consistent with the known tendency of CST axons to withdraw after spinal cord injury. However, rCOMET labeling of the same axonal population revealed a dense network of fine-caliber fibers extending along the lesion border and penetrating deep into the graft (Fig. 2C, stars). These axons were largely undetected by BDA, indicating that conventional chemical tracers may miss an entire subpopulation of regenerating axons.

This discrepancy suggests that rCOMET provides access to regenerative events that are otherwise obscured—either due to the limited uptake and transport of BDA in remodeling axons, or its reduced ability to resolve fine axonal arbors. Membrane-anchored fluorescent tracers thus offer a more accurate and sensitive readout of axonal regeneration following CNS injury.

rCOMET significantly improves quantification of regenerating axons in spinal cord grafts

To validate these qualitative observations, we quantified CST axon profiles within the graft across matched sagittal sections [33]. Axons labeled with rCOMET were significantly more abundant than those labeled with BDA, confirming that rCOMET provides superior sensitivity for detecting regenerating fibers (Fig. 2D).

Across all animals (n = 3), rCOMET yielded ~ten-fold higher axon densities within the graft compared with BDA (****p < 0.0001, unpaired t-test). These findings underscore the limitations of conventional tracers and establish rCOMET as a more accurate tool for quantifying axonal regeneration in CNS repair models.

COMET tracers reveal elusive CST pathways in intact adult mice

Beyond improving detection of regenerating axons in injury models, COMET tracers also uncovered previously underappreciated CST anatomy in the uninjured spinal cord. While the existence of a ventral corticospinal tract (vCST) in adult mice has remained controversial due to inconsistent findings across tracing methods, rCOMET provided robust and reproducible labeling of vCST axons following unilateral injection into the motor cortex (Fig. 2E). rCOMET-labeled axons were clearly detected in the ventral white matter, crossing the midline and extending longitudinally across spinal segments. This labeling pattern mirrors previous findings using CST-YFP mice [26], but had not been reliably recapitulated with conventional chemical or cytosolic tracers. These results further illustrate the sensitivity of COMET tracers for revealing anatomically subtle or poorly visualized axonal populations in the intact CNS.

COMET tracers retain native fluorescence after clearing and outperform cytosolic tracers

To assess whether membrane targeting improves tracer performance in cleared tissue, we compared COMET tracers with conventional cytosolic fluorescent proteins after whole-brain clearing. AAV5-gCOMET and AAV5-EYFP were injected into contralateral hypothalamic nuclei, as were AAV5-rCOMET and AAV5-TdTomato in separate animals. Following a modified CLARITY protocol, coronal brain sections were imaged without antibody amplification (Fig. 3). In both green and red channels, COMET tracers retained substantially stronger native fluorescence compared with their cytosolic counterparts. gCOMET-labeled neurons and axons exhibited sharp membrane delineation and robust signal throughout hypothalamic projections, whereas EYFP signal appeared dim and diffuse (Fig. 3A, B). Similarly, rCOMET maintained bright labeling of fine-caliber axons and somatic membranes, while TdTomato labeling was markedly weaker and less continuous (Fig. 3C, D). Discussion

Here, we present Codon Optimized Membrane Embedded Tracers (COMET), a family of genetically encoded, membrane-targeted fluorescent reporters for high-sensitivity neuronal projection mapping in the mammalian central nervous system. By anchoring codon-optimized fluorophores to the plasma membrane, COMET tracers substantially outperform conventional cytosolic fluorescent proteins and classical chemical tracers such as biotinylated dextran amine (BDA) [11, 12] in detecting fine-caliber axons, distal collaterals, and sparse projections. Across intact tissue, spinal cord injury models, and cleared preparations, COMET consistently revealed anatomical features that are underestimated or missed entirely by traditional tracing approaches.

The improved axonal labeling achieved with COMET arises from membrane embedding and enhanced retention of fluorescence along fine axonal processes, rather than from intrinsic fluorophore brightness alone. In experiments such as Fig. 1C, where detection relied on tyramide signal amplification and anti-GFP immunostaining, differences in intrinsic molecular brightness were not a limiting factor. Instead, restricting fluorescence to the plasma membrane increased signal-to-noise along thin axons and distal branches, enabling more continuous and complete visualization of long-range projections. While newer fluorescent proteins with exceptionally high intrinsic brightness have been developed, their suitability for long-range axonal transport and regeneration paradigms following in vivo viral delivery has not been systematically evaluated, and increased brightness alone is unlikely to overcome limitations imposed by cytosolic dilution in fine-caliber axons.

A key finding of this study is that enhanced tracer sensitivity does not simply amplify known anatomy, but can uncover genuine biological features that have remained difficult to detect using conventional methods. Most notably, COMET tracers consistently and reproducibly revealed a ventral corticospinal tract (vCST) population in intact adult mice. Although prior transgenic approaches have suggested the existence of ventral CST projections [26–28], these fibers have not been reliably detected using standard anterograde chemical tracers or cytosolic fluorescent proteins. The ability of COMET to robustly label this pathway underscores the importance of tracer design in resolving subtle or controversial aspects of circuit anatomy.

In spinal cord injury and neural progenitor grafting models, COMET tracers provided markedly improved sensitivity for detecting regenerating and sprouting corticospinal axons compared with BDA. Membrane-targeted labeling revealed a dense population of fine-caliber axons extending into graft tissue and collateralizing within spared gray matter—structures that were largely undetected by chemical tracing. These findings suggest that conventional tracers may systematically underestimate the extent of axonal regeneration and plasticity after injury, potentially contributing to underappreciation of regenerative responses in prior studies [26–28].

COMET tracers also retained strong native fluorescence following CLARITY-based tissue clearing [29], enabling high-resolution volumetric imaging without antibody amplification. Compared with cytosolic fluorophores, membrane-anchored COMET reporters preserved sharp delineation of axonal and somatic membranes in cleared tissue, facilitating visualization of fine processes across large volumes. While membrane-targeted fluorophores have been used in conjunction with tissue clearing in previous work [30], the preservation of bright native fluorescence and its direct comparison with cytosolic reporters across matched conditions is explicitly demonstrated here.

As with other AAV-based tracing strategies, COMET vectors may be taken up by fibers of passage, a limitation shared with viral and some chemical tracers. Careful selection of injection sites, injection volumes, and promoters therefore remains essential for minimizing unintended labeling and for accurate interpretation of projection patterns. In addition, because COMET tracers are based on GFP and TdTomato backbones, they are compatible with antibody-based detection strategies, including immunoperoxidase labeling. This raises the possibility of generating permanent nonfluorescent staining or integrating COMET with additional histochemical and clearing workflows, although such applications were not directly explored in the present study.

Although COMET tracers were validated here in mice and rats, their design relies on AAV vectors and codon-optimized fluorophores that are widely used across mammalian species. Differences in axonal transport efficiency and tracer performance across species have been reported for some tracing approaches, and direct validation of COMET in large animals and nonhuman primates will be an important direction for future work.

Because COMET tracers are encoded at the transgene level, they are readily compatible with alternative AAV capsids. In principle, combining COMET reporters with engineered BBB-penetrant capsids such as PHP.B/eB or cross-species capsids such as AAV9-VCAP-102 could enable widespread or whole-brain labeling of long-range axonal projections, expanding the applicability of this approach beyond focal stereotactic injections.

The COMET system can also be integrated with a broad range of genetic targeting strategies, including cell-type-specific promoters [3–5], recombinase-dependent expression [6–8], intersectional labeling schemes [9, 10], and retrograde viral delivery [34]. Together with their enhanced sensitivity for fine axonal processes, these features make COMET tracers well-suited for studies of long-range connectivity, axonal regeneration, and circuit remodeling, where accurate detection of sparse and distal projections is critical.
