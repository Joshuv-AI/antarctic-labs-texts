Ruptured Anterior Communicating Artery Aneurysm Associated with von Willebrand Disease Treated Using the Woven EndoBridge Device: An Illustrated Case
Authors: Yuki Koike, Koichiro Shindo, Hideki Endo, Tatsuya Ogino, Hirohiko Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveVon Willebrand disease (VWD) is an inherited bleeding disorder caused by quantitative or qualitative deficiencies in von Willebrand factor (VWF). Reports of intracranial aneurysms complicated by VWD, particularly ruptured aneurysms, are rare. Optimal perioperative management during endovascular treatment in this patient group remains unclear.Case PresentationHere, we report the case of a female in her 50s with type 1 VWD who presented with sudden headache and nausea. Imaging revealed a subarachnoid hemorrhage due to a ruptured anterior communicating artery (Acom) aneurysm. Considering the high risk of surgical bleeding, endovascular therapy was administered. DSA demonstrated a 5.8 × 4.4-mm aneurysm, and a Woven EndoBridge (W-EB) SL device (7 × 3 mm; Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was successfully deployed. Periprocedural hemostatic management was performed in consultation with hematology specialists, and a factor VIII/VWF concentrate was administered post-procedure to maintain VWF, which was continued for 3 days post-procedure (intravenous bolus infusions of 2000 units). Antiplatelet therapy was not administered. The postoperative course was uneventful, and no intracranial or access site bleeding occurred. Follow-up angiography on postoperative day 7 revealed thrombus formation within the device and no inflow into the aneurysmal bleb. At 4 months postoperatively, the presence of minor residual flow within the W-EB (Bicêtre Occlusion Scale Score [BOSS] grade 1) was confirmed, and its expected complete occlusion was observed during subsequent follow-ups.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that W-EB placement can be safely performed in patients with VWD and may reduce hemorrhagic risk by avoiding the need for antiplatelet therapy.

Introduction

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is a bleeding disorder caused by qualitative or quantitative deficiency of the von Willebrand factor (VWF). This factor plays 2 important roles in the hemostatic mechanism as it facilitates platelet adhesion and carries coagulation factor VIII (FVIII). Various bleeding symptoms can occur when the VWF is quantitatively deficient or exhibits qualitative abnormalities.1) The estimated prevalence ranges of VWD from 23 to 110 per million people (0.0023%–0.01%).2)

There are only a few case reports of ruptured or unruptured aneurysms complicated by VWD. Optimal perioperative management during endovascular treatment (EVT) in this patient group thus remains unclear.

Here, we present a rare case of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) secondary to a ruptured anterior communicating artery (Acom) aneurysm in a patient with VWD, who was successfully treated with the placement of a Woven EndoBridge (W-EB) device (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Case Presentation

A female in her 50s suddenly developed headache and nausea and was transported to a nearby neurosurgical hospital. On arrival, the patient was alert, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15 and exhibited no focal neurological deficits (World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies [WFNS] grade 1). MRI and CT revealed an SAH (Fisher group 3) (Fig. 1), while MRA confirmed the presence of an Acom aneurysm.

CT showing diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage of the suprasellar cistern (Fisher group 3).

The patient had a medical history of type 1 VWD, which was diagnosed 4 years previously after prolonged bleeding following tooth extraction. Since then, the patient has received FVIII/VWF concentrate (Confact F; Kaketsuken, Kumamoto, Japan) before invasive examinations or procedures with bleeding risks. She did not smoke or drink alcohol, and her family medical history was unremarkable.

Because of her VWD, the patient was considered to be at high risk for surgical complications and was referred to our institution for further treatment. DSA revealed an Acom aneurysm measuring 5.8 × 4.4 mm, with a dome-to-neck ratio of 1.35 (Fig. 2A and 2B). Owing to the high risk of hemorrhagic complications, EVT was chosen over surgical clipping. Although the aneurysm’s morphology and its dome-to-neck ratio of 1.35 suggested that simple coil embolization without adjunctive stenting might have been technically feasible, occluding the anteriorly protruding bleb would have likely required dense neck packing. Due to the patient’s VWD, we prioritized procedural safety by minimizing the risks of coil protrusion and rescue stenting, which requires mandatory antiplatelet therapy. Accordingly, we selected W-EB placement. The procedure was performed via the right femoral artery approach using an 8-French balloon-guide catheter. Following 3D rotational DSA, an initial bolus of 4000 IU of heparin was administered, with additional boluses of 1000 IU administered hourly thereafter to maintain an activated clotting time (ACT) between 250 and 300 s. A W-EB SL-type device (7 × 3 mm; Terumo Corporation) was deployed into the aneurysm (Fig. 2C). Immediately following W-EB placement, angiography revealed stagnation of blood flow within the aneurysm (Fig. 2D and 2E). At the end of the procedure, the sheath was removed, and hemostasis was achieved using a Perclose vascular closure device (Abbott Vascular, Redwood City, CA, USA). No antiplatelet agents were administered after W-EB placement. After the procedure, no signs of intracranial hypertension were observed, and no ventricular enlargement was noted on head CT; therefore, lumbar cerebrospinal fluid drainage was not performed.

Laboratory data showed a normal platelet count. FVIII activity was 49.8% and VWF activity was 76.0% (the normal/reference range used at our institution: FVIII activity 78.0%–165.0% and VWF activity 50%–150%). Periprocedural hemostatic management was conducted in consultation with the hematology department. Confact F was administered post-procedure to maintain VWF. Dosing was continued for 3 days post-procedure (consisting of intravenous bolus infusions of 2000 units of Confact F), and factor levels were monitored weekly.

Seven days after the EVT procedure, follow-up angiography was performed via distal radial artery puncture to evaluate aneurysm occlusion and the presence or absence of cerebral vasospasm. Although the aneurysm was still visible (Bicêtre Occlusion Scale Score [BOSS] grade 1),3) no blood flow to the bleb was observed (Fig. 3A and 3B). A radiopaque layer, considered to represent thrombus formation, was observed along the inner wall of the W-EB device, and the risk of re-rupture was judged to be low (Fig. 3C).

Laboratory data obtained during this follow-up procedure showed FVIII activity of 75.7% and VWF activity of 49.0%. As hemostasis was successfully achieved at the puncture site, no additional Confact F was administered.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 20 with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0.

DSA performed 4 months after discharge demonstrated residual flow inside the W-EB (BOSS grade 1) (Fig. 3D–3F).

Discussion

This case illustrates the successful endovascular management of a rare case of a ruptured Acom aneurysm in a patient with type 1 VWD. Despite the inherent bleeding risks associated with VWD, careful perioperative coordination with hematology specialists and appropriate replacement therapy with VWF/FVIII concentrate enabled the safe placement of a W-EB device (Terumo Corporation) without hemorrhagic or ischemic complications. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and adequate hemostasis was maintained throughout the treatment period, highlighting the feasibility of W-EB placement in patients with carefully managed VWD.1)

The ASH/ISTH/NHF/WFH management guidelines recommend increasing FVIII and VWF activity to ≥0.50 IU/mL (=50 IU/dL) for surgical procedures and maintaining this level for at least 3 days postoperatively (conditional recommendation) or 3–5 days for minimally invasive/minor procedures and 7–14 days for major surgeries. This target level is generally achieved by administering approximately 20–40 IU/kg of Confact F (Kaketsuken), with subsequent individualized dosing based on serial FVIII measurements and clinical bleeding status. Intracerebral coil embolization is often categorized as “minimally to moderately” invasive. Additionally, if the postoperative bleeding risk is low, then the dosage and duration should be adjusted based on daily VWF/FVIII measurements. When combined with antiplatelet therapy, the duration may be extended to balance the bleeding and thrombotic risks.1) Although aneurysm clipping has been reported in patients with VWD,4) craniotomy with aneurysm clipping generally requires higher target levels of VWF and FVIII than EVT in patients requiring perioperative hemostatic management, as it is classified as major surgery. Therefore, EVT may allow a shorter duration of perioperative replacement therapy with Confact F. In the present case, the patient progressed without developing puncture site complications by administering 2000 units of Confact F for 3 days postoperatively and suturing the puncture site using a Perclose vascular closure device (Abbott Vascular). Most neurointerventional procedures use various anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents, which can predispose patients to arterial access site complications. Even in such cases, Perclose is a mechanical suture-type vascular closure device that achieves local hemostasis by suturing the vessel wall at the puncture site5) and is safe to use to close arteriotomies after EVT in patients with VWD. Furthermore, when feasible, transradial access may be asso-ciated with a lower risk of hemorrhagic complications than EVT performed via the femoral artery6) or surgical clipping.

Similar to the W-EB device, flow diverter (FD) placement procedures aimed at aneurysm occlusion via flow diversion effects have been reported to carry the risk of acquired (shear-induced activation) VWD onset after placement.7) In the present case, a decrease in VWF was observed even after supplementation with Confact F. Considering the potential for VWF depletion following W-EB deployment, regular monitoring of FVIII activity and VWF levels, along with close observation for signs of increased bleeding tendency, is necessary. Based on these considerations, although VWF replacement therapy is essential for hemostatic management in patients with VWD who develop SAH, aggressive correction of VWF activity beyond the normal range may not necessarily be optimal from a neurological standpoint. The acute phase of SAH is characterized by pronounced inflammation and endothelial activation, conditions known to promote the elevation of circulating VWF levels.8) In this context, excessive VWF activity and an imbalance in the VWF/ADAMTS13 axis may contribute to microthrombus formation and secondary brain injury.9,10) Therefore, when administering VWF-containing concentrates, such as Confact F, maintaining VWF activity at levels sufficient for hemostasis without exceeding the normal range may be a reasonable strategy to potentially mitigate neurological deterioration. Further clinical studies are warranted to clarify the optimal target range for VWF activity. Comparative studies have suggested differences in procedural safety between W-EB placement and primary coiling in ruptured wide-neck intracranial aneurysms. Propensity score–matched analysis revealed that the W-EB group had a lower rate of procedure-related complications (approximately 3%) than the primary coiling group (approximately 8%). No hemorrhagic complications were observed after W-EB placement.11) Similarly, systematic reviews have indicated that W-EB placement is associated with fewer hemorrhagic and thromboembolic complications. However, overall complication and mortality rates were comparable between the 2 techniques.12) Coil embolization requires the use of rescue stents in some cases. Avoiding perioperative antiplatelet therapy with W-EB may reduce the risk of hemorrhagic complications during the acute phase of aneurysm rupture.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of W-EB placement for VWD. In the present case, complete occlusion of the aneurysm was not confirmed by DSA 4 months after discharge. In retrospect, the choice of the W-EB device may have contributed to delayed complete occlusion due to delayed platelet adhesion and thrombus stabilization in VWD. From an angiographic perspective, simple coil embolization might have achieved earlier complete occlusion. However, this would likely have necessitated dense neck packing to occlude the anteriorly protruding bleb, thereby increasing the risk of coil protrusion and potential rescue stenting. In patients with VWD, stent-assisted techniques requiring antiplatelet therapy pose a substantial hemorrhagic risk. Thus, our treatment decision involved a trade-off between earlier angiographic occlusion and procedural safety. In this context, W-EB placement was selected as a risk-management strategy to prioritize safety by simplifying the procedure and avoiding antiplatelet therapy.

Importantly, the risk of aneurysm rupture in BOSS 1 cases is considered low and complete occlusion may be observed during subsequent follow-up.3) The primary clinical objective of early protection against rebleeding is considered likely to have been achieved. Furthermore, compared with FD, the W-EB device is expected to result in earlier complete occlusion because of the higher metal content in the neck region of the aneurysm, which provides a scaffold for endothelialization.13,14)

Despite its advantages, W-EB placement has limitations in patients with bleeding or hemostatic disorders. One important concern is the potential need for repeated catheter-based angiographic follow-up, particularly in cases of delayed or incomplete occlusion. Such repeated invasive procedures may increase cumulative procedural risk in this patient population. Therefore, while W-EB placement allows avoidance of antiplatelet therapy, this benefit must be balanced against the possibility of additional invasive follow-up procedures. DSA remains the gold standard for both the initial diagnostic evaluation and follow-up assessment of intracranial aneurysms, particularly in the setting of SAH, where precise delineation of aneurysm morphology is essential for treatment planning. Although recent advances in MRA techniques, such as non-contrast ultrashort echo time (UTE)-MRA,15) have demonstrated promising concordance with DSA as radiation-free alternatives for surveillance imaging, data regarding their reliability in patients with bleeding disorders remain limited. Therefore, in the present case, a follow-up evaluation was intentionally performed using DSA to ensure accurate assessment of aneurysm status and device configuration.

Conclusion

EVT using the W-EB device (Terumo Corporation) for a ruptured wide-neck aneurysm may allow effective early protection against rebleeding while minimizing the required intensity and duration of VWF/FVIII replacement therapy, compared with open craniotomy, which is classified as major surgery.

The case illustrates how treatment selection can be tailored by balancing aneurysm morphology, bleeding risk, and perioperative coagulation management, rather than device safety alone.
