Validation of within-person associations with coverage and momentary EMA self-reports using an objective standard (physical activity)
Authors: Arthur A Stone, Meynard Toledo, Stefan Schneider, Joshua M Smyth, Doerte U Junghaenel, Sarah Goldstein, Olivia Pomeroy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEcological momentary assessment (EMA) is a useful tool for assessing peoples’ experiences, behaviors, and environments in everyday life. EMA is considered a standard for self-reporting, because its short reporting periods and multiple assessments in everyday life offers reduced recall bias and increased ecological validity. In this study we tested how well within-person EMA measurements of levels of physical activity (prompt-level data) match objective reports of activity from accelerometry. We looked at two kinds of validity: duration of EMA recall (5 m; 120 m) and type of physical activity response scale (verbal response scales [VRS]; numerical duration scales). Concurrent criterion validity was tested by examining level differences and with correlations of EMA with accelerometry. Construct validity was assessed by examining whether the correlations between EMA and non-physical activity variables aligned with correlations between accelerometry and those same variables. Participants (N = 315) wore a thigh accelerometer and completed EMA five times per day for 7-days. We observed that EMA estimates of activity levels were very different compared with accelerometry levels, especially for active behavior, where EMA yielded much higher levels of active behavior than accelerometry (the first type of concurrent criterion validity). We found moderate within-person correlations between EMA and accelerometry (second type of concurrent criterion validity). However, some study factors moderated the concordance: coverage EMA had slightly stronger associations than near-momentary EMA and sedentary behavior was more accurately reported than high physical activity levels. Construct validity analyses showed that EMA self-reports could be used in place of accelerometry data. With the caveat that physical activity was greatly overestimated by EMA, findings largely support the utility of EMA for analyzing within-person associations with other coverage and near-momentary variables, although they suggest some forms of EMA may be somewhat less optimal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61581-9.Keywords: EMA, Self-report, Physical activity, Accelerometry, Criterion validity, Construct validitySubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) is a self-report data collection methodology for assessing behaviors, feelings, and situations. Participants in EMA studies are typically signaled several times a day over multiple days as they go about their everyday lives, ensuring that data is captured in their typical environments and supporting the claim of a “representative design” (often referred to as ecological validity1. EMA data are generally viewed as a valid reflection of participants’ everyday experiences and behaviors, because the short reporting periods targeted in EMA questions help reduce recall bias2,3. Owing to its repeated measurements over time, EMA is a valuable tool for studying within-person processes, particularly for understanding the dynamics of variables that are not easily captured by objective methods. Given these properties, EMA has been a welcome tool for digging deeper into granular, everyday processes.

There remains, however, a largely unanswered question concerning a fundamental assumption of EMA. It is that because EMA employs brief reporting periods (ranging from the current moment to time periods including one or two hours before being prompted to report), the resulting data are presumed sound4. A theoretical underpinning of the assumption is the accessibility of information model5,6. It posits that self-reports can be accurate when respondents have access to (or knowledge of) the information requested for a question. Short recall periods encourage, but do not guarantee accuracy, because even in the face of having accessibility to information, other factors may distort self-reports, such as careless responding, momentary distractions, or biases generated by participants’ use of response scales. When people have less than full access to pertinent information, for example, when asked to answer questions with long recall periods (due to memory capacity or inattention), self-reports are more likely to be distorted by cognitive processes that emphasize certain aspects of the limited information that is currently available to the respondent or even by potentially irrelevant information such as general beliefs and heuristics7,8. The assumption that EMA accurately measures what it is intended to measure is at the heart of the methodology, and it is difficult to imagine any dispute about whether the assumption should be empirically confirmed, if possible. If momentary self-reports were not a reasonable reflection of what was experienced or how the individual behaved, then we would have serious concerns with the EMA.

The goal of the present paper was to examine this validity issue at a within-person level of analysis. Within-person analyses are at the core of EMA studies that address the variability of temporal fluctuations, concurrent associations between or among variables, and lagged associations when a variable is thought to exert an influence on subsequent levels of the same or another variable. This is likely the most common application for data collected with EMA. (Another application is to characterize individuals in between-person analyses; such associations are examined in another paper based on these data9. Notably, in strictly within-person associations, each individual serves “as their own control.” This is achieved by modeling fluctuations around a person’s average score across time10–12. The resulting estimates specifically reflect how differences (relative to the person’s mean) in one variable relate to levels in another variable within the same individual, thereby disentangling the dynamic within-person effects from potential confounding caused by between-person differences.

A major barrier for providing strong evidence of EMA validity has been the absence of an objective comparison standard. Private, subjective states (such as pain, emotions, fatigue, and some symptoms), which are often the focus of EMA studies, do not have objective standards for comparison. (There are likely clever ways to validate self-reports of environmental qualities via observer reports of those qualities, but to our knowledge, these methods are not yet widely available.) To overcome this obstacle, the present study assessed a construct that does have a reliable, objective reference – physical activity measured with accelerometry, which is part of a trend in science to incorporate wearable devices for objective assessments13. A review of 12 prior studies examining EMA and accelerometry yielded a mixed picture of criterion validity14. Most of the reviewed studies centered on the idea that validity would be conferred if various daily activities confirmed expected differences in accelerometry-measured sedentary behavior or physical activity. For example, accelerometry of counts during moments reported with sedentary activities were, on average, lower compared to moments with activities thought of as requiring physical activity. Although such an analysis may be informative, the goal of the current study was to directly compare self-reported physical activity with EMA to accelerometry measures of the same construct. We asked two concurrent criterion validity questions: first, are the levels of physical activity derived from accelerometry and from self-report EMA comparable? Second, when a person self-reports high activity (or sedentary behavior) at a prompt, how well does that predict activity (or sedentary behavior) as measured by accelerometry for the same period (within-person)? In our view, these are important facets of EMA criterion validity that have not been adequately addressed.

The observed magnitudes of agreement between EMA self-reports and objective measurement of physical activity (both level and association) may depend on many factors including EMA sampling frequency and schedule, on the reporting period for an EMA prompt, and on the types of questions employed. We note that the sampling schedules employed in this study are typical of EMA studies and, hence, may generate results consistent with many EMA protocols. To set expectations for the study, we consider how well individuals may report their physical activity. There are suggestions from the accelerometry literature that self-knowledge of physical activity is far from perfect14,15, so we did not expect an exact match of levels or a very high correspondence between EMA and accelerometry. These discrepancies may be explained, at least in part, by recognizing that accelerometry records all manner of movements (based on where the device is placed on the body), even quite small movements. In contrast, it is likely that people attend to more distinguishable bouts of physical activity such as walking, running, exercising, etc. Regarding associations, we think that correlations above 0.5 would be indicative of good self-report validity.

A second, pragmatic strategy for evaluating the utility of EMA was based on the idea that there are broadly reliable and robust associations between objective demographic and situational variables and objectively measured physical activity levels, for example, differences that may exist in sedentary behavior (SB) and physical activity (PA) over the course of the day, by location at the prompt, or by type of activity at the prompt. Our contention is that if these associations were replicated when EMA physical activity measures were substituted for accelerometry, then that would support the construct validity16 of EMA measures of activity (similar to the concept of “external responsiveness”17.

In addition to the validity questions, this study provided an opportunity to examine two other facets of EMA designs that are of considerable importance for the methodology. As just mentioned, the effectiveness of EMA may hinge upon reporting very recent experiences and most EMA applications ask about the moments or period just before a prompt. There has been interest in increasing the duration of the reporting period to improve the probability of capturing infrequent events and experiences (the “coverage” model of EMA)4,18. The measurement properties of coverage EMA have received scant methodological attention in the literature, and this study provided an opportunity for head-to-head comparisons of the two modes. We examined two reporting periods to determine if they affected validity. One was a “near-momentary” approach asking individuals to report about the previous 5 min; this procedure is very similar to a momentary version of EMA (which asks about just before a prompt). The second was the coverage approach that extended reporting to the 120 m period prior to the prompt.

Next, all self-reporting procedures ask questions that are answered with certain response formats. Response options for EMA have been open-ended responses (i.e., write-in a response), Yes-No scales, Visual Analogs scales, frequency, intensity, or duration scales, Likert scales, and Verbal Response Scales (VRS). The specific response option used has the potential to influence results; to explore this possibility, the current study used two response scales for physical activity. VRSs asked about “how much” physical activity (or sedentary behavior) occurred in the reporting period. This was meant to capture the totality of the physical activity experience, analogous to prior efforts to measure subjective experiences over time. The other response scale assessed the duration (in minutes) of several categories of PA, which were defined by the intensity of the activity. This quantitative approach to measuring physical activity could potentially avoid the imprecision and relativistic anchoring of VRSs (e.g., “extreme” activity can be interpreted in many ways), although there are mixed results concerning the precision of VRSs19–21. Five additional questions asked participants to report the number of minutes that they engaged in various intensities of PA, a common approach in the physical activity literature22.

A final consideration for the study is the recognition that physical activity is not a monolithic concept, and the physical activity field has distinguished several categories of activity22. One category is sedentary behavior, defined as any waking behavior characterized by an energy expenditure ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs), while in a sitting, reclining or lying posture. Standing is another relatively very low level of activity. These are followed by light, moderate, and vigorous activity designations and correspond to different levels of activity as defined in this study by accelerometry measurements. However, we acknowledge that individuals may subjectively perceive and label the intensity of their own activities differently. Although individual differences in physical activity perception may be important in other research contexts, they are not central to the specific research question addressed here. Therefore, for the purposes of the present analyses focusing on the validity of quantitative measure used in EMA, responses across the light, moderate, and vigorous activity categories were combined into a single, quantitative PA measure.

More broadly, our hope is that self-reports and accelerometry measures of physical activity may be considered an analog of the validity of subjective states, where we do not have objective references. Although physical activity is an observable behavior, unlike private, subjective states, we posit that the process of self-reporting recent physical activity likely shares key characteristics with reporting internal states. Individuals must access recent memories of their activity, interpret their experience of the activity (e.g., its intensity or duration), and convey this information to by answering questions. The cognitive demands involved in this process for physical activity experiences may be like those involved when individuals report about their internal constructs even if the source information (behavior vs. internal feeling) differs. The accuracy of physical activity self-reports might, therefore, be influenced by the same mechanisms affecting reports of private states.

In summary, the goals of the study were to increase our knowledge about several facets of within-person EMA data collection by comparing various approaches to EMA reports of physical activity to device-assessed physical activity. We advance the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

There will be a substantial magnitude of prompt-to-prompt associations between accelerometry-based measures of SB and PA and corresponding self-reports of the behaviors measured with EMA. This is a test of within-person criterion validity.

Hypothesis 2

That self-report EMA measures of SB and PA will replicate associations that accelerometry-based measures of SB and PA have with other time-varying variables, including time of day, type of activity engaged in, current location, type of social activity, and reported pain level. This is a test of one aspect of construct validity.

Hypothesis 3

That coverage EMA (120 min) will have weaker associations with accelerometry measures of SB and PA than near-momentary PA, given the presumed superiority of near-momentary EMA to accurate capture activity given the relative absence of retrospection in this condition.

Hypothesis 4

That quantitative questions will have stronger associations with accelerometry-measured SB and PA that Verbal Response Scales, given the relativistic nature of VRS responses.

Methods

Participant sample

The methods described here have previously been presented in part in published and under review work23,24 and, therefore, contain some of the same descriptions in those papers. The data analyzed for this study came from a larger EMA protocol examining relationships between self-reported and objectively recorded physical activity levels. Study participants were recruited from the Understanding America Study (UAS;25, a probability-based Internet panel study of about 14,000 US residents. Members are recruited to be part of the UAS through address-based sampling, and individuals without prior Internet access receive a tablet and broadband access (excluding smartphones). This ensures broad coverage in populations typically underrepresented in opt-in or volunteer online panels. UAS panelists were eligible to participate in the EMA study if they (1) were fluent in English; (2) were 18 years of age or older; (3) had no hearing impairments, (4) had no vision impairments that could not be corrected with contact lenses or glasses; (5) did not work a night shift; (6) were not on bed-rest; and (7) did not require any mobility devices to be able to move around. Selected panelists were contacted via email to be invited into the study and to complete the eligibility screening survey. Of 1363 UAS panelists who were contacted and assessed for eligibility, 1021 met the eligibility criteria. Of those, 407 provided consent and were enrolled into the study, and 359 completed the study protocol. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York Institutional Review Board (#22-183-1044) and all research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants provided electronic informed consent for participation.

Background measures

Standard demographic questions were asked about age, education, gender, racial background, marital status, and income. Height and weight questions provided information for the computation of body mass index (BMI). To enable comparability with other studies, a standard retrospective recall instrument (one week) for measuring physical activity was included, the International Questionnaire for Physical Activity (IPAQ,26).

Study procedures

The study included a 7-day EMA protocol that was executed remotely. Participants were mailed a study package to their home that contained the study devices (a smartphone with charger and an activity monitor with medical tape and alcohol swabs), and mailing materials for returning the package after completing the EMA study. Participants were randomized in batches of 6 to the near-momentary or coverage groups by assigning a random number to each individual, sorting the group in ascending order on the random number, and assigning the first half to the near-momentary EMA group and the second half to the coverage EMA group. The day before starting the protocol, participants were instructed to watch a 20-minute training video that explained how to put on and wear the activity monitor, and that guided participants through all aspects of EMA survey completion (how to interact with the device and app, the questions presented, and how to enter their responses). Starting on the next day, participants received 5 EMA prompts per day for 7 days while continuously wearing their physical activity monitor. Research staff checked the number of completed EMA prompts daily and contacted participants over the telephone and through email if there were suspected technological problems. Participants received daily email reminders about completing the study activities. Upon completion, participants returned their study package using the provided mailing materials and prepaid return label. Participants were compensated up to $200 for study participation based on their overall level of protocol completion.

Accelerometry-based physical activity measurement

The activPAL™ activity monitor was used to objectively measure participants’ physical activity levels over the study period. Participants were asked to wear the monitor for seven consecutive days, 24 h a day, except during water-based activities. Unlike hip or wrist-worn accelerometry-based activity monitors, the activPAL is worn on the anterior midline of the thigh. Numerous validation studies support the accuracy of the device (e.g.27,28. The monitor records activity categories in terms of (a) sedentary (i.e., sitting or lying down), (b) standing, and (c) stepping behaviors (i.e., step counts). We further used the cadence of stepping activities to distinguish times spent in (d) light physical activity (LPA; cadence < 100 steps/minute), (e) moderate physical activity (MPA; cadence 100–125 steps/minute), and (f) vigorous physical activity (VPA; cadence > 125 steps/minute) levels29. The physical activity variables used in the analyses are described in the Results section.

EMA data collection

EMA questions were administered using the movisensXS app on study dedicated smartphones (Motorola G Power 2021). EMA prompts were delivered with a sampling scheme with 5 prompts that could occur between 6 AM and 10 PM in the participant’s time zone, and which was stratified such that the sampled reporting periods covered by the EMA questions were at least 15 min apart. EMA items were administered using two different reporting periods such that the EMA questions asked participants about their behaviors and experiences either during “the 5 minutes before the prompt” or during “the 2 hours before the prompt”; the reporting period was assigned to participants in a between-person randomized design. The supplemental section on Randomization procedures describes the specifics of how the near-momentary and coverage groups sampling prompts were determined in a manner that yielded equivalent coverage of the waking day.

Self-reported physical activity levels were assessed with 5 EMA items asking participants how many minutes they spent (1) sitting or lying down (i.e., sedentary), (2) standing, (3) in LPA, (4) in MPA, and (5) in VPA during the targeted time (i.e., 5 min–120 min) before the EMA prompt. The contents of these EMA questions were designed to mirror the physical activity variables derived from the activPAL activity monitor. Answers were given by entering the number of minutes using an open-ended numeric response format. Two additional EMA items asked participants how “sedentary” and how “physically active” they were during the targeted time (5 min–120 min) before the prompt, with answers provided using a 5-point verbal rating scale (not at all – extremely). Respondents reported the sleep and wake time for the night during the first EMA prompt of each day. EMA items on affect, pain, and fatigue were also administered but were not analyzed here.

Participants were allowed to delay their responses to each EMA prompt for up to 15 min. After an initial alarm that lasted for 1 min, reminder alarms lasting 1 min each were administered by the app 5 and 10 min after the first alarm. The prompt expired 15 min after the beginning of the initial alarm and the participant was unable to complete the EMA survey thereafter. There were additional features built into the sampling schedule, for example, that overlap of reporting periods was not permitted, and limitations of Movisens programming for the coverage group required development of several pre-set week-long prompting schedules. Details are provided in the supplemental materials.

Analytic plan

Basic demographic characteristics for the near-momentary and coverage EMA groups are presented first. We then describe the procedure for determining the hours of waking days, which involved using information from accelerometry and from EMA reports. Next, we present the methods used for identifying outlying responses and methods for handling outliers. A summary variable for total PA was computed by summing Light Activity, Moderate Activity, and Vigorous Activity minutes separately for EMA and for accelerometry. This procedure simplified the analyses and was consistent with our broad interest in both sedentary behavior and physical activity. Nevertheless, we also present a brief description of the level differences between EMA PA and accelerometry PA broken out by level of activity, in order to determine the origin of discrepancies between EMA and accelerometry.

Determination of waking hours

To construct accelerometry-based variables for the analyses we needed to have awakening times and bedtimes. These values were used as inclusion criteria for prompts and for calculating person-level variables of physical activity. The procedures for determining these times combined data from the accelerometry record and data from a question on the EMA questionnaire as well as adjudication by the research time in a small number of instances. The procedures are described in detail in the Supplemental materials.

Outlier responses in EMA duration reports

Questions about the duration of physical activity in the various categories were presented with an open-ended numeric response format that allowed any number of minutes to be entered. This resulted in some outlier responses, including some that were not realistic or logically possible given the upper bounds of the period being rated (5 min –120 min). There were several steps in this process, and one notable transformation was that any self-report data that exceeded the logical highest value (e.g., a maximum of 5 min for the near-momentary condition and of 120 min for the coverage condition) were winsorized at those maximum values (5 min and 120 min, respectively). A full description of procedures used to handle outlier responses, including criteria for excluding prompts, are described in the Supplemental materials.

Concurrent criterion validity analyses

Levels

SB and PA levels involved computing the overall level of self-reported SB and PA durations separately for near-momentary and coverage EMA prompt types. The average levels of SB and PA were calculated with a multivariate multilevel analysis with random intercepts (STATA, “mixed” procedure) with prompts as the level 1 unit of analysis. To examine whether the mean levels differed between accelerometry minutes and EMA minutes, we estimated multivariate multilevel models in which the two variables were specified as correlated outcomes; these were computed separately for SB and PA and for coverage and near-momentary groups. Level comparisons were not computed between accelerometry and EMA VRS variables, because they were measured on different scales (duration with minutes versus VRS with a 5-point scale).

Within-person associations

These analyses were also evaluated with a multilevel mixed model with random intercepts and were run separately for the near-momentary EMA and coverage EMA groups. To determine whether the strength of associations between accelerometry and EMA self-reports differed by study factors, we tested the three factors simultaneously: EMA condition (near-momentary vs. coverage); response format (duration vs. VRS); and physical activity type (active vs. sedentary). Because many correlations were calculated, we combined the correlations to statistically compare overall (i.e., pooled average) effect sizes across the three main analysis “factors.” For each factor, we computed the difference between pooled average Fisher z-transformed correlations based on the full within-person covariance matrix among all variables. This procedure yields effect sizes Cohen’s q, where values of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 are conventionally interpreted as small, medium, and large differences in correlations, respectively.

Construct validity analyses

These analyses were based on determining the “true” association between conditions that were likely to vary at the within-person level with SB or PA accelerometry levels. We recognize that accelerometry measurement is not entirely error free, but it should yield reasonably accurate assessments of SB and PA. We extracted criterion variable information about several time-varying circumstances from the EMA assessments that could be associated with SB and PA: reporting working, reported doing chores, reported eating, reported relaxing, reported using a computer, and reported socializing. An additional time-varying measure was taken from paradata streams and accelerometry, time-of-day, and within-day cycles were analyzed separately for each EMA group.

To evaluate construct validity, we calculated the correlation between the EMA-derived variables and the first set of criterion variables (excluding time-of-day and day-of-week) and compared those values with the “true” correlations obtained from the corresponding accelerometer-derived variables. We had weak a priori expectations about the directionality and magnitudes of these true associations and found considerable variability in their magnitudes. However, our goal was not to determine which criterion variables were most influential or diagnostically informative for physical activity, as would be the focus of prioritization frameworks that assign interpretive weight to specific predictors, but rather to assess the similarity in correlational structure across measurement methods. In other words, the emphasis was on convergence in patterns of associations between accelerometry and EMA, not on weighting or ranking individual predictors. To determine which of the study factors were related to the congruence of associations of accelerometry with criterion variables versus associations of EMA variables with the same criterion variables, we tested the three factors in a single model: EMA condition (near-momentary vs. coverage); response format (duration vs. VRS); and physical activity type (active vs. sedentary). As an index of convergence, we computed the mean absolute difference (MAD) between Fisher z-transformed within-person correlations obtained from EMA and accelerometer data. Higher MAD values indicate lower similarity between the two methods’ correlation patterns with criterion variables. To account for dependencies among correlations, MAD values were calculated from the full within-person covariance matrix. Bootstrapping with 1,000 draws was used to generate 95% confidence intervals and p-values.

To compare the patterns of accelerometry and EMA self-reports of SB and PA for day-of-week, individual multilevel analyses with time-of-day as a predictor were computed for the dependent variables of accelerometry, EMA VRS, and EMA durations, separately for SB and PA and for each of the EMA groups (coverage and near-momentary). Plots of the diurnal patterns were plotted and visually inspected for comparability among the assessment methods.

Sample size and statistical power

Statistical power in multilevel models depends on several factors, including total sample size, the number of repeated observations per person, and the ratio of between- to within-person variability (i.e., the intraclass correlation; ICC)2. With an anticipated sample of about 300 participants and an average of 28 EMA prompts per person, the study was well powered to detect a range of within-person and between-condition effects at a two-sided alpha level of 0.01, adjusted for multiple comparisons. For mean-level comparisons between EMA and accelerometry (e.g., testing average differences in reported activity levels), the design allowed detection of small-to-moderate effects of Cohen’s d = 0.28 with 80% power, assuming a conservative ICC of0.50. Furthermore, the study had 80% power to detect within-person correlations as small as r = .03, based on the total number of observations, as well as between-condition differences in these correlations (e.g., comparing EMA–accelerometry associations across recall periods or response scales) as small as Cohen’s q = 0.39, assuming ICC = 0.50. While traditional power calculations are less applicable to the construct validity analyses—owing to the use of bootstrapped confidence intervals and unknown dependencies in the covariance structure of the correlation matrix—the large number of pairwise associations allowed for precise estimation of summary metrics (e.g., MAD) and adequate sensitivity to detect differences across experimental conditions.

Results

Final sample for analyses

A total of 359 people completed the protocol. For the analyses we selected participants with much accelerometry and EMA self-report information, because substantial missing data could lead to bias in the person-level summary variables (for instance, if only certain days contributed to the summaries and day-of-week was associated with activity levels). For accelerometry, we required that there be seven days of accelerometry recording and, for EMA, we required that at least half of the scheduled prompts were completed. Of 359 participants who completed the protocol, 44 were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 315. In addition, a series of checks of the quality of the EMA self-reports of physical activity were conducted and the number of prompts available for analysis was 8,734, that, counting every completed prompt for each participant included in the analytic sample. The procedures employed in this culling of prompts are described in the supplemental materials. For EMA self-reports, the average compliance rate (defined as the percentage of completed EMA relative to the total number of EMA sent) was 93%. For accelerometry, on average participants wore the device for 99% of their waketime period.

Concurrent criterion validity

Levels

Differences in means between accelerometry measures of SB and PA and those derived from EMA self-reports of duration were compared with multivariate multilevel procedures separately for coverage and near-momentary EMA groups (see Table 2). Differences between EMA and accelerometry were also evident when specific levels of PA were examined for the coverage and near-momentary groups. Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to achieve a deeper understanding about self-reporting with EMA techniques by comparison of EMA reports to an objective reference standard. Specifically, we examined how two different reporting periods and two response scale types influenced the validity of EMA self-reports of physical activity behaviors relative to device-assessed physical activity behaviors. Strengths of the study design include: having accelerometry-based measure of SB and PA taken almost continuously throughout the one-week protocol that served as a reference standard for self-reporting; obtaining self-report EMA data with both near-momentary EMA and coverage EMA (with substantial sample sizes in each EMA condition); and, using of two types of EMA response scales, Verbal Ratings Scales for appraising the overall levels of SB and PA and quantitative duration questions for obtaining estimates of the number of minutes engaged in SB and PA. To evaluate the validity of EMA self-reports we examined two forms of concurrent criterion validity and one type of construct validity. The latter determined whether self-reported SB and PA can be adequate substitutes for accelerometry data (the reference standard) when determining within-person associations with other time-varying variables of interest. These questions are significant in that they are rarely found in the literature given that studies almost always employ only a single version of EMA (e.g., near-momentary), one version of question type (e.g., Verbal Response Scale), and rarely is there an objective reference available.

With a few caveats, our findings broadly support the utility and value of EMA data for addressing within-person questions: there were many associations of at least moderate magnitude between self-reported physical activity and objectively measured PA and there was strong concordance between accelerometry and EMA associations with time-varying variables. However, the absolute levels of duration from EMA and accelerometry were different, especially for PA. The discussion begins with an overview of the findings, followed by more detailed consideration of the impact of the various design elements. It concludes with a discussion of the generalizability of the results to other constructs and limitations of the study.

Accuracy of the duration of SB and PA estimated by self-reports and accelerometry

We start with the question, do community-dwelling adults accurately self-report the levels of their SB and PA over relatively brief reporting periods as they go about their daily activities? To address this question of concurrent criterion validity, we compared self-reported minutes of SB and PA to minutes from accelerometry, where the latter measures were based on the appropriate EMA reporting period, either 120 min (coverage) or 5 min (near-momentary). In both coverage and near-momentary groups, self-reports of SB duration were 10–15% lower than accelerometry duration levels. More striking than these modest differences in SB were differences for PA: self-reports of these activities substantially overestimated the objective accelerometry reports by between 300% and 400%. Similar patterns of mean differences between self-reported and accelerometry recorded activity levels have been reported previously15. The large discrepancies for PA suggest that using EMA to estimate absolute levels of physical activity behavior is problematic given the substantial overestimation. Ignoring this finding would likely lead one to conclude that the population is less sedentary and much more physically active compared with duration estimated by accelerometry. Therefore, this test of concurrent criterion validity was not confirmed for PA and qualifies the conclusion that EMA is an accurate reflection (for level) of accelerometry.

A possible explanation for level discrepancies emerges from how participants self-reported their SB and PA with the choices provided in the EMA questionnaire. Even though examples of activities (e.g., sitting, walking, running) were included at every prompt as part of each duration question, there were likely differences between what the accelerometry algorithm captures as physical activity and the (more natural) way respondents assigned labels to physical activity levels. More broadly, participants may have different concepts about what constitutes physical activity than the implicit concept measured by accelerometry. It may also be the case the psychological factors such as the desire to see oneself as being active contribute to discrepancies between the self-reported and objective durations. Other memory and summarization processes could also contribute to the discrepancies such as cognitive heuristics, which can lead one to overemphasize certain periods. We speculate that moderate physical activity, for example, could be processed as an intense or “peak” experience30 and, hence, lead to overreporting such seen in this study. Furthermore, “duration neglect,” another heuristic that suggests nonsalient periods may be downplayed in memory31, could help explain the lower levels of self-reported SB minutes relative the accelerometry SB minutes.

Another plausible survey-design explanation is that the task of allocating either 5–120 min to the various SB and PA categories (for example, for the 120-minute condition that 60 min were spent being sedentary, 40 min in light PA, and 20 min in moderate PA) was inherently challenging and led to reporting error. We note that the presentation of activity duration questions on smartphones was not enhanced with features that could have reduced problems with the allocation of minutes. For instance, the app could have provided a running total of the minutes allocated so the total number of minutes allocated could be easily recognized and adjusted, if needed. Future studies employing similar time allocation response strategies may wish to consider including aids for respondents.

Our procedures for examining PA involved summating PA’s three components: minutes spent in light, moderate, and vigorous activity levels. Here we address the concern that analyzing a single PA variable may have obscured self-report–accelerometry differences for these PA components. The percentage discrepancies for this overall PA variable were between 300 and 400% in each of the two groups. Additional analyses (not shown) examined the three activity categories separately. For light and moderate activities, we found about the same degree of discrepancy as for the overall PA variable. This is sensible given that these two components of PA comprised almost all of the PA minutes. In contrast, the discrepancy percentages were much higher for vigorous activity (between 1,000% and 2,500%). However, given the very low minute counts for vigorous activities, we think these are relatively unstable estimates that should be interpreted very cautiously. Taken together, the component-level analyses reinforce our main conclusion: self-reported PA substantially overestimates accelerometer-derived activity, and this pattern holds across individual PA categories.

We conclude that there may be genuine issues in the accuracy of physical activity duration reported with EMA and suggest that this topic is worthy of additional study. That reporting discrepancies are present with the very brief reporting periods used in this study confirms prior reports of the poor accuracy of more traditional reports of an entire day or week’s activity durations, given their dependence on accurate recall over many days32–34.

Associations between self-reports and accelerometry

A primary question for this study was how moment to moment fluctuations (within-person) in EMA self-reports were associated with levels of objective accelerometry measures, as second index of concurrent criterion validity. We found that people’s self-reports of SB and PA and accelerometry measures were moderately associated, with within-person correlations ranging from 0.42 to 0.67. The highest associations were for sedentary behavior with duration rating, where the coefficient for the coverage group was 0.66 and 0.67 for the near-momentary group.

Overall analyses of the patterns of correlations revealed that two of the design factors had significant effects on correlation magnitudes and one did not. First, the magnitude of correlations in the coverage group were higher on average than those of the correlations in the near-momentary group. However, the difference between the groups was very small (on average, a 0.04 difference in correlations between the groups), therefore, the practical significance of this difference is low. That is, we do not think the small difference would lead a researcher to choose coverage over near-momentary EMA. To our knowledge, this is the first concurrent criterion validation of coverage EMA, and it is reassuring that the validity coefficient was at least as strong as that for near-momentary EMA. The result is encouraging for researchers who choose to use coverage EMA in the service of studying within-person associations and will hopefully inspire use of the technique in future studies where a coverage reporting period has utility for hypothesis testing.

Apart from the applied implication of the result, we wonder why the near-momentary associations were not considerably better than the coverage associations. Near-momentary data capture has the distinct advantage of brief recall and one might reasonably surmise that this should lead to more accurate reporting of SB and PA at a prompt-level. Conversely, the memory and summarization task of 2-hour recall might be presumed to lead to more error in SB and PA reports. This was not the case. Perhaps participants could “get the gist” of 2-hour period more easily by focusing on the modal activity level throughout the longer period resulting in the observed difference in correspondence.

The second significant study design effect was that sedentary behavior associations between EMA and accelerometry were considerably stronger compared with those for active behavior. Although this result may be of particular interest for physical activity researchers, it may be of less general relevance to EMA researchers who have different substantive interests. Nevertheless, it may be of value to consider how SB differs from PA and how those differences align with other content areas studied by EMA researchers, such as events like smoking behavior, social activities, work events, health behaviors and subjective states like emotion, stress, and symptoms. Sedentary behavior is likely to be consistent over longer periods, for instance, when people are watching television, are working on their computers, or are consuming a meal, compared with a higher likelihood of short bursts for intense physical activity. Perhaps longer sedentary episodes are easier to recall and report than the short bursts of PA, resulting in higher concurrent criterion validity coefficients. To extend this argument, one might suggest that episodes of strong emotions are more like PA than SB, for example, and would therefore result in lower validity of EMA self-reports. Of course, this is speculative and is difficult or impossible to test given the absence of objective emotion indicators.

Third, the overall correlational analysis indicated no difference in the validity of duration versus VRS ratings. This may be viewed as a surprising result given concerns that have been voiced about VRS scales, which are sometimes referred to somewhat pejoratively as “vague quantifiers” given the possibility that they are influenced by various factors including participants making relativistic comparisons to determine their responses19. In other words, SB or PA ratings for a given activity could result in very different results if the reference frames varied for different individuals or groups of individuals. Conversely, recent studies have shown that VRS scales can perform well in comparison to quantitative scales20,21, so perhaps our initial expectation about lower validity for VRS scales was not warranted.

Associations of accelerometry and EMA with external variables

Turning to the construct validity analyses, our goal was to determine if the within-person associations between accelerometry and many other EMA-derived contextual variables were the same as the within-person associations between EMA self-reports and the same set of contextual variables. If the answer was affirmative, then one would have confidence in substituting EMA self-reports for the objective accelerometry variables. Overall, the answer to this question was that there was remarkable consistency in the direction and magnitude of the correlations, a point that was clear in the plots of the accelerometry and EMA correlations with criterion variables and how closely the points clustered around the identity line in Fig. 1. Similarly, the diurnal plots showed that the daily pattern was very similar for accelerometry and EMA self-reports, both duration and VRS.

Nonetheless, the overall correlational analyses did show some differences attributable to study design features. First, correlations based on duration scales exhibited higher agreement than did correlations based on VRS scales. This is of particular interest considering that the concurrent criterion validity coefficients were not significantly different for duration versus VRS scales. Thus, the conclusion from the construct validity analyses may be considered somewhat at odds with the criterion validity, where the construct analyses suggest somewhat better validity for the duration scale. At this point we do not have an explanation for the difference, but it must be attributed to the unique variance (that is, not shared between accelerometry and EMA) of the duration measures contributing more strongly to the associations with the external variable than the unique variance of the VRS measures. We recognize that this is not a fully satisfactory explanation, but it does suggest a starting point for new explorations of the phenomenon.

Second, like the results of the criterion validity associational analyses, the correlations with sedentary behavior were considerably more consistent than correlations with active behavior. The speculation advanced in the prior section for the difference is also applicable here; that is, less frequent and short burst PA maybe more challenging to recall. However, this argument is perhaps less compelling with the near-momentary group given the very short (5 m) recall associated with this condition. Finally, no differences in the consistency between coverage and near-momentary correlations with the criterion variables was found.

Overall, the pattern of associations for the validity analyses suggests that dynamic processes between time-varying external variables, such as those we examined, will yield similar associations whether one employs accelerometry measures or self-report measures of the types used in this study. Of course, our selection of criterion variables was limited, and it is possible that other environmental or social variables will have different associations between accelerometry and EMA self-reports. Nevertheless, this is a strong demonstration that both EMA VRS and duration questions yielded compelling associations with other variables, confirming to at least some degree the assumptions that have made EMA attractive for studying within-person phenomena.

Considerations for EMA

Our rationale for designing the study with an objective standard was to possibly create an analog to the measurement of unobservable subjective states, where objective reference standards are generally not available. Although physical activity is a very different construct than subjective states, one may speculate that physical activity and subjective states have commonalities regarding how they are stored in and accessed from working (or episodic) memory. Furthermore, it may be that EMA self-reports are susceptible to bias and distortion from cognitive heuristics and other memory processes and that both momentary self-report of physical activity and subjective states are likely to share aspects of this property. This suggests additional research to fully understand the psychometric properties, including sensitivity to change, of near-momentary and coverage EMA. Moreover, from a dynamic viewpoint, subjective, internal states often fluctuate over the course of a typical day much like physical activity levels and they may present similar challenges for accurate self-report. To our knowledge, there are no data to support or contradict these assumptions.

Nevertheless, one may argue that recall of subjective states is qualitatively different than recall of physical activity; for example, featural characteristics of the recalled and reported construct (e.g., a behavior versus an internal state, automaticity, salience, intensity, etc.) may be related to recall processes. Again, our response to this position is that although this may be true, we are not aware of any research that directly examines these possibilities. As such, these remain important areas for future work. Our broader hope is that the present findings might inform the interpretation of EMA self-reports that typically cannot be compared with a (nonexistent) reference standard or at least raise new questions about those associations. The reader will have to evaluate for themselves whether they consider the analogy successful.

In our view, the most important takeaway is that the results provide empirical backing for the validity of within-person associations between EMA self-reporting of physical activity experiences versus objective measurement, with the caveat that estimating overall levels of SB and, especially for PA, from EMA are likely to be inaccurate.
