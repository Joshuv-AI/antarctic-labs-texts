Epidemiological, virological, and clinical characteristics of dengue in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, 2022–2023: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dalouny Xayavong, Virasack Somoulay, Bouaphanh Khamphaphongphane, Phonepadith Xangxayarath, Hiromu Osako, Kei Yamasato, Motoki Ihara, Jean Claude Balingit, Mya Myat Ngwe Tun, Kouichi Morita, Arata Hidano, Futoshi Hasebe, Yuki Takamatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue has remained a public health challenge in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Lao PDR) for several decades, with all four dengue serotypes circulating. This study aimed to investigate the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, and role of co-circulation with factors associated with severe dengue in this setting.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study to retrieve all serum samples from patients suspected of having dengue collected through the National Dengue Surveillance System between 2022 and 2023 in Lao PDR, as well as their demographic and clinical information. All samples were tested for DENV detection and serotyping by reverse-transcription PCR. In-house DENV IgM/IgG ELISAs confirmed the infections and classified them as primary or secondary infections. Multinomial logistic regression was used to analyze the factors associated with disease severity.ResultsOf the 1098 serum samples from suspected dengue patients, 615 (56%) tested DENV-positive. The median age of the DENV-positive patients was 22 years (IQR 15–33), and 339 (55.1%) were female. Overall, 178 (29%) infections were classified as primary and 437 (71%) as secondary infections. DENV-positive patients were common in the northern region (37.4%) compared to other regions, with significant geographic differences in serotype distribution (p < 0.0001): DENV-2 was identified in the south (62.5%), while DENV-1 was detected in the north (45.9%) (p < 0.0001). In the southern region, secondary infections due to DENV-2 (66.7%) were more common, whereas primary infections due to DENV-1 were more frequent in the northern region (p = 0.0002). Greater disease severity was observed in DENV-2 infection (83.3%) (p = 0.002). Severe dengue was more likely in secondary infection (OR = 2.38, 95% CI: 0.84–6.74) compared to primary infection and was significantly higher in the southern (OR = 11.16, 95% CI: 3.05–40.77) (p < 0.001) and central regions (OR = 10.34, 95% CI: 2.81–38.09) (p < 0.001).ConclusionCollectively, our findings identified the key factors that contributed to the dengue epidemic in 2022–2023. DENV-2, secondary infection, and geographic variation were associated with severe outcomes. Enhanced genomic and epidemiological surveillance is critical for addressing the dengue burden in the Lao PDR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01008-3.Keywords: Dengue virus, Severe dengue, Epidemiology, Seroprevalence, Lao PDR

Introduction

Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by the dengue virus (DENV), which is primarily transmitted to humans by Aedes mosquitoes. Approximately 390 million people are infected annually, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions of over 100 countries, with 96 million developing clinical manifestations [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that 500,000 patients require hospitalization, and approximately 2.5% of those affected by severe disease result in death [3]. Despite extensive efforts in vaccine and antiviral drug development, as well as vector control, no universally effective countermeasures are currently available, making dengue a major and growing global health concern.

The dengue virus is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus that encodes three structural proteins: capsid (C), premembrane (prM), and envelope (E), and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5) [4]. There are four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4), each further subdivided into several genotypes. The overall amino acid sequence divergence between the serotypes is approximately 30% [5]. In many endemic settings, multiple DENV serotypes co-circulate, generating complex transmission dynamics and increasing the risk of secondary or multiple dengue infections, which are associated with more severe disease.

According to the 2009 WHO dengue case classification, clinical presentations are categorized into dengue without warning signs (DWoWS), dengue with warning signs (DWWS), and severe dengue (SD). While most dengue infections are asymptomatic or result in mild illness, a subset of patients develop severe forms, such as dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS), which are characterized by increased vascular permeability, plasma leakage, and shock [2]. Severe dengue is life-threatening and requires urgent medical intervention. Previous studies have indicated that SD is significantly more frequent in secondary DENV infections with a heterologous serotype than in primary infections due to mechanisms such as antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), exaggerated cytokine response, and T cell cross-reactivity [6, 7].

Multiple factors contribute to the development of severe dengue, including viral characteristics, host immune responses, and clinical management [8]. Geographic variation in clinical outcomes has also been well documented; for example, in Indonesia [9], where substantial regional differences in disease presentation and severity have been reported. Moreover, dengue serotypes, genotypes, and infection status (primary vs. secondary infection) have been linked to distinct clinical manifestations [10]. These observations highlight the importance of elucidating the viral and host factors associated with disease outcomes. However, environmental factors, human movement, and population growth may augment the incidence by affecting vector abundance and facilitating transmission [11, 12].

The Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Lao PDR) is a hyperendemic dengue region. Since 1979, dengue has been detected in the country, and a major outbreak was reported in 1985 [13]. The National Center for Laboratory and Epidemiology (NCLE) has continuously collected nationwide dengue surveillance data through the indicator-based National Surveillance System for Notifiable Selected Diseases, with reports submitted from healthcare facilities across the country. Suspected dengue patients based on WHO 2009 are reported daily, and the data are summarized by the NCLE and disseminated in a weekly report to the Ministry of Health and relevant institutions. This surveillance system reported that in 2010, DENV-1 was the most prevalent circulating serotype, with 22,890 cases and 46 deaths [14]. A large outbreak occurred in 2013, predominantly driven by DENV-3, resulting in 48,772 reported cases and 95 deaths [15]. Subsequently, DENV-1 predominated in 2015, followed by DENV-4 in 2016. In 2019, a substantial outbreak caused by DENV-2 was documented, with 39,901 cases and 76 deaths [16, 17]. Surveillance data from 2010 to 2016 indicated that all four serotypes were continuously present in the country [18]. Spatiotemporal analyses showed that 91.61% of positive cases in Vientiane Capital were primary DENV infections, with a pattern of strain replacement; DENV-2 predominated in 2019–2020, while DENV-1 was more prevalent in 2021 [19]. However, the most prominent high-risk clusters were detected in the southern region of Lao PDR [14]. Lao PDR has experienced a shift in the dominant genotype or serotype observed during major outbreaks, which has contributed to driving these epidemics and may also be associated with increased morbidity and mortality.

Nevertheless, data on DENV co-circulation and factors associated with severe dengue in Lao PDR, particularly their impact on clinical outcomes during outbreaks, remain limited. To address this gap, we analyzed epidemiological, viral, and clinical data to elucidate the drivers of dengue epidemics in the country. A better understanding of these dynamics may guide targeted surveillance and ultimately help reduce the dengue burden in affected regions.

Methods

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the National Ethics Committee for Health Research, Lao PDR (No. 48/NECHR), and the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Tropical Medicine, Nagasaki University (No. 240215305). All sample information was anonymized, and personal identifiers were removed to protect the patients’ confidentiality. Only coded data were used for the analyses. Samples were collected as part of a National Surveillance Program for public health purposes; therefore, individual written informed consent was not required. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study population and sample collection

Serum samples and accompanying epidemiological data, including demographic information, clinical manifestations, and laboratory findings, were obtained from the National Surveillance System in Lao PDR during 2022 and 2023. This system is conducted within the indicator-based National Surveillance System for Notifiable Selected Diseases, with reports submitted from healthcare facilities across the country. The sample size was determined by the availability of serum at the time of data collection, with all samples stored at the NCLE between 2022 and 2023 included in this study. A total of 1,098 patients with suspected dengue were enrolled from 13 of the 18 provinces across the country (Fig. 1a). Samples were collected from both inpatients and outpatients receiving care at sentinel hospitals that participated in the surveillance system.

Lao PDR is a landlocked country in Southeast Asia, comprising 17 provinces and one municipality, Vientiane Capital. The country is divided into three regions: the northern region, which includes Bokeo, Huaphanh, Luangnamtha, Luang Prabang, Oudomxay, Phongsaly, Xiengkhuang, and Xayaboury; the central region, encompassing Vientiane Capital and the provinces of Bolikhamxay, Khammouan, Savannakhet, Vientiane, and Xaysomboun; and the southern region, which includes Attapeu, Champasack, Sekong, and Saravane provinces. [20]. The northern region is predominantly mountainous and has a lower population density, ranging from approximately 11 to 29 people per square kilometer (people/km2). In contrast, the central and southern regions are characterized by lowland plains along the Mekong River and generally higher population densities, ranging from approximately 18 to 45 people/km2 and 14 to 45 people/km2, respectively [21]. Climatic conditions also vary across these regions. The mountainous northern region has a mean annual temperature of approximately 20 °C and receives relatively low annual rainfall, averaging about 1,566 mm. Conversely, the lowland central and southern regions experience higher temperatures, typically ranging from 25 to 27 °C, and greater rainfall, which varies from approximately 2,237 mm in the southern region to over 3,000 mm in some provinces of the central region [22, 23].

Dengue cases were classified according to the 2009 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as dengue without warning signs (DWoWS), dengue with warning signs (DWWS), and severe dengue (SD). Dengue infection status was determined using molecular and serological tests for DENV. A confirmed dengue-positive case was defined as any sample that tested positive using at least one of the following assays: NS1, IgM, or RT-PCR. Dengue-negative cases were defined as samples that tested negative using all three assays. Notably, NS1 testing was performed in healthcare facilities. However, not all samples were tested using this assay. In contrast, all samples were tested for IgM and IgG antibodies and RT-PCR.

Viral RNA extraction and dengue multiplex RT-PCR

Viral RNA was extracted from 140 µL of serum using the QIAamp® Viral RNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the ReverTra Ace kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), as previously described [24]. DENV serotyping was performed using conventional multiplex PCR in a total reaction volume of 50 µL. The reaction mixture contained 25 µL of KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (2 ×) (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), 10 mM of the D1 forward primer together with dengue serotype-specific primers (TS1–TS4), and approximately 50 ng of cDNA.

Serological detection of anti-DENV IgM and IgG antibodies

Dengue-specific IgM was detected using an in-house DENV IgM capture ELISA, as previously described [25]. Optical density (OD) was measured at 495 nm using a GloMax Explorer plate reader (Promega, United States). Sera were considered IgM-positive when the positive/negative (P/N) ratio was ≥ 2.

An in-house DENV IgG indirect ELISA was performed as previously described to determine the IgG titers [26, 27]. Serum IgG titers were quantified using standard curves. The cut-off value was defined as 1:1,000 ± 3 standard deviations, and samples with titers ≥ 1:3,000 were classified as IgG positive. An IgG titer ≥ 29,000 was considered indicative a secondary infection, whereas a titer < 29,000 was classified as a primary infection [28, 29].

Statistical analysis

For the descriptive analysis, categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Differences between groups, including DENV-positive versus DENV-negative status, DENV serotype (DENV-1 vs. DENV-2), disease severity (DWoWS, DWWS, SD), and infection status (primary vs. secondary infection), were compared using Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Nonparametric analysis was applied to variables with non-normal distributions. Laboratory parameters are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. The IgM P/N ratios and IgG titers were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Multinomial logistic regression was used to compare the independent variables and disease severity (DWoWS, DWWS, and SD). All statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., United States) and R v4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, https://www.r-project.org). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of DENV patients in Lao PDR, 2022–2023

A total of 1,098 serum samples from patients with suspected dengue were collected from three regions (north, central, and south) of Lao PDR. Among them, 615 (56%) were confirmed to be DENV-positive by RT-PCR, NS1, or IgM testing (Table 1). Confirmed DENV cases were distributed throughout the year in Lao PDR, with a seasonal peak from June to September during the rainy season (Fig. 1b). In 2023, the proportion of DENV-positive patients (n = 424, 68.9%) was significantly higher than that in 2022 (n = 191, 31.1%) (p < 0.0001). Between 2022 and 2023, the northern region had the highest proportion of DENV-positive patients (n = 230, 37.4%), followed by the southern (n = 197, 32%) and central regions (n = 188, 30.6%) (p < 0.0001). The positivity was the highest among 10–19 years (n = 189, 30.7%), followed by 20–29 years (n = 154, 25%), with the median age of DENV patients being 22 years (IQR 15–33 years). Females accounted for 55.1% (n = 339) of the DENV-positive patients. More than half of the DENV-positive patients (n = 403, 65.5%) were hospitalized, which was significantly higher than that among DENV-negative patients (p < 0.0001) (Table 2).

Among the DENV-positive patients, serotyping based on PCR was successful in 218 serum samples, identifying DENV-1 (n = 98, 45%) and DENV-2 (n = 120, 55%); no DENV-3 or DENV-4 were detected. The distribution of serotypes differed significantly by region: DENV-1 (n = 45, 45.9%) was the most common in the northern region, whereas DENV-2 (n = 75, 62.5%) was dominant in the southern region (p < 0.0001). However, primary infection was significantly more frequent among DENV-1 cases (n = 58, 59.2%), while DENV-2 infections were associated with a higher proportion of secondary infections (n = 80, 66.7%) (p = 0.0002) (Table 3).

Seroprevalence of dengue infection

Among suspected dengue patients, 37.1% were positive for anti-DENV IgM and 64.2% were positive for anti-DENV IgG antibodies (Table 1). Between 2022 and 2023, IgM positivity was significantly higher in the southern region than in the central and northern regions, whereas IgG positivity tended to be higher in the central and southern regions than in the northern regions (Fig. 2a and b). The 20–29 age groups showed high IgM P/N ratios, while IgG positivity significantly increased with age (Fig. 2c and d). Moreover, both the IgM P/N ratio and IgG titer were significantly higher in the DWWS group than in the DWoWS group (Fig. 2e and f).

Patients from the northern region were more likely to have primary infections (45.5%) than those from the central and southern regions (p = 0.030) (Fig. 3a). Primary DENV infection was more common in the 0–9 age group, followed by the 10–19 age group (Fig. 3b).

Characteristics of confirmed dengue cases stratified by disease severity

Clinical data were available for 486 patients with DENV infection. DWoWS was most frequently caused by DENV-1 infection (n = 56, 62.9%), whereas DWWS and SD were commonly caused by DENV-2 infection (n = 34, 56.7% and n = 10, 83.3%, respectively) (p = 0.002) (Table 4). The odds of developing SD were higher among patients in the southern (OR = 11.16, 95% CI: 3.05–40.77, p < 0.001) and central (OR 10.34, 95% CI: 2.81–38.09, p < 0.001) regions than among those in the northern region. Patients with secondary infection (versus primary infection) had higher odds of developing DWWS (OR 1.96, 95% CI: 1.23–3.1, p = 0.004) and SD (OR 2.38, 95% CI: 0.84–6.74, p = 0.102) (Fig. 4). While those older than 9 years had higher odds of developing DWWS, this trend was not obvious among SD cases.

Discussion

In this study, we identified several key patterns in dengue epidemiology in Lao PDR between 2022 and 2023. We found that these regions were significantly associated with SD. Additionally, secondary infection and DENV-2 infections were more likely to develop into severe dengue. Together, these findings highlight the complex interactions between epidemiology and dengue serotype in shaping the dengue burden and clinical outcomes.

The high prevalence of dengue positivity among adolescents and young adults was consistent with previous studies in these regions [30, 31]. The higher prevalence observed in this age group may be associated with increased engagement in outdoor activities, resulting in increased exposure to mosquito vectors. In addition, changes in circulating serotypes may increase susceptibility to infection and reflect prior exposure to partial immunity that does not protect against newly circulating serotypes [32]. Compared with young children (0–9 years), all older age groups were associated with DWWS. Dengue infection in older age groups may be affected by the presence of comorbidities (e.g., diabetes and hypertension) that may increase the risk of DWWS and SD [33].

We observed that the variations among regions in the country may be related to the clinical severity experienced in each region of the country. There was a higher chance of SD in the southern and central regions than in the northern regions. These findings could be explained by the higher poverty levels and limited access to healthcare services, particularly in the southern region [14, 20]. The lower population density in the northern region may affect the spread of dengue, which is consistent with our findings that primary infection was more common in this region [20, 34]. Our results also showed that DENV-2 was most prevalent in the southern regions (62.5%), whereas DENV-1 was more common in the northern regions (45.9%). Recent studies have indicated a serotype shift in the northern region, with DENV-1 gradually increasing from 15.2% in 2020 to 79.7% by 2023. Notably, DENV-2 remained the dominant serotype in the south throughout the four‑year observation period [30]. These clusters are likely shaped by past outbreak histories, environmental conditions, and climatic factors [14]. Previous studies in Lao PDR have shown that dengue transmission is significantly associated with regional and seasonal variability in temperature and rainfall patterns [14, 35]. To clarify the relationship between dengue disease risk and SD, continuous monitoring of both epidemic patterns and clinical severity across regions is warranted. Furthermore, serotype shifts over time may have increased the risk of severe dengue, highlighting the crucial role of population immunity, particularly in terms of secondary infections, in contributing to disease severity.

Our study also revealed substantial differences in seroprevalence among regions and age groups. A high seroprevalence was observed in the central and southern regions of Lao PDR (Fig. 2). While dengue is endemic nationwide, seroconversion rates are considerably higher in the southern regions than those reported in the north [36, 37]. This geographical pattern may indicate underlying transmission dynamics and underscore the significance of these regions as dengue transmission hotspots. Seroprevalence also increased with increasing age. These findings indicate an increased risk of secondary or multiple infections, which correlates with severe dengue outcomes. Consistent with previous studies, we identified elevated seroprevalence in older age groups, particularly in endemic areas [38]. This suggests the possibility of long-term immunity and ongoing asymptomatic exposure, especially in regions with continuous dengue virus (DENV) exposure.

In this study, DENV-2 was the predominant serotype and was associated with a higher frequency of SD than DENV-1. Previous studies in Thailand and Malaysia have similarly reported an increased risk of SD, including dengue DHF and DSS, among patients infected with DENV-2 [39, 40]. In some reports, DENV-2 infection has been associated with up to a sevenfold higher risk of SD than other serotypes [41]. Moreover, our results showed that only DENV-2 was more likely to be associated with increased bleeding manifestations, although this difference was not statistically significant (Table S1).

The association between secondary infection and an increased risk of SD compared to primary infection in our study is consistent with previous studies [7, 33, 42]. However, some reports indicate that the occurrence of SD during primary infection is more common in infants and young children [43]. Another possible contributing factor to the increased risk of SD during secondary infection is antibody-dependent enhancement [44]. Additionally, our study indicated that secondary infections were associated with several clinical features (Table S2), which is consistent with the findings of a previous study conducted in Savannakhet Province [45]. These clinical manifestations support a link between secondary infection and increased disease severity, although more rigorous statistical analyses are needed to further validate these associations and determine whether other symptoms are independently associated with SD.

Our study also revealed that more than half of the dengue-positive patients required hospitalization. Reports from 2022 and 2023 also show that dengue incidence exceeded the five-year median and demonstrated a well-defined seasonal distribution [46]. The high hospitalization rate may indicate delays in healthcare access and treatment resulting from referral patterns (from district to provincial hospitals) during the rainy season, when dengue transmission peaks, particularly in rural areas [45, 47]. An unexpected result was the high rate of hospitalization, even among DWoWS (Table 4). This pattern likely reflects a combination of factors that extend beyond clinical considerations. Dengue is unpredictable, especially during the critical phase when it might worsen quickly without proper care (e.g., by caretakers outside the healthcare settings) [48, 49]. Diagnostic uncertainty may also contribute to this phenomenon, as early dengue symptoms overlap with other febrile illnesses (e.g., scrub typhus and leptospirosis), prompting admission for observation and further evolution [50–52]. Although healthcare costs may be covered by national health insurance, some individuals still face out-of-pocket expenses [53]. These findings emphasize the need to strengthen the triage system and develop context-appropriate admission criteria to better balance patient safety and the optimal use of healthcare resources.

This study provides insightful information on dengue epidemiology, but some limitations exist. Our clinical and laboratory data from patients with suspected dengue were incomplete, limiting our ability to explore the detailed correlations between dengue serotype and disease outcomes. Therefore, these findings emphasize the necessity of in-depth serological tests (neutralizing and enhancement antibodies) to better understand the drivers of dengue epidemics in Lao PDR.

In conclusion, our study highlights the epidemiological, clinical, and dengue serotype that may affect dengue pathogenesis and severity. Greater disease severity was associated with DENV-2 infection, secondary infection, and residence in the central or southern regions. Our findings suggest the need for continuous, integrated surveillance to monitor viral spread, evolution, and clinical implications, which may ultimately help reduce dengue‑related morbidity and mortality. These findings enhance our understanding of the potential causes of severe dengue in the Lao PDR.
