Genome-wide characterization of lumpy skin disease virus isolate from Bangladesh
Authors: Md Bashir Uddin, Ruhena Begum, Jibon Kumar Paul, Md Mahfujur Rahman, Ho-Seong Cho, Md Mukter Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the genome-wide analysis of the Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) isolate from recent outbreaks in Bangladesh. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) revealed high sequence conservation with minor mutations and structural variations. Phylogenetic and genomic diversity assessments revealed 99.9% concordance, and identified several point mutations and small indels that contribute to strain-specific variability. The study identifies 156 unique protein-coding sequences, with 71.8% classified as soluble and 28.2% as membrane-associated proteins, shedding light on functional diversity. Repeat analysis highlights a predominance of short tandem repeats (10–30 bp) and low copy numbers (2–3), low entropy (0.39), and high thymine content (92%), suggesting selection for compact, stable elements that support genome integrity. These repeats are closely associated with genes, showing a 2:1 preference for alignment with gene orientation, indicating possible regulatory roles. Additionally, isochore patterns and conserved motifs within the repeats suggest functionality in transcription regulation or genome organization. GC content analysis revealed a bimodal distribution (peaks at 0.05% and 0.3%), indicating non-random placement of repeats. A strong correlation (r = 0.999) between repeat size and conservation score suggests that longer repeats may be essential for genome stability, while shorter repeats offer functional flexibility. These findings suggest the development of targeted LSDV control strategies.Keywords: LSDV, Genome, Taxonomy, Tandem repeats, Evolution, BangladeshSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Evolution, Microbiology

Introduction

Lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), a member of the Capripoxvirus genus within the Poxviridae family, poses significant challenges to cattle health and agricultural productivity worldwide. Initially confined to sub-Saharan Africa, LSDV has emerged as a global concern, with recent outbreaks in the Middle East, parts of Asia, and Europe1,2. The disease, characterized by fever, nodular skin lesions, lymphadenopathy, and substantial economic losses, also impacts international trade due to movement restrictions and decreased milk and meat production3–5.

The LSDV genome comprises a double-stranded DNA molecule of approximately 151 kbp. It is distinguished by a high A-T nucleotide content and a plethora of repetitive DNA sequences distributed across the genome5. Genome-wide studies have revealed high sequence conservation, yet point mutations and structural variations facilitate viral evolution and host adaptation6. Understanding LSDV’s genetic architecture through comparative genomics and phylogenetic analysis is crucial for vaccine development and outbreak management, particularly in regions with emerging recombinant strains5.

Understanding the evolutionary dynamics and regulatory roles of repetitive elements in the LSDV genome is critical for deciphering their contributions to viral adaptability and host interactions6–8. Recent genomic studies have highlighted the concept of an “open” pan-genome for LSDV, which demonstrates significant genetic variability among strains9. This variability is largely shaped by repetitive elements, which influence genetic recombination, genome plasticity, and regulatory mechanisms10. The repetitive sequences in the LSDV genome vary significantly, ranging from 6 to 87 base pairs 2 with copy numbers between 2 and 8.26,11. These variations suggest differential functional roles, while their high conservation (percent matches > 80%) indicates evolutionary pressure to retain them for functions such as recombination hotspots or regulatory elements10,12.

Clusters of repeats, such as those spanning positions 15–21, are closely associated with gene-rich regions, suggesting potential roles in transcriptional regulation and genome organization13. Repeats exhibit high entropy values (> 1.5), reflecting sequence complexity and adaptability to diverse host environments14. Historically dismissed as “junk” DNA, repetitive elements are now recognized as regulators of gene expression and genome organization, enabling viral adaptation and immune evasion15. For example, tandem repeats facilitate genome plasticity, regulate transcription and replication, and contribute to the variability necessary for overriding host defenses7,16,17.

Specific motifs, including AT-rich and GC-rich sequences, play structural and regulatory roles, such as facilitating DNA packaging and replication control18,19. In LSDV strains from natural outbreaks, G-quadruplex-forming repeat regions were found to be differentially methylated and expressed, suggesting their involvement in viral adaptation and gene regulation. The presence of AT-rich motifs supports DNA organization, while GC-rich regions are implicated in both structural and regulatory functions. These features offer potential therapeutic applications, including RNA interference targeting G-rich mRNAs to regulate gene expression20,21.

Further, correlation analysis of LSDV genome features reveals strong relationships among repeat characteristics. Period size and consensus size exhibit a near-perfect positive correlation, indicating their intrinsic linkage22. Similarly, a robust correlation between period size and score suggests that longer repeats may have greater functional importance due to their conservation and complexity23. Conversely, shorter repeats occur at higher frequencies, potentially enhancing their accessibility for regulatory interactions24.

By contributing to genomic plasticity and regulatory complexity, repetitive elements enhance the promiscuous recombination ability of LSDV, enabling adaptive responses to host environments and immune pressures. Their structural and functional properties underscore their importance in viral evolution and pathogenesis, highlighting their regulatory and structural roles, and emphasizing the necessity of further studies to elucidate their precise roles and potential for therapeutic interventions. As LSDV continues to expand its geographical footprint, understanding these genomic features will be pivotal in developing effective strategies for disease control and prevention. The findings presented here contribute to a growing body of evidence that highlights the new avenues for therapeutic interventions and outbreak management for LSDV in animals.

Methods and materials

Ethical statement

All animal sampling procedures and protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sylhet Agricultural University, Sylhet, Bangladesh (Protocol No. AUP2020020). The informed consent was obtained from the owner of the cattle during sampling. All methods were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations set by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Sylhet Agricultural University. This study has been reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Sample collection

Clinical samples were collected from cattle exhibiting characteristic signs of Lumpy Skin Disease (LSD), including fever, nodular skin lesions, enlarged superficial lymph nodes, and reduction in milk yield. Sampling was performed during active outbreaks from geographically distinct districts in Bangladesh between January and May 2023 to ensure regional representation. Nodular skin lesions and scabs were aseptically excised from affected cattle and preserved in viral transport medium (VTM) containing antibiotics (penicillin, streptomycin) to inhibit bacterial contamination (Fig. 1). Samples were immediately transported on ice to the Laboratory of Medicine, Sylhet Agricultural University, and stored at – 80 °C until further processing. Metadata (age, sex, breed, location, clinical history) were recorded for all animals.

Samples were collected from cattle exhibiting clinical signs of LSDV Infection.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total DNA was extracted using a phenol–chloroform protocol optimized to enrich high-quality viral DNA while minimizing host contamination. Briefly, tissues were homogenized and lysed in lysis buffer containing proteinase K, followed by phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extraction and ethanol precipitation. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using the NanoDrop and Qubit fluorometer. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep kit and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end 150 bp reads with > 100 × genome coverage per sample.

Genome assembly and annotation

Raw sequencing reads underwent adapter trimming and quality filtering using Trimmomatic v0.3925 with default parameters (leading, trailing, slidingwindow:4:15, minlen:36). High-quality reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes v3.15.326 under the “– careful” option to minimize misassemblies. Contigs < 500 bp or with coverage < 5 × were removed. Assembled contigs were scaffolded using reference-guided alignment to LSDV/02/KASH/IND/2022 (NCBI Accession: OQ588787.1) using Mauve27 and BLASTn28. Gene prediction and functional annotation were carried out using Prokka v1.14.629, incorporating a custom LSDV database and using the– kingdom Viruses setting. Data Processing and Quality Control.

Sequencing data were processed using CLC Genomics Workbench v21.0.430. Low-quality bases (Phred < 20) and adapter sequences were trimmed, and reads < 50 bp were discarded. Cleaned reads were assembled into contigs using de novo assembly, then aligned to the reference genome LSDV/02/KASH/IND/2022 (OQ588787.1) with high stringency (≥ 95% similarity). Key metrics, including total reads, mapped reads, genome coverage, and consensus length, were calculated to assess assembly quality (Table S1).

Genome clustering and taxonomy

ViPTree31 was employed to construct a proteomic tree of Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) sequences based on genome-wide sequence similarities. This analysis incorporated all available LSDV genomes, including those from the queries, as well as related viral genomes, to generate a comprehensive taxonomic framework and explore the evolutionary relationships within the LSDV strains and their close relatives. The resulting tree provided a comparative overview of the evolutionary divergence among LSDV strains and allowed visualization of the relationship between field isolates and vaccine-associated genomes.

Identifying protein like sequence

Eight complete genome sequences of Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) isolated from Bangladesh were retrieved from the NCBI Nucleotide database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/?term=Lumpy+skin+disease+virus+complete+genome%2C+bangladesh ). Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.332 under default parameters optimized for viral genomes.

The translated protein sequences were then subjected to clustering using CD-HIT v4.8.133 with a sequence identity threshold of 95% to remove redundancy across genomes. Only non-redundant protein sequences were retained for downstream functional annotation and domain characterization. This approach ensured accurate representation of the unique proteomic content of the Bangladeshi LSDV isolates.

Probable protein characterization

The identified Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) protein sequences underwent comprehensive characterization. Protein solubility and subcellular localization were predicted using the SOSUI34, with a focus on distinguishing soluble proteins from membrane-associated proteins, including those with a single transmembrane span. The isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (Mw) of the proteins were computed using IPC2.035, while the Grand Average of Hydropathy (GRAVY) index and the Aliphatic Index were determined using the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). To assess functional domains, the protein sequences were analyzed using HMMER v3.3.236, querying both the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). Signal peptide prediction was performed employing multiple tools, including SignalIP6.037, to identify the presence of signal peptides.

Protein phylogeny analysis

Multiple sequence alignments of the non-redundant protein sequences identified from Bangladeshi LSDV isolates were carried out using MEGA1138, employing the ClustalW algorithm with default settings. The resulting phylogenetic trees were exported in Newick format and visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL v6) platform (https://itol.embl.de). Protein clusters were annotated to reflect sequence conservation and divergence, enabling a clearer understanding of potential functional evolution across LSDV isolates.

Comparative genomics and evolutionary relationships

Comparative genomic analyses were conducted by aligning the Bangladeshi LSDV genomes with vaccine strains, reference strains, and known recombinant variants using MEGA11. Nucleotide-level differences were identified through multiple sequence alignment using the MUSCLE algorithm, and phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method with the Tamura-Nei model and 1000 bootstrap replications to ensure reliability. In addition, sequence metadata such as coverage depth, total and mapped reads, consensus genome length, and GC content were calculated to confirm assembly quality. The phylogenetic analysis provided insights into the evolutionary proximity of Bangladeshi LSDV strains to vaccine-derived and recombinant lineages, aiding in the understanding of regional strain dynamics.

Genome data collection and sequence alignment matrix

The complete genome sequence of Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) was obtained from publicly available genomic databases, including GenBank and the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA). Genomes from multiple LSDV isolates were selected to ensure a representative dataset for diversity analysis. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.475 to identify conserved regions and repetitive elements39.

Isochore analysis

Isochore analysis of the LSDV genome was performed using the EMBOSS isochore40 tool to assess compositional variation along the ~ 150 kb genome. The analysis calculated GC content across non-overlapping sliding windows (default size of 1000 bp), enabling the identification of compositionally distinct domains. The results revealed notable heterogeneity in GC content, with values ranging from approximately 20% to 35%. Several regions exhibited distinct GC-rich peaks approaching the upper bound, indicating localized compositional bias. These variations suggest the presence of putative functional domains or regulatory elements and reflect the complex structural organization of the LSDV genome. The isochore profile may also correlate with transcriptional activity, replication origins, or evolutionary conservation across poxviruses.

Tandem repeat identification and analysis

Tandem repeats in the LSDV genome were identified using the Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) software v4.0941. Default parameters were used, allowing for flexible detection of repeat patterns, with a minimum repeat size of 6 bp and a maximum of 87 bp, based on earlier findings42,43. Metrics such as period size, copy number, consensus size, percent matches, percent indels, and entropy were calculated to characterize each repeat. Repeat orientation relative to nearby coding regions was also recorded44,45.

GC content and motif analysis

GC content analysis of repetitive sequences and the overall genome was conducted using BEDTools v2.29.246. Statistical tests, including a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, were applied to assess the distribution of GC content between repeats and the genome47. Conserved motifs within the tandem repeats were identified using MEME Suite v5.5, focusing on both AT-rich and GC-rich sequences48. Motif significance was determined based on E-values.

Proximity and correlation analysis

To explore how tandem repeats are positioned relative to coding regions, this study calculated their spatial distances using custom Python scripts powered by the Biopython library49. This research examined relationships between various repeat features—like period size, consensus length, and score—using Spearman’s rank correlation in R50. The results were brought to life with visualizations created using the ggplot2 package51.

Data validation and cross-species comparison

This studyvalidated the tandem repeats by checking their conservation and potential roles across other Capripoxvirus genomes, including those of goatpox and sheeppox viruses. By comparing their sequences to known regulatory or structural elements in the Poxviridae family, this research inferred their possible biological functions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and visualization were performed by Python (v3.8) with libraries: pandas for data manipulation; scipy for statistical calculations; numpy for numerical analysis; matplotlib for plotting. To ensure robustness, the findings were cross-checked with previously published studies on viral repetitive elements52.

Results

Genome sequencing and quality

The sequencing of LSDV genomes yielded high-quality data, evidenced by consistent coverage across the genome (Figure S1). The color-coded sequence alignment matrix confirmed accurate alignment of reads and other variations. Confidence in sequencing quality was reinforced by uniformly high base call quality scores, critical for downstream analyses such as phylogenetics and repeat characterization (Figure S2).

Genomic organization and gene annotation

The annotated LSDV genome (GenBank Accession: PP979138.1) revealed well-distributed genes marked by transcription direction, enabling insights into replication, immune evasion, and host interactions. The circular map visualization provided an overview of the genome’s structural organization and facilitated the identification of functional clusters and recombination hotspots (Figure S3).

Taxonomy assignment

In this study, a proteomic tree of Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) was constructed using genome-wide sequence similarities to explore the evolutionary relationships among all virus strains and their close relatives. The analysis incorporated all available virus genome sequences, including the query genome SAUVM-78 (Accession: PP979138.1), along with related viral genomes from the Capripoxvirus genus, including Sheeppox virus and Goatpox virus. Using ViPTree, a comprehensive taxonomic framework was established by aligning the protein sequences of these viruses and generating a phylogenetic tree. The resulting tree revealed distinct clustering patterns among LSDV strains, highlighting the genetic divergence within the species and providing insights into their evolutionary history. The analysis also facilitated the comparison of LSDV with closely related poxviruses, shedding light on interspecies relationships and evolutionary trends within the Poxviridae family (Fig. 2).

Protein identification

Here, we identify and characterize protein-coding sequences of the LSDV strain SAUVM-78 along with all WGS of LSDV isolates from Bangladesh. Using Prodigal, a total of 1,113 protein-coding sequences were identified across the genomes (Supplementary file 1). This process resulted in the identification of 156 unique protein sequences, while the remaining sequences were found to exhibit redundancy. These findings underscore the conserved nature of protein sequences within the LSDV strains from Bangladesh, providing valuable insights into the protein composition and genetic diversity of the virus in this region.

Protein characterization

Protein solubility and subcellular localization

The SOSUI tool classified the identified Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) protein sequences into membrane-associated and soluble proteins. Among the 156 total proteins analyzed, 112 (71.8%) were identified as soluble proteins, while 44 (28.2%) were membrane-associated (Fig. 3B). Further analysis revealed that 30 proteins contained a single transmembrane helix, double transmembrane helix in 8 proteins, and triple transmembrane helix found in only 3 associated (Fig. 3B).

Physicochemical properties

The biochemical characterization of a set of 156 proteins was conducted, evaluating key properties including molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI), aliphatic index, and the Grand Average of Hydropathy (GRAVY) index. The molecular weights of the proteins varied substantially, ranging from 11.6 kDa to 27.4 kDa, with a mean molecular weight of approximately 17.2 kDa, indicating a diverse set of protein sizes (Fig. 3A). Remarkably, the isoelectric point (pI) for all proteins was uniformly 7.0, suggesting a neutral charge at physiological pH, which may imply a Functional relevance in cellular environments where pH homeostasis is critical. The aliphatic index, which is reflective of the volume fraction of aliphatic residues in a protein, exhibited variability between 24.0 and 53.0, with an average of 33.4, denoting a moderate enrichment in aliphatic amino acids, which are often associated with protein stability and structural rigidity (Fig. 3C). The GRAVY index, which quantifies the hydrophobicity or hydrophilicity of the proteins, demonstrated a wide range of values from -0.914 to + 0.873. Positive GRAVY scores, indicative of hydrophobic proteins, were observed alongside negative values, signifying a diverse set of hydrophilic proteins. The mean GRAVY index of approximately 0.19 suggests an overall tendency towards hydrophilic behavior within this protein set. These results offer profound insights into the structural and functional implications of the proteins under investigation, providing a foundational understanding of their physicochemical properties that may govern their interactions and roles in cellular processes.

Signal peptide and transmembrane domain prediction

Signal peptide analysis was conducted using multiple tools, including SignalP6.0, Phobius, and TOPCONS, to ensure high confidence in the predictions. The results revealed that only 10 proteins were predicted to contain signal peptides (Fig. 3D), suggesting their potential involvement in secretion pathways or membrane localization.

Functional domain analysis

Protein sequences were analyzed using HMMER, querying both the Pfam and NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD). A total of 180 distinct types of domains were identified in the proteins. A detailed analysis was conducted on a set of protein sequences from various domains, including Poxvirus Bcl-2-like proteins, interleukins, and several unknown functions. The proteins’ characteristics were assessed using key bioinformatics parameters, including E-values, scores, and domain estimations. For instance, the Poxv_Bcl-2-like protein (PF06227.17) demonstrated an E-value of 2.5e-35 with a score of 121.9, indicating a highly significant alignment to its domain. Similarly, the IL10 (PF00726.22) protein showed a remarkably low E-value of 3.2e-43 and a score of 147.6, marking it as a highly reliable match with its functional annotation. The analysis also highlighted proteins such as IL22 (PF14565.11) and RPW8 (PF05659.16), with scores of 25.2 and 17.4, respectively, demonstrating functional insights linked to interleukins and plant resistance mechanisms (Supplementary file 2). Additionally, several proteins with unknown functions were identified, such as DUF2718 (PF10918.13), displaying a moderate score of 43.9, which may suggest potential areas for further functional elucidation. These findings provide a comprehensive insight into the diversity of protein functions, from viral defense mechanisms to immune regulation, and underscore the importance of predicting functional domains and potential biological roles.

Protein phylogeny analysis

Clustering of the 156 unique LSDV protein sequences was performed using MMseqs2 with a sequence identity threshold of 90%. The analysis revealed the formation of 155 distinct clusters, of which the majority were singletons—individual proteins that did not cluster with any other sequence (Figure S4). This result indicates a high degree of sequence diversity among the predicted protein products, suggesting minimal redundancy and widespread functional specialization across the LSDV genome. The presence of numerous singleton clusters implies that most viral proteins possess unique amino acid sequences, which may reflect evolutionary divergence, strain-specific adaptations, or selective pressures acting on different functional classes of viral proteins.

Evolutionary relationships

Phylogenetic analysis revealed a close genetic relationship between the Bangladeshi strains and reference, vaccine, and recombinant strains, with clustering near vaccine strains like Neethling and Kenyan sheep and goat pox vaccines. Minor genomic variations were predominantly localized in non-coding regions, indicating high conservation of coding sequences across different strains. This conservation supports the effectiveness of vaccines targeting these regions, despite the presence of indels suggesting ongoing viral evolution in the region (Fig. 4).

Isochore patterns

The isochore patterns (Fig. 6) in the LSDV genome reveal substantial heterogeneity in GC content, ranging between 20 and 35%, characteristic of AT-rich genomes commonly observed in poxviruses53. Several localized regions exhibited sharp GC-rich peaks, particularly near terminal genomic ends and gene-dense regions. These compositional shifts may be linked to regulatory functions, differential gene expression, or structural constraints. The observed isochore pattern aligns with the organization of other poxvirus genomes and may reflect modular genome evolution. The substantial fluctuations indicate a non-uniform genome structure, potentially reflecting different functional regions or evolutionary pressures. Sudden shifts in GC content suggest potential regulatory hotspots critical for controlling gene expression, replication, and host-virus interactions. The distinct isochore pattern may provide clues about the evolutionary history of LSDV and its genomic adaptations. Functionally, GC content heterogeneity influences DNA stability, transcriptional activity, and protein folding. This structural organization might enable LSDV to optimize its genome for efficient replication and immune evasion within the host environment.

This analysis was Further enriched by examining repeat proximity and orientation with genes, suggesting a potential role in transcriptional regulation. The high AT content and entropy in key repeats, particularly repeat 11, may facilitate flexible DNA secondary structures aiding in replication or packaging. Additionally, the observed orientation bias (2:1 same-strand repeats) strongly indicates Functional co-evolution with nearby genes. Together, these observations underscore the role of tandem repeats not merely as passive elements but as active players in genome plasticity, stability, and regulatory evolution of LSDV. The distribution of repeat sizes in the LSDV genome shows a clear trend toward shorter repeats. The histogram reveals that the majority of repeats have period sizes between 10 and 30 base pairs, with the highest frequency occurring around 15–20 bp (Fig. 7A). This predominance of short repeats suggests that the LSDV genome may have evolved to favor compact repetitive elements, possibly for reasons related to genome stability or functionality.

Copy number distribution

Analysis of the tandem repeat copy number distribution in the LSDV genome revealed that the majority of repeats occur at low copy numbers, with the most frequent being 2–3 copies per locus (Fig. 7B). This skewed distribution pattern suggests a tight genomic regulation of repeat expansion, likely to minimize genome instability while still enabling a degree of genetic plasticity. Low-copy tandem repeats are often more stable and less prone to recombination-driven genomic rearrangements, whereas higher-copy elements, though rare, may contribute to localized mutational hotspots. This controlled repeat proliferation may represent a balance between evolutionary adaptability and the preservation of genome integrity in LSDV.

Genome overview

The genome overview of LSDV presents a visual map of gene and tandem repeat distributions across the entire ~ 150 kb viral genome (Fig. 8). Blue bars, representing annotated protein-coding genes, appear to be uniformly distributed, reflecting a relatively consistent gene density along both terminal and central genomic regions. In contrast, red bars, denoting tandem repeats, show a sporadic and uneven distribution, with certain genomic loci exhibiting localized clustering of repeats. These clustered regions are often positioned near intergenic spaces or adjacent to genes implicated in host interaction and immune evasion, suggesting a possible role in cis-regulatory modulation or structural genome flexibility. This spatial separation between genes and repeat elements may indicate functional compartmentalization within the LSDV genome, balancing genome stability with evolutionary potential.

Correlation matrix of repeat characteristics

The correlation heatmap (Fig. 9) reveals several interesting relationships between repeat characteristics: (1) Period Size and Consensus Size show a near-perfect positive correlation (0.999462), which is expected due to their close relationship. (2) Period Size and Score have a strong positive correlation (0.867068), suggesting that longer repeats tend to score higher, likely because of increased sequence complexity or conservation. (3) Copy Number shows a weak negative correlation with both Period Size (– 0.217333) and Consensus Size (– 0.220953), indicating that shorter repeats may occur more frequently in higher copy numbers. (4) Score and Copy Number display a very weak positive correlation (0.051556), suggesting that the number of copies has minimal influence on the repeat score. The LSDV genome analysis reveals a preference for short, low-copy-number repeats distributed non-uniformly across the genome. This pattern may reflect evolutionary pressures to maintain genome stability while allowing for functional diversity. The strong correlation between repeat size and score suggests that longer repeats might have more significant functional roles or be under stronger selective pressure. The low average copy number (3.50) and relatively short average period size (30.62 bp) suggest that the LSDV genome has not experienced recent large-scale repeat expansions. This could be indicative of a stable genome structure, which may be important for the virus’s life cycle and host interactions.

Proximity of repeats to genes

The analysis shows that all repeats (fraction = 1.00) are located within 1 kb of genes in the LSDV genome. This striking result suggests a strong association between repetitive elements and coding regions. Such proximity could indicate potential regulatory roles for these repeats, such as: (1) influencing gene expression by acting as cis-regulatory elements; (2) providing binding sites for transcription factors;(3) affecting chromatin structure near genes; and (4) playing a role in post-transcriptional regulation. This close association may also indicate co-evolution of repeats with genes, potentially contributing to the compact genome organization typical of viruses. The perfect co-localization of repeats with genes suggests that these repetitive elements are integral to the LSDV genome structure and function. Their consistent proximity to genes implies a potential role in gene regulation or genome organization that has been strongly conserved.

GC content of repeats

The average GC content of repeats (0.26%) is identical to the genome-wide GC content (0.26%). This finding is unusual, as repetitive elements often exhibit distinct GC content compared to the rest of the genome. The distribution graph shows a wide range of GC content in repeats, from about 0.05% to 0.5%, with a peak around 0.3% (Fig. 10). The analysis of GC content further emphasizes the non-random distribution of repetitive elements, revealing a bimodal pattern that suggests structural and regulatory functions. Despite the identical average, the statistical test shows a highly significant difference in GC content distribution between repeats and the overall genome (p-value ≈ 8.44e-55). This extremely low p-value signifies that the GC content pattern in repeats is non-random and differs significantly from the genomic background. The GC content analysis presents a nuanced picture. While the average GC content of repeats aligns with the genome-wide average, the distribution shows distinct patterns. This variation may indicate different functional classes of repeats, each characterized by its own GC content profile. The extremely low p value for GC content enrichment, despite identical averages, highlights the importance of looking beyond simple averages. Together, these findings collectively suggest that the repetitive elements in the LSDV genome are subject to selective pressure and likely serve important functional roles. Their consistent association with genes and distinct GC content patterns indicates they are not merely “junk DNA” but rather integral components of the viral genome.

Repeat-gene orientation

The analysis reveals that out of 21 repeats, 14 are oriented in the same direction as their nearest genes, while 7 are in the opposite orientation. This 2:1 ratio suggests a preference for repeats to align with gene orientation (Fig. 11). This bias suggests potential functional implications. Repeats oriented in the same direction as genes may enhance gene expression by serving as auxiliary promoters or facilitating transcriptional read-through, thereby improving transcriptional efficiency. Additionally, this alignment could play a structural role by contributing to higher-order chromatin organization that is favorable for gene expression. The observed orientation bias also indicates evolutionary conservation, reflecting selection pressures that maintain this relationship between repeats and genes, underscoring its functional significance in the genome.

Common motifs in repeats

The analysis identified several highly recurring motifs within the repeats. The top motifs (ATTAGGTTTAATTGG and its variations) appear multiple times, suggesting a strong sequence conservation. These motifs could act as regulatory elements by serving as binding sites for transcription factors or other regulatory proteins, thereby influencing gene expression. Additionally, the conserved sequences may contribute to DNA packaging or genome organization within the virus particle, playing a structural role in maintaining genomic integrity. Some motifs might also function as replication signals, initiating or regulating viral DNA replication. The presence of both AT-rich and GC-rich motifs further indicates that different repeat sequences may have diverse functional roles within the viral genome.

Repeat length vs distance to nearest gene

The scatter plot and correlation analysis provide insights into the spatial relationship between repeats and genes (Fig. 11). The Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.069 indicates a very weak positive relationship between repeat length and the distance to the nearest gene. The scatter plot reveals high variability, with considerable dispersion, suggesting that repeat length is not a reliable predictor of proximity to genes. Despite this variability, many repeats are clustered within 200–400 bp of the nearest gene, regardless of their length. The lack of a strong correlation (p value = 0.7665) suggests that repeat length and gene proximity are largely independent. This observation suggests that repeat function may depend more on sequence composition than on length. Additionally, the compact nature of the viral genome may limit the potential for length-dependent positioning of repeats relative to genes.

Together, these results presents a complex picture of repeat organization and potential function in the LSDV genome: (1) the preference for same-orientation alignment with genes suggests that many repeats may have co-evolved with genes to enhance or regulate their expression; (2) the highly conserved motifs within repeats indicate specific functional roles, possibly in transcription regulation or genome organization. The diversity of these motifs advocates multiple functional classes of repeats; (3) the weak correlation between repeat length and gene proximity, along with the clustering of repeats near genes, indicates that the repeats’ functional significance of repeats may be more closely related their sequence composition and general proximity to genes rather than their exact length or distance; and (4) the compact nature of the viral genome likely influences the organization of repeats, favoring those that can perform multiple functions (e.g., structural and regulatory) close to genes. While much of the current study presents descriptive genome-wide data, it lays the foundation for hypothesis-driven functional studies. The integration of motif analysis, protein domain prediction, and correlation matrices attempts to bridge raw sequence data with biological significance. Nevertheless, this study recognize that certain interpretations, particularly regarding adaptability or regulation, require experimental confirmation through transcriptomics or reverse genetics.

Discussion

The comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the LSDV genome reveals intricate relationships between evolutionary structure, proteomic analysis, genome organization, genomic stability, and functional implications of tandem repeats, shedding light on the potential regulatory roles of repetitive elements and their contribution to viral adaptability.

The proteomic analysis of Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV) isolates from Bangladesh revealed significant genetic diversity and biochemical properties across 156 unique protein sequences. Clustering at a ≥ 95% sequence identity threshold identified 155 clusters, mostly singletons, highlighting the vast diversity within the LSDV proteome. Physicochemical characterization showed molecular weights ranging from 11.6 to 27.4 kDa, a uniform pI of 7.0, and an aliphatic index of 24.0–53.0, indicating moderate stability. The average GRAVY index of 0.19 suggests a tendency toward hydrophilicity. HMMER analysis identified 180 functional domains, including viral defense and immune regulation elements, with key proteins like Poxv_Bcl-2-like and IL10 showing strong domain alignments. Membrane-associated and secretory proteins were predicted, with 10 containing signal peptides and others exhibiting single or double transmembrane helices. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed high sequence diversity, reinforcing the evolutionary complexity of LSDV. This in-depth proteomic profiling advances the understanding of LSDV pathogenesis, immune evasion, and potential therapeutic targets, providing a valuable resource for future antiviral research.

The evolutionary analysis confirmed the close genetic relationship between Bangladeshi strains and vaccine strains, particularly Neethling and Kenyan sheep and goat pox vaccines. This high conservation of coding regions suggests that current vaccines remain effective despite minor genomic variations in non-coding regions. Such variations, including indels, may reflect local adaptations or ongoing recombination events, as reported in other Capripoxvirus studies54,55. The alignment matrix highlighted point mutations and structural variations contributing to strain-specific characteristics. These findings underscore the importance of genomic surveillance to monitor the evolution of vaccine-derived strains and field isolates56.

The observed isochore patterns emphasize the interplay between genomic composition and functional organization in LSDV. These patterns also underline the evolutionary strategy of LSDV to balance genome stability with adaptive flexibility, a feature critical for survival in varying host environments7,10,57. The observed heterogeneity may serve as a mechanism for genome compactness, ensuring high adaptability and efficient use of genetic material. Recent studies highlight the critical role of GC variability in modulating gene expression, emphasizing its functional implications in viral pathogenicity and evolution7,22,52.

Further, the observed variability in repeat size (6–87 bp) and copy number (2–8.2) reflects the functional diversity of these elements within the LSDV genome. The predominance of low-copy-number repeats suggests a controlled proliferation mechanism, likely optimized to maintain genomic stability while permitting a degree of genetic variability. This balance is crucial for the virus to adapt to dynamic host environments and immune responses6,58. Furthermore, the clustering of repeats in regions such as 15–21 positions plays their role as potential regulatory hotspots, influencing transcriptional activity or chromatin structure near associated genes5,59.

The entropy analysis highlights the complexity of these repeats, with most showing high entropy values (> 1.5), indicative of sequence diversity and evolutionary adaptability. The predominance of shorter repeats, particularly those between 10 and 30 bp, suggests an evolutionary preference for compact elements that balance genome stability with functional flexibility10,14. Exceptions, such as Repeat 11, with low entropy (0.39) and extremely high T content (92%), suggest specialized roles in genome packaging or regulation. These findings align with earlier studies on Poxviridae, emphasizing the structural and regulatory significance of conserved repetitive sequences55,60.

The spatial organization of tandem repeats, with 100% located within 1 kb of coding regions, underscores their likely regulatory roles. Such proximity suggests that these elements act as cis-regulatory elements, transcription factor binding sites, or modulators of chromatin structure61–63. The observed 2:1 orientation bias of repeats aligning with gene direction further supports their involvement in transcriptional facilitation, potentially enhancing gene expression or promoting transcriptional read-through64,65.

Moreover, conserved AT-rich motifs within the repeats, such as ATTAGGTTTAATTGG, highlight their potential for transcription regulation and viral replication44,45. These motifs may contribute to chromatin accessibility, facilitating interactions with host transcriptional machinery and influencing viral replication5,59.

The correlation between repeat features, including period size and consensus size (r > 0.99), emphasizes the intrinsic relationships governing their functional importance. Longer repeats, correlated with higher conservation scores, may play more significant roles in genome stability or viral adaptability44,45. Conversely, the prevalence of shorter repeats, with higher copy numbers, suggests their accessibility for regulatory functions and adaptability to diverse host environments52.

The bimodal GC content distribution of repeats, despite a genome-wide average of 26%, suggests distinct Functional subclasses potentially linked to regulatory and structural roles. Peaks at approximately 0.05% and 0.3% GC content indicate that some repeats may have evolved as structural elements while others serve regulatory purposes22,66. These findings support the hypothesis that LSDV tandem repeats are not mere genomic byproducts but have undergone evolutionary selection to optimize their roles within the viral genome67.

The findings suggest that tandem repeats in the LSDV genome are not random elements but integral components of its genetic architecture. While this study highlight sequence-based indicators of adaptability, including mutation hotspots and repeat-mediated genome plasticity, it is important to note that these findings are limited to direct clinical samples. Without serial passages, host adaptation experiments, or long-term surveillance, the evolutionary implications remain inferential. Thus, our use of “adaptability” reflects genetic signatures suggestive of such potential, rather than confirmed phenotypic adaptation over time. Their conservation, proximity to genes, and sequence-specific motifs indicate roles in gene regulation, genome organization, and replication. The clustering of repeats near functional regions may position them as evolutionary hotspots, allowing the virus to adapt to diverse host environments while maintaining structural stability61,67,68.

This study reinforces the importance of tandem repeats in viral evolution, supporting their potential as diagnostic markers or therapeutic targets. For instance, conserved motifs within repeats could serve as biomarkers for early detection or as molecular targets to disrupt viral replication2,8,69.

While this study highlights the importance of tandem repeats in the LSDV genome, further research is needed to elucidate their precise roles, with a large number of LSDV strains. This study acknowledge that isolation of live LSDV from clinical specimens could have enabled a more comprehensive evaluation of viral phenotypes, including replication kinetics, cytopathic effects, and host range. However, due to resource constraints and the need for high-containment lab facilities, this study prioritized whole-genome sequencing directly from field samples. Future work incorporating viral culture and in vitro assays will be essential to correlate genetic findings with phenotypic outcomes. Advanced techniques such as CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing and high-resolution transcriptomics could validate the functional implications of specific repeats. Additionally, experimental studies focusing on the interactions between LSDV repeats and host cellular machinery could uncover novel therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

The LSDV genome exhibits a tightly organized structure with a balance between stability and functional adaptability. The LSDV genome discloses that its tandem repeats are highly conserved, short, and closely associated with coding regions, suggesting regulatory roles in transcription and genome organization. The uniform gene distribution and minimal repeat expansions highlight strong evolutionary constraints maintaining genome stability. The spatial clustering of repeats near genes and their conserved motifs underscores their regulatory importance. Phylogenetic studies show high genetic conservation among Bangladeshi strains, aligning them with vaccine strains, supporting vaccine efficacy and viral evolution. The GC content and orientation bias of repeats near genes further indicate their functional significance. These findings underscore the evolutionary adaptability and compactness of the LSDV genome, offering critical insights for vaccine and therapeutic developments.
