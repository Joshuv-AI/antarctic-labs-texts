Discovery of novel METTL3 inhibitors: design, synthesis, and in silico and in vitro evaluations
Authors: Yun Zhang, Mengyan Chen, Yanwen He, Qinlan Chen, Hongdi Han, Xianling Zhang, Yihan Qian, Haiying Jiang, Xiangwei Xu, Zunyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methyltransferase-like protein 3 (METTL3) is a promising therapeutic target for Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML). To discover novel METTL3 inhibitors, we designed a series of compounds by hybridizing the natural product Genistein and the non-nucleoside METTL3 inhibitor STM2457. Among the 31 de novo-generated compounds subjected to molecular docking, the top-scoring hit H1 was selected for chemical synthesis and its structure was confirmed by NMR and MS. H1 showed an inhibitory activity against METTL3 with an IC50 value of 980.3 nM, significantly better than Genistein (37.31% inhibition at 40 µM). Molecular docking revealed that H1's binding mode was similar to STM2457, forming hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions within the METTL3 pocket. Druglikeness prediction by SwissADMET and ADMETlab 3.0 showed that H1 exhibited to be within an acceptable range. This study validated our design strategy and provided a foundation for developing more potent and selective METTL3 inhibitors.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant hematological disease marked by the clonal proliferation of hematopoietic stem cells. It's the most common leukemia in adults, with incidence increasing with age, posing a severe threat to health. During AML treatment, issues like rising drug resistance and side effects are inevitable, causing physical and psychological harm to patients.

Methyltransferase-like protein 3 (METTL3), by catalyzing the methylation of mRNA encoding oncogenic transcription factors, impacts AML progression. Its absence in AML leads to cell cycle arrest and impaired differentiation and growth.1 METTL3, identified as an AML oncogene, is overexpressed in AML cells and drives leukemogenesis through methylation modifications.2 This makes METTL3 inhibition a promising strategy for overcoming chemotherapeutic challenges like resistance in AML. Thus, targeting METTL3 via inhibition or degradation may address AML drug – resistance and recurrence.3

Studies had shown that the cofactor substrate S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) in METTL3 was responsible for the transfer of methyl groups, making METTL3 an effective target for inhibitor design.4 The activity of methyltransferase could be reduced by the competitive binding of small molecule complexes. The first nucleoside – based METTL3 inhibitor discovered was adenosine, a component of SAM and a competitive inhibitor of METTL3 with an IC50 of 495 µM.4

In April 2021, the first non-nucleoside METTL3 inhibitor, STM2457, was disclosed as a clinical candidate for anti-leukemia therapy.5 STM2457 exhibited potent enzymatic suppression against METTL3 with an IC50 of 16.9 nM. Surface plasmon resonance further revealed a dissociation constant of 1.4 nM, underscoring its high binding affinity. Importantly, the compound displayed marked selectivity for METTL3 over other RNA methyltransferases. Functionally, STM2457 not only impeded the growth of solid tumors—including lung, liver, and medulloblastoma malignancies6–8—but also targeted leukemia stem-cell subsets in AML, thereby blocking proliferation and promoting differentiation and apoptosis.5 Another potent METTL3 inhibitor, UZH2, had an IC50 value of 5 nM.9 Recently, Zhang et al. reported a pyridin-2(1H)-one derivatives exhibiting potent inhibitory activity against METTL3 (IC50 = 50 nM).10 Miao et al. designed a series of 2, 6-di-substituted imidazo[1,2-a] pyridine derivatives, and compound 30t exhibited IC50 value of 45.31 nM against METTL3.11

However, the development of small – molecule METTL3 inhibitors is still at a very early stage.12 Till now, only STC-15 from Storm Therapeutics had entered Phase I clinical trial (NCT05584111) in 2022, mainly for advanced cancer patients, especially those who had failed in standard treatment.12 The results demonstrated in 2025 that it was well tolerated and achieved tumor regressions at all dose levels evaluated, with an overall response rate of 9% and disease control rate of 67%. Now, Storm is recruiting patients for an open-label, non-randomized, multi-cohort, Phase 1b/2 Trial Investigating the safety, tolerability, and antitumor activity of STC-15 combining with PD-1 inhibitor Toripalimab (NCT06975293).13 Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to discover novel METTL3 inhibitors14 (Fig. 1).

Structures of reported Mettl3 inhibitors.

Natural products are crucial sources of lead compounds in new drug development, with over 50% of clinical antitumor drugs derived from them.15 Many natural products, such as Quercetin and Genistein, showed broad biological activity and promise in reducing RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) levels in UUO – induced renal fibrosis.16 These findings provided new insights for the discovery of METTL3 inhibitors from natural product origins.

To discover novel METTL3 inhibitors with improved potency and druggability, in this study, we employed a molecular hybridization strategy—a powerful and widely applied approach in rational drug design that merges pharmacophore elements from two or more biologically active molecules to generate hybrid compounds with enhanced or novel properties.17–19 Genistein, a natural isoflavone with reported METTL3 inhibitory activity (IC50 = 47 µM),20 and STM2457, the first non-nucleoside METTL3 clinical candidate with high potency (IC50 = 16.9 nM) and selectivity,5 were selected as lead compounds based on their complementary pharmacophore features.21,22 The hybridization principle was applied to fuse the flavone core of Genistein—which provided favorable drug-like properties, natural product scaffold diversity, and potential multi-target biological activities—with the imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine-cyclohexylamine pharmacophore of STM2457—which confered high binding affinity, established METTL3 selectivity, and proven anti-leukemic efficacy. By varying the linker connecting these two pharmacophores, a series of 31 novel hybrid molecules were designed and virtually screened via molecular docking and druglikeness prediction. The top-scoring hit H1 was selected for chemical synthesis and its structure was confirmed by NMR and MS. Preliminary activity assays demonstrated that H1 exhibited potential inhibitory activity against METTL3.The design strategy was outlined in Fig. 2.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemical reagents and solvents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without further purification unless otherwise noted. 2,4-Dihydroxyacetophenone, diethyl oxalate, 2-amino-5-cyanopyridine, 1,3-dichloropropane, bis(tert-butoxycarbonyl)amine, sodium borohydride, and HATU were obtained from Hehui Chem Co. Ltd (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Raney nickel catalyst was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Anhydrous solvents were dried according to standard procedures. Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (200–300 mesh, Qingdao Haiyang Chemical Co., Ltd). Reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using silica gel 60 F254 aluminum plates (Merck, Burlington, CA, USA).

The MTase-Glo™ Methyltransferase Assay Kit (Promega, Catalog #V7601) was used for the in vitro METTL3 inhibition screening. The kit contains the MTase-Glo™ Detection Reagent, MTase-Glo™ Conversion Reagent, METTL3/14 enzyme complex, SAM substrate, and control compounds. All assay components were stored and handled according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The CCK-8 cell counting kit (APExBIO, Catalog #K1018) was used for cytotoxicity assays. MOLM-13 cells (human acute myeloid leukemia cell line) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 µg mL−1 streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

STM2457 (MedChemExpress, Catalog #HY-145030) was used as the reference METTL3 inhibitor in both enzymatic and cellular assays. Genistein (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #G6649) was used as the natural product lead compound for comparison. Both reference compounds were dissolved in DMSO to prepare 10 mM stock solutions and stored at −20 °C.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was carried out using CDocker protocol in Discovery Studio (DS, Version 4.0, BIOVIA®) to screen out the potential molecules, and to further understand the binding properties. Before the operation, both of the lead compounds Genistein and STM2457 were also performed.

The molecular docking results indicated that several designed molecules exhibited higher binding affinities for the METTL3 protein than the positive control molecules, Genistein and STM2457, achieving higher scoring outcomes.

Molecular dynamics and MM/GBSA calculation

The complex of METTL3 with the selected compound H1 was chosen to conduct molecular dynamics (MD) using Gromacs 2020.7, while the MM/GBSA calculation of the METTL3 protein complexes were underwent by fastDRH. All the operation details were described in the SI.

ADMET prediction

In drug research and development, most candidates failed primarily due to pharmacokinetic (PK) and metabolic complications.23 The druglikeness of screened compound H1 was carried out by SwissADME (https://www.swissadme.ch/index.php)24 and ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/server/screening).25

Chemistry

Retrosynthetic analysis of H1 led to a well – designed synthetic route, as was described below.

Starting from 2,4-dihydroxyacetophenone and 2-amino-5-cyano-pyridine, phenolic esterification and nucleophilic cyclization yielded intermediate 1a, followed by ester hydrolysis to obtain the flavone-based compound 1b. Then, 2-amino-5-cyano-pyridine underwent two nucleophilic substitutions to form intermediate 2b. Subsequent hydrogenation of the cyano group in 2b, catalyzed by Raney nickel, reduced it to an aldehyde group, producing intermediate 2c. Finally, condensing intermediates 1b and 2dvia acid – amine condensation gave intermediate 2e, which was reductively aminated to yield target compound H1. The structure was confirmed by 1H, 13C NMR, MS, or HRMS, and its purity was determined by HPLC. The operation details were described in the SI.

In vitro activity assay

MTase-Glo methyltransferase assay

The conventional MTase-Glo methyltransferase assay was employed to determine the in vitro inhibitory activity of compounds against METTL3, with specific procedures referring to the kit instruction manual.

First, the positive control drug was diluted according to the reference concentration specified in the manual. The diluted solution served as the first concentration point, followed by 10 consecutive serial dilution steps with a 3-fold dilution factor each time, resulting in a total of 10 concentration points plus a 0-concentration point (blank control). Subsequently, 40 nL of each compound was transferred into a 384-well plate, with 2 replicates set for each compound in each column.

The 384-well assay plate was centrifuged at 1000 rpm. Then, 2 µL of the METTL3/14 enzyme mixture was added to each assay well, followed by the addition of 2 µL of the METTL3-Biotin & SAM substrate mixture. The plate was incubated at 25 °C for 360 minutes.

After incubation, 1 µL of the reaction reagent was added to each well, and the plate was centrifuged again at 1000 rpm. It was then incubated at 25 °C for 45 minutes. Next, 5 µL of the detection reagent was added to each well, followed by centrifugation at 1000 rpm and a subsequent incubation at 25 °C for 45 minutes.

Finally, a microplate luminometer was used to measure the luminescence intensity. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of the compounds were calculated by fitting the data to a non-linear regression equation. The IC50 values were determined by fitting the dose–response data to a four-parameter logistic equation:Y = bottom + (top − bottom)/[1 + 10((log IC50 − X) × HillSlope)]where X is the logarithm of compound concentration, Y is the inhibition percentage, top and bottom are the plateaus of the sigmoid curve, log IC50 is the logarithm of the half-maximal inhibitory concentration, and HillSlope represents the slope of the curve. Quality control

Each assay plate included a positive control (STM2457 at 1 µM, expected inhibition >90%), a negative control (DMSO, expected inhibition ∼0%), and a no-enzyme blank for background subtraction. Assays were considered valid when the Z′-factor was ≥0.5 and the coefficient of variation (CV) between replicates was <15%.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8 assay)

Cell culture and seeding

MOLM-13 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes and resuspended in fresh complete RPMI-1640 medium. Cell density and viability were determined using a hemocytometer with trypan blue exclusion (viability >95%). Cells were seeded at a density of 8000–10,000 cells per well in 100 µL complete medium into 96-well flat-bottom plates (Corning, #3599) and allowed to adhere for 4 hours at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Compound treatment

After the pre-incubation period, 100 µL of compound-containing medium (or control solutions) was added to each well in triplicate. The final concentrations of test compounds covered the range from 0.1 µM to 100 µM (H1 and STM2457) or 1 µM to 200 µM (Genistein), prepared by serial 2-fold dilutions in complete medium. The plates were incubated for 48 hours at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

CCK-8 detection

After the 48 hour treatment period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO) was added to each well. The plates were gently shaken for 30 seconds to mix and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2–4 hours until the color development was sufficient for detection.

Absorbance measurement

The absorbance at 450 nm (reference wavelength 630 nm) was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. The blank control wells (medium without cells) were used to subtract background absorbance.

The reported IC50 values represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate.

Results and discussions

Molecular docking-based screening by CDocker

CDocker is a CHARMm-based high-resolution semi-flexible docking protocol in DS software. The small molecule is fully flexible, while the macromolecule remains rigid, and exhaustive conformational sampling yields high precision docking poses. CDocker energy calculation serves as the affinity metric—lower scores indicate stronger receptor – ligand binding.26

The binding modes between METTL3 and the de novo-generated 31 compounds were investigated. First, the complex of the METTL3 kinase domain binding with inhibitor STM2457 (PDB ID: 7O2I)5 was downloaded from the protein database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Then, the complex was prepared using the “Protein Preparation” module. The de novo-generated 31 compounds, with both STM2457 and Genistein, were treated with the “Prepare Ligand” module to generate totally 271 poses. The co-crystal ligand STM2457 was used to determine the binding site of CDocker protocol and was then removed prior to docking. The default parameter settings were used under high quality modes, but Top Hits was set to 1. The top 10 compounds based-on the CDocker Energy were exhibited in Table 1.

As shown in Table 1, Genistein exhibited the lowest binding energy, indicating the most stable protein-ligand interaction. This enhanced stability could be attributed to its compact molecular structure, which enabled deep insertion into the binding pocket. In contrast, STM2457 displayed a substantially higher binding energy, suggesting a less favorable binding conformation. This thermodynamic instability likely constituted the primary reason for its discontinuation from further developmental stages. Among the de novo generated molecules, the binding energies were relatively comparable, with H1 demonstrating the most favorable (lowest) binding energy and thus representing the most stable complex. The results indicated that the binding mode of H1 is essentially consistent with that of STM2457. The flavone core and its phenolic hydroxyl groups in the structure of H1 face the solvent region, located outside the binding pocket, while the imidazo[1,2-a] pyridine and cyclohexane regions of H1 extend into the interior of the binding pocket, forming a relatively enclosed hydrophobic space. H1 is capable of forming hydrogen bond interactions with the amino acid residues ILE378 and THR408. The NH group in the cyclohexylmethylamine structure forms a salt bridge with the ASP395 residue, which further modulates the three-dimensional conformation and spatial configuration of the protein. Meanwhile, the imidazo[1,2-a] pyridine moiety in the structure forms an electrostatic attraction with the positively charged amino acid residue ARG536, and the phenyl ring of the flavone core engages in π–π interactions with PHE534.

Structure–activity relationship analysis

To elucidate the key structural features influencing METTL3 inhibitory activity and guide future optimization, a comprehensive structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis was performed based on both computational docking studies and experimental biological evaluation. The SAR analysis focused on the 31 designed hybrid compounds, with particular emphasis on the top-performing compound H1 and its comparison with the lead compounds Genistein and STM2457.

The molecular hybridization strategy employed in this study fused two distinct pharmacophores: the flavone core derived from Genistein, and the imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine-cyclohexylamine motif derived from STM2457. Analysis of the docking scores and interaction patterns across the 31 designed compounds revealed several critical SAR trends:

(1) Flavone core contribution: the flavone scaffold serves as a foundational anchor that contributes to overall molecular stability and target engagement. Compounds retaining the complete flavone moiety (including the phenolic hydroxyl groups at positions 5 and 7) generally exhibited more favorable CDocker interaction energies compared to truncated analogs. The phenolic hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bond interactions with ILE378 and THR408, as observed in the H1-METTL3 complex (Fig. 3B). However, the flavone core predominantly faces the solvent-exposed region, suggesting that its primary role is structural anchoring rather than direct catalytic site inhibition.

(2) Imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine moiety contribution: the heterocyclic imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine ring system is critical for high-affinity binding and deep pocket penetration. In H1, this moiety extends into the interior hydrophobic cavity of the METTL3 active site and forms a strong electrostatic interaction with ARG536 (Fig. 3B). Comparison across the designed compound library revealed that analogs lacking this heterocyclic core (or with modified nitrogen positioning) showed significantly reduced CDocker interaction energies (ΔΔE > 15 kcal mol−1), underscoring the essential role of this pharmacophore for target engagement. This finding aligns with the high potency of STM2457 (IC50 = 16.9 nM), which contains a related heteroaromatic system.

(3) Cyclohexylmethylamine group contribution: the aliphatic cyclohexyl ring connected via a methylene-amine linker provides critical hydrophobic stabilization and electrostatic balance. The NH group forms a salt bridge with ASP395, which serves as a conformational lock that properly orients the imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine moiety for optimal ARG536 interaction. SAR analysis of linker variants (H6, H12, H14, H18–H30, Table 1) demonstrated that cyclohexyl substitution (as in H1) provided optimal hydrophobic complementarity with the pocket interior, while aromatic ring replacements (e.g., phenyl in H19) reduced binding affinity by approximately 2–3 kcal mol−1 in CDocker interaction energy, likely due to reduced conformational flexibility and suboptimal steric fit. Furthermore, linear alkyl linkers (e.g., H21) showed decreased interaction energies, suggesting that the rigid cyclohexyl ring contributes to pre-organization of the bioactive conformation.

(4) Linker optimization: the amide linker connecting the flavone core and the imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine-cyclohexylamine pharmacophore plays a crucial role in maintaining the appropriate spatial orientation and distance between the two binding motifs. Analysis of the 31 compounds revealed that linkers with 8–10 rotatable bonds (as in H1, nRot = 8) achieved the best balance between conformational flexibility (necessary for induced-fit binding) and entropic pre-organization (favorable for binding free energy). Compounds with overly flexible linkers (nRot > 10) showed reduced CDocker energies, likely due to increased entropic penalties upon binding.

Druglikeness and ADMET prediction

It is a common view that evaluating the ADME/T (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) profiles early on is crucial for any drug candidate.27 Here, SwissADME and ADMETlab 3.0 were used to predict these properties of H1.28

SwissADME is an open-access web platform that integrates a panel of in silico models to forecast the ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) profile of small molecules, with a reported accuracies of 72–94%.29 The prediction covers aqueous solubility, plasma-protein binding, hepatic biotransformation, renal clearance, and cytochrome P450 (CYP450) interaction spectra. Its flagship “bioavailability radar” compresses oral developability into six polar axes—lipophilicity, size, polarity, solubility, flexibility, and saturation—rendered as a hexagon. Molecules whose red, twisted hexagons fall entirely within the pink “drug-like” zone are predicted to be orally bioavailable.30

It could be seen from the radar map of Fig. 4 that H1 had a good hexagon druglikeness graph within the pink region, almost the same with STM2457. But Genistein had slightly overpass in INSATU, and with still some defects in the druglikeness of the molecule. The detail predicted properties by SwissADME were given in the SI (Table S1).

LIPO = lipophilicity (XLOGP3 between −0.7 and 5.0); SIZE (molecular weight between 150 and 500 g mol−1); POLAR = polarity (TPSA between 20 and 130 Å2); INSOLU = solubility (log S not higher than 6); INSATU = saturation (fraction of carbons in the sp3 hybridization not less than 0.25); FLEX = flexibility (no more than nine rotatable bonds).

ADMETlab 3.0 is an open-available platform, which delivers a 119-endpoint ADME/Tox panel—21 physicochemical, 20 chem-informatic, 9 absorption, 9 distribution, 14 metabolism, 2 excretion, 36 toxicity and 8 toxicophore readouts—through rigorously validated machine-learning models.31 The platform returns quantitative estimates of lipophilicity (log P/D), solubility (log S), P-gp recognition, plasma-protein binding, unbound fraction, Vd, BBB penetration, CYP substrate profile, total clearance and half-life, supplying designers with high-confidence guidance for lead optimization.

The pharmacokinetic profiling of H1 (Fig. 5) exhibited to be within an acceptable range, because it was aligned within the upper and lower limit drug-like parameters. In detail, the molecule satisfied Lipinski rules (HBD 4, HBA 8, TPSA 107 Å2), yet its lipophilicity was borderline (log P/log D ≈ 2.7) and Fsp3 was sub-optimal (0.39). Permeability assays predicted low-to-medium intestinal absorption (Caco-2–5.6, HIA < 30%) and oral bioavailability in the 20–30% range. Distribution was moderate (VDss 0.47 L kg−1, fu 45%). The principal liability was extensive CYP interaction: inhibition probabilities >0.97 for CYP3A4, 2D6, 2B6 and 2C8 classified the compound as a potent pan-CYP inhibitor, while it was also a likely CYP2C19 substrate. Toxicology alerts were concerning: hERG channel blockade probability 0.84, drug-induced liver injury 0.86, genotoxicity 1.0, respiratory toxicity 0.95, plus structural alerts for skin sensitization and colloidal aggregation. Overall, H1 was predicted a privileged yet problematic lead that demanded prompt in vitro DDI, hERG, Ames and DILI assays. The detail of predicted data was shown in the SI.

Notably, the ADMET predictions correlate with the SAR analysis (Section 3.2) in identifying the cyclohexylamine moiety and flavone core as structural determinants of both target engagement and metabolic liability. The high CYP inhibition probability is likely attributable to the electron-rich flavone aromatic system, which is prone to CYP-mediated oxidative metabolism. This observation supports the SAR-guided optimization strategy (Section 3.2.4) to introduce metabolically stable substituents (e.g., methoxy blocks) at the phenolic positions to mitigate CYP liability while preserving target binding.

Inhibition activity of target compound H1 against METTL3

To verify that the optimized small molecule H1 exhibits superior inhibitory activity against the METTL3 target compared to its lead compound Genistein, the MTase-Glo methyltransferase assay was conducted on H1, with the results presented in Fig. 6.

MTase-Glo methyltransferase assay IC50 results of H1 molecule.

The experimental results confirmed that the target product H1 possessed enzyme inhibitory activity against the METTL3/14 protein complex in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 value of 980.319 nM (Fig. 6). The dose–response curve exhibited a characteristic sigmoidal shape with a Hill slope of approximately 1.2, indicating single-site competitive or non-competitive inhibition kinetics. The inhibitory potency of H1 significantly surpassed that of its natural lead compound Genistein, which showed an inhibition rate of only 37.31% at a concentration of 40 µM (estimated IC50 > 100 µM), representing a >100-fold improvement in METTL3 inhibitory activity.

However, H1's potency (IC50 = 980.3 nM) remained approximately 57-fold weaker than the clinical candidate STM2457 (IC50 = 16.9 nM). This potency gap was consistent with the SAR analysis (Section 3.2), which identified several suboptimal features in H1's structure: (i) the flavone core contributes to molecular weight and CYP liability without providing direct catalytic site inhibition; (ii) the linker region lacks conformational rigidity, resulting in entropic binding penalties; and (iii) the cyclohexyl ring, while providing hydrophobic complementarity, is not optimally sized for the pocket volume. These insights from the integrated SAR and activity data provided clear direction for the next optimization cycle.

CCK-8 assay for cytotoxicity

To evaluate whether the compounds exert potent cytotoxicity against MOLM-13 cells, their influence on cell viability was quantified with the CCK-8 reagent.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, treatment with compound H1 reduced MOLM-13 viability in a dose-dependent manner, yielding an IC50 value of 35.1 ± 1.2 µM. The cytotoxicity curve for H1 showed a gradual decline in cell survival rate from 95% at 316 nM to 45% at 31.6 µM, with the steepest decline occurring between 3.16 µM and 31.6 µM. While this value indicated measurable cytotoxicity, it was approximately 2.2-fold weaker than that of the reference inhibitor STM2457 (IC50 = 15.8 ± 0.9 µM).

Effect of compound on MOLM-13 cell viability.

The cellular potency of H1 (IC50 = 35.1 µM) correlated reasonably with its enzymatic inhibition (IC50 = 980.3 nM), considering typical compound permeability limitations and protein binding effects in cellular environments. The approximately 36-fold shift between enzymatic and cellular IC50 values was within the expected range for compounds with moderate permeability (Caco-2 = −5.6, predicted HIA < 30% from ADMETlab 3.0). This correlation supported the hypothesis that the anti-proliferative effect of H1 in MOLM-13 cells was mediated, at least in part, through METTL3 inhibition, although off-target effects could not be completely excluded at this stage.

Consequently, H1 served as a promising starting point but still required further structural refinement to achieve nanomolar cellular potency. The SAR analysis identified specific modifications (imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine electron-withdrawing substitution, linker rigidity enhancement) that may simultaneously improve both enzymatic potency and cellular permeability, thereby reducing the enzymatic-cellular potency gap.

Conclusions

METTL3 is a promising therapeutic target for AML and other disease, such as cardiovascular and neurological diseases.32,33 However, current research of METTL3 inhibitors remained clearly deficient: most studies omitted druggability profiling, and screening outputs were rarely cross-checked against ADMET parameters. Consequently, many potent hits displayed poor oral bioavailability or unfavorable metabolic/toxic profiles. In this study, we established an “online design → in silico validation → experimental evaluation” workflow. A series of compounds were first designed by fusing STM2457 and Genistein based on the principle of molecular hybridization, then evaluated for binding stability via molecular docking and for drug-like properties with SwissADME and ADMETlab 3.0. Only candidates combining high predicted potency with favorable ADMET signatures were advanced to synthesis and evaluation, which led to the compound H1.

The molecular hybridization strategy employed in this study successfully merged the pharmacophore elements of the natural product Genistein and the clinical candidate STM2457, generating a novel chemotype with distinct structural features. The comprehensive SAR analysis revealed that the imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine-cyclohexylamine motif serves as the primary driver of METTL3 binding affinity through electrostatic interaction with ARG536 and salt bridge formation with ASP395, while the flavone core provides structural anchoring and scaffold diversity. The amide linker maintains optimal spatial orientation between the two pharmacophores, with rigidity and length critically influencing the entropic cost of binding. These SAR insights established preliminary optimization priorities for future medicinal chemistry efforts.

Compound H1 was synthesized, and its structure was confirmed by NMR and MS. Preliminary activity assays demonstrated that H1 exhibited potent inhibitory activity against METTL3 with an IC50 value of 980.3 nM, significantly better than Genistein. Molecular docking revealed that H1's binding mode was similar to STM2457, forming hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions within the METTL3 pocket. Druglikeness and ADME properties were evaluated to support H1's potential as a drug candidate.

Despite the promising enzymatic and cellular activity of H1, several mechanistic aspects required further investigation. The exact mode of inhibition (competitive, non-competitive, or uncompetitive with respect to SAM) remained to be determined. As this manuscript represented our preliminary research, these enzyme kinetic studies will be conducted in subsequent studies to definitively establish the inhibition mechanism. Furthermore, site-directed mutagenesis of key residues identified in the molecular docking studies (particularly ARG536 and ASP395) will be performed to validate the proposed binding interactions and quantify their individual contributions to ligand affinity. These mutagenesis experiments, which require construction of point mutants (e.g., ARG536 → Ala, ASP395 → Ala), recombinant protein expression, and comparative binding assays, will be pursued in our future work to provide definitive structural validation of the SAR model.

While H1's enzymatic potency (IC50 = 980.3 nM) and cellular activity (IC50 = 35.1 µM) were modest compared to advanced METTL3 inhibitors such as UZH2 (IC50 = 5 nM) and compound 30 t (IC50 = 45.31 nM), H1 offered distinct strategic advantages as a lead compound. First, H1 represented a structurally novel chemotype derived from natural product hybridization, occupying a different intellectual property space and potentially circumventing existing patent barriers. Second, the balanced drug-like properties of H1 by compliance with Lipinski's rules, acceptable oral bioavailability profile comparable to STM2457, and moderate lipophilicity, contrasted with many ultra-potent inhibitors that suffered from poor pharmacokinetic profiles or high lipophilicity-induced promiscuity. Third, the natural product-derived flavone core may confer favorable polypharmacology and reduce toxicity risk compared to fully synthetic heteroaromatic systems, a hypothesis that will be tested in comprehensive in vivo safety profiling in our subsequent studies.

These primary results confirmed our strategy could preserve the efficiency of virtual screening while remedy the technical shortcomings of existing METTL3 inhibitor discovery campaigns. The integrated workflow demonstrated that early ADMET profiling and SAR analysis could guide lead selection and optimization, ensuring that only candidates with both predicted potency and developability are advanced to resource-intensive synthesis and biological testing. This approach addresses a critical gap in current METTL3 inhibitor research and can guide future efforts in RNA methyltransferase inhibitor discovery.
