A synovial cyst–induced vertebral artery dissection in bow hunter’s stroke: illustrative case
Authors: Masahito Yamashita, Hideki Hayashi, Kazushi Kitamura, Ryota Ishibashi, Hiroki Toda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDBow hunter’s stroke (BHS) is a rare condition characterized by occlusion of the vertebral artery (VA) due to a head rotation. BHS typically results from neck hyperrotation, often due to anatomical factors. The authors present a case of BHS in a young male patient exacerbated by os odontoideum (OD), resulting in atlantoaxial dislocation, which ultimately led to VA dissection.OBSERVATIONSA man in his 20s presented with persistent dizziness and nausea and initially received a diagnosis of a brain infarction. However, imaging revealed VA dissection caused by an OD with an associated synovial cyst. Surgical intervention, specifically atlantoaxial posterior fixation, resolved the instability and allowed for natural regression of the synovial cysts, preventing further vascular events. In this case, the OD with atlantoaxial dislocation was identified as the causative factor for BHS. The coexistence of a synovial cyst was a unique finding, likely triggered by chronic irritation caused by the OD.LESSONSThis case emphasizes the importance of thorough cervical spinal evaluation in young patients presenting with posterior circulation stroke. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2487

Bow hunter’s stroke (BHS) is a rare occlusive vascular disorder caused by vertebral artery (VA) occlusion resulting from head rotation. We present a case of BHS in a young male patient that was exacerbated by an os odontoideum (OD), leading to atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD) and subsequent VA dissection. This case was further complicated by the presence of a synovial cyst. In this case, where the synovial cyst was implicated as a cause of BHS leading to a stroke, posterior atlantoaxial fixation was chosen.

Illustrative Case

A man in his 20s experienced intermittent dizziness and nausea for a week before presenting to our clinic. He had previously consulted another medical facility where magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a stroke. He presented to our clinic with clear consciousness and no neurological deficits. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) of the head revealed hyperintense areas in the right occipital lobe, right thalamus, and left pons (Fig. 1 A and B). Head computed tomography (CT) angiography showed stenosis in the left VA V3 segment (Fig. 1C and D). Blood and coagulation tests showed no abnormalities. Carotid ultrasonography and transthoracic echocardiography showed no evidence of stenosis or embolic sources. Cervical radiography revealed a 6.3-mm atlantoaxial interval, suggestive of atlantoaxial subluxation (Fig. 1F–H). Cervical CT demonstrated an OD (Fig. 1E). Cervical MRI showed no initial signs of compression of the cervical cord by pseudotumor (Fig. 2A). Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI revealed a synovial cyst at the stenotic site of the left VA V3 (Fig. 2C and D). The arterial wall at this location exhibited high signal intensity on MRI contrast-enhanced T1 volumetric isotropic turbo spin echo acquisition (VISTA), suggestive of arterial dissection (Fig. 2B). Cerebral angiography demonstrated dissection of the left VA in the neutral position, with interrupted blood flow upon right rotation and flexion of the head (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, we diagnosed BHS, attributing it to atlantoaxial subluxation caused by an OD, leading to synovial cyst formation. Chronic irritation from head rotation induced arterial dissection, resulting in an embolic stroke. The patient underwent cervical immobilization with a cervical collar and started oral intake of daily aspirin (100 mg). After 1 month, posterior atlantoaxial fixation was performed using the Tan1 method (Fig. 4A and B). Postoperative angiography on day 4 confirmed continuous blood flow during head rotation (Fig. 4C). A follow-up CT scan of the head at 3 months postoperatively revealed a cyst reduction and improved stenosis of the left VA (Fig. 4D). On coronal contrast-enhanced T1 VISTA MRI at 2 years postoperatively, the synovial cyst observed preoperatively had disappeared (Fig. 4E). The patient wore a cervical collar for 6 months without worsening neurological symptoms, and there was no recurrence of stroke for 2 years. Discussion

Observations

BHS is a rare occlusive vascular disorder caused by VA occlusion due to head rotation.2 It has been reported that BHS is often caused by AAD.3 In the present case, BHS was attributed to AAD with an OD, a condition in which the odontoid process is separated and exists as an independent bone. Theories about OD suggest congenital fusion failure of the odontoid process and axis vertebral body or trauma-induced development.4 Symptoms can include neck pain and spinal symptoms, although some cases can be asymptomatic and incidentally discovered. Ten cases,5–12 including the present one, have been reported in which an OD was identified as the cause of BHS (Table 1). The average age in these cases was 21.1 years (median 19.5, youngest 5, oldest 43 years), with 9 of 10 individuals being male. Two patients had a history of obvious trauma, and in most cases, posterior atlantoaxial fixation was performed. Literature review of cases in which the OD caused BHS

In the present case, a synovial cyst was observed at the axis vertebral level. Synovial cysts arise from the degeneration of the facet joint, causing herniation of the synovial lining through a disrupted part of the synovial capsule, which leads to cyst enlargement. They are often associated with vertebral instability.13 While synovial cysts in the spine generally occur in the lumbar region, they are observed in approximately 1%–4% of cases in the cervical spine, with 30.1% located in the atlantoaxial region.14, 15 Although most occur within the spinal canal, variations like extraspinal occurrences, as seen in this case, have been reported.16 Chronic stimulation from AAD with an OD likely contributed to the development of the synovial cyst in our case. The patient, who operated a takoyaki shop, frequently turned his head to the right due to the confined kitchen space, potentially leading to chronic head rotation and stimulation of the VA adjacent to the synovial cyst, resulting in arterial dissection.

While there is no established treatment for BHS, there have been reports of improvement with conservative therapy.7, 11 Conservative management can be considered, especially in cases in which there is improvement without recurrent strokes or a high risk of instability in the cervical spine. However, if conservative treatment fails or there is a high risk of recurrent strokes due to cervical instability, surgical intervention can be considered. Surgical options include decompression through the lateral mass opening of the atlas or axis,17 posterior atlantoaxial fixation to prevent traction-related changes in the VA,3 and endovascular treatments such as stent placement18 or parent vessel occlusion.19

Surgical removal of a synovial cyst can be considered when it is a causative factor for cervical myelopathy due to compression. However, there is a report of synovial cysts naturally resolving with immobilization.20 In this case, where the synovial cyst was implicated as a cause of BHS leading to a stroke, posterior atlantoaxial fixation was chosen. This surgical approach aimed to prevent traction-related changes in the VA and improve the instability of the atlantoaxial joint, with the expectation of a natural resolution of the synovial cyst. Postoperatively, atlantoaxial instability was resolved, mechanical stimulation to the vascular endothelium ceased, and improvements were observed in the resolution of the synovial cyst and the arterial dissection–related changes in the VA.

Lessons

We report the case of a patient with atlantoaxial subluxation due to an OD, leading to the formation of a synovial cyst and subsequent VA dissection, resulting in BHS. The patient underwent posterior atlantoaxial fixation, which not only improved instability but also facilitated the natural resolution of the synovial cyst, preventing the recurrence of strokes. In young patients presenting with posterior circulation strokes, comprehensive evaluation of the cervical spine, including radiographs and CT scans, is crucial.
