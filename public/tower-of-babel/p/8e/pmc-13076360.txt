Real-world patient characteristics, treatments, and outcomes during and after severe ulcerative colitis hospitalization
Authors: Tim Raine, James D Lewis, Gareth C Parkes, Millie D Long, Parambir S Dulai, Jeffrey A Berinstein, Adam S Cheifetz, Yiyu Chen, Carrie Huisingh, Jason Eccleston, Michelle Kujawski, Valencia P Remple, Lani Wegrzyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsReal-world data are needed to better understand the burden and outcomes of patients hospitalized with severe ulcerative colitis (UC).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed US electronic health record (EHR) data with linked insurance claims to identify adults hospitalized for UC who received intravenous corticosteroids during an inpatient admission (index hospitalization) between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2022, with ≥180 days of prior EHR activity. Results were analyzed for the overall cohort, in three subgroups: (1) no prior UC diagnosis in the EHR, (2) prior UC diagnosis without prior advanced therapy, and (3) prior UC with prior advanced therapy, and in a nested cohort of patients discharged without colectomy. Multivariable analyses assessed factors associated with colectomy before discharge.ResultsOverall, we identified 9716 patients (mean [SD] age, 46.3 [17.4] years); 83.3% had a previous diagnosis of UC and 23.8% had prior biologic use for UC. During hospitalization, 13.1% received advanced therapy; 12.2% underwent colectomy. The rate of colectomy was 12.6% in subgroup 1, 9.2% in subgroup 2, and 19.6% in subgroup 3 (P < .0001). Prior UC diagnosis with prior advanced therapy use and abnormal/missing albumin labs were associated with higher risk of colectomy. The cumulative risk of colectomy <1 year after index hospitalization was 20.4% overall and 18.5%, 16.1%, and 32.7% in subgroups 1, 2, and 3, respectively. In the nested cohort (n = 4383), one-third received advanced therapy within 90 days; 38.4% experienced a UC-related hospitalization <1 year after index hospitalization.ConclusionThese data from a large contemporary cohort elucidate the burden and outcomes for patients hospitalized with severe UC.Keywords: advanced therapy, biologics, colectomy

1. Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, systemic, immune-mediated disease marked by mucosal inflammation of the colon.1 The disease course for UC is variable and unpredictable, with periods of relapse and intervening symptom-free periods of remission.1 Acute severe UC (ASUC) is a serious and potentially life-threatening manifestation of UC that requires hospitalization and may necessitate colectomy.2 In ASUC, inflammation is sudden and intense, characterized by six or more bloody bowel movements per day with signs of systemic toxicity.2 Patients with ASUC typically present with frequent bloody bowel movements, abdominal pain, fever, anemia, and tenesmus.2,3

Guidelines for ASUC recommend immediate initiation of intravenous corticosteroids (IVCS), rescue treatment with infliximab or cyclosporine for patients who do not respond to IVCS, and colectomy for those who fail to respond or deteriorate despite medical therapy.4,5 Colectomy is a life-altering and resource-intensive procedure that can have short- and long-term implications, including surgical complications, decreased quality of life, and persistent changes in bowel function.6 Data from prior cohort studies show heterogeneous outcomes for patients who avoid colectomy during their index hospitalization, including readmission rates within 90 days of up to 40% and colectomy rates of up to 26% within the first year after discharge7–10; however, these studies are generally small and contain a limited number of patients exposed to advanced therapies before hospitalization. Management of patients with prior exposure to advanced therapies is an emerging challenge with limited data to guide treatment decisions and our understanding of treatment outcomes. Therefore, a greater understanding of patient characteristics, current treatment practices, and the factors that influence patient trajectory in clinical practice is needed to improve outcomes for patients hospitalized with severe UC.

Electronic health record (EHR) databases are increasingly being used for pharmacoepidemiology research because they cover diverse real-world patient populations and offer rich clinical and treatment information, reflecting routine clinical practice for medications, procedures, imaging, laboratory evaluations, and healthcare resource utilization. Herein, we report the baseline characteristics, treatment patterns, healthcare resource utilization, and outcomes of a large cohort of US-based patients hospitalized with severe UC using data from a large, representative, longitudinal US EHR database with linked claims data.

2. Materials and methods

Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary Materials. In brief, we used de-identified longitudinal data from Optum’s de-identified Market Clarity Data (Market Clarity), which deterministically links medical and pharmacy claims with EHR data. We identified adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with an inpatient admission between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2022, with a diagnosis code for UC in the primary position and receipt of IVCS. The definition of severe UC was based on the standard of care per guidelines1,4,11,12 and has been used in previous retrospective analyses of severe UC hospitalizations7,13,14; it may capture severe UC cases that do not meet all criteria for ASUC. A nested cohort of patients who were discharged without undergoing colectomy during the index hospitalization was assessed separately (Figure S1). The primary outcome was colectomy; other outcomes included 30-day readmissions for UC, rates of UC-related hospitalization, and use of steroids and advanced therapies. We also assessed three subgroups of patients: those with no prior UC diagnosis (subgroup 1), those with prior UC diagnosis and no prior advanced therapy use (subgroup 2), and those with prior UC diagnosis and prior advanced therapy use (subgroup 3). The full list of codes for diagnoses, procedures, and medications can be found in Table S1. Patient consent and institutional review board approval were not applicable to this research because the database only includes secondary data without patient identifiers.

Descriptive statistics were reported for study measures in the overall study population and in the nested cohort. A chi-square test for categorical measures and Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous measures were used to compare subgroups. The Cochran–Armitage test was used to analyze linear trends in colectomy rates and use of advanced therapies. Associations between baseline characteristics and the risk of colectomy before discharge were assessed using logistic regression. Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ .05.

Six separate sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the overall results. To ensure that all treatments patients received were captured in the EHR and linked data, sensitivity analysis 1 restricted the sample to patients with EHR and linked medical and pharmacy claims (approximately 20% of the sample) and removed the requirement for a healthcare encounter within 180 days before index and >365 days after index for the nested cohort. Sensitivity analysis 2 restricted the study period start to January 1, 2018, to reflect more recent clinical practice. To increase the likelihood that prior UC diagnoses were captured, sensitivity analysis 3 required a longer pre-index window of activity (≥365 days) in the EHR. Sensitivity analyses 4 and 5 evaluated patients with laboratory results of prognostic significance for treatment failure or colectomy within 24 h of index (high C-reactive protein [>30 mg/L; sensitivity analysis 4] and low albumin [<3.4 g/dL; sensitivity analysis 5]).1,10,11,15,16 Laboratory results were used only for sensitivity analyses 4 and 5. The proportion of patients with abnormal values was biased by systematically missing data and are not reported. As treatment choices may differ if a Clostridium difficile infection is suspected,16 sensitivity analysis 6 restricted the sample to patients without C. difficile infection during the index hospitalization.

3. Results

Among patients diagnosed with UC, the incidence rate of severe hospitalization for UC was 1.4 per 100 person-years during the study period. Overall, 14 751 patients with a qualifying index hospitalization during the study period were identified (Figure S2). After exclusions for insufficient activity in the EHR (n/N [%], 1968/14 751 [13.3%]), age <18 years (1137/14 751 [7.7%]), no recent encounter in the EHR (1210/14 751 [8.2%]), and planned or elective colectomy procedure within the first 2 days of the index hospitalization (720/14 751 [4.9%]), 9716 of 14 751 (65.9%) patients remained in the study population. Of these 9716 patients admitted for severe UC, 1573 (16.2%) had no prior diagnosis of UC and no prior UC-specific medication (subgroup 1), 5840 (60.1%) had prior diagnosis of UC and no prior advanced therapy use (subgroup 2), and 2303 (23.7%) had prior diagnosis of UC and prior advanced therapy use (subgroup 3). These exclusions and subgroup definitions were applied to ensure elective or semi-urgent planned surgeries were not included in the analysis as well as correct classification of newly diagnosed UC with and without advanced therapy use.

3.1. Baseline characteristics

In the overall study population, the mean (SD) age was 46.3 (17.4) years, and 5119 of 9716 (52.7%) patients were female (Table 1). Among patients with a prior UC diagnosis, more than half (4047/7384 [54.8%]) had pancolitis. Less than one-quarter of patients (2310/9716 [23.8%]) had been prescribed an advanced therapy before admission, including a small number (7/9716 [<0.1%]) for indications other than UC (eg, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis). The primary biologic therapies prescribed before admission were infliximab (1367/9716 [14.1%]) and adalimumab (899/9716 [9.3%]). Prior use of 5-aminosalicylic acid medications was evident for less than half of patients (4207/9716 [43.3%]). Within the 6 months before index admission, 635 of 9716 (6.5%) patients had a hospitalization with IVCS for UC that did not meet the study inclusion criteria. In patients who were excluded due to colectomy on day 0 or day 1, baseline characteristics were generally comparable to those of the overall study population (Table S2). We observed differences in healthcare activity between patients who had and did not have C-reactive protein/albumin laboratory results (Table S3). Therefore, to avoid misinterpretation of data, abnormal lab values at baseline are not presented in Table 1.

3.2. Medication use and outcomes during the index hospitalization

During the index hospitalization, 1271 of 9716 (13.1%) patients received advanced therapy, mostly biologics (1233/1271 [97.0%]; Table 2). Advanced therapy use during the index hospitalization was significantly lower in subgroup 1 (121/1573 [7.7%]) and subgroup 2 (754/5840 [12.9%]) vs subgroup 3 (396/2303 [17.2%]; P < .0001). Use of advanced therapies during hospitalization increased in all patients from 10.7% in 2014 to 17.5% in 2022; subgroup 3 showed a significant increase from 12.1% to 25.3% (P < .0001 for trend; Figure 1A).

3.3. Rates of readmission related to UC

Overall, 1334 of 9716 (13.7%) patients experienced a UC-related readmission within 30 days post-discharge (Table 2). A significant difference in the proportion of patients with a UC-related admission within 30 days post-discharge was observed across subgroups, with the lowest proportion in subgroup 1 (167/1573 [10.6%]) and the highest in subgroup 2 (871/5840 [14.9%]; P < .0001). The median (IQR) length of stay including readmissions within 30 days was 6 (3–11) days in the overall population and was significantly longer in subgroup 1 (7 [4–16] days) vs subgroup 2 (5 [3–10] days; P < .0001) and subgroup 3 (5 [3–9] days; P < .0001).

3.4. Post-discharge outcomes among patients discharged without colectomy

Overall, 4383 of 9716 (45.1%) patients were discharged from the hospital without undergoing colectomy and remained under sufficient follow-up to contribute data to an analysis of outcomes in the nested cohort of patients with an intact colon at the time of initial discharge (Figure S2). Clinical and ­demographic characteristics were comparable with the overall population (Table S4).

Within the nested cohort of patients discharged without colectomy, in the 365 days post-index hospitalization, 2240 of 4383 (51.1%) patients had claims for oral corticosteroids and 1908 of 4383 (43.5%) patients had claims for any biologic. 5-Aminosalicylic acid medications were used by 1534 of 4383 (35.0%) patients. Subgroup 1 had significantly lower advanced therapy use (175/544 [32.2%]) vs subgroup 2 (1101/2847 [38.7%]; P < .0001) and subgroup 3 (650/992 [65.5%]; P < .0001; Table 4). In subgroup 3, 637 of 992 (64.2%) patients used any biologic, and 535 of 992 (53.9%) patients used oral corticosteroids. Advanced therapy use increased over time in all subgroups (Figure 1C).

In patients discharged without colectomy, 358 of 4383 (8.2%) patients underwent colectomy within 365 days post-index hospitalization, with a median (IQR) time to colectomy of 98 (33–194) days (Figure 2). Subgroup 3 exhibited the highest rate of colectomy post-discharge (130/992 [13.1%]) of any subgroup (P < .0001 vs other subgroups), with a median (IQR) time of 92 (32–187) days to post-discharge colectomy. The median (IQR) time to colectomy was longer in subgroup 1 vs subgroup 2 (148 [74–210] vs 91 [33–195] days). Rates of colectomy remained stable over time (Figure 1D). UC-related hospitalization within 365 days post-index hospitalization was observed in 1684 of 4383 (38.4%) patients, with the highest rate in subgroup 3 (424/992 [42.7%]; P < .0001 vs other subgroups; Table 4).

Overall, the robustness of the initial findings was affirmed by results from the sensitivity analyses (Figures S4–S7 and Tables S5–S22).

4. Discussion

In this large retrospective real-world study of a contemporary cohort of patients hospitalized with severe UC, a quarter of whom had prior exposure to biologics, approximately 12% underwent colectomy during their index hospitalization, 14% experienced a UC-related readmission within 30 days of index hospitalization, 38% had a UC-related hospitalization within 1 year of index hospitalization, and 20% underwent colectomy within 1 year of index hospitalization. Despite the substantial morbidity associated with colectomy, advanced therapy use was only observed in 13% of patients during index hospitalization and in 33% of patients within 90 days following index hospitalization. The proportion of patients with antibiotic use observed in this study aligns with expectations, as some physicians may initiate antibiotics upon admission and discontinue them after ruling out infections. Anticoagulant use was low and varied between subgroups. Consistent with prior studies, abnormal albumin was associated with a greater risk of colectomy before discharge.17,18 These data highlight the burden and outcomes of patients with severe UC hospitalizations in current clinical practice and provide insight into real-world management of patients with varying levels of prior biologic exposure.

As biologics and targeted synthetic small molecule drugs become more available worldwide, greater information on patient characteristics, treatment use, and disease outcomes in contemporary settings is needed. Previous studies have not captured information on patients’ history of advanced therapy use,8–10 or they have included only small subpopulations of patients with prior use of biologics.3,7 This study used a real-world population, with integrated EHR and claims data, to study more than 5000 patients with a prior UC diagnosis but no documented prior advanced therapy and more than 2000 patients with a history of existing UC and prior use of advanced therapy.

Exploratory analysis indicated that patients with existing UC and prior use of advanced therapy were at increased risk of colectomy. This population was distinct in terms of age, prior medication use, and treatment during the hospitalization. The decision to proceed to colectomy is informed by clinician and patient assessment of multiple factors, including disease severity, availability of further treatment options, perceived likelihood of response treatment, and patient preferences (ie, quality of life with a stoma vs with active disease).19,20 During a patient’s disease journey, these variables will change, which probably explains the increased risk of colectomy associated with a history of recent prior hospitalization observed for ­subgroup 3. These findings reinforce the need for additional therapeutic options for patients admitted with severe UC.

The finding that fewer than one-third of patients admitted with severe UC were treated with advanced therapies as an outpatient soon after discharge was surprising, since these therapies have been shown to reduce hospitalization rates.21 Across all subgroups, rates of advanced therapy use during and after hospitalization were relatively low, and a relatively small proportion of patients received an advanced therapy other than infliximab, which could be explained by barriers that impair patient access to the full range of approved treatments.22 In comparison, in a recent real-world study of treatment patterns in the European Union, 25.6% of patients with moderate-to-severe UC used an immunosuppressant with a biologic in the first-line, increasing to 35.7% in the second-line and to 45.5% in the third-line.23

Our longitudinal analysis showed a trend towards increasing use of advanced therapies after discharge, although absolute rates of usage remained below 60% overall. Factors contributing to these observations could include incompleteness of the database and limitations inherent to observational studies, but there was consistently low use of advanced therapies in patients with EHR and linked claims (Tables S5–S7). Interestingly, in subgroup 3, the rate of colectomy within 1 year peaked in 2018 and then declined, coinciding with a notable increase in the proportion of patients treated with advanced therapy after discharge. These data highlight a potential opportunity to improve patient outcomes through greater access to advanced therapies during and after hospitalization. A robust, comparative analysis of treatment subgroups would require a targeted study design with additional data considerations beyond the scope of the current investigation. Future studies are needed to compare outcomes across treatment subgroups in pre-hospitalization, inpatient, and post-hospitalization settings.

Patients with UC have increased VTE risk.24 Here, the rates of VTE during the hospitalization were high across all subgroups. Notably, the rate of VTE was 2-fold higher in subgroup 1 vs subgroups 2 and 3. This may be related to delays in confirming UC diagnosis and initiation of therapy to lower the inflammatory burden.25 Although this analysis did not distinguish whether anticoagulants were used prophylactically, any in-hospital anticoagulant use was observed in only 55% of patients hospitalized with severe UC in this study. This rate is consistent with the rate in another large multi-institutional study of patients hospitalized for IBD (including Crohn’s disease and UC).26 Individualized clinical decision-making—which can depend on factors such as patients’ prior IBD history or other coexisting conditions—may have led to the observed ­differences in anticoagulant use between subgroups.

A key strength of this study is the geographically diverse EHR data source, which includes many healthcare networks and allows for the examination of a large cohort of patients with severe UC, as well as the analysis of a wide array of patient characteristics and in-hospital treatments that are typically not available in administrative claims data. The EHR population includes individuals with various insurance statuses, enhancing the representativeness of the study cohort.

As with all retrospective studies, these findings must be interpreted considering limitations inherent to EHR and claims data, including potential coding errors, incomplete information, and incomplete capture of all care received during and after hospitalization. Laboratory data in this real-world data source were incomplete; the observed rates of missingness probably reflect variability in clinical practice and limitations in the capture of laboratory results across different EHR systems and provider notes; however, lab values had informative missingness, as missing laboratory data were associated with increased risk of colectomy before discharge. Future studies should account for this when reporting the proportion of patients with abnormal lab values to avoid biased results. These results are based on descriptive analyses and are not able or intended to detect causality. In observational research, it is necessary to make decisions that could unintentionally bias the results. The sensitivity analyses helped allay this concern, as the results in patients with EHR data and linked medical and pharmacy claims, or those with a longer baseline activity period in the database, were largely consistent with the overall study results. Advanced therapy use may be underrepresented, as patients may have initiated treatment outside of the hospital or have been transferred to a tertiary care center not included in our dataset. However, we conducted a claims overlap sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of therapy utilization across settings and found results similar to the main analysis. We identified patients with no prior UC based on the detection of the hospital diagnosis code for UC in the primary position, with no prior inpatient or outpatient codes for Crohn’s disease, UC, or indeterminant IBD. Some of these patients may have had a prior diagnosis code from a different healthcare system not included in the database. Despite this, the cohort entry criteria required active EHR participation within integrated delivery networks, reducing the likelihood that patients sought care outside the Optum EHR system during the baseline period and minimizing the risk of missing prevalent conditions and treatments.

Post-hospitalization data were only reported for the nested cohort of patients discharged without colectomy, but patient characteristics were comparable with those in the overall study population. There may be incomplete capture of prescriptions during follow-up, particularly those given before hospital discharge. Post-discharge steroid use may be a combination of post-hospitalization taper and use for new flares. Colectomy rate before discharge may be underestimated, as 720 patients were excluded to avoid counting elective colectomy. As a result, a small number of patients who had colectomy due to impending perforation or toxic megacolon may be missing from the dataset. Finally, the dataset captures treatment practices only during the study period, which partially overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic and ended in 2022, and does not account for recent changes including increased use of Janus kinase inhibitors.

In this retrospective observational analysis of patients with hospitalizations for severe UC, we observed high colectomy rates and relatively low advanced therapy use during and after inpatient admission. We identified a subgroup of over 2000 patients with prior use of advanced therapies, the largest contemporary cohort to date. These patients were young and more likely than other subgroups to undergo colectomy during the severe UC hospitalization, even after correction for markers of disease severity. Despite having the highest rates of UC-related hospitalization and colectomy in our study, more than a third of these patients did not have advanced therapy use within the first year after discharge. These data provide insight into a cohort with prior biologic exposure and highlight the need for effective therapies and strategies during and after severe UC hospitalization.
