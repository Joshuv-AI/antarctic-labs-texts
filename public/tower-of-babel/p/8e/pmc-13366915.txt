White adipose tissue expansion as the driver of insulin resistance and secondary endocrine disturbances in obesity– a narrative review
Authors: Marta Dobrzycka, Monika Karczewska-Kupczewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity represents a rapidly growing and alarming global health issue, with its prevalence nearly tripling over the past four decades. White adipose tissue enhances fat storage by increasing adipocyte size (hypertrophy) or recruiting new adipocytes (hyperplasia). The imbalance between adipocyte hyperplasia and hypertrophy, visceral adiposity, and dysfunction of white adipose tissue, rather than total fat mass, better predicts insulin resistance (the most common hormonal alteration in obesity) and related complications. Moreover, abdominal adiposity is a significant determinant of hormonal disturbances that link obesity to thyroid, adrenal, and gonadal dysfunctions. In addition, alterations in the physiology of hormones regulating food intake (leptin, ghrelin, incretins) are incontestably present in obesity. Importantly, weight loss can help restore metabolic and hormonal imbalances. This review summarizes the selected hormonal disturbances associated with white adipose tissue expansion.Keywords: White adipose tissue, Obesity, Insulin resistance, GLP-1, Leptin, Ghrelin, Hormonal alterations

Background

Obesity represents a rapidly growing and alarming global health issue. According to the World Health Organization projections for 2035 indicate that over 1.77 billion people will be overweight and 1.53 billion will be living with obesity, representing over half of the global adult population [1]. The most severe health consequences of obesity stem from metabolic and endocrine disturbances, particularly type 2 diabetes (T2D), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and an increased risk of certain cancers. These conditions rank among the leading causes of preventable and premature death worldwide [2]. Notably, it is projected that by 2025, more than 300 million adults globally will develop T2D as a result of obesity [3].

White adipose tissue (WAT) is specialized in storing and releasing lipids and plays a critical role in maintaining energy homeostasis. In obesity, excessive fat accumulation leads to WAT dysfunction, characterized by altered adipokine secretion, impaired WAT expandability, adipocyte hypertrophy, disrupted lipid metabolism, and increased local and systemic inflammation [4–6]. These disturbances significantly contribute to the development of metabolic syndrome. A key pathological mechanism linking obesity to metabolic complications is insulin resistance (IR), a reduced biological response to insulin in target tissues—primarily skeletal muscle, liver, and WAT [7]. Additionally, the distribution of WAT and the size of adipocytes are critical factors in the progression of obesity-related complications [6].

Through altered adipokine secretion and IR, dysfunctional WAT interferes with neuroendocrine pathways controlling hunger and satiety, including leptin- and gastrointestinal hormone–mediated signaling [8]. Secondly, WAT expansion affects multiple endocrine axes through its role in steroid hormone metabolism and hypothalamic–pituitary regulation. Consequently, hormonal alterations involving thyroid, adrenal, and gonadal systems are commonly observed in obesity and largely represent secondary adaptations to WAT dysfunction [9–11].

This review focuses on WAT dysfunction as the core axis underlying obesity-related metabolic and hormonal disturbances. We emphasize the role of IR in relation to body fat distribution and adipocyte morphology, and discuss selected secondary endocrine adaptations associated with WAT expansion, including alterations in appetite-regulating hormones, thyroid function, glucocorticoid metabolism, and sex steroid balance (Fig. 1).

White adipose tissue and insulin resistance

Insulin in action

Insulin is a key anabolic hormone that regulates systemic energy homeostasis by coordinating the uptake, utilization, and storage of nutrients. It is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to elevated blood glucose levels and acts primarily through coordinated actions on insulin-sensitive primary target tissues such as skeletal muscle, liver, and WAT. It promotes glucose utilization and storage in skeletal muscle by enhancing glucose transport and stimulating glycogen synthesis. In the liver, insulin activates glycogen synthesis, increases lipogenesis (synthesis of fatty acids from non-lipid precursors), and inhibits gluconeogenesis (synthesis of glucose using non-carbohydrate precursors) [12]. In WAT, insulin plays a central role in the formation of lipid-storing adipocytes. Upon binding to the insulin receptor (IR), insulin triggers tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrates (IRS-1/2), leading to activation of downstream signalling pathways, particularly the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt axis. Akt activation promotes the expression of key adipogenic transcription factors, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and members of the CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) family, which together drive the adipogenic program. As differentiation progresses, insulin further facilitates the acquisition of the mature adipocyte phenotype by promoting lipid accumulation and the expression of genes involved in fatty acid uptake, triglyceride synthesis, and storage. Additionally, insulin increases glucose uptake via translocation of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane, thereby providing essential substrates for de novo lipogenesis, as well as supresses lipolysis (the release of free fatty acids (FFAs) and glycerol) [13]. In addition, insulin activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which is primarily involved in the regulation of gene expression, cell growth, and differentiation, complementing the metabolic actions mediated by the PI3K/Akt pathway [14].

White adipose tissue expansion and insulin resistance

Obesity is defined as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation resulting from an imbalance between energy intake and energy expenditure, which may negatively affect health. Importantly, the regional distribution and dysfunction of WAT, rather than total fat mass, are stronger predictors of IR and related complications [15]. WAT comprises tightly packed, spherical adipocytes embedded within a richly vascularized loose connective tissue matrix. In addition to adipocytes, WAT contains a stromal vascular fraction (SVF) that includes adipose stem cells (ASCs), preadipocytes, and various immune cells, such as WAT-resident macrophages [16]. Notably, the WAT is a highly plastic organ that can rapidly respond to changes in energy status. This adaptability is mediated through extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, which enables adipocytes to expand within the tissue during weight-gain–induced WAT expansion [17].

Excessive expansion of WAT in obesity is accompanied by inflammatory dysfunction within the tissue, contributing to chronic low-grade systemic inflammation. This is characterized by increased recruitment and activation of immune cells, particularly proinflammatory macrophages, leading to elevated secretion of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1. This inflammatory environment plays a key role in the development of insulin resistance (IR) [18]. Once initiated, inflammation and IR reinforce each other through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Proinflammatory cytokines impair insulin signalling by inducing inhibitory serine phosphorylation of IRS-1/2, thereby attenuating PI3K/Akt pathway activity and reducing glucose uptake. In parallel, IR in adipocytes leads to enhanced lipolysis and increased release of FFAs, which impair insulin sensitivity (IS) in skeletal muscle and liver, further exacerbating systemic IR. These FFAs further activate inflammatory pathways, including nuclear factor-κB and c-Jun N-terminal kinase, amplifying cytokine production and sustaining a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [19]. Consistent with these mechanisms, adipocytes from individuals with IR and/or obesity exhibit reduced expression of key insulin signalling components, GLUT4 and IRS1, as well as impaired insulin-stimulated PI3K/Akt signalling [20] and decreased expression of key adipogenic transcription factors [21].

Systemically, IR leads to reduced glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and increased hepatic glucose production, resulting in elevated fasting and postprandial glucose. The compensatory mechanism of the pancreas is beta-cell hyperplasia and hypersecretion of insulin to maintain normoglycemia. In this vicious circle, the constant overexpression of insulin leads to dysfunction of pancreatic beta cells, resulting in the subsequent development of T2D [22]. Moreover, both obesity and IR predispose to the development of numerous disturbances that may also occur in people without impaired glucose tolerance (e.g. hypertension, dyslipidemia, MASLD) [23].

Distribution of white adipose tissue and insulin resistance

The two main types of WAT are visceral adipose tissue (VAT), located within the abdominal cavity and mediastinum, and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), found in the hypodermis. SAT accounts for approximately 85% of total adipose tissue mass, whereas VAT represents around 10%. SAT serves as the primary site for lipid storage in the form of triglycerides and is characterized by a relatively lower rate of lipolysis [24], whereas VAT exhibits higher lipolytic and proinflammatory activity [25].

The relationship between adipose tissue distribution and metabolic dysfunction is closely linked to the storage capacity of SAT. During chronic energy excess, reduced expandability or dysfunction of SAT limits its ability to effectively store excess lipids. As a result, lipids are redistributed to ectopic depots, including VAT and non-adipose tissues such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and heart. In humans, increased VAT accumulation is commonly used as a surrogate marker of impaired SAT function and ectopic fat deposition, particularly in the context of abdominal obesity [26, 27].

Clinical studies support the differential metabolic impact of WAT depots. A recent meta-analysis by Zhang et al. found that VAT accumulation is the strongest predictor of IR, as assessed by the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR). Notably, in their work, the gluteofemoral SAT had no significant correlation with IS [29]. However, other studies have demonstrated positive associations between gluteofemoral SAT and IS in humans, including a slower rate of lipolysis, a lower proinflammatory response, and increased secretion of insulin-sensitizing adipokines [30]. Notably, in a large population-based study (the Framingham Heart Study) with 3,093 nondiabetic participants who underwent multidetector computed tomography, both abdominal SAT and VAT were significantly correlated with IR. Still, VAT remained more strongly associated with IR [31]. Moreover, in another study, only VAT has been associated with incident hypertension in nonobese individuals [32] and with subclinical atherosclerosis, hepatic steatosis, and metabolic syndrome in participants with obesity [33]. Importantly, people with obesity and a normal metabolic profile also are at higher risk of T2D and CVD compared with healthy nonobese people [38].

Taken together, the accumulation of SAT and VAT in the abdominal region is associated with the development of obesity-related comorbidities and even all-cause mortality [34, 35]. Conversely, the accumulation of gluteofemoral SAT is associated with a favourable lipid and glucose profile and a lower prevalence of cardiovascular and metabolic disturbances [36, 37].

When size matters - hyperplastic and hypertrophic obesity and insulin resistance

WAT expands through adipocyte hypertrophy (an increase in cell size) and/or the formation of new adipocytes via adipogenesis (hyperplasia), resulting in enhanced lipid storage. Adipogenesis is a multi-step process involving two main phases: the commitment of ASCs to the preadipocyte lineage and the terminal differentiation of preadipocytes into mature adipocytes. This process is primarily regulated by mentioned specific transcription factors [39].

A recent study suggests that mature adipocytes constitute a terminally differentiated cell population in adults, although approximately 10% of total adipocytes are renewed annually. This implies that WAT is a dynamic organ, with continuous adipocyte turnover resulting from balanced processes of adipogenesis and adipocyte death. Moreover, individuals with obesity produce more adipocytes per year than lean individuals [40]. However, the generation rate of new adipocytes was also significantly lower in subjects with adipocyte hypertrophy than in those with hyperplasia. They produced 70% fewer adipocytes yearly than those with hyperplasia [41]. It is well-established that adipocyte hypertrophy is associated with IR, T2D, and dyslipidemia. Conversely, hyperplastic obesity may protect against obesity-associated metabolic comorbidities [42–44]. In detail, excessive adipocyte growth triggers insufficient angiogenesis, excessive ECM deposition, chronic inflammation, and altered adipokine secretion, which in turn impair glucose metabolism. By contrast, hyperplastic WAT expansion distributes excess fat among newly formed small adipocytes, reducing hypoxic stress on the fat depot and the subsequent inflammation by decreasing the number of hypertrophic adipocytes [45, 46]. Some studies have shown that adipocyte hypertrophy is the primary abdominal SAT phenotype in non-obese men with T2D [47]. In addition, in a group of nonobese first-degree relatives of patients with T2D, abdominal SAT cell size was associated with decreased IS and markers of WAT dysfunction [48]. However, many studies have revealed relationships between increased IR or the presence of T2D and hypertrophy from both fat depots [49–51], while others have demonstrated an association only with VAT cell size [52–54] or a stronger correlation with VAT than with SAT cell size [50, 51].

In both women and men with obesity, dietary weight loss reduced SAT cell size [55]. McLaughlin et al. demonstrated in insulin-resistant, overweight adults that improvements in IS were more strongly associated with reductions in adipocyte size and waist circumference than with body weight [56]. In patients after bariatric surgery, the improvement in IS correlated with reduced SAT cell volume, but not with weight loss per se [57]. In addition, another study showed that in normal-weight subjects, overfeeding led to an increase in upper-body SAT mass associated with adipocyte hypertrophy, whereas the increase in lower-body SAT mass was linked with adipocyte hyperplasia [58]. Moreover, subsequent diet weight loss in the same group indicated a decrease in adipocyte size in both regions, but adipocyte numbers were similar [59]. Other investigators found that abdominal SAT cell numbers were unaffected by weight loss, even when participants lost 25 kg [60] or 33% of their initial body mass [57]. This apparent stability may reflect a balance between adipogenesis and adipocyte loss, maintaining a relatively constant cell number and indicating that weight reduction primarily affects adipocyte size rather than number. These results are partially relevant from a clinical perspective, as diet remains a key component of obesity treatment.

Several authors have demonstrated that ASCs from individuals with obesity have a reduced ability to differentiate into adipocytes in vitro, as assessed by intracellular lipid accumulation and/or adipogenic gene expression [61–63]. Many studies have demonstrated also that VAT ASCs secrete more proinflammatory, pro-migratory, and pro-angiogenic molecules than SAT ASCs, in both obese and nonobese subjects. Moreover, this may subsequently lead to the spread of inflammation to ASCs and other environmental cells, resulting in low-grade inflammation within WAT and IR [64–66]. Interestingly, Silva et al. demonstrated that massive weight loss induced by bariatric surgery does not restore normal ASC cell physiology, including lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses [67]. Taken together, strategies to increase the recruitment of adipocyte progenitor cells to expand WAT by adipogenesis could be protective against the metabolic consequences of obesity. Moreover, a better understanding of the morphological changes associated with metabolic disturbances within each WAT depot would enable better patient categorisation and more targeted development of future therapeutic options.

Secondary endocrine adaptations to white adipose tissue expansion

Leptin resistance

Leptin, a product of the obese gene, is synthesized and secreted by WAT, after which it binds to and activates the leptin receptor (LEP-R). Its secretion follows a circadian rhythm, with circulating levels peaking at night in humans [68]. LEP-R plays a crucial role in body weight regulation through a negative feedback loop between WAT and the central nervous system (CNS). Specifically, leptin acts on hypothalamic receptors to mediate its homeostatic, appetite-suppressing effects [69], and it also modulates neural circuits involved in the reward system, thereby reducing the motivational drive to seek and consume food [70, 71]. In addition, numerous studies support leptin’s beneficial effects on glucose and insulin metabolism under physiological conditions, both centrally and peripherally [72].

Leptin is overexpressed at the gene level in the WAT of individuals with obesity [73]. As a result, serum leptin levels are elevated in individuals with obesity, accompanied by disrupted circadian rhythm oscillations [74]. Moreover, a strong positive correlation exists between plasma leptin concentrations and body fat percentage [75]. However, clinical studies have shown that treatment with leptin or its analogues does not significantly affect body weight, fat mass, glucose and insulin homeostasis, lipid profile, or food intake in individuals with obesity compared to placebo [76–79]. In obesity, excessive fat accumulation leads to chronically elevated leptin levels without adequate regulatory feedback, resulting in leptin resistance. On the other hand, a recent elegant study showed that individuals with relatively elevated leptin concentrations for their fat mass exhibit whole-body and WAT insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, even when BMI and adiposity were matched in groups, indicating that leptin itself can drive metabolic dysfunction [80]. Studies have demonstrated that high leptin levels predict incident T2D in both obese and non-obese populations, and this association persists after adjusting for BMI [80–82]. Leptin resistance is believed to result from impaired signaling at both the receptor and post-receptor levels, as well as diminished leptin transport across the blood–brain barrier [83]. Moreover, excess leptin activates pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in WAT and immune cells, promoting local and systemic low-grade inflammation and further impairing insulin action [84].

Most studies have shown reduced serum leptin levels after weight loss on low-calorie diets. Consistent data show that a 5–10% reduction in body mass may cause a 17–48% decrease in leptin levels [86–88]. In addition, leptin levels correlated negatively and independently with improvements in IS. Thus, it may suggest that changes in leptin independently modulate IR following weight loss [88]. Both higher baseline leptin levels [89, 90] and greater leptin reductions [91] during weight loss have been proposed as predictors of weight regain, yet findings remain inconsistent, with some evidence linking larger early leptin decreases to better long-term weight and fat loss [92, 93].

The gastrointestinal hormone disturbances

Ghrelin

Ghrelin, a stomach-derived peptide and ligand for the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHS-R), is the only known hunger-stimulating hormone that acts directly on CNS regions and also modulates reward and motivated feeding behaviour [94]. In addition to its central effects, ghrelin influences peripheral glucose and lipid metabolism. Under normal physiological conditions, ghrelin levels rise before meals and decline after food intake. Both glucose and insulin are key regulators of postprandial ghrelin suppression [95]. Moreover, ghrelin acts peripherally via GHSR on endothelial cells in WAT to promote triglyceride clearance and fat storage through PPARγ signaling, an effect that can occur independently of hyperphagia [96].

Several published studies have reported an association between ghrelin and obesity, with plasma ghrelin levels negatively correlated with BMI [97] and IR [98, 99]. People with obesity were shown to have reduced fasting ghrelin levels and blunted postprandial suppression, so food intake fails to suppress ghrelin levels in humans with obesity [97]. Notably, studies using diet-induced obesity have reported ghrelin resistance in the CNS following peripheral ghrelin administration [100]. Many studies showed the rise of ghrelin levels during diet-induced weight loss, which may act as a counterregulatory mechanism and be a predictor of weight regain due to overeating [101]. On the other hand, a recent study indicated that a weight-reducing dietary intervention restored hyperinsulinemia’s ability to suppress serum ghrelin concentration, suggesting an enhanced sense of satiety after moderate weight loss in individuals with overweight/obesity [102]. However, the pleiotropic nature of ghrelin hinders a complete understanding of its contribution to the etiology of obesity.

Incretin hormones – glucagon-like peptide- 1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide

The incretin hormones, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), are secreted from gastrointestinal epithelial cells in response to nutrient intake. Acting via their respective receptors (GLP-1R and GIP-R), they contribute to up to 70% of postprandial insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner (the incretin effect) in healthy individuals [103]. Both GLP-1 and GIP enhance insulin secretion; however, they exert differential effects on glucagon, with GIP stimulating glucagon release during hypoglycemia and GLP-1 suppressing it under hyperglycemic conditions [104]. In humans, limited data indicate GLP-1R expression in WAT. However, some authors have reported a low level of GLP-1R expression in SAT and VAT in humans [105, 106]. Other authors suggest that there is no prominent direct effect of GLP-1 on WAT [107]. Conversely, GIP-R is abundant in human WAT. Ceperuelo-Mallafré et al. showed across cohorts that SAT GIP-R expression in obesity is inversely associated with IR, with adipocytes displaying a GIP-resistant phenotype. In their experiments using human adipocyte cell lines and adipocytes derived from human adipose-derived stem cells, they suggested that GIP acts as an insulin-sensitizing incretin [108]. Many studies have reported reduced GLP-1 secretion, but not GIP, in T2D, prediabetes, and obesity [103].

Conversely, meta-analyses generally did not demonstrate systematic differences between diabetic patients and normal glucose-tolerant subjects in GIP [109] and GLP-1 [110] secretion after a meal. In T2D, the reduced incretin effect mainly reflects impaired GIP responsiveness, while GLP-1–mediated insulin secretion is partially preserved [111].

Recently, long-acting GLP-1R agonists (e.g. liraglutide, semaglutide) and a dual GIP/GLP-1R (tirzepatide) agonist have been confirmed to be highly effective and are currently widely used in obesity and T2D treatment. The SCALE trial showed that administering liraglutide with lifestyle modification was associated with significant weight reduction and improvement of IS in nondiabetic overweight/obese adults [112]. In the same trial, with further follow-up, liraglutide showed an 80% reduction in T2D risk compared with the placebo group [113]. Moreover, in other trials involving both groups of drugs, authors demonstrated broad cardiovascular benefits [114, 115]. Moreover, recent meta-analyses have shown that treatment with GLP-1 analogues significantly reduces VAT, with a similar absolute but higher percentage reduction compared with SAT [116], as well as liver [117] and epicardial [118] fat, in patients with T2D. Another meta-analysis focusing on VAT and ectopic fat found that liraglutide was highly effective in reducing VAT and liver fat, but not epicardial fat, in subjects with obesity and without T2D [119]. Unquestionably, an incretin-based therapy for obesity and T2D has been a tremendous success. However, the underlying mechanisms of the beneficial effects of GLP-1R and GIP/GLP-1R agonists treatment have not yet been fully explained.

Thyroid

In individuals with obesity, the rates obtained by a recent meta-analysis showed the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism around 14.0% and the frequency of subclinical hypothyroidism around 14.6% [9]. However, treatment of overt hypothyroidism leads to only a moderate body weight reduction (generally of less than 10%) [9, 120], indirectly indicating that obesity is usually not a symptom of hypothyroidism. Recent data also indicate that thyrotropin (TSH) variations may be secondary to obesity. Moreover, symptoms of hypothyroidism (e.g., fatigue, low mood, menstrual disturbances) are nonspecific and can be confused with those of obesity. Notably, obesity and hypothyroidism (both subclinical and overt) are associated with metabolic syndrome and are independent risk factors for CVD [121, 122].

Results of thyroid function tests in subjects with obesity may differ from those in lean subjects, with higher serum TSH concentrations but no consistent decrease in thyroid hormone (THs) levels. Several studies have shown a positive association between TSH and BMI [123, 124]. In addition, evidence suggests that progressive central WAT accumulation is associated with increased TSH and FT3 serum levels, independent of IS and metabolic parameters [125]. THs and TSH independently control the mass and function of WAT. The direct confirmation of the influence of THs on WAT is the expression of THs receptors in human adipocytes [126]. In subjects with obesity, elevated TSH concentrations may result from stimulation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis by centrally acting leptin, which activates thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) neurons, leading to increased TSH and THs secretion [127]. Another possible mechanism is a compensatory increase in TSH and FT3 secretion to overcome decreased tissue responsiveness to circulating THs, due to reduced expression of both TSH and THs in adipocytes of individuals with obesity [128]. Interestingly, weight loss obtained by lifestyle modification or bariatric surgery tends to improve the slightly abnormal thyroid function in the majority of patients with obesity [129]. In addition, randomised controlled trials have demonstrated no significant difference in BMI between levothyroxine treatment and placebo in subclinical hypothyroidism [130]. However, the authors showed that BMI was an independent risk factor for the frequency of hyperthyrotropinaemia only in the presence of thyroid autoimmunity [131]. The data that TSH, FT3, and leptin concentrations are increased in individuals with obesity, and that weight loss reverses these changes, support the assumption that the change in thyroid function (in the absence of thyroid autoantibodies) observed in subjects with obesity is probably an adaptive mechanism rather than the primary event.

Sex steroid imbalance in obesity

Androgen excess and IR, both associated with metabolic disturbances, are often present in women with obesity [132]. Most of the recent evidence demonstrates that females with obesity are at increased risk for menstrual dysfunction and infertility/subfertility. Studies have shown that abdominal body fat is more predictive of ovulatory dysfunction than total body fat. However, even in women with ovulation, obesity seems to prolong the time to conception [133, 134]. Obesity has a negative effect on reproductive potential primarily due to functional alterations of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis. In detail, VAT accumulation is strongly associated with IR, which may lead to hyperandrogenemia through direct effects on the ovary and reduced hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) production. Hyperandrogenemia, altered leptin and other adipokine concentrations, and impaired granulosa cell function all contribute to menstrual irregularities by causing hypothalamic dysfunction, decreased folliculogenesis, and lower luteal progesterone levels [135–137]. Studies showed that the general alterations related to obesity (hyperinsulinemia, low-grade inflammation, and dyslipidemia) expand directly into the ovarian follicular microenvironment (elevated intrafollicular insulin, triglycerides levels). In addition, ovarian follicles from females with obesity exhibit inflammatory characteristics and altered androgen activity [137]. Importantly, in both groups, weight loss reduces abdominal fat and IR and improves menstrual function, hyperandrogenism, and metabolic features [135].

Recent meta-analysis reported that male obesity-secondary hypogonadism (associated with low plasma testosterone and low to normal gonadotrophin concentrations) is observed in almost 33% of men with obesity [9]. Studies consistently show reduced testosterone concentrations in men with obesity, with an even higher prevalence in those with T2D or metabolic syndrome [138]. This functional condition may impair sexual function, fertility, WAT metabolism and body composition, bone mineralisation, and cognitive and emotional functions [139]. In men with obesity, testosterone deficiency is thought to result from multiple interrelated mechanisms, including increased aromatization of androgens to estrogens in WAT, VAT dysfunction associated with chronic inflammation and insulin IR, reduced sex SHBG levels, and impaired leptin signaling, which together may contribute to suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular axis and reduced gonadotropin secretion [140]. Importantly, weight loss achieved by diet [141], pharmacologically with liraglutide [142], or with bariatric surgery [141] significantly improved testosterone and gonadotropin levels and reversed the functional hypogonadism caused by WAT excess.

Functional hypercortisolism

Obesity-related hypercortisolism is increasingly recognized as a tissue-specific phenomenon driven primarily by altered local glucocorticoid metabolism within WAT rather than by overt systemic cortisol excess. Many studies indicate that peripheral cortisol metabolism in adipocytes plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of abdominal obesity. The balance between cortisol (active) and cortisone (inactive) regulates glucocorticoid receptor activity in WAT. The enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (11β-HSD1), expressed mainly in WAT and liver, catalyses the regeneration of cortisol from cortisone, whereas 11β-HSD2 catalyses the reverse reaction [143]. In men with obesity, an increased conversion of cortisone to cortisol in abdominal SAT has been observed [144]. Moreover, increased 11β-HSD1 activity has been demonstrated in SAT in subjects with obesity [143, 145], and correlated with the degree of obesity and IR [145, 146]. However, data on depot-specific expression of 11β-HSD1 remain inconsistent [147]. Desbiere et al. reported increased 11β-HSD1 expression in both SAT and VAT in women with obesity, with VAT expression positively correlating with waist circumference and fat mass [148]. In contrast, Alberti et al. demonstrated higher expression of 11β-HSD1 in VAT than in SAT, although VAT expression was not associated with metabolic disturbances [149]. Other studies have suggested that enhanced cortisol regeneration in SAT, rather than VAT, is linked to visceral adiposity in women [150]. Nevertheless, increased local cortisol availability in WAT may exert effects similar to those observed in Cushing syndrome (CS), including the induction of IR and exacerbation of obesity [151], despite stable circulating glucocorticoid levels (in contrast to CS) [152]. Moreover, cortisol may interfere with leptin signalling and contribute to leptin resistance in obesity [153]. Interestingly, several studies have shown decreased 11β-HSD1 expression in SAT following diet-induced [155] or bariatric surgery–induced weight loss [27, 85]. Taken together, these findings support a link between functional hypercortisolism and metabolic syndrome.

Conclusions

WAT dysfunction underlies IR through adipocyte hypertrophy, inflammation, impaired expandability, and alterations in progenitor cell function. In parallel, WAT expansion is associated with alterations in adipokine secretion, interactions with incretin pathways, and changes in local steroid metabolism, accompanied by disturbances in hypothalamic–pituitary axes and systemic hormonal balance. Importantly, weight loss may restore metabolic and hormonal imbalance. WAT-specific phenotyping may be essential for identifying subgroups at increased risk of metabolic diseases and for optimizing prevention and treatment strategies.
