From viral injury to chronic instability: recurrent COPD exacerbations after COVID-19
Authors: Christian Kjer Heerfordt, Niklas Dyrby Johansen, Tor Biering-Sørensen, Pradeesh Sivapalan
Published: 2026

Respiratory infections have long been recognised as acute triggers of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations.1 Increasing evidence, however, suggests that severe infections may also influence the longer-term trajectory of the disease.2 Through persistent airway inflammation, immune dysregulation, and structural lung injury, infections may leave behind a state of heightened vulnerability, one in which the risk of future exacerbations remains elevated long after the pathogen has cleared.

In this issue of eBioMedicine, Henry and colleagues raise the question: did COVID-19 leave a lasting imprint on the COPD lung? In this large retrospective cohort of patients with pre-existing COPD, hospitalisation for COVID-19 was associated with a substantially higher rate of recurrent exacerbations during long-term follow-up compared with matched controls without documented infection.3 The excess risk persisted for several years and was consistent observed across multiple sensitivity analyses. In absolute terms, the incidence of exacerbations was more than doubled among patients hospitalised with COVID-19 compared with matched controls. In contrast, non-hospitalised COVID-19 was not associated with a statistically significant increase in exacerbation risk.

The study has several notable strengths. It draws on a large dataset with access to detailed information on comorbidities, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, allowing adjustment for important determinants of health.4 The analyses appear carefully conducted, with rigorous statistical methods and multiple sensitivity analyses, and the extended follow-up provides a rare opportunity to examine the long-term consequences of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Importantly, the absence of a measurable association among non-hospitalised patients offers a useful internal comparison, suggesting that the observed signal may be specific to more severe infection rather than a universal consequence of SARS-CoV-2 exposure. This perspective is particularly relevant given the growing interest in how acute infectious insults may influence the long-term trajectory of chronic lung disease.

However, important limitations warrant careful consideration. The dataset lacks information on baseline COPD severity and prior exacerbation history, two of the strongest predictors of future exacerbations.5 Without the ability to adjust for these factors, interpretation of the observed associations becomes challenging. Patients hospitalised with COVID-19 are likely to represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup of individuals with COPD who were already at increased risk of exacerbation. Whether the findings therefore reflect a causal effect of severe COVID-19 or simply the underlying risk profile of patients prone to severe infection remains uncertain. As with most observational studies, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Additional sources of uncertainty also merit attention. The study relies on electronic health data to define exacerbations, which may introduce misclassification, particularly for milder outpatients’ events. Furthermore, because the cohort is drawn from a single urban health system, the findings may not fully generalize to population with different healthcare access patterns or healthcare engagement. Socioeconomic factors, including insurance status and unmet social needs, also emerged as predictors of exacerbation risk, underscoring the roles of biological vulnerability and structural determinants of health.

Despite these caveats, the findings raise important questions about the long-term consequences of severe respiratory infections in patients withs COPD. Whether the association reflects persistent biological effect of severe viral injury, the downstream consequences of critical illness, or the vulnerability of patients already at highest risk remains uncertain. Clarifying these mechanisms will require prospective studies integrating clinical data with biological markers of inflammation and lung injury.

The study highlights the need to better understand how infectious insults may induce sustained inflammatory responses that shape long-term outcomes in chronic disease. Importantly, this question extends far beyond COPD exacerbations alone, as infection-driven inflammation has been implicated in downstream complications including cardiovascular events, kidney injury, and stroke.6 Understanding how infectious insults translate into sustained risk may open new opportunities for prevention and long-term management in vulnerable populations.
