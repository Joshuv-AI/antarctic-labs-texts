Functional analysis of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiac tissue during endothelial cell network changes
Authors: Toshiharu Koike, Katsuhisa Matsuura, Shinako Masuda, Takuma Takada, Tatsuro Iida, Tatsuya Shimizu, Junichi Yamaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTissue structure stability is essential for effective contractile function in cardiac tissue. Although endothelial cells (ECs) serve as critical sources for functional bioengineered cardiac tissue, the process of network changes in ECs affects the cardiac tissue structure. However, how the process precisely influences the synchrony of cardiomyocytes (CMs) contraction and the contraction regularity in the cardiac tissue remains unclear.MethodsHuman induced pluripotent stem cell-derived CMs (hiPSC-CMs) were cultured in well-plates for 9–10 days until they displayed stable spontaneous beating. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs), or NHDFs alone, were then seeded and cultured with hiPSC-CMs for 3 days (NHDF + HUVEC + CM, NHDF + CM). Changes in the EC network and cardiac functionality were analyzed over the 3-day period following seeding.ResultsThe EC network expanded until day 2, after which it stabilized. The EC network was observed at the layer of hiPSC-CMs on day 3 in NHDF + HUVEC + CM cultures. Motion capture analysis revealed that hiPSC-CMs in NHDF + HUVEC + CM exhibited more synchronous contractions on day 2 than those in NHDF + CM, with no notable differences on days 1 and 3. The irregularity of spontaneous beat rates, measured using cardiac calcium imaging, was significantly higher in NHDF + HUVEC + CM than in NHDF + CM on day 1. However, the irregularity of spontaneous beating in NHDF + HUVEC + CM was more stabilized on day 3 compared with day1. Other parameters, including contraction amplitude, spontaneous beat rate, and calcium uptake and release, did not significantly differ between NHDF + HUVEC + CM and NHDF + CM over time.ConclusionsAlterations in the EC network may induce irregular spontaneous contractions in hiPSC-CMs at the early phase of co-culture followed by more regular contractions over time, with improving synchrony in the contractions within the cardiac tissue transiently.Keywords: Angiogenesis, Cardiac function, Human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte, Vascular endothelial cell

Introduction

Bioengineered human cardiac tissues incorporating human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) are being developed globally. HiPSC-derived cardiac tissues are instrumental in drug screening for cardiac toxicity assessment, modeling cardiac diseases, and regenerative therapies to potentially repair damaged heart tissue [1]. Despite these advancements, creating cardiac tissue with native-like characteristics remains a major challenge [2,3]. Essential factors in developing native-like cardiac tissue include electrical connectivity among CMs, synchronous contraction of CMs, CM maturation, a three-dimensional structure, and vasculature to supply oxygen and nutrients to the tissue [2,3]. Additionally, the presence of non-CMs, such as vascular mural cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells, which interact with CMs through direct cell-cell contact and paracrine signaling, is critical for fabricating native-like cardiac tissue [4,5]. Among non-CMs, endothelial cells (ECs) are among the most prevalent, forming the fundamental structure of blood vessels to supply oxygen and nutrients to CMs [5]. ECs interact with CMs through direct contact and paracrine signaling, impacting cardiac metabolism, growth, contractile performance, and heart rhythmicity to maintain homeostasis [6]. However, the effects of migrating and proliferating ECs during angiogenesis on cardiac function remain poorly understood. In the context of hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue, co-culture with hiPSC-CMs and ECs has shown improvements in sarcomere organization and contractility after 1–2 weeks [[7], [8], [9], [10]], coinciding with the establishment of a robust EC network through early co-culture phases, marked by substantial EC proliferation and migration [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, little is known about how cardiac tissue functionality is influenced by fluctuations in EC network dynamics, particularly variations in EC proliferation and migration over time.

ECs also play a role in tissue structural remodeling during angiogenesis as the EC network undergoes changes [11,12]. During this process, ECs alter their cytoskeleton, extend and contract, advance into the extracellular matrix (ECM)—primarily composed of collagen and hyaluronan—resulting in a newly formed EC network [12]. These changes can affect non-ECs and induce multiple structural modifications in cardiac tissue, including ECM degradation to facilitate EC migration and cell-cell junction reorganization [12], CMs hypertrophy through endothelium-derived nitric oxide (NO) [13,14], and phenotype changes in fibroblasts that alter ECM properties [15]. Consequently, the dynamic remodeling of the EC network is a complex process that leads to various structural changes in the heart [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. For bioengineered human cardiac tissue, maintaining structural stability—characterized by aligned cellular organization [16], an ECM providing strength and flexibility [17], cooperative physical and electrophysiological connections between cells [18], and paracrine interactions between CMs and non-CMs to sustain homeostasis—is essential for achieving functional benefits such as electrophysiological stability [18], synchronized CMs contraction [16], and mature energy metabolism in CMs [16,18]. In contrast, our previous findings demonstrated that unaligned CMs negatively impact contractility and synchronous contraction in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue [16], suggesting that structural instability could impair cardiac function. Nonetheless, the effects of structural alterations during EC network changes, particularly when tissue stability may fluctuate dynamically, on cardiac functional changes—such as synchronous contraction, calcium handling, and metabolism—remain unclear.

This study aimed to fabricate a hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue model during EC network changes and to investigate the associated cardiac functional changes.

Materials and methods

hiPSCs and cardiac differentiation

The hiPSC line 201B7 [19] was obtained from RIKEN (Tsukuba, Japan). hiPSCs expressing α-myosin heavy chain and rex-1 promoter-driven drug-resistance genes were cultured on inactivated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (Repro CELL, Yokohama, Japan) following a previously described method [20]. Cardiac differentiation of hiPSCs was induced in a stirred bioreactor system (Bio Jr.8; Able, Tokyo, Japan) based on a published protocol [21]. On day 17 of cardiac differentiation, cell aggregates were dissociated using 0.05 % trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (043–30085; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere as described previously [16] (Fig. 1a).

Time schedule and experimental schema diagram. (a) Time schedule of the experiment. (b) Experimental schema diagram of the three protocols. A stepwise co-culture process was adopted to obtain human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived (hiPSC-derived) cardiac tissue exhibiting spontaneous beating alongside endothelial cell (EC) network changes over time. On day 29, hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) were seeded onto a 96-well plate. Spontaneous beating of the hiPSC-CM plate was observed on days 34–36. On day 38 or 39, normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs) treated with mitomycin C (MMC), with or without human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), were seeded onto the hiPSC-CMs according to the following protocols: (a) hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs, cultured with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF); (b) hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs, without VEGF and bFGF; (c) hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs, cultured with VEGF and bFGF. Following the seeding of NHDFs (with or without HUVECs) onto hiPSC-CMs, analysis of EC network formation and cardiac function was conducted over the subsequent three days. D, day.

Purification and culture of hiPSC-CMs

On day 20 following the initiation of hiPSC cardiac differentiation, cells were treated with 1.5 μg/mL puromycin for 23–25 h to eliminate non-CMs as described previously [16]. On day 22, cells were harvested using 0.05 % trypsin/EDTA and seeded onto culture dishes at a density of 6.4–12.7 × 104 cells/cm2. A second puromycin treatment (1.5 μg/mL) was administered on day 27, and on day 29, hiPSC-CMs were harvested with 0.05 % trypsin/EDTA and filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh cell strainer as described previously [16]. hiPSC-CMs were then seeded at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells/cm2 on a 96-well microplate. Cultures were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. From days 34–36, when spontaneous beating of the hiPSC-CMs was observed, the medium was replaced with a mixture of DMEM with 10 % FBS (medium A) and RPMI 1640 (11875093; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 2 % B27 supplement without insulin (A3695201; Thermo Fisher Scientific) (medium B), along with 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. Medium A and medium B were mixed in a 1:9 ratio for EC network analysis or 3:7 for quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), motion capture analysis, cardiac calcium imaging, and metabolic studies. Cultures were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, and the medium was refreshed every 2–3 days (Fig. 1a).

Culture of fibroblasts and endothelial cells

Normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs) (CC2511; Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (C2517A; Lonza) were purchased from Lonza Group Ltd., and green fluorescent protein (GFP)-HUVECs were obtained from Angio-proteomie (cAP-0001GFP; Boston, MA, USA). These cells were cultured in a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C using commercially available growth media: FGM-2 (CC3132; Lonza) for NHDFs, and EGM-2 (CC3162; Lonza) for HUVECs and GFP-HUVECs. Experiments utilized cells with passage numbers below 9, and the culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days.

Stepwise co-culture of hiPSC-CMs, NHDFs, and HUVECs

On day 38 or 39 after initiating the cardiac differentiation of hiPSCs, when spontaneous contractions of hiPSC-CMs were observed, NHDFs and HUVECs were harvested using 0.05 % trypsin/EDTA (CC-5012; Lonza). NHDFs were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/cm2 with or without HUVECs, onto hiPSC-CMs in a 96-well plate. HUVECs were seeded at a density of 6.0 × 104 cells/cm2 for EC network analysis, qRT-PCR, and motion capture analysis, or at a density of 4.0 × 104 cells/cm2 for cardiac calcium imaging and metabolic analysis (Fig. 1a and b). NHDFs were pre-treated with 10 μg/mL Mitomycin C (2089821; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for 6 h before harvest and seeding onto hiPSC-CMs to suppress proliferation. A mixed medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS (medium A), RPMI 1640 with 2 % B27 supplement without insulin (medium B), and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (medium A: medium B = 1:9 for EC network analysis or 3:7 for qRT-PCR, motion capture analysis, cardiac calcium imaging, and metabolic analysis) was used for the 3-day co-culture. The following three culture conditions were applied: (a) NHDFs, HUVECs, and hiPSC-CMs were cultured with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) at 50 ng/mL (10020; Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) at 50 ng/mL (10018B; Peprotech) for 3 days; (b) NHDFs, HUVECs, and hiPSC-CMs were cultured without VEGF and bFGF for 3 days; (c) NHDFs and hiPSC-CMs (excluding HUVECs) were cultured with 50 ng/mL VEGF and 50 ng/mL bFGF for 3 days. During co-culture, cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, and the medium was refreshed every 2 days.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were fixed with 5 % dimethyl sulfoxide (045–24511; Wako Pure Chemical Industries) in methanol (138–14521; Wako Pure Chemicals Industries). For evaluation of the CD31-positive cell network (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2), cells were blocked with 5 % FBS in PBS (14249-24; Nacalai Tesque). For multi-staining, cells were blocked with 2 % donkey serum (017-000-121; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) and 2 % bovine serum albumin (A7906-100G; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS (containing 0.1–0.2 % NP40 (25223-04; Nacalai Tesque)). Fixed cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used for immunocytochemistry were rabbit polyclonal anti-cardiac Troponin T (TnT) antibody (1:200, 45932; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and mouse monoclonal anti-Vimentin antibody (1:300, V9; Sigma-Aldrich). For secondary antibodies, goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody Alexa Fluor ™ 647 (1:100, A-21245; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and FITC-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:100, AB_2340792; Jackson ImmunoResearch) were used. After washing, cells were stained with a mouse phycoerythrin-conjugated monoclonal antibody against human CD31/PECAM1 (1:140, FAB3567P; R&D Systems, Tokyo, Japan). Nuclei were visualized with Hoechst 33342 (1:2000, H3570; Thermo Fisher Scientific). For CD31-positive network evaluation, only the anti-CD31 antibody was used as the primary antibody. Samples were imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope (FV1200; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) or a BZ-X810 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

High-throughput image acquisition and data analysis for EC network

Images for quantitative analysis of the CD31-positive cell network (i.e., EC network) were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X810; Keyence) with a 4 × objective lens. These images were tiled in a 3 × 5 format to construct a complete EC network image across a 96-well plate. The EC network was analyzed using AngioTool (National Institute of Health, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA) [22]. Total vessel area, number of junctions, and vessel length within the EC network were quantified using AngioTool software, with analyses performed at 15, 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding NHDFs and HUVECs on hiPSC-CMs.

Time-lapse analysis of EC network in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue with spontaneous beating

On day 38 or 39 post-initiation of cardiac differentiation from hiPSCs, NHDFs were seeded at 1.5 × 105 cells/cm2 and GFP-HUVECs at 6.0 × 104 cells/cm2 onto hiPSC-CMs. The cultures, including NHDFs, GFP-HUVECs, and hiPSC-CMs, were supplemented with or without 50 ng/mL VEGF and 50 ng/mL bFGF and maintained for three days. During this period, time-lapse imaging of the EC network within hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue was performed, capturing an image every hour with a Biostation-CT Timelapse system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Dye excitation was conducted at 488 nm, and emission was collected at 515 ± 10 nm. Cultures were kept in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, with media replaced every two days. Individual frames were compiled into a movie using ImageJ software.

Motion capture analysis of hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue

Motion capture analyses were conducted at 15 (14–16), 24 (23–25), 48 (47–49), and 72 (71–73) hours post-seeding of NHDFs, with or without HUVECs, on hiPSC-CMs, following this procedure: Cardiac tissue videos were recorded at 30 frames per second using an inverted microscope equipped with a 3CCD digital camera (acA1300-30uc; Basler, Ahrensburg, Germany). Each time point had a range of approximately 2 h, and videos were recorded around the same time for all conditions. The X-axis and Y-axis movement distances of hiPSC-CMs were calculated over time at five designated points (four corners and the center of the cardiac tissue) using the ViewPoint motion capture analysis tool (Glenallan Technology Inc., Clinton, NY, USA), according to the instrument manual and previous studies [16,23]. Maximum movement distances, calculated as X2+Y2, were analyzed over time for each point. Additionally, the time from the start of measurement to the first peak contraction for X2+Y2 at each of the five points was recorded. Cardiac calcium imaging

On day 29 of cardiac differentiation, hiPSC-CMs were seeded onto a 96-well microplate with a black polystyrene frame at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells/cm2. On day 30, hiPSC-CMs were transfected with the GCaMP6f calcium indicator using AAV6 (AAV6 L(VB220427-1461cuw)-K1; Vector Builder, Hyogo, Japan) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.5 × 104 infectious units (IU) per cell. On day 31, the culture medium was refreshed. Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5 % CO2, with the medium refreshed every 2–3 days. On day 38 or 39, NHDFs were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/cm2 with or without HUVECs at 4.0 × 104 cells/cm2 onto the hiPSC-CMs. The co-culture, comprising NHDFs and hiPSC-CMs with or without HUVECs, was supplemented with VEGF and bFGF for 3 days. Calcium transients in the hiPSC-derived cardiac tissues were recorded using an immunofluorescence microscope (BZ-X810; Keyence) for 5 min at 30 frames per second on days 1, 2, and 3 post-NHDF seeding, with or without HUVECs. Excitation was performed at 488 nm, and emission was collected at 515 ± 10 nm. The region of interest (ROI) encompassed the entire cardiac tissue within the 96-well plate. Data were further processed using custom plugins (Spiky) within the ImageJ software [24]. Parameters analyzed included amplitude (relative peak amplitude from baseline), spontaneous beat rate, LW20 (time from 20 % of amplitude on the left side of the peak to the peak), RW20 (time from peak to 20 % amplitude on the right), slope max-to-peak (maximum slope from peak start to maximum), and slope max-to-baseline (maximum slope from peak to baseline). Arrhythmic events were assessed via the beat rate regularity index (BRRI) [25], calculated as a percentage index of beat regularity based on the coefficient of variation (SD of beat rate/mean beat rate) of inter-spike intervals:

A BRRI of 100 % indicates highly consistent inter-spike intervals and a steady beat pattern.

Metabolic analysis

Glucose, glutamine, and lactate concentrations in the culture medium were measured over a 30-h incubation period, following a previous study [16]. On day 2 of co-culture, the culture medium was refreshed, and 30 h later (on day 3 of co-culture), the medium was collected. Changes in concentrations of glucose, lactate, and glutamine from day 2 to day 3 were quantified using the Cedex Bio Analyzer (Roche, Basel, Switzerland).

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs, with or without HUVECs, and supplemented with or without VEGF and bFGF on multiple wells of 96 well plate in each experiment on day 41 or 42 after initiating the cardiac differentiation of hiPSCs (co-culture day 3) using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as described previously [16], following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quantity and quality were assessed with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on an Applied Biosystems ProFlex PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as previously described [16]. qRT-PCR was conducted on an Applied BiosystemR ViiA™ 7 RT-PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per manufacturer's instructions, as previously described [16]. Gene expression levels were analyzed using the ΔCT method with the following primers: GAPDH, SCN4A, SCN5A, CACNA1C, RYR2, ATP2A2, PLN, SLC9A1, KCNH2, KCNJ2, KCNJ12, KCNQ1, KCNA2, GJA1, GJA5, GJC1, MYL2, MYL7, MYH6, MYH7, TNNT2, NPPA, NPPB, COL1A1, COL3A1, and ACTA2 (Table 1). Results were analyzed by comparing the plotted 2−ΔCT values, normalized to GAPDH for each sample, under different conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD, or median and range, where applicable. Statistical comparisons between two groups were made using Student's t-test or the Wilcoxon test. Multiple-group comparisons were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc analysis using Tukey's HSD or Dunnett's test as a parametric method, and by Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc analysis using Steel test as a non-parametric method. Analyses were performed using JMP 16 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), with a P value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Analysis of EC network changes following NHDF and HUVEC seeding on hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue

To assess the localization of NHDFs, HUVECs, and hiPSC-CMs, immunohistological staining was performed. On day 3 of co-culture in all three protocols, cardiac TnT-positive cells and vimentin-positive cells, which include hiPSC-derived non-CMs and NHDFs, were detected in the cardiac tissue (Fig. 2a–c). CD31-positive cells were observed at the layer of hiPSC-CMs in cardiac tissue seeded with both NHDFs and HUVECs on day 3 (Fig. 2a and b), suggesting that ECs might migrate into the layer of hiPSC-CMs. Additionally, a CD31-positive cell network was evident in cardiac tissue co-cultured with NHDFs and HUVECs when VEGF and bFGF were present, whereas no CD31-positive cell network formed in tissue seeded with NHDFs alone, even in the presence of VEGF and bFGF (Fig. 2c).

Immunohistochemical staining images of hiPSC-derived cardiac tissues. (a) hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs, cultured with VEGF and bFGF; (b) hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs, without VEGF and bFGF; (c) hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs, cultured with VEGF and bFGF. CD31 (red), cardiac TnT (white), vimentin (green), and nuclei (blue) were stained. All images were captured on day 3 of co-culture. Scale bar = 100 μm.

To examine the temporal dynamics of the EC network, we tracked CD31-positive cells over time in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs, with or without HUVECs (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1). In cardiac tissue co-cultured with NHDFs and HUVECs in the presence of VEGF and bFGF, a CD31-positive cell network appeared 15 h after co-culture initiation, progressively elongating (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). By contrast, cardiac tissue co-cultured without VEGF and bFGF showed an initial engraftment of CD31-positive cells, which gradually decreased over time (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Furthermore, cardiac tissue seeded only with NHDFs and cultured with VEGF and bFGF showed no CD31-positive network formation (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 1c). For a quantitative assessment of changes in the CD31-positive cell network over time in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs, vessel area, total junction number, and total vessel length were analyzed. This analysis compared tissues seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF to those cultured without these growth factors (Fig. 3d–f). After 24 h of co-culture, all three parameters were significantly elevated in tissue cultured with VEGF and bFGF compared to tissue without these factors. In cardiac tissue cultured with VEGF and bFGF, vessel area, total junction number, and total vessel length of the CD31-positive cell network increased, peaking 48 h after co-culture initiation. In contrast, in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs and cultured without VEGF and bFGF, vessel area, total junction number, and total vessel length did not increase substantially over time, significantly decreasing after 72 h compared to the 15-h mark. To further observe EC network dynamics, GFP-HUVECs were seeded onto hiPSC-CMs in place of HUVECs (Supplementary Movies 1–4). In cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and GFP-HUVECs and cultured with VEGF and bFGF, GFP-HUVECs gradually branched and fused over time. Conversely, GFP-HUVECs gradually disappeared in tissue cultured without VEGF and bFGF.

In summary, by seeding NHDFs and HUVECs onto spontaneously beating hiPSC-CMs and culturing with VEGF and bFGF, we created an hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue model that demonstrates dynamic structural remodeling and EC network evolution over time to analyze cardiac functional changes during EC network changes.

Gene expression analysis of cardiac tissue

We examined mRNA expression levels in cardiac tissues using qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 4, the mRNA expression levels of specific contractile proteins, calcium-handling genes, gap junction proteins, and collagen production genes did not differ significantly across the three protocols. In contrast, the expression of KCNJ2 was significantly higher in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF than in tissue seeded with NHDFs alone under the same conditions. These results suggest that the EC network may influence the profile of hiPSC-CMs.

Functional analysis of hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue during EC network change

To assess functional changes in cardiac tissue during network changes in ECs, we conducted motion capture analysis, cardiac calcium imaging, and metabolic assays. We compared cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF to tissue seeded with NHDFs alone under similar conditions.

Based on previous studies [16], we hypothesized that structural variations in cardiac tissue could affect CM contraction synchrony. To determine whether the process of EC network changes influences the synchrony of hiPSC-CM contraction, we performed motion capture analysis over three days of co-culture (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Movies 5–6). As shown in Fig. 5b, contraction synchrony was analyzed using the SD of the first peak time of X2+Y2, representing the movement distance at each point along the X and Y axes. A smaller SD indicates greater synchrony in CM contraction. This analysis suggests that the CMs within the cardiac tissues contract synchronously. As shown in Fig. 5c, the SD of the time from the start of measurement to the first peak contraction at five specified points was significantly lower 48 h after the start of co-culture in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs than in tissue seeded with NHDFs alone. No differences in synchrony were observed between the two conditions at 15-, 24-, and 72-h post-co-culture. These findings indicate that the process of EC network changes may transiently contribute to improved synchrony of hiPSC-CM contraction in cardiac tissue.

We next performed cardiac calcium imaging to examine whether the process of EC network changes affect calcium transients in cardiac tissue (Supplementary Movies 7–8). In tissues seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF, a CD31-positive cell network was observed on day 3 of co-culture (Supplementary Fig. 2a and 2c), whereas no CD31-positive network was seen in tissues seeded only with NHDFs under similar conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2b and 2d). A schematic illustration of calcium transient parameters is shown in Fig. 6a, and representative calcium transient waves from the two protocols are displayed in Fig. 6b. Amplitude, spontaneous beat rate, LW20, RW20, slope max to peak, and slope max to baseline did not differ significantly between conditions at any time point (Fig. 6c–e). However, the beat rate regularity index (BRRI) of cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF was significantly lower on day 1 than in tissue seeded with NHDFs alone, with no significant differences observed on days 2 and 3. Furthermore, BRRI in cardiac tissue with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF was significantly higher on day 3 than that on day 1. In contrast, in cardiac tissue with NHDFs alone cultured with VEGF and bFGF, BRRI did not show any significant differences among the co-culture period. These results suggest that the process of EC network change in cardiac tissue might stabilize the rhythm of cardiac tissue over time although the initial phase of EC network change disrupt the contraction regularity of cardiac tissue transiently.

Discussion

The primary findings of this study are as follows: 1) We developed a hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue model to examine cardiac function during EC network changes, using a stepwise co-culture of NHDFs and HUVECs on pre-cultured hiPSC-CMs that beat spontaneously in the presence of VEGF and bFGF. 2) The EC network progressively increased over time and the ECs migrated into the layer of hiPSC-CMs in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs, cultured with VEGF and bFGF, while the ECs gradually diminished in the absence of these growth factors. 3) KCNJ2 expression levels in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs in the presence of VEGF and bFGF were significantly higher than those in cardiac tissue seeded only with NHDFs under the same conditions. 4) EC network changes in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs in the presence of VEGF and bFGF promoted the synchronous contraction of the entire cardiac tissue on co-culture day 2. 5) The initial phase of EC network changes caused irregular beating in the cardiac tissue but did not influence calcium uptake and release through co-culture period. 6) The beating rhythm of cardiac tissue with EC network was more stabilized on co-culture day 3 compared with day 1. 7) Changes in the EC network did not affect glucose and glutamine consumption or lactate production in the cardiac tissue.

Previous studies have shown that hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue fabrication using a co-culture technique involves simultaneous seeding of hiPSC-CMs, ECs, and/or other non-CM cell types such as fibroblasts and pericytes [[8], [9], [10]]. A co-culture period of 1–2 weeks promoted structural and functional maturation of hiPSC-CMs, resulting in elongated sarcomere length, enhanced contractility, rapid contraction and relaxation, and improved electrophysiological stability [[8], [9], [10]]. Hanna et al. used a stepwise co-culture technique differing from the present study, where hiPSC-CMs were seeded on ECs and NHDFs co-culture after day 6, at which point the EC network had already developed, and the cells were cultured without VEGF and bFGF for approximately two weeks [7]. They demonstrated that hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue with an established EC network displayed more mature hiPSC-CMs, including elongated morphology and higher expression of contractile protein genes such as MYH6, MYH7, and TNNT2 compared to monocultured hiPSC-CMs [7]. While prior studies focused on the effects of an already-formed EC network on hiPSC-CM maturation in cardiac tissue [[7], [8], [9], [10]], we focused on the functional changes in cardiac tissue during EC network changes in once structurally stabilized hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue after approximately 10 days of hiPSC-CM monoculture. Using a stepwise co-culture of ECs and NHDFs on hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue, we developed a hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue model during EC network changes.

During angiogenesis in cardiac tissue, adequate vascularization through EC proliferation and migration is essential for supplying sufficient oxygen and nutrients [26]. In this study, we observed notable EC sprouting and migration in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF. Gehardt et al. demonstrated that EC sprouts extend multiple filopodia at their distal tips, indicating alterations in the EC cytoskeleton with specialized tip structures that may play roles in migration in response to VEGF [27]. Additionally, bFGF induces EC proliferation in vitro [28] and promotes in vivo angiogenesis, which encompasses EC migration and proliferation [29]. These previous findings support our observations.

EC migration into the ECM is crucial for angiogenesis in the native heart, involving directional movement toward a gradient of soluble chemoattractant and immobilized ligands [[12], [30]]. It is expected that the cell-cell junctions among CMs in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue dynamically change during EC network development, potentially leading to dyssynchronous CM contraction. In this study, the process of EC network development prompted more synchronous contraction of the entire cardiac tissue on co-culture day 2 transiently. This suggests that the process of EC network development in cardiac tissue, during which CM junctions undergo dynamic changes due to EC proliferation and migration, might improve the synchrony of hiPSC-CM contraction. Daria et al. demonstrated that a preformed EC network promoted synchronous contraction of neonatal CMs and increased connexin 43 expression on co-culture day 3, using a stepwise co-culture approach in which neonatal CMs were seeded on an already preformed EC network [31]. This previous finding supports our results.

In the present study, the initial phase of EC network change on co-culture day 1 caused irregular beating of the cardiac tissue; however, this irregularity was not observed on co-culture days 2 and 3. Beat rate variability (BRV) in hiPSC-CMs was utilized to evaluate the effects of antiarrhythmic drugs on these cells [32]. Some previous studies defined increased BRV in hiPSC-CMs as an indicator of arrhythmogenicity [25,33]. In the heart, ECs interact with CMs through paracrine effects mediated by neurohormonal factors derived from ECs, such as endothelin-1 (ET-1), which affect cardiac function, including contractile properties and electrophysiological stability [5]. ET-1 is a vasoconstrictive peptide secreted by ECs. Elevated levels of ET-1 in the atrium lead to electrical remodeling that predisposes the atria to persistent arrhythmia [34]. Li et al. demonstrated that ET-1 stimulation of atrial myocytes increased basal diastolic cardiac calcium ion levels and induced spontaneous arrhythmogenic events [35]. In this context, cell-cell interactions between ECs and CMs during the initial phase of EC network change may contribute to the irregular beating of hiPSC-CMs. In contrast, the current study found that the process of EC network changes did not disrupt the synchronous contraction of hiPSC-CMs and transiently promoted synchronous contraction. This result suggests that EC network changes might not negatively affect electrical propagation, one of the factors contributing to irregular beating in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue.

No significant differences were observed among the three conditions in the gene expression levels related to contractile proteins, calcium handling, ion channels, and gap junctions, except for KCNJ2. The expression level of KCNJ2 on co-culture day 3 was significantly higher in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF compared to that seeded with NHDFs alone under the same conditions. Hanna et al. demonstrated that KCNJ2 expression levels in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue, fabricated by a stepwise co-culture of hiPSC-CMs on a preformed EC network using HUVECs and NHDFs, were significantly higher on co-culture day 12 compared to hiPSC-CMs in monoculture [7]. This finding is consistent with our results. KCNJ2 encodes the Kir 2.1 channel, which is responsible for the inward rectifier potassium current and plays a crucial role in maintaining the resting membrane potential of hiPSC-CMs [2]. Furthermore, Jingjun et al. showed that overexpression of KCNJ2 in hiPSC-CMs could enhance cellular functional maturation in terms of electrophysiology and calcium handling [36]. In the present study, hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF exhibited irregular beating on co-culture day 1 compared to that seeded with NHDFs alone cultured with VEGF and bFGF. However, the beating rhythm of cardiac tissue with EC network changes was more stabilized on co-culture day 3 compared with day 1. Consistent with previous findings [7,36], the increased gene expression levels of KCNJ2 may help normalize the irregular beats as the co-culture period progresses in cardiac tissue seeded with NHDFs and HUVECs cultured with VEGF and bFGF.

In the current study, the process of EC network change did not impact calcium transients in the entire hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue. Consistent with these findings, the mRNA gene expression levels associated with cardiac calcium handling were not significantly different among the conditions. In contrast, previous studies have shown that hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue co-cultured with ECs for approximately 1–2 weeks—longer than the duration of the present study—exhibits the maturation of cardiac calcium handling [[8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, a longer co-culture period of hiPSC-CMs and ECs may be necessary for mature calcium handling in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue.

In the present study, the process of EC network changes did not affect glucose and glutamine consumption or lactate production in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue during ATP production [2,37]. Glutamine and glucose serve as energy sources in hiPSC-CMs, whereas human adult CMs primarily rely on fatty acid oxidation for energy production [2,37]. Moreover, in the absence of glucose and glutamine, lactate may also be utilized as an energy source by hiPSC-CMs [38]. However, lactate accumulation can lead to acidosis in the culture medium, which may influence cardiac function [39]. Our results suggest that the process of EC network change in hiPSC-derived cardiac tissue can occur without excessive energy consumption. Additionally, differences in medium components among the various protocols may have had minimal impact on the cardiac functional analysis over time in the present study.

In the present study, we cultured hiPSC-CMs for 9–10 days before co-culture. Although we seeded hiPSC-CMs after 2 rounds of puromycin purification, we could not exclude the possibility that the purity of hiPSC-CMs might be changed during the culture period before co-culture.

In the native heart, angiogenesis is a dynamic process involving the proliferation and differentiation of ECs, which play critical roles in physiological and pathological remodeling, such as cardiac tissue growth, homeostasis, and wound healing. This process occurs through direct cell-cell interactions and paracrine signaling between CMs and ECs [4,5,12]. Further development of cardiac tissue models containing a perfusable vascular network may provide important insights into the functional changes in the myocardium during angiogenesis in the native heart.
