The proteomic and metabolomic signature of inherited chromosomally integrated HHV‐6 and its role in all‐cause dementia and mortality risk: The UK Biobank study
Authors: May A. Beydoun, Minkyo Song, Choa Yun, Nicole Noren Hooten, Jordan Weiss, Hind A. Beydoun, Michael R. Duggan, Keenan A. Walker, Lenore J. Launer, Michele K. Evans, Alan B. Zonderman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVETo examine associations of inherited chromosomally integrated human herpesvirus 6 (iciHHV‐6) with incident dementia and mortality, characterize proteomic and metabolomic correlates of carrier status, and evaluate whether these biomarkers relate to dementia and mortality risk.METHODSWe analyzed UK Biobank participants ≥50 years of age with plasma metabolomics (N = 138,676) and proteomics (N ≤ 15,416) data and ≈15 years of follow‐up. Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for key confounding factors evaluated associations of iciHHV‐6 with dementia and mortality. Multivariable linear models assessed iciHHV‐6 associations with metabolomic and proteomic profiles. Mediation and interaction were evaluated using structural equation models and Cox models with biomarker interactions. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression identified independent proteomic and metabolomic predictors using imputed biomarker data.RESULTSiciHHV‐6 was associated with higher dementia risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.32), particularly among women (HR = 1.66) and individuals with elevated Alzheimer's disease polygenic risk (HR = 1.49). Branched‐chain amino acids (leucine and isoleucine) were elevated among carriers without mediating dementia risk. Proteomic analyses identified 126 nominally associated iciHHV‐6–related proteins, many of which (Neurofilament Light Chain (NEFL), Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), Yes‐Associated Protein 1 (YAP1), Sialic Acid‐binding Immunoglobulin‐like Lectin 5 (SIGLEC5), Interleukin 19 (IL19), A Disintegrin And Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs 16 (ADAMTS16), Sphingomyelin Phosphodiesterase 1 (SMPD1)) were also associated with dementia and/or mortality. Exploratory pathway analyses suggested enrichment of proteins related to immune regulation and post‐translational modification. Predictive models identified NEFL, GFAP, Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A (VEGF), Brevican (BCAN), remnant cholesterol, and polyunsaturated fatty acids as dementia predictors (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.83), whereas NEFL, Growth Differentiation Factor 15 (GDF15) Latent Transforming Growth Factor Beta Binding Protein 2 (LTBP2), Ectodysplasin A2 Receptor (EDA2R) and Advanced Glycosylation End‐product Specific Receptor (AGER) predicted mortality (AUC = 0.70).DISCUSSIONiciHHV‐6 was associated with increased dementia risk, particularly among women and genetically susceptible individuals, with neuroimmune and metabolic biomarker profiles potentially relevant to brain aging and mortality risk.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias are progressive disorders marked by cognitive decline and impaired daily functioning, 1 arising from genetic and socio‐environmental factors. 2 , 3 Emerging evidence also implicates infections, including latent herpesviruses such as human herpesvirus 6 (HHV‐6). 4 , 5 HHV‐6 is a neurotropic virus acquired early in life that can persist latently. 6 In ≈1% of individuals, it is inherited as chromosomally integrated HHV‐6 (iciHHV‐6), embedding near telomeric regions and present in all nucleated cells. 6 Although clinically silent, iciHHV‐6 has been linked to immune signaling, metabolism, and cellular senescence, with potential relevance to chronic diseases such as atherosclerosis and cancer. 7 , 8

Neuropathologic studies have detected iciHHV‐6 and other herpesviruses (HHV‐7, cytomegalovirus [CMV], herpes simplex virus 1 [HSV‐1]) in postmortem brains of individuals with AD, Parkinson's disease, and vascular dementia. 9 , 10 , 11 These viruses may contribute to AD‐related pathology, including amyloid accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, 9 , 10 and have been identified in memory‐related brain regions such as the hippocampus. 11 However, their role in AD remains debated, with evidence both supporting 4 , 11 , 12 and refuting 13 , 14 involvement. Susceptibility may vary by host genetics, epigenetics, co‐infections, 15 , 16 and viral reactivation in immunocompromised states. 17 , 18

Omics signatures may precede dementia onset, but their interplay with host–viral interactions is not well understood. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Viral reactivation may shape these molecular profiles, 23 overlapping with cardiometabolic and immune processes linked to brain atrophy. 12 , 24 Proteomics and metabolomics offer non‐invasive approaches to study these interactions, 25 , 26 , 27 while sex and genetic factors may modify vulnerability, including higher risk among individuals with elevated AD polygenic risk and differences in immune responses between women and men. 28 , 29

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Latent viral infections have been hypothesized to contribute to Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, although evidence remains inconsistent. Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV‐6), a neurotropic virus acquired early in life, can persist latently and has been detected in postmortem brain tissue, but findings regarding its role in neurodegeneration are mixed. Approximately 1% of individuals carry inherited chromosomally integrated HHV‐6 (iciHHV‐6), representing lifelong viral DNA integration. Epidemiologic evidence linking iciHHV‐6 to dementia risk is limited, and few large population‐based studies have evaluated associations between viral carrier status and host proteomic or metabolomic profiles. Although omics signatures predictive of dementia and mortality have been identified in cohorts such as the UK Biobank, their relationship with chronic viral exposures remains insufficiently characterized. Interpretation: In this large prospective cohort, iciHHV‐6 carrier status was associated with an increased risk of incident dementia, particularly among women and individuals with elevated Alzheimer's disease polygenic risk, but not with mortality. Metabolomic analyses identified limited associations, with elevated branched‐chain amino acids that did not mediate dementia risk. Proteomic analyses identified multiple nominal associations, and exploratory pathway enrichment suggested involvement of immune and regulatory processes; however, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating. Combined proteomic–metabolomic models demonstrated predictive utility for dementia and mortality, whereas prediction of iciHHV‐6 status was limited. Overall, the findings indicate that iciHHV‐6 is associated with host omic profiles and dementia risk, without establishing underlying biological mechanisms. Future directions: Further research is needed to clarify the biological and clinical significance of iciHHV‐6 in neurodegeneration. Studies incorporating longitudinal neuroimaging, immune profiling, and markers of viral activity may help distinguish whether associations reflect direct biological effects or shared underlying processes such as immune dysregulation or genetic susceptibility. Replication across diverse populations and experimental models will be important. Future work integrating viral carrier status with proteomic, metabolomic, and genetic data may improve risk stratification and advance understanding of host–virus interactions in aging and dementia.

Clarifying the role of persistent herpesvirus infections in dementia and mortality, and their links with the metabolome and proteome, may improve etiologic understanding and identify targets for intervention. Using a systems biology framework, this study analyzed UK Biobank data to: (1) assess associations of iciHHV‐6 with dementia and mortality; (2) identify plasma biomarker profiles linked to iciHHV‐6; (3) evaluate metabolomic and proteomic mediation or moderation; and (4) examine proteomic pathway enrichment and overlap across dementia, mortality, and iciHHV‐6.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Database

This study used UK Biobank data, a prospective cohort of ≈500,000 adults 40–69 years of age recruited between 2006 and 2010. 30 Baseline assessments included sociodemographic, lifestyle, health, cognitive, and biological data, with linkage to hospital, primary care, prescription, and death records, as well as genotyping and multi‐omic resources. 30 Ethical approval was granted by the North West Multi‐centre Research Ethics Committee; this study was conducted under application #77963 with National Institutes of Health Institutional Review Board approval.

Dementia and AD incidence outcomes

Dementia outcomes were defined using UK Biobank algorithms based on linked hospital (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions [ICD‐9/10]), primary care, and death records. Subtypes included AD, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, and unspecified dementia. AD was defined using ICD‐10 codes (F00, G30), with earliest diagnosis as onset. Prevalent cases were excluded. Follow‐up extended to 2023. 31

Mortality outcome

All‐cause mortality was obtained from national registries through March 2023. Survival time was calculated from baseline; participants were censored at last follow‐up. Prior studies suggest omic markers may partially mediate exposure–mortality associations 32 (Appendix I, supplementary materials 1).

iciHHV‐6 Exposure

iciHHV‐6 was identified using whole‐genome sequencing and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), detecting integrated viral DNA near telomeres. Analyses modeled iciHHV‐6 as a binary carrier status; viral activity and mechanistic features were not assessed (see Appendix I, supplementary materials 1 for more detail).

Plasma proteome

Plasma protein concentrations were assessed using the OLINK Explore 3072 platform, which applies proximity extension assay (PEA) technology in combination with next‐generation sequencing. 33 About 3000 proteins associated with various physiological domains, such as inflammation, neurology, cardio‐metabolism, and oncology are covered by the platform. NPX (Normalized Protein eXpression) is a relative log2 scale used to express protein abundances.

Plasma metabolome

The Nightingale Health high‐throughput nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) platform quantified 249 plasma metabolites, including lipids, lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acids, amino acids, ketone bodies, glycolysis‐related metabolites, and inflammatory markers such as glycoprotein acetyls (GlycAs). 34 Values were reported in molar concentrations following standardized preprocessing (outlier detection, normalization, and missing data handling). 34 Single imputation was applied to both metabolomic and proteomic datasets 34 (Appendix II, Supplementary materials 1), with imputed proteomics used only in joint analyses of proteins and metabolites predicting dementia, mortality, and iciHHV‐6.

Data were processed using UK Biobank pipelines: metabolites were winsorized, square‐root transformed, standardized, while proteins (OLINK NPX scale) underwent platform‐specific normalization and quality control. All biomarkers were standardized and modeled as continuous variables in regression, Cox, and generalized structural equation modeling (GSEM) analyses, and high‐dimensional analyses used Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) on imputed datasets. Further details are provided in Appendix I (Supplementary materials 1).

Covariates

We included covariates informed by prior UK Biobank studies. Demographics comprised age, sex, and race/ethnicity (White vs non‐White). Genetic predisposition to AD was captured using a standardized polygenic risk score (AD PRS), linked previously to brain structure and cognition. 34

Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using the Townsend Deprivation Index, income, and education, 34 with household size as a proxy for social and economic support. Cardiovascular health was measured using the Life's Essential 8 (LE8) score, reflecting lifestyle and health factors associated with dementia risk 34 ; the cholesterol component was excluded due to high correlation with metabolomic (particularly lipidomic) biomarkers.

We also included a binary indicator of hospital‐treated infection burden, 12 , 24 along with measures of overall health: self‐rated health and number of physician‐diagnosed conditions, capturing general health and multimorbidity linked to cognitive decline and mortality. Polypharmacy was assessed by number of medications, whereas serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D and red cell distribution width (RDW) captured nutritional status and inflammation/oxidative stress. Finally, the first 20 genetic principal components were included as potential confounders 34 (Appendix II, Supplementary materials 1).

Study sample

We restricted the study sample to individuals 50 years of age or older at baseline (Figure S1, Supplementary materials 7). Participants were then excluded if they had missing exposure data, missing key covariates, or evidence of prevalent dementia at baseline. Two analytic samples were derived: one with complete plasma metabolomics data (N = 138,676, Sample A) and another with complete proteomics data (N = 15,416, Sample B).

Statistical methods

Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R (v4.4.1) on the UK Biobank Research Analysis Platform (RAP). Person‐time was calculated from age at baseline to age at diagnosis, death, loss to follow‐up, or December 31, 2023. Cumulative incidence and incidence rates (per 100,000 person‐years) were estimated overall and by sex.

Descriptive statistics were generated overall and by sex for Samples A and B. Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests compared outcomes by iciHHV‐6 status in Sample A. Cox models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for dementia and mortality by iciHHV‐6 overall, by sex, and across AD PRS tertiles, with interaction terms testing heterogeneity. Model 1 adjusted for age, age2, sex, race/ethnicity, AD PRS, SES z‐score, household size, and LE8 score; Model 2 additionally included infection burden, self‐rated health, comorbidities, medication use, serum vitamin D, RDW, and the first 20 genetic principal components. iciHHV‐6 was modeled as a binary exposure, with associations interpreted as differences by carrier status rather than direct measures of viral activity.

In Sample A, metabolite–iciHHV‐6 interactions were tested using two‐way interaction terms (Appendix III, Supplementary materials 1), and mediation was assessed using generalized structural equation modeling (GSEM) with Weibull outcomes; indirect effects were estimated using the delta method (Appendix IV, Supplementary materials 1). Given the few significant metabolites, no pathway enrichment was conducted.

In Sample B, iciHHV‐6–protein associations were assessed using linear regression (Model 1 covariates). As no proteins met Bonferroni significance, nominally associated proteins (p < 0.05) were used for exploratory pathway enrichment (OLINK, STRING). Bonferroni correction was applied in downstream analyses of protein associations with dementia and mortality, with shared pathways identified.

LASSO logistic regression with cross‐validation (glmnet, R) identified minimal proteomic and metabolomic predictors of dementia, mortality, and iciHHV‐6, using imputed data (Appendix V, Supplementary materials 1). Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and log‐minus‐log plots. Metabolomic data underwent Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE)–based single imputation, whereas proteomic data were analyzed in unimputed form for primary analyses and were median‐imputed only for exploratory joint metabolomic–proteomic LASSO models. Statistical significance was defined as two‐sided p < 0.05. Code and supplementary outputs will be publicly available upon publication at https://github.com/baydounm/UKB‐paper25‐supplementarydata.

RESULTS

Descriptives

Table 1, along with Table S1 (Supplementary materials 1), summarize characteristics of 138,676 UK Biobank participants included primarily for metabolomic analyses (Sample A; 52.7% women). Women were younger and had better diet quality, blood pressure, and overall modified LE8 scores but reported more comorbidities and medication use. Men had higher sleep and physical activity sub‐scores. During follow‐up, dementia and mortality occurred more frequently among men (2.86% and 13.6%) than women (2.10% and 7.75%). Several biomarkers and genetic principal components differed by sex, including AD PRS, RDW, and vitamin D, whereas racial/ethnic distribution was similar. Table S2 (Supplementary materials 1) shows comparable patterns in the smaller metabolomic–proteomic sample (Sample B). In both samples, the prevalence of iciHHV‐6 positivity was ≈1.3%.

Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models

Kaplan–Meier curves indicated worse dementia‐free survival among iciHHV‐6–positive individuals (log‐rank p = 0.026) but no difference in all‐cause mortality (p > 0.05) (Figure 1A, B). In Cox models adjusted for demographics, AD PRS, lifestyle, and health factors (Table 2, Model 1), iciHHV‐6 positivity was associated with increased dementia risk (HR = 1.32, p = 0.034). Associations were stronger among women (HR = 1.66, p = 0.005) and among participants in the highest AD PRS tertile (HR = 1.49, p = 0.013). Additional adjustment for comorbidities, self‐rated health, medication use, genetic ancestry, and infection burden (Model 2) produced similar estimates. The results indicate that the stronger associations observed among women are not explained by differences in dementia prevalence or incidence, which have been shown in previous studies to be greater among men.

Metabolomics and proteomics of iciHHV‐6

Among 249 metabolites, only leucine and isoleucine, both branched‐chain amino acids, were associated with iciHHV‐6 at p < 0.05 in multivariable models (Model 1, Sample A). Proteomic profiling identified 126 plasma proteins associated with iciHHV‐6 at nominal significance (p < 0.05) in Model 1 (Sample B), although none remained significant after Bonferroni correction. These nominally associated proteins were retained for downstream pathway enrichment analyses as part of an exploratory hypothesis‐generating framework (Supplementary datasheets 1 and 2 (Supplementary materials 2 and 3), Figure 2).

Proteomics of dementia and mortality risk

Cox analyses of 2923 z‐scored proteins identified 589 proteins associated with dementia risk at p < 0.05, including 12 that passed Bonferroni correction. Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) and Neurofilament Light Chain (NEFL) were among the strongest predictors (Figure 3A). For mortality, 1313 proteins were associated at p < 0.05, with 693 passing Bonferroni correction (Figure 3B). Leading mortality markers included Growth Differentiation Factor 15 (GDF15), WAP Four‐Disulfide Core Domain Protein 2 (WFDC2), and Ectodysplasin A2 Receptor (EDA2R). Proteomic profiling of iciHHV‐6 status which had identified 126 nominally associated proteins, included 7 (NEFL, GFAP, Yes‐Associated Protein 1 (YAP1), Sialic Acid‐binding Immunoglobulin‐like Lectin 5 (SIGLEC5), Interleukin 19 (IL 19), A Disintegrin And Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs 16 (ADAMTS16), and Sphingomyelin Phosphodiesterase 1 (SMPD1)) overlapping with dementia and/or mortality. Exploratory pathway enrichment analyses based on nominally significant proteins (p < 0.05) from the iciHHV‐6 proteomic models are summarized in Figure S2 (Supplementary materials 8) and detailed in Appendix V. Because no proteins met Bonferroni‐corrected significance in the primary iciHHV‐6 proteomic analyses, these enrichment results should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. Using the OLINK Pathway Browser and STRING DB, we identified ≈300 proteomic pathways shared across proteins associated with iciHHV‐6, dementia, and mortality involving immune, synaptic, apoptotic, and metabolic processes. Dementia‐specific analysis identified 859 unique pathways, whereas 11 were shared exclusively between HHV‐6 and dementia and involved immune evasion, interferon suppression, and transcriptional regulation. STRING and OLINK provided complementary enrichment metrics (false discovery rate [FDR]‐adjusted p‐values and log10[observed/expected] strength). Complete pathway results are available at https://github.com/baydounm/UKB‐paper25‐supplementarydata.

Generalized Structural Equation Modeling for mediation via metabolome

Although iciHHV‐6 positivity was associated with higher leucine and isoleucine levels, these metabolites did not mediate associations between HHV‐6 and dementia or mortality in mediation models adjusted for demographic and health‐related covariates (Table 3).

Interactions between metabolites and iciHHV‐6

Interaction models revealed antagonistic interactions between iciHHV‐6 and branched‐chain amino acids or BCAAs (leucine and isoleucine) for dementia risk, suggesting possible buffering effects. In contrast, synergistic interactions were observed with phospholipids in large very low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) particles (Figure S3A (Supplementary materials 9); Supplementary datasheet 3 (Supplementary materials 6)). For mortality, synergistic interactions were observed between iciHHV‐6 and lipid‐related metabolites—including total cholesterol, LDL/VLDL subclasses, cholesteryl esters, phospholipids, and apolipoproteins—indicating super‐multiplicative effects on mortality risk (Figure S3B (Supplementary materials 9); Supplementary datasheet 4 (Supplementary materials 5)).

Top proteomic and metabolomic predictors of iciHHV‐6, dementia, and mortality risk

LASSO logistic regression models were used in an exploratory framework to identify proteomic and metabolomic predictors of dementia and mortality (Appendix VI, Table S2, Supplementary datasheet 5), Supplementary datasheet 6). Dementia risk was predicted by established demographic and behavioral factors (age, sex, SES, and LE8 components), along with proteins including NEFL, GFAP, Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A (VEGF), and Brevican (BCAN). NEFL and GFAP were positively associated with dementia risk, whereas VEGF and BCAN showed inverse associations, consistent with neurodegenerative and neurovascular pathways. Two NMR metabolites—remnant cholesterol and total polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)—were also inversely associated with dementia risk. For mortality, key proteomic predictors included NEFL, GDF15, Latent Transforming Growth Factor Beta Binding Protein 2 (LTBP2), and Ectodysplasin A2 Receptor (EDA2R) as risk markers, and Advanced Glycosylation End‐product Specific Receptor (AGER) as a protective marker, alongside metabolites such as alanine, phospholipids in medium LDL, and cholesteryl esters in large LDL. Model discrimination was stronger for dementia (Area Under the Curve, AUC = 0.832) than for mortality (AUC = 0.703), supporting the relevance of these signatures for established outcomes. In contrast, prediction of iciHHV‐6 positivity was limited (AUC = 0.523). The selected proteins (e.g., Neuroligin 2 (NLGN2), Photoreceptor Cilium Actin Regulator (PCARE), RALBP1 Associated Eps Domain Containing 1 (REPS1), and Sialic Acid Acetylesterase (SIAE)) did not overlap with the primary dementia predictors, and these findings are therefore presented as exploratory, highlighting potential neuroimmune signals that warrant further investigation rather than definitive inference.

DISCUSSION

Summary of findings

In this large UK Biobank multi‐omic study, iciHHV‐6, present in ≈1% of participants, was associated with increased risk of all‐cause dementia—particularly among women and those with high AD polygenic risk—but not with mortality. Metabolomic analyses showed elevated BCAAs in iciHHV‐6–positive individuals that did not mediate dementia risk, although interaction models indicated attenuation of dementia associations by BCAAs and amplification of mortality associations by lipid‐related metabolites. Proteomic profiling of iciHHV‐6 status identified 126 nominally associated proteins, including 7 (NEFL, GFAP, YAP1, SIGLEC5, IL‐19, ADAMTS16, and SMPD1) overlapping with dementia and/or mortality. Integrative models combining metabolomic and proteomic markers predicted dementia with high accuracy, with NEFL, GFAP, VEGF, BCAN, remnant cholesterol, and PUFA as key predictors. Overall, these findings suggest that iciHHV‐6 carrier status is linked to dementia risk and host proteomic signatures related to neuroimmune and metabolic pathways, with effects most evident among women and individuals with elevated AD genetic susceptibility.

iciHHV‐6 and dementia risk

Although some studies have failed to find a significant relationship or to detect viral material in AD brains, 13 , 35 others have reported detection of HHV‐6 DNA or antigens in brain tissues, implicating this virus in biological processes such as neuroinflammation, amyloid aggregation, and tau pathology. 36 A comprehensive multi‐cohort analysis reported inconsistent HHV‐6 enrichment in AD, suggesting the need for harmonized methods of detection as well as longitudinal approaches. 37 Even in the absence of active viral replication, iciHHV‐6 may have a subtle effect on host immunity and metabolism, thereby contributing to neurodegenerative risk. 5 These latent viral effects could accelerate brain aging through chronic neuro‐inflammatory pathways. 16

As hypothesized, iciHHV‐6 was associated with higher dementia risk, particularly among women and individuals with elevated AD genetic risk. These patterns may reflect sex differences in immune function, as women generally exhibit stronger innate and adaptive responses, including heightened antiviral activity and cytokine signaling, 28 , 38 which may also increase susceptibility to chronic neuroinflammation in the presence of persistent viral elements such as iciHHV‐6. 39 Hormonal factors may further contribute, as estrogen influences immune activation and viral latency/reactivation, with menopausal changes potentially affecting neurodegenerative risk. 40 Genetic susceptibility, including apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 or AD polygenic risk, may further amplify inflammatory and neurodegenerative processes such as microglial activation and impaired neuronal repair. 11 Together these findings support a model in which iciHHV‐6 acts as a latent stressor interacting with sex‐specific immune regulation and genetic vulnerability to influence dementia risk. 11 , 28

iciHHV‐6 and host omic landscapes

Herpesviruses, including HHV‐6, have been proposed to influence host biological systems through pathways involving oxidative stress, chromatin remodeling, and cell cycle regulation. 16 Some herpesvirus proteins can also impair the function of neuronal RNA‐binding proteins, compromising neuronal integrity. 27 , 41 Proteome‐wide Mendelian randomization has identified blood protein signatures linked to viral burden, highlighting proteins such as HLA‐E and ULBP2. 42 , 43 Metabolomic analyses further indicate that viral infections can disrupt pathways in lipid metabolism, glycolysis, and amino acid turnover, particularly along the tryptophan–kynurenine axis, which is implicated in neuroimmune signaling and neurotoxicity. 44 Our exploratory findings indicate that iciHHV‐6 may be associated with differences in host proteomic and metabolomic profiles, including pathways related to immune regulation, lipid metabolism, and neuronal integrity, which are also implicated in dementia risk. Although these associations are consistent with potential biological mechanisms linking latent viral integration to neurodegeneration, they do not establish that iciHHV‐6 directly alters these molecular pathways or causally contributes to dementia development. These patterns may instead reflect shared upstream processes, such as chronic immune activation, host genetic susceptibility, or cumulative environmental exposures, which may influence both viral persistence and systemic omic profiles.

Plasma proteome versus dementia and mortality

Circulating proteins have emerged as early indicators of dementia risk. UK Biobank analyses show that plasma proteins linked to immune activation, cardiometabolic dysfunction, and neuronal health can predict dementia several years prior to diagnosis. 45 Some may also be causally related, as suggested by Mendelian randomization studies. 46 These markers appear to mediate or moderate the effects of established risk factors, such as cardiovascular health and AD genetic susceptibility. 47 Of note, integrating proteomics with imaging, polygenic scores, and metabolomics can improve risk stratification and offer insights into brain aging‐related molecular mechanisms, such as immune and proteostasis/autophagy pathways. 48 Converging with prior evidence, 49 , 50 our study found that elevated GFAP and NEFL were key proteomic predictors for dementia risk, both of which were retained after implementing machine learning on the entire proteome and metabolome. Among proteins, elevated GDF15 emerged as a key predictor and remained significant in machine‐learning models integrating the plasma proteome and metabolome. Thus, these proteins emerged as important predictors in multivariable models and are consistent with prior literature on aging, neurodegeneration, and mortality risk.

Plasma metabolome versus dementia and mortality

A detailed description of the literature regarding associations among plasma metabolome, proteome, dementia, and mortality risks is provided in Appendix VII (Supplementary materials 1). In brief, metabolomics provides important insights into biological processes underlying dementia. Studies in the UK Biobank have shown that alterations in lipid, amino acid, and energy metabolism may precede dementia onset. Prior studies have suggested that disruptions in pathways involving kynurenine, glutamate, and acylcarnitines may relate to neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, although the present study did not conduct formal metabolomic pathway enrichment analyses. Because metabolomic profiles reflect environmental and behavioral exposures—such as diet, pollution, and physical activity—they may also help distinguish dementia subtypes and clarify disease mechanisms. In the present study, several lipid‐related metabolites interacted synergistically with iciHHV‐6 in predicting mortality. Remnant cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, and LDL particles—key contributors to vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis—suggest that viral integration may exacerbate cardiometabolic dysregulation and endothelial damage. In contrast, the branched‐chain amino acid isoleucine showed antagonistic interactions with dementia and mortality, possibly reflecting involvement of the Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. Tyrosine displayed a similar antagonistic relationship with mortality risk, potentially indicating compensatory responses to oxidative stress, although it did not modify dementia risk. Consistent with prior research, both metabolomic and proteomic markers were associated with mortality.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its large, deeply phenotyped prospective cohort with integrated proteomic and metabolomic data. The use of both qPCR and whole‐genome sequencing improves classification of iciHHV‐6 status, and longitudinal follow‐up with advanced methods, including GSEM mediation and machine learning, enhances interpretability. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, iciHHV‐6 was modeled as a binary exposure, and we did not assess viral transcriptional activity, reactivation, integration sites, or epigenetic regulation; thus, findings reflect host omic correlates rather than direct mechanisms. Second, blood‐based biomarkers may not capture CNS processes. Third, residual confounding, dementia subtype misclassification, and exclusions due to missing data may introduce bias. Fourth, proteomic mediation could not be directly tested due to disclosure risks; instead, overlap between HHV‐6– and dementia‐associated proteins was examined using adjusted and multivariate approaches, similarly for mortality. Pathway enrichment was conducted only for proteomics, with metabolomics limited to individual associations. Finally, enrichment analyses relied on nominally significant proteins, as none met Bonferroni thresholds, and should be considered exploratory.

Conclusions

This study suggests that iciHHV‐6 is associated with increased dementia risk and distinct host proteomic and metabolomic profiles. Although elevated BCAAs did not mediate outcomes, interaction patterns and lipid‐related synergy for mortality indicate complex host–virus dynamics. Shared proteomic markers and dementia‐related pathways support a role of viral latency in immune and transcriptional processes relevant to brain aging. Future studies should validate these findings and assess whether incorporating latent viral markers improves understanding of aging and neurodegeneration.
