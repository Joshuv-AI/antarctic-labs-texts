Positron emission tomography reveals increased myocardial glucose uptake in a subset of Friedreich ataxia patients
Authors: R Mark Payne, Thomas M O’Connell, P Melanie Pride, Gregg R Wagner, George J Eckert, Tiffany R Johnson, Weinian Shou, Gary D Hutchins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhy some but not all patients with the rare disease Friedreich ataxia (FRDA) are at increased risk of poor cardiovascular outcome and death is unclear and unpredictable. We investigated the hypothesis that mitochondrial dysfunction in FRDA leads to altered patterns of myocardial metabolic substrate utilization. We recruited 5 healthy controls (Ctl) and 11 FRDA participants. All underwent fasting myocardial positron emission tomography (PET scan) with 15O–H2O, 18F-FDG, and 11C-Palmitate. We conducted cardiac transcriptomics on mice with ablation of the Frda gene in heart to explore mechanisms of fuel substrate utilization. Five (45%) FRDA participants had an LV mass index (LVMi) less than 51 g/m2.7 (Group I), and 6 (55%) FRDA participants had an LVMi greater than this (Group II). 73% (8/11) of all FRDA participants had evidence of increased myocardial FDG uptake relative to controls. All of Group II had FDG/Palmitate utilization ratios > 95% versus controls, as well as cTnI leak (p = 0.007) when compared to Ctl (p = 0.008) or Group I (p = 0.022). RNA transcriptomics from FRDA mouse heart showed upregulation of genes for glucose uptake and glycolysis with decreased genes associated with mitochondrial energy production. In summary, PET scan identified 2 metabolically distinct subclasses of FRDA cardiomyopathy. FRDA participants with an LVMi greater than 51 g/m2.7 had greater FDG uptake than those with an LVMi less than 51 g/m2.7, or Ctl, and this correlated with LV systolic and diastolic dysfunction. Introduction

Friedreich’s Ataxia (FRDA) is a rare mitochondrial disease that affects the heart, brain, and multiple other organ systems1,2. It is a progressive disease that can cause cardiomyopathy, leading to death in the third to fifth decade of life3,4. This autosomal recessive condition is caused by decreased frataxin (FXN) protein expression due to a homozygous GAA triplet-repeat expansion in intron 1 of the FRDA gene on chromosome 9q13–21.115. Frataxin is targeted to the mitochondrial matrix, where it participates in the assembly of iron-sulfur clusters used within the mitochondria, such as complexes I, II, and III of the electron transport chain, and throughout the cell6,7.

Mitochondrial proliferation within the cardiomyocytes can lead to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy1,8. These hypertrophic hearts develop severe fibrosis and significant microvascular pathology. Although left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF) is often not significantly decreased in FRDA participants, energy production is abnormally low in FRDA hearts, regardless of hypertrophy9. Patients can develop heart failure (HF) in the presence of preserved ejection fraction,3,10 and they can be at increased risk of poor outcomes and death with events that cause cardiovascular stress, such as scoliosis surgery11,12.

Our previous metabolomic analysis of plasma samples from FRDA patients had revealed significant disruption in several pathway-related metabolite clusters13. Thus, we sought to test the hypothesis that mitochondrial dysfunction in FRDA leads to altered patterns of myocardial metabolic substrate utilization. We used PET scan to measure glucose and palmitate uptake in the heart for both normal controls and participants with FRDA. This is the only clinically useful technique for assessing metabolic substrate utilization in the living human heart14. This technique follows radiolabeled metabolism tracers, such as [11C]palmitate (PAL) and [18F]-2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG), to interrogate myocardial substrate metabolism in vivo in real-time. We then correlated this data with echocardiographic measures of cardiac function and biomarker assays of cardiac injury. We also analyzed cardiac gene expression and metabolomics in a mouse model of FRDA cardiomyopathy to gain mechanistic insight into the human findings. Our results show that in a subset of FRDA patients, there is a shift towards glucose as the primary metabolic substrate, which is associated with markers of cardiac injury. These findings may have implications for the clinical management of patients with FRDA.

Based on echocardiographic analysis, Group I comprised 5 of the 11 FRDA participants with an LV mass indexed to height2.7 (LVMi) of less than 51 g/m2.7. Group II consisted of 6 FRDA participants with an LVMi of 51 g/m2.7 or more, indicating severe LV hypertrophy (LVH)15–19. The mean age of Group II was significantly lower (20.5 years) than Group I (32.4 years) (p = 0.035) but was not different from Ctl (p = 0.071). The age at diagnosis was younger in Group II than in Group I, and the shorter allele GAA repeat numbers trended higher in Group II but were not statistically significant20.

Mean systolic blood pressure was significantly lower in Group II (102.7 mmHg) than Ctl (122.6 mmHg) (p < 0.008), and 5 out of 6 participants in this group were on medication that would lower blood pressure. There was no significant difference in blood pressure between Groups I and II. Heart rates were higher in Group II (73.8 bpm) than Ctl (56.8 bpm), despite their use of beta-blockers (67.7%) or calcium channel blocking agents (16.7%). However, there was no significant difference in heart rates between Groups I and II. Six of the 11 FRDA participants were on metoprolol or propranolol, 1 was on verapamil, and 1 was on losartan. Four of the 11 FRDA participants were not on cardiac-related medication (all were Group I). Body surface area (BSA), body mass index (BMI), height, and weight were not different between the three groups.

Electrocardiography: The QTc (Bazett’s formula) was significant between Ctl and Group II but not between Ctl and Group I (Table S1 and Fig. S1, panel A). QRS duration and QRS axis were not different between the 3 groups (Table S1 and Fig. S1, panels B, C). The T-wave axis of Group II was significant compared to Ctl or Group I (Table S1 and Fig. S1, panel D). Five of the 6 participants in Group II had a superior T-wave axis, whereas none of the 10 participants in the Ctl and Group I cohorts had a superior (negative) T-wave axis. A representative EKG for leads I and aVF for each cohort is shown in Fig. S1, panel E.

Echocardiography: All 16 participants underwent echocardiography using conventional views and measurements at the time of study (Fig. 1 and Table S2)21. This identified 2 sub-groups of FRDA participants, with 6 participants having an LVMi ≥ 51 g/m2.7 (mean of 60.38 g/m2.7 ± 3.75 g/m2.7) (Group II) and 5 having an LVMi significantly less than this (mean of 38.30 g/m2.7 ± 2.40 g/m2.7) (Group I) (Fig. 1, panel A, p = 0.008, and Table S2). There was no overlap in LVMi between Groups I and II. The Ctl cohort had a mean LVMi of 27.93 g/m2.7 ± 3.40 g/m2.7, with an upper 95% confidence interval (CI) of 37.36 g/m2.7. LVMi in the Ctl group was significantly lower compared with Group II (p = 0.008) but was not different from Group I (p = 0.095)22. Two of the 5 Group I participants had an LVMi that fell within the 95% for the Ctl cohort, and the other 3 had an LVMi > 37.36 g/m2.7.

All participants were also plotted as LVM indexed to BSA (LVM/M2) and are shown in Fig. 1, panel B, and Table S2. When adjusted for sex, all participants remained within the same groupings as in Panel A. The aggregate Group II cohort (143.60 g/M2 ± 13.55 g/M2) remained significantly different from both Ctl (70.50 g/M2 ± 10.71 g/M2) (p = 0.008) and Group I (86.26 g/M2 ± 4.37 g/M2) (p = 0.008). As expected, comparing Ctl with Group I was not significantly different. There was no overlap between Group II and either Ctl or Group I.

Figure 1, panel C, and Table S2 show the lateral wall peak velocity of systolic annular mitral motion (S’)23. The S’ of the LV was significantly reduced in Group II (0.075 m/s ± 0.008 m/s) compared with both Ctl (0.130 m/s ± 0.010 m/s) (p = 0.008) and Group I (0.124 m/s ± 0.020 m/s) (p = 0.035). This was expected given that annular displacement is reduced in hypertrophic hearts with normal ejection fraction23. There was no difference in contractility as measured by S’ between the Ctl and Group I. Ejection fraction was not significantly different between all 3 groups (Fig. 1, panel D, and Table S2).

To examine LV diastolic function, the peak velocity of early diastolic mitral annular motion (e′) was quantified (Fig. 1, panel E, and Table S2)24. The Ctl cohort had an e′ of 0.178 m/s ± 0.0199 m/s, Group II was 0.0717 m/s ± 0.0125 m/s, and Group I was 0.138 m/s ± 0.0096 m/s. Thus, Group II was significantly depressed compared with the Ctl group (p = 0.0002) and Group I (p = 0.006), but there was no significant difference between the Ctl and Group I. Finally, the peak velocity of the early diastolic transmitral flow (E) was measured, and LV E/e′ was calculated as an indication of LV filling pressure (Fig. 1F and Table S2). Consistent with the peak diastolic mitral annular velocities, Group II had a significantly higher ratio (12.11 ± 1.58) than both the Ctl (5.22 ± 0.93) (p = 0.014) and Group I (5.64 ± 0.46) (p = 0.008) cohorts indicating increased LV filling pressures. There was no significant difference between the Ctl cohort and Group I for LV E/e′.

Estimates of FDG uptake rate and PAL utilization are derived from the pharmacokinetics of each tracer using tracer kinetic models (see Fig. S2 and Supplemental Methods for details).

The LV myocardial metabolic ratio was calculated as FDG uptake rate / PAL utilization25. The coefficient of determination (R2) for the metabolic ratio to FDG utilization rate was 0.9866 (Fig. S3). Figure S4, panel A, shows the metabolic ratio for the Ctl cohort and all FRDA participants combined. Control participants had a mean metabolic ratio of 0.0211 ± 0.0045 (95% CI 0.0858–0.0337). All FRDA participants had a mean metabolic ratio of 0.1788 ± 0.0936 (95% CI -0.0298–0.3875). The distribution of the FRDA metabolic ratio data was significantly different from Ctl by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p = 0.028), but the means did not reach significance by the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. This suggested that the total FRDA cohort may have multiple phenotypes. To explore this possibility, the PET data was segregated based on the LVMi (Figs. 1A and S4B).

The rate of FDG uptake alone was plotted against LVMi as a continuous variable for all 3 cohorts (Fig. 3B), again using the Ctl cohort for the 95%. This identified the same 8/11 FRDA participants (73%) from Fig. 3A as above the 95% for their FDG uptake. Of these, 6 (55%) were again those participants with an LVMi > 95%. Three FRDA participants with an LVMi slightly above 95% fell below the 95% for FDG uptake. Two of Group I again had a significantly higher FDG uptake, suggesting these 2 participants may be different in their disease course. To compare specific radiotracer utilization between the three groups, the Z-score was calculated for each subject and plotted against the Z-score average of the Ctl cohort for both FDG uptake and PAL utilization (Fig. S4C and D). This confirmed that FDG uptake rate is the primary determinant of the higher metabolic ratio in FRDA participants and not PAL uptake.

The rate of FDG uptake relative to systolic and diastolic function measures was plotted to understand the relationship of substrate utilization to cardiac function. Figure 3C shows the rate of FDG uptake relative to a measure of global contractility, peak systolic annular velocity (S′), plotted on the X-axis26. Of the 8/11 FRDA participants with an FDG uptake rate greater than the 95%, 5 (45%) had depressed systolic function as indicated by their location in the upper left quadrant of the graph (< 5% CI for S′ for Ctl cohort). All of these were in the Group II cohort. Two Group I participants had a depressed S′ but did not have an FDG uptake rate above the 95%, and 3 Group I participants had normal systolic function by S′. Of these 3, 2 had a rate of FDG uptake greater than the 95%. All of the Ctl and 1 of the Group I participants had a systolic function above the 5%, and below the 95% for FDG uptake.

Figure 3D plots the rate of FDG uptake relative to peak mitral annular velocity during early filling (e′)27. As expected, all of the Ctl cohort fell within the lower right quadrant, indicating a low rate of FDG uptake and a higher velocity of e′ consistent with normal diastolic function. Three of the Group I cohort had an e′ comparable to the Ctl group, and 2 were just below the 5% for e′ velocity. Among the Group II cohort, all participants had rates of FDG uptake > 95%, and 5/6 (83%) were below the 5% for e′ velocity (upper left quadrant. Two participants overlie each other). Thus, 6/11 FRDA participants had lower values for e′, suggesting impaired diastolic function, and this correlated with increased glucose uptake. In summary, Fig. 3C and D collectively show that tissue Doppler measures of systolic and diastolic function track closely with the FDG uptake rate.

Finally, the perfusable tissue fraction (PTF) and Myocardial Blood Flow (MBF) were measured in all three groups using15O-water (Fig. S5A, B). Surprisingly, the MBF, which provides quantitative data on coronary blood flow, was not different between the three cohorts in the pharmacologically unstressed state28. However, the signal distribution in the Group II cohort was quite wide (Fig. S5B). The mean MBF for the Ctl group was 0.8168 mL/g/min ± 0.1088 mL/g/min, the Group II cohort was 0.7492 mL/g/min ± 0.1476 mL/g/min, and the Group I cohort was 0.8531 mL/g/min ± 0.06499 mL/g/min. The myocardial flow reserve was not measured in these studies. The PTF, which is a measure of tissue viability, did not differ between the 3 groups (Fig. S5A)29.

Plasma biomarker evaluation: Cardiac troponin (cTnI) leak was identified in all Group II participants but not in any Ctl or Group I subject (Table 2 and Fig. S6). No subject in the Ctl cohort (mean of 0.014 ng/mL ± 0.0245 ng/mL) or Group II cohort (mean of 0.012 ng/mL ± 0.0020 ng/mL) had an elevated cTnI above 0.03 ng/mL. In contrast, all participants in Group II had levels of cTnI above the 99th percentile of 0.03 ng/mL and significantly higher than Ctl or Group I cohorts30. This group had a mean of 0.6617 ng/mL ± 0.5199 ng/mL with a range that was also quite broad at 3.21 ng/mL (0.04 ng/mL to 3.25 ng/mL). Thus, severe hypertrophy in this FRDA population was associated with cTnI leak and, in some cases, reached levels associated with acute injury.

Interestingly, plasma glucose, insulin, total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol, and triglyceride content were not different between the 3 cohorts, nor were 2 markers of fibrosis, soluble ST2 and Galectin-3. Gene expression in the FRDA mouse heart: To obtain a more mechanistic understanding of the cardiac fuel switch in FRDA patients, we turned to a murine model for molecular profiling of heart tissue. The Frdaf/f::MCK-Cre conditional ablation mouse (FXN MCK-KO) has a loss of FXN expression in the heart and skeletal muscle and develops HF, leading to death. The floxed Frdaf/f mouse has normal expression of FXN and no disease phenotype (Ctl)31,32. We performed bulk RNA sequencing on cardiac ventricular tissue from both models to identify changes in pathways associated with central energy metabolism. Table 3 presents those metabolic genes with significant differences, log2 fold change, and false discovery rate (FDR), and supplemental Table S3 contains the complete listing of these genes.

Male and female mice were analyzed separately to look for sex-dependent effects and mapped onto pathway diagrams in Fig. 4; Table 3. The glycolytic pathway (Fig. 4A) displays a mixture of genes upregulated in the Ctl or the FXN MCK-KO mice. The Slc2a1 gene, which codes for the Glut1 transporter, is upregulated in the male FXN MCK-KO heart, suggesting that insulin-independent glucose uptake is increased. The Slc2a4 gene, which codes for the Glut4, insulin-dependent transporter, is higher in male and female Ctl mice. This is consistent with the observation that the FRDA mice display signs of insulin resistance33,34. In the next step, hexokinase 1 (Hk1) expression is upregulated in the FXN MCK-KO mice, and hexokinase 2 (Hk2) is upregulated specifically in the female mice. This irreversible step suggests more glucose is being committed to the glycolytic pathway.

The next several steps in the glycolytic pathway (Fig. 4A) show predominantly higher gene expression in the Ctl than in the FXN MCK-KO mice, consistent with an overall reduction in energy production in the KO mice. Expression of the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase, Pdk2 isoform, is upregulated in the Ctl females, whereas the Pdk4 isoform is upregulated in both sexes of the FXN MCK-KO mouse. This kinase blocks the action of pyruvate dehydrogenase (Pdh), which is responsible for transforming pyruvate into acetyl-CoA. When the levels of Pdk are elevated, it inhibits Pdh, which leads to the conversion of pyruvate to lactate via lactate dehydrogenase (Ldh). We measured lactate in these hearts by mass spectrometry, revealing a significant increase in the lactate levels of both sexes in the FXN MCK-KO mice (Fig. 4B). This suggests that the Pdk4 perturbation may be dominant, which further supports the glycolytic phenotype in the FXN MCK-KO heart.

The gene expression changes within the TCA cycle are mapped to the pathway in Fig. 4C. Nearly all the genes displayed higher expression in the Ctl mice, which predicts lower overall energy production in the FXN MCK-KO mice. The one exception is the expression of mitochondrial phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 2 (Pck2). This isoform is the only one that bridges the TCA cycle and glycolysis. Table 3 shows that the change in Pck2 represents the most significant gene upregulation in the FXN MCK-KO mice. Figure 4C also shows that Pck2 transforms oxaloacetate (OAA) into phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP). This can be transported into the cytosol using the citrate-isocitrate transporter. PEP then moves back up the glycolytic pathway to feed biosynthetic pathways such as serine biosynthesis or glyceroneogenesis. Thus, the RNA data from the FRDA cardiac model confirms a shift towards glycolysis and reduced energy production.

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate fuel substrate utilization in the human FRDA heart. This novel approach is crucial because metabolic flexibility is a key finding in healthy myocardium; loss of this flexibility is associated with failure and death35. Fatty acids are the preferred fuel in the heart, but in pathologic conditions, such as HF or ischemia, the heart can shift fuel utilization to glucose, which is more efficient and requires less oxygen but generates less energy36.

The primary finding of this study is that fuel substrate utilization by the heart in a subset of FRDA patients is altered to emphasize glucose uptake over fatty acid oxidation. All of the Group II participants had a rate of FDG uptake and an FDG/PAL ratio significantly above the 95% for the Ctl. This would suggest that these hearts rely more on glycolysis as a source of ATP generation than those in which the FDG / PAL ratio was not different from Ctl participants. In addition, 2 participants from Group I also had significantly increased FDG/PAL ratios with increased rates of FDG uptake, suggesting that there are intermediate phenotypes, i.e., these hearts may be progressing in their cardiomyopathy. Previous studies looking at FDG and PAL uptake in dilated cardiomyopathies showed a similar increase in myocardial glucose uptake but differed by a decrease in fatty acid metabolism37. In this study, we found that the primary driver of the FDG/PAL ratio is the increased rate of FDG uptake and not a decrease in the rate of fatty acid uptake. This is important because positron emission tomography with FDG uptake is readily available in most major medical centers as a clinical tool, e.g., for evaluation and detection of cancer. FDG PET has also been widely used to evaluate coronary artery disease, myocardial viability, and HF14. Radiolabeled PAL, however, is typically available only in a research setting. Thus, the rate of FDG uptake is informative when quantified in the heart and would predict an alteration of substrate utilization in the FRDA heart when compared with Ctl.

The mechanism(s) of increased reliance on glucose metabolism in the hypertrophic human FRDA heart is informed by the changes in gene expression in the conditional mouse heart. Here, genes associated with insulin-independent glucose uptake and favoring glycolysis are upregulated in the FXN MCK-KO mouse. In contrast, genes associated with fatty acid oxidation decreased in the FXN MCK-KO state, and gene expression supporting the TCA cycle generally decreased. This emphasizes the diminished role of the TCA cycle in energy generation in FRDA. Additionally, the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (Pck1 and Pck2) is the rate-limiting enzyme of gluconeogenesis. The increased expression of Pck2 strongly favors the export of a valuable fuel substrate from mitochondria back to the cytosol, where it participates in other biosynthetic pathways38. In addition, mitochondrial Pck2 is a direct binding target of Lin28a in cardiomyocytes, which increases the expression level of Pck2 and promotes hypertrophic growth39.

Multiple authors have reported that the mean LV ejection fraction was not different between controls and FRDA participants, as we found in this study3,40. However, tissue Doppler interrogation in this study demonstrated clear differences in systolic and diastolic parameters compared with Ctl41. Those participants in Group II had evidence of significantly worse systolic and diastolic function, and this correlated strongly with the increased rate of FDG uptake. In contrast, those participants in Group I had diastolic and systolic indices that were not different from Ctl. The clinical implication of these findings is that under stressful conditions like major surgeries or illnesses, the FRDA heart with hypertrophy may face a higher risk due to mitochondrial dysfunction.

In this study we also found that severe hypertrophy in FRDA was associated with cTnI leak. None of the participants with an LVMi < 51 g/m2.7 (Group I) had evidence of troponin leak in this study, whereas all of Group II participants had evidence of cTnI leak at the time of study. Although the presence of troponin in blood is typically associated with coronary insufficiency, such as acute coronary syndrome, there are multiple etiologies of cTnI leak that predict poor outcomes30,42.

Study strengths and limitations: This study has two main strengths. First, FRDA is a rare disease that makes enrolling large cohorts for studies challenging. Nonetheless, the current study shows that cardiac metabolism is significantly affected in a subset of FRDA patients with severe LVH. Gene expression changes in the cardiac conditional murine model of FXN ablation support a potential mechanism for this metabolic shift. This study also suggests that a single tracer PET scan using FDG can identify these patients without the need for assessment of fatty-acid oxidation. Thus, PET scan may be useful for evaluating the FRDA heart prior to stressful events. Second, there is a strong correlation between LVMi and cTnI release with the finding of higher metabolic ratios of glucose uptake to fatty acid utilization in the heart. Thus, these may be predictive biomarkers for FRDA patients who are at risk of HF during stress, such as scoliosis surgery, which has been reported to have a higher risk of perioperative complications and death12,43,44.

There are several limitations to this study. First, as a rare disease the cohort size of the FRDA participants is small. Although no attempt was made to select specific participants, and statistical significance was high for the primary results, it is possible that enrollment of greater numbers of participants with FRDA would reveal intermediate phenotypes. This is suggested by Fig. 3A and B, where 2 of the participants in the Group I cohort have an FDG / PAL ratio that is as high as the Group II cohort, although their LVMi is normal. It is also possible that these participants may be progressing in their cardiac disease state. Second, investigational PET scans of participants are expensive and not widely available. Whereas FDG is commonly used in clinical practice, PAL use is restricted to institutions with a cyclotron. Additionally, the estimation of rates of metabolic substrate utilization from the kinetics of PET radiotracers has inherent limitations associated with tracer kinetic model assumptions, limitations associated with the use of venous blood samples to infer arterial blood metabolite levels45, limitations imposed by patient motion (cardiac cycle, repiratory cycle, and gross movement), and the spatial resolution and sensitivity of PET scanners. Collectively these limitations lead to bias and variance in PET estimates of metabolic substrate utilization. Third, FRDA is a progressive disease affecting multiple organs. This study provides only one snapshot of its progression and cannot show how metabolism changes over time. Fourth, although a diagnosis of diabetes was an exclusion criterion, FRDA patients can still have abnormalities in glucose utilization. Additionally, certain medications, such as β-blockers can affect serum glucose levels: 40% of Group I and 67.7% of Group II were on β-blockade. Although the glucose and serum insulin levels were not significantly different between groups, these factors could potentially affect uptake of FDG by heart and can be explored in future studies. Finally, exercise testing was not possible due to advanced neuromuscular disability. This would provide correlation between cardiopulmonary function relative to the PET scans.

Conclusions

In this cohort of FRDA patients, a PET scan identified two metabolically distinct subclasses of FRDA cardiomyopathy. FRDA patients with severe LVH had greater FDG uptake relative to palmitate utilization than FRDA patients without severe LVH. FRDA patients with severe LVH have systolic and diastolic dysfunction, as well as ongoing cardiac damage as indicated by cTnI leak. These findings suggest that FRDA patients with significant LVH may be at increased risk of complications from surgery or major illness.
