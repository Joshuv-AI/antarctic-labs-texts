Transcription factor PpWRKY13 targets a cell wall modification gene in peach to regulate fruit softening
Authors: Shaolei Guo, Jianlan Xu, Shenge Li, Binbin Zhang, Ruijuan Ma, Mingliang Yu, Zhijun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe postharvest preservation of peach (Prunus persica) fruit has always been a concern, and research on the mechanisms behind peach fruit softening is important for improving postharvest preservation technologies. The WRKY gene family is a multitude of transcription factors in plants that broadly participate in the regulation of various biological processes. Nonetheless, the roles of WRKY family members in peach fruit softening remain largely unknown.ResultsHere, we report a peach WRKY family member PpWRKY13 that may be involved in peach fruit softening through the regulation of fruit softening-related gene PpXTH33. Investigations of gene expression levels across different peach organs revealed that PpWRKY13 may play a critical role in peach fruit during storage. A multiple alignment analysis indicated that the PpWRKY13 protein contains a WRKY domain and structural features diagnostic of the Group IIa subfamily. A subcellular localization assay demonstrated that the pBI121-PpWRKY13-EGFP fusion protein is highly localized in the nuclei of tobacco leaf cells, suggesting that PpWRKY13 is characteristic of a transcription factor. We identified a candidate target gene, PpXTH33, of PpWRKY13 using DNA affinity purification sequencing. Additionally, a luciferase assay and transient overexpression suggested that PpWRKY13 may activate the transcription of PpXTH33.ConclusionsIn summary, PpWRKY13 may activate the transcription of PpXTH33 and function as a putative modulator of peach fruit softening. This work advances our knowledge of peach fruit softening mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08453-6.Keywords: Peach, PpWRKY13, DAP-seq, Fruit softening

Background

Peach (Prunus persica) is an economically important horticultural crop widely cultivated in the subtropical and temperate zones. As a typical climacteric fruit, peaches can rapidly soften and perish after harvest without effective preservation techniques, and this normally results in substantial economic losses. Typically, fruit softening is caused by alterations in cell wall structures [1], and hydrolytic enzymes, including polygalacturonase, pectin methylesterase, xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase (XTH), β-galactosidase, and pectate lyase, involved in cell wall degradation play crucial roles during this structural modification process [2–4]. It is well known that ethylene is one master regulator of these hydrolytic enzymes [3, 5], and that ethylene response factors, transcription factors (TFs) involved in the signaling pathway, regulate different ripening responses in fruit [6]. PpeERF2 binds to the promoter of PpePG1, a cell wall degradation gene, and suppresses the expression of PpePG1 to regulate fruit ripening in peach [7]. MaDEAR1 as an ethylene response factors sub-family member, directly binds to promoters of several genes related to fruit softening including MaEXP1/3, MaPG1, MaXTH10, MaPL3, and MaPME3, to participate in banana fruit ripening [8]. Fruit ripening and softening involve a complex regulatory network [2], which includes many associated TFs [9–12].

WRKY TFs can serve as transcriptional activators and repressors, broadly participating in the regulation of various biological processes, such as plant growth and development, stress responses, hormone signal transduction, leaf senescence, seed dormancy and germination, and flowering, as well as fruit ripening and softening [13–15]. The WRKY family has been found mainly in plants and is characterized by the highly conserved amino acid sequence (WRKYGQK) at the N-terminus, and as well as C2H2 or C2HC zinc finger-like motif at the C-terminus [16, 17]. WRKY family members are divided into three groups based on the differences in numbers of WRKY domains and zinc finger motif structure [18]. Recently, the WRKY family members involved in fruit ripening and softening have been investigated. In Fragaria vesca, FvWRKY48 directly binds to the promoter of a pectate lyase gene FvPLA, and upregulates its expression, giving rise to pectin degradation and fruit softening [14]. In apple, MdWRKY31 overexpression enhances fruit firmness, and a decrease in the expression of MdXTH2, whereas the MdNAC7 interacts with MdWRKY31 to form a protein complex, and their interaction inhibits MdWRKY31 binding to the promoter of MdXTH2, thus, the fruit softening is regulated by the network [4]. Additionally, MaWRKY49/111 in cooperation with MabHLH28 regulates the transcription levels of softening-related genes in banana [10]. Moreover, a PpEIL2/3–PpNAC1–PpWRKY14 module orchestrates ethylene production to regulate peach fruit ripening [19]. Generally, melting peach fruit softens markedly after harvest, whereas stony hard peach remains firm during ripening, both on- and off the tree [20]. The expression level of PpWRKY13 is elevated in melting peach during room temperature storage after harvest, but the level remains low in stony hard peach. However, stony hard peach fruit softens rapidly after 1-naphthylacetic acid treatment, and the expression level of PpWRKY13 is strongly induced and upregulated during the process [21]. Thus, PpWRKY13 is regarded as a candidate gene in peach fruit softening regulatory network, whereas its regulatory roles have not been elucidated.

In the present study, a real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of different peach organs indicated that PpWRKY13 plays important roles in peach fruit softening during storage. Its nuclear localization supports its function as a TF. A DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq) analysis was used to identify the downstream regulatory genes of PpWRKY13, and result from luciferase assay and transient overexpression suggested that PpWRKY13 may regulate peach fruit softening through cell wall degradation.

Materials and methods

Peach [Prunus persica (L.) Batsch] cultivars of ‘Xiahui8’(‘XH8’) and 'Xiacui' used in this study were from the peach orchard of Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences. These trees were managed using conventional cultivation practices. 'XH8' fruit samples were randomly selected and checked to be of uniform commercial maturity without any external damage, and stored at an air-conditioned room with 25 ± 1℃ and a relative humidity of 75%–85% for 9 days in 2025. 15 fruits were sampled every three days during storage with five fruits serving as one biological replicate. The full bloom flowers and mature leaves were randomly selected. 'Xiacui' peach fruit samples at approximately 10 days before commercial maturity and free of mechanical damage were used for transient expression. The experiments were carried out at Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The pulp flesh, flower and leaf samples were swiftly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 ℃ until use.

Multiple alignment analysis of PpWRKY13 with other WRKY proteins

The WRKYs protein sequences of Group IIa, including MD09G1224500 from Malus domestica [22], as well as SlWRKY40 (Solyc06g068460.2.1) from Solanum lycopersicum [23], used in the multiple alignment analysis were retrieved from the Phytozome database [24]. The protein of AcWRKY40 (Ach26g329191.1) of Actinidia chinensis [25] was downloaded from the Kiwifruit Information Resource [26]. AtWRKY18 (AT4G31800.1) and AtWRKY40 (AT1G80840.1) from Arabidopsis thaliana were downloaded from the Arabidopsis Information Resource [27]. Additionally, other WRKY members, PaWRKY40 (XP_021821871.1) of Prunus avium and PmWRKY40 (XP_008228795.1) of Prunus mume, were obtained from National Center for Biotechnology Information using the protein blast program (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Additionally, the National Center for Biotechnology Information conserved domain database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) was used to confirm the conserved WRKY domain. The WRKY protein sequences of different species were aligned using the ClustalX [28]. The result of multiple alignment was illustrated using the ESPript 3.2 [29].

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using RNAprep pure plant plus kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) in according with the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cDNA first-strand synthesis was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with a gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). RT-qPCR was conducted on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in a total reaction volume of 20 µL using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) under the following conditions: 1 cycle of 30 s at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 5 s at 95 °C and 34 s at 60 °C. Primer sequences of PpWRKY13 and PpXTH33 used for RT‐qPCR are according to the previsous study [21, 30]. The TEF2 gene was used as the internal control [31]. Primers used for the RT‐qPCR are listed in Additional file 1.

Gene cloning and recombinant vector construction

The specific primers were designed based on the prunus persica reference genome v2.0.a1 [32]. The PCR amplification was performed using PrimeSTAR® Max DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). PCR products were purified with a FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing. China). Finally, recombinant vectors were constructed using the ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Primers used for the vector construction are listed in Additional file 1.

Subcellular localization assay

The PpWRKY13 coding sequence (CDS) without the stop codon was inserted into the vector to generate a fusion protein with enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) using ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing. China). The PBI121-EGFP vector and the recombinant vector PBI121-PpWRKY13-EGFP were independently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain GV3101), and independently transiently expressed in the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana, which was stably transformed with a specific nucleus-localized red fluorescent protein (RFP) construct for 60 h. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was used to detect fluorescence signals.

DAP-seq and candidate genes identification

Peach genomic DNA was extracted, then proceed to DAP-seq described by the previsous study [33]. The DNA was fragmented into fragments by ultrasonic, and then, the fragments were ligated to the Illumina sequencing adaptors. PpWRKY13 protein expression using TNT SP6 high-yield wheat germ protein expression system and captured with Magne TM HaloTag beads. DNA fragments were eluted after the incubation of the PpWRKY13 protein with the prepared sequencing library. Finally, the eluted DNA was PCR amplified, and an Illumina NovaSeq 6 000 from Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China), was used for sequencing.

Clean reads were obtained after filtering using the following conditions: reads with adapter sequences were removed, the low quality reads with more than 40% bases having quality values ≤ 10, and reads containingg more than 10% of unknown base (N) were removed, and reads that could not be identified were removed. Clean reads were mapped to the prunus_persica genome v2.0.a1 [32] using Bowtie2 (v2.2.8) software [34]. MACS2 v2.1.2 [35] with a parameter q-value < 0.05 was used for peak calling. Peak-related genes were annotated by the ChIPseeker R package [36]. The peak motifs were identified using MEME-chip in meme suite [37]. The transcriptomic data used for analysis were obtained according to the previous study [21].

Luciferase assay

To investigate whether PpXTH33 could be activated by the PpWRKY13, an approximately 1.5-kb fragment upstream of the start codon (ATG) of PpXTH33 was inserted into the pGreen II 0800‐LUC vector. The full‐length CDS of PpWRKY13 was inserted into the pGreenII 62-SK vector driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter [38]. The constructs were independently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup‐p19), then simultaneously transformed into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves for 60 h. A Chemiluminescence imaging system was used to detect fluorescence signals.

Transient expression in peach fruit

‘Xiacui’ peach fruit samples at approximately 10 days before commercial maturity and free of mechanical damage were used for transient overexpression. The PBI121-EGFP empty vector and the recombinant vector PBI121-PpWRKY13-EGFP were independently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain GV3101). The suspensions containing the empty vector and the recombinant vector were individually injected into peach fruits, and stored at an air-conditioned room with 25 ± 1℃ for 3 days. Fruit samples were individually taken around the injection sites from each fruit, and frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 ℃.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2016, and the Duncan’s multiple comparison method with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

To assess the organs-specific expression of PpWRKY13, we performed RT-qPCR across various peach organs. As shown in Fig. 1, the expression of PpWRKY13 was lower in the flower and the early storage phase peach fruit, while a higher expression level was detected in leaf and the late storage phase peach fruit. Notably, the expression level of PpWRKY13 showed an increasing trend as the storage duration was extended. These results suggest that PpWRKY13 may play a critical role in peach fruit and leaf, especially in fruit softening during storage.

RT-qPCR analysis of the PpWRKY13 expression in various peach organs of ‘XH8’. Different lowercase letters above bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups using Duncan’s multiple comparison method with p < 0.05

A WRKY-like gene (Prupe.3G098100.1) was obtained from the transcriptome data described in a prior study [21], and named PpWRKY13 according to the previsous study [39]. Gene cloning revealed that the CDS of PpWRKY13 in ‘XH8’ is 975 bp in full length, encoding a polypeptide of 324 amino acids. PpWRKY13 sequence was determined to be highly conserved among other WRKY members, possessing a highly conserved, invariant amino acid sequence, WRKYGQK that comprises the WRKY domain, and PpWRKY13 also contains one putative zinc-finger C2H2 motif (C-X4–5-C-X22–23-HX1-H) (Fig. 2), which suggested that this protein be classified into Group II [18]. Interestingly, PpWRKY13 belongs to the Group Ⅱa as AcWRKY40 [25, 39, 40], and they share a high sequence conservation, suggesting that PpWRKY13 may have similar functions as AcWRKY40, which mediates fruit postharvest ripening and softening.

Of the 37,730 putative binding sites of PpWRKY13, 8,350 were located in the promoter regions (within 2.0 kb upstream from the transcriptional start sites), and 1,995 were distributed in 5’ untranslated regions. Interestingly, a significant proportion of binding sites was distributed in distal intergenic regions (Table 1). As expected for a TF, PpWRKY13-binding sites are abundant in proximal to the transcriptional start sites (Fig. 4a). A MEME-Chip was used to analyze the putative DNA binding motifs of PpWRKY13. The most significantly enriched motifs, RGTCAAMN (MEME-1) and BTTGACY (DREME-1), were used to further research (Fig. 4b). Research indicates hydrolytic enzymes involved in cell wall degradation play crucial roles during cell wall structural modification process and closely related to fruit softening [2–4]. Thus, the hydrolytic enzyme genes involved in cell wall degradation may be considered as the candidate genes related to fruit softening. Melting peach fruit softens markedly after harvest, whereas stony hard peach remains firm during ripening, both on- and off the tree [20]. In our previous study, 159 differentially expressed genes were identified as showing higher expression in melting peach fruit than those in stony hard peach fruit during storage [21]. A total of 35 genes (Additional file 5) were obtained by analyzing the differentially expressed genes and the most significantly enriched motifs annotated genes. Finally, 24 genes that were up-regulated during the storage of melting peach fruit were considered candidate genes for further analysis. (Fig. 4c). Notably, a candidate gene PpXTH33 (Prupe.1G255100.1), was annotated as a XTH protein, which may be involved in fruit softening through cell wall degradation for its transcription level increases with prolonged storage time (Fig. 4c).

To further investigate the role of PpWRKY13, we generated PpWRKY13 overexpression constructs. ‘Xiacui’ peach fruits at approximately 10 days before commercial maturity were selected for transient gene expression. The RT-qPCR results showed that the expression level of PpWRKY13 in the PpWRKY13-overexpression peach fruit was significantly higher than in the empty vector injection fruit. Similar results were observed for the expression level of PpXTH33 (Fig. 5b). This suggested that PpXTH33 may be the strong candidate gene regulated by PpWRKY13.

Discussion

Our study suggested that PpWRKY13 may activate the transcription of PpXTH33 and function as a putative modulator of peach fruit softening. This discovery provides evidence for the involvement of the WRKY family in the regulatory mechanism of peach fruit softening.

WRKY, as one of the largest TF families in plants, has been extensively studied [41, 42]. WRKY family members have been identified in various species, including 81 members in tomato [23], 58 members in peach [39], 164 members in banana [43], and 97 members in kiwifruit [40]. It well known that WRKY family members have functions related to stress tolerance [44, 45], and recently, their WRKY family members in plant growth and developmental processes have been extensively studied. This information has been summarized in a review article [13]. However, the WRKY family members involvement in peach fruit softening needs further investigation.

In peach fruit, PpWRKY33 positively regulates the polygalacturonase-inhibiting gene PpPGIP1 to enhance defenses against Monilinia fructicola [46]. Recently, a study showed that the expression of PpWRKY14 is increased during peach fruit development, and the gene binds to the promoters of PpACS1 and PpACO1 to stimulate their expression, and the PpNAC1 forms a dimer with PpWRKY14 to enhance the induction, thus PpWRKY14 regulates peach fruit ripening [19]. However, the involvement of the WRKY gene family members in the mechanisms of peach fruit softening remains largely unexplored. WRKY family members are divided into three main Groups, Ⅰ, Ⅱ and Ⅲ based on the number of WRKY domains and the features of their zinc-finger-like motifs, and Group Ⅱ has been classified into five subgroups IIa-e [18]. Based on sequence alignment and protein domain analyses of the PpWRKY13 sequence, it was determined that PpWRKY13 contains a typical WRKY domain along with a C2H2-type zinc finger motif, which suggests that PpWRKY13 belong to Group Ⅱ. A phylogenetic tree analysis indicated that PpWRKY13 is a member of the Group Ⅱa subfamily [39]. Interestingly, research has revealed that many WRKY TFs related to fruit ripening and softening are found in Group Ⅱ. The WRKY family member MaWRKY49, which accelerates the banana fruit ripening process and induces the transcription of two softening-related genes PL3 and PL11 [47], belongs to Group Ⅱe [43]. Additionally, FvWRKY48 that directly binds to the promoter of FvPLA and activates its expression, resulting in pectin degradation and wild strawberry fruit softening, is classified within Group Ⅱc [14]. MdWRKY31 belongs to Group Ⅱb [4, 22], and forms a complex with MdNAC7, and the transcriptional repression of MdXTH2 by MdWRKY31 is decreased, which ultimately regulates apple fruit softening [4]. Interestingly, a WRKY family member in kiwifruit, AcWRKY40, belonging to Group Ⅱa, is involved in fruit postharvest ripening by directly binding to the promoters of the ethylene biosynthesis genes AcSAM2, AcACS1, and AcACS2 [25, 40]. Thus, these results may suggest that the WRKY family members involved in the regulation of fruit ripening and softening belong to the Group Ⅱ. And the different regulatory mechanisms may be due to their classification into distinct subgroups within the Group Ⅱ. Definitive conclusions require more research for confirmation.

Subcellular localization in stably transformed nucleus-localized signal tobacco showed that PpWRKY13 was localized to the nucleus, which is consistent with prior research [48]. The PpWRKY13 assay of subcellular localization was in line with its predicted function as a TF. Thus, uncovering PpWRKY13’s target gene is critical for deciphering its role in peach fruit softening.

DAP-seq is an effective technique for identifying genome-wide binding sites of TFs [49], and it has been obtained the target genes of TFs in various species. The flavonoid biosynthetic gene CsF3’H has been identified by DAP-seq as the target gene of TF CsMYB60 in cucumber [33]. Moreover, DAP-seq revealed that PpNAC1 can directly bind to the promoters of multiple ripening-related genes in peach [50]. In the present study, we identified a XTH gene, PpXTH33, using DAP-seq. Moreover, a unique DNA-binding site W-box is present in the promoter of PpXTH33, which suggested that the PpXTH33 may be the target gene of PpWRKY13. The softening of fruit results from the collective activity of an array of enzymes that modify the cell wall [2–4]. XTH is classified as a subgroup within the glycoside hydrolase family 16, which contains both the Glyco_hydro_16 and XET_C conserved domains [51–53]. XTHs play crucial roles in fruit softening, by breaking down xyloglucan and loosening the cell wall, preparing it for subsequent modification by other cell wall-associated enzymes [54]. Overexpression and silencing of the MdXTH2 gene in tomato and apple, respectively, lead to upregulation and downregulation of cell wall modification-related gene expression, respectively, indicating that the MdXTH2 gene may be involved in the fruit softening process [4, 55]. The expression levels of DkXTH2 and DkXTH3 increase during the ripening of persimmon fruit, which is accompanied by a decrease in firmness [56]. In the present study, the PpXTH33 transcriptome data showed an upward trend during the storage (Fig. 4c), and the expression of PpXTH33 was upregulated as the storage period increased in melting peach fruit according to the previous study [30], suggesting that PpXTH33 may participate in the fruit softening process through cell wall modification. Additionally, a luciferase assay and transient overexpression suggested that PpXTH33 may be the strong candidate gene regulated by PpWRKY13. Thus, PpWRKY13 may contribute to fruit softening by regulating a cell wall degradation gene PpXTH33. WRKY family members are involved in fruit softening [4, 10, 14, 25, 47], a process that may also involve other genes. Generally, fruit softening is regulated by ethylene [3], and numerous studies have shown that WRKY TFs regulate the transcription of target genes through interactions with other proteins [4, 10, 19]. Therefore, elucidating WRKY TFs specific roles and their associated genes in peach fruit softening requires further investigation.

Conclusion

PpWRKY13 may activate the transcription of PpXTH33 and function as a putative modulator of peach fruit softening. This discovery provides evidence for the involvement of WRKY TFs in the regulatory mechanisms of peach fruit softening. This provides new insights for extending the shelf lives of peach fruit.
