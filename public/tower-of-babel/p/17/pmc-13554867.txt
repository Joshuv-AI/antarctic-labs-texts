Rapidly Progressive Immune-Mediated Meningococcal Pericarditis Presenting as Cardiac Tamponade
Authors: Christine Robert M. Pagotan, Aryan Meknat, Anisha Ganesh, Neusha Hollingsworth, Masaki Tsukashita, M. Scott Halbreiner, Candice Lee, Walter McGregor, Stephen Bailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPericardial involvement in meningococcemia is uncommon and often immune complex mediated. Initial effusions may appear insignificant but can rapidly progress to cardiac tamponade, even as systemic infection markers improve.Case SummaryA 65-year-old man presented with septic shock secondary to Neisseria meningitidis bacteremia. Initial imaging demonstrated a small pericardial effusion without clinical manifestations. Despite antibiotics and improving systemic markers, he developed sudden hemodynamic deterioration because of rapid effusion progression. Repeat echocardiography revealed tamponade physiology, necessitating emergent subxiphoid tube pericardiostomy. He was successfully stabilized through prompt intervention despite vasopressor dependence.DiscussionThis case illustrates a paradoxical clinical trajectory in immune complex–mediated meningococcal pericarditis, in which tamponade develops after bacteremic control. This dissociation between infectious and pericardial trajectories underscores the importance of serial imaging and proactive surgical planning independent of laboratory trends.Take-Home MessagesIn meningococcal infection, hemodynamic instability despite improving infection markers should prompt urgent echocardiographic reassessment. Early recognition and timely drainage may be lifesaving.

Meningococcemia is associated with high morbidity and mortality, primarily because of septic shock and multiorgan failure. Cardiac involvement is rare but is well described, most commonly presenting as immune complex–mediated pericarditis that occurs days after bacteremia onset. Unlike purulent pericarditis, this inflammatory process can lead to rapid pericardial fluid accumulation accompanied by minimal early clinical warning signs. What distinguishes the present case is the discordant trajectory in which cardiac tamponade develops despite concurrent improvement in septic markers. This pattern is particularly hazardous because reassuring laboratory trends may divert clinical attention away from evolving obstructive physiology, delaying recognition of tamponade until hemodynamic collapse occurs. This case therefore highlights the necessity of continued echocardiographic surveillance independent of infectious marker trends in patients with meningococcemia and known pericardial involvement. History of Presentation

A 65-year-old man presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain, chills, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. The patient reported waking up at approximately 3 AM with chills and gastrointestinal symptoms, followed later in the day by sharp, pleuritic, substernal chest pain without radiation. He denied dyspnea, syncope, cough, hemoptysis, or urinary symptoms. He additionally endorsed acute left knee pain with difficulty ambulating but denied swelling, erythema, warmth, or trauma. He had recently traveled internationally to Portugal, Austria, Slovakia, and Hawaii.

On presentation, he appeared acutely ill and in moderate distress. Vital signs were notable for fever (rectal temperature: 39.1 °C/102.3 °F), hypotension (79/56 mm Hg), and tachycardia. Physical examination demonstrated tachycardia and mild jugular distention without clear evidence of cardiac tamponade.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of coronary artery disease status post percutaneous coronary intervention, prior non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, heart failure with recovered ejection fraction (50%-55%), hypertension, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for this presentation included septic shock due to bacterial infection, infectious or inflammatory pericarditis, viral myocarditis, and immune-mediated pericarditis secondary to systemic infection.

Investigations

Laboratory results revealed leukocytosis, lactic acidosis (4.4 mmol/L), acute kidney injury (creatinine: 1.44 mg/dL), elevated procalcitonin (37 ng/mL), thrombocytopenia, hypomagnesemia, and elevated inflammatory markers. High-sensitivity troponin was mildly elevated and rising, consistent with demand ischemia. Viral studies were negative. Blood cultures grew Neisseria meningitidis.

Computed tomography of the chest revealed a small pericardial effusion and nonspecific subcarinal lymphadenopathy (Figure 1A). Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia without ischemic changes. Initial transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a small circumferential pericardial effusion without tamponade physiology (Figure 1B and 1C).

Management

The patient was treated with aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support for septic shock. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated and subsequently narrowed to intravenous ceftriaxone after blood cultures confirmed N. meningitidis.

Despite improvement in inflammatory markers, the patient developed worsening hypotension and increasing vasopressor requirements (Figures 1D and 1E), prompting early cardiothoracic surgical consultation before confirmatory imaging. Repeat transthoracic echocardiography then demonstrated rapid enlargement of the pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology.

Given the degree of hemodynamic instability, concern for rapidly reaccumulating inflammatory effusion, and the possibility of purulent or complex fluid not amenable to percutaneous drainage, the decision was made to proceed emergently to the operating room for subxiphoid tube pericardiostomy. Due to profound hypotension and high vasopressor requirements, the patient was unable to tolerate induction of general anesthesia. Therefore, with bag-mask ventilation provided by the anesthesia team, local anesthesia was administered and a subxiphoid approach was initiated. Upon entry into the pericardial space, a large volume of inflammatory effusion was evacuated under controlled decompression. The patient demonstrated immediate hemodynamic improvement, allowing for safe induction of anesthesia and endotracheal intubation.

A pericardial drain was placed for continued postoperative decompression and monitoring. Given the presence of large bilateral pleural effusions, bilateral tube thoracostomies were also performed. The patient subsequently demonstrated sustained improvement in blood pressure with decreasing vasopressor requirements following decompression.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperatively, the patient required mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support for hemodynamic stabilization but was successfully extubated. His course was complicated by bilateral pleural effusions, requiring prolonged chest tube drainage, and acute kidney injury, which improved to near baseline with supportive care and nephrology consultation. Volume overload was managed with diuresis.

Atrial fibrillation was managed with amiodarone, with successful conversion to and maintenance of sinus rhythm. Systemic anticoagulation was deferred during this acute phase. The patient was initiated on colchicine for ongoing management of pericarditis before discharge.

He demonstrated gradual clinical improvement and was discharged home in stable condition, with outpatient follow-up arranged with cardiology, infectious diseases, and primary care.

Discussion

Pericardial involvement in meningococcal infection is rare but well described, most commonly presenting as immune complex–mediated pericarditis that occurs days after bacteremia onset. Three clinical forms have been reported: primary meningococcal pericarditis, reactive pericarditis in disseminated disease, and delayed immune complex–mediated pericarditis.1, 2, 3,4, 5, 6 Although these presentations differ in timing and pathophysiology, all carry the potential for rapid pericardial effusion progression and cardiac tamponade. The present case highlights a clinically important divergent pattern in which cardiac tamponade develops despite apparent improvement in septic markers.

Immune complex deposition within the pericardium with complement activation has been demonstrated in inflammatory pericardial disease and may drive intense local inflammation and capillary leak, leading to rapid pericardial fluid accumulation, even after systemic infection is controlled.7,8 This process can persist despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, suggesting ongoing immune-mediated pericardial inflammation independent of active infection and contributing to rapid reaccumulation of inflammatory effusions.

Multiple case reports have emphasized that early imaging may demonstrate only a small pericardial effusion before abrupt clinical deterioration and the development of tamponade physiology.1, 2, 3,9 This case reinforces that fulminant progression may occur even as systemic infection appears to improve, highlighting a key diagnostic pitfall in which effusion size alone may not reliably predict hemodynamic risk and reassuring laboratory trends may obscure evolving obstructive shock.

Contemporary pericardial disease guidelines emphasize that clinical trajectory and hemodynamic status should guide management more than effusion size alone.10 In patients with hemodynamic instability, prompt recognition of tamponade and emergent pericardial drainage are essential regardless of etiology. In this context, procedural decision-making must account for both the dynamic nature of inflammatory effusions and the patient's physiologic reserve.

In unstable patients with suspected inflammatory or complex pericardial effusions, surgical drainage may be favored over percutaneous approaches because of the need for definitive evacuation, continuous decompression, and improved source control. This case further illustrates that in the setting of profound shock, initial decompression under local anesthesia may be required to achieve hemodynamic stabilization before safe induction of general anesthesia. The subxiphoid pericardiostomy provides several advantages in this case, including direct visualization, evacuation of loculated or fibrinous fluid, and placement of a durable drainage catheter to reduce recurrence.

This case illustrates several key clinical lessons. Small pericardial effusions in meningococcemia should not automatically be considered benign, particularly in patients with persistent or worsening shock physiology.1, 2, 3,10 In such cases, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and prompt evaluation for obstructive physiology. Proactive intervention and serial cardiac imaging should be pursued to prevent the sudden progression of fatal cardiac tamponade.

Early cardiothoracic surgical consultation and a low threshold for definitive drainage may be lifesaving, particularly when hemodynamic deterioration occurs out of proportion to, or despite improvement in, systemic infection.

Conclusions

Immune complex–mediated pericardial involvement is a rare but potentially fatal complication of N meningitidis infection. Rapid progression from a small pericardial effusion to cardiac tamponade may occur despite resolution trends in systemic infection markers.

An incongruent persistence of shock in meningococcemia should prompt early reassessment for obstructive physiology, including repeat cardiac imaging to evaluate for evolving tamponade. Early cardiothoracic surgical consultation and timely pericardial drainage are critical to preventing catastrophic hemodynamic deterioration.
