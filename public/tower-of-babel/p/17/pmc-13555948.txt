Navigating Advanced Illness Goals And Treatment with Digital Engagement (NAVIGATE): a protocol for a randomised controlled trial of a website for caregivers of patients with serious illness
Authors: Vinh Anh Huynh, Gerald Choon Huat Koh, Mervyn Jun Rui Lim, Tau Ming Liew, Vincent Diong Weng Nga, Tseng Tsai Yeo, Noreen Chan, Jia Neng Tan, Foong Pin Sym
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurrogate decision-makers (SDMs) of patients with serious illnesses (PSI) face significant challenges in making end-of-life decisions, including feeling unprepared and uncertain due to lack of clarity on what patients and they themselves value. We developed a digital SDM-focused intervention (Careverse) that provides educational materials and tools for SDMs to initiate and discuss values conversation with patients and clinicians. The aims of this study are (i) to test the impact of Careverse on SDMs self-reported engagement in care planning behaviours and (ii) to evaluate whether Careverse increases caregivers’ and patients’ self-reported satisfaction with care and improves patient outcomes.MethodsThis is a two-armed, parallel-design, single-blinded, participant-level randomised controlled trial of 100 identified decision-makers for patients with serious illness, along with their patients in a public university hospital in Singapore. Participants are English-speaking adult SDMs (21 years and older) of PSIs, including brain tumours, spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage, and late-stage chronic kidney disease. Physicians in charge must assess that the patient is an appropriate candidate for advance care planning (ACP). Eligible caregivers of these patients with serious illness are then assigned to the intervention or control group using randomisation stratified by patients’ conditions. We will assess SDM outcomes (engagement in care planning behaviours as a surrogate decision maker within 6 weeks—primary outcome; satisfaction with care; goals of care congruence, wellbeing) and patient outcomes (healthcare utilisation at end-of-life).DiscussionThis trial will provide much-needed empirical evidence about a SDM-focused digital care planning intervention to empower caregivers to engage more effectively in care planning behaviours and be better prepared for their SDM role. This trial will also test the effect of Careverse on improving both SDM’s- and patient-centred outcomes, and inform clinicians, payers and health systems seeking to adopt and scale effective and efficient digital strategies for supporting SDMs in real-world serious illness care settings.Trial registrationClinicalTrial.gov NCT07134881. Introduction

Delivering quality patient-centred end-of-life (EOL) care is increasingly prioritised in healthcare systems. The rising demand from ageing populations and a growing burden of chronic conditions necessitate the shift towards holistic, person-centred models that emphasise improving quality of life, symptom relief, and respecting patient preferences throughout the end-of-life journey [1–3]. In advanced stages of illnesses where curative options are no longer available, treatment decisions are increasingly driven by patient’s values and preferences. Evaluating care options and the accompanying tradeoffs, especially in EOL situations, requires the patient and family to consider and communicate their values among themselves and with the healthcare providers. Values elicitation processes encourage patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers to engage in discussions to clarify the goals and values that guide treatment decisions and to ensure that medical care aligns with the patient’s personal beliefs, values, and preferences, especially in situations where the patient loses the capacity to make decisions. As such, care planning conversations, variously labelled goal-centred care or advance care planning (ACP), have been recognised as a key component in delivering high-quality, patient-centric end-of-life care.

While care planning helps to empower and involve patients in the treatment decision making, there is a lack of support for caregivers to effectively serve as a surrogate decision maker (SDM). When patients cannot or will not participate in the decision-making process, which could be due to temporary or permanent loss of decision-making capacity, the burden of making treatment decisions often falls onto caregivers as SDM to advocate and make decisions on the patients’ behalf. SDMs are estimated to be involved in decision-making for 50% of hospitalisations and increasingly so toward the patient’s end of life [4, 5]. Asian studies suggest that caregivers are more likely than their Western counterparts to be involved in decision-making for their seriously ill patients, with a notably higher occurrence of patients leaving all decision-making to their caregivers [6, 7]. SDMs face unique challenges, such as significant emotional and practical demands balancing the patient’s preferences, clinical recommendations, and the family’s sometimes conflicting values [8–10]. Qualitative research has shown that while most SDMs strive to honor the patients’ values—what the patients deem as most important, conducting the actual elicitation conversations remains challenging [11, 12]. When surrogates lack preparation and involvement in values conversations, they often do not fully understand the patient’s treatment values, leading to less congruent decisions and worse bereavement outcomes [13, 14].

Current implementation of ACP and EOL conversations does not adequately help support and involve caregivers in the care planning process and prepare them to serve as SDMs. Existing care planning research has focused on patient-centric interventions that culminate in formal documentations of ACP [15–18], despite evidence indicating that both patients and caregivers preferences constantly change over the course of the disease and caregiving journey [19]. It is recommended that patients and surrogates go beyond ACP documentation and towards serious illness conversations (SICs) and preparation, which entails deeper and more holistic understanding the patient’s values and preferences [17]. Without these frequent conversations and updates, the current process may undermine providing goal-concordant care for patients. Furthermore, current interventions are often designed for use with facilitators [20], who face significant barriers in facilitating such conversations, most notably time and resource constraints [11]. Last, most interventions were designed to be patient-focused, which may not directly translate to caregivers being more prepared for SDM role [21, 22]. Interventions that were dyad-focused also do not adequately incorporate caregivers’ specific needs and challenges [18, 23–26]. Consequently, many designated SDMs feel under-prepared to take up their role [27].

Bakke et. al. have suggested five areas where experienced SDMs have asked for greater support: (1) better and earlier preparation for and understanding of ACP, (2) self-efficacy to initiate ACP conversations with patients, (3) learning about and incorporating patient’s values and preferences into decision-making, (4) support SDMs in communicating with clinicians and advocating choices, and (5) making informed surrogate decisions [27]. Multiple studies suggest that open conversations between SDMs and patients about future care preferences could help in facilitating consensus [28, 29].

Therefore, to help SDMs be better prepared and ready to make crucial in-the-moment decisions, we developed Careverse, a novel website addressing caregivers’ needs, informed by previous studies on caregiver needs, decision-making frameworks, and existing intervention [30]. The website aims to promote iterative, high-quality conversations between caregivers, patients and clinicians to determine what matters most to the patient and align care decisions accordingly. This intervention focuses on supporting the SDMs, while being complementary with existing patient-doctor interventions. One key strength of a digital tool over traditional ACP processes is the enhanced accessibility and user-friendly design, which may also allow and encourage caregivers and patients to easily revisit and update their values to better reflect their medical preferences [31].

The objectives of the study are (i) to determine the effectiveness of Careverse in increasing ACP engagement among caregivers of seriously ill patients from baseline to 6 weeks post-randomisation (primary outcome) using a randomised controlled trial design, and (ii) to evaluate the impact of Careverse on caregivers’ actual ACP behavioural outcomes—self-reported SICs, satisfaction with care, relationship with patients, goals of care congruency, quality of life, utilisation of aggressive care, overall health care costs, and bereavement outcomes among caregivers of decedents.

Materials and methods

Study design and schedule

The trial is a prospective multi-site, two-arm, single-blinded participant-level randomised trial comparing a digital intervention to usual care for caregivers of patients with serious illness (PSI). The trial schedule is as follows. Potential patient participants are screened and identified based on reviews of administrative lists and clinic schedules in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria detailed in following the “Study setting and participants” section. Consent and recruitment then follow as described in the the “Recruitment and informed consent” section. Upon consent, patient participants complete the baseline survey, which collects socio-demographic characteristics and patient outcomes. Patients in both intervention and comparator groups will receive usual care, i.e. there is no different treatment exposure between arms as the digital intervention will only be administered to the caregivers. Patients are followed-up at three subsequent time points—1-week (T1), 2-week (T2), and 3-month (T3) post enrolment. Data collection will be conducted in-person (during scheduled clinic or home visits) or via phone by the trained research coordinators (RCs). At T2 and T3, RCs will also retrieve patients’ health care utilisation from the electronic health record (EHR). The study design and schedule are presented in Fig. 1. Fig. 1Study design and schedule

Study design and schedule

At baseline, consented patient participants also identify their caregivers who can make medical decisions on their behalf. Caregivers are then approached for recruitment either in-person during the patient’s clinical visit or by telephone. The option for caregivers to provide remote informed consent is designed to maximise participation for caregivers who may not be present when the patient is initially approached. Caregiver participants follow a similar and concurrent study schedule to patient participants (Fig. 1).

For caregiver participants, upon consent and completion of the baseline survey, they are randomised into either the comparator or intervention arm (Careverse) via the randomise function on the survey platform, stratified by the patients’ illness conditions. Caregiver participants are presented with links that direct them to either a digital ACP booklet (comparator arm) or the intervention website (intervention arm). Caregiver participants allocated to the intervention arm will be guided by the present RCs to create an account and log into the website. RCs will ensure that participants have successfully registered and accessed the website for the participant to be considered onboarded to the study. There is no registration required for a digital booklet provided to participants in the comparator arm. After successful onboarding for both arms, participants are encouraged to view and use the tools provided in their own time.

Similar to patient participants, caregiver participants are followed-up at three subsequent time points—1-week, 6-week, and 6-month post enrolment (Fig. 1). At each time point, individualised survey links will be sent to caregiver participants via their registered email address for data collection. RCs will conduct up to two (2) reminder phone calls to caregivers who have not completed the surveys. RCs will regularly review the EHR to identify caregivers whose patient passes away after signup to administer the appropriate instruments, i.e. bereavement questionnaire instead of ACP engagement, in subsequent follow-ups.

Study setting and participants

The trial is conducted at the National University Hospital (NUH), one of the three largest university hospitals in Singapore, operating as both a tertiary referral institution and academic medical centre. With more than 1200 beds and 50 specialties across medical, surgical and dental, NUH serves more than one million patients annually.

The ACP framework in Singapore is adapted from the Respecting Choices model based in the United States and called Living Matters [32]. Singapore’s Ministry of Health commissioned the Agency of Integrated Care to coordinate health initiatives for older adults, including ACP processes. Attending family physicians can help to document any care preferences and decisions that their patients may express and share these with the family or other clinicians helping in the medical care of the patient, if and when the need arises. For patients with more complex conditions, ACP discussions may need to be referred to the patients by the physicians and subsequently facilitated by a trained healthcare professionals, who are currently available in most Singapore public hospitals. Despite efforts and initiatives to increase ACP awareness, uptake remains low [33, 34]. Furthermore, current ACP processes in Singapore remain patient-centric and often do not actively include caregivers, despite their central role in decision-making [35]. While patients are encouraged to complete their ACP processes with their family members, patients are hesitant to begin the ACP due to various barriers, and thus do not have a standard concurrent ACP process [11, 36].

Recruitment and informed consent

We have created a practical and systematic approach to recruitment, with the goal of minimising administrative costs, disruption to clinical activities, and selection bias. Potential patient and caregiver participants are identified from hospital electronic medical records by study team members based on the eligibility criteria. The RCs will then confirm with the treating physician that it would be appropriate for the patients to receive ACP. If the ACP is deemed appropriate, RCs will then approach eligible patients in-person at the clinics on the day of their appointment to assess their eligibility and interest in participating in the study. If a patient expressed interest and could identify a caregiver responsible for medical-decision making, the study was further explained, and informed consent was obtained. RCs then administer the AMT to determine the patient’s cognitive status, using the standard AMT guidelines that account for the patient’s age and years of education, before obtaining consent and enrolling the eligible patient in the study [42].

RCs then contact the caregivers either in-person during the patient’s clinical visit or by phone to assess eligibility, explain the study, and obtain informed consent. A caregiver of an eligible patient can be recruited and enrolled for the study even if the patient does not consent to participating in the study. Study activities will begin only after consent taking.

In both arms, all participants are required to complete four surveys: at baseline (T0), 1-week (T1), 2-month (T2) and 6-month (T3) post randomisation. Patient participants receive Singapore Dollar (SGD) 30 upon completion of the baseline survey; SGD40 upon the completion of both the first (T1) and second (T2) follow-up surveys; and SGD15 upon the completion of the final (T3) follow-up survey, to a total of SGD85 (~USD66). Caregiver participants receive SGD30 upon completion of the baseline survey and successful signup to the allocated treatment (T0); SGD50 upon the completion of both the first (T1) and second (T2) follow-up surveys; and SGD20 upon the completion of the final (T3) follow-up survey, to a total of SGD100 (~ USD78). All reimbursements will be done via cash or cashless payment based on participants’ preference.

Randomisation and blinding

This study is a single-blind, participant-level randomised trial. Upon providing consent and completion of the baseline survey, enrolled caregiver participants will be randomly assigned 1:1 to the experimental and comparator arms using the built-in randomise function in the online survey software (Qualtrics). Balance between the allocation arms will be achieved by stratifying based on illness. Treatment allocation will be masked from participants for the entirety of the study. We do not anticipate unblinding of participants to treatment status. Effort to maintain blinding of participants includes training our RCs to strictly follow a protocol and script in all interactions with participants, e.g. during initial signups, onboarding and enrolment to the website and during follow-up calls for reminders and/or data collection. This blinding procedure also minimises the risk of selection bias from the RCs. RCs are blinded to treatment allocation during recruitment, consent-taking and baseline survey process. Once participants are randomised into their allocated arm, RCs will be aware of the participant’s treatment allocation as RCs will need to ensure that participants successfully complete the signup process if the participants are allocated to the intervention arm (Careverse website). We expect minimal and non-differential observer bias between treatment arms due to this unmasking of RCs as follow-ups data collections for caregivers participants are completed via self-administered online survey without the RCs being present. Lastly, RCs will also strictly follow a protocol script during follow-up reminder calls to caregivers and data collection calls to patients to minimise potential observer bias and risks of unmasking the participants.

Intervention and comparator

Careverse

Careverse is a digital psychoeducational website designed to assist caregivers and patients to clarify their values, end-of-life preferences, enable surrogates to further their understanding of the patient’s wishes, and prepare surrogates for the role and responsibilities of a medical decision maker [30]. To address challenges with motivation [11, 43], we placed values elicitation in the context of decision-making, followed by interactive tools that provide opportunities for practice and customisation. We structured the content for linear reading beginning at the homepage. However, since caregivers may come to the site at different points in their journey and with varying needs, Careverse components are designed to be modular and can be used in any order.

Careverse was iteratively designed [44] to address the key needs identified in Bakke et al.’s list of SDM challenges [27]. The website contains articles and tools to help direct and guide caregivers along the caregiving journey in their interactions with patients and clinicians [9, 44]. Educational materials include curated articles, offering both foundational knowledge (e.g., caregiver roles, communication tips, decision points) and step-by-step support for navigating surrogate decision-making. These weekly emails serve as a guide and reminders for using the Careverse websites as well as highlighting the different tools available to the participants.

Comparator—usual care

Recognising that there is no standard of care ACP programs for caregivers, we use The ACP Workbook as the comparator for the Careverse digital intervention. The ACP Workbook is a resource booklet created by the Agency of Integrated Care (AIC) to help individuals explore their values, care preferences, and end-of-life goals in a structured, reflective way. To ensure blinding, caregivers in the comparator arm will receive a digital version of standard advanced care planning education material prepared by AIC upon randomisation [45].

For patient participants in both arms, usual care is unaffected by the study design. The study does not alter any of the usual care and processes in place as prescribed by the clinical care team. Instead, we only conduct observational measurements of patient participants over the course of the study.

Outcome measures

SDM measures

ACP surrogate decision making (primary outcome) and behaviours

The primary outcome is caregiver’s engagement in ACP as a surrogate decision maker, measured using the 17-item ACP Surrogate Decision Making Instrument (ACP-17 SDM) developed by Sudore et al. [46]. The ACP-17 SDM is a validated questionnaire designed to assess how engaged SDMs are in the advance care planning process for patients who may not be able to make their own medical decisions [47, 48]. The instrument is adapted from the longer, patient-focused ACP Engagement Survey and allows for parallel assessment of patients and their SDMs. It has demonstrated strong reliability and internal consistency in studies. The ACP-17 SDM measures engagement across three domains: (1) Serving as SDM (7 items), which includes measurements of SDM’s knowledge, contemplation, and self-efficacy regarding acting as a surrogate decision-maker on the patient’s behalf, (2) Contemplation (4 items), which includes measures of the extent to which the SDM has thought about end-of-life care issues for the patient, and (3) Readiness (6 items), which measures how prepared the SDM feels to engage in ACP activities. Each item is answered on a 5-point Likert scale (1—not at all/never to 5—extremely/A lot), and higher total scores indicate higher engagement in ACP by the surrogate. We also ask participants to report actual ACP engagement behaviours using a modified version of the readiness items. The full instrument used for the primary outcome is shown in Appendix Table 1.

Satisfaction with care

We measure patients and caregivers’ satisfaction with care that they (or their patient) received using the Short Assessment of Patient Satisfaction (SAPS), a brief, validated, and generic 7-item questionnaire designed to efficiently measure patient satisfaction across a range of health care settings [49–51]. Each question is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 0 to 4), and the items cover key domains of patient satisfaction including: (1) Satisfaction with treatment, (2) Explanation of treatment results, (3) Clinician care and thoroughness, (4) Involvement in decision-making, (5) Respect received from the clinician, (6) Adequacy of time with the clinician, and (7) Overall satisfaction with care received. Total scores range from 0 (extremely dissatisfied) to 28 (extremely satisfied).

We also measure how well patients and caregivers feel heard and understood by those caring for them in the hospital environment using the following point-of-care item: “Over the past 1(6) months, how much have you felt heard and understood by the doctors, nurses and hospital staff?”. Response is rated on a 5-point scale (completely, quite a bit, moderately, slightly, not at all) [52].

We hypothesise that the intervention improves participants’ satisfaction with care by facilitating discussions (and potentially alignment) between caregivers and patients regarding their preferred type of care and enhancing their ability to communicate these preferences effectively to healthcare providers. Together, these mechanisms increase the likelihood that delivered care reflects the goals of both patients and caregivers, thereby improving satisfaction with care.

Caregivers wellbeing

We assess caregiver’s quality of life using the Singapore Caregivers Quality of Life Scale (SCQOLS), a validated instrument developed specifically to assess the quality of life (QoL) of family caregivers of patients with advanced illnesses in Singapore [53, 54]. The 10-item SCQOLs measures five domains of quality of life of family caregivers of patients: (1) Physical Well-being (2 items); (2) Mental Well-being (2 items); (3) Experience & Meaning (2 items); (4) Impact on Daily Life (2 items); (5) Financial Well-being (2 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point scale (0—Not at All to 4—Very Much), with higher scores indicating better quality of life.

We also measure symptoms of depression and anxiety among the caregivers using the 4-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4), an ultra-brief, validated self-report instrument designed to screen for symptoms of both depression and anxiety in clinical and non-clinical populations [55–58]. The PHQ-4 has four items: two for depression (the PHQ-2 subscale: “little interest or pleasure in doing things”; “feeling down, depressed, or hopeless”) and two for anxiety (the GAD-2 subscale: “feeling nervous, anxious or on edge”; “not being able to stop or control worrying”). Each item is rated based on symptom frequency over the past two weeks using a 4-point Likert scale (0—not at all to 3—nearly every day). Scores for each subscale range from 0 to 6, with a subscale score of 3 or greater suggests possible anxiety (GAD-2) or depression (PHQ-2), while severity of total distress is categorised as: normal (0–2), mild (3–5), moderate (6–8), and severe (9–12) [55]. We hypothesise that the intervention will improve participants’ mental wellbeing as caregivers feel more prepared and certain in their roles from the educational components and conversation tools of the intervention.

Goals of care

We assess participants’ preferences for aggressive care using two goals of care questions adapted from the CanCORS Study [59, 60]. In the first question, caregiver participants are asked to trade-off between life-extension treatments or those that minimise pain/discomfort (“If you had to recommend a choice now, would you recommend treatment that extends life as much as possible, or would you recommend a treatment that gives minimal pain and discomfort for the patient?”). In the second question, caregiver participants are asked to trade-off between life-extension treatments or those that were less costly (“If you had to recommend a choice now, would you recommend treatment that extends life as much as possible, or would you recommend a treatment that costs less”). For both questions, participants respond on a scale of 1 to 9, where 1 represents maximal life extension with severe pain or discomfort (or with higher costs for the second question), 5 represents moderate life extension and moderate pain or discomfort (or moderate cost for the second question), and 9 indicates no life extension but minimal pain or discomfort (or lower costs for the second question). Scores 1 to 4 are categorised as prioritising life extension, and scores 6 to 9 are categorised as prioritising symptom management or cost containment. Prior work has used this measure to investigate dyad goal concordance and healthcare utilisation and cost at end-of-life [61, 62].

Dyadic relationship

We assess the caregiving relationship between the caregiver and patient using the Dyadic Relationship Scale (DRS), a validated instrument measuring negative dyadic strain and positive dyadic interaction [63]. The caregiver version includes 11 items and are rated on a four-point scale (1—strongly disagree to 4—strongly agree). Higher scores on each of these scales indicate higher levels of strain and positive interaction, respectively.

Patient outcomes

ACP engagement

The main patients’ outcome is the change in patient-reported engagement in ACP, measured using the 4-item ACP Engagement Survey [64, 65]. The 4-item instrument measure patients’ readiness to appoint a health care proxy, discuss goals of care with their health care proxy, talk to their healthcare providers about the kind of medical care they would want if they were very sick or near the end of life, and sign official documents to put their wishes in writing. Patients respond on a 5-point Likert scale, including (1) “I have never thought about it”, (2) “I have thought about it, but I am not ready to do it”, (3) “I am thinking about doing it in the next 6 months”, (4) “I am definitely planning to do it in the next 30 days”, and (5) “I have already done it”. Those who answer that they have done it were further asked to clarify when they have done so.

Satisfaction with care and goals of care

Similar to caregivers, we measure patients’ satisfaction with care using the 7-item SAPS instrument and the “Heard and Understood” question. Patients are also asked about their preferences for aggressive care using goals of care based on the two trade-off questions: (1) between life-extension treatments or those that minimise pain/discomfort and (2) between life-extension treatments or those that were less costly. The questions were modified to “If you had to make a choice now, would you choose a treatment that extends life as much as possible, or would you choose a treatment that gives minimal pain and discomfort (or: that costs less) for yourself?”.

Patients wellbeing

We assess patient’s quality of life using the EQ-5D-5L instrument, a simple, and standardised measure of health status. The EQ-5D-5L descriptive system comprises five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression), on five response levels: no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems, unable to/extreme problems. The overall health states can be converted to an index value using a value set [66].

Healthcare utilisation

We will extract and compile data from patients’ hospital EHR to evaluate their utilisation of aggressive interventions during the 6 months before and after enrolment, as well as within the 12 months preceding death. Based on review of previous literature and physicians’ opinions, we assess the following aggressive measure—emergency department (ED) visits, admission to Intensive Care Unit (ICU) or high dependency (HD) ward, use of any anti-cancer treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal or targeted treatment) for brain metastases and tumour patients, and life-sustaining procedure (e.g. cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), intubation), transplant and emergency hemodialysis for CKD patients [67–70]. We will analyse the dichotomised outcome (Yes vs No aggressive treatment), the total count number of aggressive interventions received by each patient, and total healthcare costs. Other measures

We collect data regarding SDMs’ socio-demographic characteristics, co-residency with patients, and dyadic relationship [63]. We assess SDMs’ financial difficulties by asking them about the adequacy of their finances in (1) covering the cost of treatment, (2) meeting daily needs, and (3) affording small luxuries. A total score was calculated by summing the response options (range, 3–9; higher scores denoted greater financial difficulty) [71]. As our intervention is digital and web-based, we also assess SDMs’ self-reported comfort with using digital devices and monitor participants’ website activities, including page views and tools usage. Intervention caregiver participants are required to log in using their account to access the tools. SDMs of decedents respond to Decision Regret Scale regarding use of end-of-life treatments [72] and Brief Grief Questionnaire [73].

Data analyses

Data quality assurance

Survey interviews will be monitored for quality control on a regular basis. The online Qualtrics surveys are programmed with logical checks and appropriate allowed input range (for numerical free text entry questions) for consistency and quality control. For interviewer-administered surveys, comprehensive training and detailed protocols will be provided to the RCs to ensure that the data obtained from this research is accurate, complete and reliable.

We will record and report all reasons for study drop-out using a withdrawal/termination form to assess the missing data mechanism (missing completely at random, missing at random, or non-ignorable missingness, meaning the data missingness is related to the actual value). Missing data can arise under the following scenarios: when participants withdraw from the study before completion and when they do not complete a survey questionnaire. Possible reasons for study withdrawal or incomplete responses include (1) Participant (patient/caregiver) is uncomfortable answering the survey, (2) Participant (patient/caregiver) is experiencing stress and does not have the energy to continue with study participation. Cause of missing data and attrition (attrition due to death/attrition due to illness/attrition at random) will be reported.

Sample size calculation

We selected our sample size to provide sufficient power to assess differences in the primary outcome—ACP-17 SDM. Based on our prior results from a pre-post single-arm pilot efficacy trial study, the anticipated effect size of the digital intervention on ACP-17 SDM is 0.70 [30]. However, we assumed a conservative and smaller effect size of 0.50. We assumed a 6-point increase with a standard deviation (SD) of 12 points in ACP-17 SDM score that would generate an effect size of this magnitude or greater.

Statistical and analytical plan

Data will first be summarised using summary statistics such as mean (SD) and median (minimum, maximum) values for continuous variables, and frequency (proportions) for categorical variables, by randomisation group as well as by site of recruitment. For preliminary analyses, two-sample t-test and Mann–Whitney test will be used to compare continuous variables between treatment groups and Fisher’s exact test or Chi-square test for categorical variables where appropriate. Results will be reported following the CONSORT guideline [74].

We will use a generalised linear mixed model with the appropriate link function and distribution to compare the primary and secondary outcomes across the two arms using an intention-to-treat approach. We will estimate models with and without controlling for covariates, including baseline engagement, health status and other socioeconomic characteristics. Mixed-effects models allow for inclusion of data from participants with missing data. Disease condition will be included as a random effect to evaluate and accommodate clustering effects. We will examine within-individual covariance to determine the appropriate variance–covariance structure to be used in the model. As robustness checks, we will also perform similar analytical approach controlling for intervention participants’ usage of the digital website and tools. For missing data, we will conduct sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation using M = 10 imputations, which assumes missing at random.

All analyses will be performed using STATA or R and results will be assessed on a 5% significance level with 95% confidence intervals.

Trial monitoring

A Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) has not been established for this trial. This decision was based on the low-risk nature of the intervention and the absence of planned interim analyses for efficacy or harm. In accordance with guidance from the SPIRIT Statement, a DMC is not considered necessary for trials where participant risk is minimal and ongoing safety concerns are unlikely.

No formal interim analyses for efficacy or effectiveness outcomes are planned for this randomised controlled trial. Interim monitoring will be restricted to trial conduct and feasibility indicators, including recruitment rates, adherence to the allocated intervention, participant retention, and the occurrence of adverse events. These indicators will be reviewed periodically by the study team to ensure the integrity and feasibility of trial procedures, rather than to inform early stopping decisions. No formal stopping rules based on statistical criteria have been specified and the trial will not be stopped early on the basis of interim outcome data. Premature termination will only occur if required by changes in regulatory requirements, institutional policies, or other external mandates.

Adverse events will be recorded, monitored, and reported in accordance with protocol-defined safety procedures. However, given the low-risk nature of the intervention, we do not anticipate serious adverse events leading to early trial termination, and no predefined stopping boundaries for safety outcomes have been established.

The study management team serves as the coordinating centre and is responsible for the overall day-to-day management and operational delivery of the study. The team comprises a multidisciplinary group including researchers, data analysts, RCs, and case managers. This team will oversee participant recruitment, data collection, data management, protocol adherence, and regulatory compliance across all study sites. RCs and case managers will support site implementation and communication, while data analysts will be responsible for data processing, quality assurance, and preparation for analysis.

Day-to-day trial management will be supported through regular meetings. The core study team will meet on a biweekly basis to review recruitment progress, adherence to the intervention, retention rates, and operational issues. In addition, monthly meetings will be held involving the Principal Investigator (PI), site Principal Investigators (site-PIs), and senior researchers to provide broader oversight, review trial progress, and address strategic and methodological considerations. No separate steering committee was established due to the low-risk nature of the intervention. Oversight of trial conduct, data integrity, and participant safety will therefore be undertaken by the study management team. Executive decisions will be made by the PI in conjunction with the site-PIs, who are clinician-researchers responsible for the participating study sites.

Discussion

NAVIGATE is a single-blind, participant-level randomised trial that compares the effectiveness of a digital educational website versus a digital ACP booklet among family caregivers of patients with serious illnesses. By comparing the effect of these two interventions on family caregiver and patient outcomes, implementation costs and healthcare utilisation at end of life, this trial will provide robust data about whether a digital web-based intervention is effective in increasing caregivers’ engagement with ACP and being more prepared for SDM duties. The protocol is prepared in accordance with the SPIRIT guidance (Appendix Table 2).

Our trial addresses a major public health problem in providing high-quality end-of-life care for seriously ill patients and their family caregivers. Evidence suggests that ACP behaviours help patients to understand and communicate their personal values, life goals, and preferences for future medical care [22] receive care reflective of their values and preferences [75, 76] and decrease use of unwanted aggressive EOL treatments [77, 78]. Importantly, greater involvement of caregivers in serious illness conversations (SIC) can help to decrease burden on surrogate decision makers and bereaved family members [21]. When caregivers participate in advance care planning and EOL discussions, they are better informed about the patient’s wishes, leading to reduced decisional conflict [21, 22], lower emotional distress, and improved satisfaction and preparedness [79, 80]. Research has also shown that families are forced to make decisions, especially regarding life extending treatments, without a clear understanding of what the patients’ values are [32]. Consequently, caregivers’ opting for more aggressive EOL care is associated with worse bereavement outcomes in caregivers [79]. In Singapore, only a minority of patients engage in discussions about EOL wishes with their physicians, and documentation of care goals remains limited [81]. Developing a scalable solution that equips caregivers to act as SDMs can help ensure that care decisions are more consistently aligned with the values of both patients and caregivers.

Our intervention focuses on preparing SDMs for their role through illness education and promoting meaningful discussions about patient preferences and goals. This approach may lead to better goal-concordant and patient-centred care than simply traditional ACP practices, which often emphasise documentation via written advance directives (ADs) over conversation, have not reliably improved EOL care quality for many patients [29, 82]. We anticipate that innovative ACP approaches, such as using interactive web-based tools, decision aids, or guided conversations, will increase engagement, empowerment, and alignment of care with patient goals. Ultimately, the emphasis on equipping SDMs and clinicians with skills for person-centred communication, rather than just focusing on ACP documentation, is increasingly recognised as essential for high-quality, goal-concordant EOL care [17, 83].

The trial design incorporates several pragmatic components intended to maximise feasibility, implementation, and reach among caregivers of patients with serious illness. First, recruitment of seriously ill adults and their caregivers is conducted during routine clinical care encounters. This approach fosters a natural sense of urgency for patient–caregiver dyads to engage in SICs, while avoiding the disruption associated with acute care settings. With the exception of patients with SICH stroke, recruitment is diagnosis-based rather than event-based, e.g., following an emergency department visit or hospitalisation. This decision reflects recognition that patients immediately following acute health crises are often physically or cognitively unable to participate meaningfully in SICs, and such timing is unlikely to support high-quality engagement. Second, the design also ensures that recruitment contexts align with situations in which treating physicians are receptive to SICs and supportive of potential ACP discussions initiated by patients and caregivers. This alignment is intended to create opportunities for caregivers to feel empowered and better prepared to discuss patient values and to advocate for care plans consistent with those values. Last, a key barrier to integrating palliative approaches into health systems lies in the heterogeneity of clinical care pathways across conditions and illness trajectories. To address this, the intervention is delivered digitally, allowing caregivers and patients to engage with materials at home, initiate conversations independently, and subsequently integrate these discussions into clinical encounters. Embedding educational content directly into clinical workflows—delivered to caregivers at the time of ACP assessment by treating physicians—also reduces demands on clinical staff and resources. Through these design considerations, the intervention is hypothesised to improve self-reported ACP engagement and to positively influence key caregiver and patient outcomes with the broader aim is to integrate Careverse into the health system as standard of care for caregivers of patients with serious illness.
