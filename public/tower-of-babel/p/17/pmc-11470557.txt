Prognostic implications of obstructive sleep apnea in patients with unstable angina stratified by remnant cholesterol and triglyceride: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yun Zhou, Wei Gong, Yan Yan, Xiao Wang, Wen Zheng, Bin Que, Siyi Li, Zekun Zhang, Xiuhuan Chen, Jingyao Fan, Wenlong Zhao, Qingjie Xin, Hui Ai, Shaoping Nie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic significance of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in patients with unstable angina (UA) based on remnant cholesterol (RC) or triglyceride (TG) levels remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the effects of the interaction between RC, TG, and OSA on cardiovascular outcomes in UA patients.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, OSA was diagnosed when apnea-hypopnea index of ≥ 15 events/h. Patients with high RC (HRC, n = 370) or high TG (HTG, n = 362) included RC or TG in the highest tertile, and those in the middle and lowest tertiles were defined as normal RC (NRC, n = 736) or normal TG (NTG, n = 744). The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), including cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, ischemia stroke, ischemia-driven revascularization, or hospitalization for UA.ResultsA total of 1,106 eligible UA patients were enrolled, among which 560 (50.6%) had OSA. RC and TG levels were increased in OSA patients, but there was no difference in the prevalence of OSA between the NRC and HRC or NTG and HTG groups. During a median follow-up of 1.9 (1.1, 3.0) years, OSA was associated with an increased risk of MACCE occurrence compared to non-OSA in UA patients with HRC (adjusted HR 2.06; 95% CI 1.20–3.51, P = 0.008), but not in those with NRC (adjusted HR 1.21; 95% CI 0.84–1.75, P = 0.297). The incremental risk in HRC was attributable to higher rates of hospitalization for UA and ischemia-driven revascularization. Results for HTG and NTG were similar.ConclusionOSA was associated with a worse prognosis in UA patients with HRC or HTG, emphasizing the necessity of identifying OSA presence in this population.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov; No: NCT03362385.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04214-1.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Obstructive sleep apnea, Remnant cholesterol, Triglyceride

Background

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) stands as a prevalent sleep-disordered breathing disorder [1, 2] affecting 17% of women and 34% of men in the general population [3], escalating to 56% in individuals with unstable angina (UA) [4]. Despite the unclear underlying mechanisms of OSA, metabolic disruptions induced by OSA emerge as a pivotal link connecting this condition with cardiovascular disease. Notably, disturbances in lipid metabolism, recognized as a significant risk factor, come to the forefront [5–7].

Remnant cholesterol (RC), residing in triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs), plays a contributory role in atherosclerosis development by depositing in the arterial intima and inciting local inflammatory responses [8]. Robust evidence from multiple studies strongly indicates that sustained elevation of circulating RC concentrations, closely associated with triglycerides (TGs), plays a causal role in lingering cardiovascular risk, even in instances where low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) levels are under control [9]. While studies on the relationship between OSA and dyslipidemia yield inconsistent conclusions, the prevailing evidence suggests a frequent association beta ween OSA and dyslipidemia, particularly influencing TG metabolism [9–13].

OSA and RC share analogous pathophysiological pathways in the progression of coronary atherosclerosis, including their mediation of endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation [8, 14]. However, to date, it remains unclear whether the interaction between RC or TG levels and OSA, as well as the prognostic significance of OSA in UA patients, varies based on different levels of RC or TG. To address this question, utilizing a large prospective cohort, this study investigates the enduring influence of OSA in UA patients in relation to RC and TG levels.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a sub-study of the OSA-ACS project (NCT03362385), a prospective, observational, single center study that enrolled patients with UA who were admitted to Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, from June 2015 to January 2020, wherein portable sleep apnea monitoring was performed on all enrolled patients according to the inclusion criteria, to evaluate the association between OSA and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with UA. UA was defined as symptoms or accelerating patterns of anginal symptoms with or without ECG changes indicative of ischemia but without elevation in cardiac biomarkers [15]. All patients received standard care during hospitalization according to current guidelines [16].

Inclusion criteria included: patients aged 18–85 years; a discharge diagnosis of UA; signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: cardiac arrest or cardiogenic shock; history of malignancy; inability to complete sleep monitoring tests; missing total cholesterol (TC), TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) or LDL-C values on admission; patients with predominantly central sleep apnea (≥ 50% central events and central apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 10 breaths/hour) and those already receiving regular continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment (>4 h/day or >21 days/month) and lost to follow-up after discharge.

The study protocol aligns with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as substantiated by prior approval from the ethics committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2013025). Written informed consent for trial participation was obtained from all enrolled patients.

Overnight sleep study and management

Eligible patients underwent an overnight sleep study utilizing a portable cardiopulmonary polygraph (Apnea Link Air; ResMed) following clinical stabilization during hospitalization. Apnea was defined as the complete cessation of airflow lasting ≥ 10 s. Hypoventilation was characterised by a decrease in airflow of more than 30% for ≥ 10 s, associated with a decrease in arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) of more than 4%. The AHI was established as the quantification of apnea and hypoventilation events per hour of total recording time. In accordance with the International Classification of Sleep Disorders-Third Edition and the guidelines outlined by the Adult Obstructive Sleep Apnea Working Group of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, patients were categorized into either the OSA group (AHI ≥ 15 events/h) or the non-OSA group (AHI < 15 events/h). In addition, nasal airflow, thoracic and abdominal movements, snoring episodes and nocturnal SaO2 were carefully recorded. Hypoxic exposure was rigorously quantified by calculating the hourly oxygen desaturation during sleep (ODI) and the percentage of time with SaO2 < 90% (T90). Those with an AHI ≥ 15 events/h, particularly those with excessive daytime sleepiness assessed by the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) score, were subsequently referred to a sleep center for potential consideration of OSA treatment, including CPAP. To ensure accuracy and consistency of analysis, 50% of all studies were thoroughly reviewed by a masked investigator with expertise in sleep medicine.

Definitions and measurement of lipid profiles

Serum samples were collected after an overnight fast and the lipid profile was assessed using standard hospital assays. Calculations for LDL-C were performed using the Friedewald equation, unless TG exceeded 400 mg/dL, in which case direct measurement was used. RC was calculated as TC minus LDL-C minus HDL-C. The lack of consensus on the optimal RC concentration in patients has been noted in the existing literature [17–19]. Therefore, in this study, patients were categorised according to tertiles of RC levels, with the highest tertile of RC defined as high RC (HRC, RC ≥ 0.85 mmol/L) and the middle and lowest tertiles of RC defined as normal RC (NRC, RC < 0.85 mmol/L). For TG, patients were also categorised into a high TG group (HTG, TG ≥ 1.91 mmol/L) and a normal TG group (NTG, TG < 1.91 mmol/L) according to the highest tertile.

Follow-up and endpoints

The primary endpoint in this study was major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), comprehensively defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction (MI), hospitalization for unstable angina (UA), ischemia stroke, or ischemia-driven revascularization. Secondary endpoints included the individual components of MACCE and all-cause death. All endpoints were defined according to the definitions proposed by the Standardized Data Collection for Cardiovascular Trials Initiative [20]. Clinical visits were conducted by investigators who rigorously upheld the confidentiality of patient data. Patient follow-up occurred through outpatient clinic visits, chart reviews, or telephone calls at months 1, 3, 6, and 12 post-hospital discharge, followed by subsequent assessments every 6 months thereafter.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, sleep monitoring results, and prognosis were systematically stratified based on median RC levels and OSA status. Continuous variables were succinctly presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range: first and third quartiles), and comparisons were conducted using the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and subjected to comparison using the χ2 statistic or Fisher’s exact test, respectively.

For outcome analyses, Kaplan-Meier curves were employed to illustrate time-to-event data and the cumulative incidence of primary and secondary outcomes based on the interaction between RC and OSA. Differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

Both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were executed within each RC stratum to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), evaluating the risk of MACCE in OSA patients relative to those without OSA. Covariates included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model were primarily determined based on clinically relevant baseline variables or those exhibiting a univariate association with the primary outcomes. These covariates comprised age, sex, body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, previous MI, previous stroke, smoking status, statin treatment, fibrate use, revascularization, and baseline LDL-C.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS), and a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Study population and association of OSA with RC and TG levels

Ultimately, 1,106 UA patients, with a mean age of 57.0 ± 10.1 years, formed the subject of further analyses (Fig. 1). Among this cohort, 560 patients (50.6%) received a diagnosis of OSA. In patients with OSA, levels of RC, TG and HDL-C exhibited a significant increase (P < 0.05 for all) (Supplementary Table 1), but the prevalence of OSA did not differ significantly between the NRC and HRC groups or between the NTG and HTG groups (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Baseline clinical characteristics according to RC, TG and OSA classification

Compared with patients in the NRC group, patients in the HRC group were younger, had a higher prevalence of obesity, had more traditional risk factors, and were more likely to have undergone percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). They also had significantly worse sleep-related indicators, including AHI, ODI, ESS score, and minimum SaO2 (Supplementary Table 2). Baseline clinical characteristics, organized by RC highest tertile and OSA status classification, are detailed in Tables 1 and 2. Irrespective of RC levels, the OSA group had higher BMI, waist-hip ratio, diastolic blood pressure and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein than the non-OSA group. Within the HRC group, OSA patients were more likely to be male. Among NRC patients, those with OSA had higher levels of TG and RC, but lower levels of HDL-C. They were also more likely to receive Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitor or Angiotensin II Receptor Blocker medication post-discharge. Other clinical details remained generally consistent. In addition, TG was categorised into two groups based on the highest tertile, and baseline characteristics aligned with the HRC and NRC groups, as detailed in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

Outcomes analyses between patients stratified by RC and TG levels

Over a median follow-up duration of 1.9 years (1.1, 3.0), 192 patients (17.4%) encountered a MACCE. The incidence of MACCE exhibited similarity, with no statistically significant difference noted between patients in the NRC and HRC groups (adjusted HR 1.03; 95% CI 0.76–1.41; P = 0.838) (Supplementary Table 5 Supplementary Fig. 1). The most frequently observed secondary endpoints were hospitalization for UA (147 events) and ischemia-driven revascularization (73 events). Other cardiovascular events also demonstrated no significant disparities between the two groups. Analyses of outcomes based on TG levels are detailed in Supplementary Tables 6 and Supplementary Fig. 2, revealing results analogous to those observed with RC.

Outcomes of OSA vs. Non-OSA groups stratified by RC and TG categories

OSA significantly heightened the cumulative incidence of MACCE in patients with HRC (adjusted HR 2.06; 95% CI 1.20–3.51, P = 0.008) and HTG (adjusted HR 2.10; 95% CI 1.22–3.63, P = 0.008) compared to the non-OSA group. Conversely, the incidence of MACCE demonstrated similarity between OSA and non-OSA groups in patients with NRC or NTG (adjusted HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.84–1.75, P = 0.297 for NRC; adjusted HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.85–1.74, P = 0.295 for NTG). The elevated risk of MACCE in patients with HRC or HTG associated with OSA was predominantly attributed to a significantly higher hospitalization for UA (adjusted HR 1.96, 95% CI 1.05–3.66, P = 0.035 for HRC, adjusted HR 1.90; 95% CI 1.02–3.55, P = 0.043 for HTG) and ischemia-driven revascularization (adjusted HR 3.61, 95% CI 1.31–9.98, P = 0.013 for HRC, adjusted HR 3.50; 95% CI 1.28–9.64, P = 0.015 for HTG). In the stratified analysis based on RC or TG levels, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of cardiovascular death, stroke, ischemia-driven revascularization, and hospitalization for heart failure between the OSA and non-OSA groups (Fig. 2; Table 3, and Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, no significant interaction was observed between OSA and RC or TG for MACCE and other secondary outcomes (interaction P>0.757 for all).

Discussion

The present analysis reveals a synergistic relationship between RC, TG and OSA, where in patients with OSA and HRC or HTG had an increased risk for MACCE compared with patients without OSA after UA onset. In contrast, among patients with NRC or NTG, there was no significant difference in the incidence of MACCE between the OSA and non-OSA groups. This study also found that the presence of OSA elevated RC and TG levels in UA patients, while baseline elevation in RC and TG correlated with the occurrence of MACCE in UA patients afflicted with OSA. To the best of current knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effect of OSA on recurrent events in UA patients stratified by RC category.

A large amount of evidence indicates an association between OSA and the incidence of dyslipidemia. Trzepizur et al. demonstrated a correlation between ODI, TG, and low HDL-C, but not with TC and LDL-C [21]. Another cohort study from the European Sleep Apnea Database independently linked dyslipidemia with ODI, showing a positive correlation between TG concentration and both ODI and AHI [12]. Consistent with these findings, a significant increase in TG and RC levels was observed in UA patients with OSA. These results imply a closer relationship between OSA and TG/RC metabolism. Mechanistically, OSA and chronic intermittent hypoxia contribute to dyslipidemia by increasing lipid transport to the liver during the fasting state, decreasing clearance of TRLs, and inhibiting adipose tissue lipoprotein lipase activity in the postprandial period [7, 22]. Notably, there may exist a reverse causal relationship between OSA and increased RC or TG. It was observed that although there was no significant difference in OSA prevalence between HRC and NRC patients or between HTG and NTG patients, sleep-related indicators were significantly worse. This association may be attributed to the fact that, compared to UA patients with NRC, those with HRC were more prone to obesity, possessing a higher BMI. This condition may reduce airway calibre by depositing fat in upper airway tissues and in the thorax, thereby reducing chest compliance and functional residual capacity. Consequently, this process promotes and exacerbates nocturnal hypoxemia and OSA severity.

In atherogenic dyslipidemia, TRLs exhibit greater abundance and size, enabling them to carry more cholesterol than LDL-C. Given that most cells degrade TG but not cholesterol, RC, generated through the hydrolysis of TRLs, is deposited in the arterial intima to a similar, if not more pronounced, extent than cholesterol produced by LDL-C. This deposition further stimulates local inflammation through the activation of inflammation and the coagulation cascade, potentially leading to plaque rupture [8]. Several studies, including the PREDIMED study, have demonstrated that RC levels are independently associated with cardiovascular prognosis [17]. However, in this study, there was no significant difference in the incidence of MACCE in UA patients in the high RC and high TG groups compared to the normal RC and normal TG groups, unlike previous studies. Possible explanations include the focus of this study population on diagnosed UA patients, whereas most prior investigations were conducted in populations without pre-existing UA [17, 23]. It may also be attributed to the relatively limited number of patients and events in each group, resulting in inadequate statistical power. These results should be interpreted with caution and warrant further validation in larger cohorts.

The impact of OSA on cardiovascular outcomes can vary across specific populations, including diabetes, gender, obesity, and hyperuricemia, but the influence of RC and TG stratification remains unclear [5, 6, 24]. Mechanistically, the pronounced interaction between OSA and RC or TG may exert a synergistic effect in instigating and exacerbating atherosclerosis [8, 25]. In line with this inference, the study identified that, in comparison with non-OSA patients, OSA significantly elevated the risk of MACCE in UA patients with HRC or HTG but not in those with NRC or NTG. Presently, data from randomized controlled trials do not substantiate the reduction in cardiovascular event incidence with CPAP in patients with established cardiovascular disease [26], and its inconsistent impact on lipid lowering further complicates the interpretation [27]. This negative outcome might, in part, be attributed to patients receiving optimal medical therapy following guidelines. Importantly, the RICCADSA study encompassed the entire spectrum of patients with coronary artery disease, overlooking the possibility that the adverse effects of OSA may not be uniformly distributed across the population, potentially attenuating the benefits of CPAP [26]. This observation prompts a reconsideration of intervention study designs to unveil potential benefits of OSA treatment for secondary cardiovascular prevention in specific risk phenotypes, such as elevated RC or TG levels. In this study, UA patients with OSA and non-traditional risk factors, like elevated RC or TG levels, exhibited a significantly heightened risk of future events, signifying a high-risk subgroup that might be most responsive to interventions in this population. Screening and treating OSA could be pivotal, and further investigations are warranted to assess the impact of OSA treatment in this high-risk subgroup.

This study possesses certain limitations. Firstly, being a single-center observational study, it inherently carries the risk of confounding biases. To address this limitation, efforts were made to minimize bias by ensuring that professional researchers collecting follow-up data remained blinded to patients’ sleep results and baseline characteristics. Additionally, several crucial confounding variables were included in the outcome analyses, enhancing result reliability to a certain extent. Secondly, OSA diagnosis relied on portable polygraphy, which, potentially overestimating actual sleep duration, might result in an underestimation of the AHI. Nevertheless, the effective validation of portable polygraphy as a substitute for polysomnography in prior studies lends support to its reliability [28]. Thirdly, the limited utilization of CPAP treatment within the cohort restricts a comprehensive assessment of its therapeutic efficacy in UA patients. Fourth, the study focuses on East Asian patients, and further exploration is needed to ascertain the generalizability of these findings to other ethnicities. Finally, the use of calculated RC instead of directly measured RC might lead to a conservative estimate of risk. However, calculated and measured RC are closely related [29], and the calculated RC offers the advantage of easy clinical calculation using existing lipid measurements at no extra cost.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed that in UA patients with HRC and HTG, the incidence of MACCE was significantly increased in patients with OSA compared to patients without OSA. This finding highlights the importance of screening for OSA in UA patients with elevated RC and TG and provides recommendations for future clinical trials to better screen high-risk patients.
