Distinct and compensatory roles of STAG1 and STAG2 in post-mitotic genome refolding
Authors: Manzhu Wang, Yabing Zhang, Fengnian Shan, Chongren Pei, Fuhai Liu, Sijian Xia, Lirong Shu, Bicheng Li, Dannan Jing, Yongjia Weng, Han Zhao, Yinzhi Lin, Yali Yu, Baiyue Wang, Haoyue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 3D architecture of the eukaryotic genome is largely shaped by cohesin complexes containing either STAG1 or STAG2 subunits. Yet, their roles in post-mitotic genome refolding remain unclear. Here, we establish STAG2 as the predominant paralog and primary orchestrator of this process. We find that upon mitotic exit, STAG1 depletion imposes negligible effects on genome refolding or transcription reactivation, whereas STAG2 regulates genome remodeling in a stage- and chromatin-context-dependent manner. In early-G1, STAG2 promotes small euchromatic structural loops, enhancer–promoter contacts and transcriptional refiring; in late-G1, it suppresses large loops by limiting the more processive STAG1-cohesin. STAG2 processivity is constrained by CTCF roadblocks rather than genomic traveling distance. Co-depletion causes synergistic loss of structural loops and stronger transcriptional dysregulation, yet residual chromatin-bound cohesin retains measurable extrusion capacity. Together, these results establish STAG2 as the principal regulator of post-mitotic spatiotemporal chromatin reorganization, while STAG1 provides compensatory support for robustness.

Introduction

The eukaryotic genome is intricately folded, exhibiting a multi-layered hierarchical three-dimensional (3D) structure. At the megabase scale, chromatin can be partitioned into the euchromatin-enriched A-type compartments and heterochromatin-enriched B-type compartments1–3. At finer scales, chromatin is characterized by topologically associating domains (TADs) and chromatin loops1,4–6. One major organizer of interphase genome structure is cohesin, a multi-subunit complex initially known for its role in cohering sister chromatids after DNA replication7,8. Recent work has revealed another critical role of cohesin in interphase: organizing genome architecture via a process termed loop extrusion. In this process, cohesin actively translocates along the DNA fiber, forming progressively larger loops until it is arrested by convergently oriented CTCF molecules9–12. By maintaining these structures, cohesin and CTCF cooperate to either facilitate or insulate contacts between gene promoters and distal enhancers, thereby regulating transcription13–15.

The cohesin complex consists of two structural maintenance of chromosome proteins (SMC1 and SMC3), a kleisin subunit (RAD21), and one of the two paralogous STAG subunits, STAG1 or STAG216,17. Despite sharing broadly overlapping functions, accumulating evidence indicates that STAG1- and STAG2-associated cohesin exhibit specialized roles in genome organization and transcription regulation18,19. For instance, while both STAG proteins occupy a common set of binding sites on chromatin, they also display unique genomic positioning20. Furthermore, STAG1-associated cohesin exhibits significantly longer chromatin residence time than STAG2-associated cohesin21. Therefore, STAG1 may be primarily involved in establishing stable, long-range chromatin loops. In contrast, STAG2-associated cohesin may mediate transient, short-range interactions, including those between enhancers and promoters within TADs20,21. Consistent with these findings, deleting STAG2 results in enlarged chromatin loops, likely due to the increased availability of STAG1-associated cohesin, which favors large loop formation21. Despite these discoveries, how each STAG protein contributes to the de novo establishment of structural loops remains unknown.

3D genome structure is dynamically remodeled throughout the cell cycle. During mitosis, hallmark chromatin architectural features including A/B compartments, TADs and chromatin loops are disrupted22. During this process, cohesin is evicted from chromatin23,24. As cells exit from mitosis, interphase genome structure is gradually rebuilt, accompanied by a progressive reloading of cohesin onto the genome, which drives chromatin re-looping25. A recent study utilizing a quantitative imaging approach illustrates that STAG1 is rapidly imported into daughter nuclei and rebinds chromatin, while STAG2 nuclear import is delayed, leading to a gradual chromatin binding over a course of several hours into G1 phase26. This temporal uncoupling may indicate stage-specific roles of STAG1 and STAG2 in post-mitotic genome refolding.

Here, we adopt the mitosis-to-G1 transition as a platform to systematically dissect how STAG1- and STAG2-associated cohesin independently regulate chromatin structural loop reformation and gene reactivation after cell division. We find that STAG1 and STAG2 share redundant and unique functions. STAG1 depletion elicits no dramatic effect on post-mitotic chromatin loops or transcription. Conversely, STAG2 exhibits two regulatory modes: (1) a loop size-dependent regulation, in which it constitutively stabilizes small loops ( < 100 kb) throughout G1 phase while progressively suppressing large loops ( > 300 kb), potentially by constraining STAG1 loop extrusion capacity; and (2) a chromatin context-dependent regulation, in which it rapidly engages euchromatin in early-G1 to ensure efficient transcription reactivation, followed by a delayed association with heterochromatin in late-G1. Co-depletion of both STAG proteins triggered synergistic collapse of structural loops, revealing a functional compensation between them. These results delineate STAG2 as the primary architect among STAG paralogs regulating post-mitotic genome refolding.

Results

Impacts of STAG1 and STAG2 on post-mitotic chromatin re-compartmentalization

To examine the roles of STAG1 and STAG2 in the re-establishment of chromatin structure following mitosis, we aimed to selectively delete them during the transition from mitosis to G1-phase. To achieve this, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9-directed genome editing on the well-characterized G1E-ER4 erythroblast line27–29. Two sublines were generated, each carrying a minimal auxin-inducible degron (mAID) endogenously fused to either STAG1 (STAG1-mAID-GFP, Stag1mAID) or STAG2 (STAG2-mAID-mCherry, Stag2mAID) (Fig. 1a)30,31. Western blot validation with pararlog-specific antibodies targeting endogenous STAG1 or STAG2 in whole cell lysates confirmed that addition of the mAID tag with fluorescent protein did not significantly alter basal protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). 5-Ph-IAA treatment led to complete degradation of the target proteins within 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). Notably, while the Stag1mAID cell line exhibited a slower baseline growth rate than the parental line, prolonged depletion of STAG1 did not further affect cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 1e). To further corroborate the efficiency of the mAID system, we performed calibrated ChIP-seq analysis with or without 4 h of 5-Ph-IAA treatment. Under basal conditions, we identified 20,365 STAG1 and 18,552 STAG2 peaks. As expected, the majority of them (∼83.9% and 80.0% for STAG1 and STAG2 respectively) were co-occupied by CTCF (Supplementary Fig. 1f). We also observed substantial overlap between the two paralogs, with 14,394 peaks shared on the genome (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Importantly, 5-Ph-IAA treatment resulted in the near-total loss of ChIP-seq signals for both STAG1 (∼97.6% of peaks lost) and STAG2 (∼98.1% of peaks lost), confirming the completeness of protein degradation in our system (Supplementary Fig. 1h–j).

The acute nature of mAID-mediated protein degradation enabled us to investigate the effects of STAG1 or STAG2 depletion during cell division. Stag1mAID or Stag2mAID cells were arrested at prometaphase through nocodazole treatment (Fig. 1b). Meanwhile, 5-Ph-IAA was applied to allow protein degradation (Fig. 1b). Upon nocodazole release, cells devoid of STAG1 or STAG2 were collected at 1 h (early-G1) and 4 h (late-G1) time points, respectively, using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (Fig. 1b; and Supplementary Fig. 1k, l). Cells without 5-Ph-IAA treatment were collected in parallel as controls (Fig. 1b; and Supplementary Fig. 1k, l). Of note, short-term depletion of STAG proteins did not affect mitotic progression (Supplementary Fig. 1k, l).

It is well-established that cohesin-mediated loop extrusion counteracts chromatin compartmentalization32,33. To test whether deleting STAG proteins could affect post-mitotic chromatin re-compartmentalization, we performed in-situ Hi-C experiments (Supplementary Data. 1). Of note, the control samples displayed contact probability decay curves (PS curves) similar to those of the parental cell line at both early- and late-G1 stages; this further demonstrates that mAID-tagging and fluorescence protein did not measurably affect protein function (Supplementary Fig. 1m).

Consistent with prior reports, the control samples exhibited the characteristic checkerboard-like A/B compartments in early-G1 phase, which were progressively intensified and expanded across the entire genome by late-G1 phase (Fig. 1c, e)25,34. Deleting STAG1 did not induce any noticeable changes in post-mitotic chromatin re-compartmentalization (Fig. 1c, d; and Supplementary Fig. 2a-c). Visual inspection of the contact maps suggested that STAG2 depletion induced only very weak changes in compartmentalization. However, closer examination of the saddle plots revealed a mild reduction in A/B compartment segregation specifically during late G1(Fig. 1e, f; and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d), which was not seen in STAG1-deficient cells (Fig. 1d; and Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). This finding contrasts with previous reports that complete cohesin loss (e.g., via RAD21 depletion) sharpens the separation between A and B compartments32,35. We reason that the remaining STAG1-associated cohesin, not only compensates for the loss of STAG2-mediated loop extrusion but does so with enhanced activity or increased chromatin occupancy, resulting in a net decrease in A/B segregation. Together, our data unveils distinct roles of STAG1 and STAG2 in regulating higher-order chromatin re-organization after mitosis.

STAG1 depletion subtly affects post-mitotic loop reformation

Reformation of structural loops after mitosis is tightly controlled by cohesin recruitment25,34,36. While studies in HeLa cells have shown that STAG1 and STAG2 exhibit distinct reloading kinetics, with STAG1 re-associating with chromatin more rapidly post-mitosis26, the functional consequences of these divergent kinetics on post-mitotic chromatin re-looping are not completely understood. To address this, we employed a modified HICCUPS approach to systematically identify chromatin loops in early- and late-G1 phase samples, both with or without STAG proteins. In total, we identified 23,482 non-redundant chromatin loops. Among these, 9,428 were classified as structural loops, characterized by co-occupancy of CTCF and cohesin peaks at both anchors with at most one anchor marked by cis-regulatory elements (CREs) (Supplementary Data. 2). Surprisingly, aggregated peak analysis (APA) of genome-wide structural loops revealed that STAG1 depletion did not measurably alter post-mitotic structural loop strength (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Quantification of loop signal intensity revealed only a subtle reduction upon STAG1 loss after mitosis (Supplementary Fig. 3b). In line with these results, the distance-dependent contact probability decay curve PS was unchanged upon STAG1 depletion after mitosis, suggesting that overall cohesin loop extrusion activity was unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Together, these results indicate that the overall impact of STAG1 loss on post-mitotic chromatin structural reformation was very minor.

To gain further insight into these observations, we performed chromatin fractionation experiments in asynchronous Stag1mAID cells to investigate the chromatin occupancy of cohesin subunits. Interestingly, we found that basal levels of the chromatin-associated STAG1 were reduced compared to the parental line, despite that total STAG1 levels remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). We reason that this reduction may stem from either a lower chromatin-binding affinity of the mAID-tagged STAG1 or partial degradation of the tagged protein during the sample preparation steps required for chromatin fractionation. Nevertheless, upon 5-Ph-IAA treatment, the chromatin association of other cohesin subunits, including the core subunits RAD21 and SMC1, as well as the paralog STAG2, remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). In line with these results, calibrated ChIP-seq revealed that STAG2 chromatin binding profiles were unaffected by STAG1 loss (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). This stable occupancy of the broader cohesin machinery likely explains why STAG1 depletion failed to trigger large-scale structural impacts. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that STAG1 is less abundant in the mAID-tagged cell line, which may potentially lead to an underestimation of its full functional role.

STAG2 is the dominant STAG paralog

The relatively mild impact of STAG1 loss on post-mitotic genome refolding suggested a potential functional compensation by STAG2. We next sought to determine the relative ratio of STAG1 to STAG2 in G1E-ER4 cells. First, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to generate two sublines, endogenously tagging either STAG1 or STAG2 with a FLAG-P2A-HALO tag (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Validation with paralog-specific antibodies confirmed that this tagging did not interfere with the endogenous protein levels of either STAG1 or STAG2 (Supplementary Fig.4b–e). By utilizing a common anti-FLAG antibody, we were able to simultaneously detect both proteins on a single western blot, thereby eliminating the confounding variable of differing antibody affinities. This analysis revealed that the ratio of STAG1 to STAG2 is ~1:5 (Supplementary Fig.4f, g).

To further substantiate this finding, we performed quantitative mass spectrometry on parental G1E-ER4 cells. By comparing the intensities of the top three paralog-specific peptides for each protein, we estimated a STAG1 to STAG2 ratio of ~1:5(Supplementary Fig.4h, i), consistent with our western blot results and previous proteomic studies in other cell types37. These findings establish STAG2 as the predominant paralog within the G1E-ER4 cohesin pool. This dominance potentially provides a robust functional buffer, explaining why the loss of STAG1 fails to induce more dramatic phenotypic impact.

STAG2 plays a bifurcated role in post-mitotic loop reformation

Given that STAG2 constitutes the vast majority of the STAG-cohesin pool, we reasoned that its depletion would exert a more profound impact on post-mitotic genome re-organization than the loss of STAG1. To test this, we measured the dynamic reformation of chromatin loops in STAG2-deficient cells after mitosis. Our analysis revealed that 1,624 structural loops were gained upon STAG2 depletion (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 5a). Of note, the gained loops were detected almost exclusively in late-G1 phase and were absent in early-G1 (Fig. 2b). This delayed phenotype is consistent with the gradual nuclear accumulation of STAG2-associated cohesin. Furthermore, the gained structural loops were significantly larger than those that were maintained or lost, confirming previous findings that STAG2 depletion promotes large loop formation (Fig. 2c)21. Conversely, 973 loops were lost following STAG2 depletion (Fig. 2a; and Supplementary Fig. 5a). These lost loops were significantly smaller than both maintained and gained ones (Fig. 2c). This implies that STAG2 plays a role in establishing small structural loops after mitosis, a function distinct from its suppressive effect on large loop formation. To further elucidate the impact of STAG2 loss on loops of different sizes, we systematically quantified the log2 fold change (log2FC) of loop intensity across all identified structural loops. Our analysis revealed that STAG2 depletion triggers two distinct, loop size-dependent effects that are temporally uncoupled during G1 phase. First, large loops ( > 300 kb) were predominantly intensified by STAG2 loss in late- but not early-G1 phase (Fig. 2d, e; and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Second, small loops ( < 100 kb) were attenuated rapidly in early-G1 upon STAG2 degradation and remained weakened throughout G1 phase (Fig. 2d, e; and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Our findings support a model in which STAG2 orchestrates post-mitotic chromatin re-looping through a dual mechanism. During early-G1, its low nuclear abundance allows the establishment of small loops. As STAG2 progressively accumulates by late-G1, this increased concentration allows it to both maintain small loops and competitively inhibit STAG1-associated cohesin, thereby suppressing large loop formation. Consequently, upon STAG2 loss, STAG1-associated cohesin becomes functionally unconstrained, enabling the aberrant formation or stabilization of larger loops. Thus, the kinetics of STAG2 nuclear import could dictate the temporal partitioning of its functions in 3D genome reorganization after mitosis.

STAG2 loss does not improve STAG1 chromatin occupancy

The observed intensification of large loops upon STAG2 loss may originate from either a functional suppression or a competitive eviction of STAG1 by STAG2 during late-G1 phase. To test whether STAG2 directly limits STAG1 chromatin-bound levels, we performed calibrated ChIP-seq targeting STAG1 in asynchronous Stag2mAID cells with or without 4 h of 5-Ph-IAA treatment. We reasoned that asynchronous populations, where the majority of cells have progressed beyond early-G1, serve as a reliable proxy for the late-G1 stage when the most dramatic structural changes were observed. Our analysis, validated with two independent STAG1 antibodies, revealed that STAG2 loss did not measurably increase STAG1 occupancy on the chromatin (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). This lack of compensatory loading was further supported by calibrated RAD21 ChIP-seq, which showed no significant change in core cohesin levels following STAG2 loss (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).

To further substantiate this finding, we performed similar STAG1 ChIP-seq experiments in sorted late-G1 cells. Consistent with our asynchronous data, STAG1 peaks were not improved by the absence of STAG2 in late G1 phase(Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Finally, chromatin fractionation experiments in asynchronous cells confirmed that while STAG2 was completely eliminated from the genome upon 5-Ph-IAA treatment, the amount of chromatin-associated STAG1 remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h). Furthermore, the levels of the core cohesin subunits RAD21 and SMC1 were also unaffected, indicating that the total cohesin pool on chromatin remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h). Collectively, these data indicate that STAG2 loss does not lead to a corresponding increase in STAG1 occupancy, thereby ruling out a competitive eviction model.

Paralog-specific Micro-HiChIP reveals divergent looping capabilities of STAG1 and STAG2

To directly assess the functional diversities of STAG1 and STAG2, we employed Micro-HiChIP, a high-resolution method to selectively map chromatin contacts associated with specific targeting factors. We performed Micro-HiChIP on asynchronous parental G1E-ER4 cells using paralog-specific antibodies against STAG1 and STAG2, respectively38,39. (Supplementary Fig. 7a). At comparable library depths, we identified a greater number of loops mediated by STAG1-associated cohesin than by STAG2-associated cohesin (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Representative contact maps revealed that STAG1 displays more robust corner dot puncta signals at larger genomic distances (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Accordingly, the overall loop size associated with STAG2 was significantly smaller than that of STAG1 (Supplementary Fig. 7d).

Further stratification of these interactions showed that loops associated exclusively with STAG1 were significantly larger than both the “shared” loops (mediated by both paralogs) and those associated exclusively with STAG2 (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Conversely, loops mediated solely with STAG2 were significantly smaller than the shared population (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Taken together, by selectively targeting paralog-specific contacts, we provide direct evidence that STAG1 is more capable of mediating large structural loops, whereas STAG2 is primarily responsible for smaller chromatin loops.

To distinguish between these constraints, we used STAG2 depletion as an indirect readout of its loop-forming capacity. We reasoned that if STAG2 is proficient at forming a specific loop, its loss should weaken that loop. However, if STAG2 is inefficient, for certain large loops, it would interfere with the more processive STAG1-associated cohesin. In this scenario, STAG2 depletion would actually strength the long-distant loops by “unleashing” STAG1 to complete the extrusion process.

We stratified loops into a hierarchy: Tier 1 (nested, primary loops), Tier 2 (loops encompassing Tier 1), and Tier ≥ 3 (higher-order loops), which contain increasing numbers of intermediate CTCF obstacles (Fig. 3b). While STAG2 depletion weakened small Tier 1 loops, it significantly strengthened Tier 2 and Tier ≥ 3 loops (Fig. 3c–e; and Supplementary Fig. 8a–d). Crucially, a size-matched analysis showed that even when loop length was held constant, higher-order loops (with more intermediate CTCF) showed greater enhancement upon STAG2 loss than nested ones (Fig. 3f–i). This indicates that the presence of CTCF roadblocks, rather than genomic distance alone, is the primary factor limiting STAG2’s capacity for higher-order loop formation.

We first investigated whether genomic travel distance independently limits STAG2 by focusing on Tier 1 loops. These loops lack internal nested loops, effectively ruling out the confounding factor of intermediate roadblocks and providing a clear background to assess the impact of physical distance alone. Within this “clean” context, we found a distance-dependent phenotype: STAG2 depletion preferentially reduced the intensity of smaller loops, confirming its requirement for their formation (Fig. 3j). Importantly, larger Tier 1 loops ( > 300 kb) remained largely unaffected (Fig. 3j). This suggests that STAG2’s loop-forming capacity is not fundamentally limited by genomic distance, provided the intervening chromatin is devoid of roadblocks.

However, as structural complexity increases in Tiers 2 and 3, the specific requirement for STAG2 in small-loop formation disappears, replaced by a dominant roadblock-driven phenotype. Within Tier 2, we detected a modest distance-dependent increase in intensity for loops between 100 to 400 kb upon STAG2 loss (Fig. 3j). Tier 3 loops exhibit universal enhancement upon STAG2 loss, regardless of genomic distance (Fig. 3j).

Collectively, these findings indicate that while travel distance can modestly affect STAG2-mediated loop formation in the absence of intermediate barriers, this effect is overridden by the presence of CTCF roadblocks, which constitute the dominant constraint on STAG2’s loop-forming capacity.

To provide direct mechanistic evidence that CTCF roadblocks are the primary constraint on STAG2-cohesin, we performed STAG2 Micro-HiChIP in a CTCF degron cell line (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Upon CTCF depletion, canonical STAG2-anchored loops were abolished (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c), replaced by a dramatic gain in long-range, “trespassing” contacts that traversed former CTCF boundaries (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). To quantify the traveling distance of cohesin from its points of origin, we followed the model that cohesin loading is concentrated at cis-regulatory elements (CREs) where NIPBL is enriched40,41. By treating CREs as viewpoints for aggregate virtual 4 C profiles, we tracked how effectively STAG2-associated cohesin bridges these loading sites to distal genomic regions. In the absence of CTCF, these CRE-centered profiles shifted significantly: local interactions were reduced, while contacts at distal genomic distances were markedly enhanced (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e). This reorganization demonstrates that while STAG2-associated cohesin possesses the intrinsic capacity to travel over large distance scales, its processivity is functionally restricted by CTCF-mediated arrest at intermediate barriers.

As expected, STAG2 depletion reduced Tier1 loop puncta signals in early-G1, consistent with its role in promoting small loop establishment after mitosis (Fig. 4c). This reduction persisted throughout G1 phase (Fig. 4c). Notably, the decrease was relatively uniform across all three epigenetic loop clusters, with no dramatic differences among them (Fig. 4c). These findings imply a chromatin context-independent role of STAG2 in the establishment and maintenance of stable structural loops after mitosis.

In contrast, internal loop contacts exhibited pronounced spatiotemporal and chromatin context-dependent regulation by STAG2. In early-G1 phase, STAG2 depletion reduced these contacts across all structural loop clusters (Fig. 4d). However, the magnitude of this effect varied dramatically: being the greatest in the euchromatic loops (cluster 1) and mildest in the constitutive heterochromatic loops (cluster 3) (Fig. 4d–f; and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). This observation suggests a preferential role of STAG2 in initiating extrusion in open chromatin. Strikingly, this relationship was completely reversed in late-G1. Upon STAG2 loss, constitutive heterochromatic loops (cluster 3) showed the most severe disruption in intra-loop contacts, while euchromatic loops (cluster 1) were less affected (Fig. 4d–f; Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). This temporal inversion highlights that STAG2 is increasingly required for loop extrusion in repressive chromatin as cells progress into late-G1, likely reflecting its delayed recruitment to these regions.

To test whether this shift is driven by the redirected distribution of the cohesin loading machinery, we performed calibrated ChIP-seq targeting NIPBL in early- vs. late-G1 phase. Consistent with several recent reports, NIPBL was predominantly centralized at cis-regulatory elements (CREs) in both cell cycle stages40,41(Supplementary Fig. 10c). Importantly, we observed a significant gain in NIPBL signal at regions outside of open chromatin as cells progressed into late-G1 (Supplementary Fig. 10d). This enrichment at non-CRE regions, suggests a temporal shift in the cohesin loading landscape. These results provide a mechanistic basis for the late-G1 dependency on STAG2 in repressive chromatin, suggesting that the delayed extrusion in these regions is linked to the late-stage recruitment of the NIPBL-cohesin complex.

Our study reveals that STAG2 contributes to the formation of stable, CTCF-anchored structural loops across various epigenetic states. Besides, it also mediates dynamic loop extrusion in a spatiotemporally-regulated and chromatin context-specific manner, driving early extrusion in euchromatin and facilitating late extrusion in heterochromatin after mitosis.

HICCUPS analysis revealed a massive loss of structural loops after mitosis, in the absence of both STAG1 and STAG2 (Fig. 5c). In line with this, visual examination of Hi-C contact matrices at individual genomic loci and genome-wide APA plots both unveiled a drastic elimination of structural loop signals in both early- and late-G1 phase (Fig. 5d, e). Taken together, our data demonstrate that at least one STAG protein is required for post-mitotic loop reformation. Although the loss of either STAG1 or STAG2 alone induces only marginal defects, their combined ablation synergistically prevents the re-establishment of the loop architecture, demonstrating their essential and redundant roles in this process.

Co-depletion of STAG1 and STAG2 impairs chromatin association of cohesin without affecting its loop extrusion processivity

To investigate the mechanism of loop loss, we performed calibrated ChIP-seq against RAD21 in late-G1 phase cells lacking STAG proteins. While CTCF binding remained unaffected, cohesin peaks were massively eliminated upon STAG1 and STAG2 co-depletion (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). This profound loss of peaks is consistent with the dissolution of structural loops. However, it does not necessarily imply a failure in cohesin loading. Rather, it likely reflects the established requirement for STAG proteins in mediating cohesin arrest at CTCF sites42.

To distinguish whether the observed loop loss resulted from a genuine defect in cohesin chromatin loading or a reduction in loop extrusion processivity, we performed chromatin fractionation in asynchronous cells (as a proxy for late-G1) treated with 5-Ph-IAA. This treatment led to a complete elimination of STAG1 and STAG2 from the chromatin (Fig. 5f, g; and Supplementary Fig. 12e, f). Intriguingly, the levels of core cohesin subunits SMC1 and Rad21 were only reduced to ~50% of their original levels (Fig. 5f, g). This indicates that nearly half of the cohesin population remains chromatin-associated in the absence of STAG1 and STAG2, suggesting that these subunits are not absolutely required for cohesin loading.

To verify that no residual STAG proteins were present, we performed SMC1 co-IP experiments. While STAG1 and STAG2 were detectable in control precipitates, they became completely undetectable after 5-Ph-IAA treatment (Supplementary Figs. 12c, d). Crucially, Rad21 remained associated with SMC1 at levels comparable to the control, confirming that the core heterotrimeric holocomplex (SMC1-SMC3-RAD21) remains intact without STAG proteins (Supplementary Figs. 12c, d). While we acknowledge that trace amounts of STAG proteins below the detection limit could theoretically contribute to cohesin loading, our biochemical data suggest they are not strictly required for DNA association.

However, we cannot exclude the possibility that a portion of this residual pool represents ‘legacy’ complexes that were loaded prior to STAG depletion and remained tethered to the chromatin, rather than complexes loaded de novo in the absence of the STAG paralogs.

To assess whether the STAG-free cohesin population retains loop extrusion activity, we analyzed the PS curve using SMC3 depletion as a benchmark for complete extrusion failure and CTCF loss as a benchmark for full extrusion without loops (Fig. 5h; and Supplementary Fig. 13a-g). Co-depletion of STAG1 and STAG2 produced a PS curve shift characterized by reduced contact frequency specifically at loop- and TAD-size genomic ranges (100kb–1 Mb), suggesting a decrease in loop extrusion compared to control cells (Fig. 5i). Notably, this reduction was significantly less severe than the dramatic collapse of contacts seen upon SMC3 loss (Fig. 5h, i; and Supplementary Fig. 13h). Furthermore, STAG-deficient samples retained higher extrusion properties than NIPBL-depleted cells, which have been shown to maintain a basal level of extrusion43 (Supplementary Fig. 13i). These comparative genomics data indicate that while loop formation is abrogated, extrusion activity is attenuated rather than completely abolished.

We next examined whether STAG proteins are required for the processivity of these moving complexes. In contrast to NIPBL-deficient cells, which exhibit a preferential loss of long-range contacts due to impaired travel distance, STAG1 and STAG2 co-depletion led to a uniform reduction in structural loops across all size ranges (Supplementary Fig.14a–c). This lack of size-dependent attenuation indicates that the intrinsic extrusion processivity of the remaining chromatin-bound complexes remains largely intact. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the core cohesin holocomplex is capable of both loading onto and extruding DNA independently of STAG1 and STAG2.

STAG1 and STAG2 distinctly influence post-mitotic gene reactivation

To approach this, we utilized TT-seq to measure nascent transcription during early- and late-G1 phases in cells acutely depleted of either STAG1 or STAG244. Notably, STAG1 depletion failed to induce any significant transcriptional changes, which aligns with its negligible effect on post-mitotic genome refolding (Fig. 6a, b; and Supplementary Fig. 15a; Supplementary Data. 3). Of note, these DEGs were not caused by 5-Ph-IAA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 15c, methods). Among these, 17 and 26 DEGs were down-regulated in early- and late-G1 phases, respectively, suggesting that STAG2 primarily participates in transcription activation. Discussion

Distinct impacts of STAG1 and STAG2 loss on post-mitotic genome refolding

Our study systematically dissected the individual and combinatorial contributions of STAG1 and STAG2 to post-mitotic genome refolding. We establish that STAG2 is the predominant paralog, maintaining a roughly 5:1 ratio over STAG1. This stoichiometric dominance aligns with clinical observations that STAG2 mutations significantly outnumber STAG1 mutations in human diseases, including numerous cancers46,47. While this ratio may fluctuate across the cell cycle, STAG2’s predominance appears to be a conserved feature, explaining why it can seamlessly mask the loss of the less abundant STAG137. Indeed, genome reconfiguration and transcriptional reactivation were largely unaffected by STAG1 loss. This result mildly contrasts with prior reports of transcriptional alterations upon STAG1 loss, a discrepancy that may reflect cell type-specific differences or technical variations48–50.

However, the distinct roles of these paralogs are not driven simply by abundance. If the two subunits differed only in quantity but not function, STAG2 depletion should trigger a global reduction in loop strength. Instead, we observed a non-uniform shift: STAG2 loss triggered an immediate weakening of small structural loops ( < 100 kb) but a delayed strengthening of larger loops (Fig. 7h), consistent with previous steady-state Hi-C studies51. Interestingly, although STAG2 is the dominant form and its depletion theoretically ablates the majority of cohesin complexes, the structural changes remain relatively limited. The fact that the genome preserves most of its architecture through the minor STAG1 pool suggests that cells maintain a functional surplus of cohesin. This indicates that the cell does not require its full complement of cohesin to maintain global chromatin structure; rather, the minor STAG1 pool provides sufficient compensatory support to ensure functional robustness.

The temporal specificity of this large-loop suppression (occurring primarily in late-G1) is likely explained by the distinct recruitment kinetics of STAG2. Previous live-cell imaging experiments in HeLa cells demonstrated that STAG2 enters the nucleus more slowly than STAG1, with nuclear accumulation typically completing by ~2 h after mitosis26. However, notably, the colocalization of STAG2 with CTCF in those cells continued to increase throughout late-G1, suggesting that functional chromatin loading may proceed significantly slower than mere nuclear entry26.

Our functional data in G1E-ER4 cells support a similar model. While we do not have direct loading kinetics for the individual paralogs and acknowledge potential cell-type specificities between our system and HeLa cells, our previous study demonstrated that total chromatin-bound cohesin progressively accumulates on the genome throughout the G1 phase25. Given that STAG2 is the dominant form in these cells, it is highly likely that STAG2 recruitment also follows this progressive trajectory, only reaching peak levels in late-G1. This continuous loading explains the temporal specificity of our findings: the initial STAG2 population in early-G1 is sufficient to establish small structural loops, but it is only as STAG2 occupancy reaches a higher threshold toward late-G1 that it effectively suppresses larger-scale loops.

Mechanistic interplay: functional suppression vs. binding competition

A key question is how STAG2 limits the formation of the larger loops typically mediated by STAG1. We formulated two potential scenarios for this interplay: (1) STAG2 physically occupies a limited pool of cohesin core subunits, thereby excluding STAG1 from the chromatin; (2) STAG2 inhibits STAG1’s extrusion processivity through physical hindrance or track occupancy, without altering STAG1’s binding. The displacement model predicts that losing STAG2 would lead to a compensatory increase in STAG1 chromatin loading, as seen in some long-term depletion studies49,52. However, our acute depletion data contradict this: STAG2 loss does not trigger a concurrent increase in STAG1 chromatin occupancy, and the total pool of chromatin-associated cohesin core complexes remains stable. Notably, while the intensity of STAG1 peaks remained unchanged, the connectivity between these peaks was significantly rewired, reflecting the formation of larger loops and increased cohesin extrusion upon STAG2 loss.

These results argue in favor of a functional suppression system. In this model, STAG2, driven by its numerical dominance, acts as the primary occupant of genomic tracks. Because STAG2 is intrinsically less processive than STAG1, its prevalence creates a physical hindrance that restricts the extrusion distance of any co-existing STAG1-cohesin. The discrepancy between our findings and previous reports potentially stems from our use of acute depletion, which captures primary mechanistic effects before the cell can initiate homeostatic proteomic shifts49,52.

Spatiotemporal and structural regulations on STAG2-associated Cohesin

It was recently reported that CTCF halts loop extrusion by significantly increasing the DNA-binding affinity of STAG1-cohesin complexes53. We speculate that a similar, potentially more stringent, “stickiness” mechanism governs STAG2, which would explain why it is more strictly confined to smaller genomic intervals compared to its more processive STAG1 paralog. Future biochemical assays comparing both subunits will be effective to determine if this differential sensitivity to CTCF-induced arrest is the primary driver of their distinct looping capacities.

Regarding recruitment location, a recent study emphasized that CREs only account for a small subset of total cohesin loading54. Our work complements this finding; while we observed that STAG2 progressively loads into heterochromatic regions toward late-G1, we also found that it maintains a distinct preference for euchromatin during the earliest stages of G1. This suggests that cohesin loading is governed by stage-specific regulation rather than a static affinity for specific chromatin states. Whether this transition is driven by a concentration-dependent “overflow” from euchromatin to heterochromatin, or by the delayed recruitment of auxiliary factors that facilitate heterochromatin loading later in G1, remains an open question for further investigation.

The role of STAG proteins in cohesin-chromatin association

Our findings demonstrate that while STAG proteins are major regulators of cohesin dynamics, cohesin loading and loop extrusion remain partially active even upon the acute co-depletion of both STAG1 and STAG2. This observation provides an in vivo context to the ongoing discussion regarding the biochemical requirements for cohesin function. Numerous studies have established the importance of STAG subunits for stable cohesin loading and DNA entrapment, both in vitro and in vivo55,56. Specifically, STAG proteins have been shown to be essential for the high-efficiency “opening” of the cohesin ring and the subsequent stabilization of the DNA-binding state required for loop extrusion55–58.

However, our data suggest that STAG proteins are not an absolutely requirement for the physical association of cohesin with chromatin. This aligns with classic biochemical reconstitutions where the yeast cohesin trimer (Smc1/3/Scc1), in the presence of its loader, was shown to successfully entrap DNA topologically even in the absence of the STAG ortholog Psc3, albeit at reduced efficeincy56. While STAG significantly accelerates and stabilizes this entrapment, consistent with the marked reduction in chromatin-associated cohesin we observed, it appears that a basal level of loading can occur through STAG-independent mechanisms.

While we acknowledge the possibility that a subset of the residual pool observed in asynchronous cells could represent complexes loaded prior to depletion, this is unlikely given the rapid turnover of the complex. Given that the mean residence time of chromatin-bound cohesin is ~20 min59, our 4-h depletion window significantly exceeds the time required for the majority of the cohesin pool to undergo multiple cycles of unloading and re-binding. It is therefore likely that the observed residual levels reflect an equilibrium of STAG-independent recruitment rather than merely stable “legacy” complexes.

Recent evidence further clarifies how this residual association might be maintained. Beyond the contribution of STAG subunits, NIPBL itself possesses a high intrinsic affinity for DNA60. This strong DNA-binding capacity allows NIPBL to theoretically “drag” or tether the cohesin core trimer to the chromatin fiber, even when the cooperative stabilization provided by STAG1 or STAG2 is missing. Our results, therefore, support a model where STAG proteins act as critical catalysts that convert a labile, NIPBL-mediated DNA association into the stable, processive holoenzyme required for robust genome architectural organization.

Impact of STAG protein loss on transcriptional reactivation

The impact of STAG loss on transcription is not uniform across the genome. We observed that genes with relatively weak promoters, which rely heavily on distal enhancers, are the most sensitive to STAG loss. This suggests that these “loop-dependent” genes strictly require the early recruitment of STAG to re-establish the structural architecture necessary for their activation.

Interestingly, while most CRE contacts are lost during mitosis and rebuilt de novo, we and others have recently noted that a subset of CRE interactions is maintained throughout mitosis in the absence of cohesin61,62. We speculate that these “mitotic remnants” represent exceptionally strong, high-affinity CRE interactions that can drive rapid gene reactivation independently of the cohesin machinery. This creates a two-tier system for post-mitotic transcription recovery: high-priority genes may utilize these cohesin-independent “seeds” to maintain cellular homeostasis immediately upon mitotic exit, whereas more complex, highly regulated genes depend on the cohesin-mediated formation of small loops to achieve full transcriptional competence.

In summary, our study in synchronized post-mitotic cells reveals a functional division of labor between STAG1 and STAG2 during genome refolding. STAG2 acts as the primary regulator, playing a critical role in the formation of small loops in early-G1, a process that is important for efficient transcriptional reactivation after mitosis. It also restricts large loop formation in late-G1. In contrast, STAG1 provides supportive and compensatory functions, contributing modestly to larger loop formation and serving as a failsafe mechanism that maintains genome architectural integrity in the absence of STAG2. This cooperation ensures robust transcriptional regulation and could prevent catastrophic structural failure after mitosis, underscoring the essential yet distinct contributions of both STAG paralogs to cohesin-mediated genome organization.

Methods

Cell culture

The parental G1E-ER4 cell line was kindly provided by the laboratory of Dr. Mitchell Weiss29. G1E cells are an immortalized GATA-1-deficient erythroblast cell line, and G1E-ER4 is a subclone stably expressing an estrogen-inducible GATA-1-ER fusion protein. G1E-ER4 cells were cultured in suspension in IMDM medium supplemented with 13% fetal bovine serum (FBS, v/v), 0.2 mM penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), 179 ng/ml murine stem cell factor, 3.47 ng/ml Epogen, and 0.001% 1-thioglycerol (v/v). The cell density was maintained below 1 × 106 cells/ml. G1E-ER4 sublines carrying endogenously tagged STAG1 or STAG2, including STAG1-mAID-GFP, STAG2-mAID-mCherry, the dual STAG1-mAID-GFP/STAG2-mAID-mCherry line, and STAG1-FLAG-P2A-HALO or STAG2-FLAG-P2A-HALO lines, were maintained under the same culture conditions as the parental line. All cell lines were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative.

CRISPR/Cas9 mediated genome editing

To generate Stag1mAID and Stag2mAID knock-in cell lines, ~800 bp homology arms flanking the stop codons of the Stag1 and Stag2 genes were PCR-amplified from the genomic DNA of G1E-ER4 cells and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. The coding sequences of GFP-mAID (for Stag1) or mCherry-mAID (for Stag2) were inserted immediately upstream of the stop codon to construct homology-directed repair (HDR) donor plasmids(Supplementary Data. 5. Supplementary Data. 6). Single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the 3′ ends of the Stag1 and Stag2 genes were designed using the Benchling online tool and cloned into the pX330 vector. G1E-ER4 cells were electroporated with 18 µg of the HDR donor plasmid and 18 µg of sgRNA-containing pX330 plasmid using an Amaxa 2b Nucleofector (Lonza) with program G-016. 24 to 48 hours post-electroporation, single cells with positive GFP or mCherry signals were sorted into 96-well plates using FACSAria III (BD Biosciences). Clones were expanded and genotyped by PCR using primers flanking the integration site (Supplementary Data. 4). Positive clones were further verified by Sanger sequencing.

To generate Stag1FLAG and Stag2FLAG knock-in cell lines, the same CRISPR/Cas9-mediated strategy was used. Briefly, ~800 bp homology arms flanking the stop codons of Stag1 or Stag2 were cloned into pcDNA3.1, and the FLAG-P2A-HALO cassette was inserted immediately upstream of the stop codon to generate HDR donor plasmids (Supplementary Data. 7. Supplementary Data. 8). sgRNAs targeting the 3′ ends of Stag1 or Stag2 were cloned into pX330. G1E-ER4 cells were electroporated with the corresponding donor and sgRNA-containing pX330 plasmids using the Amaxa 2b Nucleofector (Lonza) with program G-016. Clones were isolated and validated by PCR genotyping and Sanger sequencing as described above. These cell lines were used for quantitative comparison of the relative abundance of STAG1 and STAG2 proteins.

To generate the double knock-in Stag1mAID Stag2mAID cell line, Stag2mAID cells were electroporated with donor and sgRNA containing pX330 plasmids corresponding to Stag1 using Amaxa 2b Nucleofector (Lonza) with program G-016. Genotyping and validation were performed as described above.

To enable rapid degradation of STAG1 or/and STAG2, OsTIR2 was introduced into Stag1mAID, Stag2mAID, and Stag1mAID Stag2mAID cells by retroviral transduction25,34,62. Briefly, a retroviral vector encoding an OsTIR2-TagBFP fusion protein was co-transfected into HEK293T cells together with pCL-Eco packaging plasmid. Virus-containing supernatant was collected 48 and 72 h later. Target cells were infected using a spin infection protocol, which included polybrene and HEPES. 48 h after infection, cells expressing the TagBFP protein were isolated by sorting using a BD FACSAria III. The donor design information is provided in Supplementary file.

Cell growth curve

To examine the impact of STAG1 and/or STAG2 depletion on cell growth, 50,000 parental G1E-ER4, Stag1mAID, Stag2mAID or Stag1mAIDStag2mAID cells were seeded into untreated non-adhesive 6-well plates with 2 ml of fresh culture medium per well. Cells were then treated with or without 1 μM 5-Ph-IAA. Cell numbers for each condition were counted every 24 h over a total period of 96 h. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each condition.

Western Blotting

Western blotting was performed as follows63. 3 × 106 cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, p0013b) supplemented with 1:100 protease inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich, P8340) on ice for 20 min. Cell lysates were sonicated using a QSonica Q800R3 sonicator (40% amplitude, 5 seconds ON and 5 seconds OFF) for 5 min. After clarification, protein samples were subjected to SDS–PAGE and transferred to membranes for immunoblotting. Membranes were probed with antibodies against STAG1 (Abcam, ab317301, 1:1000) and STAG2 (Cell Signaling Technology, #58822, 1:1000), followed by imaging using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System.

For chromatin fractionation, subcellular fractionation was performed using the Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit for Cultured Cells (Thermo Scientific, 78840) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were sequentially fractionated to obtain cytoplasmic, nucleoplasmic, and chromatin-bound fractions. Equal amounts of protein from each fraction were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, boiled at 100 °C for 10 min, centrifuged at 13,000 g for 1 min, and then subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

The following primary antibodies were used for immunoblotting: STAG1 (Abcam, ab317301, 1:1000), STAG2 (Cell Signaling Technology, #58822, 1:1000), RAD21 (Abcam, ab992, 1:1000), SMC1 (Bethyl, A300-055A, 1:1000), and CTCF (Sigma, 07-729, 1:1000).

Western blot band intensities were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ). For chromatin fractionation experiments, only target protein signals in the chromatin fraction were quantified and normalized to histone H3. For detection-range calibration, serial dilutions of untreated 0 h chromatin-fraction samples were used to generate a linear standard curve for each target protein by plotting band intensity against relative input. For each 5-Ph-IAA-treated sample, the target-protein signal was converted to an equivalent relative input using the corresponding standard curve. This value was then corrected for loading by multiplying by the ratio of the H3 signal in the 0 h 100% lane to that in the matched 4 h lane, yielding the final residual chromatin-bound protein level. For all other conventional and fluorescent Western blot assays, target protein signals were normalized to β-actin. Fluorescent western blot images were acquired using a LI-COR Odyssey CLx Imaging System. For quantification of FLAG-based fluorescent western blots, only the lane loaded with 30 μg of protein from each sample was used. Integrated density was measured in Fiji (ImageJ). After background subtraction, the signal intensity of each target protein was normalized to that of the corresponding loading control. Normalized values were used for subsequent statistical analysis and data visualization.

Nocodazole mediated prometaphase arrest/release and protein degradation

To examine post-mitotic chromatin re-configuration in the absence of STAG1 and/or STAG2, nocodazole-mediated prometaphase arrest, release, and subsequent cell sorting of Stag1mAID, Stag2mAID or Stag1mAIDStag2mAID cells were synchronized by nocodazole-mediated prometaphase arrest, released into G1, and subsequently sorted25,62. Toward the end of nocodazole arrest, 5-Ph-IAA (1 μM) was applied to these cells for 1 h. After nocodazole treatment, cells were released into warm fresh medium containing 5-Ph-IAA but not nocodazole for 1 h (early-G1) and 4 h (late-G1), respectively. Cells without auxin treatment were processed in parallel as controls.

In-situ Hi-C

In-situ Hi-C was performed using a DpnII-based in situ proximity ligation protocol63 Briefly, formaldehyde-fixed cells (105) were lysed on ice. Nuclei were isolated, washed, and chromatin was opened with SDS at 65 °C for 10 min. SDS activity was quenched with Triton X-100, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Chromatin was digested overnight with DpnII (25 U) at 37 °C, with an additional 25 U added for 2 h the next day. DpnII was inactivated at 65 °C for 30 min. DNA ends were filled-in using Klenow fragment, with dGTP, dCTP, dTTP and biotin-14-dATP at 37 °C. Proximity ligation was performed with T4 DNA ligase (2000 U) at 16 °C (4 h) and then 25 °C (2 h). Crosslinks were reversed overnight with proteinase K and SDS at 65 °C, followed by RNase digestion. DNA was extracted and sonicated (15 s ON / 15 s OFF, 12.5 min in total). Biotinylated fragments were captured using pre-washed Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin beads. Beads were washed sequentially with 1×B&W buffer and T4 ligase buffer. End repair, adapter ligation and bead washing were performed using Vazyme general MGI library construction kit (N607). Bound DNA was eluted in 0.1% SDS at 98 °C and purified using 1.8× VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411-02). DNA was PCR amplified (9 cycles), purified with 0.9×VAHTS DNA Clean Beads, and quantified by Qubit. Final libraries were sequenced on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing (ChIP-seq)

ChIP-seq experiments were performed using 293T-cell spike-in controls63. Briefly, for each IP, 1 × 107 cells were mixed with 293 T cells corresponding to 10% of the total cell number as spike-in controls prior to lysis. Cells were lysed in cold cell lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, P8340) and PMSF (Sangon Biotech, A100754) on ice for 20 min. Nuclei were then lysed in nuclear lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) containing protease inhibitors on ice for 20 min. Chromatin was sonicated using a QSonica Q800R3 sonicator (80% amplitude, 20 s ON / 40 s OFF, 8 min total). The sonicated chromatin was diluted with 4 volumes of IP dilution buffer and pre-cleared for 4–6 h at 4 °C with 25 μl protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz, sc-2003). Pre-cleared chromatin was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with beads coupled to the indicated antibodies. The antibodies used for ChIP included STAG1 (Abcam, ab4455 or ab317301), STAG2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 5882), RAD21 (Abcam, ab992), CTCF (Sigma, 07-729), and NIPBL (Bethyl, A301-779A).

Following the overnight incubation, beads were washed sequentially with IP wash buffer 1 (20 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS), high-salt wash buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.01% SDS), IP wash buffer 2 (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP-40/Igepal, 1% sodium deoxycholate), and TE buffer. Bound DNA was eluted in 200 μl elution buffer (100 mM NaHCO₃, 1% SDS) for 5 min at 65 °C. Crosslinks were reversed by incubating the samples overnight at 65 °C. Samples were then treated with 2 μl RNase A (10 mg/ml) at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by 3 μl proteinase K (20 mg/ml) at 65 °C for 2 h. DNA was purified using an Omega PCR clean-up kit, eluted in 60 μl water, and used for library preparation with the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme, ND610). Final libraries were sequenced on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Micro-HiChIP

Micro-HiChIP was performed by combining double crosslinking, chromatin immunoprecipitation and proximity ligation38,39. Briefly, 1×107 to 2×107 cells were collected and resuspended in 9 ml PBS. Crosslinking was first initiated by adding 37% formaldehyde to a final concentration of 1%, followed by incubation at room temperature (RT) on a roller for 10 min. The reaction was quenched by addition of ice-cold 2 M glycine to a final concentration of 0.25 M and incubation for 5 min at RT. Cells were then washed once with 10 ml ice-cold PBS and resuspended in 1× PBS containing 3 mM EGS (MCE, HY-130458) at a density of 1 × 106 cells/ml. A second round of crosslinking was carried out for 40 min at RT, followed by quenching with 2 M glycine to a final concentration of 0.4 M for 10 min at RT. After one wash with 1 × PBS containing 0.5% BSA, pellets were either processed immediately or snap-frozen and stored at -80 °C.

Double-crosslinked samples were split into aliquots of 5 × 106 cells for parallel processing. Cells were lysed in 1 ml ice-cold cell lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40/Igepal) supplemented with 1×protease inhibitor cocktail (Yeasen, 20135ES03) for 20 min on ice. After centrifugation, chromatin was digested with 35 U MNase (NEB, M0247S) in 400 μl MNase reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 0.1 mM CaCl₂) for 30 min at 37 °C with shaking at 850 rpm. Digestion was stopped by adding EGTA to a final concentration of 5 mM, followed by incubation at 65 °C for 10 min. Nuclei were then washed once with 0.2 ml ice-cold complete MB#2 buffer (50 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl₂, 100 μg/ml BSA).

End repair and proximity ligation were performed in a single reaction containing 0.1 mM dTTP (Diamond, BN10057-0002), 0.1 mM dGTP (Diamond, BN10057-0004), biotin-14-dATP (Active Motif, 14139), biotin-11-dCTP (MCE, HY-D1668), 1× T4 ligase buffer, 2.5 mM EGTA, 10 U Klenow fragment (NEB, M0210L), 60 U T4 DNA ligase (Thermo, EL0013), and 20 U T4 PNK (NEB, M0201L). The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h with shaking at 850 rpm, and then at 16 °C overnight with shaking at 200 rpm. To remove biotin from unligated DNA ends, proximity-ligated chromatin was resuspended in 200 μl 1× NEBuffer 1 (NEB, B7001S) containing 200 U Exonuclease III (NEB, M0206L) and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Nuclei were subsequently lysed in 200 μl nuclei lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail) and sonicated using a Qsonica Q800R3 sonicator (80% amplitude, 20 s ON / 40 s OFF, 5 min total ON time).

The resulting supernatant was diluted at a ratio of 1:4 with IP dilution buffer (1% Triton X-100, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% SDS, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). Immunoprecipitation was carried out by incubating the diluted chromatin overnight at 4 °C with rotation in the presence of antibodies against STAG1 (Abcam, ab317301) or STAG2 (CST, #58822). Antibody-bound chromatin was then captured with 30 μl pretreated Protein A/G magnetic beads (Vazyme, PB101) for 4 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed twice with IP Wash Buffer 1 (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS), twice with High-Salt Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.01% SDS), once with IP Wash Buffer 2 (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 1% Igepal, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA), and twice with 1× TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA).

Chromatin was eluted and reverse-crosslinked by incubating the beads overnight at 65 °C in 200 μl elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA, 300 mM NaCl, 1% SDS, 300 μg/ml Proteinase K). DNA was purified using the ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo, D5201) and eluted in 140 μl TE buffer.

Library construction, including end repair, dA-tailing, and adapter ligation, was carried out on beads using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina or MGI (Vazyme, ND610-02 / NDM607-02) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were amplified for 9–15 PCR cycles using VAHTS HiFi Amplification Mix, purified with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411), and sequenced on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Transient transcriptome sequencing (TT-seq)

Cells were synchronized and released based on the aforementioned nocodazole arrest/release protocol. To metabolically label newly synthesized RNA, 4-thiouridine (4sU; Sigma Aldrich, T4509) was added to the cell culture medium to a final concentration of 500 µM. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min before harvesting. ~1 × 107 cells were collected at 1 h and 4 h after mitosis. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596026CN), followed by chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation. The resulting RNA pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water. The RNA was then fragmented using NaOH, immediately followed by neutralization with Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), DTT (1 mM; Sigma Aldrich, 43815), and sodium acetate (pH 5.5). The fragmented RNA was purified by isopropanol precipitation. Fragmented RNA was biotinylated by incubating it with EZ-Link HPDP-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21341) with gentle rotation. After chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation, the biotinylated RNA was captured using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To remove unbound RNA, the beads were washed sequentially with a 1×B&W buffer (200 µl) and high-salt buffer (200 µl). The captured RNA was eluted with 100 mM DTT, purified, and then resuspended in RNase-free water. Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 100 ng of the purified RNA using the VAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for MGI (Vazyme, NRM605), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The prepared libraries were then sequenced on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

To exclude the possibility that the transcriptional changes observed in our depletion experiments were caused by 5-Ph-IAA treatment itself, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in control cells lacking any mAID-tagged endogenous proteins. Cells were treated with or without 5-Ph-IAA for 4 h, and then directly collected in TRIzol reagent for total RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified RNA samples were subsequently submitted to a commercial sequencing provider for library preparation and next-generation sequencing. The resulting RNA-seq data were used to evaluate transcriptomic changes induced by 5-Ph-IAA treatment alone.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Total protein samples from G1E-ER4 cells were prepared in three independent biological replicates and subjected to DIA-based LC–MS/MS analysis to estimate the relative abundance of STAG1 and STAG2. No separate treatment-control group was included, as STAG1 and STAG2 peptide abundances were quantified from the same total-protein samples. Proteins were processed using a filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, protein samples were loaded onto 10-kDa molecular-weight cutoff filters (Microcon 10 K, Millipore, MRCPRT010) and washed three times with denaturation buffer containing 8 M urea and 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate to remove detergents and residual solvents. Proteins were reduced and alkylated with TCEP and chloroacetamide in denaturation buffer for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark. After removal of residual urea, TCEP and chloroacetamide by repeated washes with digestion buffer, proteins were digested on-filter with 1 μg trypsin at 37 °C for 16 h. Peptides were collected by centrifugation, dried using a SpeedVac and stored at -80 °C before LC–MS/MS analysis. Before injection, peptides were reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid and centrifuged to remove insoluble material.

NanoLC–MS/MS analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system through an EASY-Spray ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated using an EASY-Spray capillary column (2 μm, 150 μm × 15 cm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, ES906) with a guard column (3 μm, 300 μm × 5 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 174500). Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile. Peptides were separated over a 24-min gradient. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive-ion DIA mode with a spray voltage of 2,100 V. MS1 spectra were acquired at 240,000 resolution over a scan range of 380-980 Th, with an AGC target of 500% and a maximum injection time of 5 ms. MS2 spectra were acquired with an AGC target of 500%, an isolation window of 2 Th, a maximum injection time of 3 ms, a cycle time of 0.6 s, normalized collision energy of 25%, and an MS2 scan range of 150-2,000 Th.

Raw files were analyzed using Spectronaut 19.0 (Biognosys) in direct DIA mode with default BGS factory settings, with cross-run normalization disabled. Spectra were searched against the Mus musculus UniProt reference proteome database (UP000000589_10090, 21,856 entries). Trypsin/P was specified as the digestion enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. The maximum number of modifications per peptide was set to five. Protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were set as variable modifications, and cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification. The DirectDIA+ (Deep) workflow was used, with the false discovery rate for peptide-spectrum matches, peptides and protein groups set to 0.01. Quantification was performed at the MS2 level using peak area. STAG1 and STAG2 relative abundance was estimated from unique peptide intensities across the three biological replicates. Statistical analysis of STAG1 and STAG2 peptide levels was performed as described in the figure legend (Supplementary Data. 9).

Quantification and data analysis

ChIP-seq

Paired-end reads were first aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) with default parameters and soft clipping enabled (“--local”). Reads that did not map to the human genome were subsequently aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm9). Low-quality alignments (MAPQ < 30), PCR duplicates, and reads mapped to mitochondrial DNA, unplaced contigs, or ENCODE blacklisted regions were removed using SAMtools (v1.9) and BEDTools (v2.27.1). For data normalization, reads were processed using the bamCoverage function from deepTools (v3.1.3) with the normalization method set to counts per million mapped reads (CPM).

For spike-in calibrated normalization (SCF), the workflow involves first aligning sequencing reads to both the mouse (mm9) and human (hg19) genomes. Unique human reads, originating exclusively from the spike-in material, are then counted to estimate relative recovery efficiency between samples and converted to scaling factor through the equation: scaling factor = constant/human reads (constant as 2000000 in our projects). The resulting scale factors are then applied to the corresponding mouse aligned bam files. In practice, normalized signal tracks were generated using the bamCoverage tool from the deepTools suite, which converts aligned bam files into bigWig format. The scaling factors were passed through the --scaleFactor option, resulting in calibrated signal tracks that enable accurate cross-sample comparison of chromatin binding patterns. This approach has been shown to significantly reduce batch effects and improve the reproducibility of ChIP-seq analysis.

Peak calling

Peak calling was conducted separately for each biological replicate using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) with default parameters and a q-value cutoff of 0.01 for narrow peaks. The corresponding input samples were used as background controls for MACS2 peak calling.

Hi-C and Micro-HiChIP data preprocessing

Hi-C data from each biological replicate and replicate-merged samples were processed using the HiC-Pro (v3.0.0) pipeline25,34,62,63. Sequencing reads were first mapped to the mouse reference genome (mm9) using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) with optimized global and local alignment parameters (For global alignment, the parameters were --very-sensitive -L 30 --score-min L, -0.6,-0.2 --end-to-end --reorder, and for local alignment, the parameters were --very-sensitive -L 20 --score-min L, -0.6,-0.2 --end-to-end --reorder). The resulting alignments were then filtered to identify valid interaction pairs by removing invalid ligation products, self-ligated fragments, and PCR duplicates. The filtered pairs were subsequently converted into.hic and.cool file formats for visualization with Juicebox and for quantitative analysis with HiCExplorer (v3.7), respectively. All contact maps, saddle plots, and aggregate peak analysis (APA) plots in the main figures were generated from merged datasets of all biological replicates.

Micro-HiChIP data were processed using Microcket-1.4. Raw sequencing data in fastq.gz format were analyzed with Microcket-1.4 using the mouse reference genome (mm9) with the parameters -g mm9, while all other parameters were kept at their default settings. This step generated a.final.pairs file for each sample. The.final.pairs files were then converted into.hic format using juicer_tools_1.22.01.jar pre with the resolution settings -r 2500000, 1000000, 500000, 250000, 100000, 50000, 25000, 10000, 5000, 2500, 1000, using mm9 as the reference genome and default values for all other parameters. The resulting.hic files were used for downstream visualization and analyses.

Eigenvector decomposition and saddle plots

The first eigenvector (EV1) was derived from a Pearson correlation matrix generated from 25 kb-binned, Knight-Ruiz (KR)-balanced cis-interaction matrices. The orientation of the EV1 was determined based on gene density. Following this, saddle plots were generated to assess chromatin compartmentalization strength. Contact probability decay curves (PS curves)

For each sample, the contact probability decay curve PS was calculated using the “expected-cis” function of Cooltools (v0.4.0).

Loop calling

Hi-C loops were called from KR-balanced contact maps using the HICCUPS62,63. Briefly, preliminary loops were identified from KR-balanced contact maps using the HICCUPS function in juicer_tools (v1.22.01) at two resolutions: 10 kb and 25 kb. Specific parameters were used for each resolution: for 10 kb, the parameters were p = 4 bins, i = 16 bins, and an FDR of 0.2, while for 25 kb, the parameters were p = 5 bins, i = 15 bins, and an FDR of 0.1. Because redundant loops can be called across different samples, a greedy clustering algorithm was used to merge them. Loops were first ranked in ascending order based on their minimum q-value across all samples. Starting with the top-ranked loop, a search was performed for nearby pixels within a 20 kb radius. If found, these pixels were grouped into a loop cluster, and their centroid was calculated. This process was repeated iteratively until all loops were processed. For each cluster, the pixel with the lowest q-value was designated as the cluster summit, representing the final non-redundant loop for that resolution. This step was performed for both 10 kb and 25 kb identified loops. The non-redundant loop lists from the 10 kb and 25 kb resolutions were then merged. During this step, if a 25 kb loop cluster overlapped with a 10 kb loop cluster, the 25 kb loop was removed to avoid redundancy and prioritize higher-resolution data.

To validate that our loop-based conclusions were not dependent on a single calling framework, loops were also identified using the dots function in cooltools as an alternative to HICCUPS. Loop calling was performed on 10 kb-resolution.cool files using precomputed cis expected contact frequency tables for each sample. The key parameters were set as follows: --max-loci-separation 5000000, --clustering-radius 20000, and --fdr 0.1. Dots called by cooltools were used as an independent loop set for downstream comparison with the HICCUPS-derived results.

Loop calling for Micro-HiChIP data was performed separately from the Hi-C pipeline. Briefly, contact pairs from each sample were first converted into.hic files using juicer_tools v1.22.01. Preliminary loop candidates were then identified with the hiccups function in juicer_tools_1.22.01.jar at 10 kb resolution using the following parameters: -m 1024 -r 10000 -f 0.2 -p 4 -i 16 -d 10000 --ignore-sparsity. To obtain a unified candidate loop set across samples, preliminary loops from all samples were pooled and consolidated by iterative distance-based merging, such that nearby loop calls were grouped into shared candidate clusters and represented as unified loop regions spanning the outermost anchors within each cluster. Only merged candidate loops with genomic spans greater than 50 kb were retained in the final request list. Next, loop calling was repeated in each sample using hiccups with the same parameters as above, but restricted to the specified candidate regions provided in the request list. Finally, the loop calls from this second round were merged across samples to generate the final merged loop table, which was used for downstream analyses.

Loop partition

Structural loops were defined as those with both anchors occupied by CTCF and cohesin ChIP-seq peaks and at most 1 anchor occupied by CREs.

Structural loops were classified hierarchically based on their nesting relationships. A singleton loop is a structural loop that exists independently, neither containing nor being contained within another loop. A loop that is nested within another but contains no loops of its own is classified as a Tier 1 primary structural loop. A Tier 2 structural loop directly encompasses a Tier 1 loop without any intermediate loops. This classification scheme extends to higher tiers, where each tier represents a structural loop that directly contains a loop from the tier immediately below it.

Primary tier1 structural loops were further classified based on their local chromatin contexts. For this analysis, the internal ChIP-seq signals for H3K27ac, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, and H3K9me3 were quantified for each structural loop. Each histone modification signal was normalized genome-wide, transformed into z-scores, and then subjected to unsupervised k-means clustering. This clustering resulted in three distinct clusters:

Cluster 1: Characterized by enrichment of H3K27ac, indicative of active euchromatin.

Cluster 2: Enriched for H3K27me3, corresponding to facultative heterochromatin.

Cluster 3: Marked by H3K9me3 enrichment, representing constitutive heterochromatin.

Aggregated plots for loops and domains

Aggregated plots were generated using the Python package Coolpup (v1.1.1). For unscaled aggregated peak analysis, loops smaller than 20 kb were removed from the plots to avoid influence from pixels close to the diagonal.

TT-seq preprocessing and differential expression analysis

TT-seq reads were processed to quantify gene expression in a strand-specific manner. First, sequencing reads were aligned to the longest RefSeq-annotated mm9 mouse reference genome transcript set using STAR v2.7.10b. After alignment, quality control and filtering were performed with Samtools v1.6 using the parameters “-q 10 -f 0×2” to remove low-quality alignments. PCR duplicates were subsequently removed using Picard v2.25.5. Next, the strand-specific BAM files were separated into forward and reverse strands. Forward-strand reads were isolated using the Samtools parameters “-f 128 -F 16 -f 80”, and reverse-strand reads were isolated using “-f 144 -f 64 -F 16”. BigWig files were then generated using the bamCoverage function in deepTools v3.5.4. The multicov function in bedtools v2.31.1 was used to obtain fragment counts across genomic features, which served as the basis for gene quantification. For positive-strand genes, forward-strand reads were quantified, while for negative-strand genes, reverse-strand reads were used.

Differential expression analysis was carried out in R using the DESeq2 package v1.42.1. Genes were classified based on their statistical significance and fold change. Specifically, genes with an adjusted P value (padj) <0.05 and a log2 fold change (log2FC) > log2(1.5) were identified as upregulated. Conversely, genes with a padj <0.05 and a log2FC < -log2(1.5) were considered downregulated. Genes that did not meet these criteria were classified as non-differentially expressed.

ABC model predicts enhancer-promoter interactions

To predict enhancers of active genes and establish promoter–enhancer (P–E) and promoter–promoter (P–P) connections, we applied the Activity-by-Contact (ABC) model (https://github.com/broadinstitute/ABC-Enhancer-Gene-Prediction)64.Enhancer activity was estimated using H3K27ac ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq signals from asynchronously growing G1E-ER4 cells. This dataset was combined with replicate-merged Hi-C datasets from Stag1mAID, Stag2mAID, and Stag1mAIDStag2mAID cells. For each sample, a 5 kb resolution Hi-C contact matrix was used to calculate the contact frequency between each regulatory element and its target gene.

Enhancer activity was assessed across two post-mitotic cell-cycle stages, with or without 5-Ph-IAA treatment: early G1 (“–5-Ph-IAA” and “+5-Ph-IAA”) and late G1 (“–5-Ph-IAA” and “+5-Ph-IAA”), to predict P–E and P–P connections between enhancers and their target genes. The ABC score for an element on a gene was calculated by multiplying the enhancer activity by the contact frequency between the enhancer and the gene, and dividing this product by the sum of such products for all candidate elements within a 5 Mb window. Increasing the ABC score threshold reduces the number of interactions classified as high-confidence.

An ABC score threshold of 0.025 was applied consistently across all samples, and predicted connections from each sample were combined to generate a set of high-confidence P–E and P–P connections. An interaction was considered valid if its ABC score met the threshold in at least one sample. Each P–E or P–P pair was then mapped to its target gene and classified based on differential expression status (upregulated, downregulated, or non-significant). For comparisons between differentially expressed and non-significant genes, the non-significant group was defined using expression-matched controls. Specifically, for each differentially expressed gene, five non-differentially expressed genes with expression levels within ±10% of that gene were selected and included in the ABC-based analysis. This strategy enabled a more accurate comparison of promoter–enhancer interaction strength between differentially expressed and non-significant genes while minimizing bias caused by differences in basal expression level.

Calculation of intra-loop contact frequency

To determine the intra-loop contact frequency, pixels within each structural loop domain were extracted. The average observed/expected value across all pixels within a given loop domain was then calculated. This average represents the intra-loop contact frequency for that specific loop.

Statistics and reproducibility

In this study, sample size was not pre-determined using statistical methods and no data was excluded. No randomization was performed. Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Data distribution was assumed to be normal although not formally tested. All information related to statistical analyses including test method, P values and the number of samples were included in the figure legends. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (v4.3.2). Plots were generated either using R software (v4.3.2) or using Graphpad Prism 9.5.
