Proteomic and metabolomic correlates of sociodemographic factors and their associations with kidney and cardiovascular outcomes: findings from the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort
Authors: Insa M. Schmidt, Aditya Surapaneni, Ashish Verma, Sarah Arbaugh, Lawrence J. Appel, Casey M. Rebholz, Tessa K. Novick, Runqi Zhao, Carina M. Flaherty, Eugene P. Rhee, Josef Coresh, Sushrut S. Waikar, Morgan E. Grams, the CRIC Study Investigators
Published: 2027

Introduction

Adverse social conditions, including lower socioeconomic status, limited access to health care, neighborhood disadvantage, and chronic psychosocial stress, have consistently been associated with higher kidney and cardiovascular disease burden and risk of disease progression. To explore potential biological pathways linking socioeconomic disadvantage to adverse health outcomes, we investigated associations of plasma proteins and metabolites with measures of SES and adverse kidney and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD).

Methods

We tested associations of 4091 plasma proteins and 605 metabolites with two separate measures of SES, education and income, in individuals from the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) study. The CRIC study is a prospective, multicenter cohort of adults aged 21-74 years with mild to moderate CKD. Cross-sectional associations were evaluated using race-stratified (Black and non-Black due to low representation of other races) multivariable-adjusted linear regression models in 3247 participants with available proteomic (SomaScan) and 1800 with available metabolomic (Broad Institute) data. Cox proportional hazards models assessed the relationships between SES-associated proteins and metabolites and kidney disease progression (kidney replacement therapy or 50% eGFR decline) [1], incident cardiovascular disease (CVD), incident congestive heart failure (CHF), and all-cause death. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, diabetes, systolic blood pressure, ACE-inhibitor/ARB/aspirin/statin use, prior CVD, LDL cholesterol, eGFR, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, and clinical center. Statistical significance was determined determined using Bonferroni-corrected thresholds for proteinsand metabolites. Analyses were performed in Stata 18.0.

Results

Among participants with proteomic measurements, 44% were female, 41% were Black, and mean ± standard deviation eGFR was 43±16 ml/min/1.73 m²; characteristics were similar among those with metabolomics measurements (Fig. 1A). After Bonferroni correction, higher income was associated with higher levels of protein C (PROC) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGF-R), along with lower levels of transcobalamin-1 (TCN1), adenosine deaminase CECR1 (CECR1), and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 1 (TREM1), with similar directionality in both Black and non-Black individuals (Fig. 1B). Higher education was associated with higher levels of phosphatidylethanolamines (PE) C34:0 and C38:2, as well as sphingomyelin (SM) C14:0 in Black individuals, with concordant associations for C34:0 PE and C14:0 SM in non-Black individuals (Fig. 1C). Discussion

In this study, we used proteomic and metabolomic profiling to identify molecular markers associated with SES in a cohort of individuals with CKD. Several proteins and metabolites involved in inflammation, lipid metabolism, and vascular health were associated with SES measures, indicating that these molecular patterns may represent correlates of cumulative social and environmental exposures linked to social disadvantage. A large population-based analysis of cohorts from the UK, Finland, and the United States has also demonstrated that social disadvantage is associated with increased risk of age-related diseases, including diabetes, heart failure, and CKD, as well as with proteomic signatures of accelerated aging, particularly within immune and kidney pathways [2]. Compared to prior studies conducted predominantly in general population cohorts, our findings extend this evidence to a high-risk CKD population using large-scale proteomics and metabolomics assessments.

In our study, both PROC and EGF-R were associated with higher SES, and higher levels of each were linked to lower risks of adverse outcomes. PROC is a vitamin K–dependent anticoagulant with anti-inflammatory properties. Lower SES has been shown to be associated with chronic inflammation and pro-thrombotic biomarkers, including elevated C-reactive protein and fibrinogen, which may help explain our observation that individuals with CKD and higher SES have higher PROC levels that may indicate a lower inflammatory burden and a more favorable hemostatic profile. Similarly, EGF and its receptor EGF-R play essential roles in renal cell survival and regeneration, with higher EGF levels indicating preserved tubular and regenerative capacity. In this context, higher EGF-R levels observed in participants with higher SES may reflect better tubular health and greater metabolic resilience. A prior proteomic study of socioeconomic disparity in CVD in the general population also found EGFR to be positively associated with higher educational attainment, supporting the consistency of this finding across different disease contexts [3].

For three proteins, including TCN1, CECR1, and TREM-1, we observed an association with lower SES, and these same proteins were also linked to higher risks of adverse outcomes. TCN1 (also known as haptocorrin) is a binding protein for vitamin B12 that is stored in neutrophil granules, which can rise in inflammatory states and malignancies. Disturbances in vitamin B12 metabolism are also common in CKD. CECR1 (adenosine deaminase 2) functions as a monocyte-derived enzyme that modulates adenosine receptor signaling in immune and endothelial cells, with downstream effects on immune activity and vascular integrity. Higher levels of CECR1 have been described in the serum of patients with SLE and correlated with clinical disease activity [4]. TREM1 is an immune receptor on neutrophils and monocytes that is typically upregulated during acute infections. In experimental CKD models, it has been shown to modulate macrophage polarization, thereby mediating tubular injury [5]. Individuals in socioeconomically disadvantaged settings may be exposed to higher levels of infections, stress, and pollutants, leading to persistent low-grade activation of innate immune pathways, with the elevated markers observed in this study potentially reflecting greater microinflammation and increased susceptibility to endothelial dysfunction over time.

Metabolomic studies have increasingly shown that social conditions shape circulating metabolic patterns, reflecting differences in inflammation, diet, and chronic disease risk. Lower SES has been associated with metabolic profiles characterized by heightened inflammation and oxidative stress. A study in 6400 women identified over 260 metabolites that differed by race and ethnicity, largely within lipid pathways, which correlated with coronary heart disease risk [6]. We observed that higher socioeconomic status was associated with three lipid species. Prior studies showed that PE C34:0 and SM C14:0 were associated with dairy and lower meat consumption, dietary patterns that were associated with lower type 2 diabetes risk [7].

Several limitations warrant consideration. SES is multidimensional, and our use of income and educational attainment alone may not fully capture social disadvantage. Although we adjusted for key clinical covariates, residual confounding or potential mediation by some of these variables cannot be excluded. It is also possible that the identified molecular findings may reflect disease susceptibility associated with lower SES rather than direct biological effects of SES itself. Future analyses incorporating external validation and longitudinal proteomic and metabolomic data will be essential to determine whether these molecular markers may also act as mediators on the pathway from SES to adverse outcomes.

In conclusion, large-scale omics investigations revealed proteins and metabolites involved in inflammation, lipid metabolism, and vascular health that are associated with measures of SES. Several proteins were also associated with future disease risk, providing insights into the biological pathways linking socioeconomic disadvantage to adverse health outcomes in CKD.
