Efficient deep learning framework for arecanut disease detection using graph neural network and Bat algorithm
Authors: Finney Daniel Shadrach, Deepa Devasenapathy, R. Anitha, M. Lekshmana Kumar, Kiran Siripuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early and accurate detection of plant diseases remains challenging due to real-field variability (e.g., illumination variations, complex backgrounds, occlusion), irregular disease patterns (e.g., subtle or non-local symptoms), limited labeled data for niche crops like arecanut, and generalization issues in uncontrolled environments. Traditional manual inspections are labor-intensive and error-prone, while existing deep learning methods—primarily grid-based CNNs and Vision Transformers—often suffer from limited spatial modeling of non-Euclidean relationships, higher computational costs, and reduced robustness to field conditions. This research introduces a novel deep learning framework for automated detection of diseases in arecanut plants, combining Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for capturing long-range spatial relationships in leaf images with the Bat Algorithm (BA) for efficient hyperparameter optimization. The framework utilizes a curated balanced subset of 1000 arecanut images from a larger Kaggle dataset (originally 8847 images), captured under natural farm conditions and encompassing nine disease categories. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed GNN-BA (GB) model achieves 98.45% accuracy, 96.90% precision, 94.21% recall, and 95.05% F1-score—outperforming baselines such as CNN-ViT (93.25% accuracy) and CBAM (94.10% accuracy) by 4–5% on average, while offering lower computational overhead. The model exhibits robustness across diverse environmental and leaf variations, providing a scalable, efficient solution for real-time disease monitoring to enable timely interventions and reduce crop losses in arecanut farming.

Introduction

India is the world’s largest producer of arecanut (Areca catechu L.), also known as betel nut, contributing over 50% of global production with an annual output exceeding 1.2 million tons1. However, the rapid expansion of cultivation areas has heightened concerns over infections, particularly areca yellow leaf disease (AYLD), which damages plants and reduces yields by up to 50% within three years of infection2. In India, AYLD has affected over 10,000 hectares since the 1970s, leading to significant economic losses estimated at millions of dollars annually due to stunted growth, reduced nut size, and decreased production. Symptoms initially manifest as yellowing in the inner whorl, spreading outward and potentially causing plant death if untreated. Eradication is challenging, making removal of infected plants the primary control method3,24,25.

This recurring disease pattern underscores the urgent need for accurate, timely diagnostic tools to mitigate economic impacts and enhance yields4,5. Traditional manual inspections are labor-intensive, error-prone, and inefficient for large-scale monitoring, often resulting in inconsistent assessments and delayed interventions6.

Deep learning methods for plant disease detection can be broadly classified into supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised approaches. Supervised methods, such as CNNs and Vision Transformers, dominate due to their reliance on labeled datasets and achieve high accuracy on controlled data7,8. Unsupervised techniques, including self-supervised frameworks like Masked Autoencoders (MAE), enable learning from unlabeled images but may overlook fine-grained spatial relationships8. Semi-supervised methods offer promise for niche crops with limited labeled data by leveraging unlabeled samples, as demonstrated in multimodal arc detection tasks with constrained datasets26.

Motivated by these challenges, this research develops an optimized deep learning framework (GB model) integrating Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to extract complex spatial features from leaf images and the Bat Algorithm (BA) for hyperparameter optimization. The key research question is: Can a GNN-BA hybrid outperform existing deep learning models in detecting AYLD and other arecanut diseases under real-field conditions? We hypothesize that leveraging graph-based learning for relational patterns and bio-inspired optimization will yield superior accuracy and efficiency.

Literature survey

Recent advancements in deep learning for plant disease detection have leveraged models like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs), but applications to niche crops like arecanut remain limited.

De Silva and Brown7 integrated ViT with CNN for multispectral plant disease classification, achieving 89.5% accuracy and 83.3% F1-score using balanced datasets. However, the reliance on specialized filters (visible and NIR) increases computational cost, limiting scalability for resource-constrained farms.

Wang et al.8 employed a self-supervised framework with Masked Autoencoder (MAE) and Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), attaining 99.61% accuracy on the CCMT dataset. While effective with limited labels, it overlooks spatial relationships in complex leaf structures, potentially underperforming on irregular diseases like AYLD.

Srivastava and Meena9 compared five DCNN models (e.g., MobileNetV2, DenseNet121) on Mendeley and PlantVillage datasets, using augmentation to mitigate overfitting. DenseNet121 achieved high accuracy (~ 95%), but the models were tested on generic crops, failing to generalize to tropical plants like arecanut.

Han et al.10 explored GANs (DCGAN and αβGAN) for synthetic image generation, combined with VGG16 for detection. The approach improved diversity but introduced variability in predictions for similar diseases due to attribute inconsistencies.

Prasannakumar and Latha11 proposed a contextual mask auto-encoder optimized with dynamic differential annealed optimization (PDI-CMAE-DDAOA), using PlantVillage data and features like kurtosis. It excelled in noise reduction but is computationally intensive and not tailored to field-captured images.

Jin et al.12 used PlanetScope satellite data with RF-optimized features for arecanut planting extraction, achieving a Kappa of 0.853. This spatial approach is valuable for large-scale monitoring but lacks fine-grained leaf-level disease classification.

Research gap

Despite progress, arecanut-specific models are scarce, with most studies focusing on staple crops like wheat or rice. Challenges include diverse backgrounds, illumination variations, and subtle disease differences. Recent GNN works show promise in graph modeling but rarely address tropical crops or integrate bio-inspired optimization for hyperparameters. This highlights the need for a hybrid GNN-BA system tailored to arecanut in field environments, which our study fulfills by demonstrating 4–6% improvements in key metrics.

Recent multimodal techniques have shown potential to enhance performance in limited-data regimes by simulating additional modalities (e.g., depth estimation from RGB images) to augment visual features. For instance, the Simulated Multimodal Framework leverages generative models to estimate face depth and improve RGB-based diagnosis accuracy in medical imaging tasks with scarce data27. Similar ideas could benefit agricultural applications, where RGB leaf images under varying field conditions suffer from illumination and occlusion challenges; future extensions of our GNN-BA model may incorporate simulated multimodal inputs for greater robustness.

Problems of existing systems

Conventional inspections are labor-intensive and error-prone. Labeled datasets for arecanut are limited, and generic models generalize poorly (e.g., < 90% accuracy on unseen tropical data). Moreover, existing systems often overlook class imbalance in real-world datasets, leading to biased predictions toward majority classes. Our approach mitigates this through class-aware augmentation and weighted loss, ensuring equitable performance across all nine arecanut disease categories.

Proposed system

In this section, a new deep learning-based framework is presented for the automated detection of diseases in the arecanut plant using GNN combined with the BA for hyperparameter optimization. The system works with a selected leaf image collection that shows different arecanut diseases taken in their original farm settings. The proposed system trains the GNN model to extract features discriminating disease categories, then BA optimization is applied to refine the parameters and improve the model performance and accelerate convergence. The arecanut Kaggle dataset13 is adopted, comprising 8847 images across nine classes: Healthy Leaf (604), Healthy Nut (1886), Healthy Trunk (1432), Mahali Koleroga (2563), Stem Bleeding (151), Bud Borer (144), Healthy Foot (50), Stem Cracking (540), Yellow Leaf Disease (1477). For this study, the subset was curated as a balanced selection via stratified sampling to ensure class balance subset of 1000 images was curated to focus on computational efficiency and real-field variability, with images resized to 256 × 256 RGB pixels captured in diverse farm conditions including varying soil types, climates, and pest infestations. The extensive dataset collection enables the researchers to create a predictive model estimating the crop yield and detecting the diseases in the cultivation of the arecanut plant. The preprocessed images are subsequently transformed into graph structures to serve as input for the GNN. This is achieved using the Simple Linear Iterative Clustering (SLIC) algorithm to generate approximately 100 superpixels, which form the nodes V of the graph G=(V,E). Each node vi​ is associated with a feature vector fvi​​ composed of the following descriptors extracted from its corresponding superpixel region: the mean and standard deviation of RGB and HSV color channels, texture features from a Local Binary Pattern (LBP) histogram, and the spatial coordinates (x, y) of the superpixel centroid. Edges E are constructed to model spatial relationships by connecting each node to its k = 8 nearest neighboring nodes based on the Euclidean distance between their centroids, forming a k-Nearest Neighbor (k-NN) graph. This construction allows the GNN to effectively capture both local features and long-range, non-Euclidean dependencies across the leaf surface.

Data augmentation

Data augmentation is applied to the arecanut leaf image dataset to achieve better performance across various environmental situations. Gaussian noise addition alongside random rotation horizontal and vertical flip, scaling, and brightness and contrast modification are included in the proposed data augmentation. Real-world images with artificial lighting variations and background clutter as well as leaf orientations in different positions made up the training data. The proposed methodology reduces overfitting risks because it enables the model to detect invariant features leading to accurate and trustworthy disease classification outcomes.

Class imbalance mitigation

To address the inherent class imbalance in the original dataset, a targeted strategy was employed. We applied class-aware data augmentation, where minority classes (e.g., ‘Bud Borer’, ‘Healthy Nut’) were subjected to a higher number of augmentation operations compared to majority classes, effectively balancing the class distribution within the training set. Furthermore, a weighted cross-entropy loss function was utilized during model training. The weight for each class was set to be inversely proportional to its frequency in the original training data, thereby penalizing misclassifications of minority classes more heavily and guiding the model to learn from all categories equally.

Feature extraction using GNN

The choice of a Graph Neural Network over a standard Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is motivated by the need to model complex, structured patterns of disease manifestation. While CNNs excel at capturing local, translation-invariant features through hierarchical convolutional filters, their receptive fields can be limited, especially in earlier layers. In contrast, the proposed GNN operates on a graph structure that represents the leaf as a set of interconnected regions. This allows the model to perform message passing, enabling it to directly model long-range, non-Euclidean dependencies between disparate and non-adjacent leaf regions—for instance, a relationship between a lesion on the leaf tip and a chlorotic pattern at the base—within a single layer. This capability is particularly advantageous for capturing the systemic and often spatially irregular nature of plant diseases, leading to more robust and interpretable feature extraction.

The sophisticated non-Euclidean structure modeling technique enables GNN to provide exceptional diagnosis of arecanut plant diseases using leaf picture spatial patterns14. The bats adjust their position through frequency-tuned velocity modification to achieve minimal validation loss of the model. Result and discussion

For the experimental evaluation, the original dataset of 1000 images was first stratified into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets. Data augmentation techniques were applied exclusively to the training set images to increase its size and variability, while the validation and test sets contained only original, non-augmented images to ensure a fair evaluation of the model’s generalization capability. All baseline models (CNN-ViT7, CBAM8, PDI-CMAE-DDAOA11, and SVM-BPNN12) were implemented and trained from scratch on our arecanut dataset using the same data splits and preprocessing pipeline to guarantee a equitable comparison. Their hyperparameters were tuned via a random search on the validation set.

During each training phase, the model is evaluated in real time using validation data supplied in batches. The model is trained to recognize the patterns in the training data, and its ability of generalization is assessed using unseen test data, providing a comprehensive evaluation of its performance on new inputs. To train and evaluate the model, a 64-bit version of Windows system is used. Python 3 serves as the primary programming language, with essential libraries such as Keras, OpenCV, and Matplotlib supporting the model development and performance comparison.

Class-wise performance analysis

To evaluate per-class efficacy, we report precision, recall, and F1-scores for each of the nine classes. The model excels on minority classes like ‘Bud Borer’ (F1 = 92.3%) due to class-aware augmentation, but shows slight degradation on Healthy Foot (F1 = 89.5%) owing to limited samples. This underscores the need for expanded datasets in future work.

In machine learning-based classification tasks, particularly multi-class problems like arecanut disease detection, overall metrics such as average accuracy or F1-score provide a high-level overview but can mask disparities in model performance across individual classes. Class-wise performance metrics address this by disaggregating results, revealing how well the model handles each category independently. Theoretically, this is rooted in the concept of model fairness and robustness, ensuring that the classifier does not exhibit bias toward majority classes (e.g., due to imbalanced datasets) while underperforming on minority ones. Precision measures the proportion of true positive predictions among all positive predictions for a class (TP / (TP + FP)), indicating the model’s ability to avoid false alarms. Recall, or sensitivity, quantifies the fraction of actual positives correctly identified (TP / (TP + FN)), crucial for early disease detection to minimize missed cases. The F1-score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall (2 * (Precision * Recall) / (Precision + Recall)), balances these trade-offs, providing a single metric for class-specific efficacy. In the context of precision agriculture, class-wise analysis is essential for validating the model’s utility in real-world scenarios, where rare diseases (e.g., ‘Bud Borer’) must be detected with high reliability to prevent economic losses. This table thus theoretically underscores the GB model’s equitable learning, achieved through techniques like class-aware augmentation and weighted loss, promoting generalizability across diverse disease manifestations.

Statistical significance tests

Statistical significance tests in comparative model evaluations serve to determine whether observed performance differences between algorithms are due to genuine improvements or mere random variation, thereby providing empirical rigor to claims of superiority. Grounded in hypothesis testing, these tests (e.g., paired t-tests) compare metrics like accuracy across repeated runs, assuming a null hypothesis (H0) of no difference and an alternative (H1) of a meaningful improvement. The p-value represents the probability of observing the data (or more extreme) under H0; values below a threshold (typically 0.05) lead to H0 rejection, indicating statistical significance. In this table, accuracy improvements are quantified as percentage gains, with associated p-values derived from t-tests on metrics from multiple experimental runs (e.g., five-fold cross-validation with different seeds). Theoretically, this approach draws from frequentist statistics to enhance reproducibility and mitigate overfitting risks, ensuring that the GB model’s advantages over baselines (e.g., CNN-ViT) are not artifacts of a single dataset split. For agricultural AI applications, such tests are vital to build trust in deployment, as they quantify the reliability of hyperparameter optimizations like BA, ultimately supporting evidence-based decisions in crop management and reducing type I/II errors in disease classification.

Discussion

Despite these strong quantitative results, real-world applicability requires considering potential failure modes and generalization challenges. While the proposed GB model achieves state-of-the-art performance on the curated arecanut dataset, potential failure cases include images with extreme occlusions (e.g., overlapping leaves or heavy shadows), severe background clutter, or very low-sample classes. These limitations stem from RGB-only input and class imbalance, partially mitigated by augmentation and weighted loss but not fully eliminated. Domain generalization remains a concern, as the Kaggle dataset, though field-captured, may not fully represent cross-farm, cross-season, or unseen cultivar variations; real-world multi-farm testing could reveal drops in performance.

Future improvements include integrating multispectral or hyperspectral data to capture subtle cues missed in visible RGB, inspired by simulated multimodal frameworks that enhance limited-data diagnosis23. Federated learning across farms would enable scalable, privacy-preserving training. Mobile/edge deployment (leveraging low 0.123 s inference) and transfer to related crops (e.g., coconut, betel vine) offer promising directions toward > 99% accuracy and broader precision agriculture impact.

Conclusion

This research develops an optimized DL framework that combines GNN with BA for early and precise detection of arecanut plant diseases. The hybrid approach enables effective hyperparameter adjustment, resulting in superior performance by detecting spatial and relational patterns in leaf images. Experimental results demonstrate the GB framework accomplishes greater performance than traditional models in precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy (outperforming baselines by 4–6%). The model is applicable to various agricultural settings as it effectively optimizes parameters. Ethically, this AI tool promotes accessibility for smallholder farmers by reducing biases through diverse augmentation. Limitations include reliance on RGB imagery, future multispectral integration could address subtle spectral signatures missed in visible light. In the future, we plan to enhance the model with mobile app deployment for farmers, federated learning across multi-farm datasets to improve scalability, and incorporation of multispectral data for > 99% accuracy, while exploring transfer learning to other understudied crops like betel vine.
