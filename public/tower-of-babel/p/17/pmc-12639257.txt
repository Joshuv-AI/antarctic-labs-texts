Can transient neonatal osteosclerosis be differentiated from malignant infantile osteopetrosis?
Authors: Sarah A. Ackah, Boaz Karmazyn, Alden Dewey, Michael J. Econs, Gang Peng, Corinne Parks-Schenck, Jodi L. Skiles, Paul Niziolek, Linda A. DiMeglio, Stuart J. Warden, Erik A. Imel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteosclerosis in infancy requires careful evaluation as it may indicate the presence of osteopetrosis. Osteopetrosis is a rare disorder of high bone density due to impaired osteoclast resorption. Infantile forms of osteopetrosis have neurological and hematopoietic defects. However, some infants may present with a transient neonatal osteosclerosis (TNO) appearance without skeletal consequences. The aim of this case series is to compare TNO with infantile osteopetrosis.Radiographic reports of infants less than 12 months old were searched for the terms osteosclerosis and osteopetrosis from 2006 to 2025 at a single tertiary care children's hospital. Serial radiographs performed clinically were extracted and assessed for resolution and the clinical features described.TNO was identified in 7 infants and osteopetrosis was identified in 6 infants. The children with TNO presented at younger age (median age 6 days) than those with osteopetrosis (median age 73 days). All infants with osteopetrosis had genetic confirmation (TCIRG1 n = 3, CLCN7 n = 2 [one dominant, one recessive], IKBKG n = 1). Four of 6 infants with autosomal recessive osteopetrosis had thrombocytopenia and 3 had hypocalcemia, 5 had elevated parathyroid hormone, and 3 had elevated alkaline phosphatase. One of the 7 infants with TNO had thrombocytopenia and 2 had mild hypocalcemia. Rachitic changes were present in 3 infants with osteopetrosis, and metaphyseal widening in all 6 infants, but none of the TNO infants had these features. In TNO, resolution was evident by mean age of 8.5 months (range 1 to 31 months).TNO and infantile osteopetrosis have distinguishing clinical, radiographic and laboratory features.

Introduction

Osteosclerosis is a radiologic descriptor indicating increased bone density. In neonates, transient neonatal osteosclerosis (TNO) is a rare, benign, and self-limited condition that has been described in only a few case reports (Hauck et al., 2024; Monaghan et al., 1991; Nadvi et al., 1999). TNO is characterized by diffusely dense, sclerotic bones seen on radiographs, without clinical symptoms or long-term sequelae related to the skeleton. In contrast, infantile osteosclerotic dysplasias—most commonly infantile osteopetrosis (IOP)—represent a group of rare, life-threatening skeletal disorders that require early recognition and intervention (Stark and Savarirayan, 2009; El-Sobky et al., 2016; Boudin and Van Hul, 2018; Even-Or and Stepensky, 2021).

Osteopetrosis is a rare bone disorder of abnormally dense bone due to impairments in either osteoclast number or their resorption activity which predisposes individuals to increased fracture risk. In the United States, the incidence of the infantile and adult forms is about 1:250,000 people and 1:20,000, respectively (Sobacchi et al., 2013). The infantile form typically presents in the first year of life with increased bone mineral density (BMD) that can cause macrocephaly, frontal bossing, exophthalmos, choanal stenosis, micrognathia and growth retardation. Other abnormalities may include neurological defects secondary to decreased cranial foramina size, leading to compression of cranial nerves (resulting in blindness or deafness), or of the spinal cord or blood vessels, as well as hydrocephalus, Chiari 1 malformations, and consequent respiratory and feeding difficulties (Stark and Savarirayan, 2009; Even-Or and Stepensky, 2021). Radiographic features include generalized osteosclerosis, Erlenmeyer flask deformities, and pathological fractures (Stark and Savarirayan, 2009; Even-Or and Stepensky, 2021). Often children with severe infantile osteopetrosis develop rickets with hypocalcemia, as the skeleton sequesters more than 99 % of total body calcium in osteopetrosis, limiting the ability to mobilize calcium (Gonen et al., 2013; Kaplan et al., 1993). Hypocalcemia may be severe with resulting seizures.

Bone marrow failure also occurs in osteopetrosis leading to extramedullary hematopoiesis and hepatosplenomegaly. Bone marrow biopsy is usually not recommended due to complications of the overly dense bone and high risk of osteomyelitis (Wu et al., 2017). Without stem cell transplant, death occurs in the infantile form due to complications of insufficient bone marrow production. Infantile osteopetrosis can be successfully treated with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation to replace the osteoclast pool with normally functioning osteoclasts (Even-Or and Stepensky, 2021); however, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is associated with high rates of engraftment failure and other end-organ complications including mortality.

Infantile osteopetrosis is typically recessive and caused by pathogenic variants in several genes. The most common causes of severe osteopetrosis in infancy are recessive variants in T cell immune regulator 1 (TCIRG1) representing about 60 % of reported cases, followed by chloride channel 7 (CLCN7), osteopetrosis-associated transmembrane protein 1 (OSTM1), pleckstrin homology domain-containing protein family member 1 (PLEKHM1), carbonic anhydrase II (CA2) and sorting nexin 10 (SNX10) (Even-Or and Stepensky, 2021; Del Fattore et al., 2008; Bo et al., 2016; Moore et al., 2017). TCIRG1 loss of function variants on the alpha3 subunit of the vacuolar ATP pump impairs acid secretion and vesicular trafficking and fusion in the osteoclast. Loss of function CLCN7 variants also cause defects in acidification of resorption lacunae and lysosomal trafficking. CLCN7 variants can cause either recessive osteopetrosis or dominant osteopetrosis with incomplete penetrance. Loss of function OSTM1 (osteopetrosis associated transmembrane protein 1) variants result in underdevelopment of the osteoclast ruffled border and impaired osteoclast lysosomal trafficking of osteoclast resorptive enzymes. SNX10 variants impair vesicular trafficking in osteoclasts. Carbonic anhydrase II (CA2) deficiency results in osteopetrosis with impaired lacunar acidification and intracranial calcifications. PLEKHM1 variants cause osteopetrosis due to abnormal endolysosomal trafficking and abnormal fusion of osteoclasts (Even-Or and Stepensky, 2021; Fujiwara et al., 2016). PLEKHM1 protein resides in the cytoplasm and binds with Rab7 and DEF8 to help with localization of endosomal/lysosomal compartments to microtubules (Van Wesenbeeck et al., 2007). Typically, PLEKHM1 related osteopetrosis is autosomal recessive, although dominant forms have been reported, ranging from mild to severe phenotypes (including the presence of cytopenias) in adults (Del Fattore et al., 2008; Bo et al., 2016). These forms all represent “osteoclast rich” osteopetroses. In contrast, RANK and RANKL loss of function variants on the other hand decrease osteoclastic differentiation, resulting in “osteoclast poor” osteopetrosis (Stark and Savarirayan, 2009).

Differentiating TNO from IOP is critical. While TNO resolves spontaneously without treatment, IOP may necessitate hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), as the only curative therapy available for certain subtypes (Sobacchi et al., 2013; Schulz and Moshous, 2023). Indeed, the discovery of osteosclerotic appearance on neonatal radiographs necessitates further evaluation to delineate the cause. In this study, we present our institutional experience of neonates presenting with osteosclerosis and the differentiation of TNO from IOP, emphasizing the clinical, radiographic, and genetic features that distinguish these two entities.

Material and methods

The goal of this study was to describe the clinical and radiologic features of infants presenting with transient neonatal osteosclerosis versus those having infantile osteopetrosis. This study was governed by two separate institutional review board (IRB) protocols. One protocol was for a prospective osteopetrosis natural history study (Child 1 and his mother). Written informed consent was provided by the parent. The second protocol was for a retrospective observational chart review study with waiver of informed consent, using radiology and electronic medical records of infants presenting with osteosclerotic features (all other children in the study). Both IRB protocols are HIPAA-compliant single center studies approved by the Indiana University IRB. All patients were evaluated at the same tertiary children's hospital in Indiana.

We queried the institutional radiology information system from January 2006 to May 2025 to identify all infants with a diagnosis of osteopetrosis or osteosclerosis mentioned in the radiology report. All children meeting the following criteria were included in analysis: age less than 1 year at the time of the first radiograph mentioning osteopetrosis or osteosclerosis. We excluded patients that did not have at minimum radiographs available for both the spine and long bones to assess for generalized osteosclerosis.

Plain radiograph images were extracted and evaluated by a pediatric radiologist with musculoskeletal disease expertise. From the medical records we retrieved demographic information, clinical characteristics and laboratory test results at presentation, and genetic evaluation results when available. The mother of one of the infants was also evaluated in the musculoskeletal research core with bone densitometry using both dual energy X-ray absorptiometry and spine quantitative computed tomography.

Results

Patient population

From January 2006 to May 2025, 21 children less than 1 year of age had radiograph reports indicating osteosclerosis or osteopetrosis. Of these, 1 was excluded due to having craniometaphyseal dysplasia, 7 were excluded because of insufficient radiographs to assess both the spine and long bones to assess for generalized osteosclerosis. The remaining 13 children had available radiographs of both the spine and long bones, of which 7 were classified as transient neonatal osteosclerosis, based on evidence of resolution, and 6 children demonstrated osteopetrosis (all with genetic confirmation).

Initial case presentation (Case 1)

A 4-week-old male infant of a diabetic mother with multiple congenital anomalies, including caudal regression, imperforate anus, atrial septal defect, cleft lip and palate, facial nerve paralysis, and conductive hearing loss, was transferred to our institution for evaluation of infantile osteopetrosis and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Radiographs showed osteosclerosis of axial and appendicular skeleton (see Fig. 1A). Genetic testing revealed heterozygous PLEKHM1 variant and a 15q14 duplication, both of unclear significance and both maternally inherited. There were no variants detected in other osteopetrosis or dense bone dysplasia genes tested, including AMER1, ANKH, CA2, CLCN7, COL1A1, CTSK, DLX3, FAM20C, FERMT3, GJA1, HPGD, LEMD3, LRP4, LRP5, MTAP, OSTM1, PTDSS1, SLCO2A1, SNX10, SOST, TBXAS1, TCIRG1, TGFB1, TNFRSF11A, TNFRSF11B, TNFSF11, TYROBP, and IKBKG. The mother had a history of cleft lip and normal skeletal radiographs with no clinical bone disease. The mother had increased BMD (DXA BMD z-score of +4.3 and femoral neck areal BMD z-score + 2.2). Infantile osteopetrosis

There was no case of persistent osteosclerosis without genetic confirmation of osteopetrosis. Six children were diagnosed with IOP (three males, three females) with an average age of presentation of 130 days (37–317 days). Two children were born preterm (child 9 at 34 weeks and child 10 at 36 weeks). Four children had severe autosomal recessive osteopetrosis with the following pathogenic variants: recessive TCIRG1 (n = 3, one homozygous, and two compound heterozygous), CLCN7 (n = 1 with paternal uniparental disomy), and one had autosomal dominant osteopetrosis due to CLCN7 (from a family with known autosomal dominant osteopetrosis). One child had osteopetrosis-lymphedema-ectodermal dysplasia-immunodeficiency syndrome due to a hemizygous pathogenic variant in IKBKG. Clinical characteristics are outlined in Table 1.

Of the 5 severe IOP patients, one of the children underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and the other 4 died (between the ages of 3 and 8 months). Four of these five children developed significant neurological symptoms, including two with cranial nerve compression. The infant having dominant osteopetrosis due to CLCN7 is now 17 years old and has had issues with multiple fractures due to osteopetrosis.

The radiographs of all infants having TNO had increased density of the bones with dense sclerotic bands at the vertebral endplates with minimal or no midbody lucency, along with increased density of the cortex of long bones with complete or near complete obliteration of the medullary cavity (Fig. 1). Only one child also had sclerotic stripes in the long bones (Fig. 2). In addition, this child had a vertical lucency in the left superior pubic ramus that was a synchondrosis mimicking a fracture. In all seven children the increased bone density resolved on subsequent radiographs which were obtained on median at the age of 81 days (range 33 days to 941 days, or 1 month to 31 months).

Infantile osteopetrosis

All infants presenting with osteopetrosis had radiographs demonstrating increased density of all imaged bones. All had increased density of the vertebral body and all had varying degrees of metaphyseal widening (Erlenmeyer flask deformity) and multiple fractures (Fig. 3). Three patients also had rachitic changes with fraying of metaphyses (Fig. 3). Comparison between neonates with transient osteosclerosis and infantile osteopetrosis

Infants with TNO were significantly younger (median age of 6 days, range 1–16 days) at diagnosis as compared with infants with IOP (median age 73 days, range 33–317) with a mean difference of −61 days (95 % CI -301, −29; p = 0.003). Furthermore, only one child with transient osteosclerosis presented due to suspicion for rib fractures, but none of these children had fractures. In contrast, 5 of 6 children with infantile osteopetrosis were evaluated initially due to fractures, and all of these infants had pathologic fractures. In addition, there were several other distinctive radiographic findings in infants with osteopetrosis not seen in neonates with transient osteosclerosis including metaphyseal widening in all infants, rachitic changes in 3 infants, and bone within bone (endobone) pattern in one infant.

In the osteopetrosis group, 4 had laboratory testing within one day of radiographs, one at −51 days, and one at +40 days after radiographs. Thrombocytopenia was evident in 4/6 patients with IOP versus none of those with TNO, though one TNO patient had transient mild thrombocytopenia (119 k/mm3) at a later date. The difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06). Interestingly, none of the patients with IOP had low white blood cell counts, but half had elevated WBC, compared to none with abnormal WBC in the TNO group (p = 0.18). Anemia was evident in 4/6 patients with IOP versus 1/6 of those with TNO (p = 0.24).

Similar proportions had hypocalcemia (IOP patients, 3/6; TNO 2/6; p = 1.0) though secondary hyperparathyroidism was evident only in those with IOP (5/6 had elevated PTH vs TNO 0/3; p = 0.048). Hypocalcemia was severe in one infant with IOP, while the 2 hypocalcemic patients with TNO had only mild hypocalcemia. Additionally, among IOP 3/5 tested had high alkaline phosphatase versus none in the TNO group (p = 0.17). Serum phosphorus was normal in the TNO group, but 4/6 patients with IOP had hypophosphatemia (p = 0.06). All hypophosphatemic patients had elevated PTH. One single patient with IOP had low alkaline phosphatase.

Discussion

This is the first case series directly comparing TNO and IOP. We evaluated seven neonates with TNO and six infants with genetically confirmed IOP. TNO was incidentally detected on neonatal radiographs obtained for unrelated clinical indications, consistent with prior isolated case reports (Hauck et al., 2024; Monaghan et al., 1991; Nadvi et al., 1999). In contrast, all IOP cases presented symptomatically with skeletal related issues, primarily with fractures.

Infants with TNO were significantly (p = 0.003) younger at diagnosis (median age 6 days; range 1–16 days) compared with those with IOP (median 73 days; range 33–317 days). This age distribution aligns with prior reports of TNO where presentation occurred between 2 and 12 days after birth (Hauck et al., 2024; Monaghan et al., 1991; Nadvi et al., 1999). In our IOP cohort, the earliest diagnosis occurred at 37 days. Neonatal osteopetrosis presentation is rare and typically associated with hypocalcemic seizures (Engiz et al., 2012; Kurt et al., 2006) or incidental findings when evaluating for other medical conditions (Vomero et al., 2019).

Among the TNO group, 4 of 7 neonates had multiple congenital anomalies and 4 had genetic abnormalities but none linked to known osteosclerotic disorders. In two cases, skeletal surveys were initiated for evaluation for fractures and suspicion of nonaccidental trauma. The limited number of reported TNO cases may reflect the infrequent use of skeletal surveys in asymptomatic neonates.

Radiographic resolution of TNO was documented as early as 31 days of life and up to 2.5 years, comparable to prior cases showing normalization between 3 month and 2.3 years (Hauck et al., 2024; Monaghan et al., 1991; Nadvi et al., 1999). However, because no cases were diagnosed beyond 16 days of life, this transient phenotype could be missed without imaging in the infant period.

The pathophysiology of TNO is unknown. Calcium, phosphate and magnesium are actively transported to the fetus by the placenta and maintained at higher than the maternal levels which contribute to bone mineralization (Sethi et al., 2020). Most children in our series were term at birth which supports the contribution of the mineralization process in late pregnancy to the higher bone density in the neonatal period. Human neonates are born with trabecular bone that is highly dense and relatively isotropic, consisting of many thin trabecular struts packed closely together. As infants develop, particularly in the first year, trabecular bone becomes more anisotropic, aligned with principal loading direction, with trabeculae becoming wider spaced and thicker (Acquaah et al., 2015).

Similar to TNO, attenuated autosomal recessive osteopetrosis can present without fractures or bone marrow failure. However, these milder forms of osteopetrosis typically manifest later in childhood rather than during the neonatal period (Baer et al., 2019; Campos-Xavier et al., 2003). In our patients, those diagnosed with osteopetrosis in infancy did have a severe phenotype, with 4 of the 6 dying in infancy. The presentation of dominant osteopetrosis in infancy in one patient in this series was due to suspicion of fractures, though he also had a known family history of dominant osteopetrosis.

It is not certain whether children with TNO later have mild elevations in bone density in later childhood or adulthood, which would suggest a persistent though mild abnormality in skeletal metabolism. One child demonstrating an appearance of TNO also had a mother with asymptomatic mild elevations in bone mass in adulthood. It is possible that this could represent a dominant abnormality, and it is of note that both family members harbored the same heterozygous variants of uncertain significance in PLEKHM1 (c.864G > A, pMet288Ile) and the 536 kb duplication of 15q14. The duplication does not contain known osteopetrosis or high bone mass genes other than possibly AQR (aquarius intron-binding spliceosomal factor) which has been possibly associated with increased fractures, and type 2 diabetes (Song et al., 2018; AQR, 2025). However, we cannot rule out a contribution of this chromosomal duplication to the phenotype. Of note, the infant's bone resorption marker (urine NTX/creatinine ratio) was not suppressed for age, as would have been expected for osteopetrosis, though they were about −1 standard deviation below the mean of healthy one month old infants (Yamashita et al., 2016). Neither the child nor mother had chronic hematologic cell abnormalities.

Genetic testing is likely to be also informative in estimating prognosis and determining the treatment plan. Among those with osteopetrosis, three had confirmed recessive TCIRG1 variants as causative of osteopetrosis. Two of those have died in infancy, while the other received hematopoetic stem cell transplant. One patient had a severe phenotype consistent with their IKBKG variant, and another had homozygous CLCN7 variants with severe neurologic symptoms who also died of their disease. On the other hand, the dominant heterozygous CLCN7 variant, though severe enough to be detected in infancy, has survived, though has complications in his adolescence from osteopetrosis and skeletal fragility. Although the PLEKHM1 variant is a variant of uncertain significance, PLEKHM1 related osteopetrosis has sometimes been described as a more mild osteopetrosis in adult patients who demonstrate Erlenmeyer flask deformities, rugger jersey spine and sclerotic lesions in those with heterozygous mutations, or as sometimes causing severe phenotypes with cytopenias (Del Fattore et al., 2008; Moore et al., 2017; Van Wesenbeeck et al., 2007). Almarzooqi et al. (Almarzooqi et al., 2011) reported a full term male infant with IOP and juvenile xanthogranuloma in the newborn period who had severe anemia and thrombocytopenia and was found to be heterozygous for a maternally inherited point variant in PLEKHM1.

Distinguishing TNO from IOP is critically important as IOP has a risk of mortality due to abnormal expansion of bone that decreases medullary hematopoiesis leading to pancytopenia. This leads to secondary expansion of hematopoeisis at non-bone sites causing hepatosplenomegaly. Transplantation is often required for treatment of IOP, especially with TCIRG1 variants, due to severe pancytopenia. However, transplantation also carries a high risk of mortality. One child with transient osteosclerosis was originally transferred to the tertiary children's hospital specifically for possible transplantation, but we recommended monitoring due to minimal hematologic abnormalities and apparent early radiographical improvement. Subsequent skeletal improvement occurred, and hematology indices also improved. In contrast 4 of the 6 osteopetrosis patients presenting in infancy died of complications of their disease.

In our patient population differences were suggested between groups for various laboratory indices, notably those with IOP often having high WBC (though pancytopenia is commonly reported in IOP), lower hemoglobin, lower platelets, hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, and secondary hyperparathyroidism. Most of these differences generally did not reach statistical significance due likely to the small sample size and patient variability. However, the presence of these features in an infant with osteosclerosis should raise suspicion for osteopetrosis.

The main limitations in this study relate to the small sample size and the retrospective review of radiology reports and medical information. Seven children potentially with TNO were excluded from the study as they did not have both long bones and spine radiographs performed serially to fully characterize them. In addition, as implied above there is selection bias in TNO as these children do not have symptoms related to the osteosclerosis and the prevalence may be much higher than reported. This is compared with infants with IOP who all will develop symptoms and it is likely that all of them were included. Another limitation of the study is that subsequent radiographs after diagnosis were not performed in a systematic fashion. Consequently, the precise timing of resolution of TNO was not evident for several patients as many months had passed between radiographs. Additionally, all laboratory measurements were conducted based on clinical suspicion and most patients did not have a full mineral metabolism evaluation.

Conclusion

In a neonate with incidentally detected osteosclerosis, the differential diagnosis should include TNO alongside infantile osteosclerotic dysplasias. TNO may mimic osteopetrosis but can be distinguished based on clinical, radiographic, and laboratory findings. TNO typically presents within the first two weeks of life, without fractures, with usually normal complete blood count, and normal serum calcium levels. In contrast, IOP often presents later in infancy and is frequently associated with pathological fractures, cytopenias, hypocalcemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism. Additionally, osteopetrosis in this age often had metaphyseal rachitic changes. In the presence of these findings suggesting osteopetrosis, genetic testing is important in establishing prognosis. In the absence of bone marrow failure, particularly thrombocytopenia, or hypocalcemia, ongoing monitoring is still important, therefore we recommend clinical observation and follow-up radiography in approximately three months as there may be early signs of resolution consistent with TNO.
