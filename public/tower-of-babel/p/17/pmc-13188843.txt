Estimating Mastectomy Weight From Standard Breast Measurements, BMI, and Sister Bra Size: A Simple Clinical Formula
Authors: Meghan N Miller, Eliana Jolkovsky, Derrick Lin, Ainaz Dory Barkhordarzadeh, Samantha Rabinovich, Stacy Piva, Nimmi S Kapoor, Amy M Kusske, Farnaz Haji, Ginger C Slack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurately estimating breast weight is essential for surgical planning, particularly in mastectomy and immediate reconstruction. Current prediction methods are often impractical in routine clinical settings because of cost, complexity, or accessibility.ObjectivesThe authors sought to derive and validate a simple formula that can be utilized by both patients and providers to estimate mastectomy specimen weight using standard breast measurements, BMI, and sister bra size (SS).MethodsThe authors performed a retrospective review of 177 mastectomy patients (333 breasts) from University of California, Los Angeles. Data collected included nipple-to-inframammary fold (N-IMF) distance, sternal notch-to-nipple (SN-N) distance, BMI, Regnault ptosis grade, and SS. Spearman and Pearson correlation tests were conducted to identify terms with strong predictive strength. A formula with 3 strong predictive terms was derived using linear regression.ResultsThe 3 terms included in the predictive equation were BMI × N-IMF (ρ = 0.857; P < .001), SS × N-IMF (ρ = 0.832; P < .001), and SN-N2 (ρ = 0.72; P < .001). The final formula, which was Mastectomy specimen weight (g) = 1.32 × (BMI × N-IMF) + 2.0 × (SS × N-IMF) + 0.13 × (SN-N)2 − 32 demonstrated strong predictive performance (R2 = 0.783; root mean square error = ±119 g).ConclusionsThe authors present a simple formula that can accurately estimate mastectomy specimen weight using BMI, N-IMF distance, SN-N distance, and SS.Level of Evidence: 4 (Therapeutic) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

Accurately estimating mastectomy specimen weight is a critical component of preoperative planning in both reconstructive and oncologic breast surgery. Surgeons often rely on experience and rough visual estimates to anticipate breast weight. This impacts intraoperative decisions and reconstruction outcomes, such as directing implant selection. Although breast volume estimation techniques, such as 3-dimensional imaging and MRI, exist, they are expensive, time-intensive, or unavailable in many surgical settings.1 Consequently, there remains a need for a simple, reliable, and universally accessible tool to predict mastectomy weight using routine clinical measurements. Previous studies have attempted to correlate variables such as breast base width (BW), BMI, and sternal notch-to-nipple (SN-N) distance with breast volume or specimen weight.2 However, these models are complicated, with many additional variables, and difficult for patients to use or require technological resources like the Vectra system (Canfield Scientific, Inc., Parsippany, NJ).3,4 Additionally, they overlook the practical information embedded in a patient's bra size.5 Unlike previous formulas, ours is the first to utilize the predictive power of sister bra size (SS), which utilizes the patient's own bra cup and band size. Our previous work found that sister size, a metric that incorporates both bra band and cup size, is a strong predictor of breast weight. Cup size alone fails to account for variations in band size, which significantly affects true breast volume. SS offers an accurate and anatomically relevant proxy for breast footprint.5 This approach applies standardized equivalency rules to account for volume preservation across varying band–cup combinations, providing a more anatomically consistent estimate of breast volume. Building on our previous validation of SS as a clinical variable, we derive a straightforward, clinically applicable formula for estimating mastectomy specimen weight using easily obtainable measurements.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Collection

We conducted a retrospective chart review of patients who underwent bilateral or unilateral mastectomy at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) from January 2018 to November 2023. The study protocol was approved by the UCLA Institutional Review Board. All cases were identified by a UCLA Clinical and Translational Research Institute report, and relevant preoperative and postoperative data were manually extracted by our team from surgeon clinic notes and pathology reports. The report included all mastectomy types, including skin-sparing, nipple-sparing, and non-skin-sparing procedures, and excluded patients with previous breast implants or a gender identity disorder diagnosis. The mastectomies in this study were performed by 10 different surgeons. Because 10 different surgeons recorded measurements, we attempted to overcome potential interobserver variability in how distances were obtained. Demographic variables for each patient were collected by chart review, and SSs were obtained from chart notes or by contacting patients. Surgeon-documented preoperative breast measurements were obtained by chart review and included SN-N distance, nipple-to-inframammary fold (N-IMF) distance, BW, and Regnault ptosis grade (0-3). N-IMF distance was measured with the patient standing and the breast at rest, consistent with the technique documented in surgeon clinic notes.

Individual mastectomy specimen weights were obtained from pathology reports. Preoperative bra sizes were primarily obtained through chart review. Patients whose bra cup and band sizes were not available in the medical record were contacted through email and/or phone call to collect this information. SS was determined using the Sister Bra Size Conversion Chart (Table 1). Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion for all correlation analyses and listwise deletion for the multivariate regression model, resulting in a final regression sample of 282 breasts.

Formula Creation

Pearson's and Spearman's correlation coefficients were calculated in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) to determine the associations between N-IMF, SS, BMI, SN-N, BW, and ptosis grade with mastectomy specimen weight. Correlation tests of the interactions between these variables were performed. Squared values of the 6 variables were also assessed for correlation with mastectomy weight (Table 2). Three terms with strong correlation coefficients were selected for inclusion in the formula. A multivariate linear regression was then performed using Python stats models to determine formula coefficients. Performance metrics (R2, root mean square error [RMSE]) were calculated using Python scikit-learn. Variable selection was based on previously validated anatomical relationships and physiologic plausibility.

Model Visualization

Diagnostic plots were generated using Python matplotlib to assess model performance and verify the accuracy of our predictive formula. Predicted vs actual mastectomy specimen weights were plotted against a line of perfect prediction. A residual plot was generated to evaluate the distribution of prediction errors across the range of predicted values. A random, symmetric scatter of residuals around zero would indicate that the model does not exhibit systematic bias and that variance remains consistent across different prediction magnitudes.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 177 female patients were included in the study, with no male patients represented. The mean age at surgery was 45.3 years (range, 23-78 years). The largest age cohort was 35 to 44 years (36.9%), followed by 45 to 54 years (29.5%), whereas 13.6% of patients were between 55 and 64 years. A smaller proportion were younger (23-34 years, 15.3%) or older than 65 years (4.5%; Table 3). Preoperative BMI averaged 26.3 kg/m2 (range, 16.1-45.0 kg/m2). Nearly half of patients (49.2%) had a BMI within the normal range (16-24 kg/m2), whereas 27.7% were overweight (25-29 kg/m2), and 18.1% were obese (30-35 kg/m2). Only 5.1% of patients had a BMI >36 kg/m2. Most patients identified as not Hispanic or Latino (77.4%), whereas 15.8% were Hispanic or Latino, and 6.8% chose not to disclose ethnicity. The largest racial group was White or Caucasian (44.1%), followed by patients who identified as “Other” (25.4%) and Asian (11.9%). Black or African American patients comprised 4.0% of the study population, whereas smaller proportions identified as Middle Eastern or North African (1.7%), American Indian or Alaska Native (0.6%), and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (0.6%). Race was not reported for 11.8% of patients (Table 3).

Other variables such as cup size, band size, ptosis grade, and breast BW showed progressively weaker correlations (Table 5). Interaction product terms between top individual predictors showed very strong correlations, particularly N-IMF × BMI (r = 0.857, ρ = 0.857), N-IMF × SS (r = 0.832, ρ = 0.825), and SS × SN-N (r = 0.796, ρ = 0.804; Table 6).

DISCUSSION

In this study, our goal was to present a simple, patient- and surgeon-friendly formula for estimating mastectomy specimen weight using 4 easily obtainable variables: N-IMF distance, BMI, SN-N, and SS. These inputs, which are routinely available in most clinical settings, demonstrated strong individual and interactive correlations with actual mastectomy weights. The final model offered high predictive accuracy, with an R2 of 0.783 and an RMSE of ±119 g. These values are within the margin of clinical acceptability for most reconstructive and oncologic planning needs. This model was chosen for its strong predictive performance and interpretability, balancing anatomical relevance with (N-IMF × SS) and (SN-N)2 and physiologic contribution (BMI × N-IMF).

Importantly, our dataset included all mastectomy types, including skin-sparing, nipple-sparing, and non-skin-sparing procedures. As such, specimen weights may have varied depending on the presence or absence of skin. However, this heterogeneity reflects real-world surgical practice and supports the generalizability of our model across diverse mastectomy techniques. Although this RMSE indicates a meaningful average deviation, we recognize that a ±119 g difference could be clinically significant in smaller-breasted patients whose specimen weights are near 300 to 400 g. We also acknowledge that bra misfitting and inconsistencies in patient-reported cup size may introduce variability into SS measurement, although using sister size groups partially mitigates this by standardizing breast volume across differing band sizes.

The selection of variables reflects a balance between anatomical relevance and clinical practicality. BMI serves as a broad proxy for overall body habitus and adiposity, whereas N-IMF distance captures vertical projection and lower pole fullness.6 SS, a refined metric that adjusts cup size for band size, emerged as a particularly valuable predictor.5 Unlike raw cup size, which fails to account for variations in chest circumference and actual breast volume, SS reflects both the horizontal and surface-area distribution of the breast. Our previous work validated SS as a reproducible, anatomically meaningful construct, and its integration into this formula further supports its utility in breast surgery. Additionally, it is easily obtainable through patient history and clothing labels.

In addition to the clinical simplicity of our approach, more advanced technologies—such as 3-dimensional (3D) scanning and preoperative imaging—have been explored for mastectomy volume estimation. For instance, Kim et al used the Vectra H2 system to estimate mastectomy weight with an RMSE of 38 g, demonstrating the high precision achievable with imaging-based techniques.4 Similarly, Utsunomiya et al and Kayar et al evaluated 3D scanning and comparative volumetric methods, respectively, reporting strong correlations between predicted and actual mastectomy weights.2,7 Although these methods are highly accurate, they require expensive imaging systems not readily available in all clinical environments. Our formula offers a cost-free, accessible alternative that can be used preoperatively and intraoperatively in settings lacking such technology.

When compared with previous literature, our approach addresses several key limitations. Past attempts to estimate mastectomy weight have typically relied on isolated anthropometric measurements such as BW or SN-N distance, which, while relevant, do not fully account for the nuanced variability in breast morphology.8-10 Other studies have incorporated cup size as a variable, but this approach often overlooks the confounding effect of band size.11 By using SS instead of raw cup size, our model offers a more standardized and anatomically meaningful reflection of true breast volume. Furthermore, no other published formulas incorporate both vertical projection (N-IMF) and overall body habitus (BMI), making our model uniquely integrative.12

One of the key advantages of our formula lies in its simplicity. With rounded coefficients, it is easy to calculate and interpret. This makes it well suited for busy clinical environments, where surgeons need quick, reliable estimates to guide decision making. Intraoperative estimation of specimen weight—such as water displacement or direct measurement—remains simple and effective but is not relevant to preoperative discussion between patient and physician that builds rapport and helps answer preoperative patient questions. We considered the inclusion of breast density as documented by mammograms, but we wanted our clinical formula to be readily usable by all physicians, and information provided by mammograms was not readily available. The final equation, which was derived from common clinical measurements, can be applied using basic arithmetic, eliminating the need for specialized software or training. Additionally, compared with existing tools that rely on advanced imaging, 3D modeling, or less accessible measurements, our formula offers a pragmatic alternative that can be quickly applied in preoperative planning or even during patient consultations.

Although the model performed well across a wide range of breast sizes and patient body types, variability beyond ±100 g in some cases may reflect intraoperative differences in mastectomy technique, subcutaneous tissue handling, or specimen processing. Notably, we chose not to stratify by mastectomy type (eg, skin-sparing and nipple-sparing) to maximize generalizability and ensure the formula could be used across a range of surgical contexts. Future prospective, multi-institutional validation will be critical to confirm reproducibility and potentially refine coefficients by mastectomy type or ptosis grade.

A limitation to our study includes using data from a single institution. Future studies should prospectively validate this formula in diverse populations and explore whether tailoring coefficients by mastectomy type or ptosis grade could further improve accuracy. Nonetheless, the strength of our findings lies in their clinical applicability. With just 4 measurements—BMI, N-IMF distance, SN-N distance, and SS—surgeons and patients can now estimate mastectomy weight with a high degree of confidence, supporting more informed decisions in flap selection, implant sizing, and patient counseling. This study is further limited by its relatively small sample size, the involvement of multiple surgeons whose measurement techniques may introduce interobserver variability, and the potential for recall bias among patients contacted to supplement incomplete chart data.

To facilitate clinical adoption, the formula can be seamlessly embedded into electronic health record systems, such as Epic (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI).13 For instance, an Epic smartphrase or calculator can be created where surgeons input the N-IMF, SN-N, patient BMI, and SS, and the system automatically outputs a mastectomy weight estimate. This integration could support preoperative planning by helping with decisions around implant size, flap selection, or timing of reconstruction. It could also be used as an educational tool to enhance resident training and patient counseling. Our team is currently creating a website that functions as a calculator with our formula, where one could input their bra size, BMI, SN-N, and N-IMF to easily obtain the predicted amount of tissue that would be removed in a mastectomy.

Looking ahead, the next step is prospective validation. By integrating the formula into the clinical workflow and collecting predicted vs actual specimen weights over time, we can assess real-world performance and potentially refine coefficients or adapt the model to specific subgroups (eg, skin-sparing vs nipple-sparing mastectomy). Additionally, exploring its utility across different patient populations and surgeons could help ensure generalizability. Ultimately, the goal is to develop a reliable, low-barrier tool that enhances surgical planning and improves patient outcomes across diverse practice settings.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we developed a formula to estimate mastectomy specimen weight using 4 routinely obtainable clinical variables: BMI, N-IMF distance, SN-N distance, and SS. The final multivariate model demonstrated strong predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.783, RMSE = ±119 g) and incorporates both vertical and horizontal breast dimensions as well as body habitus. By relying solely on standard preoperative measurements, this formula provides a practical method for estimating mastectomy weight without the need for imaging or specialized technology.
