Exploring second victim syndrome among surgeons at the University of Toronto and assessing the need for peer support
Authors: Jordyn Vernon, Kimberley Lam-Tin-Cheung, Bailey Russell, Marisa Louridas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Second victim syndrome (SVS) is a term encompassing the psychological changes experienced by health care providers after an adverse event, and it is believed that nearly all surgeons experience SVS. We sought to explore SVS experiences of surgeons at our institution and assess the need for a peer support program.Methods:We distributed a web-based questionnaire to staff surgeons at the University of Toronto, which covered demographics, the validated Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST), and interest in a peer support program. We performed quantitative assessment to determine at-risk demographics for SVS.Results:We surveyed 120 participants. Scores on the SVEST were highest in the psychological distress and institutional support domains. There was no difference in scores by gender. General surgeons had higher scores in the turnover intentions dimension. Mid-career surgeons had higher scores in several dimensions. More than half of participants were in favour of a peer support program.Conclusion:Our results confirm that surgeons at our institution experience SVS, mainly in the form of psychological distress, and that most surgeons are interested in a peer support program. Further research to understand at-risk demographics and characterize survey nonresponders is warranted.

Adverse events in medicine inevitably occur, and their impact on practitioners can be substantial.1,2 The deleterious effect of these complications on providers has been termed second victim syndrome (SVS) — with the first victim being the patient — and has become an accepted term to encompass the emotional, psychological, and behavioural changes experienced by health care providers after a complication, error, or near-miss event.2 Symptoms of SVS include feelings of guilt, anxiety, sadness, and depression, difficulty concentrating, and repetitive intrusive memories.3 These symptoms can result in decreased physician confidence and increased risk of medical errors, thus directly affecting patient care and potentially leading to poor patient outcomes.3–7

Surgeons may experience adverse events differently than their medical physician counterparts, as the conduct of an invasive procedure relies more directly on the experience and skill of the operating surgeon.1 Global surveys have found that up to 91% of surgeons identify with having had symptoms of SVS at some time throughout their careers.8,9 Many of those surveyed identified that support from colleagues was most helpful in their recovery.5,6 Recent literature has described the development of peer support programs targeting those at risk of developing SVS.5,6,10–13 One surgery-specific SVS peer support program at a large American institution was used by 81% of affected individuals after an adverse event, with all attesting to its positive impact.13

In our institution, little is known about the impact of SVS on surgeons, and there is no current infrastructure for a peer support program. To our knowledge, the only described Canadian peer support program for surgeons exists at Western University in London, Ontario.1 In this study we sought to explore the prevalence of SVS in the Department of Surgery at the University of Toronto, identify characteristics of surgeons who may be at greater risk, and determine whether there is an unmet need for a peer support program.

Methods

Study design

We designed our methodology according to the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet e-Surveys (CHERRIES) framework.14 We conducted a cross-sectional study in which we administered a web-based survey, which included sections on demographics, characterization of personal experiences with adverse events, the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST), and a gauge of interest in a local peer support program. We chose the SVEST as it has been specifically validated for health care workers.15 The SVEST includes 29 items representing 7 dimensions (psychological distress, physical distress, colleague support, division support, institutional support, non–work-related support, professional self-efficacy); 2 outcome variables (turnover interventions, absenteeism); and a 7-item scale, which assesses desirability of different forms of support.15 Each variable is assessed with 2 to 4 different questions on a 5-point Likert scale. We made 1 minor modification to the original questionnaire in the wording of the institutional support dimension to reflect both the individual hospital sites and the university as the supervising institutions in our setting. The complete survey is included in Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.005125/tab-related-content).

The target population included all faculty surgeons currently working at hospitals fully affiliated with the University of Toronto. All divisions within the Department of Surgery were included (general surgery, orthopedic surgery, plastic surgery, neurosurgery, cardiac surgery, vascular surgery, thoracic surgery, and urology). Otolaryngology, ophthalmology, and obstetrics and gynecology were not included as they do not fall under the Department of Surgery at our institution. We did not include trainees and retired faculty.

Informed consent process

A recruitment email was sent from the principal investigator containing an information sheet describing the purpose of the study, survey details, planned analysis, and details regarding data storage and confidentiality. We did not collect any identifying information in the questionnaire; however, those interested could list their email at the end of the survey for future involvement in a follow-up qualitative exploration of this topic. These responses, if provided, were not linked to the stored questionnaire responses to protect participant anonymity.

Survey development and administration

The primary goal of the survey was to administer the SVEST. In the development of the survey, we added a selection of demographic questions to understand the characteristics of respondents and facilitate subgroup analyses. Once the survey content was finalized, we designed it within SurveyMonkey and tested it among the study team members for online functionality before distribution.

We distributed the survey link electronically using the Department of Surgery mailing list. We sent 2 reminder emails, in addition to the initial recruitment email, to encourage participation. The survey was not otherwise advertised. We collected survey responses using SurveyMonkey from July 2023 to July 2024. Survey participation was voluntary, and we did not provide any incentive to participate. There was no randomization or alternation of survey items. The survey was displayed over 8 webpages, with a maximum of 16 questions per page. Completeness checks were not performed before or after survey submission. Respondents could navigate between pages and change their answers before survey submission.

We did not include any measures to prevent multiple submissions from the same individual, given the nature of the survey and study population. We reviewed responses submitted from identical IP addresses for uniqueness and assumed these to be due to survey completion at a mutual location (i.e., hospital site).

Statistical analysis

We determined the survey response rate using the number of unique responses, divided by the total number of eligible participants. We still counted a survey response toward the total if not all questions were answered, with no minimum number of questions answered for a response to be included. We analyzed each question independently with its unique denominator to ensure accurate calculation of the response percentage.

We amalgamated demographic data and analyzed data using descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SDs), and frequencies, as appropriate. We scored the SVEST as per the original validated protocol of 7 dimensions (psychological distress, physical distress, colleague support, division support, institutional support, non-work-related support, professional self-efficacy), 2 outcome behaviour variables (turnover interventions, absenteeism), and a 7-item scale of desirability of different forms of support.14 Each dimension is assessed with 2 to 4 different questions on a 5-point Likert scale, and we calculated the average of responses to these questions to give an overall score. We calculated the mean and SD for each dimension and outcome behaviour variable, with higher scores corresponding to more pronounced SVS. We also calculated the percentage of respondents scoring 4 or higher on each variable to represent the percent agreement with a given dimension. We scored the desirability of potential supports on a 5-point Likert scale, with responses of 4 or higher representing high desirability. We conducted subgroup analyses to investigate differences in dimension and outcome behaviour scores by gender, surgical subspecialty, and years in practice. We used nonparametric Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests to perform these comparisons, as appropriate. For the purposes of the comparative analysis, we grouped subspecialties with single-digit responses together. We performed quantitative analysis using SPSS version 29.0.2.0).

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the University of Toronto Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (no. 44338).

Results

A total of 120 participants completed the survey, for a response rate of 21.2% (120/565). Five participants stopped early and did not provide any SVEST data, resulting in 115 responses included in the final analysis. The study population was 68.7% male; 53.0% were general surgeons, and 77% worked in academic hospitals. Complete demographic characteristics are provided in Table 1. All participants had experienced an adverse event, with anxiety (73.8%) and guilt (69.2%) cited as the most common resulting emotions.

Subgroup analyses

Mean scores for dimensions and outcome behaviours were compared by gender, with results summarized in Table 4. We used nonparametric analysis (Mann–Whitney), given the lack of normal distribution in the SVEST scores. There were no significant differences detected between genders. Only the absenteeism outcome behaviour, referring to time taken away from the work environment because of SVS symptoms, had a difference approaching the significance threshold, with women scoring higher than men (p = 0.07).

Desire for local peer support

The conclusion of the survey asked respondents to identify whether a peer support program in our institution would be valuable, and whether they would use it if made available. Results showed that 56.9% of participants felt that a peer support program at the University of Toronto would be valuable. However, only 43.1% of participants felt that they would access the peer support program if it were created.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized Canadian surgeons’ experiences with SVS using a validated questionnaire. Every surgeon in our cohort reported experience with an adverse event leading to serious patient morbidity or death. Surgeons at our institution most frequently reported psychological distress and lack of understanding from institutions. Despite moderately high scores in these dimensions, outcome behaviours in the form of turnover intention and absenteeism remained infrequent. The most highly desired source of support was a senior colleague with whom to debrief, although fewer than half of the surveyed participants endorsed that they would access a peer support program if one were to be created. Our response rate was in line with those from similar survey-based studies of surgeons.16,17

The findings of the current study align with previous literature demonstrating that surgeons frequently experience psychological distress after an adverse event. A study of academic surgeons in Boston, Massachusetts, found sadness, guilt, and anxiety to be the most prominent emotions experienced by surgeons after an intraoperative adverse event.6 Similarly, a national study of British surgeons showed that nearly half of surgeons developed anxiety after severe patient complications, and more than one-third endorsed difficulty sleeping and feelings of anger or irritability.4 These symptoms were found to be more pronounced if the complication was owing to a clinical error rather than an unpreventable adverse event, highlighting the deep sense of personal responsibility surgeons feel regarding the outcomes of their patients.4 We did not differentiate between reactions to complications and medical errors in the current study, but the high proportion of psychological distress reported in our population may suggest that either occurrence is internalized by the surgeon in a harmful way.

We did not observe significant differences in SVEST scores when we stratified analyses by gender, contrary to findings in the literature which have suggested that women surgeons may be at higher risk of SVS and endure more psychological consequences from adverse events.3,8,18 It has been postulated that women physicians in fields traditionally dominated by men, such as surgery, may have lesser access to supportive mentors and require different strategies for stress management than their male colleagues.3,19,20 It is unclear why this difference was not found in our population, but could reasonably be a result of participation bias, given the sensitive nature of the survey content. The finding that mid-career surgeons scored worse on the SVEST was also unexpected, as less experienced surgeons were thought to be more at risk of poor coping with adverse events owing to lacking confidence and fear of consequences.8,21 One possibility is that junior surgeons lack the insight and experience to appreciate the full spectrum of adverse outcomes that can occur, providing some protection from the full experience of SVS. Further, burnout in surgeons may increase with years in practice, resulting in negative quality of life and emotional distress.22 The concept of a mid-career crisis has been described, wherein the cumulative result of dealing with professional challenges over time leads to a period of decreased motivation and increased anxiety.23 These conditions could reasonably decrease a surgeon’s ability to cope with the added stress of an adverse event, and may explain the higher scores in turnover intentions in this group.

We did find a significant difference in turnover intentions by specialty, with general surgeons scoring higher in this domain than other surgical subspecialists. Threats to retention of Canadian general surgeons have been described in the literature previously and include the substantial burden of on-call work, the requirement for personal sacrifices related to the lack of control over lifestyle, and challenges with access to surgical resources.16 Similar studies of American general surgeons have shown that, despite overall high ratings of job satisfaction, more than 25% of surgeons have considered leaving surgery, with “overall work time demands” and “personal time requirements” as the most cited reasons.17 The role of SVS has not been explicitly explored in the literature in relation to career retention, but lack of collegiality and of support have been described as factors leading to career dissatisfaction.16 This may suggest that improved peer support could contribute positively to job satisfaction and retention among general surgeons.

Further research is required to better understand the potential risk factors for SVS. As highlighted, there is some dissonance in the literature regarding which groups may be more susceptible. A more accurate understanding could help direct resources such as peer support programming. The Canadian surgical workforce is particularly diverse, and future research may also consider the effect of training location (Canadian v. international) on SVS. The development of peer support programs in surgery has been a topic of much interest in recent years, with many prominent academic institutions reporting on their experiences with successfully implemented programs.1,5,12,13 Important lessons have been learned from these reports, notably that many surgeons are initially hesitant to seek help, and that they feel most comfortable discussing adverse events with other surgeons who they perceive to have been in their position before.10,13 In our study population, the notable difference between the number of respondents interested in a peer support program and the number who felt they would actually access a program themselves requires further exploration, as understanding barriers is an essential step in creating resources that will achieve their desired effect in practice.1 We suspect that the stigma surrounding adverse events remains a barrier to help-seeking behaviours and may explain the discrepancy between those who want support and those who actually seek it. We are planning future qualitative work to better understand how to address this effectively. Institutions may consider local initiatives to improve the culture of support as a means of encouraging help-seeking behaviour. One example is the modification of morbidity and mortality rounds to quality improvement rounds, attempting to shift the focus to improvement rather than error and creating a safe space for discussion of adverse events. This is a strategy that has been anecdotally successful in our context.

Limitations

Our response rate was only 21%, despite our use of reminder emails to encourage participation. Although this is in line with the response rates of previous surveys of large groups of surgeons,16,17 this may introduce an element of bias by capturing the input of only those with interest in this topic. More than half of respondents were from general surgery, although this division represents only about one-third of our department. This may limit the generalizability of our results. It would be important to gain insight into the perspectives of those who did not respond to our survey to better understand the impact of adverse events and attitudes toward peer support in our population of surgeons more broadly. Although proportionally fewer participants identified as women than men (27.8% v. 68.7%), this is in line with recent data demonstrating that women constitute around 30% of the surgical workforce in Canada24 and is also reflective of the proportion of female surgeons within our department. The other demographic measures of our survey population are in line with available data on national surgeon demographics, although data are somewhat limited to academic surgeons.25 Finally, a study that depends on the reflections of individuals has strong potential for recall bias and responses may be coloured by the most memorable experiences rather than more common ones. Recall bias may explain some of the more surprising findings in our results, such as the relatively high proportion of participants who reported zero adverse events in the previous 12 months.

Conclusion

In this survey study, we explored surgeons’ experiences with SVS. Surgeons in our cohort were primarily affected by psychological distress and lack of institutional support after experiencing an adverse event. Most surgeons expressed that support from a senior colleague would be most beneficial in recovering from SVS, and more than half were in favour of a local peer support program. This study contributes a Canadian perspective to the growing body of literature describing SVS among surgeons and underscores the importance of peer support. Our hope is that in presenting these findings, we can encourage institutions to continue working to address the substantial effects of SVS on surgeons and mitigate the known effects of provider distress on patient care.26,27 At a time when surgeons are already burdened with system pressures, resource limitations, and ever-growing patient wait-lists, the added distress of suffering in silence must be addressed.
