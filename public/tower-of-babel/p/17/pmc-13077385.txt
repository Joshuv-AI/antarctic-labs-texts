Optimal management of potentially curable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: what are the pillars of therapy?
Authors: Ulrich Ronellenfitsch, Florian Lordick, Jörg Kleeff
Published: 2026

Despite recent epidemiological transitions (1), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains one of the most prevalent gastrointestinal cancers worldwide and continues to have a significant mortality rate. Although the incidence of ESCC is now lower than that of esophageal adenocarcinoma and has declined below 5/100,000 in Northern Europe and North America, it still exceeds 10/100,000 in many Asian countries and East Africa (2). The 5-year survival rate for patients with the disease remains limited, ranging from 10% to 40%, depending on the regional setting and efforts to detect the disease early (3). However, patients without distant metastases, corresponding to International Union for Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stages I–III, are amenable to a potentially curative treatment approach. While it is now widely accepted that chemoradiotherapy (CRT) forms a cornerstone of treatment in patients with stage II or III ESCC (3), the benefit of trimodality therapy, i.e., the addition of surgery in the form of esophagectomy with regional lymphadenectomy, has remained an issue of debate over the last few decades (4).

Sakowitz et al. provide a comprehensive real-world analysis of the treatment of patients with stage II or III ESCC in the United States based on data from the National Cancer Database spanning the years 2004 to 2021 (5). The first noteworthy finding is that in this population comprising unselected patients from more than 1,500 accredited cancer programs, only 24% received trimodality therapy, while 71% received definitive CRT without planned surgery. A small proportion of patients (5%) underwent salvage esophagectomy, defined as resection later than 90 days after the end of CRT, typically due to tumor persistence or local recurrence. Thus, in less than a third of patients, surgery formed part of the therapeutic approach. This reflects evidence from randomized controlled trials available at the time from Asia and Europe, including the landmark trials by Bedenne et al. and Stahl et al., which suggested comparable overall survival for definitive CRT compared with trimodality therapy, with considerable morbidity and mortality associated with surgery, the latter exceeding 10% in some of the trials (6-9). In the series from the US, a high anticipated risk of surgery based on co-morbidities and physical status as well as unfavorable very proximal tumor location, might have influenced the decision for definitive CRT and against trimodality therapy, as reflected by older age and a higher Charlson-Deyo co-morbidity Index in those patients who received definitive CRT. Postoperative mortality is reported at 3% in patients who underwent surgery. However, no stratified figures are presented for upfront trimodality therapy and salvage resections. Thus, it is considerably lower than in the above-referenced trials. Even when considering selection effects, this reflects the fact that esophagectomies for ESCC, both as part of upfront trimodality therapy and as salvage resection, have become much safer over the past two decades (10).

A second important finding from Sakowitz et al.’s study is that, after risk adjustment, both trimodality therapy and salvage esophagectomy are associated with a 9–10-month survival benefit compared with definitive CRT (5). Interestingly, there was no significant difference in survival time between patients who underwent trimodality therapy and those who underwent salvage esophagectomy, but the number of patients in that comparison was small. It is important to note that the study did not include information on the exact regimen and dose of CRT or clinical response for patients who underwent definitive CRT. Therefore, it remains unclear what proportion of patients was actually eligible for active surveillance and which should have been considered for salvage esophagectomy instead. This must be kept in mind when comparing the results of the present study to those of the Dutch SANO (Surgery As Needed for Oesophageal cancer) trial (10). In that trial, patients who achieved a complete response after CRT according to the Chemoradiotherapy for Oesophageal Cancer Followed by Surgery Study (CROSS) trial regimen were randomized to either upfront esophagectomy or active surveillance at 3-month intervals, with salvage esophagectomy in the event of isolated locoregional regrowth. The primary analysis at 2 years showed the non-inferiority of CRT with survival rates of 74% and 71% in the CRT and trimodality arms, respectively. These absolute numbers are comparable to the two-year survival rates reported in the Sakowitz study. Importantly, approximately half of the patients randomized to active surveillance in the SANO trial developed isolated locoregional regrowth. Of those patients, about 85% underwent salvage esophagectomy, and the completeness of resection and postoperative morbidity and mortality were comparable to those of patients randomized to trimodality therapy. Health-related quality of life was better for patients who underwent active surveillance during the first 9 months after completion of CRT. After that, no difference was seen. The ongoing global NEEDS (NEoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy for Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma versus Definitive chemoradiotherapy with salvage Surgery as needed) trial randomizes patients upfront to receive either standard neoadjuvant CRT followed by planned surgery or platinum-based definitive CRT (maximum radiation dose of 50.4 Gy) and salvage esophagectomy in the event of locoregional regrowth. The trial is conducted in North America, Europe, Australia, and Asia. The primary endpoint is non-inferiority of overall survival, and the co-primary endpoint is superiority of quality of life (11).

Based on all available evidence, what should be the recommended treatment for patients with stage II or III ESCC? The typical CRT uses total radiation doses between 41.4 and 50.4 Gy in a scheme commonly employed in a neoadjuvant setting. The regimen also includes a combination chemotherapy with platinum-taxane or platinum-fluoropyrimidine. Based on the SANO trial, active surveillance is an option in complete clinical responders as confirmed by imaging and thorough biopsies, if sufficiently frequent and detailed exams (endoscopy with biopsies, imaging) can be reliably done. With this strategy, organ preservation is attainable while at the same time salvage esophagectomy can be carried out safely with no greater perioperative risk and no worse oncological outcome than that associated with upfront esophagectomy. If the availability of high-frequency exams or the compliance of the patient is questionable, upfront trimodality therapy including planned surgery should be preferred. In the absence of complete clinical response, patients should proceed to esophagectomy unless they are deemed unfit for surgery by an experienced esophageal surgeon or have a very proximal tumor location. Escalation of radiation dose to 61.6 Gy or more was not shown to improve locoregional recurrence-free or overall survival in the recent phase III ARTDECO (A Randomized Trial of Dose Escalation in definitive Chemoradiotherapy for patients with Oesophageal cancer) trial, which employed modern radiation techniques (12). In case of tumor persistence or regrowth, salvage esophagectomy can be considered on an individual basis, with awareness of the associated morbidity and mortality risks (13). For all patients who underwent macroscopically complete resection without pathological complete response in the resection specimen following neoadjuvant CRT, adjuvant nivolumab for 1 year is recommended (14).

Neoadjuvant or adjuvant immunotherapy based on immune checkpoint inhibition may lead to a profound change in the curative treatment of esophageal SCC. These drugs already constitute an integral part of first- and second-line treatment in metastatic or locally advanced unresectable disease. Several randomized controlled trials have established regimens combining cytotoxic agents with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), or even dual ICI therapy without chemotherapy. Most studies showed a compelling effect only in tumors with a certain level of programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression (15,16), whereas others did so irrespective of PD-L1 expression (17-19). It is only logical to extend this approach to the neoadjuvant setting. In fact, phase II trials with ICI have shown very promising results with higher rates of pathological complete response and an equivalent safety profile compared to neoadjuvant CRT (20-24). Phase III trials with survival as primary endpoint are ongoing, with some of them having published encouraging interim results regarding pathological regression and safety (25-28). Positive survival results would most likely establish ICI as a new standard element of neoadjuvant therapy. The challenges ahead include defining which patients would benefit most from this novel approach, determining how long the treatment should last, identifying the most effective combinations with cytotoxic drugs and radiotherapy, and establishing whether active surveillance is warranted after a complete response to treatment including ICI. Overall, it is expected that neoadjuvant therapy for potentially curable ESCC will become more personalized. Depending on patient and tumor characteristics including molecular signatures ascertained at different times during therapy, which potentially allow for identifying subgroups of patients benefiting most from certain treatments such as ICI or surgery (29,30), the pillars of treatment—chemotherapy, radiotherapy, checkpoint inhibition, and surgery—will all play valid and important roles.
