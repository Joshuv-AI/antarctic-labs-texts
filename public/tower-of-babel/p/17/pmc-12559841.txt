Synaptic Plasticity of D1 and D2 Types of Neurons in the Central Amygdala After Sucrose and Cocaine Exposure
Authors: Łukasz Bijoch, Justyna Wiśniewska, Anna Beroun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDopamine‐sensitive neurons are organized in two classes of cells, expressing D1‐ or D2‐type dopamine receptors, and often mediate opposing aspects of reward‐oriented behaviors. Here, we focused on D1‐ and D2‐type neurons in the central amygdala, a brain structure critically involved in processing emotion‐related stimuli. We demonstrated the distinct composition of the central amygdala based on these receptors: the lateral part (CeL) consists almost exclusively of D2‐type neurons, while the medial part (CeM) contains a mix of both D1‐ and D2‐type neurons. Recording excitatory postsynaptic currents' frequency in D1 and D2‐type neurons after exposing mice to rewarding stimuli, we showed that in the CeM, cocaine injections trigger opposite changes of spontaneous excitatory synaptic transmission than exposure to sucrose.

Introduction

The brain's dopaminergic system is critically involved in reward and fear learning, as well as responses to novel stimuli, serving as an indicator of novelty. This system also holds strong clinical importance, as dopaminergic dysregulation is implicated in substance‐related disorders, with drugs of abuse directly or indirectly affecting its balance (Weinshenker and Schroeder 2007; Luscher 2016). Consequently, much effort has been made to characterize dopamine function in the mammalian brain. Although dopamine is released from two predominant brain areas: the substantia nigra (SN) and the ventral tegmental area (VTA), dopamine‐sensitive neurons are widely distributed throughout the brain where they play a fundamental role in enabling motivation and reinforced learning, affect decision‐making and attention, participate in various forms of learning and plasticity mechanisms to name just a few. The universally acknowledged model divides them into D1‐ and D2‐type cells, best described in the striatum, where they reside in nonoverlapping cellular populations (Surmeier et al. 2007). In general, D1‐type cells express dopamine receptors D1 (DRD1; the most abundant of D1‐type) and D5 that bind to stimulatory G protein and promote cAMP production. Conversely, D2‐type cells express dopamine receptors D2 (DRD2; the most abundant of D2‐type), D3, and D4 that decrease adenylate cyclase activity through inhibitory G protein binding. Thus, as dopamine elicits fundamentally different responses, depending on their type, cells expressing DRD1 or DRD2 are believed to opposingly regulate brain activity and participate in various aspects of reward‐related behaviors. This opposing regulation has been particularly well‐studied in the nucleus accumbens (NAC)—a key brain structure involved in the execution of motivated behaviors (Gerfen and Surmeier 2011; Kravitz et al. 2012; Berridge and Kringelbach 2015), where medium spiny projection neurons (MSN) expressing DRD1 or DRD2 respond differently to natural rewards (e.g., sucrose) and drugs of abuse (e.g., cocaine and morphine) (Calipari et al. 2016; Graziane et al. 2016; Bijoch, Klos, Pawlowska, et al. 2023). More recently, it became apparent that functionally and genetically distinct populations of the NAC DRD1(+) MSNs drive various aspects of reward‐ and addiction‐related behaviors (Zhao et al. 2022).

Although the striatum is crucial for addiction development, recent studies show that the central amygdala (CeA) is critically involved in reward processing and drug‐related memory formation. As a part of the amygdala complex, which serves as a hub for processing emotionally rich stimuli, its central nucleus help assign valence to stimuli and thus to mediate appetitive and aversive memory formation that later drives behavior (Lu et al. 2005; Knapska et al. 2007; Knapska et al. 2013; Li et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2017; Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023). The central amygdala with its subnuclei: medial, lateral, and capsular (CeM, CeL, and CeC, respectively) is functionally and genetically immensely heterogeneous, and its various distinct neuronal populations are recruited by appetitive and aversive stimuli (Kim et al. 2017).

We have recently demonstrated that sugar and cocaine exposures, thus both considered rewarding, triggered different plasticity changes in the CeL and CeM nuclei. The central lateral nucleus turned out to be involved in the processing of cocaine exposure while the CeM nucleus was engaged by both rewards (Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023). Thus, in this study, we looked closer at the cellular composition of the CeM nucleus and investigated the involvement of D1‐ and D2‐type neurons in response to cocaine or sugar exposures. With electrophysiological recordings of spontaneous synaptic transmission in these neurons, we demonstrated the opposing effects of cocaine and sugar on DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells' frequency of excitatory currents.

Results

DRD1 and DRD2 Are Expressed in nonoverlapping Populations in Subnuclei of the CeA

The CeA, even though often described as a single entity, is a heterogeneous structure composed of nuclei with different functions and cell types (Knapska et al. 2007; Kim et al. 2017). To study the expression patterns of dopamine receptors in its two major parts, CeM and CeL subnuclei, we examined the expression of reporter proteins TdTomato and EGFP in a transgenic mouse line: Drd1a‐TdTomato/Drd2‐GFP (Ade et al. 2011; Nelson et al. 2012; Wei et al. 2018).

The total number of cells and percentage of neurons (characterized by NeuN labeling) did not differ between CeM and CeL (Figure S1A–D). We found that 45.9% of all cells in the CeL and 39.9% in the CeM had dopamine receptors (shown via either tdTomato or GFP fluorescence). However, the composition of these populations varied between the nuclei: In the CeM, DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells formed sparse populations, whereas in the CeL, almost all fluorescent neurons were DRD2(+) (Figure 1A–C, Figure S1C). Cells co‐expressing both these receptors were only a minor portion in both nuclei. To further check the identity of these reporter protein‐expressing cells, we used three well‐known markers of the CeA: protein kinase C delta (PKCδ), protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 1B (PPP1R1B, also known as dopamine‐ and cAMP‐regulated neuronal phosphoprotein DARPP‐32), and corticotropin‐releasing factor (CRF). PKCδ was previously shown to be mostly limited to the CeL (De Bundel et al. 2016; Dilly et al. 2022), and our analysis revealed that indeed 32.5% of cells in CeL and only 6.8% in CeM were PKCδ(+) (Figure S1F–H). Labeling this protein in slices obtained from D1‐TdTomato/D2‐GFP mice showed that PKCδ (+) in both CeM and CeL was mostly expressed in DRD2(+) cells (8.7% in CeM and 35% in CeL). Interestingly, only 0.6% of DRD1(+) cells in the CeM were PKCδ (+) (Figure 1D,E).

Cocaine and Sugar Induce c‐Fos Expression in DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) Neurons in the CeA

Immediate early gene c‐Fos is a marker of neuronal activity that induces synaptic plasticity (Sheng and Greenberg 1990; de Hoz et al. 2018; Yap and Greenberg 2018). Following our previous observations that repeated exposures to cocaine or sucrose trigger c‐Fos expression in the CeM, while neurons in the CeL expressed c‐Fos only in response to cocaine (Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023), we wanted to investigate whether these two rewards affect distinct dopamine receptor‐expressing neuronal populations in these nuclei. Therefore, we examined the expression of c‐Fos protein in DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons in the CeA of mice exposed to cocaine or sucrose.

We employed a 7‐day reward exposure protocol, previously shown to induce plasticity changes in the NAC and the CeA (Bijoch, Klos, Pawlowska, et al. 2023; Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023). Drd1a‐TdTomato/Drd2‐GFP mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of cocaine for seven consecutive days (Figure 2A). During this period, the locomotor activity of mice was recorded for 30 min using a camera positioned above the cage (Figure 2B). The recorded videos were later analyzed with DeepLabCut software (Mathis et al. 2018) to calculate the locomotor activity of mice (Figure S2B). Analysis revealed that mice exposed to cocaine were more mobile and travelled longer distances than those receiving saline injections (Figure 2C,D; Figure S2C). The drug effects were rapid, with a peak occurring already 1.5 min after the injection, and demonstrated increasing velocity over the first 3–4 days of repeated exposure to cocaine (Figure 2E,F). However, this increasing locomotor activity, measured as the distance travelled, was only visible on the first and second days (Figure 2C). Cocaine‐exposed mice displayed stereotypic behaviors, including frequent, abrupt changes in velocity, alternating between rapid acceleration and sudden stopping. Rather than engaging in continuous running, these mice exhibited fragmented locomotor patterns consisting of multiple discrete running bouts (Fig. S E–G).

Additionally, the locomotor paths of mice exposed to cocaine were characterized by higher tortuosity (i.e., more winding and irregular trajectories) compared to those of saline‐exposed controls (Figure S2H). Finally, based on DeepLabCut tracking data we trained a SimBA random forest behavioral classifier (Goodwin et al. 2024) to find the presence of rearing and grooming behaviors, and we found that cocaine‐exposed mice expressed fewer of them (Figure S2I,J). Two hours after the onset of the final reward exposure, mice were sacrificed and perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Their brains were sliced on a vibratome, and slices were immunolabeled for c‐Fos protein. Cocaine increased c‐Fos levels in both DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons in the CeM, indicating that it is processed by both these populations (Figure 2G–K). In the CeL, on the other hand, this increase was limited only for DRD2(+) because of a very low number of DRD1(+) cells in that nucleus (Figure 2L).

To evaluate the effects of sucrose exposure, another set of mice received access to a bottle containing sweet water (7.5% sucrose solution) for 2 h per day over seven consecutive days (Figure 3A–C). This bottle was connected to a custom‐made lickometer to measure time spent on drinking. Like previous observations, we noted a strong preference for sweet water (Figure 3C) (Bijoch, Klos, Pawlowska, et al. 2023; Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023).

Similarly to cocaine, sucrose also increased c‐Fos levels in both DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons in CeM and only in DRD2(+) for CeL, indicating that both these rewards were processed by a similar fraction of cells (Figure 2D–I).

Although the effects of cocaine and sucrose were similar on the c‐Fos expression in the CeM and CeL, it is worth noting that DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) were only a fraction of the cells expressing c‐Fos. By analyzing all c‐Fos(+) cells in the CeA, we noticed that while neurons in the CeM, were activated by both cocaine and sucrose (Figure S3A–C), in the CeL, only cocaine significantly increased the c‐Fos level (Figure S3B,C). Although most imaged slices came from transgenic mice expressing the reporter gene in either DRD1(+) or DRD2(+) neurons, some slices from double‐transgenic animals allowed us to compare c‐Fos levels in neurons expressing tdTomato and GFP to the entire population of c‐Fos–positive neurons in the CeM and the CeL. Despite the limited number of double‐labeled samples, our analysis indicated that DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells combined constituted less than 50% of all c‐Fos–positive cells, indicating that numerous neuronal populations in CeA were also activated during sucrose and cocaine exposures (Figure S3D,E).

Cocaine and Sucrose Exposures Induce Opposing Changes in the Frequency of Spontaneous Excitatory Currents in DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) Neurons in the CeM

Immediate early gene expression products are useful tools for identifying cells undergoing plasticity changes in response to various stimuli. However, the manner of plasticity changes can involve the strengthening or weakening of synaptic connections between neurons, which can be studied by examining the electrical properties of neurons. Therefore, in another set of experiments, we performed ex vivo whole‐cell patch clamp experiments on brain slices from Drd1a‐adTomato/Drd2‐GFP mice. In these experiments, we focused on the CeM, where both DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells are present.

Two hours after the final exposure to cocaine or sucrose, mice were sacrificed, and their brains were sliced using a vibratome. DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons were identified based on their endogenous fluorescence, and spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) were recorded to assess their excitatory (AMPA receptor‐mediated) drive on recorded neurons (Figure 4A).

Neuronal Classification and Excitability of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) Neurons in the CeM

We assessed the neuronal excitability of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons following exposure to either cocaine or sucrose. To this end, neurons were injected with increasing current steps ranging from 0 to 200 pA (increments of 10 pA, 600‐ms pulses). Among all recorded neurons, we observed considerable variability in their firing patterns. Amid both DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells, we found two types of neurons with different adaptation to stimulation patterns. First class, called tonic, fired action potentials throughout the whole 600‐ms period of stimulation. The second class, called phasic (rapidly adapting), was characterized by action potentials evoked only at the beginning of a 600‐ms pulse, and their lack at the end of the stimulus (Figure 7A). The majority of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons exhibited tonic firing patterns (70.4% and 72.6%, respectively) (Figure 7B,C).

Within the tonic‐ and phasic‐firing groups, neurons were further categorized as low‐threshold or high‐threshold based on whether their peak response occurred by stimulation with current lower or higher than 60 pA. In both DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) populations, most tonic neurons were classified as high‐threshold (77.9% and 77%, respectively). In contrast, for phasic neurons, DRD1(+) were mostly low‐threshold (57.5%), and DRD2(+) were mostly high‐threshold (53.6%).

Additionally, we identified a small subset of neurons that fired 2–3 action potentials rapidly at the onset of stimulation, indicative of a bursting firing pattern. Bursting neurons accounted for only 3% of DRD1(+) cells but 10% of DRD2(+) cells (Figure 7B,C).

Since tonic neurons constituted the largest fraction of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells, they were selected for comparing excitability between control and reward‐exposed mice (Figure S4). We observed that DRD1(+) neurons from cocaine‐treated mice exhibited reduced excitability (Figure S4A). We observed no change in the excitability of neurons after exposure to sugar (Figure S4B,D).

Discussion

The CeA is an immensely complex structure with diverse inputs and cell types, reflecting its key function in processing and interpreting emotional responses in various behaviors. Growing evidence supports its role in the development of addiction to substances, such as alcohol, opioids, or stimulants (Stefaniuk et al. 2017; Chaudun et al. 2024). Although the CeA is not traditionally considered a primary site of dopaminergic activity, recent research has shown that dopamine signaling plays a crucial role in modulating emotional responses, stress, and addiction‐related behaviors. In this article, we described that DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons (i) differentially populate the CeA nuclei, (ii) are both engaged in reward processing, and (iii) exhibit opposing changes in their spontaneous excitatory event frequency after reward exposure.

DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) Cells Distribution in the CeA

The view on dopamine receptor distribution in the CeA has changed over the years, starting with studies in the ‘90s describing the central amygdala of the rat as being populated mainly by the Drd2‐expressing cells, while the BLA was predominantly expressing Drd1a (Weiner et al. 1991; Scibilia et al. 1992). More recently, in RNA sequencing studies in mice and rats, DRD1(+) cells were assigned to intercalated cells of the amygdala, CeM, and CeL nuclei, while DRD2(+) cells were found in the CeC, CeM, and CeL (McCullough, Daskalakis, et al. 2018; Dilly et al. 2022; Hochgerner et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023).

In our study, we used transgenic mouse lines and analyzed the distribution of reporter proteins tdTomato and EGFP that should correspond to the expression of Drd1a and Drd2 genes. Though we did not validate the colocalization of the endogenous D1 and D2 receptor expression with the reporter protein, the distribution of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells in the CeA nuclei we observed aligned well with the distribution observed with the RNAscope (McCullough, Daskalakis, et al. 2018) and other transgenic mouse lines labeling D1 and D2 cells, such as Drd1a‐GFP (Bjerke et al. 2022), Drd1a‐Cre and Drd2‐Cre (Wei et al. 2018).

Overall, our data confirm that the CeA is predominantly composed of DRD2(+) cells. However, we also found that the CeL nucleus is almost entirely lacking DRD1(+) neurons. In contrast, the CeM nucleus contains both DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons in roughly equal proportions, together making up nearly 40% of all cells in this region. In this regard, we show that CeA resembles other brain structures exhibiting such a clear separation of dopamine receptors. Neurons expressing both receptors are rare and limited to specific brain regions, such as the ventral part of the striatum. Similarly, the presence of only one of these receptors within a single region is uncommon. Examples of such structures include the medial part of the striatum, which contains only DRD2(+) cells, and the intercalated nuclei, which exclusively contain DRD1(+) cells (Giros et al. 1996; Zhang et al. 1998; Gangarossa et al. 2019).

The RNA sequencing studies revealed that the CeA is a brain region rich with diverse cell types, each characterized by specific receptors or proteins that define their physiological role. In this study, we colocalized DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells with PPPR1B1, which is a marker associated with inhibitory cells in the CeA and excitatory cells in the BLA (Kim et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2017). Using RNA sequencing methods, others have seen a similar picture (Hochgerner et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023), showing that some of the PPP1B1(+) cells also express Drd1a and Drd2 genes. Similarly to our observation, that not all DRD2(+) and DRD1(+) cells colocalize with PPPR1B1(+) cells. Wang et al. (2023) saw Drd1a‐ and Drd2‐expressing cells in population clusters that do not express Ppp1r1b gene. These findings suggest that, unlike the canonical dopaminergic pathways in the striatum, where D1 and D2 receptors participate in the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of PPP1R1B, dopamine receptor pathways in the CeA engage more heterogeneous, noncanonical protein signaling (Nishi et al. 1997).

We also checked for the colocalization of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells with PKCδ, a protein especially abundant in CeL neurons (McCullough, Morrison, et al. 2018). PKCδ(+) neurons in the CeL are known to participate in defensive behaviors and processing of painful stimuli (Lin et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2022), while cocaine i.p. injections, as well as DRD2 agonist, increase the number of c‐Fos(+) in the CeL (Casey et al. 2022). Similarly to the earlier report (McCullough, Morrison, et al. 2018), we showed only a partial overlap of DRD2 and PKCδ, with the majority of cells expressing one marker or the other. We saw that DRD2(+) cells in the CeL (and to a lesser extent DRD1(+)) also colocalize with the CRF marker. CRF(+) cells in the CeA were shown to participate in discriminating fearful cues (Sanford et al. 2017). In the early life adversity models, inhibition of these cells was shown to reduce the startle to fearful stimuli (Demaestri et al. 2024). Others showed that CRF mRNA levels were increased in the CeA in alcohol binge‐drinking models (Kreifeldt et al. 2022). Moreover, blocking CRF(+) cells in the CeA projecting to the lateral hypothalamus, by chemogenetic and genetic means, reduces binge‐like drinking but only in male mice (Bendrath et al. 2024). Together, since CRF(+) and PKCδ(+) neurons represent a distinct population within the CeA, these findings suggest that DRD2 expression is not specific to either, but rather distributed across both cell types (Sanford et al. 2017).

DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) Cells Plasticity in Reward Processing in the CeA

The role of D1‐ and D2‐expressing neurons in the amygdala has been mainly studied by applying dopamine or dopamine receptor agonists/antagonists on brain slices or injecting into the brain via implanted cannulas. On a cellular level, dopamine modulates cell excitability. In the BLA acute slices, the application of dopamine or activation of D1 receptors (but not D2) enhanced the excitability of projection neurons and interneurons (Kroner et al. 2005). In the ITC nuclei, which are composed of DRD1(+) cells only, dopamine application reduces excitability, which in turn causes disinhibition of the BLA and CeA (Marowsky et al. 2005). The direct role of D1 and D2‐receptor activation in the CeM is unclear. On a behavioral level, in the aspect of feeding behavior, it was reported that activation of D2‐expressing cells reduced food intake and impaired sucrose operant conditioning in rats, while inhibition or activation of D1‐expressing cells did not have much effect on these behaviors (Anderberg et al. 2014). On the other hand, D1‐receptor blocking in the CeA reduced orexin‐strengthened saccharin sweetener preference (Risco and Mediavilla 2018). In other studies, activation of D2 cells in the central amygdala showed their involvement in rewarding pain relief properties. As the number of D2 receptors is decreased, activating D2 cells with an agonist reverses pain relief (Huang et al. 2022). Others showed the involvement of dopamine‐sensitive cells in cocaine withdrawal, which was shown to increase the D1 receptor level in the CeL after 2 weeks of abstinence from cocaine (Krishnan et al. 2010), while activation of D2 receptors (but not D1) decreased cocaine self‐administration and cocaine‐induced reinstatement of seeking behavior (Thiel et al. 2010). Others showed in rats that D1 receptor antagonist blocked cocaine seeking reinstatement and c‐Fos expression in the cortex and BLA after short‐ and long‐term abstinence (Ciccocioppo et al. 2001). Studies show that amygdalar D2‐type cells, located mainly in the CeL, are involved in defensive behaviors in many fear‐related behavioral assays. Chemogenetic activation of D2 cells increased freezing during extinction sessions in fear‐conditioned mice (McCullough, Daskalakis, et al. 2018). Knock‐out of D2 receptors in neurons that express Wolfram syndrome 1 gene have been shown to reduce active avoidance learning and regulate food‐seeking behaviors (Castell et al. 2024). Moreover, bilateral deletion of D2 receptors in the CeL increased active avoidance behaviors (less time spent in the open arms of the elevated plus maze and lit area of lit/dark box) (Casey et al. 2022). Blocking D2 receptors with antagonist infusions into CeL and BNST (areas highly populated by PKCδ cells) increased fear generalization, manifested as increased freezing to sound cues not paired with a foot shock (De Bundel et al. 2016). Another study showed that dopaminergic ventral PAG/dorsal Raphe neurons (that co‐express glutamate) projecting to CeA and synapse onto PKCδ and SST cells are the main source of dopamine in the amygdala following a foot shock. Ablation or chemogenetic blocking of these neurons reduced fear learning (Groessl et al. 2018). D2 neurons projecting from CeA to the BNST regulate impulsive behaviors, as their optogenetic stimulation was shown to reduce the number of premature nosepokes to obtain food reward.

Focusing specifically and separately on D1‐ or D2‐type cell populations, we show that in the CeM, both D1 and D2 populations exhibit similar firing patterns that can be characterized into tonic, phasic, and bursting subtypes. Only cocaine exposure reduced the excitability of DRD1(+) phasic cells.

Repeated reward exposures trigger the expression of plasticity marker c‐Fos in similar numbers of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells. It indicates that both these rewards engage both types of cells. Moreover, it is worth noting that the percentage of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons among all c‐Fos(+) cells was less than 50%. It indicates that these cells were not the only subpopulation participating in cocaine or sugar processing in the CeA.

Typically, c‐Fos expression is induced in the amygdala by re‐exposure to drug‐related cues after prolonged abstinence (Neisewander et al. 2000; Ciccocioppo et al. 2001; Radwanska et al. 2008), marking cells that were engaged in cocaine‐exposure processing. Here, similarly to our previous observations while studying immediate early genes expression (c‐Fos and Arc (Bijoch, Klos, Pawlowska, et al. 2023; Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023)), we observed that repeated exposure to cocaine or sucrose (without prolonged abstinence) triggers c‐Fos expression in the central amygdala. It is an open question whether a new population is activated after each reward exposure or if the same cells are constantly engaged in their processing.

c‐Fos is referred to as a marker of neuronal activity that triggers synaptic plasticity (Kaczmarek 1992). Studies show its involvement in late phases of both LTP and LTD (Kemp et al. 2013; Gandolfi et al. 2017). However, questions remain about whether the electrophysiological changes we observed reflect specific plasticity changes in DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) populations, or rather, c‐Fos reflects overall circuit engagement. One of cocaine's pharmacological actions is blocking the dopamine reuptake, leading to increased dopamine levels in the synaptic cleft. Because D2‐type receptor activation decreases neuronal excitability, cocaine's exposure would predominantly inhibit the activity of D2‐type neurons. These cells, however, still receive excitatory inputs from other structures such as the BLA. Thus, we speculate that c‐Fos expression induced by cocaine exposure could be a hallmark of ongoing plasticity triggered by decreased excitatory drive. Similarly, c‐Fos expression triggered by repeated cocaine exposure in D1‐type cells could signify D1‐type receptor stimulation or synaptic reorganization due to the increased excitatory input. Thus, c‐Fos expression appears to signify an ongoing change in neuronal plasticity. Its direction, whether it leads to a larger excitation or inhibition, is specific to the neuronal population and overall circuitry activity.

The electrophysiological recordings of synaptic function revealed that both D1‐ and D2‐type cells underwent opposing changes in their synaptic response to sucrose and cocaine. With no effect on spine density in DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) populations, the observed change in the frequency of spontaneous EPSCs points to a presynaptic mechanism, such as changes in the probability of neurotransmitter release or increased presynaptic activity of the network. Our data suggest that extended (7‐day) exposure to i.p. cocaine injections increases glutamatergic drive to DRD1(+) neurons and decreases it to DRD2(+). Sucrose exerts the exact opposite effect—after 7‐day exposure, DRD2(+) neurons would receive stronger excitatory input than DRD1(+) cells. Overall, without distinguishing different neuronal populations, our previous study reported an increase in the number of silent synapses—immature glutamatergic connections that were triggered by both sucrose and cocaine exposures (Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023). Their appearance and subsequent maturation would increase glutamatergic transmission to the CeM neurons. Here we report that DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) neurons in the CeM do not change their spine number after exposure to rewards. Thus, it appears that presynaptic adaptations depend on the cell type, and rewards shift the balance of how DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells would be engaged in their processing.

Interestingly, we observed how DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells differ in their baseline sEPSCs frequency values. Compared to DRD1(+) neurons, DRD2(+) cells have a 3‐times lower incidence of sEPSCs when mice were exposed to water, but a 2‐times higher incidence when exposed to saline. In previous experiments where we randomly sampled cells from heterogeneous populations of the CeM, we observed a baseline sEPSCs inter‐event interval to be in‐between the levels of DRD1(+) and DRD2(+) cells (Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023), which further highlights differences in various CeM neuronal populations and shows the necessity to analyze them separately.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All experiments were performed on adult, DRD1‐TdTomato/DRD2‐GFP (B6.Cg‐Tg (Drd1a‐tdTomato)6Calak/J, RRID:IMSR_JAX:016204, crossed with B6.FVB (Cg)‐Tg (Drd2‐EGFP)S118Gsat/KreMmucd, RRID:MMRRC_036931‐UCD), mice of both sexes, 3–5 months old. Mice were housed under a 12‐h/12‐h light/dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. Because the electrophysiology technique allowed us to record one mouse per day, the behavioral protocol had a staggered entry. Thus, mice were housed in individual cages. Only the mice used exclusively for imaging experiments to visualize D1‐ and D2‐cell distributions were group‐housed. Because of the staggered entry and clearly visible changes in behavior after reward exposures, the experimenter was not blinded to the treatment. At the level of data analysis, however, mice were coded with numbers, and results were assigned to the appropriate group when the analysis was finished.

All studies were performed following the European Council Directive of November 24, 1986 (86/609/EEC), Animal Protection Act of Poland, and approved by the 1st Local Ethics Committee in Warsaw (permission numbers 463/2018 and 1225/2021). All efforts were made to minimize the number of animals and their suffering. Sample sizes were determined using standard power calculations (power = 0.90, α = 0.05), with variance and effect size estimates derived from our previous studies. In line with the guidelines of the Local Ethics Committee, the number of animals was minimized to the lowest level consistent with obtaining statistically reliable results. Numbers of animals in each experiment are indicated in the figure legends.

Cocaine or Sucrose Exposure of Freely Moving Animals

To study the cocaine's effect on freely moving, Drd1a‐TdTomato/Drd2‐GFP mice received two 100‐μL injections of saline for 2 days. Then, we injected them with 100 μL of saline for another 7 consecutive days or 100 μL of 20 mg/kg body weight of cocaine hydrochloride dissolved in saline. Immediately after the injection, mice were placed in another, larger cage (33 × 21 cm), and their locomotor activity was recorded for 30 min with a camera suspended above the cage. DeepLabCut software was used to track several points on the mouse body. Based on these tracking points the distance covered by animals, and other parameters of their locomotion were calculated. Based on DeepLabCut data, SimBA random forest behavioral classifier was trained to measure the occurrence of rearing and grooming behaviors.

For the sweet water exposure experiment, mice were moved to another cage for 2 h, where they had access to a bottle with either sweet (7.5% sucrose solution) or fresh water. All mice were habituated to the new cage for 2 days before the experiment. During that time only freshwater bottle was available. Then, mice were exposed to water or 7.5% sucrose solution for 7 consecutive days. To measure drinking time, we used a previously described device based on the programmable microcontroller Arduino Leonardo, which detects the closing of the electric circuit (Bijoch, Klos, Pekala, et al. 2023).

Immunolabeling and Confocal Imaging

Mice were perfused with cold PBS followed by 4% PFA dissolved in PBS. Their brains were removed and kept in 4% PFA for 24 h in the 4°C and later in 4°C PBS. Brains were sliced on a vibratome (Leica VT 1200 S) into 100‐μm slices. Brain slices from DRD1‐TdTomato/DRD2‐GFP mice were permeabilized at room temperature in 0.25% Triton X‐100 in PBS and then incubated for 3 h in a blocking solution (3% BSA and 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS). Slices were then incubated for 24 h at 0°C in 0.1% Triton X‐100 and 0.1% BSA in PBS containing a mix of antibodies against endogenous fluorophores: anti‐GFP (Abcam ab13970, 1:1500) and anti‐RFP (ThermoFisher M11217, 1:1500). Additionally, a mix contained antibodies against either c‐Fos (Synaptic Systems 226,003, 1:2000), or PKCδ (Abcam ab182126, 1:1500), or CRF (Synaptic Systems 266,011, 1:500), or PPP1R1B (abcam ab40801, 1:1000). Later, brain slices were incubated for 3 h at room temperature with a mix of secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher A‐11039 and A32733, 1:400 and Abcam ab150158, 1:400) that had been previously centrifuged at 20,000 g for 10 min.

Brain slices were placed on coverslips with a mounting medium (Fluoromont‐G‐DAPI) and imaged using Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope with 10× (dry, NA = 0.3) and 40× (oil, NA = 1.4) objectives. Diode lasers (405 nm and 561 nm) and an argon laser (488 nm) were used for fluorescence excitation. Signals were collected at the spectra 340–350, 510–530, and 575–590 nm. Images were later analyzed with a Cell‐Counter plugin in FIJI software for manual neuron counting. Percentages of cells labeled by different markers were calculated relative to the number of DAPI‐positive nuclei.

Electrophysiology and Neuronal Dendritic Spine Density Measurements

Two hours after the last reward exposure, mice were sacrificed by decapitation. Their brains were removed and sliced in ice‐cold NMDG solution (135 mM NMDG, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 10 mM D‐glucose, 20 mM choline bicarbonate) saturated with carbogen (5% CO2 and 95% O2) using a vibratome (Leica VT 1200S). 250 μm brain slices were incubated in 31°C ACSF solution (119 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 20 mM D‐glucose; 2.5 mM CaCl2) for 10 min, and then for at least another hour in room temperature ACSF solution saturated with carbogen.

Electrophysiology experiments were performed in a carbogen‐saturated ACSF solution with 50 μM picrotoxin to block inhibitory transmission. Borosilicate electrodes (4–6 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution (pH 7.1, osmolarity 290–295 mOsm, 120 mM K‐gluconate, 2 mM MgCl2, 58 0.4 mM EGTA, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 10 mM HEPES, 2.5 mM Na2‐ATP, 0.25 mM Na3‐GTP) containing also 5 mM biocytin. All recordings were performed using Igor PRO (Wavemetrics) and a setup equipped with a universal amplifier (npi ELC03XS) and Multi‐Channel Data Acquisition Interface (ITC‐18, InstruTECH/HEKA). Signals were recorded at 10 kHz and filtered at 2 kHz.

In the current‐clamp mode, cells were injected with an increasing step current ranging from 0 to 200 pA (10 pA increments, step duration 600 ms). Each current step was repeated 3 times. Action potential numbers were calculated using Clampfit (10.3) software.

In the voltage‐clamp mode, the membrane potential was set to −60 mV and sEPSCs were recorded for 20 min and later analyzed using Clampfit (10.3) software. After each sEPSC recording, the cell was switched to the current‐clamp mode and was stimulated for 10 min with 20 pA current to spread the biocytin within the cell. Brain slices were then collected and fixed for 30 min in 4% PFA in PBS at 4°C and labeled with streptavidin conjugated with Alexa Fluor 568 (ThermoFisher S11226) for 3 h at room temperature. Labeled brain slices were placed on coverslips and imaged using a Zeiss Laser Scanning Microscope 780 with a 63× objective (561‐nm argon laser and 575‐ to 590‐nm filters). Images were taken at 1024 × 1024 px with a 1.15‐μm z‐step and analyzed using a Dendritic Spine Counter, a plugin for Fiji software.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software and Matlab R2022a. For each group, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using Levene's test, followed by analysis of differences in statistical means. Statistical significance was determined for p < 0.05. Statistical comparisons were performed based on the number of animals in each group. Group sizes are presented in each figure legend, as n(m), where n is the number of animals and (m) is the number of recorded cells/analyzed images/dendrites, where indicated.
