Placental Malperfusion Is Associated With Adverse Outcomes in Congenital Heart Disease and With Genetic Variants in Placental Developmental Pathways
Authors: Rebecca Josowitz, Stacy Woyciechowski, Tanaya Jadhav, Tejas Jammihal, Rebecca L Linn, Jack Rychik, J William Gaynor, Ramakrishnan Rajagopalan, Nancy B Spinner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPlacental malperfusion (PMP), a vascular disorder of impaired maternal or fetal placental perfusion, is common in fetuses with congenital heart disease (CHD), yet its impact on outcomes and underlying mechanisms are unclear. Perturbation of shared developmental pathways between the fetal heart and placenta may play a role in PMP development.OBJECTIVEThis study sought to determine whether CHD fetuses with PMP have adverse outcomes and genomic variation in vascular or placental developmental pathways.METHODSThis was a retrospective case-control study of 299 CHD fetuses with and without PMP. Maternal, genomic, placental, and outcomes data were reviewed. Placentas with PMP met pathologic criteria for maternal or fetal vascular malperfusion (Amsterdam criteria), or placental weight <10%. Rare, predicted deleterious exonic/putative splicing single nucleotide variants, small insertion/deletions, and de novo variants from trios were identified from genome sequencing data, followed by burden analysis and pathway enrichment. Mann-Whitney and Student’s-t test or chi-square and Fisher exact tests were performed.RESULTSPMP was present in 51% of fetuses. Nonsyndromic fetuses with PMP (n = 122) vs without PMP (n = 124) had decreased birth weight (2,986 g vs 3,330 g, P < 0.001), length (48 cm vs 48.9 cm, P < 0.001), and head circumference (33 cm vs 34 cm, P < 0.001); a longer index hospitalization (21 days vs 15 days, P = 0.04); and a trend toward increased mortality (12.3% vs 5.6%, P = 0.07). No significant differences were found in syndromic subjects. PMP was associated with increased mortality in univariable analysis (unadjusted HR: 1.5; 95% CI: 1.03–2.17; P = 0.03), with borderline significance in multivariable analysis (adjusted HR: 1.44; 95% CI: 0.995–2.09; P = 0.054). The proportion of fetuses with and without PMP with ≥1 angiogenesis pathway or de novo variant was similar and not associated with outcomes. All fetuses with PMP demonstrated distinct enrichment in de novo variants in Notch signaling (NOTCH1, NOTCH3, DLL4, SMAD6, MMP2, ID2, SOX9, CHD7) and extracellular matrix regulation and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (COL4A4, FN1, MMP2, VIM, TIE1, DSP, PUF60, HNRNPK, SPON1) in genes critical for trophoblast and placental vascular development.CONCLUSIONPMP is associated with impaired fetal growth, longer hospitalization, and possibly increased mortality in CHD. CHD fetuses with PMP harbor deleterious de novo variants in several key placental and cardiac developmental pathways, which may represent important mechanisms underlying PMP in CHD.Keywords: congenital heart disease, de novo variants, placental malperfusion

Recent data implicate the maternal–fetal environment, and specifically placental health, in the development and outcomes of congenital heart disease (CHD).1–3 Placental malperfusion (PMP), the pathologic sequelae of impaired maternal or fetal placental perfusion, is observed in higher frequencies in mothers carrying fetuses with CHD.4–8 Additionally, clinical PMP conditions such as preeclampsia increase the risk of having a fetus with CHD.9–11 Some data suggest placental abnormalities may vary depending on the presence of left- vs right-sided obstructive lesions, which can significantly alter fetal blood flow patterns, as well as the degree of fetal and placental hypoxemia.6,12,13 PMP is associated with adverse outcomes in the general population such as intrauterine growth restriction, preterm birth, stillbirth, and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes.14,15 We have previously demonstrated that PMP is associated with impaired fetal growth in CHD6; however, the extent to which PMP affects outcomes in CHD and the mechanisms leading to PMP development are unclear.

The fetal–placental–cardiac axis is defined by the interdependence of the developing placenta and fetal heart. The 2 organs develop concurrently during human gestation and share several key developmental pathways.1 For example, Notch and Wnt signaling regulate both cardiomyocyte specification and extravillous trophoblast invasion, whereas vascular endothelial growth factor and Connexin 43 are required for both endocardial cushion development and trophoblast cell fusion.16–20 Alterations in angiogenesis and vascular developmental pathways have been implicated in both isolated preeclampsia and CHD.21–23 Maternal and fetal genetic variants in angiogenic pathways have been implicated in preeclampsia,24,25 whereas in CHD, genetic variants in proangiogenic genes have been associated with abnormal umbilical artery Doppler profiles and decreased fetal growth.26

Given the shared developmental signaling of the fetal heart and placenta and possible dysregulation of shared pathways leading to both placental and cardiac disease, we hypothesized that genomic variation in pathways involved in vascular or placental development underlie PMP in CHD. We sought to evaluate the extent to which PMP is associated with an array of adverse outcomes in various CHD subtypes and determine whether genetic variants in shared developmental pathways play a role in PMP and CHD outcomes.

METHODS

STUDY DESIGN.

This was a retrospective case-control study of 299 CHD subjects with and without PMP, prenatally diagnosed with CHD at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) Fetal Heart Program. Subjects and parents were consented and consecutively enrolled in the CHOP Birth Defects Biorepository (BDB) between 2019 and 2023 under an Institutional Review Board approved protocol (IRB 18–015525). Abstracted and deidentified data from the electronic health record were provided by the BDB to study investigators, which included maternal demographics, maternal and fetal clinical data, clinical genetic testing results, and postnatal outcomes data. Clinical genetic testing results available for review included previously performed chromosomal microarray in 75% of the cohort and whole exome sequencing in 16.7% of the cohort.

Syndromic CHD was defined as the presence of aneuploidy, pathogenic or likely pathogenic chromosomal abnormalities, pathogenic or likely pathogenic single-gene defects, or clinical suspicion of a genetic syndrome associated with CHD. Extracardiac anomalies were not sufficient for syndromic classification without a clinical or molecular diagnosis of a genetic syndrome. All mothers delivered their infants either in the CHOP Special Delivery Unit or the adjacent Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. Cord blood, parental blood, and placentas were collected at delivery, and additional whole genome sequencing was performed on all probands and most parental samples. Exclusion criteria were multiple gestation, nonstructural heart disease, or lack of placental examination. Length of follow-up for survival analysis varied according to time since study enrollment.

PLACENTAL ANALYSIS.

All placentas were examined in the pathology department at CHOP according to the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group 2016 classification.27 Macroscopic examination included recording the trimmed placental weight, membrane insertion, gross appearance, dimensions of the placental disc, and the insertion, length, and diameter of the umbilical cord. Pathologic criteria for placental lesions were defined as previously described.6 Presence of a small-for-gestational age placenta (placental weight < 10%) was included as a quantitative criterion of maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM), often considered a diagnostic feature.28

SPECIMEN COLLECTION AND GENOMIC DNA EXTRACTION.

Cord blood was collected from all neonates at delivery. Parental blood or saliva samples were collected by a phlebotomist in the CHOP Center for Human Phenomic Science or in conjunction with clinical laboratories. Blood was stored at room temperature until processing within 24 to 72 hours of receipt. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIA-symphony DSP DNA Midi Kit (Qiagen).

GENOME SEQUENCING.

Whole genome sequencing was performed on all 299 study subjects at the Broad Institute (Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA) at a targeted mean coverage of 60×. Then, 350 ng of genomic DNA was fragmented using a Covaris focused-ultrasonicator, targeting 385-bp fragments. Additional size selection was performed using solid phase reversible immobilization technology. Library preparation was performed using the KAPA Hyper Prep kit without amplification module (product KK8505; KAPA Biosystems), with palindromic forked adapters with unique 8-base index sequences embedded within the adapter (Roche). Libraries were quantified using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (KAPA Biosystems) with adapter-specific probes, normalized to 2.2 nM, and pooled into 24-plexes.

Cluster amplification and sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 using sequencing-by-synthesis kits to produce 151-bp paired-end reads. Raw image files from the sequencer (bcl files) were converted to fastq using the Illumina bcl2fastq software tool. Raw sequence reads were independently mapped to the reference genome (hg38) using the BWA-mem and further processed using the GATK Best Practices workflows to produce variant calls (VCF). Variants that passed quality filters (FILTER=PASS) were annotated using the VEP software (v106)29 to include several annotations including functional consequences of variants (missense, nonsense, synonymous, etc), population allele frequencies from gnomAD v3.2,30 computational prediction scores such as REVEL (computational score for deleteriousness of missense variants), and conservation scores such as GERP.

VARIANT FILTRATION AND IDENTIFICATION OF POTENTIALLY DELETERIOUS DE NOVO VARIANTS.

Several predetermined thresholds were used to identify rare, conserved, and deleterious variants. Variants at a global minor allele frequency threshold of #0.5% in gnomAD v3.1.2 were considered rare, and variants with a GERP score of ≥3 were considered conserved. Potentially deleterious missense variants were identified using a REVEL score ≥0.5, a composite scoring metric for predicting pathogenic missense variants based on a variety of independent metrics (MutPred, FATHMM, VEST, PolyPhen, SIFT, PROVEAN, MutationAssessor, MutationTaster, LRT, GERP, SiPhy, phyloP, and phastCons).31 A SpliceAI score ≥0.2 was used to determine cryptic splice variants.32 Putative loss-of-function variants included frameshift, nonsense, and obligatory splice variants and were retained as they are. Variants solely in noncoding transcripts were excluded from downstream analysis. IGV screenshots surrounding all exonic, potentially deleterious de novo variants were produced and manually reviewed to confirm the quality of variants.

A subset of 252 probands had parental genome sequencing data available in the cohort and was used to identify de novo variants. First, candidate de novo variants were identified using the bcftools filter command to identify strict heterozygous variants in probands that passed the site quality filter (FILTER=PASS) and homozygous reference genotypes in the parents. Further filtering strategies included the depth filter (≥30), variant allele frequency filter (between 0.2 and 0.8), and a cohort allele count filter (<5) to retain high-quality, rare de novo variants.

BURDEN TESTING.

Gene-wise, exome-wide burden testing was performed using the exactCMC method33 implemented in rvtests34 with variants restricted to exonic and functionally consequential (VEP impact MODERATE and HIGH) with a global minor allele frequency threshold of 0.5% (gnomAD v3.1.2). Resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction method. Multiple gene sets curated from the Gene Ontology (GO) database including those associated with the terms “angiogenesis” and “vasculature development” and the broader GO biological process (825 gene sets) were tested for exome-wide burden of rare variants. The proportion of individuals with at least 1 rare, conserved, and deleterious variant was compared in those with and without PMP using a chi-square test.

PATHWAY ENRICHMENT ANALYSIS.

Genes with de novo deleterious variants were analyzed for enrichment in functional protein interaction networks using the STRING database (string-db.org). K means clustering was performed to identify similar clusters of networks. Gene set enrichment analysis was additionally assessed using Enrichr (Ma’ayan Lab)35 across multiple reference databases. Statistical significance was determined by a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 as determined by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. A hypergeometric test was performed on de novo variants using a custom gene set derived from genes commonly expressed by first trimester hearts and placenta36 to assess for enrichment during early development.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS.

Continuous variables are presented as median (Q1-Q3), whereas categorical variables are presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Standard descriptive statistics were performed to compare groups using Student’s t-test and Wilcoxon test and chi-square and Fisher exact tests depending on the variable type and distribution. Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to assess the HR of mortality for a variety of covariates. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

STUDY POPULATION.

A total of 299 women carrying fetuses with structural CHD were analyzed (Table 1). Of these 299, 153 (51%) had evidence of PMP on placental pathology. Of those with PMP, 64 subjects (41.8%) had fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM) lesions and 135 subjects (88.2%) had MVM lesions and/or small-for-gestational-age placenta. Forty-six subjects (30.1%) with PMP had both FVM and MVM and/or small-for-gestational-age placentas. Examining the study population based on PMP status as an outcome found no significant differences in maternal or fetal characteristics, demographics, conception, or delivery type between CHD fetuses with or without PMP. There were no significant differences in the prevalence of maternal hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, maternal diabetes, or maternal smoking, which often define an “impaired maternal–fetal environment.” Additionally, a similar percentage of mothers were exposed to aspirin and antihypertensive medication between groups. Nationally normed z-scores for the childhood opportunity index, a neighborhood metric of socioeconomic status, were also not different between groups. Those with PMP had an increased third trimester Doppler umbilical artery pulsatility index and a decreased cerebroplacental ratio, which represents the ratio of middle cerebral artery pulsatility index to umbilical artery pulsatility index.

SYNDROMIC CHD.

A genetic syndrome was present in 17.7% of the total cohort (Table 2). The presence of a genetic syndrome was determined from review of clinical genetic testing and clinical examination records, and variants were confirmed with whole genome analysis. There was no significant difference in the prevalence of overall aneuploidy in those with PMP vs those without; however, trisomy 21 was somewhat less common in those with PMP (2.6% vs 7.5%, P = 0.051). Syndromic chromosomal copy number alterations were more common in those with PMP (9.8% vs 2.0%, P < 0.01), with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome found in 4.6% of those with PMP vs 0.7% of those without (P = 0.04). There was no difference in prevalence of single-gene or clinical syndromes, such as Noonan, Alagille, and VACTERL syndromes. Given the known association between genetic syndromes and adverse outcomes, further analysis was stratified on presence of a genetic syndrome.

CHD SUBTYPES.

The largest subtype of CHD present overall was single-ventricle disease (24.4% of total cohort) followed by tetralogy of Fallot (16% of total cohort). CHD fetuses were categorized into anatomical and physiological groupings. The physiological grouping was divided into left-sided obstructive lesions, right-sided obstructive lesions, and transposition. Lesions that did not fit into these categories were designated as “other.” Severe CHD was defined as lesions that required surgery or interventional catheterization within the first 6 months of life or those expected to if the fetus or neonate had survived. In nonsyndromic fetuses with and without PMP, there was no difference in frequency of CHD subtype by anatomical or physiological grouping of the largest CHD categories (Figure 1). In syndromic fetuses, atrioventricular canal defects were less common in those with PMP, possibly due to the decreased prevalence of trisomy 21 in this group. There was no difference in frequency of CHD subtype by physiological grouping in syndromic fetuses with and without PMP.

OTHER PLACENTAL PATHOLOGIES.

Nonsyndromic fetuses with PMP had a slightly lower placental weight-to-birth weight ratio, a marker of increased placental efficiency (Table 3). Umbilical cord abnormalities, defined by the presence of a single umbilical artery, abnormal cord insertion (velamentous, marginal, or furcate) or hypercoiled cord, were more common in nonsyndromic fetuses with PMP compared with those without PMP (41.8% vs 27.4%, P = 0.02), which themselves can predispose to FVM lesions. There was no difference in frequency of other placental pathologies such as chronic or high-grade acute inflammation or delayed villous maturation in either nonsyndromic or syndromic fetuses with or without PMP.

ADDITIONAL CLINICAL OUTCOMES.

Additional clinical outcomes were analyzed assuming PMP as an independent variable. In nonsyndromic CHD fetuses with PMP, there was no difference in frequency of extracardiac anomalies or birth gestational age compared with those without PMP (Table 4). In nonsyndromic fetuses, those with PMP demonstrated significantly decreased birth weight (2985.5 g vs 3329.9 g, P < 0.001), length (48 cm vs 48.9 cm, P < 0.001), and head circumference (33 cm vs 34 cm, P < 0.001) compared with those without PMP. Nonsyndromic fetuses with PMP had a longer index hospitalization length of stay (20.5 days vs 15 days, P = 0.04) and a trend toward increased extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) use (9.8% vs 4%, P = 0.07) and increased overall mortality (12.3% vs 5.6%, P = 0.07) compared with nonsyndromic fetuses without PMP. Unadjusted time-to-event analysis in nonsyndromic fetuses with PMP demonstrated a trend toward decreased overall survival (log rank P = 0.07) (Figure 2). There was no difference in 30-day survival, which was 97% for all nonsyndromic fetuses regardless of PMP status. In those with PMP, survival was 89% at 1 year and 86% at 3 years of follow-up, whereas survival was 94% at 1 and 3 years of follow-up in those without PMP.

In syndromic fetuses, extracardiac anomalies were significantly more prevalent in those with PMP (70.1% vs 40.1%, P = 0.03) (Table 4). There was no difference in birth gestational age, fetal growth metrics, ECMO use, or overall mortality between syndromic fetuses with and without PMP. There was a nonsignificant trend toward a longer index hospital length of stay in syndromic fetuses with PMP (44 days vs 26 days, P = 0.06). There was 1 in-utero fetal demise in a fetus with XXX triploidy who had evidence of PMP.

EXOME WIDE RARE VARIANT ANALYSIS.

The impact of genomic variation on the outcome of PMP status was assessed. Gene-level burden testing using the combining and collapsing multivariate test revealed 194 genes with a higher burden of variants in subjects with PMP (unadjusted P < 0.05), although these differences did not reach statistical significance after adjusting for multiple comparisons (Supplemental Table 1). String protein interactome analysis demonstrated enrichment of 1 cluster of genes in the GO: extracellular matrix gene set (GO: 0031012; FDR: 0.04). Burden testing of all GO Biological Process gene sets did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between groups. The frequency of nonsyndromic subjects with at least 1 variant in the GO: Positive Regulators of Angiogenesis gene set (GO: 0045766), previously found to be associated with impaired neonatal growth and increased umbilical artery pulsatility index in CHD fetuses,26 was similar in CHD fetuses with and without PMP (30.3% vs 28.2%, P = 0.72). Fetuses harboring at least 1 variant in the Positive Regulators of Angiogenesis gene set had no difference in extracardiac abnormalities, birth gestational age, fetal growth, hospital length of stay, ECMO use, or overall mortality compared with fetuses without a variant, irrespective of PMP status (Supplemental Table 2A). De novo single nucleotide variants were similarly prevalent in nonsyndromic CHD fetuses with and without PMP (27.2% vs 33.3%, P = 0.33) and were not significantly associated with adverse outcomes (Supplemental Table 2B).

Gene set enrichment analysis was performed, and string protein interactomes were generated for the gene list with de novo variants from all CHD fetuses with and without PMP (including syndromic fetuses) (Supplemental Tables 3A and 3B) to assess pathway enrichment. Genes with de novo variants in all subjects showed enrichment for several cardiac developmental pathways; however, those with PMP demonstrated distinct enrichment in several key placental developmental pathways (Figure 3). Specifically, CHD fetuses with PMP harbored gene variants enriched in Notch signaling (NOTCH1, NOTCH3, DLL4, MMP2, SMAD6, ID2, SOX9, CHD7) (FDR P < 0.001), extracellular matrix regulation and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (TIE1, FN1, DSP, VIM, MMP2, PUF60, HNRNPK, COL4A4, SPON1) (FDR P < 0.05), and chromatin remodeling pathways (KDM5B, ASH1L, KAT6B) (FDR P < 0.01). De novo variants from CHD fetuses without PMP were enriched for growth factor signaling (FDR P < 0.01) and glycolysis and carbohydrate metabolism pathways (FDR P < 0.01). Hypergeometric testing revealed genes harboring de novo variants in subjects with PMP were significantly enriched in a list of 609 commonly expressed genes in first trimester fetal heart and placental cells36 compared with those without (5/41 [P = 0.007] vs 2/46 [P = 0.4]) Three genes were identified with >1 de novo variant. Two fetuses with PMP harbored distinct deleterious variants in DNAH14, encoding dynein axonemal heavy chain 14. Two fetuses without PMP harbored distinct deleterious variants in PTPN11, a known RASopathy family member causative of Noonan syndrome. One fetus with PMP and 2 fetuses without PMP harbored distinct deleterious variants in the myosin heavy chain genes MHY7 and MYH7B.

DISCUSSION

Despite decades of advancement in operative and postoperative management of patients with CHD, morbidity and mortality remain high.37–39 Our evolving understanding of the role of the placenta in CHD suggests the prenatal environment is the next frontier in improving CHD outcomes; however, actualizing this is limited by the lack of mechanistic studies. This study provides further clinical and mechanistic evidence of this relationship by directly comparing clinical and genomic variation in CHD fetuses with and without PMP.

MVM lesions, believed to be a consequence of defective spiral artery remodeling by placental trophoblasts, and FVM lesions, often due to umbilical cord abnormalities, reflect placental hypoxic–ischemic injury and are associated with adverse birth outcomes.40 Our findings both validate our previous work demonstrating that PMP is associated with impaired fetal growth in CHD6 and reveal additional adverse outcomes associated with PMP in CHD. CHD fetuses with PMP have a longer initial hospital stay and a trend toward increased ECMO use. A multivariable model suggests an independent association between PMP with increased mortality at a 3-year follow-up that approaches significance. Interestingly, there is no difference in 30-day mortality, suggesting the presence of PMP can have delayed adverse effects. These adverse effects are independent of the presence of a genetic syndrome; however, similar albeit nonsignificant trends are present in the smaller group of syndromic CHD, suggesting a potential independent additive effect of PMP on adverse outcomes in syndromic CHD that warrants examination in a larger cohort.

PMP was quite common in our cohort, affecting 51% of patients, irrespective of CHD anatomical or physiological subtype. Thus, these adverse effects of PMP are likely widespread in the CHD population. Examination of de novo variants in CHD fetuses with PMP reveals an enrichment in affected genes in pathways crucial for normal placental development (Table 6). Accordingly, affected genes from subjects with PMP are also enriched in those expressed early in first trimester development of both the fetal heart and placenta. Notch signaling has well-established roles in angiogenesis but also regulates trophoblast formation, differentiation, migration, and survival in both mouse and human and is required for normal decidualization.41 Notch pathway dysregulation has been implicated in disorders of abnormal placentation because of defective trophoblast invasion such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction (FGR).42,43 Several genes we identified with damaging variants in this pathway have well-characterized roles in placental development. NOTCH1, NOTCH3, and DLL4, a transmembrane NOTCH receptor ligand, have been shown to regulate human extravillous trophoblast formation and trophoblast stem cell expansion, migration, and differentiation.44–46 Signaling by DLL4 is mediated by placental growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor.47 SMAD6, a downstream target of Notch signaling, is also a key negative regulator of transforming growth factor-β signaling. SMAD6 regulates endothelial cell function in vascular development,48 and disruption of inhibitory SMADs has been implicated in preeclampsia through its effects on trophoblast growth and differentiation.49

Damaging variants are also enriched in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and extracellular matrix pathways in those with PMP. Extracellular matrix and collagen deposition play a key role in regulating normal trophoblast invasion into the maternal decidua and facilitates cellular communication and function.50,51 The matrix metalloproteinase MMP-2 is secreted by extravillous trophoblasts in early pregnancy to remodel the endometrial basement membrane and facilitate invasion. Decreased levels of matrix metalloproteinases have been found in placentas with preeclampsia.52 Decreased expression of another key extracellular matrix component, fibronectin (FN1), impairs trophoblast invasion and survival in fetuses with FGR and spontaneous abortion.53,54 Several other proteins we identified in this pathway such as VIM and TIE1 have been implicated in abnormal trophoblast function and are key regulators of angiogenesis and vascular development.55,56 We additionally identified chromatin remodeling pathways as enriched for de novo variants in fetuses with PMP. Chromatin remodeling is crucial for normal extravillous trophoblast development,57 and alterations in epigenetic modifications are implicated in FGR and preeclampsia.58

Several genes identified as crucial in placental development are also established causes of CHD (NOTCH1, DLL4, CHD7, PUF60, SMAD6, SOX9, HNRNPK),59–61 raising the possibility of an underlying genetic etiology for adverse development of both organs (Table 6). However, many identified genes have no known role in cardiac development. Interestingly, DNAH14 variants, found in 2 fetuses with PMP, are linked to neurodevelopmental disorders62; however, its function in placental or cardiac development has not been well established. Our study identifies a relatively high proportion of subjects (30% of nonsyndromic cohort) with a de novo damaging variant compared with larger studies by the Pediatric Cardiac Genetics Consortium. Their studies examined up to 2,871 patients and found that de novo variations account for ~20% of all patients with CHD; however, <10% harbor single damaging genic variants, and an even lower incidence exists in isolated CHD without extracardiac or neurodevelopmental abnormalities.63–65

We hypothesize that several factors may contribute to this difference. First, the genome sequencing data used in our study were produced using a polymerase chain reaction–free library preparation protocol and has a mean coverage of 60× (twice the average coverage of standard of care genome sequencing), enabling us to achieve high sensitivity across the genome including the regions where exome sequencing is shown to have low sensitivity (eg, tail ends of the target probes, GC-rich regions, etc).66,67 Differences could also be attributed to the use of different tools and thresholds for computational predictions to determine potentially deleterious variants. However, the incidence of loss-of-function variants in our cohort was similarly higher compared with those found in the Pediatric Cardiac Genetics Consortium cohort, and increasing the REVEL threshold to 0.75 did not meaningfully change the incidence of de novo variation in our cohort (data not shown). We manually reviewed each variant to ensure confidence in each variant call. In addition, the BDB is an inception cohort and includes the most severe cases, whereas the Pediatric Cardiac Genetics Consortium cohort may be affected by survivor bias. BDB phenotyping of extracardiac abnormalities was based on diagnosis codes, which may not be comprehensive. Likewise, neurodevelopmental outcomes data were unavailable for the entire cohort, and those with neurodevelopmental abnormalities were included. Sixteen of 87 genes with de novo variants we identified (18.4%) overlapped with those reported by the Pediatric Cardiac Genetics Consortium.65 Although de novo variants have been associated with decreased transplant-free survival in CHD, we did not find an association between the presence of at least 1 de novo variant and adverse outcomes in our cohort, likely because of our smaller sample size.

Placental genomic aberrations are commonly associated with placental dysfunction and adverse birth outcomes.68,69 Albeit very preliminary, our data suggest that shared genetic perturbations in mutually conserved developmental pathways between the fetal heart and placenta could lead to maldevelopment of both organs. Indeed, placental dysfunction can be causative of CHD in animal models.70,71 Our data suggest that abnormal placental development leading to impaired placental blood flow and oxygenation can compound the altered hemodynamics of CHD and result in worse short- and long-term outcomes. Understanding the early molecular dysregulation occurring in PMP in CHD through integration of multiomics approaches will lead to the identification of targetable disease biomarkers, just as molecular biomarkers of PMP conditions have led to the widespread use of aspirin for preeclampsia72 and investigation of in utero therapies such as maternal vascular endothelial growth factor gene therapy for severe FGR.73 Thus, placental structure and function should ideally be systematically assessed in the fetus with CHD, including routine collection of placentas for characterization at delivery. Abnormalities in umbilical vein flow, microvascular flow, and placental magnetic resonance imaging are associated with CHD, placental dysfunction, and adverse birth outcomes.6,74,75 These imaging biomarkers could be used in combination with genetic or molecular biomarkers to identify early PMP in CHD, improving risk stratification and enabling therapeutic monitoring. An increased umbilical artery pulsatility index and decreased cerebroplacental ratio, as was found in this study, reflect increased placental resistance and compensatory “brain-sparing” cerebral vasodilation and correlate with PMP in obstetric cohorts.76 Abnormalities in these Doppler indices should raise suspicion for PMP; however, these metrics may be normal in the setting of placental pathology.77,78

Existing data suggest PMP mechanisms and outcomes may vary by CHD subtype,6,12,79 which result in heterogeneous effects on fetal hemodynamics and oxygenation. When compared with fetuses with hypoplastic left heart syndrome, placentas from fetuses with dextro-transposition of the great arteries demonstrate similar histological abnormalities yet preserve cell activity and nutrient transport pathways, possibly leading to increased placental efficiency and preserved fetal birth weight.79 Similar to some others,5,7 our work demonstrated no difference in frequency of PMP among various CHD subtypes. Although mechanisms and outcomes associated with PMP likely vary by CHD subtype, meaningful interpretation of this variation requires larger cohorts.

STUDY LIMITATIONS.

This is a single-center retrospective study in which most enrolled fetuses had, by definition, severe CHD that necessitated delivery in the CHOP Special Delivery Unit. Although most studies have focused on severe forms of CHD, there is evidence to suggest PMP is equally prevalent among simpler forms of CHD,7 and thus its negative impact may have broad implications that warrants further investigation. The modest sample size may limit the ability to detect clinically significant differences with statistical significance, in particular the association between genetic variation and outcomes as well as CHD subtype-specific outcomes. Accordingly, none of the burden testing analysis was significant after correcting for multiple testing, which would require a much larger cohort. In addition, the BDB does not enroll normal control subjects, so this population was not available for comparison. Similarly, the study was limited by the deidentified phenotypic and outcomes data available within the BDB that did not include important outcomes such as neurodevelopmental testing, which is likely impacted by PMP and genetic variants, and warrants further study. Multivariable regression modeling for survival may have been impacted by collinearity of predictor variables. Finally, in our study, fetal genetic variants identified from cord blood are assumed to also represent placental genetic variants. However, confined placental chromosomal mosaicism is believed to affect 1% to 2% of all pregnancies80 and can be associated with FGR and adverse pregnancy outcomes.81 A potentially higher rate of single nucleotide variants confined to the placenta exists.82 Thus, direct examination of placental gene variation as well as correlating genetic variation with placental gene and protein expression in CHD is warranted.

CONCLUSIONS.

We demonstrate that PMP is highly prevalent in various forms of CHD and is associated with a variety of adverse outcomes including impaired fetal growth, longer hospital stay, and a trend toward increased mortality. Our work implicates de novo variants in several pathways vital to normal placental and cardiac development in PMP in CHD, including Notch signaling, extracellular matrix regulation and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and chromatin remodeling (Central Illustration). Further study in larger cohorts and correlation with multiomics approaches will allow the identification of signaling pathway dysregulation underlying PMP in CHD, which will ultimately improve CHD outcomes through early detection of placental disease in utero, improved prenatal risk stratification, and identification of biomarker-guided therapeutic targets to improve placental blood flow and function.
